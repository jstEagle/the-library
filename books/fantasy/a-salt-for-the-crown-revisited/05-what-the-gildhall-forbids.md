## 5. What the Gildhall Forbids

The child slept in the counting room, curled beneath Zephyrine's own cloak on a pallet of ledger-leather that had been deemed too worn for records and too fine for kindling. Her breathing had the rhythm of tide against a seawall—in, and out, and in again, patient as something that had been waiting a very long time to be heard.

Zephyrine did not sleep. She sat at the long table with the oldest ledger open before her, the one that wrote back, and she watched the ink of the newest entry dry by lamplight.

*The Remnant sleeps. She dreams in the tongue before tongues. Her keeper counts what she cannot: that the covenant was not broken. It was paid.*

She had read the line eleven times. The ink did not change. That was somehow worse.

"You're going to wear a groove in the floor," said Sefa Vane from the doorway, "walking the same circle of thought."

Zephyrine hadn't heard the under-clerk approach, which was unlike her, and which she chose not to examine. Sefa was a narrow woman with ink-stained cuffs and the particular stillness of someone who had spent forty years among objects that sometimes objected to being catalogued. She carried two cups of chicory and set one down within Zephyrine's reach without ceremony.

"The Gildmaster wants her inventoried by morning," Zephyrine said.

"I know. I'm to draw up the valuation schedule." Sefa lowered herself into the chair opposite, and for a moment she only looked at the child—the mud still dried in the creases of her ears, the small hands curled like ferns before they unfurl. "Zeph. What is she?"

"You read the ledger same as I did."

"The ledger says *Remnant*. The ledger says *covenant*." Sefa's voice dropped to the register Gildhall clerks used for dangerous words, the one that kept them from taking root in the air. "It doesn't say what those mean. And you've been in this hall eleven years, and you've never once looked at a ledger the way you're looking at that one. Like it owes you an apology."

Zephyrine wrapped her hands around the chicory. The cup was warm. The warmth felt borrowed.

"There's a law," she said. "You've been here longer than me. You'll know it better than I do."

Sefa's eyebrows rose. "You know the law. Everyone knows the law. It's the only law we have."

"Recite it for me anyway. All of it. Not the shorthand we use with clients."

The under-clerk glanced at the child, then at the ledger, then at the door, as though checking whether the walls had grown ears. In the Gildhall of Impossible Things, this was not paranoia. It was professional courtesy.

"No memory," Sefa recited, "may be traded freely given."

"That's the shorthand."

"No," Sefa said slowly, "that's the law. That's the whole of it. One sentence. It's carved over the founding door in a script nobody's translated, and the translation beneath it is the sentence. One law, Zeph. Every other trade in this building—names, griefs, years, debts, the color of a stranger's childhood—every one of those has schedules, valuations, appeal processes, seventeen kinds of paperwork. But that one's just *no*. No schedule. No valuation. No appeal. You can sell your memory of your mother's face. You can sell your memory of your own wedding. You can sell the memory of a betrayal so complete it unmade you, and we'll broker it at fair market rate and notarize the grief. But the moment a memory is *freely given*—" she made a small, sharp gesture, cutting the air, "—the trade is void. Not refused. *Void*. As if it never happened. As if the memory was never offered at all."

"Void," Zephyrine repeated. "Meaning what, exactly? Meaning the Gildhall won't record it?"

"Meaning the Gildhall *can't*." Sefa's voice had gone quiet in a way that made Zephyrine look up. "I asked old Master Ellery that once, when I was new. Why can't we record it? A trade's a trade. He told me—"

She stopped. Her hands had gone still around her cup.

"He told me that a memory given freely isn't a trade at all. It's a *sealing*. That's what he said. Sealing. Like wax. Like a door. He said the founding brokers learned it in the first century, when someone tried it, and what came of it was—" She shook her head. "He never said what came of it. He just stopped teaching that day and took the vow of silence the novices take, and he never spoke again, and he watered the courtyard plants for thirty years until he died."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that had no source. In the pallet's corner, the child murmured something in her sleep—a syllable, two, in that language older than any kingdom—and the ink in the open ledger shivered, the letters rippling like a reflection disturbed by a thrown stone, before settling back into their lines.

Zephyrine's oath-mark burned.

It was a faint thing, usually—a dull warmth at the base of her throat, the brand of the silent god she had sworn to thirty years ago and never once heard from since. She had learned to read it like weather. A flicker meant a lie told in her presence. A dull ache meant a debt going unpaid somewhere within her god's jurisdiction, whatever that jurisdiction had been before the silence. For three decades it had been, at most, a candle guttering.

Now it flared like a struck match against the inside of her skin.

She set down the cup because her hands were no longer steady enough to be trusted with it, and she pressed her palm flat against the base of her throat, and she breathed through it the way she'd been taught—count the entries, one through ten, count them again—until the burning ebbed to an ache.

"Zeph?" Sefa was on her feet. "What is it? Your mark—"

"It's nothing. It does this." It did not do this. "Tell me about the founding brokers. The one who tried it. What did they trade?"

"I told you. Nobody knows. Ellery wouldn't—"

"Then tell me why." Zephyrine heard her own voice come out stripped, the weariness scraped off it, and underneath something raw and new. "Why that law. Why one law, out of everything we trade, everything we broker, everything we've priced and sold and notarized for a thousand years—why is *that* the one thing forbidden? Sefa. A freely given memory. Why?"

Sefa sat back down. Slowly. The way people sit when they have just realized the room has changed around them.

"Because of what it does," she said. "That's what Ellery said, before he stopped saying anything. A memory sold is a memory *moved*—from one ledger to another, one head to another, and the world keeps its shape. But a memory *given*—" she turned her cup in her fingers, not drinking, "—given freely, with nothing asked in return, that memory doesn't move. It *binds*. The giver to the receiver. The one who pays to the one who's paid. It's not currency. It's a knot. And a knot like that—" her eyes came up, and they were frightened, "—a knot like that doesn't hold two people together, Zeph. It holds *realms*. That's the rumor, anyway. The story the founding brokers told. That the world is full of doors, and the doors don't have locks. They have *knots*. And the only thing that ties a knot strong enough to hold a door shut between one realm and another is a memory given freely, by someone who means it."

The lamp flame stood very straight.

Zephyrine looked at the ledger. At the line that had written itself in ink she had not dipped her pen into:

*The covenant was not broken. It was paid.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink, as though the ledger had been waiting for her to understand:

*She is beginning to count.*

Her god. Her silent, absent, thirty-years-dead-to-her god. She had sworn herself at the ruined chapel in the salt marshes—young, furious, certain that an oath was a rope you threw to a drowning world—and she had spent thirty years keeping accounts for a voice that never came. She had mourned it the way you mourn a parent who left without a note: with anger folded inside the grief, so tightly you couldn't tell which was holding which up.

*He didn't leave,* she thought. The understanding did not arrive so much as surface, the way drowned things surface, slowly and all at once and too late to be a surprise. *He didn't abandon the oath. He didn't abandon* me. *He paid.*

A memory, freely given. A god's memory—of his worshippers, of his name, of the world he kept—given freely into the knot that held the door between realms. That was the silence. Not abandonment. Not death. *Payment*. He had tied himself into the lock of some door that had to stay shut, and the price of the knot was everything he was, and what remained on this side of the door was a brand on a mercenary's throat and thirty years of quiet.

"He gave it," she said aloud. Her voice sounded like someone else's, someone younger, standing in a ruined chapel with rain coming down. "He gave it freely. That's why he's silent. That's why—" She stopped, because the next part arrived with its own terrible weight. "That's why there's a law. Because if anyone in this hall gave a memory freely, even one, even the smallest—"

"It would tie a knot," Sefa whispered.

"And knots hold doors. And doors—" Zephyrine looked at the child, asleep in the lamplight, breathing in and out with the patience of tide. "And doors between realms, Sefa, don't all want to stay shut."

The silence that followed was the specific silence of the Gildhall at night, when the bottled griefs on their shelves settled and the forgotten names in their drawers dreamed and the building itself seemed to listen. Zephyrine had always found it companionable. Tonight it sounded like held breath.

"The child," she said. "The Remnant. If she's a fragment of the covenant—if she's what's left of the knot—"

"Then she's not inventory." Sefa's jaw had set. "She's not a trade. Corvane wants to catalogue her, put a valuation schedule on her, and Zeph, you can't put a price on—"

"I know what Corvane wants." Zephyrine rose. Her oath-mark still ached, a low banked fire, but the ache had changed character. It no longer felt like weather. It felt like attention. "Corvane wants to own the door. He's been hoarding impossible things for twenty years, and now the most impossible thing in the world has walked mud into his hall, and he thinks it's stock."

"And you?"

Zephyrine looked down at the ledger. The ink had written one more line while they spoke, small and neat and in a hand that was, she realized with a vertigo she felt in her teeth, her own:

*Ask what it costs to untie a knot. Then ask who pays.*

"I think," she said, "that I've been keeping accounts my whole life for a god who couldn't answer. And I think I've just learned why. And I think—" her hand rose without her permission to the mark at her throat, "—I think the law over the founding door isn't a prohibition, Sefa. It's a warning label. It's there because the founding brokers saw what a freely given memory *does*, and they were afraid of it, and they should have been. Because it's the only thing in any realm strong enough to seal a door."

"Or open one," Sefa said.

The lamp guttered. On the pallet, the child opened her eyes—not with the start of a waking child, but with the calm of someone surfacing from a depth she had chosen—and looked directly at Zephyrine, and spoke.

One word. The old tongue, the language older than kingdoms, the syllables that made ledger ink crawl. But this time, for the first time, Zephyrine understood it. Not translated—*understood*, the way you understand your own name, the meaning arriving whole in the marrow before the mind can object.

The word meant *soon*.

The child closed her eyes again and slept, and the ink in the oldest ledger dried into a new entry, and Zephyrine stood in the lamplight with her hand at her throat and understood, at last, with the terrible clarity of a balanced column, what reforging the covenant would cost, and who would be asked to pay it.

She was still standing there when the knock came at the founding door—three slow blows, formal as a summons—and Sefa, rising, went pale.

"That's the night entrance," she said. "Nobody uses the night entrance except—"

Through the door, muffled by a thousand years of oak, came a voice Zephyrine had last heard reading proclamations in a Crown court: measured, courteous, and carrying the particular authority of a man who had never once doubted his own righteousness.

"Gildhall of Impossible Things. Open in the name of the Crown. We come for the abomination."

* * *

Zephyrine's hand was on the ledger before Sefa finished speaking.

"Take her," she said. "The child. Out through the scriptorium, down the salt stair, into the marsh cellars. There's a boat."

"Zeph—"

"*Take her.*" The words came out with a ferocity that surprised them both, that surprised her down to the marrow, and she understood in the same instant that this too had been written somewhere, in some ledger, in a hand like her own, long before tonight. "If the Crown takes her, she's a specimen. If Corvane takes her, she's stock. Go."

Sefa went. The child went with her, small feet silent on the stones, and at the scriptorium door she turned and looked back at Zephyrine with those eyes that held the patience of tide, and said nothing, because she had already said the only word that mattered.

*Soon.*

Zephyrine closed the oldest ledger, and her palm rested on its cover for one long breath, and she felt—distinctly, unmistakably, for the first time in thirty years—something on the other side of the silence press back.
