## 6. Hunters of the Hollow Oath

The road to Vellamere smelled of salt and rot, and Inquisitor Dain Maret had always believed that was fitting. Sin, in his experience, smelled much the same—sweet at first, then souring, until a man could not remember why he had ever wanted it.

He rode at the head of six riders, his gray cloak snapping in the marsh wind, the Crown's seal of interlocking chains hanging cold against his chest. Behind him the Gildhall rose out of the fog like something that had grown there rather than been built—a crooked tower of black timber and pale stone, its windows lit with a light that did not flicker. He had passed it twice before in his career and had never once been asked inside. The Guild traded in impossible things, the writs said, and the Crown tolerated it for reasons no inquisitor was ever told.

Today he had been told. Today he carried a sealed order bearing the Privy Seal itself, and the word *abomination* written in a clerk's careful hand.

"The child," said Brother Cassel, riding at his left, a thin man whose piety showed mostly in his knuckles, which were white around his reins. "You believe it's truly her? The one from the reports?"

"I believe the reports," Maret said. "A girl found in the salt flats, mud to the waist, speaking a tongue that predates the Concordance. A tongue that made a hedge-priest in Tharrow weep blood when he heard it." He paused. "I believe that whatever came out of those flats is not a child anymore."

"And if it resists?"

Maret looked at him. Cassel was young enough to still think faith was a wall rather than a door. "Then we do what the Crown sent us to do," he said, and heard how flat his own voice sounded, like a coin dropped on stone.

They reached the gate as the tide turned. The Gildhall's doors stood open—not, he would note later, because they had been opened, but because they had never quite been closed—and a woman in a ledger-keeper's gray apron waited on the steps with the stillness of someone who had known they were coming since morning.

"You're expected," she said. "Gildmaster Corvane asks that you conduct your business quickly and quietly. We have inventory that dislikes noise."

"Inventory," Maret repeated.

"Everything here is inventory, Inquisitor. Even you, briefly." She stepped aside. "This way."

* * *

The interior of the Gildhall was worse than any church Maret had ever raided, because nothing in it pretended to be holy. Shelves climbed into shadow, crowded with jars that glowed faintly, boxes that hummed, cages that should not have held what moved inside them. A man at a counter was buying something small and dark wrapped in oilcloth; he paid with a ring pulled from his own finger and walked away lighter, whistling. The air tasted of beeswax and old rain.

Corvane received them in a room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers. He was a tall man gone soft at the edges, with rings on every finger and eyes like a moneylender doing arithmetic behind a smile.

"Inquisitor Maret." He spread his hands. "The Crown honors us."

"The Crown requires a child," Maret said. "Found on the flats three days past. Taken in by this house."

"Taken in." Corvane's smile thinned by a degree. "Inquisitor, let us be precise with one another, since precision is our trade. The child entered these premises uninvited, spoke gibberish at our ledgers, and has been under observation since. Observation is not sanctuary. If the Crown wants her, the Crown may have her—but I'll want the extraction noted properly. Inventory leaves this house by receipt or not at all."

"You'd hand over a living child with a receipt."

"I'd hand over a storm with a receipt, if the storm crossed my threshold." Corvane laced his fingers. "You've read the reports. You know what she is. Or what the priests say she is. I make no claims about souls, Inquisitor; souls are above my station. But things that frighten hedge-priests tend to frighten my clients too, and frightened clients are bad for trade. Take her with my blessing."

Something in the smoothness of it snagged Maret, the way a fisherman feels the line go slack just before the hook sets. *Too easy.* But he had ridden four days through marsh stink for this, and ease was a gift he was in no mood to refuse.

"Where is she?"

"Below, with our keeper of accounts." Corvane rang a small bell. "Zephyrine Yarrow will take you down. Do try not to damage anything. Some of the inventory down there is older than the Crown, and unlike the Crown, some of it bites."

* * *

She met them at the stairhead: a woman perhaps forty, gray-streaked hair bound back, an oath-mark inked at her throat in a script Maret did not recognize—the mark of one of the old covenant orders, the kind that had starved out decades ago when their gods went quiet. Her eyes were the color of a winter harbor, and they went over him the way an assayer goes over a coin: weight, edge, purity, flaw.

"Inquisitor." No bow. "The child is below. Before you go down, you should know two things."

"Keeper, I don't require—"

"One," she said, as if he hadn't spoken, "she isn't dangerous. She has been fed, warmed, and slept more peacefully than any child I've ever seen, including ones who hadn't spent three days alone in the flats. Two—" and here her voice changed, dropped into something quieter and far more dangerous than anger, "—whatever your order says she is, look at her before you decide. That's all I ask. Look at her. Not the report. Her."

Maret had burned churches full of people who asked him to *look*. He said, "Lead on, Keeper."

The lower vault was warm, which surprised him. Lamps burned low among shelves of bottled griefs—he recognized the pale blue light of them now, having confiscated a shipment once—and in the center of the room, on a pallet that someone had made up with genuine care, sat the child.

She was small. Seven years old, perhaps eight, though the flats had scoured her so clean of everything soft that age seemed a rumor about her. Mud still dried in the creases of her elbows. She was sitting cross-legged, utterly still, watching a sparrow.

The sparrow lay on the stones beside her, on its side, wings at wrong angles, breath coming in small broken hitches. It must have come down through some vent and broken itself against the vault floor. Its eye was already glazing.

"She brought it down herself," Zephyrine Yarrow said quietly. "Carried it in her hands for an hour. Wouldn't eat until it was settled."

"It's dying," Cassel said.

"Yes," the keeper said. "It is."

Maret stepped forward, and the child looked up at him.

He had prepared himself for many things. The reports had used words like *wrongness* and *uncanny* and *abomination*, and he had built a picture out of them, the way clerks build pictures out of columns: something twisted, something with too-old eyes, something that needed putting down for the world's health.

What he saw was a tired, mud-caked little girl looking up at a stranger with an expression he knew intimately, because he had seen it on the faces of the condemned and the bereaved and once, in a mirror, on his own after his wife's fever took her: the expression of someone waiting to find out if the world was going to be cruel today.

"You've come for me," she said—in Trade-Tongue, clear and ordinary, a child's voice with a child's grammar.

"I have my orders," Maret said.

"They always do." She looked back at the sparrow. "So did the men who broke the ford at Callowmere. So did the priest in Tharrow, afterward, when he couldn't stop weeping." She said it without accusation, the way a child recites a lesson. Then she cupped her hands around the bird, bent her head, and spoke.

Not Trade-Tongue. Not anything. The sound that came out of her was older than sound—low, syllableless, like wind moving through a cave that had been closed since the world was made. The lamps guttered. Every bottle on the shelves brightened at once, a hundred pale blue flames leaning toward her like grass toward sun. And the oath-mark at the keeper's throat flared white through her collar, and Maret felt the seal of chains on his own chest go suddenly, painfully cold, as if the metal remembered being something else.

The sparrow shuddered. Righted itself. Stood—wings folded neat, both legs whole, the shattered angles simply *gone*—and flew a slow circle around the child's head before settling on her shoulder and beginning, absurdly, horribly, wonderfully, to preen her hair.

Silence took the vault. Even the humming boxes had stopped.

Cassel had backed against the shelving, one hand pressed to his mouth, whispering scripture in a voice cracked down the middle. Maret stood very still, and in the stillness he became aware that his certainty—that great gray wall he had built stone by stone over twenty years of burnings and bindings and signed orders—had developed a crack running through it from top to bottom, and through the crack he could hear something on the other side breathing.

"That," he said finally, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, "is not possible."

"No," Zephyrine Yarrow agreed. "But it happened. Welcome to the Gildhall of Impossible Things, Inquisitor. Would you like to revise your assessment of the inventory?"

The sparrow chirped. The child watched Maret with those patient eyes, and said nothing else, and somehow that was worst of all—because abominations raged and hissed and gave a righteous man something to strike at, and this small creature only looked at him as if she already knew exactly which way his heart would break, and was sorry for it in advance.

* * *

He wrote the first report that night in the guest cell the Gildhall provided, by candlelight, and found he could not finish it.

*Subject displays pre-Concordant speech,* he wrote. *Subject displays thaumaturgic capability inconsistent with any registered discipline. Subject healed a common sparrow of mortal injury via vocalization. Recommend immediate transfer to—*

His pen stopped at *transfer*. Transfer where? To whom? The Ecclesiarchy's doctrine on pre-Concordant speech filled half a shelf: such tongues were the language of the sundered realms, and anything that spoke them was either a demon wearing innocence or—here the old texts grew cautious, evasive, almost frightened—*a remainder of the First Covenant, which the Crown's founders swore was ended.*

Ended. Sworn ended. By whom, and at what cost, the texts did not say. Maret had always assumed the omission was scholarly modesty.

Now, sitting in a cell that smelled of beeswax while somewhere below him a child slept with a sparrow roosting in her hair, he wondered if the omission was something else entirely. He wondered what a founding generation would have had to do to end a covenant with a god. He wondered what they would have had to promise their descendants never to ask.

He wondered, most of all, why the Privy Seal's order had specified the child be taken *alive and unexamined*, delivered not to the Ecclesiarchy but to a nameless holding at Greywater Fort—and why the courier who brought it had refused to meet his eyes.

Brother Cassel knocked, entered, and stood wringing his thin hands. "Inquisitor. I've been praying."

"Has it helped?"

"No." Cassel sat heavily. "That word she spoke. I've heard it before. Not the sound—the *shape* of it. In the oldest office of the Rite of Severance, the part they cut from the liturgy two centuries ago. There's a line the celebrant whispers, facing east, when the covenant is being formally dissolved." His voice dropped. "*And let the voice be given unto silence, that the worlds may lie apart.* Inquisitor, the shape of her word was the shape of that line. Reversed."

Maret set down his pen. "Reversed."

"As if—" Cassel swallowed. "As if she wasn't dissolving a covenant, sir. As if she was *beginning* one."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Down in the vaults, faint as a heartbeat, Maret thought he heard the ledgers turning their own pages.

He looked at the unfinished report, at the word *abomination* waiting in his instructions, at the careful clerk's hand that had written it. Then he took a fresh sheet, and wrote instead: *Assessment suspended pending further observation,* and signed it, and did not sleep.

* * *

Near dawn, a rider came up the marsh road through the fog—hard-spurred, mud-splashed, wearing no colors—and demanded entry at the Gildhall's door. Maret watched from his window as the woman in the gray apron admitted him, and saw the packet he carried bear not the Privy Seal but a private signet he recognized from intelligence briefings: the mark of Lord Chancellor Vessic's own household.

An hour later, Gildmaster Corvane's voice floated up the stairwell, warm and oiled and carrying perfectly in the salt air:

"A second offer, you say. How flattering. And the Crown's inquisitor—does he factor into your master's arithmetic, or shall we consider him... overhead?"

Maret stepped back from the window slowly. Below him, the child slept. On the marsh road, the fog was thickening, and somewhere in it, he was suddenly certain, rode more riders than the six he had brought.

He had come to Vellamere to hunt an abomination. He was beginning to understand, with the cold clarity of a man watching the tide come in over his boots, that the only hunters here were the ones who had hired him—and that the thing they feared was sleeping peacefully downstairs, guarded by a sparrow and a keeper with a burning oath-mark, and that his own sealed orders had never once mentioned bringing her back.

Only taking her. Alive, unexamined, and alone.

Maret buckled on his sword, woke Brother Cassel, and went down to find Zephyrine Yarrow.

"We're leaving," he said. "Tonight. All of us. Before whoever bought the road arrives to collect."
