## 7. Flight Through the Salt Marshes

They left by the coal door, the one Corvane pretended did not exist because its existence implied the Gildhall could be entered without paying.

Zephyrine had the child on her hip before she fully understood she was carrying her — a small, mud-crusted weight that clung with the desperate competence of something that had survived alone too long to trust being held. The girl's hair smelled of river silt and old smoke. Her bare feet were gray to the ankle.

"Name," Zephyrine whispered as they slipped into the alley behind the tallow-render's yard. "I need a name for you. Ledgers want names."

The child looked up at her with eyes the color of shallow water over slate and said nothing in any language Zephyrine knew. But somewhere behind her sternum, the oath-mark — cold for thirty years, a scar of silver script beneath her collarbone — gave one slow pulse of heat, like a banked coal remembering fire.

"Fine," Zephyrine said. "Unnamed, then. Unnamed things are worth more anyway. Everyone knows that."

It was a joke. The child didn't laugh, but she pressed her forehead briefly against Zephyrine's jaw, and Zephyrine felt the gesture land somewhere no ledger line could reach.

Behind them, the Gildhall's bells began to ring — not the hour, not the trade-bell, but the third bell, the one rung only when inventory walked out of the building under its own power. Zephyrine had heard it twice in eleven years. Both times, someone had been dragged back through the coal door before dawn.

She turned down the tanner's lane and ran.

* * *

The salt marshes began where the city's refuse ended, a gray-green country of channels and sedge and half-drowned alders, stitched together by causeways that appeared at low tide and betrayed you at high. The locals called it the Weeping Ground, because the wind through the reeds made a sound like a woman grieving, and because everything buried there eventually floated back up.

Zephyrine knew it the way she knew ledgers: entry by entry. She had catalogued what came out of it for the Gildhall — drowned bells that rang themselves, letters written by the dead to their own younger selves, jars of tide-water that would not dry. She had never once gone in past the first causeway.

Now she went in to the shoulders.

The mud took her boots with every step, sucking, reluctant, as though the marsh itself were weighing whether to keep her. She carried the child across the worst of the channels, water to her ribs, the girl's arms locked around her neck, and thought — absurdly, in the middle of all that cold — about the audit she'd left unfinished on her desk. A bottled grief, third shelf, mislabeled. She'd felt nothing when she held it. That frightened her more than the riders, more than the bells. A mercenary who could hold grief and feel nothing was a mercenary already halfway to being inventory herself.

"Almost," she told the child, who had not once cried out, not in the Gildhall, not in the water, not now. "Almost there. There's a place — an old eel-fisher's stilt-house. It leans. Everything there leans. But it holds."

The child said a word.

It was short. Two syllables, maybe three, worn smooth like a stone from a river that no longer existed. And Zephyrine — who did not know the word, who could not have repeated it if you paid her — understood it completely, the way you understand a blow before the pain arrives.

*Hold.*

Her oath-mark burned. Not the slow pulse this time but a bright, specific heat, tracing the script beneath her skin as though a finger were reading it aloud.

She stopped, chest heaving, water streaming off her elbows. "What did you say?"

The child tilted her head. Said it again. The same syllables, the same impossible comprehension flooding through Zephyrine like water finding a channel it had cut long ago.

"You can't—" Zephyrine started, and then stopped, because the marsh around them had gone quiet. Not silent — quiet. The weeping wind still moved the reeds, but the sound had changed key, softened, as if the ground itself had leaned in to listen.

*Hold,* the word meant. Or perhaps: *be held.* The distinction, she suspected, mattered enormously in whatever language the child dreamed in.

They went on.

* * *

The stilt-house stood where the map said it wouldn't and wasn't where the map said it would, which was the marsh's way of keeping its accounts balanced. It listed eastward on legs black with salt-rot, its roof a pelt of moss and gull filth, its single room smelling of fish oil and decades of weather. Someone had lived here once. Someone had died here, probably. In the Weeping Ground those were often the same person.

Zephyrine got a fire going from driftwood and a twist of waxed tinder she kept in her coat the way other mercenaries kept knives. The child sat close to the flames, hands out, watching the light move across her fingers as though confirming it obeyed the same rules here as wherever she had come from.

"Eat." Zephyrine set out hard bread, dried eel, a heel of cheese gone waxy at the edges. The child ate with terrible efficiency, wasting nothing, and Zephyrine found herself tearing her own portion smaller so there would be more to give. She noticed herself doing it. She noticed noticing, and disliked it. Care was a debt, and debts were how the Gildhall owned people.

But the child looked up at her between bites, and the look said *thank you* in no language at all, and Zephyrine's mark warmed like a hand laid flat against her chest.

Outside, far off across the flats, a horn sounded. Once. Then, after a long pause, twice more — closer.

Maret's riders. They'd reached the marsh edge faster than she'd guessed. Of course they had; the Crown kept the causeways mapped even if no one else dared. Inquisitor Dain Maret rode with six men and a warrant that named the child *an abomination against the order of realms*, which was Crown language for *something we cannot tax or kill cleanly.*

Zephyrine banked the fire to embers and pulled the child away from the one window.

"They'll search the near causeways tonight," she murmured, mostly to herself. "Then the stilt-villages. Then—" She stopped. She was planning aloud in front of a child who might understand every word, given what her words did to ink and oaths and the fabric of quiet.

The child touched her wrist. Pointed at the place beneath Zephyrine's collarbone where the mark lay.

"No," Zephyrine said. "You don't get to read that either."

But the child's expression — grave, patient, unbearably gentle — suggested she already had.

* * *

Sleep took her in fragments, the way it always did on the run: twenty minutes, an hour, the knife in her hand each time she surfaced. Between the fragments, the dreams came.

In the first dream she stood in the Gildhall's counting room, except the shelves went up forever and the labels were written in the child's language, and she could read every one. *Bottled griefs, vintage of the Sundering. Forgotten names, sorted by who misses them.* In the second dream she was drowning in warm water that spoke to her in two syllables, and the meaning rose through her like bubbles: *hold, be held, holding, held* — the same root branching and reblooming, a whole grammar of keeping.

In the third dream, the child stood on a shore of white salt beneath a sky with no sun in it, and spoke a longer phrase, and Zephyrine's sleeping mind translated it word by word as the mark burned:

*The door is closed from the inside. The keeper paid himself for the lock. Ask him why.*

She woke with salt tears on her lips and no memory of crying, and the child was sitting beside her in the dark, awake, watching, as if she had been standing at the edge of the dream making sure Zephyrine came back.

"The keeper," Zephyrine rasped. "Paid himself. What does that mean? Who—"

The child put one small finger against Zephyrine's mouth. Then, slowly, she drew that finger down — from Zephyrine's lips, along her throat, to rest over the oath-mark.

The fire had burned to almost nothing. In its last red light, Zephyrine saw the child's face clearly: not feral, not empty. Waiting. The face of someone carrying a message too large for her body, who had been waiting thirty years for the recipient to be ready.

"No," Zephyrine said again, but softer, and the word came out sounding exactly like *not yet.*

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin through the marsh fog. With it came hoofbeats.

Not on the causeway — worse. Out on the flats themselves, where the mud was firm enough at low tide to bear a light horse if the rider knew the paths, and Maret clearly knew the paths. Zephyrine counted shapes in the fog: four riders, spread wide, sweeping methodically north to south. Behind them, fainter, two more holding the causeway as a net.

She woke the child with a hand over her mouth — gently, an apology in the pressure of her palm — and pointed to the rear wall, where a hatch opened onto a maintenance walk, a plank road built by eel-fishers a century dead, half-sunk, running west toward deeper marsh.

They went out into water to their knees. Fog closed behind them like a door.

For an hour they moved by plank and channel, Zephyrine navigating by habits she hadn't known she had — the lean of certain reeds, the smell of deep silt versus shallow, the way the water darkened over holes. Twice she lifted the child onto her shoulders. Twice the child, unasked, pressed both palms flat against the top of Zephyrine's head, and warmth flowed down through her scalp and spine like sun through window glass, and her exhausted muscles forgot they were exhausted.

"You can't keep doing that," Zephyrine muttered. "Whatever it is. It has a price. Everything has a price. I do this professionally."

The child said a word against her hair.

This one was longer. Four syllables, maybe five, and the translation arrived not as meaning but as memory — a memory that was not hers: a hall of white pillars, a figure kneeling whose face she could not see, a voice like the space between stars saying *this will cost you everything you are, and I will not ask it of you* — and the kneeling figure answering, in a voice Zephyrine almost recognized—

Hoofbeats. Close. Off to the right, no more than fifty yards, cutting between channels.

Zephyrine dropped off the plank road into the sedge, pulling the child down with her, and went still the way she'd been taught stillness thirty years ago by a sergeant whose name she suddenly could not recall — couldn't recall, and the absence had edges, like a tooth pulled clean.

The rider emerged from the fog ten yards away. Dain Maret himself, alone, his warrant-case strapped to his saddle, his face drawn and sleepless. He reined in at the plank road and sat there, listening.

Zephyrine's knife was in her hand. She did not remember drawing it. The child's small hand closed over her wrist — not stopping her, just resting there, a question.

Maret turned his horse along the planks. Toward them. Away from them. Toward.

And then, from the mist ahead of him, a sound: a sparrow, fallen from some roost, fluttering broken-winged in the shallows, crying its small ruined cry.

Maret dismounted. Zephyrine watched him crouch over the bird, watched him lift it in two careful hands — an inquisitor's hands, hands that had signed burn orders — and she watched the child slip out of her grip before she could catch her, wade forward through the sedge into full view, and speak one soft syllable over the sparrow's cupped body.

The wing straightened. The bird shivered, stood, and flew up into the fog singing.

Maret did not move for a long time. When he finally raised his eyes to the child, then to Zephyrine rising out of the reeds with her knife still bare, his certainty — that vast, load-bearing certainty she had seen in a hundred zealots' faces — showed its first crack, fine as a hairline in fired clay.

"That's not possible," he said quietly. It was not an accusation. It was a man reading an entry in his own ledger that his own hand could not have written.

"Ride back the way you came, Inquisitor," Zephyrine said. "Or don't. But stand there much longer and your men will find us both, and then neither of us gets to learn what that was."

He looked at the sparrow's wake fading on the water. At the child. At Zephyrine's mark, which must have been glowing visibly through her soaked shirt, silver script lit like a lamp.

"There's a chapel," Maret said at last, and his voice sounded strange, unmoored, like a man quoting scripture in a language he'd just realized he didn't believe. "Ruined. West, past the drowned orchard. My men won't search it — the Crown struck it from the maps forty years ago. I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Yes, you do," said the child, in the common tongue, her first words in it, clear as a bell over the water.

Maret went pale. He mounted, turned his horse, and rode back into the fog without another word, and the fog took him the way the marsh takes everything — patiently, completely, keeping the receipt.

* * *

They walked west through the morning, past the drowned orchard where apple trees stood to their waists in black water, still bearing fruit no one dared eat. The child rode on Zephyrine's hip, heavy with sleep, and Zephyrine carried her and thought about the dream-memory: a kneeling figure, a voice saying *I will not ask it of you*, and an answer given anyway.

She knew the kneeling figure's build. She knew the set of its shoulders. She had spent thirty years not knowing why her god's silence felt less like abandonment than like a debt she personally owed.

The chapel appeared out of the fog all at once, the way drowned things surface: a roofless shell of gray stone, walls furred with lichen, a doorway with no door opening onto darkness and the smell of old incense gone to salt. Above the lintel, worn nearly smooth, a carved inscription in the same language the child spoke.

Zephyrine could not read it.

But her oath-mark could, and it translated against her skin, letter by letter, in heat:

*Here the silence was chosen. Here it may be unchosen. Enter owing nothing.*

The child stirred against her shoulder, half-asleep, and murmured a word into Zephyrine's neck — the word from the dream, the keeper's word, the one that meant *ask him why* —

—and inside the chapel, in the dark, something answered.

A heartbeat of sound. One syllable, deep as the floor of the sea, passing through stone and water and bone.

Then silence again. But not the old silence. A listening one.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway with the child in her arms and her whole body shaking, and understood that she had been walking toward this door for thirty years without knowing the road existed.

"Right," she whispered, to the god, to the child, to herself. "I'm coming in. And I want my answers itemized."
