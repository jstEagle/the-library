## 8. The Chapel of No Answer

The marsh gave them nothing and asked for everything in return.

Zephyrine waded with the child on her hip, the girl's legs locked around her waist like a vise grown from fear, her face pressed into the hollow of Zephyrine's throat. The water came to Zephyrine's thighs, black and warm as old tea, thick with the rot of seasons no one had bothered to count. Reeds hissed against her arms. Somewhere behind them—two ridges of drowned land back, maybe three—a horn sounded, thin and patient, the way Crown horns always sounded, as though the men blowing them had never once doubted they would find what they hunted.

Zephyrine did not look back. Looking back was a tax she could not afford.

The chapel rose out of the mist an hour past dusk, or what she guessed was dusk; under the cloud-bellies the light never quite committed to dying. It stood on a knoll the salt had been gnawing at for a century, a hunched gray shape like a kneeling animal too tired to rise. Half its roof had gone. The bell tower leaned at an angle that suggested prayer rather than collapse, and its bell was long since stolen—or perhaps it had never rung at all. She remembered it leaning this way. That was the cruelty of the place: she remembered everything, every stone, every splinter, and none of it had ever answered her.

*Here.* The thought arrived without language, the way the child's meanings had begun arriving in her skull over the last two days of flight. *This is where.*

"I know," Zephyrine said aloud, because saying things aloud had become a way of proving to herself that her voice still worked, that she was still one creature speaking to another and not simply a ledger walking through the world recording itself.

She climbed the knoll on hands and knees when the mud refused footing, and dragged them both through the doorway, which had lost its door sometime before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

* * *

Inside, the chapel smelled of salt and bird droppings and something older beneath both—the dry mineral smell of places that had once held a great deal of holiness and now held only its residue, the way a bottle that once held grief still smells faintly of weeping if you know how to breathe it in. Moonlight fell through the broken roof in slanted columns, laying pale stripes across the floor stones, each stripe full of drifting motes that might have been dust or might have been ash, and Zephyrine supposed that after thirty years there was very little difference between the two.

The altar stood at the far end, intact, stubborn. A slab of gray granite on two pillars, its surface scored by weather and by knives—she remembered the knife-scored nights, the blood she'd let into those grooves, the oaths she'd spoken until her voice cracked. Oath-takers came here once, in the years before the Gildhall swallowed the practice whole. They came to swear themselves to the god whose name was not written anywhere because writing it was a trade no one could afford. They came, and they spoke, and sometimes—if the stories were true, and Zephyrine had spent thirty years needing them to be true—the god answered.

He had answered her. Once. One word, on a night of storm, when she was nineteen and desperate and willing to give anything: *Counted.*

Two syllables. She had built her entire life in the shelter of those two syllables the way a snail builds in the shelter of its shell, and then the shell had closed, and for thirty years—

For thirty years, silence.

She set the child down. The girl's bare feet touched the floorstones and she stood very still, head tilted, listening to something in the walls. Mud flaked off her in small dark continents. In the striped moonlight her eyes were the color of deep water, and they were fixed on the altar with an attention that made the hairs rise along Zephyrine's forearms.

"Not yet," Zephyrine said softly. "Let me—"

She didn't finish the sentence, because she didn't know how it ended. Let me what? Pray? She hadn't prayed in eleven years. She had audited, catalogued, appraised, balanced. She had kept accounts for the Gildhall of Impossible Things with a precision that had made Gildmaster Corvane call her *invaluable*, and she had done it all in the long shadow of a silence she told herself was mystery, was doctrine, was the nature of gods. *He speaks in trades,* the elders said. *Every covenant is a conversation conducted in goods.* And so she had traded, and traded, and listened for him between the entries, and heard nothing, and called the nothing faith.

But tonight she had run through a marsh with a feral child burning against her side while Crown horns sang patience behind them, and her left forearm throbbed where the oath-mark sat beneath the skin—a thin line of script older than any alphabet, inked into her by no needle, and hot now for the first time in decades, hot as a brand fresh from the fire.

Tonight she had no audits left to hide behind.

"Answer me." Her voice came out low, scraped. She walked toward the altar, boots squelching, and the sound of her own footsteps in the ruined nave was obscene, too loud, a merchant's tread in a house of silence. "You hear me? I know you hear me. You heard me when I was nineteen and stupid and I gave you—" She stopped at the altar's edge. Laid both palms on the granite. Cold, wet-cold, ordinary cold, the cold of stone and nothing else. "*Counted.* You said I was counted. Do you know what I did with that word? Do you have any conception—"

Her voice cracked. She hated it. She pressed harder against the stone as if she could push through it into whatever lay beneath, the realm behind the realms, the place where he had gone quiet.

"Thirty years." Now it came faster, uglier, the grief finally tearing loose from the ledger-lines she kept it bound in. "Thirty years I kept your books. Not theirs—yours. Every impossible thing that passed through that hall, I checked twice, because I thought—because you said I was *counted* and I thought someone should keep count back. I bottled griefs for widows who couldn't carry them. I sold forgotten names back to men who'd sold their own. I watched Corvane weigh a mother's mourning on a scale and mark it fair market value, and I did it, I did all of it, in your name, in the name of a god who—" Her fist struck the granite. Pain lanced up her wrist. She struck it again anyway. "Who went *quiet*. Who let a child be hunted. Who lets *her*—"

Behind her, the child made a sound.

Not a word. A soft exhalation, almost a sigh, the kind of sound a person makes when setting down something heavy after a long road. Zephyrine turned. The girl had not moved from where she'd been set down, but the moonlight seemed to have gathered around her, pooled at her feet like spilled milk, and her eyes were on Zephyrine—not on the altar, not on the broken roof, but on Zephyrine's face—with an expression no feral child should have been able to compose: pity. Old, gentle, terrible pity.

"Don't," Zephyrine said. Her voice shook. "Don't you look at me like that. Don't you *pity* me. I'm the one keeping you alive. I'm the one—"

The child raised one small, mud-crusted hand and pointed past her, at the altar.

And the oath-mark on Zephyrine's arm burned so suddenly, so fiercely, that she cried out and staggered, catching herself on the granite edge. Beneath her palms the stone warmed—not sun-warm, not body-warm, but a warmth with texture to it, a warmth like a hand turning over in sleep, like breath through cloth. The motes in the moonlight stopped drifting. All of them, every mote in every column of light, hung motionless as if the air itself had been entered into a ledger and frozen mid-entry.

Then the silence broke.

It did not break the way thunder breaks, outward and loud. It broke the way ice breaks on a spring river—one hairline crack running through the whole of it, felt more than heard, a single syllable passing through the fabric of the world. Zephyrine heard it in her teeth and her breastbone and the scar-script on her arm, and it was a word, she realized, one word stretched across the length of her hearing, and the word was:

*—remembered—*

One heartbeat. Perhaps less. A warmth against her palms like the ghost of a hand pressing back from the other side of the stone. The sense—faint, fraying even as she grasped it—of something vast turning its attention toward her after thirty years of holding its face away, of lips parting around a reply already forming, already *counting*—

Gone.

The motes resumed their drifting. The stone went cold under her hands, ordinary and dead. The child swayed on her feet, and Zephyrine lunged and caught her before she fell, and the girl's weight against her chest was real and mortal and shaking.

"No." The word tore out of Zephyrine raw. She sank to her knees on the chapel floor with the child clutched against her, and her voice came apart entirely. "*No.* Come back. Come *back*, you don't get to—I waited, do you understand? Thirty years I waited and you give me one heartbeat? One crack in the ice? What was the point of—" She pressed her mouth to the child's hair, salt-stiff and muddy, and wept the way she had not wept since she was nineteen years old: ugly, graceless, unledgered. "What was the point of any of it, if you were going to leave me *one heartbeat*?"

The child's small hands came up and gripped her collar, and held on, and did not let go.

* * *

Later—it might have been hours; the moon moved and the light-columns crawled and Zephyrine had stopped trusting time somewhere in the marsh—she built a fire in the lee of the fallen roof beams. Small, shielded, the smoke thin enough to lose itself in the cloud. The child ate dried fish from Zephyrine's pack with the efficient ferocity of a creature who had known real hunger, and then, fed, grew strange and quiet, sitting cross-legged near the flames and watching them the way scholars watch difficult texts.

Zephyrine unwrapped the bandage on her forearm. The oath-mark had cooled, but the script had changed.

She stared at it for a long time. Thirty years it had read as it had always read: a single line of pre-kingdom glyphs, the covenant's signature, identical on every sworn mercenary's arm and inert as scar tissue since the Silence began. Now, beneath the old line, in the same impossible hand, a second line had appeared—fresh, faintly raised, pink at the edges like a wound three days healing. She could not read it. But she knew its shape the way you know a face seen once in a crowd, and her stomach dropped through the chapel floor into the marsh below.

"It's the same," she whispered. "It's the same as what the ledgers write."

Because she had seen it. Three nights ago, in the Gildhall, in the oldest ledger—the one that wrote itself, the one that had contained an entry describing her own arrival written a century before her birth—she had seen this exact sequence of glyphs crawling across the page in ink that was still wet. The child's speech transcribed. The covenant's terms, restated. And now restated again, in living skin, on the arm of a woman who had sworn herself to a silent god and been counted, once, long ago.

The child looked up from the fire. Tilted her head. Then she reached across the space between them and laid two fingers, gently, deliberately, on the new line of script.

The dreams came before sleep did.

* * *

She dreamed of the chapel, but the chapel was whole—roof intact, bell hanging, candlelight instead of moonlight—and she was nineteen again, kneeling where she had knelt, speaking the oath with blood in the knife-grooves. But in the dream she saw what she had not seen then: the god was not absent from the room. He was everywhere in it, in the pressure of the candle flames, in the weight of the dark beyond the windows, and when he answered *counted*, his voice cost him something. She felt that now, from the outside of the memory, from nineteen years' distance and thirty years' grief: the answer had been torn out of him like the first coin of a payment that would ruin him. And she saw, dimly, at the edge of the dream, a second figure standing in the doorway of the whole chapel—young, mud-caked, barefoot, eyes like deep water—waiting to be born into a story already written.

Zephyrine woke with salt on her face that was not from the air.

The child was asleep against her side, breathing slow, one hand still curled loosely in Zephyrine's collar. Above them the broken roof framed a sky going gray at its eastern hem. On Zephyrine's forearm, the second line of script had darkened overnight, and beside it a third was rising now, glyph by glyph, slow as frost forming on glass.

She watched it come, and understood, with the calm of a woman who has finished grieving at least for one night, that the mark was translating—that the child's sleeping breath, her small sounds, her wordless meanings, were being written into Zephyrine's skin in the oldest tongue, and that soon—days, perhaps hours—there would be enough of it to read.

Far off across the marsh, a horn sounded. Closer than yesterday. Patient as arithmetic.

The child stirred, opened her deep-water eyes, and looked at Zephyrine with an expression of such grave apology that Zephyrine's breath caught—because it was not the apology of a child. It was the apology of a messenger who knows the letter she carries will break the heart of whoever opens it.

"What is it?" Zephyrine asked quietly. "What are you trying to tell me?"

The girl reached up and touched Zephyrine's lips with one muddy finger. Then she pointed east, toward the drowned lands, toward the border of something that was not on any map the Crown printed—and spoke a single word in the language older than kingdoms.

The third line of script on Zephyrine's arm flared white-hot, complete.

And though she could not yet read it, she knew, with absolute certainty, what it said:

*There is a price, and you have already paid it once.*
