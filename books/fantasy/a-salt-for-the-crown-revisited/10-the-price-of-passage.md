## 10. The Price of Passage

The river did not appear on any map Zephyrine had ever audited.

She had checked—of course she had checked. Three nights in the salt marshes with a child asleep against her ribs had left her little to do but think, and thinking, for a woman who kept accounts for a living, meant cross-referencing. She had gone through every chart the Gildhall kept of the borderlands between realms: the Cartographer's Concordance, the drowned surveys, even Corvane's private folio of unlicensed passages, which she had copied by candlelight before they fled. No river. Not one line of blue ink anywhere it should have been.

And yet here it was, wide as a sea's first thought, running silver-black through a country that had been dry scrubland an hour ago. The water moved without wind. It made no sound against its banks. It simply *was*, the way grief is, the way oaths are—present, total, indifferent to whether anyone had drawn it.

The girl stood at the waterline with her toes in the mud, and the language came out of her low and continuous, like a psalm remembered rather than spoken. Zephyrine's oath-mark burned in answer—not the sharp flare of warning she had learned to dread, but something slower, deeper, a heat that rose from the bone as though her own blood were trying to translate.

*Passage,* the mark said, in the wordless way it said things now. *Passage. Passage.*

"I know," Zephyrine murmured. She shifted the bundle on her back—the child's blanket, the last of their food, the ledger she had stolen from the Gildhall's oldest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth against her spine like a second heart. "I know. But knowing and affording are different countries, little one."

The girl looked up at her. Her eyes were the color of the space between stars, and they held no fear at all, which frightened Zephyrine more than anything the Crown could send after them.

There was a boat at the bank.

Zephyrine was certain there had been no boat when they crested the last dune. There was certainly one now: a long, narrow craft of black wood, older than any joinery she knew, its planks fitted without nail or peg as though the tree had been persuaded to grow already shaped. A lantern hung at the prow, burning with a flame that gave off light but no warmth, and beneath the flame sat a figure.

He was not large. That was the first thing that unsettled her. Stories of ferrymen across all the ledgers she'd catalogued agreed on certain features—vastness, shadow, eyes like coins—and this man had none of them. He was spare and grey as driftwood, dressed in robes the color of wet slate, and his hands rested on a pole across his knees with the patience of someone who had been holding it since before holding was invented.

His face was wrong in a way she couldn't name until she could: he had no expression because he had never needed one. Every line of him was exactly as old as his work.

"The Gildhall of Impossible Things," he said, and his voice was the sound the marsh made when the tide turned—vast, unhurried, arriving from everywhere at once. "I remember its founding. I carried the first traders across, before there was a hall to carry them *to*. I have watched your order grow fat on other people's losses." His head turned toward her with the slow gravity of a millstone. "You keep accounts, daughter of the oath."

"I do."

"Then you will understand that nothing crosses my water unpaid. Not you. Not the Remnant. Not the breath in your lungs or the name on your tongue." He extended one hand, palm up, and the lantern flame bent toward it like a listener. "Name what you carry. All of it. My rates are honest, if nothing else about me is."

Zephyrine set down the bundle slowly. Her mind was already moving the way it moved over a ledger—itemizing, weighing, hunting for the entry that would balance. It was the only skill she had ever trusted, and she clung to it now the way a drowning woman clings to rope.

"I have coin," she said. "Gild-minted. Thirty years' wages, unspent."

"Coin is a promise made metal. Your promises are already spent. You swore an oath to a silent god; the silence holds the lien." He said it without cruelty, the way winter says it will be cold. "What else?"

"A ledger. The oldest in the hall. Written in a hand no living member can date."

Something moved behind his eyes then—a flicker, brief as a fish under ice. "That is not yours to trade. That book belongs to the crossing itself. Bring it aboard and it will return to my keeping, but it buys you nothing. What else?"

Her mouth had gone dry. She looked at the child, who was watching the ferryman with an expression Zephyrine had never seen on her feral little face: recognition. Sorrow. The look of someone greeting a relative at a funeral.

"What else do I have?" Zephyrine asked, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"You have thirty-one years of memories," the Ferryman said. "You have the taste of the bread your mother baked in a kitchen that burned. You have the sound of your god's voice, the last time it spoke, the night you knelt in a ruined chapel and gave yourself away. You have the weight of every grief you ever bottled for a paying customer and never let yourself feel. You have—" and here he paused, and the pause had edges, "—one memory you do not know you are carrying. The oldest one. The one you paid once already."

The marsh went very quiet. Even the child's psalm faltered.

"I don't understand," Zephyrine said.

"No," the Ferryman agreed. "You wouldn't. That is rather the point of it." He drew the pole from across his knees and set its tip in the water, and where it touched, rings spread outward without ripples, circles of stillness moving against the current. "Here is my rate, keeper of accounts, and I will say it once, so attend. One memory. Freely given. Not sold, not stolen, not bargained down to a fair price—*given*, the way a gift is given, with no expectation of return. That is the toll for crossing into the realm of the silent god. It has always been the toll. It is why your Gildhall forbids such trades, and why your Gildhall has never once crossed my water with clean hands."

"Why?" The question tore out of her rawer than she intended. "Why does it have to be given? Why can't I sell it? I've sold everything else. Everyone in that hall has sold everything else. That's the whole *trade*—"

"Because a covenant is not a contract," the Ferryman said. "A contract is two parties guarding their own interests across a table. A covenant is two parties opening their hands. The realm beyond this water was sealed shut by a god who poured himself out freely, and it will only be opened by the same currency. Nothing coerced. Nothing purchased. Nothing owed." His gaze settled on her, and it was heavier than any audit she had ever failed. "One memory, freely given. Choose well. Or choose poorly. The water does not care which. Only that it is yours, and that you mean it."

Zephyrine stood very still.

She took inventory, because inventory was all she had. Her memories were the walls of the house she lived in. Take the mother and the bread and the fire, and the house lost a room. Take the chapel and the kneeling and the voice, and the house lost its foundation. Take the child—the mud-caked, impossible child who had walked into her ledgered life speaking the end of the world—and—

No. Not the child. She heard herself refuse it before the thought finished forming, a flat finality in her chest she hadn't known she possessed. She had known this girl for eleven days. Eleven days of fevered flight and shared bread and small hands gripping her sleeve in sleep, and some part of her had already signed herself over without reading the terms.

*Fierce protectiveness she didn't know she possessed.* She almost laughed. She knew exactly how much she possessed of it now. It was the largest asset on her books.

"How do I choose," she said quietly, "a memory to give away? How do I decide which piece of myself I can live without, when I don't know what I'll be afterward?"

"That," said the Ferryman, "is what makes it free."

The child tugged at her hand. Zephyrine looked down. The girl was pointing—at Zephyrine's chest, at the oath-mark beneath her shirt, and then across the black water, toward the far shore where no shore should be. And the language came again, soft, and this time the mark translated it whole, the words blooming in Zephyrine's mind like ink in water:

*The door opens from the giving side.*

"I know," Zephyrine whispered. "I know, sweetheart. Let me do the arithmetic."

But the arithmetic refused to balance. That was the truth she stood with while the lantern flame bent and the rings of stillness widened and the Ferryman waited with the patience of stone. Every memory she priced, she found she could not pay. Every memory she could bear to lose, she found too small—the toll wanted weight, and the things she could spare weighed nothing, and the things that weighed everything she could not spare, because she was, it turned out, made of them. Strip away the chapel and the voice and the mother and the fire and the eleven days of small warm hands, and what remained was a function. An auditor with no accounts. A wall with no rooms.

She thought of Corvane, who hoarded trades the way other men hoarded gold, and understood suddenly, completely, why the ledgers had begun writing entries he could not read. Some balances could not be struck from the outside.

"One memory," she said at last. "Freely given. And if I—" Her voice caught. She steadied it the way she steadied a trembling column of figures, with discipline and a steady hand. "If I give it, will I know I gave it? Will there be anything left of me to know?"

"There is always something left," the Ferryman said. "Whether it is enough is not a question I answer. I only carry."

Zephyrine closed her eyes.

And then—because she was a creature of the Gildhall, because thirty years of deferred payments and structured trades and clever clauses lived in her marrow deeper than any oath—she opened her mouth and did the one thing the Ferryman's rate was designed to make impossible.

"I'll pay on return," she said.

The rings of stillness stopped widening.

"I'll cross now," she went on, and the words came fast and smooth, trader's cadence, the voice she used on clients who flinched at prices. "The passage is necessary; the payment is certain. I'm good for it—you can see my books, ask anyone at the hall. When the covenant is mended and we stand on the far side of this, I'll give you the memory, freely given, same terms, same weight. You have my word. You have my *oath*." She lifted her wrist, bared the mark that had burned for eleven days. "This binds me. You know what it is. You knew the god who put it there."

The Ferryman regarded her for a long moment. The lantern flame straightened, neither bending toward him nor away.

"Daughter of the oath," he said, and there was something new in his voice—not anger, not amusement. Something almost gentle, which was worse than either. "Do you know how many have stood on this bank and offered me credit?"

"No."

"All of them." He stepped back, and the boat slid a hand's width from the bank, and slid back, an invitation and a refusal at once. "Every trader who ever learned the toll and found themselves unable to pay it has stood where you stand and promised me later. Later, later, later. I have heard *later* from mouths three thousand years dust. And I carried none of them." He raised the pole. "But."

Zephyrine's breath stopped.

"But the Remnant may board," he said. "The child is not a debtor. She is the debt—the last outstanding balance of a covenant broken, and debts of that kind travel free. She may cross whenever she wishes. Alone."

The girl's hand tightened in hers. Small fingers, mud-cracked nails, grip like iron.

"And if I send her alone," Zephyrine said, very evenly, "she arrives at the covenant's broken heart with no one to speak for her. No one who knows the ledgers. No one who knows what a freely given thing looks like, because she's never been taught, because she's a child who was raised by reeds and crows." She heard her own voice drop, go rough. "You want a gift? Watch me refuse to spend the only currency I have left, and tell me that isn't worth something."

"It is worth nothing," the Ferryman said. "It is worth everything. It is both, and I accept neither, and I am sorry for it—I am, in whatever way I am capable of being sorry. The rate does not bend. It has never bent. It will not bend for love, and it will not bend for need, and it will not bend because a keeper of accounts finally understands what her whole profession was hiding from her." He looked at her, and for one instant—one flicker, like the fish under ice again—something ancient and tired moved behind his eyes. "Choose, Zephyrine Yarrow. The water is patient, but the Crown is not."

At the name, she flinched. She had not told him her name.

Behind them, back along the way they had come, the marsh birds went up all at once—a dark sheet of them rising against the grey sky, wheeling, crying. Hooves. Far off still, but coming, and coming fast. Maret's riders had found the trail, or Corvane had sold it, and either way the arithmetic had just changed: the price of hesitation was no longer zero.

The child looked up at her. Then at the boat. Then back.

And Zephyrine saw, with terrible clarity, what the girl was offering to do—to step aboard alone, to spare her, to walk into the mending without her—and she knelt down in the mud and took the small face in both her hands.

"Not alone," she said. "Whatever happens. Not alone. Do you hear me? We go together or not at all, and I will find a way to pay, because I always find a way to pay, it's the only prayer I know." She pressed her forehead to the child's. "Give me until the far bank. Give me until the door. I'll have it by then. I swear it on the silence itself."

The Ferryman watched them with his expressionless face, and when he spoke, the tide-turning voice was quieter.

"On return, then," he said. "One memory, freely given, at the threshold of the realm. I will hold you to it, keeper—and understand what you have just done. A debt deferred to a freely given price is the cruelest arithmetic there is. Every step you take toward the door, the payment will grow heavier. Every hour you spend deciding what to lose, losing will cost more. By the time you stand where you must stand, you will have nothing left you are willing to give—and you will give it anyway, or the covenant dies, and the child with it, and the silence forever." He extended the pole toward the bank. "Board. Both of you. May you prove better than everyone who has ever stood on that mud."

They boarded. The black wood took their weight without sound. The lantern flame leaned forward like a figure stepping into the dark, and the river began to move beneath them—or perhaps they began to move above the river; on this water, Zephyrine suspected, the distinction had never mattered.

Halfway across, the child touched her hand and spoke one word of the old tongue, and the oath-mark translated it not as a warning or a direction but as comfort:

*Belonging,* it said. *Even here. Even now.*

Zephyrine did not cry. Keepers of accounts did not cry over open liabilities. But she held the small hand all the tighter, and she did not look back at the shore where hoofbeats were gathering, and she began, silently, methodically, the audit no ledger had ever survived: going through her life item by item, pricing each memory, asking of each one *could I live without this*—and hating herself, with growing desperation, because the answer was always no, always no, always no.

She did not see the rider who reined in at the southern bank a quarter hour after the boat had vanished into the mist. She did not see Inquisitor Dain Maret sit his horse at the waterline for a long time, staring at the place where the impossible river ran, his warrant case open on his knee and the sealed orders inside it unread.

She did not hear him say, to no one, in a voice cracked down the middle: *"Abomination,"* and then, quieter, testing the word like a coin he'd begun to suspect was clipped: *"Abomination."*

The mist took the boat. The mist took the bank. And somewhere behind it all, in a ledger wrapped in oilcloth against a stranger-to-herself's spine, a fresh line of ink appeared in handwriting no one had written yet:

*Received in trust: one memory, freely given, payable at the door. Debtor: Zephyrine Yarrow. Terms: crueler than she knows.*
