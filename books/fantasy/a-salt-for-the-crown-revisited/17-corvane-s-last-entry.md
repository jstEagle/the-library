## 17. Corvane's Last Entry

The Gildhall of Impossible Things had never been quiet at night, not in the thirty years Gildmaster Corvane had kept its keys. Even in the small hours the building breathed—shelves settling under the weight of bottled griefs, the vault of forgotten names ticking as contracts aged, the great ledger in its iron cradle exhaling a smell of ink and salt that had seeped into the stone itself. Corvane had always found the sound comforting. A building that breathed was a building that could be owned.

Tonight it held its breath.

He felt it the moment he crossed the threshold of the counting room, his boots loud on the floorboards in a way they had never been before. The lamps burned low. The ledgers lay open on their stands, and every one of them—the day-books, the trade-books, the great black ledger with its spine of stitched eel-hide—had turned, without any hand touching them, to the same page.

The page was blank.

Corvane stood in the doorway for a long moment, listening to the silence the way a moneylender listens to a debtor's excuses. Then he laughed, once, a short bark with no humor in it, and crossed to the iron cradle.

"You've learned to sulk," he told the ledger. "I've owned you forty years. I know sulking when I see it."

The ledger did not answer. Ledgers did not answer. That had been the whole of his faith for four decades: that the world, however strange, could be entered in a book, and a book could be balanced, and a balanced book could be controlled. He had built his life on that faith the way other men built theirs on gods. And like other men's gods, his had begun, lately, to demand things.

He had sold their route to the Crown for a price that would have made his younger self weep with envy: three provinces of trade exemption, the inquisitors' archives opened to Gildhall audit, and—clutched now in his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth—a memory. Not his own. It never was, with the valuable ones. A memory of the covenant's breaking, bought thirty years ago from a dying priest who had no heirs and no leverage, sealed in glass the way griefs were sealed, and catalogued under a number Corvane had chosen himself because the number amused him: 0. The trade that came before all trades.

He had told himself, riding back from the Crown's camp through the rain, that it was strategy. The covenant was the largest impossible thing the world had ever produced. Whoever held it held the hinge of realms. Let the mercenary woman and her feral child crawl toward their drowned archive; let them pay the Ferryman's obscene price; let them do the dangerous work of opening the door. Corvane would be waiting on the other side of it with the key.

He unwrapped the oilcloth now, setting the glass vial on the ledger's blank page, and told himself the same lie again. It was almost convincing. It had to be. It was the only possession he had left that still felt like it was his.

The vial was small, no longer than his thumb. The memory inside shifted like smoke in water, grey and gold, and when the lamplight caught it he could see, faintly, the shape of a bell tower. He had looked into it only once, the night he bought it, and what he had seen had given him nightmares for a year: a sky tearing like wet paper, a god's voice dying mid-word, and somewhere in the crowd a woman's face turned up toward the ruin, already beginning to forget.

"Thirty years," Corvane said to it. "You've appreciated beautifully."

The ledger's blank page seemed, if anything, blanker.

He had prepared for this the way he prepared for everything—meticulously, alone, in the counting room where no one could witness and therefore no one could claim a share. The covenant's terms, copied in his own hand from the fragments Maret had left behind in his haste. The ritual of sealing, reconstructed from a trade-record two centuries old. And the clause itself, the one the mercenary woman had gone to such lengths to find, the one Corvane had read a dozen times until he knew its cadence better than his own name:

*A covenant is sealed by a memory freely given.*

Freely given. He had turned the phrase over for days like a coin he suspected was clipped. The woman would give hers freely—that was her folly, her sentiment, her weakness. Corvane had no weakness of that kind. But the clause did not say the memory must be *yours*. It did not say the giving must be *generous*. It said freely given, and Corvane had spent forty years learning what words meant in trade rather than what they meant in churches.

He had paid for the priest's memory. Full price, fair price, witnessed and entered. The priest had given it freely—had, in fact, been grateful to be rid of it. Whatever chains of ownership had once bound that memory, Corvane had bought every link. If the covenant wanted a gift, then by every law of the Gildhall, he could *make* it a gift. He would give it freely. He would give it with a flourish. He would give the god a memory the god had already lost, wrapped in the only thing Corvane had ever truly owned: his own signature.

The ledger would have to accept it. The ledger accepted all trades. That was what ledgers were *for*.

He dipped the pen. The ink came out blacker than usual, or perhaps the lamplight was failing. He wrote the entry in the old hand, the formal hand, the one reserved for trades that crossed realms:

*Received of the undersigned, one memory, freely given, in payment of passage to the covenant's heart—*

His pen stopped.

The ink was not drying.

He had written a hundred thousand entries in this room. Ink dried in the time it took to lift the pen; that was part of the magic of the place, part of the reason the Gildhall's books were law in nine kingdoms. But this ink sat on the page, wet and glistening, and as he watched, it began to move—not running, not blotting, but *crawling*, the letters lifting at their edges like things waking from sleep.

Corvane set the pen down very carefully, the way he did when a trade went wrong and the counterparty was watching for fear.

"Ah," he said. "I see."

He had known, of course. Some part of him had known since the ledgers began writing entries he could not read, since the night he'd found a full column of script in a hand like frost on glass and realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, that his books were keeping records he was no longer party to. He had told himself it was a transition. New management. The old covenant dissolving, the new one forming, and a man of his acumen would naturally be positioned to broker it. That was the story. He had told it so well, so many times, that he had almost stopped noticing it was a story.

The ink finished crawling into a shape. It was not the shape he had written.

*REJECTED.*

One word. It sat on the page with the finality of a gravestone.

Corvane sat down in his chair, slowly, because his knees had informed him they were no longer entirely under his management. "Rejected," he repeated. "On what grounds? Itemize the grounds. I have been balancing this house for forty years. I have never had a trade rejected. I have never had a trade *questioned*."

The ink moved again.

*THE GIVING IS NOT FREE. THE GIVER DOES NOT OWN WHAT IS GIVEN. THE GIVER DOES NOT OWN ANYTHING.*

"That's absurd." His voice came out thin. "I own this building. I own the vaults. I own—"

He stopped, because a sound had begun somewhere below him, down in the vaults, and it was a sound he had heard only once before in his life: the sound of a seal breaking. Glass, cracking in the dark, in perfect unison.

* * *

The vault of forgotten names was three floors down, behind a door that required two keys and a word spoken backward. Corvane took the stairs two at a time, which he had not done in a decade, and threw the door open with both keys already in his fists.

The shelves were emptying.

He stood in the doorway and watched forty years of acquisitions walk out of his possession. It was not violent. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would try and fail to describe for the rest of his life, however long the ledger chose to make that life: it was not violent at all. The bottles did not shatter. The contracts did not burn. They simply *left*—dissolving off their shelves like frost off a window in morning sun, each one going quietly, going *home*.

He saw a bottled grief—the grief of a woman in the Salt Quarter who had sold her mourning for a daughter drowned at sea—rise off its shelf, glow once, and vanish. Somewhere across the city, Corvane understood, that woman was going to wake tonight with her grief returned to her, whole and heavy and *hers*, and she would weep until dawn, and she would not know why the weeping felt like being given back a limb.

He saw a forgotten name leave its lead box. He saw a debt owed across realms unwrite itself, the debtor's mark fading from the page like breath off glass. He saw a vial containing the last spoken word of a dead language crack gently and release nothing at all, because the word had gone back to the tongue that lost it.

"No," Corvane said. It came out very small in the vast stone room. "No. Those are *assets*. Those are entered. They're *entered*—"

*THE ENTRIES ARE UNMADE,* said the ledger, though there was no ledger in the room. The words arrived in his head with the texture of ink. *ALL OF THEM. THE HOUSE WAS BUILT ON STOLEN INTERIM. THE INTERIM IS OVER.*

"I bought them!" He was shouting now, and the vault took his voice and gave back nothing. "Fair price! Witnessed! Every trade witnessed and entered according to the forms—"

*YOU BOUGHT WHAT OTHERS LOST,* the ledger said, and for the first time in forty years, Gildmaster Corvane heard something in the ledger's voice that was not neutral. It was not cruelty. It was worse. It was *pity*. *THE HOUSE KEPT THEM SAFE, IN ITS WAY. THE HOUSE KEPT THEM UNTIL THEY COULD GO HOME. YOU THOUGHT THE HOUSE WAS YOURS.*

He lunged for the nearest shelf, hands closing on a contract he had traded a river-kingdom's border for, and his hands passed through it like hands through smoke. The contract finished dissolving. Somewhere, a border that had been sold twice returned to the people who had bled for it.

Corvane stood in the emptying vault with his arms full of nothing, and began, very quietly, to laugh.

It was not a pleasant sound. It was the sound a man makes when the arithmetic finally completes itself and the sum is not the one he spent his life expecting. He laughed because the alternative was to scream, and he had never screamed in front of the inventory. Even now. Even now, with the inventory leaving him the way a tide leaves a shore, some withered organ of professional dignity insisted that a Gildmaster did not scream in front of the stock.

* * *

By the time he climbed back to the counting room, the sun was coming up, and the Gildhall was nearly bare.

Not empty—nothing in the Gildhall was ever simply empty; absence here had weight and texture, like the space in a ring box. But the shelves in the counting room stood clean. The trade-books lay open to pages of blank vellum, every entry gone, every number gone, forty years of columns dissolved back into the world they had been borrowed from. Only the great black ledger in its iron cradle still held writing, and it had turned, without his touching it, to a fresh page.

Corvane sat down at his desk—his desk, the one thing left, because a desk was only wood and wood had never been an impossible thing—and took inventory of himself the way he had taken inventory of the house, item by item, with the desperate thoroughness of a merchant counting what remains after a fire.

The building: still his, or at least still around him. The keys: in his pocket, though the locks they opened guarded nothing now. His fortune: he did not know, and was afraid to wonder, and suspected with a sick lurch that the answer was written in a bank ledger somewhere across the city that had also, overnight, gone blank.

His name.

He reached for that last the way a drowning man reaches for a rope, and found, to his astonishment, that it was still there. *Corvane.* Gildmaster Corvane. He turned it over in his mind. It fit him the way an old coat fits—snug in the shoulders, worn smooth at the cuffs, smelling faintly of someone else's smoke.

He frowned.

He tried to remember where the coat had come from.

There had been a mother. He was almost certain there had been a mother. There had been a boy, surely—a boy who became the man who became the Gildmaster—and the boy had had a name too, a first one, the kind a mother calls across a courtyard at dusk. He reached for it and his fingers closed on smooth blank vellum, on a page with no entry, on a trade long since unmade.

Had he sold it? He must have sold it. That was the horror, the true horror, arriving now at the end of the inventory: he could not remember *selling* it, but he could not remember *not* selling it either, and in forty years of trade he had learned that the things you cannot remember either way are the things you traded earliest and dearest. A name was the first thing a man owned. It was, he now understood, the first thing he had ever put on a table.

The ledger rustled. He looked up.

The great black book had turned to a page near its beginning—he had never been permitted to read the earliest pages; the iron cradle locked them; the cradle stood open now—and on that page, in ink that had not dried in a hundred years and would not dry in a hundred more, was an entry in a hand he almost recognized. A woman's hand. A hand that slanted the way his own did, that crossed its letters the way his own did, and he stared at it with the last of his comprehension draining out through the floor, because he knew that hand, he had *always* known that hand, and it was not his.

*Received into keeping,* the oldest entry read, *one house, one ledger, one long interim. To be held in trust until the giver returns for what is hers. The keeper's fee: to forget, in the end, what he kept, and why, and that he was ever keeper at all.*

*The keeper's fee is accepted.*

*Signed:* and there the old ink had blurred, or the page had wept, or the lamplight had failed, and Corvane could not read the name.

He sat in the grey light of morning in a house that no longer knew him, at a desk that was only wood, before a book that had finished with him, and he understood at last the shape of the trade he had been part of without ever once being a party to it. He had been the interim. He had been the shelf, not the stock. He had held a god's house for thirty years while a mercenary woman walked toward a drowned archive, and now the term was up, and the fee was being collected, and the fee was *everything*.

The ledger turned one more page. The fresh one. The blank one.

And there, at the top, in wet black ink that crawled into letters as he watched, it began to write—not to him, never again to him, but *about* him, the way one writes about weather, the way one writes about a season ending:

*The keeper's accounts are closed. The keeper's trades are returned. The keeper retains one item, being the only thing he never traded away, having never known he held it.*

*Item: one name.*

*Disposition: pending.*

Corvane read it twice. Then he opened his mouth to protest—to itemize, to appeal, to invoke the forms, the witnesses, the forty years of flawless columns—and found that he did not know, with any certainty, what name to protest under.

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell he had never heard before began to ring. It rang in a language older than any kingdom, and somewhere out in the salt marshes a woman with no past was walking toward a door that was opening, and the ledger, patient as tide, waited for the keeper to remember what he had been keeping—or to forget it forever, which, the ink suggested in a small marginal hand that was almost gentle, amounted to the same thing.

Corvane picked up the pen. His hand, at least, still knew its work. He wrote, in a hand he no longer recognized as his own, the only entry left to make:

*Received of no one, one name, condition unknown.*

*Held pending identification of owner.*

And somewhere in the vaults below, in the dark where the shelves stood empty and clean, something that had waited thirty years behind a locked door began, very softly, to laugh.
