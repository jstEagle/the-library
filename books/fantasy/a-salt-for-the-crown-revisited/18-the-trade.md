## 18. The Trade

The place had no name that Zephyrine could find in any of the ledgers, though she had spent the whole of the climb down looking for one. The Gildhall catalogued everything—every grief, every grudge, every debt owed across the veils between worlds—and yet the heart of the covenant, the wound at the center of all that bookkeeping, had been left deliberately blank. A space where an entry should have been. She understood now that the blankness was itself a kind of record. Some things were so large the Gildhall could only mark them by their absence.

She stood at the edge of it with the child's hand in hers.

It was not a room. It was not a cavern, though it had the hushed depth of one. It was a *gap*—a place where the world had been sewn shut over a missing piece, and the stitching had begun to rot. The air here tasted of salt and old iron, the taste of blood long dried on the tongue. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the color of the moment just before dawn, and it pooled around them like water finding its level. At the center of the gap hung the broken thing itself: the covenant, or what remained of it. Two great shapes of light that should have been joined and were not, their severed edges drifting apart like the halves of a broken wishbone, still faintly glowing where they had once touched.

The child let go of her hand and stepped forward.

Zephyrine's whole body wanted to reach after her. She made herself stand still. There were perhaps a hundred paces between the edge of the gap and the broken covenant, and every one of them belonged to the child now. That was the shape of the trade. That had always been the shape of it.

"You don't have to be brave," Zephyrine said, and heard how the words cracked in the middle. "Brave is for soldiers. You just have to be *willing*. That's all a trade ever asks."

The child turned. In the pre-dawn light her mud-caked skin looked almost luminous, and her eyes—those old, old eyes that had watched a god die from the inside—were fixed on Zephyrine with an expression no child should have had to learn. Not fear. Not sorrow. *Recognition.* The look of someone greeting a person they have been waiting a very long time to meet.

She spoke then, in the language older than any kingdom, and Zephyrine's oath-mark translated it against her skin, letter by burning letter, the way it had done in her dreams for all the miles of marsh and moor between the Gildhall and this place:

*You will not remember me. But I will remember being remembered. That is enough. That is more than the god ever had.*

"I know," Zephyrine said. Her voice was steady. She was surprised by that—she had expected to come apart by now, and instead she felt strangely assembled, every piece of her fitted to its purpose, the way a ledger balances at the close of a fiscal year when every column agrees. "I know what it costs you. I've read the terms. I signed them once, they tell me, though I don't remember the ink drying."

The child tilted her head, and something almost like a smile touched her mouth.

*You never remember the ink drying,* the mark translated. *That is the mercy in it. That is why it must be freely given and never taken. A taken thing screams. A given thing says: yes, and again, yes.*

Zephyrine knelt. The stone beneath her—or whatever passed for stone in a place like this—was cold through her trousers, and she was grateful for it. Cold kept her honest. She had spent thirty years grieving a silence she had mistaken for abandonment, and the grief had been the truest thing she owned. She would not cheapen it now by pretending she came to this lightly.

She thought about what she was about to give away, and she gave herself the courtesy of looking at it, all of it, one last time.

Her mother's kitchen, and the smell of bread and tallow, and the song her mother hummed that she had never been able to find again in any tavern in any city. Her father's hands, cracked and careful, teaching her to count on his fingers before she could read. The road out of her village at fourteen, and the rain, and the terrible freedom of it. The Gildhall door, and Corvane young and already ambitious, and the ledgers rising around her like a city built of paper. The night she had climbed to the ruined chapel and sworn her oath to a god whose name she could not pronounce, and the mark that had bloomed on her wrist like a brand made of starlight. Thirty years of small kindnesses and small cruelties, of trades fair and foul, of the particular loneliness of a woman who keeps accounts for the impossible and owns almost nothing herself.

Maret's face, when he let them pass. Maret's voice, saying *go*, and the sound of the ford behind them, and the name she had promised to carry to the ledger. She carried it now. She would carry it as far as she could, and then she would carry it no further, and someone else would have to remember that a man could crack like green wood and still choose, at the end, to be an oak.

The child's face. The child's hand in hers, small and mud-caked and certain.

All of it. Freely given.

"I have one question," Zephyrine said. "And then I'll pay."

The child waited. The broken covenant hung above them both, its severed edges drifting, shedding a light like slow snow.

"Does it hurt him?" Zephyrine asked. "The god. All this time, holding the realms apart with his silence—does it *hurt*?"

The child's old eyes closed. When she opened them again, the mark on Zephyrine's wrist burned so brightly it threw shadows.

*He does not know the word for it anymore,* the child said. *He gave his voice away first, and pain needs a voice, or it is only weight. He has been carrying the weight of two realms for thirty years without a single word to set it down in. When your memory returns to the covenant, he will feel what you felt. Every year of your grief for him. He will feel it and know, at last, that someone was grieving. That he was not alone in the dark with the door he chose to hold shut.*

"That's cruel," Zephyrine whispered.

*It is a trade,* the child said. *Trades are cruel. That is why the Gildhall exists—to make the cruelty honest.*

Zephyrine laughed. It came out of her ragged and wet, half sob, but it was a laugh. The child laughed with her, a bright startling sound that had no business in a place like this and made the broken covenant shiver in its hanging, the two severed edges drifting a finger's width closer, as if even light leaned toward laughter.

Then Zephyrine stood, and brushed the stone-dust from her knees, and did what she had done every working day for thirty years.

She opened her ledger.

It was the small one, the pocket book she had carried since her first week at the Gildhall, its cover soft as old leather gloves, its pages crowded with thirty years of entries in her own close, careful hand. She had brought it for this. She had not let herself know she had brought it for this until this moment, and now the knowing arrived whole and calm, like a debt come due that you had somehow already saved against.

She turned to the last blank page and wrote, in a hand that did not shake:

*Received of Zephyrine Yarrow, mercenary and oath-keeper, one life entire: every memory, every year, every grief, every name. Condition: freely given. No consideration asked or offered. Witnessed by the last Remnant of the covenant, who will not survive to countersign.*

She read it over. It was, she thought, a clean entry. Corvane would have found it wanting—no valuation, no terms of exchange, nothing held in reserve. But Corvane was not here. Corvane was somewhere behind them, unmade by his own hoarding, and the ledgers had already written what they thought of him.

She signed it. The ink sank into the page and did not dry so much as *settle*, the way water settles into earth, and she understood that the page had accepted the entry, and that the entry was now true in the way that only ledger-true things are true—truer than the world, because the world could be argued with and the ledger could not.

The child took her hand.

*It is time,* the mark translated, and then, softer, in words that needed no translation at all: *Thank you for finding me. Thank you for the bread. Thank you for not selling me.*

"I would never—" Zephyrine began, and stopped, because the child was already fading.

Not dying. *Fading*—the way a word fades when everyone who spoke it has gone, the way a name fades when the last person who bore it is forgotten. The child was becoming language. Zephyrine could see it happening: the mud-caked skin going luminous and then translucent, the small body resolving into lines of that old, old tongue, hanging in the pre-dawn air like a page being written by an unseen hand. And the words were not random. They were *her*—every moment Zephyrine had shared with her, transcribed into the covenant's own script, being carried home to the place where all the old words lived.

"No," Zephyrine said, and her voice broke properly now, at last, the way it was supposed to. "No, wait, I haven't—there are things I wanted to tell you. There are things I wanted you to *keep*—"

*You gave them all,* the child's voice said, from everywhere now, from the light itself. *That was the trade. You gave me everything, and so I go to him carrying everything. Every year of your grief. Every year of your stubbornness. Every year of the bread you shared with a mud-caked stranger at your door. He has been silent for thirty years, Zephyrine Yarrow. When I reach him, he is going to have so very much to say.*

The child—the light, the words, the last Remnant—drifted upward toward the broken covenant, and Zephyrine watched her go, and her memories went with her. She felt them leave. It did not hurt, exactly. It felt like a tide going out: her mother's kitchen sliding away, her father's hands, the rain on the road, the chapel and the oath and the thirty years of silence, all of it drawn out of her gently, gently, each memory a small bright fish swimming home to deeper water. She stood in the ebb of her own life and wept without knowing entirely what she wept for, because some of the things leaving her were already gone, and she was mourning them from the far shore of a self that was becoming, moment by moment, a stranger.

The two severed edges of the covenant drifted together.

They touched.

The sound was not a sound. It was the memory of a sound—the first word ever spoken, remembered by the world's oldest stone—and it went through Zephyrine like a bell through water, and the gap that had been a gap was a *place* again, whole, stitched, singing. The covenant blazed with a light like noon, and in the light she saw—or almost saw, or would remember almost seeing—a shape vast and weary and *relieved*, a shape that had been holding a door shut for thirty years and had just, at last, heard the lock turn from the other side.

And the god spoke.

Not to the world. Not yet. The voice came through the mended covenant and through the child's dissolving light and through Zephyrine's own emptied self, and it was not a voice like thunder or like bells. It was a voice like someone setting down a weight they had carried so long they had forgotten it was heavy. It said, in the language older than any kingdom, and Zephyrine's oath-mark translated it even as the mark itself began to fade, its work finished, its covenant mended, its purpose complete:

*Received. In full. Freely given.*

A pause, vast as a season turning.

*And witnessed.*

The light took her.

* * *

She woke on cold stone with salt on her lips and no idea who she was.

That was the first thing. The second thing was that she was not afraid of it. She lay still and took inventory the way a body takes inventory after a long fall—limbs present, breath present, a small dark book clutched in one hand with a grip that had not loosened even in sleep. The book was hers. She knew that the way she knew her own hands. She did not know what was in it.

She sat up. Dawn was coming through a broken window, the color of the moment just before the world begins, and somewhere above her—far above her, beyond the sky, beyond the stitched and singing seam of things—a voice was speaking. Not to her. To everything. To the realms, to the salt marshes, to the Gildhall and its ten thousand shelves of bottled grief and forgotten names. It was saying something old in a language older than kingdoms, and though she did not know the language, she found that she understood it anyway, the way one understands one's own heartbeat.

The voice was saying, over and over, like a man weeping with joy, like a god setting down his burden:

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*

Zephyrine Yarrow—who did not yet know that she was Zephyrine Yarrow, who knew only that she was a keeper of accounts and that her accounts had been wiped clean—opened the small dark book to the first page, and found the last entry in a hand she did not recognize and somehow trusted completely:

*Paid in full. One life, freely given.*

*Balance: to be determined.*

She picked up the pen that lay beside her on the stone, dipped it in the ink that was somehow already there, and turned to a fresh page.

Her hand, at least, still knew its work.
