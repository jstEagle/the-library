## 1. What the Marmots Knew

The marmots told me about the soldiers a full day before I saw them, which was typical. Marmots are gossips. They're also cowards, liars about the size of loaves of bread, and better scouts than any man the crown ever paid, which is why I trust them with my life and pay them in dried plums.

"Three," chittered Old Sorrel from her rock above the pass, in that flat, whistling way marmots have. "Two-legged. Metal skins. Smell of fire and bad bread."

"Which pass?" I asked, not looking up from the pack straps I was checking. You learn early never to look up when you're talking to an animal in open country. People notice where your eyes go.

"The low one. The one you were going to use, because you're lazy and it's easy."

"I'm efficient," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm still alive."

Sorrel made a sound I can only describe as a shrug. If you've never been shrugged at by a marmot, count yourself lucky; it does something to a person's self-regard.

I rerouted. That was the whole art of my trade, if you could call it a trade: I moved things through Thornfell that other people couldn't move—salt mostly, sometimes medicine, once a very indignant crate of hens—and I did it by listening to everything the mountain said and saying nothing to anyone in it. The beasts gave me the news. The people got silence. It had kept me fed and unremarkable for twenty-six years, and I had come to think of invisibility as a skill I owned, like knife-work or reading weather.

That was the mistake. But we'll get there.

* * *

The run that week was salt and a bolt of southern linen for a widow in Hollowmere who paid honestly and asked nothing, which made her my favorite client in three valleys. The route ran along the spine of the Thornfells, up through scree and pine, past the waystation at Cold Saddle—a squat stone hut where the king's road-wardens kept a stove, a ledger, and opinions.

I meant to skirt Cold Saddle wide, as always. But Sorrel's soldiers were camped on the low pass, and the high route added two days I didn't have before the widow's deadline, so I compromised: I came down within earshot of the waystation and settled into the rocks above it to wait out the daylight hours, as I'd done a hundred times. From there I could see the yard, the tethered horses, the woodsmoke. Two of the metal-skins were road-wardens. The third wore grey and silver, and he stood like a man who'd been sent rather than stationed.

A courier arrived near dusk. I remember because the light went long and gold across the yard, and the horses threw their heads at something on the wind—the courier's smell, maybe, or their own unease—and one of them looked directly up at my rocks with big wet eyes and held the look a beat too long. Horses always know. Horses are terrible at keeping secrets and wonderful at knowing them.

The man in grey and silver unrolled a paper and read aloud, because proclamations are read aloud; that's what makes them proclamations instead of letters.

I'll give it to you as I heard it, standing very still among the stones with my hand buried in a marmot colony's worth of loose earth, feeling every whisker around me go stiff.

*"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Second, of that name the second, protector of the realm and its creatures—"*

The irony arrived early and stayed late.

*"—let it be known throughout the marches of Thornfell and all lands under the crown: that certain persons practice the forbidden art of speech with beasts, an abomination against the natural order, and that such persons are henceforth declared traitors to the crown. They are to be sought, taken alive where practicable, and delivered to the assizes at Carrowmere. Any who shelter them share their guilt. Any who report them shall be rewarded from the treasury. So it is spoken. So it is law."*

The road-wardens nodded along the way men nod at weather reports. One of them asked a question I didn't catch. The grey-and-silver man answered, and I caught that: *"There's a list. Names gathered over years. Most of them don't know they're on it yet."*

Most of them don't know they're on it yet.

Around me, the earth began to chatter.

Marmots don't panic the way people do. They go precise. Every animal in that hillside started relaying at once—*metal-skins, metal-skins, names, names, down, down, deep*—a ripple of alarm moving through the colony like wind through wheat, except wheat doesn't understand what the wind means. I put my palm flat on the cold ground and thought, very clearly, *quiet*, and they quieted, because that's the bargain we'd always had: I listen, they talk, and nobody gets hurt.

Nobody had ever gotten hurt before. That was the part I hadn't examined closely enough.

I stayed until full dark, because moving in daylight is how smugglers become examples. I watched the grey-and-silver man eat his bad bread by the stove-light, and I studied his face, and his face told me nothing except that he believed the paper he'd read. Belief is worse than cruelty. Cruelty can be bribed.

Then I shouldered my pack—salt and linen, the widow's honest goods, suddenly contraband by association with me—and I walked north through the dark with forty pounds of my old life on my back, and I did not look at the low pass where the soldiers slept.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being invisible: it only works while everyone agrees not to see you.

I'd built my life on a series of agreements I'd never signed. The villagers of Hollowmere agreed I was a quiet woman who walked a lot. The road-wardens agreed I was nobody worth entering in a ledger. The crown agreed, in its vast indifference, that the mountains were empty except for what could be taxed. And the animals agreed—well, the animals never agreed to anything, but they kept my secrets, because secrets kept were plums earned, and because I think some of them simply liked me, though I'd die before saying that out loud where a raven could hear it.

One piece of paper, and every agreement evaporated. Not because anything about me had changed. Because someone with a seal had decided to look.

I made camp in a hollow below the treeline, no fire, and shared dried plums with Sorrel and two of her granddaughters, who had followed me out of pure nosiness. They sat in a row on a stone like judges at a fair, and I ate my own portion and tried to do arithmetic.

The proclamation named the art, not the person. Fine. But the grey-and-silver man had a list. Lists get filled by neighbors, by debtors, by the spurned and the bored. I had spent twenty-six years being forgettable, and I had been good at it, but good is a relative term and somewhere in three valleys there was a person who remembered my face and owed me a grudge or owed someone else a favor. Invisibility wasn't mine. It had been loaned to me by everyone who'd never bothered to look twice, and the loan had just been called in by a man eating bad bread in a waystation.

"You're making the face," said Sorrel.

"What face?"

"The face the tall ones make when the sky changes. The one where you pretend you're thinking about something small."

"I'm thinking about salt."

"You're lying. Your smell goes sour when you lie. Like green apples."

"Everything smells like green apples to you. You'd say that about a landslide."

"A landslide does smell like green apples," she said with dignity, "if it's going to kill you."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and then the laugh died in my throat because that was the thing, wasn't it. The marmots had smelled this coming for days. The horses had known at dusk. The whole living world had been reading the wind while I sat in the rocks congratulating myself on my hearing, and the wind had been screaming *names, names, down, down, deep.*

I hadn't needed a proclamation to know I was in danger. I'd needed to stop believing I was exempt from it.

* * *

Near midnight, a raven landed on the dead pine above my camp and said, in its croaking, borrowed-human voice, "Salt-walker. Salt-walker."

Ravens pick up words the way children pick up curses—from exposure, without comprehension. This one knew my trail-name because I'd traded with its flock for years: shiny buttons for news, news for plums. I held up a plum and waited.

It took the plum, swallowed, and said, "Many wings. Many boots. Coming up the king's road. Asking." A pause, the raven equivalent of dramatic effect. "Asking about *you*."

The word dropped through me like a stone through pond water. Not asking about beast-speakers in general. Not reading a list to an indifferent yard. Asking. By name, or near enough.

"Who's asking?" I said, and hated how my voice came out.

"Grey man," said the raven. "Grey man says—" and here it did something ravens almost never do, which was hesitate, rearranging its feet on the branch, feathers tight. "Grey man says the salt-walker has a face he knows."

I sat very still in the dark with a marmot asleep against my boot and a raven looking down at me with one flat eye, and I understood that the borrowed time was up, and the interest was coming due, and I had perhaps a day, maybe two, before the mountains I had spent my whole life disappearing into stopped hiding me and started delivering me.

"So it is spoken," I said to nobody. "So it is law."

Above me, the raven croaked once—agreement or mockery, with ravens it's always both—and lifted off into the black, toward whatever came next.
