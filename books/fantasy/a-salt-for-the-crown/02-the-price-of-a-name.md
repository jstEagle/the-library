## 2. The Price of a Name

The raven found me three miles east of my own front door, and it was laughing.

That's the thing about ravens. They don't laugh the way people do, with the mouth. They laugh with the whole body, a rough, rolling *kra-kra-kra* that shakes the branches, and they laugh at everything—dead things, living things, especially at me, who had once made the mistake of sharing a rabbit carcass with one and had been paying for the friendship ever since.

This one dropped out of the grey morning sky and landed on a fence post I was about to pass, and it said, in the way ravens say things, which is less words and more weather: *soldiers, soldiers, the tall one with the rope, they have the small man, they have the small man's mouth.*

"Which small man?" I asked, because I'm a fool, and because there is only one small man in my life, if you don't count the marmots, and Dario Reyes is exactly as tall as a lie needs room to stand up in.

The raven fluffed its feathers, pleased with itself. *The one who smells of tar and bad decisions. They gave him bread. He gave them a name.*

I stood very still on the frozen road with my pack straps cutting into my shoulders, and I watched my breath come out in little clouds, and I thought: *he wouldn't.*

Then I thought: *he would, actually, he absolutely would, and you've known that for eleven years and called it charm.*

The marmots had warned me about Dario the first week I met him. I'd been running contraband through Thornfell for two seasons by then—nothing dramatic, mostly medicine and salt and the occasional fugitive who could pay—and Dario had appeared at my campfire with a smile like a door left open in winter. *Lena Zhou,* he'd said, *I've heard you're the best guide these mountains have.* And my marmots, who can smell a liar the way other creatures smell fear, had gone silent in a way I should have heeded and didn't, because he'd also brought coffee, real coffee, and I was twenty-three and lonely, and loneliness makes idiots of us all.

Eleven years of runs together. Eleven years of him talking enough for both of us, which I'd always told myself was a kindness, him carrying the conversation so I didn't have to. Eleven years of me telling myself that a man who never shut up couldn't possibly also be keeping secrets, because where would he put them?

The raven hopped along the fence post beside me, keeping pace, narrating. *The tall one asked him questions. He cried a little. Then he stopped crying and got very helpful. They wrote it down. They're burning something now.*

"Burning what?"

The raven cocked its head, and I understood before it answered, the way you understand a fall has already happened while you're still in the air.

*Your house.*

I ran.

I'm not going to pretend I made a clever decision or weighed my options like a sensible smuggler. I ran, down off the road, through the pines, my pack banging against my spine, the raven flapping overhead and shouting directions it was enjoying far too much. Three miles. I'd walked those three miles a thousand times, easy, unhurried, the way you walk toward home. I'd never once run them, and it turns out three miles is a very long way when your lungs are full of knives and the smoke is already visible above the trees, a grey smear against the grey sky like the mountain itself had bruised.

I came out of the treeline at the top of my own field, and I stopped, because there was nothing to do but stop.

The barn went first. I could see that even from the ridge—the barn was the dry-timber building, full of hay and eleven years of stored runs, and it went up like it had been waiting its whole life for this, a column of orange climbing into the morning. The soldiers had done it properly. Of course they had. The crown doesn't do anything halfway; that's how you know it's the crown and not just some bandits with better stationery.

My house was next. I watched two soldiers carry out my mother's chair—the good chair, the one she'd brought across three kingdoms before I was born—and set it in the yard, and I watched an officer look at it, and I watched him nod, and I understood that the chair was evidence. A chair, evidence. A loom, evidence. A woman's whole life, laid out in a farmyard and sorted into *burn* and *keep*, and somewhere in the *keep* pile was going to be everything they needed to find me, because I'd lived thirty-four years in that house and you cannot live thirty-four years anywhere without leaving a map of yourself behind.

I sat down in the snow. I don't remember deciding to. My legs just ended the argument.

The marmots found me there—I don't know how long after, an hour, maybe two, the fire down to a black wound in the field, the soldiers gone, the crows already circling what was left. They came up out of their burrows and pressed against my sides, all six of them, warm and trembling, and I put my hands on them because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"They gave him bread," I told them. My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "They gave him bread and he gave them me."

The eldest marmot, the one I call Grandmother because she's older than the mountains' patience and twice as unimpressed by it, looked up at me and chittered something that translated, roughly, to: *and what did you expect, feeding a stray?*

Fair. That was fair.

Here's what I kept turning over, sitting in the snow while my life burned down to its foundations: it wasn't even the betrayal that made me sick. Betrayal I could price. I'd been robbed before, shot at, left for dead once in a snowslide by a partner whose name I'd scrubbed from my memory so thoroughly I couldn't tell you if it was a man or a woman. The world of smuggling runs on betrayal the way mills run on water. You factor it in. You keep your real name in one pocket and your real heart in another and you never let the two pockets touch.

No. What made me sick was the arithmetic of it. Eleven years. Eleven years of campfires and bad jokes and him learning exactly how I took my coffee and exactly which trails I trusted and exactly where I'd be on any given morning of any given week—because that's what partnership is, isn't it? You make yourself legible to one person. You hand them the map of your life, all the little folded creases of it, *she sleeps light, she takes the high pass, she'll come home if the barn's burning, she can't not.* And he'd spread that map out on a crown officer's table and put his finger on it and said *there, there she is,* and they'd paid him in bread.

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, ugly, and scared the marmots, and the raven up in the scorched pine answered with its rolling *kra-kra-kra*, and I thought: *yes. Exactly. That's the sound.*

Because here's the vertigo part, the part I couldn't get my boots under: I didn't know who I was anymore. Not in the dramatic way, not in the *who am I really* way that poets whine about. In the practical way. Every route I knew, Dario knew. Every cache I kept, Dario knew. Every friend I had—which is to say, every marmot colony and two ravens and a suspiciously intelligent goat—Dario knew about, and a man who'd sold me once would sell them next, and the crown had just declared that everything I was made me a traitor, and the man who knew the map of me was walking free somewhere with bread crumbs on his collar and a new debt of gratitude to the people burning my house.

I had been invisible for thirty-four years. I'd built the invisibility myself, plank by plank—said nothing to people, kept my gift in the dark, let the mountains do my talking. And I'd told myself it was mine, that invisibility was something I owned. It wasn't. It was borrowed. It was loaned to me by everyone who never bothered to look, and the loan gets called in the moment one person decides to look, and there is no appeal, and there is no getting the house back.

The house. I should say something about the house, since I'm never going back to it.

It was small and badly built and my mother had hated the draft in the north wall for twenty years without ever fixing it, because she said the draft kept her honest. There was a loom in the corner that I'd never used. There was a loose floorboard under the bed where I kept the things I couldn't carry: a pressed flower from a woman in Vessany—I'll get to that, maybe, someday, if I live long enough to explain it—and my father's knife, and a letter I'd never sent. All of it ash now, or in the *keep* pile, in an officer's saddlebag, being read by strangers who'd never know what any of it meant.

I didn't cry. I want that on the record. I sat in the snow until my legs worked again, and I stood up, and I took inventory like a smuggler takes inventory, because that's the only prayer I know.

What I had: one pack, half-full. Six marmots who'd follow me into a fire. One raven with a taste for gossip. A knife. A bow. Forty feet of good rope. And a name that was now worth a bounty, printed on paper in some inquisitor's office, in a hand that had learned its letters from Dario's dictation.

What I had lost: everything else.

I turned south, away from the road, away from every trail Dario and I had ever walked, and started walking, and the marmots fell in around me like a small furious honor guard, and the raven rode the wind above my shoulder shouting directions nobody had asked for.

I made it four miles before I let myself think the thought I'd been holding behind my teeth, the one that tasted worse than the smoke:

*Ash Corvane will be the one they send.*

I'd heard the name that morning at the waystation, before the proclamation, before any of it—soldiers talking, the way soldiers talk, about the crown's new hound for Thornfell, the inquisitor who hunts beast-speakers, who knows the mountains, who *knows the people.* And I'd felt the cold come up through my boots then, too, because I knew exactly one person from my childhood who'd gone east to the capital, exactly one person who used to share my bread on the high trails, back when we were both nobody's, back when she'd told me she'd never work for a crown that couldn't hear its own mountains.

They say people change. They say it like it's a comfort.

I walked into the deep pines as the light went, with my house burning behind me and my oldest friend's name in my mouth like a stone, and I made myself a promise in the language I don't speak to people: whatever they sent me, whatever face it wore—

I would not be the one who stopped listening first.

The raven, above me, laughed.

* * *

I want to be clear about one thing before this story goes any further: I did not cry over Dario Reyes. I want that understood. What I did, two days later, crouched in a hollow above the Kestrel Road with marmot scouts posted at both ends and the raven sulking in a hawthorn, was watch him.

He came up the road at midday, alone, walking the careful walk of a man who knows he's been paid too easily. They'd given him his freedom and a purse—I could see the purse, the smug little bulge in his coat—and he kept touching it the way you touch a wound, to check it's still there. His face was doing something complicated. Guilt, probably. Dario wore guilt the way other men wore hats: visibly, and only when the weather required it.

I could have put an arrow in him. Let's establish that. I had the shot, I had the wind, and I had six marmots who would have testified at my trial, had trials featured marmot testimony. Grandmother was actually chittering up at me, a small insistent *do it, do it, I've hated him since the coffee,* and I confess the temptation was not small.

But a dead Dario tells you nothing, and a watched Dario tells you everything. So I watched him stop at the fork in the road—the fork, the one place in these mountains where a man chooses which liar he'll be next—and I watched him stand there for a long time, turning his purse over, turning something else over too, something behind his eyes.

And then he looked up, directly at my hollow, directly at me, forty yards of frozen brush away, and he said, out loud, to the empty air, in the voice of a man who has just realized exactly how his partner works:

"Lena. I know you're there. You always were."

Grandmother's chittering stopped. The raven went silent. Even the wind, I swear, held its breath.

Dario Reyes stood at the crossroads with my death in his pocket and something else in his hands—I could see it now, a leather tube, the kind maps travel in—and he said:

"I stole something from them. From the archive. And you're going to want to hear about a country that isn't on any of your maps."
