## 3. Inquisitor Ash

The crown, I have learned, does not send you to hunt monsters unless it suspects you are one.

I arrived in Thornfell on the last day of autumn with a warrant sealed in red wax, a company of twelve soldiers who feared the mountains more than they feared me, and a reputation that preceded me by three weeks of rumor. They said Inquisitor Corvane could smell a speaker through stone. They said she'd taken a confession out of a man in Vell Harbor using nothing but a chair and her own patience. The rumors were half true, which is the most dangerous ratio a rumor can be. I had never needed a chair.

What I actually smelled, stepping down from the coach at the Thornfell waystation, was woodsmoke, wet pine, and the particular sourness of soldiers who had been fed mutton four days running. The mountains did not care about my reputation. The mountains cared about snow, and they were already winning.

"Corvane." Captain Hessler met me at the station door, a broad man with the complexion of someone who'd been cold since birth. "You'll want to see this first."

He meant the proclamation, nailed to the waystation's post in the crown's best lettering: *All who hold speech with beasts are traitors to the realm. Let them be named, seized, and brought to the capital alive.* Alive. Underlined twice, as if the clerk hadn't trusted his own hand.

"You've read the supplementary orders," Hessler said.

"I wrote them."

"Then you know why we're standing in the wind instead of burning barns."

Because the edict without names was a sword without an edge, and the crown wanted its edge. Because somewhere in these valleys lived perhaps two dozen people who could do what no one else could—walk up to a wolf or a hawk or a marmot and be understood—and the king had decided, in his wisdom, that this made them dangerous rather than useful. Useful speakers had been the crown's for generations: scouts, trackers, the quiet women who kept the high passes open in winter. But usefulness curdles into fear when you can't control it, and fear, in the capital, is a crop that grows itself.

My orders were simple. Find them. Name them. Bring them in alive, because a dead speaker proves nothing and a living one can be made to prove anything.

"I have a list," Hessler said, producing it with the reverence men reserve for documents. "Three names so far. A goatherd near Lowmark who talks to his dogs—everyone talks to their dogs, but his neighbors swear the dogs talk back. A widow in Millbrook. And—" he hesitated, which told me everything before he said it, "—a smuggler. Caught on the eastern pass with crown-stamped goods. He bought his freedom with information."

"A name."

"He gave us a name."

* * *

Dario Reyes talked the way water runs downhill—constantly, finding every crack, pooling wherever resistance was lowest. I interviewed him in the waystation's back room with the door open, because I've found that men confess faster when they believe someone might overhear and think better of them.

He was younger than I expected. Hands soft except for rope callus. Eyes that kept apologizing before his mouth did.

"She didn't know I sold her," he said, unprompted, ten minutes in. "That's—I want that understood. She never knew me well enough to trust me, so she couldn't be betrayed by me, exactly. It was more—" he searched the air for a better shape and failed, "—it was more like I betrayed the idea of her."

"The idea of her," I repeated.

"You'd understand if you knew her." He almost smiled. "She doesn't waste words. Not on people, anyway. She'd stand in a field talking to groundhogs like they were old friends, and then you'd ask her the time and she'd look at you like you'd asked her to undress." He rubbed his wrists where the irons had been. "Lena Zhou. That's the name. She runs the high passes—the ones your soldiers can't cross in winter. Marmots scout for her. Ravens carry messages, maybe. She saved my life twice before I ruined hers once, which I believe makes us even by smuggler arithmetic, though I'm told the crown uses different sums."

I wrote the name down. My hand was steady. I remember being proud of how steady it was.

"Describe her," I said, because procedure demanded it, because the file required it, because every procedural step was another plank between me and the thing underneath.

"Dark hair, always braided back. Younger than me—twenty-two, twenty-three. Walks like the ground owes her money." He paused. "You've heard of her, haven't you."

"No."

"You went still when I said the name. People go still when they've heard something."

"People go still when a prisoner starts narrating at them," I said, and closed the interview.

But that night, in the room the waystation gave me—a narrow bed, a narrow window, the mountain breathing against the glass—I took out the name again and turned it over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit.

Zhou. Lena Zhou.

There had been a girl on the trails above Lowmark, years ago, when I was fourteen and furious at the world for reasons I've since forgotten and she probably remembered precisely. She'd been fifteen and silent and better than me at everything that mattered on a mountain: reading weather, reading scat, knowing which silence meant danger and which meant peace. We'd shared bread once. Just once. I'd brought a loaf up from the valley, dense black rye my mother baked, and we'd sat on a shelf of granite with the whole valley turning gold below us, and she'd torn it in half without asking, which was the most intimate thing anyone had ever done to me at that age.

She'd said eleven words that day. I counted, even then. Eleven words, and one of them was a joke, and I laughed so hard a marmot two ledges down screamed at us in outrage, and Lena said something to it—actually said something, low and easy, the way you'd soothe a spooked horse—and the marmot shut up and settled, and I looked at her and she looked at me, and I understood that I had just seen something the world would eventually try to kill.

I was right. It just took longer than I thought, and it sent me to do the killing.

* * *

Here is what I told myself, in that narrow room, with the warrant on the table between me and sleep:

Duty and mercy point the same direction. That was the sentence. I built it carefully, load-bearing, and I hung everything off it.

If Lena stays hidden, she'll be caught eventually—by someone less careful than me. Someone will burn her out, or shoot her on a pass and claim she ran. The edict has teeth now, and teeth don't distinguish between the guilty and the merely rumored-guilty. If I find her first—if I bring her in myself, gently, under my protection—she lives. She goes to the capital, yes. She stands trial, yes. But trials can be survived. Winters in the mountains cannot, not forever, not alone, not with every soldier in the province authorized to put a bolt in her.

Alive. The order says alive. And I am very good at keeping things alive; it's the other thing I'm good at that I intend not to use.

You see how it works. You take the order you've been given and you fold it until it looks like kindness. You tell yourself that obedience is a form of shelter, that the cage is warmer than the open sky where the hawks are already circling. And the terrible thing—the thing I want written down somewhere, in case anyone ever asks—is that I believed it. For days, I believed it completely. Duty and mercy point the same direction. I repeated it like a prayer, and prayers work precisely because you don't examine their grammar.

We moved up-valley over four days. Hessler's soldiers complained about the cold, the altitude, the mutton, each other. I asked questions in the villages, quietly, the way I do—not *have you seen a fugitive*, but *hard winter coming, who knows these passes best?* Fear does half my work for me; the other half is done by people who want to be helpful more than they want to be loyal.

The answers pointed east. High country. An abandoned homestead with a barn that still had hay in it, according to a shepherd who looked at his boots the entire conversation.

On the fifth morning, we found the barn.

Or rather—we found what the barn had become overnight. Charcoal ribs against the snow. The smell of it still hanging in the frozen air like an accusation nobody had signed. No body, Hessler reported, sweeping the ash with his boot. No body, but a set of tracks leading north into the trees, small-footed, unhurried, and beside them—this was the detail I made him show me twice—tracks of a large bird walking. Walking. A raptor, grounded, pacing along at a woman's side like a dog.

"She had warning," Hessler said. "Hours, maybe. Fire like that doesn't start itself."

No. It doesn't. Someone had come ahead of us—or behind us—with torches and impatience, some local officer interpreting *alive* as a suggestion. Or someone had wanted her gone before the inquisitor arrived, which raised questions I filed away and did not enjoy.

I stood in the ashes while the soldiers searched the treeline uselessly, and I looked at those paired tracks going north, and I felt the crack.

Not a break. A crack. The kind you get in ice that will hold you today and drop you next week. On one side of it: the crown, the warrant, the careful architecture of duty-as-mercy I'd built. On the other side: a girl tearing bread without asking, eleven words, a marmot screaming with laughter's echo, and the knowledge—cold and certain as the mountain—that I had known, at fourteen, exactly what the world would do to her, and I had grown up to be the world.

"Inquisitor?" Hessler was watching me. "Orders? The tracks head into the high country. Weather's turning. We could lose her up there."

I looked north, where the trees stood close and patient, where somewhere a woman walked with a broken-winged shadow at her heel, running from a fire toward a colder kind of dark.

"We follow," I said. "Alone. Your men will slow me down."

Hessler frowned. "The supplementary orders say—"

"I wrote the supplementary orders, Captain." I was already tightening my pack straps. "And I'm telling you the speaker moves fastest through terrain like this, and twelve soldiers sound like twelve soldiers. I'll send word from the next station."

It was a lie. I knew it was a lie while I said it, and I let it stand, because the crack had opened far enough that I could see through it, and what I saw through it was this: I did not want witnesses to what I was about to decide.

Duty and mercy point the same direction, I told myself one last time, climbing into the trees, following the tracks of a woman and a bird into the white silence of Thornfell.

Somewhere behind me, faintly, I thought I heard the ice creak.
