## 4. Hawk at Her Shoulder

I found him on the third day of running, which felt about right. The world had decided I was to be hunted, so naturally it sent me something else that was bleeding.

He was a red-tailed hawk, crumpled against the roots of a lightning-split pine like a thrown coat, one wing bent at an angle wings are not meant to hold. A snare line—thin wire, crown-stamped lead seal still knotted at the anchor. Even up here, three days from anywhere with a name, the crown's little signatures followed me like lice.

I should have walked past. Let me be clear about that, because everything after came from not walking past. I had a pack with four days of food, a marmot's map of safe hollows scratched into my memory, and a face that was now worth more than my weight in silver if delivered breathing. Every instinct I'd sharpened over twenty-six years in Thornfell said: *invisible things survive. Broken loud things do not.*

But he looked at me. Not the way animals usually look at people—with that flat accounting, weighing whether you're threat or weather. He looked at me the way the marmots did when they chittered warnings. Like he knew I could hear him, and he was deciding what to say.

*Cold,* he said, or the shape of cold did, the way pain speaks before words. *Wing. Cold. Done.*

"You're not done," I told him, and immediately hated myself for it, because talking to a dying hawk in the open is exactly the sort of thing that gets beast-speakers burned.

I cut the snare anyway. I splinted the wing with a peeled branch and strips from my spare shirt—the spare shirt, mind you, the one standing between me and the mountain wind—and fed him strips of dried venison softened in water, which he ate with the offended dignity of a creature who believes himself above charity. Then I built a small fire, because shock kills faster than exposure, and sat there in the firelight thinking: *this is how they'll find me. Drawn by smoke, following a hawk.*

His name arrived without my asking. Sef. It wasn't a word so much as a feeling—a hard little knot of selfhood, fierce and specific, the way a stone has a shape under moss. When I thought *Sef* at him, his good eye fixed on me with unmistakable satisfaction, as though I'd finally passed some test I hadn't known I was taking.

"Absolutely not," I said aloud. "You don't get a name. Names make things real."

He tucked his head under his good wing, which I chose to interpret as agreement, because I was already lying to myself and might as well commit.

* * *

By the fifth day I had tried to leave him twice.

The first time was honest strategy. His wing would take weeks to heal properly; my legs worked fine; every hour I sat in one hollow was an hour Ash Corvane's trackers gained. So at dawn I packed, stepped over his indignant glare, and climbed out of the gully. I made it perhaps two miles before the sky darkened and there he was above me, flying crooked but flying, riding the thermals with his splinted wing held stiff, screaming that thin rasping scream red-tails make when they have opinions.

*Stupid,* he informed me, circling. *Slow. Alone. Stupid.*

"I'm not alone," I said to the empty air, which fooled no one, least of all me.

*Alone,* he repeated, with the merciless precision of hawks, and landed on a boulder directly in my path, and refused to move until I turned back toward camp.

The second time I didn't even pretend it was strategy. I just stood at the edge of the hollow at dusk, pack on, and said, "If you follow me, you'll die. They hunt hawks that fly wrong. They'll see you and know someone's near, and that someone will be me, and they will put wire around your neck too."

He looked at me for a long moment. And then he did the thing that broke me, the thing I still think about: he hopped over and pressed his head against my boot. Just once. Briefly. The way you touch someone when you're trying to say something your kind doesn't have words for.

*I know,* he said. *Stay anyway.*

Tenderness, I have learned, is not a soft thing. It's a debt. Every kindness you accept becomes a chain-link, and the crown had taught me this month what chains cost. But I picked up my pack, and Sef flapped up to my shoulder—which he would insist on doing for days despite weighing as much as a loaf of bread and having talons like fishhooks—and we went north together, and I hated how much lighter I felt, and I hated knowing that lighter was another word for louder.

* * *

On the seventh night, the wolves found us.

Not hunting—I knew hunting-wolves the way I knew weather. These moved along our flanks at a distance, pacing us through the pines, and when I stopped, they stopped, and their silence had a listening quality to it. Word travels between beasts faster than any rider. Somewhere south of us, the marmots were still telling the story: *the speaker runs, the speaker runs, the crown burns barns.*

"They can't help me," I muttered to Sef. "Word of mouth doesn't stop arrows."

Sef preened, unimpressed by my pessimism. He had developed, in seven days, the unshakeable confidence of a creature who has decided I am competent, and I resented how much I wanted to live up to it.

It was near midnight, feeding the fire down to coals, that I heard the other sound. Boots. One set, careful, coming up the game trail—not the heavy cadence of soldiers but the deliberate step of someone trained to walk quietly and failing slightly on the left foot, favoring an old injury.

I killed the fire with dirt and pulled Sef low, feeling his heart hammer against my palm, feeling mine answer. In the black between the trees, a shape resolved. Broad-shouldered. Cloaked. Moving with purpose.

And then the shape stopped, sniffed the air like a man catching woodsmoke, and said, in Dario Reyes's voice, cracked and exhausted:

"Lena. I know you're there. Your hawk screamed at me for half a mile."

Sef, traitor, lifted his head.

I should have put a knife in Dario Reyes. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the person writing them is a fugitive talking to a bird. He sold my name. He turned my life into currency and spent it on his own neck, and I had watched my barn burn from a ridge with a raven's report still ringing in my skull, and there was a version of me—that old Lena, the invisible one—who would have simply melted into the dark and let him wander Thornfell until winter did the work.

But Sef was on my shoulder, warm and alive and stubborn, and I had spent a week learning that belonging costs something, and apparently I had decided the price included mercy I hadn't budgeted for.

"Step into the light," I called softly, "slow, hands where I can see them, and understand that the hawk has opinions about your continued health."

"He already expressed them," Dario said, emerging with his palms out and a long scratch healing on his cheek. "Repeatedly. From altitude." He looked terrible—hollow-eyed, unwashed, wearing guilt the way Sef wore his splint. "I brought something. From the soldiers' archive, before they moved it. Maps. Of a country that isn't supposed to exist anymore."

Behind him, far to the south, a raven crossed the stars, heading north, and I felt its message pass like a cold draft: *the hunter comes. The hunter remembers you.*

Sef tightened his grip on my shoulder—not painfully, just firmly, the way a hand grips yours.

"Well," I said. "You'd better talk fast."
