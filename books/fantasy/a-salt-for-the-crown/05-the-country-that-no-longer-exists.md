## 5. The Country That No Longer Exists

There are two kinds of men who sell other people. The first kind sleeps fine afterward, and I hate them for it. The second kind doesn't sleep, and I've decided that's not mercy either—it's just the same crime with a longer sentence attached.

I was three days without sleep when I found her.

I'd tracked her wrong twice first, which should have told me something about how badly my head was working. Lena Zhou did not want to be found by anyone, least of all me, and she had a mountain full of eyes—well, ears, mostly, and noses—to make sure of it. But guilt makes a man clever in stupid ways. I stopped thinking like a hunter and started thinking like her: where would water be, where would wind cover sound, where would a hawk not bother circling? I sat down on a cold rock above a stream that ran under old pine roots and waited like a stone myself, and near dusk a wren landed on a branch over my head and screamed at me for a full minute.

I took that as a fair review of my character.

Then she stepped out of the trees with a red-tailed hawk on her shoulder like she'd been carved that way, bow half-drawn before I finished standing up.

"Dario." My name in her mouth sounded like a coin being tested against a counterfeit. "You have until I count to three."

"That's generous."

"One."

"I brought you something worth more than an apology," I said quickly, "which is good, because I don't have one."

"Two."

"It's paper," I said. "It's the only paper in Thornfell with your name not written on it anywhere, and I think right now that's what you'd pay for. But if you want to put an arrow in me instead, I understand. Genuinely. I'll even hold still."

The hawk shifted its weight. She didn't lower the bow.

"Three," she said, and didn't shoot.

I've thought a lot about why. Partly it was the hawk—I learned later his name was Sef, and he had opinions about me from the start, most of them involving my liver. Partly it was that she wanted to hear whatever I'd say next more than she wanted me dead, which is its own kind of damning. But mostly, I think, it was because Lena Zhou had spent her whole life being careful with people who weren't careful with her, and some part of her needed to know exactly what shape of fool had sold her, so she could stop imagining worse ones.

So we talked. If you can call it that—one man talking into the space between an arrowhead and his chest, while a woman listened the way mountains listen to weather.

* * *

"You sold me for a pardon," she said, when I'd laid out the maps between us on a flat stone and weighted their corners with pebbles. "Not gold. Not passage out. A *pardon*."

"They offered gold too," I said. "I took the pardon instead. Make of that what you will."

"What I make of it is that you're a coward with a conscience, which is the worst combination there is. A plain coward would at least stay bought."

"That's fair."

"Don't say 'that's fair.' It isn't. Nothing about this is fair." She crouched across the stone from me, close enough that I could see she hadn't slept either, and that the hawk's talons had left little constellations of scabs on her shoulder through her shirt. "Say what you came to say, Dario. You have as long as it takes me to decide whether to believe you."

I told her about the archive. About the soldiers' camp at the waystation, where they kept the confiscated papers of everyone the crown had already ground up—smugglers' ledgers, heretics' letters, maps seized off dead men. How I'd been sitting in a cage waiting to trade my life for my silence when an officer had tossed a bundle on a desk to show his superior how thorough he was, and how one corner of yellowed vellum had caught the lamplight, and how I'd seen a word on it that no living map used anymore.

"*Vessany*," I said.

Her face didn't change. But Sef's feathers lifted along his neck, and I'd spent enough years reading birds to know that meant she'd gone very still inside.

"There's no such country," she said.

"No. There hasn't been for two hundred years. The cartographers burned every chart after the annexation—the crown called it correcting an error." I tapped the topmost sheet. "This officer's grandfather looted a surveyor's office during the border wars and never knew what he had. It's been sitting in a trunk marked *miscellaneous* for three generations. Until yesterday, when I stole it out from under forty soldiers who were planning to hang me anyway."

"Why?"

"Because I read the margins," I said. "And the margins are in a hand that isn't cartographer's script. Someone annotated those charts in a language that isn't any language I know—and the officer couldn't read them either, which is why they were miscellaneous. But I've run cargo past people who could. And the notes all cluster around one place." I moved the pebble off the largest chart and turned it toward her: a mountain range drawn in faded ink, a pass, a river, and at the river's bend a symbol like a doorway with lines radiating out of it, the way a child draws sound.

"A gate," I said. "On the old Vessany border. The notes call it *the listening door*. The kingdom that got erased from the maps kept its last door open, Lena. And the door hears."

* * *

She didn't touch the charts for a long time. She looked at the fire instead, or at the dark beyond it, and when she finally spoke her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went flat and careful around soldiers.

"The marmots talk about a place in the high passes where the stones hum. They won't go near it. I always thought it was superstition. Marmots are very superstitious about things that eat them."

"And now?"

"Now I think maybe they were superstitious about something else." Her eyes came back to me, and I made myself hold them, because flinching would cost me everything I had left. "Here's what I don't understand, Dario. Why bring this to me? You traded my name to save your neck. Now you're risking your neck again—for what? So I'll forgive you? I won't. So I'll thank you? I won't do that either."

"So you'll live," I said.

The words came out smaller than I meant them to. I made myself keep going anyway.

"When they burned your barn, I was still standing in the officers' tent. I heard them plan it. I heard them say *she'll run for the lowlands where the roads are*, because that's what fugitives do, and the roads are where Inquisitor Corvane will be waiting. They aren't hunting you, Lena. They're *herding* you. Every patrol, every burned building, every rumor they let loose—they're laying a funnel, and the mouth of it is Ash Corvane herself." I spread my hands. "Every way south is a trap. Every way east is watched. The west is the sea, and neither of us has a boat. That leaves north, into the worst country in Thornfell, following a word off a stolen map. Which is insane. Unless the word is true."

"And if it's true, and it's a trap?"

"Then it's a trap I walked into with you, and we're even."

"We are not *even*," she said, with a heat that actually made me feel better, because heat meant I still mattered enough to burn. "Do you know what betrayal costs, Dario? Not to the person betrayed. To the betrayer. You spend a name like mine and you think the transaction ends. It doesn't. From now on, every kindness you offer anyone gets weighed against that day. Every trust extended to you is a test you might fail again. You didn't buy a pardon. You bought a debt that compounds."

"I know."

"You don't know. You *suspect*. Knowing comes later, at three in the morning, forever." She stood, and Sef mantled his wings slightly, resettling. "But here's what I know about you that you apparently don't know about yourself. You could have taken the gold. A coward takes the gold—gold spends cleanly. You took the pardon, and then you stole maps, and then you climbed into the high country alone to find a woman whose death warrant you signed, carrying the only thing you own that's worth anything. That's not a coward. That's a man trying to crawl back into a house he burned down, brick by brick, knowing the fire's still inside him."

I looked at the fire. It seemed suddenly very loud.

"So here's my price," Lena said. "Not forgiveness—you haven't earned it and I'm not selling it cheap. My price is that you carry these maps, and you walk north with me, and when we reach whatever's at the end of them, if it's a door, you stand in front of it with me. Because I need someone beside me who knows what it costs to choose wrong. You'll remember better than I will."

"That's it? That's the price?"

"That's all of it. And it's more than you think it is." She held out her hand—not warmly; precisely, the way you'd hand a rope to a drowning man while making clear the drowning was his own fault. "Well, smuggler? Do we have terms?"

We shook. Her grip was cold from the evening air and steady as bedrock, and Sef fixed me with one amber eye the whole time, memorizing my face for future grievances.

* * *

We walked north that night, because she said night-walking was safer and because I suspected she slept better moving than she ever did still. Somewhere past midnight, crossing a scree slope under a fat indifferent moon, she said, without turning around:

"The raven that warned me about the ambush. It described you. It said *the two-legs with the crooked smile smells of fear and ink*. Was it right about the smile?"

"Crooked since a mule kicked me at fourteen."

"Hm." A pause. "The ink was accurate."

I laughed—a short, surprised bark that echoed off the rocks and immediately felt like the loudest thing in ten miles. She didn't laugh, but the line of her shoulders changed, some fraction of an inch, the way ice changes just before it decides to be water.

An hour later, the hawk left her shoulder, spiraled up into the moonlight, and began flying ahead of us—not hunting, not ranging. Pointing. North, and slightly east, toward a pass I had never heard named, along a road that hadn't appeared on any map for two hundred years except, now, the ones digging into my back with every stride.

"He does that?" I asked. "Lead?"

"He does that," she said. "Since the pass. He's been pulling north for days. I thought it was migration." She was quiet a moment. "Dario. What if it wasn't?"

Ahead of us, faint as breath on glass, the mountain hummed.
