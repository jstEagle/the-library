## 6. Ashes on the Pass

I had been hunting Lena Zhou for eleven days, and in that time I had learned three things about the crown's newest treason.

First: that a woman could vanish into Thornfell so completely that forty soldiers and two bloodhounds and me—the Inquisitor Corvane, terror of three provinces—might as well have been searching for a specific snowflake.

Second: that my hounds were useless, because whatever Lena said to animals, they listened, and the dogs spent their nights lying by the fire looking embarrassed, like men who'd been talked out of their wages.

Third: that I was not, strictly speaking, trying very hard to catch her.

I told myself the third thing wasn't true. I told myself it with the same conviction I used to tell myself that the proclamation was just, that beast-speaking was corruption of the natural order, that the girl who'd once split her last heel of bread with a shivering child on the Kestrel Trail was now an enemy of everything I served. Conviction, I was learning, is mostly repetition. Say a lie enough times and it stops sounding like one. It starts sounding like policy.

The pass above Hollowmere was where I finally stopped lying to myself, though I didn't know it yet when I set out that morning. The scouts reported tracks—a single set, small boots, heading up toward the saddle where the pines gave out and the wind did all the talking. She was traveling alone. No smuggler. No dog. Just her, and something overhead that the scouts kept describing as "a large bird" with the uneasy tone of men who suspected the bird was reporting back.

It probably was.

I went up alone too. I told my lieutenant I wanted quiet for the arrest—an Inquisitor's prerogative, all incense and mystery—and left him grumbling at base camp with orders not to follow until dawn. Whether those orders were tactical or cowardly, I genuinely couldn't say. Both, probably. Most things are both, if you look at them long enough.

* * *

The wind on the pass came off the high snowfields and smelled like nothing human had ever touched. I climbed the last stretch with my warrant case under my arm—leather, brass-cornered, stamped with the sun-and-scales—and found her standing at the saddle with her back to me, watching a hawk ride the thermals.

She didn't turn around. Of course she didn't. She'd heard my boots twenty minutes ago, and every bird between here and the valley had already given her a full report, including, I suspected, an unflattering assessment of my chances.

"Ash." My name in her mouth after ten years. It hadn't aged well. Neither of us had, but hers had aged interestingly, which is the better failure. "You brought dogs last time."

"They were more decoration than anything."

"You always were bad at pretending." She turned then. Thinner. Sharper around the eyes. There was a hawk feather caught in her collar and she didn't know it was there, and something in my chest did a stupid, painful thing at the sight of it. "You've come to take me to the capital."

"I've come with a warrant," I said. "Whether you come along is still under negotiation."

"Between you and who?"

"Me and me. It's a crowded negotiation. Everyone wants a chair."

Almost—almost—she smiled. Ten years ago on the Kestrel Trail, sharing bread in the rain, she'd had a laugh like a rockslide starting somewhere deep. I'd thought about that laugh more times than I'd ever admit to anyone, including myself, including especially during sermons on the sin of improper attachments.

"The edict says alive," she said. "That's the part everyone finds generous. Alive. As if being dragged to the Bastion in chains and questioned by the Choir of Scales is some kind of mercy."

"It's better than the alternative."

"Is it?" She looked past me, down the long fall of the mountain, and I understood she wasn't asking about herself. Somewhere below us there were other speakers—shepherds who murmured to their flocks, children who talked to crows because crows answered. The edict didn't distinguish. Edicts never do; that's what makes them edicts instead of judgments. "You've read the interrogations from the southern trials, Ash. You know what 'alive' means once the Choir gets involved."

I knew. I'd signed transit orders for three of them. I'd told myself each time that the law was the law, that mercy without structure is just sentiment wearing a nice coat, that someone had to hold the line even when the line ran through people. I'd been very good at telling myself things. It was practically my gift. Maybe I was a speaker too, and the only beast I'd ever mastered was the one in the mirror.

"Lena." I opened the warrant case. The paper inside had my seal, my signature, her description in my own careful hand: *Subject displays unnatural communion with beasts. Subject is to be considered extremely dangerous.* I'd written *extremely dangerous* while remembering a girl feeding crumbs to a one-legged sparrow, and I hadn't let myself feel anything about that contradiction, because feeling things was how Inquisitors ended up in the archives next to the people they'd failed to catch. "Give me your hands."

"No."

"No?"

"Not today. Ask me again tomorrow, maybe. I hear I'll be very cooperative tomorrow."

The hawk screamed once from the thermal—high, sharp, deliberate—and dove. Not at her. At me. It pulled up three feet short of my face, close enough that I felt the wind off its wings, close enough to see its eye, which was brown and furious and intelligent in a way that made my skin crawl and my heart ache in the same breath. Then it banked and landed on her shoulder like it had been cast there.

"Sef," she said mildly. "We talked about this."

The hawk stared at me. I stared back. This is not a recommended activity, incidentally. Being stared at by a raptor is like being audited by God.

"He stays with you," I said. "Even running. That's a beacon. Any tracker worth his salt—"

"—follows him straight to me. Yes. I know." Her jaw tightened. "He knows too. He doesn't care. Stubbornness runs in feathers as well as families."

And there it was again, that stupid ache. Because I remembered her at fourteen, arguing with her mother about exactly this—about loving things you couldn't afford to love, about how tenderness was a debt you paid in grief. I'd sat on their hearthstone eating stew and thought, *what a strange family*, and gone home to my barracks bed thinking about it for weeks.

"Ten years," I said. "Where did you go?"

"Away."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I can afford." Her hand came up to rest on the hawk's back, and the bird leaned into it like it weighed nothing, like trust was light. "Ash. Why are we talking? You have forty men at the tree line and a warrant in your hand and orders. Why are we standing on a mountain having a conversation like two people?"

Because I'd hoped you'd run sooner. Because every day you didn't run was another day I got to pretend I was hunting instead of catching. Because I wrote your description and gave you gray eyes when yours are brown, and hoped no one would notice, and no one noticed, because no one reads an Inquisitor's paperwork closely enough to catch mercy in it.

"I'm thorough," I said.

"You're stalling."

"I'm *thorough*. Stalling is what cowards do. I have a reputation."

The wind gusted, hard enough to make us both brace, and in the middle of it she said, quietly, so it almost got lost: "They'll burn them, Ash. The ones they catch. You know that. Whatever the edict says about trial and transport, the Choir burns, and you know."

I knew.

I had watched a shepherd from the Vell marches kneel in the courtyard at Casterhale and ask the guards' horses—not the guards, the *horses*—to forgive them, because he didn't believe the men could. And the horses had bowed their heads. And I had stood at attention through all of it, holding the line, holding my face blank, holding nothing at all by the end of it except the growing suspicion that I had chosen the wrong side of a door and locked it behind me.

"They gave me a choice," I heard myself say. "Back at the Bastion. High Inquisitor Marren. He said, Corvane, you grew up in Thornfell, you know the terrain, you know these people. Bring her in alive and gentle, if you can. And then he smiled, Ash. He smiled like a man handing you a knife and calling it a gift. Because he knew. Somehow he already knew what I'd only just figured out myself."

Her eyes narrowed. "Knew what?"

"That I'd rather be hunted than be his hound." I took the warrant out of its case. Heavy paper, official ink, the sun-and-scales gleaming like it meant something. It had cost the crown four months of my life and most of my certainty, and it was worth exactly as much as I decided it was worth, which was the whole secret of power nobody tells you: it only works if everyone agrees to pretend.

I struck flint to steel. The edge of the paper caught, curled, went orange.

Lena watched it burn with an expression I couldn't read—or maybe I could, and reading it hurt. Not gratitude. Something sadder. Pity, maybe, or recognition, the look you give someone stepping off a cliff beside you.

"You understand what you've done," she said. "Marren anticipated doubt. Men like him always do. He'll have a second team on you already. Shadowing your camp. Reading your letters. The moment that warrant stops moving toward the capital, someone will ask why."

"I know."

"And you're smiling."

"I am," I said, and discovered it was true. My face actually hurt from it, like a muscle remembered from childhood. "God help me, Lena, I haven't been terrified like this since I was nine years old, and I'd forgotten—" I laughed, breathless, absurd, ash lifting off the burning paper and streaming east over the pass. "I'd forgotten that fear could feel like *standing up*."

The warrant burned down to my fingers. I let it. A small price. A salt, as my grandmother used to say, rubbed in the wound to keep it honest.

Then the horn sounded from the tree line below.

One note. Then two. Then the long rolling triple-call that meant *target sighted*—and it wasn't pointed at her. The horns were pointing at my camp. At my tent. At the place where my second-in-command was, right now, opening sealed orders that had traveled with us from the Bastion in Marren's own courier case, orders I'd never been allowed to read.

Lena's hand found the hawk's feathers. "Ash," she said carefully. "What was in those orders?"

"I don't know." My stomach had gone cold and bright, the way it does before a battle or a confession. "But I think I'm about to find out the way everyone finds out. Publicly."

Down in the valley, a signal fire bloomed—three smokes, the old signal, the one that meant *the hunter has turned*. And above the smoke, rising fast against the evening, came riders. Six of them. Wearing no colors at all, which was worse than any colors, because the Choir's hounds never wore colors.

Sef screamed and launched off her shoulder. Lena grabbed my arm—her grip stronger than it looked, a climber's grip—and pulled me toward the far side of the saddle, toward the scree chute where no rider would dare follow.

"Can you climb?" she demanded.

"I grew up in Thornfell."

"Good." For the first time since the Kestrel Trail, that rockslide laugh broke loose of her, wild and scared and alive. "Because you're about to learn what it's like on *my* side of the hunt, Ash Corvane. Try to keep up."

Behind us, the last of the warrant's ashes blew off the mountain, and ahead of us the scree chute fell away into the blue dark of the high country—and below, closing fast, came the men whose warrant I should have been carrying.
