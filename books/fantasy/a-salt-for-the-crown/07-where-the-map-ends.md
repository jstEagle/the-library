## 7. Where the Map Ends

The hawk woke us on the fourth morning by screaming directly into Dario's open mouth.

I want it noted that I did not laugh. Much.

"Sef," I said, and he lifted off Dario's chest with the smug satisfaction of a creature who has discovered a new hobby. Dario lay there gasping at the grey sky, one hand pressed to his heart like a man who'd been shot.

"That bird is trying to kill me."

"He's trying to keep you moving. Same thing, from his perspective." I was already rolling my bedroll. Four days of the old Vessany road had taught me that much about Sef: he did not tolerate idleness, and he considered Dario's sleeping face an affront to something. Possibly the gods. Possibly just hawks.

The road itself was the strangest part, and I include the screaming bird in that assessment. Two centuries of abandonment should have swallowed a road whole—shoulder-high brush, frost-heaved stones, trees walking across it in slow vegetable conquest. But this road refused to die. The stones sat flush and true beneath our boots, and when I crouched to touch them, they hummed.

Not metaphorically. They hummed. A low, patient note, like a struck bell heard through a wall of earth, and when I pulled my hand back the note faded, almost disappointed.

"You feel that too," Dario said. It wasn't a question. He'd watched me do this every morning since the pass, touching stone, listening, and every morning his expression had moved a little further from *skeptical smuggler* toward *man reconsidering his entire understanding of geography*.

"The road remembers being walked," I said. Which sounded like poetry until you put your palm flat on it and felt the truth of it vibrate up your arm. "Vessany wasn't just erased from maps, Dario. Someone tried to erase it from everything else, too. And the road is still arguing."

"Arguing with whom?"

"With being forgotten." I stood, brushed frost from my knees. "It's losing. But slowly."

Sef wheeled overhead and screamed once—the *follow* call, sharp and imperative—and we followed, because what else were we going to do? Go back? Back was Ash Corvane and a burned warrant and a commander who now hunted two fugitives instead of one. Back was a country where my gift was treason and my name was worth a pardon to any man desperate enough to sell it. Forward was a door out of a country that no longer existed, guarded by strangers who might or might not let three exhausted liars through.

Honestly, the arithmetic wasn't hard.

* * *

We crossed into Vessany—or what had been Vessany—on the sixth day, and the land told us before any map could have.

It started with the wolves.

I heard them at dusk, four voices weaving through the pines, and my whole body went tight with old habit: *danger, distance, hide.* But the song was wrong for hunting. Too loose. Too public. Wolves announcing a kill sound like triumph; wolves stalking sound like nothing at all. These four sang like escorts.

"They're pacing us," I said.

Dario's hand went to his knife. "Define 'pacing.'"

"Like honor guards." I listened harder, letting the meanings settle into shapes the way I'd done since childhood, since before I knew other people couldn't. *Come. Come. The road is safe. We walk it with you.* "They're saying the road is safe. That they're... walking it with us."

"Why?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Beasts didn't do courtesy for free. Marmots warned me because I fed them; ravens traded information because I paid in shinier information. Everything in the wild world was barter, and I had never once been offered an escort without having earned it first.

"I don't know," I said, which I hated saying. "But they're not herding us, and they're not driving us anywhere. If they wanted us dead we'd already be dead."

"Wonderful. I love a mystery with teeth."

The stones hummed louder as we went deeper. By the second day inside the old border, the humming had become a chord, and by the third I stopped needing to touch the ground to hear it—it rose up through my boot soles, through the marrow of me, and I realized with a lurch that the chord changed when I spoke. When I muttered to Sef, the road brightened under us, literally brightened, frost steaming off the flagstones in a thin morning mist that hadn't been there a moment before.

The land was answering me. Not tolerating me. *Answering* me.

In Thornfell, my gift had always been a small, furtive thing—a whisper to a marmot, a bargain with a raven, conducted in the cracks between human attention. Here it was a lamp, and everywhere I walked lit up.

"It likes you," Dario said quietly. He'd stopped flinching at my conversations with Sef somewhere around day five, mostly, I think, because Sef had stopped attacking him, which the hawk apparently considered sufficient diplomatic relations.

"It doesn't like anybody. It's a road."

"It's singing, Lena. Roads don't sing. Whatever this place was—" he gestured at the ruins beginning to show through the trees, low walls furred with moss, a fountain basin holding two hundred years of rainwater and one indignant newt, "—it was built by people like you. For people like you. This whole country was probably one big—"

He stopped. We both stopped, because the trees ended.

* * *

The gate stood alone in a bowl of mountain meadow, and it was the only thing in that valley that looked maintained.

A standing arch, maybe twenty feet high, of a dark stone I didn't recognize—black shot through with veins of white that caught the afternoon light like frozen lightning. No wall attached to it. No ruin around it. Just the arch, upright and clean-edged in a sea of wild grass, humming so loudly now that I could feel it in my fillings. The air inside the arch shimmered faintly, like heat over a summer road, though the season was deep autumn and the wind had teeth.

And there were people.

Seven of them, maybe eight, rising out of the grass on either side of the arch where I would have sworn no one stood. They moved wrong for ambush—not closing, not surrounding. Forming up. An honor guard, or a tribunal; with the light the way it was, I genuinely couldn't tell which.

They were dressed in undyed wool and fur, and they ranged in age from a boy of perhaps twelve to a woman so old she seemed carved from the same dark stone as the gate. Every one of them was watching me. Not Dario. Not Sef circling above. Me.

"Lena Zhou," the old woman said. My name in her mouth sounded like a verdict. "Thornfell-born. Speaker. You are expected."

"We're expected," Dario said, stepping up beside me with the easy confidence of a man whose survival strategy had always been audacity. "Both of us. Three counting the bird."

"We know what you are, smuggler." She said the word the way Ash said *traitor*. "You are expected because the road sang your companion's name for six days. It does not sing names for liars." Her eyes came back to mine, pale as winter water. "It sang yours the moment you crossed the border. The land has been waiting a long time for someone it could talk to."

Something in my chest went warm and stupid at that. *Waiting for someone it could talk to.* Two hundred years of humming into the dark, and here I was, the first listener to come down the road. I had to physically bite down on the feeling, because I'd learned—recently, expensively—that wanting to belong makes you stupid. Wanting to belong had gotten my barn burned.

"The door," I said. "The one out of the country. Is it real?"

The old woman turned and looked at the arch, and the others turned with her, seven heads moving together, and for a moment nobody spoke. The shimmer inside the arch pulsed once, slow, like breathing.

"It is real," she said. "It is the last door, and it opens only for voices the stone can hear. Your voice could open it. Perhaps only your voice, now." She paused, and I felt the pause the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. "But doors are not opened for free. Not this one. Not anymore."

"Here it comes," Dario murmured beside me. "The price list. There's always a price list."

"There is a price," the old woman said, unbothered, "and it is not coin." She lifted her chin toward the arch, toward whatever waited on the other side of that shimmer—refuge, escape, a country that might still exist somewhere the crown's edicts couldn't follow. "Before you pass, speaker, you will swear an oath. Swear that the gate closes behind you forever. That no word of its location leaves your lips, that no speaker follows you through, that Vessany's last door becomes Vessany's silence. Seal it behind you, and you may go free."

The warmth in my chest curdled all at once, and I thought of the rumors that had dragged us here in the first place. Rumors travel. Rumors had brought *us*, half-dead and hunted, following a smuggler's stolen maps. How many others were out there right now doing exactly what we'd done—hearing whispers, burning their own names, walking toward a door that was supposed to be their way out?

"How many people are coming behind us?" I asked.

The old woman's face didn't change. "All the more reason to seal it."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer there is." Behind her, the other guardians had gone very still, and I understood suddenly that this was not one woman's demand. This was a wall of them, older than the edict, older than the hunt, and every one of them had decided that survival meant shutting the door in the faces of everyone still running toward it.

Sef dropped out of the sky and landed on my shoulder, talons biting in just enough to remind me he was there, and far off down the valley, faint but unmistakable, the wolves began to howl.

Not an escort's song this time.

An alarm.

The old woman's head snapped toward the sound, and when she turned back to me her winter-pale eyes had gone narrow and sharp. "Someone else is on the road," she said. "Coming fast. And they are not singing."
