## 8. The Guardians' Bargain

The elders of the gate did not look like the last free beast-speakers of a hidden kingdom. They looked like my grandmother's sewing circle, if my grandmother's sewing circle had been armed and had opinions about me.

There were seven of them, seated in a crescent around a fire that burned in a pit carved directly into the mountain's living stone. The arch stood behind them, maybe forty paces off, humming like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. I could feel it in my teeth. In my sternum. In the place behind my ribs where Sef's grief had lodged when I first pulled the arrow out of his wing.

*It hears us,* I'd thought, when we'd crested the ridge and seen it. *The door actually hears.*

Now its hearers were looking at me like I was a debt come due.

"You are Zhou," said the eldest, a woman whose face was a topography of old burns, her voice dry as seed husks. "Lena Zhou, of the Thornfell line."

"That's me." I kept my hands loose at my sides. Dario, beside me, had gone very still in the way he did when he was calculating odds, which was always. "And you're the people who've been letting wolves walk escort for strangers. Which, for the record, is terrifying. A courtesy note would have sufficed."

No one laughed. The fire popped. Somewhere above us, on the arch itself, a bird called — one of Sef's kin, or something older — and the hum of the gate shifted key to answer it.

"The Thornfell speakers kept to themselves," the eldest said. "Generations. We heard them through the stone sometimes, faint as water under ice. They understood what we understand: that a gift spoken aloud is a gift taken away."

"Then you'll love this," I said. "I talked to marmots for a living. Out loud. On smuggling routes. With witnesses."

"We know," she said. "That is why you are here."

I let that sit. Around the crescent, the other elders watched me with expressions ranging from pity to something harder. One man, young for an elder, maybe fifty, had a falcon perched on his shoulder with its eyes half-closed in the way of birds who trusted absolutely. I envied him and hated him in about equal measure.

"You've brought refugees," I said. "We passed them on the road. Families. Two men carrying an old woman in a chair lashed to poles. They're camped at the tree line waiting for your permission to approach."

"They are not our concern," the eldest said.

"They're fleeing the same edict that's hunting me."

"They are fleeing," she agreed. "That is different from being hunted. Anyone can flee. Only we can be found by what we are." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved across her burn scars like water. "The crown has never known where this gate stands. In two hundred years, no soldier has crossed the Vessany border, because Vessany does not exist. That is our protection. Not strength. Erasure. And now you arrive with a smuggler, a hawk that screams your name to every predator in three valleys, and an inquisitor's warrant two days behind you—"

"You know about Ash?" My voice came out flatter than I intended.

"The stones told us," she said simply. "The stones tell us everything. She burned her warrant on the pass. Her commander did not burn his. He is coming, girl, and he is coming here, because she led him here as surely as if she'd drawn him a map. So now you understand why the gate must be sealed."

"Sealed," I repeated.

"Forever. The arch answers only to voices it can hear — to speakers. If there are no speakers left to call it, it becomes stone. Stone cannot be tortured, tracked, or betrayed. Our kind scatters, goes quiet, survives the way we have always survived: by being nothing to anyone." She spread her hands. "You came seeking a door out of a country that no longer exists. We are offering you the only exit that matters. But a door must close to be safe, and so we will close it — tonight, before the solstice, before the soldiers come."

"And everyone at the tree line?"

"Will turn back."

"Turn back," I said, "into the country where the crown burns barns with people's names nailed to the doors."

"Yes."

I looked at Dario. He was studying his boots with the concentration of a man hoping to be mistaken for furniture. "Say something," I muttered.

"This seems like a family discussion," he said.

"You sold my name to save your neck. Consider this your penance. Talk."

He sighed, straightened, and turned on the charm I'd watched him use on customs officers and angry mule dealers. "Respected elders. Speaking as a man whose entire moral framework collapsed recently and has been rebuilt mostly out of guilt—"

"Dario."

"—the refugees at the tree line include four children, a woman nine months pregnant, and a one-eyed goatherd who saved my life once and therefore outranks most saints. You seal that gate tonight, they die. Maybe not tomorrow. Maybe not this winter. But the edict doesn't expire, and neither do informants. I'd know. I'm one."

Silence. The falcon on the young elder's shoulder opened one eye, considered Dario, and closed it again, which was more mercy than anyone else in the crescent showed him.

"The smuggler speaks truth he should not," said a second elder — a broad-shouldered man with a raven on each fist. "That is the trouble with letting strangers reach the gate. Truth leaks."

"The trouble," I said, "is that you've spent two centuries being safe and you've started calling it being right."

The eldest's eyes narrowed. "You are young."

"I'm twenty-six and I've been invisible my whole life, so let me tell you what invisibility costs." I stepped forward, into the firelight, because apparently I was doing this. Apparently the words had been queuing up since a waystation clerk read a proclamation in a bored voice while marmots screamed warnings in my skull. "I kept quiet. I kept small. I spoke to beasts and never to people, and I told myself that was wisdom. It wasn't. It was just fear wearing wisdom's coat. And the day the crown decided my silence wasn't enough — the day some clerk wrote *beast-speaker* next to the word *traitor* — all those years of being nothing bought me exactly nothing. Do you know why? Because being nothing isn't a place you own. It's a place you borrow. And the landlord can evict you whenever he wants."

The fire cracked. Behind the elders, the arch hummed, low and patient, like it was listening. Of course it was listening. Everything out here listened.

"Seal the gate," I went on, quieter, "and you don't erase yourselves. You just move the eviction notice down the road. To the goatherd. To the pregnant woman. To every speaker still hiding in attics and shepherd huts who'll never find this door because you locked it to keep your own hands clean. You'll survive. Congratulations. You'll survive the way a stone survives."

"You would have us fight," the eldest said. "With what? Seven elders and a smuggler?"

"With the door open," I said. "Not with swords. With the thing they can't take. There are speakers coming here from every direction — I've heard them on the wind, felt them in the stones, same as you have. Every one of them adds a voice. The gate opens to heard voices? Then let it hear an army of them. Let it stand open through the solstice and pour every fleeing soul in this country through it, and then—" I stopped. Because the rest of the sentence was the part I hadn't let myself think yet, and saying it aloud would make it real.

"And then?" the eldest prompted.

"And then whoever's left holds it," I said, "until the last one's through."

Dario made a small sound. When I glanced at him, he was looking at me with an expression I'd never seen on him before — something between horror and awe, the way you look at someone walking a rope over a gorge.

The council split the way ice splits: all at once, along lines that had been there far longer than me. The raven-fisted elder stood for holding the gate. The falcon man wavered, then stood with him. Two others argued for sealing in voices that shook — not with cruelty, I realized, but with terror, the specific terror of people who had buried friends and told themselves it was strategy. The eldest sat unmoving, her scarred hands folded, watching me the whole time like I was a weather pattern she hadn't planned for.

"You speak well," she said at last, "for someone who claims to prefer talking to animals."

"I prefer animals. They don't ask me to choose who lives."

"No," she agreed. "They leave that to us. That is what a gift is." She rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and the crescent rose with her. "Here is what remains of our bargain, Lena Zhou. The council is divided, and a divided council cannot seal the gate — the sealing requires every voice raised together, and half of ours refuse. So the gate stays open through the solstice. Through dawn after. Any speaker who reaches it may pass." Her eyes held mine, and there was no mercy in them, only arithmetic. "But understand what you have purchased. An open gate is a lit window in a dark country. The soldiers are coming. The stones say they will be here by solstice night. And when they come, it will not be seven elders standing between them and the door."

"It'll be me," I said.

"It will be you," she said. "You wanted to speak for those who cannot. Very well. From this moment, you do. Their lives are in your mouth, girl. Pray your gift extends to carrying that weight."

She turned and walked toward the arch, and the others followed, and I stood alone in the firelight with Dario and a hawk on my shoulder whose talons had gone tight enough to hurt.

"Well," Dario said finally. "For what it's worth, I've talked my way out of a lot of rooms, and that was the worst deal I've ever watched anyone make."

"It's not a deal," I said. I reached up and touched Sef's breast feathers, feeling the quick hammer of his heart against my knuckle. "Deals end."

Above us, the arch changed its note — a rising tone, clear and questioning, like a voice asking *who's there?* — and far off down the valley, carried on wind that smelled of snow and smoke, something answered.

Hooves. Many of them. Coming early.

* * *

The solstice was supposed to give us two more days. The stones gave us one night.

By moonrise the tree-line camp had swollen to nearly sixty souls, and the elders moved among them with grim efficiency, sorting the strong, the slow, the sick. I stood at the arch with my hand flat against cold stone that pulsed like a sleeping animal's flank, and I fed it everything I had — names, mostly. The goatherd's name. The pregnant woman's, which was Marta, and the name she'd already chosen for the child. Names were the coin the gate liked best; I could feel it drinking them.

Sef launched off my shoulder and wheeled up into the dark, screaming once — his carrying scream, the one any tracker could follow, the beacon I'd never managed to shed.

Good, I thought. Scream loud. Let them aim for me.

Because far below, torchlight had begun to thread up the old Vessany road like fire finding a fuse, and at the head of it rode a rider I knew even at this distance, even in the dark, by the set of her shoulders alone.

Ash had found the gate. And she hadn't come alone.
