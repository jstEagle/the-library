## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Ember Guild kept its accounts in a cellar that smelled of tallow, old paper, and the particular mineral cold of things that had been buried a long time and dug back up on purpose. Isandro Brightwater had worked down there for six years, and in all that time she had never once been late, never once been caught resting, and never once told anyone what she could hear.

She was auditing the storm account.

This was not a figure of speech. Shelf eleven, row three, held forty-one glass jars, each one stoppered with wax and wire, each one containing weather. Most were ordinary—rain from a wedding nobody wanted to end, a gust of wind off a mountain that had since been quarried flat—but jar nineteen had begun, over the past month, to sulk. Its little grey cloud sat at the bottom of the glass like a wet cat, and when Isandro lifted it to the light, the light went bruised and yellow.

"Depreciating asset," she murmured, and wrote it down. *Jar 19: thunderstorm, minor, temperamental. Value declining. Recommend sale before it learns to hail.*

Master Corvel did not care for her marginalia. He cared very much for her totals. In six years he had read perhaps four sentences she'd written, and all four had been numbers, which was exactly as she intended. Numbers said nothing about you. Numbers were a kind of silence you could get paid for.

Isandro had gotten very good at silences.

She set jar nineteen back between its neighbors—the fog of a drowned village (steady seller, the fishermen's widows bought it by the spoonful) and a jar labeled only *Tuesday* in Corvel's cramped hand, contents unknown, appraisal pending eleven years now—and turned to the ledger proper.

The ledger of impossible things was not one book but many, and today's was a slim green volume titled, in faded gilt, *Accounts Receivable: The Dead, the Departed, and the Otherwise Engaged.* She opened it to the ribbon and began where she'd left off, at entry 114:

*Owed to the Guild by the ghost of Tammas Reeve, ferryman, deceased nine years: one crossing, unpaid; one apology, undelivered; interest accrued in sorrow, compounding.*

Under normal circumstances a debt owed by a ghost would be written off after a decade. But Tammas Reeve's ghost still stood his ferry, or thought he did, out on the mist flats past the tanneries, poling passengers who couldn't see him across water that wasn't there. Twice a year the guild sent a junior clerk out with a coin and a message, and twice a year the clerk came back pale and richer by one fare. The apology, however, remained outstanding. Apologies always did. They were the worst-performing asset in the entire catalogue.

Isandro ruled a neat line under the entry, noted *fare collected, Michaelmas,* and moved on.

Entry 115 concerned a miller who had sold the guild his shadow in a bad winter and now wanted it back at the original price, which was not how commerce worked, whatever the ballads said. Entry 116 was a promissory note from a woman who claimed to be her own granddaughter and produced documents to prove it. Entry 117—

Entry 117 made her stop.

It wasn't the entry itself. Entry 117 was routine: *One song, sung once, purchased from a nightingale, price: worms in perpetuity. Held in trust.* The guild bought songs from birds the way other houses bought wool, and the nightingale contracts were among their most reliable holdings. No, what stopped her was the sound coming from the corner of the cellar, behind the barrels of bottled riverwater, where the guild kept its live inventory.

Someone was crying. And someone was speaking. And both someones were the same someone, and the someone was a lark.

*"Please,"* said the lark. *"Oh, please, please, please."*

Isandro's pen hovered. She listened the way she always listened—with her whole body still, her breath shallow, her ears doing the work her face refused to do. This was the discipline of it. Anyone watching would have seen a clerk pausing over her figures, frowning slightly at an addition gone wrong. What they would not have seen was a woman hearing a bird beg in words.

Because that was the thing. It wasn't chirps translated, not really. It wasn't the loose impression other people sometimes claimed—a dog's growl meaning *anger*, a cat's purr meaning *contentment*, the sort of fortune-telling any fool could do with a squint. When animals spoke to Isandro, they spoke in words. Whole sentences. Grammar. The lark's voice was small and bright and cracked with fear, and it said, in the common tongue of Vessany-that-was, *"They have forgotten I am here. Please. Please remember me."*

Her heart did what it always did, which was to break a little and then harden over the break, like ice over a well.

She knew the lark. Inventory item 44, a singer, acquired in spring from a peddler who had caught it near the Salt Gate. The guild didn't buy larks to keep them; it bought them to sell, the way it bought everything. A singing lark in a fine cage fetched good coin from merchants' wives who wanted music without the trouble of loving anything. Item 44 had been on the shelf two months because the cage order hadn't come in, and in two months no one—not the factor, not the porter, not Master Corvel himself—had thought to feed it more than scraps, or clean its cage, or notice that it had stopped singing somewhere around the third week.

No one but Isandro, who had been slipping it seed from her own supper ever since, and hating herself for the slowness of her kindness, and telling herself every evening: *not tomorrow. Tomorrow I will say something. Tomorrow I will tell Corvel the bird is inventory and inventory is being neglected and it is hurting the books, and I will say it in numbers, safely, and the bird will be fed properly, and no one will ever know why I noticed.*

And every tomorrow she had chosen the silence instead, because the silence was armor, and she had worn it so long she'd forgotten there was any other way to be dressed.

*"You came,"* the lark said, as her boots crossed the flagstones toward the barrels. Hope in a voice that small was almost unbearable. *"You always come. I knew you'd come."*

"I come every day," Isandro said quietly. She checked, first, the door at the top of the stairs—it was shut, and beyond it the counting room murmured with the ordinary sounds of clerks who were not listening, because no one ever listened to the cellar—and then she knelt by the cage. "I'm sorry it's only seed again. There's a apple core in my bag. Don't tell anyone I gave you the good part."

The lark didn't laugh, because larks don't, but it did something with its feathers that was unmistakably the same gesture. It ate with the desperate elegance of the half-starved, and Isandro watched, and hated the peddler, and hated the cage, and hated most of all the careful clerk-shaped person she had built out of herself, the one who noticed everything and said nothing.

"How long," she asked, "have you been able to talk? To me, I mean. Not—" she gestured vaguely, "—general conversation."

"All my life," said the lark. "But no one ever answered before. Do you know what it's like, talking and talking into a world that doesn't hear you?" It cocked its head, one black eye fixed on her. "You do. I can see it. We're the same, you and I. Two things in cages, pretending to be furniture."

"I am not in a cage."

"No," the lark agreed mildly, hopping closer to the bars. "But you've measured yours. I can tell. You know exactly how wide it is and exactly where the door is, and you stand next to the door every single day, and every single day you don't open it." It preened once, briskly. "I used to do that too. Then the net came down anyway. Waiting doesn't keep the net away. It just means you're caught somewhere smaller."

Isandro sat back on her heels. Above her, muffled through floorboards, the counting room went about its morning: the scratch of pens, Corvel's cough, the porter whistling something tuneless. The great safe machinery of the guild, running on paper and habit, with her down in the dark as one of its gears.

"The door of your cage has a latch," she said. "I could open it right now. You'd die by winter. Larks don't last in a city."

"I know," said the lark. "I'd rather die having flown than live having perched. But that's a bird's arithmetic, isn't it. Yours runs the other direction. Yours says: *endure, endure, stay useful, stay unseen, and maybe nothing bad will find you.*" It hopped onto its swing, which it had never once used, and looked at her with terrible gentleness. "Has it worked?"

She thought of her mother, dead eleven years, who had held one finger to her lips and taught her the first rule before teaching her letters: *never let them hear you talk to anything.* Thought of the innkeeper's boy in her home village who had spoken to snakes, and of how quickly the village had stopped being able to see him, and of how quickly after that he had simply stopped, in the way people stop. Thought of six years of perfect attendance and perfect silence and a life so narrow she could have audited it in an afternoon.

"It's worked so far," she said.

"So far," the lark repeated, softly, the way you'd repeat a debt amount you didn't believe would ever be paid.

* * *

At noon she carried the green ledger up the stairs and into the counting room, where Master Corvel presided behind a desk carved with the guild's sigil—an ember in an open palm, though the carving was so old and so polished the ember had become a smooth bump and the palm a suggestion. Corvel himself was a long, dry man, folded into his coat like a letter into an envelope, with spectacles he cleaned when he was thinking and removed when he was disappointed. He removed them now, as she approached.

"Brightwater."

"Master Corvel. The quarterly reconciliation." She laid the ledger on his desk, squared to the desk's edge, because Corvel believed a crooked ledger revealed a crooked soul. "Three items require your seal. The Reeve ghost account—we collected the Michaelmas fare, and I recommend we write down the apology as unrecoverable. The shadow repurchase request—I've drafted a refusal citing clause twelve, but you may wish to soften it, the miller cries easily. And the nightingale trust has matured; the song can be sold at solstice for best return."

Corvel read. He read the way he did everything, thoroughly and without pleasure, and while he read, Isandro stood in the particular posture she had perfected over six years: attentive, unremarkable, a fixture. A good clerk, the counting room agreed, was like a good chair. Useful, silent, and beneath notice.

"You've marked jar nineteen for early sale," Corvel said.

"The storm's sulking. If it turns to hail it'll crack the glass, and then we're sweeping lightning off the cellar floor, and you remember what that cost us in the Year of the Wet Ledger."

Something happened at the corner of Corvel's mouth that, in a younger man, might have become a smile. "I remember." He signed the three items, pressed his seal into the wax with the tenderness he reserved for no living creature, and then, instead of dismissing her, took off his spectacles entirely and looked at her with his naked, shortsighted eyes.

"Brightwater. How long have you been with the guild?"

"Six years, sir."

"And in six years you have never asked for a raise."

"The guild pays fairly, sir."

"The guild pays *adequately,*" Corvel corrected, "which is different, and you know it is, and you have never once mentioned it. You have never been ill. You have never taken leave. You have never, in six years, given me a single reason to notice you, and you are the best auditor I have ever employed, which means you must understand precisely how strange that is." He put the spectacles back on, and behind the glass his eyes were sharp as awls. "People want to be seen, Brightwater. It's the one appetite everyone has. Yet you starve yours like a monk. Why?"

Every answer she owned rose up at once—*because seen things get caught, because my mother died whispering, because there was a boy who talked to snakes*—and every answer was a door, and she stood beside all of them and did not open any.

"I was raised to be careful, sir," she said. "Careful clerks keep their positions."

"Hm," said Corvel, and she could not tell whether the sound meant *true* or *liar* or *interesting,* and the not-knowing sat in her chest all afternoon like a swallowed stone.

He waved her back to the cellar. At the door she paused, because there was one more item, and she had debated all morning whether to raise it, and the lark's voice was still in her ears—*you know exactly where the door is*—and so, in the flattest voice she owned, in the language of columns and margins, she opened the smallest crack.

"One more matter. Inventory item 44, the lark. It's been on our shelf two months awaiting a cage order that hasn't arrived. Depreciating asset. A singer that isn't singing sells for half. I recommend we either source the cage this week or release the stock before it loses value entirely."

Corvel looked up, faintly surprised, and Isandro understood that in six years this might be the longest sentence of opinion she had ever offered him.

"Release it?" He sniffed. "Waste of acquisition costs. Very well—order the cage from Ferro's, and if Ferro dithers again, take the bird to the market yourself and get what it's worth." He returned to his papers, already done with her. "Efficiency, Brightwater. That's all I ask."

"Yes, sir," said Isandro, and meant, with her whole hidden heart, *yes.*

* * *

She went down the stairs lighter than she had climbed them. A plan, at last, with terms and a deadline: within the week, the lark would fly. She would carry it to the market as instructed, walk past the market, keep walking to the Salt Gate, and open the cage on the downs where the grass grew and the wind ran, and the lark would go up singing, and no one would ever know, and the loss on the guild's books would be four coppers, and it would be the best-spent four coppers in the history of accounting.

The lark heard the whole scheme with its head tipped, and when she finished, it was quiet a moment.

"That's the door," it said. "That's you opening the door."

"It's asset management," said Isandro.

"It's absolutely asset management," the lark agreed solemnly, and then, gentler: "Thank you. Whatever happens to me—thank you. Now listen. While you were upstairs, someone else came to the hatch. The fat rat from behind the flour barrels. He talks too, you know. They all talk. Nobody listens, so nobody knows the city is *full* of conversation." The lark's feathers flattened; its voice dropped. "He brought news from the docks. Rats hear everything first. Ships came in before dawn, crown ships, quiet ones, and men came off them in grey coats who aren't merchants and aren't sailors. The rats say they carry writs. The rats say the writs name a crime." A pause, small and terrible. "The crime is talking."

Isandro stood very still in the lamplight, in the smell of tallow and cold stone, with the ledger closed under her arm and her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

"That's gossip," she said. "Rats exaggerate."

"Rats," said the lark, "never exaggerate about nets."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the counting room door opening, and boots, and voices—more voices than usual, official ones, and Master Corvel's dry tones rising to meet them, and underneath it all, unmistakable even through wood and stone, the ring of a herald's staff striking the floorboards of the Ember Guild, once, twice, like a knock at the door of the world.

The lark looked up at her. Isandro looked down at the lark.

"Well," said the bird quietly. "So much for so far."
