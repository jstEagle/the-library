## 2. Proclamation at the Salt Gate

The heralds arrived on a Tuesday, which Isandro thought was a coward's choice of day. Tuesdays were when the Ember Guild received its grain shipments, and the whole Salt Gate quarter smelled of chaff and axle grease and the particular sweetness of spilled molasses that no one ever fully cleaned from the cobbles. Nobody paid attention to anything on Tuesdays. The city had learned to sleepwalk through them.

She was in the counting room with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, reconciling the account of a bottled storm — the storm had been leaking, three drops a month, and each drop cost the guild four silver in dampness damage to the neighboring shelf — when she heard the drums.

Not festival drums. Festival drums were warm and slightly drunk. These were dry, precise, spaced like footsteps on gravel.

Isandro set down her pen. Through the counting room's one window, high and narrow as a letter slot, she could see a sliver of the Salt Gate square: the fountain with its chipped mermaid, the awnings of the pie-seller who watered his ale, and now, marching into all of it, six riders in crown livery with a seventh figure ahead of them on foot.

The herald wore grey. Not the grey of poverty or the grey of mourning, but the grey of paper. He climbed the fountain's rim with the unhurried competence of a man who had done this in forty towns and would do it in forty more, unrolled a scroll the length of an arm, and waited for the drums to stop.

The square did not stop. That was the thing about Salt Gate; it never stopped for anyone. Pie-sellers kept selling. A woman argued with a fishmonger about the freshness of something that had clearly given up arguing for itself days ago. Two boys raced hoops between the herald's legs, and he simply stepped aside and went on waiting, patient as frost, until — gradually, the way weather changes — the noise thinned. People noticed the grey man. People noticed the drums. And then, without any order being given, the square grew quiet, because everyone knew what a herald's silence meant, even if they'd never heard one before.

"By order of the Crown," the herald read, in a voice like a receipt being itemized, "a bounty is declared upon the crime of beast-speaking."

Isandro's hands went still on the ledger.

"The practice of converse with beasts, birds, and creatures of field and fen is hereby named a corruption of the natural order. Persons so corrupted are to be surrendered to the Crown's hunters for examination. Reward: fifty crowns per head, one hundred crowns where the speaker is of age and sound of body." A pause, the scroll turning with a small dry whisper. "Informants will be rewarded. Informants will be protected. Ignorance of a neighbor's corruption is no excuse, and shall be treated as complicity."

There it was. Isandro had heard proclamations before — tax levies, curfews, the year they banned unlicensed fortune-telling and half the tea-houses in Salt Gate had gone quiet overnight. Proclamations always had that same architecture: a crime invented, a price attached, and then, tucked into the middle like a knife slipped between ribs, the clause that turned neighbor against neighbor. *Ignorance is no excuse.* It meant that not reporting your own grandmother became a crime. It meant that silence itself was now evidence.

The herald rolled up his scroll. The drums started again. Six riders wheeled, and the square exhaled, and the pie-seller called out that yes, the pies were fresh, yes, today only, and everything resumed as though nothing had happened, which was exactly how such things worked. Fear didn't arrive like a storm. It arrived like paperwork.

Isandro stood very still for a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat count out the seconds.

Then she went back to the storm ledger, because Master Corvel expected the dampness column balanced by noon, and because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

She lasted until the fishmonger.

It happened on her walk home — she lodged above a chandler's shop two streets from the guildhouse, close enough to smell the tallow, far enough that Corvel couldn't ask why her lamp burned past midnight. She took the long way through Tanner's Row, as she always did, because Tanner's Row had cats, and cats were honest company in a way people rarely managed.

Old Marn was at his stall, as always, hawking grayling and eels with the enthusiasm of a man selling his own funeral. Marn had sold Isandro fish every week for six years. He overcharged her shamelessly and slipped an extra smelt into her parcel when he thought she wasn't looking, which she never let him know she noticed. His stall cat — a scarred tortoiseshell named Duchess by nobody in particular — slept in the ice trough and opened one eye at Isandro as she passed.

*You smell of ink,* said Duchess, in the way cats say things, which is less words than weather. *And worry.*

*I always smell of ink,* Isandro said back, silently, lips still.

*Today you smell of worry under it.*

"Fresh grayling!" Marn bellowed at a passing cooper. "Caught this morning, still arguing!"

And that was when the riders came back down Tanner's Row.

Two of them, peeling off from the main party, moving at a walk — the deliberate, browsing walk of men reading a street the way Isandro read a ledger. One held a paper. They stopped at the corner. The paper was consulted. Then they looked up, and looked along the row of stalls, and stopped looking at Marn's fish entirely, because they were looking at Marn.

No. Not at Marn. At the ice trough.

At Duchess.

Isandro felt it happen inside her chest, some cold mechanism engaging, gears of a machine she hadn't known the Crown possessed. Of course. Of course it made sense. If you wanted to find beast-speakers, you didn't hunt the speakers. You hunted the beasts that spoke *to* them. You watched for animals that watched people too closely. You watched for a cat that opened its eyes at the wrong moment, that tracked a stranger down a row of stalls with the attention of an old friend.

Duchess had looked at her. In front of witnesses. In front of the whole street, Duchess had lifted her head and followed Isandro with her gaze, and somewhere in the Crown's new arithmetic that was worth fifty crowns.

"Marn Hessel?" the lead rider said, consulting his paper. "Keeper of this stall?"

"That's me." Marn's voice did something complicated. "That's my stall, sirs, finest grayling on the Row—"

"You're reported as keeping a beast of unusual familiarity. Step aside from the animal, please."

"It's a *cat*, sir. She sleeps in the ice. She's lazy as Sunday—"

"Step aside from the animal."

Marn stepped aside. That was the part Isandro would remember afterward, lying awake under the eaves with the chandler's tallow-smell in her nostrils: that Marn stepped aside. Quickly. Without argument. Fifty crowns sat on the counter between him and his own conscience, and whatever else Marn was — generous, funny, a man who gave away smelt — he was also a man with rent due and a bad knee and winter coming, and the Crown had priced his loyalty at a number he could almost touch.

They took Duchess in a sack. The cat did not struggle much, which Isandro understood better than anyone on that street ever would: Duchess had been taken before, in her long scarred life, and had learned that struggling only extended the arithmetic. But as the sack swung from the rider's fist, the cat's voice came through the burlap, thin and clear and addressed directly at Isandro, who stood frozen among the customers with her basket on her arm like any other woman buying fish.

*Ink-girl,* said Duchess. *Don't be stupid.*

Then the riders turned their horses, and the sack swung once, twice, and Tanner's Row went back to its business, and Marn began hawking grayling again in a voice that cracked only slightly, only on the word *fresh.*

Isandro walked home. She did not run. Running attracted notice. She walked home at the speed of a woman with nothing on her mind but supper, and she climbed her stairs, and she bolted her door, and then she sat down on the floor with her back against the bed and shook for a while, quietly, the way you shake when you've learned to do it economically.

*Don't be stupid,* Duchess had said. Which meant: *don't come after me.* Which meant the cat had understood, in the space of one swing of a sack, exactly what Isandro was and what it would cost her to be seen caring. Animals always understood the ledger faster than people did.

Fifty crowns. For a cat. What was the rate for a woman?

* * *

The guildhouse that evening smelled of beeswax and old vellum, and Master Corvel was in his office doing what he did every evening: losing gracefully at solitaire against himself and pretending the game required concentration. He was a small, neat man built like a well-kept filing cabinet, with spectacles he polished when he was thinking and removed when he was about to say something unpleasant. Isandro had worked for him five years and had catalogued perhaps nine of his smiles, all of them identical, all of them deployed like stamps on documents.

"Balance the storm account?" he asked, without looking up.

"Balanced. The leak's worsening. I'd recommend we sell before midsummer; another season of dampness and we'll be paying more in shelf-damage than the storm's worth."

"Mm. Sell it to the opera house. They'll pay a premium for authentic thunder." He moved a card. Lost, apparently, and gathered the deck with a sigh. "You heard the proclamation."

Not a question. Isandro kept her face arranged into the mild blankness of a clerk discussing weather. "Hard to miss it, master. Drums in the square at noon."

"Fifty crowns a head," Corvel said. "A hundred for adults. Do you know what the guild pays its senior auditor?"

"Eleven crowns a month, plus lodging."

"So a single denunciation would buy the informer nearly a year of your salary, with change left over for drink." He set the deck down and finally looked at her over the spectacles. "I mention it only as an observation on market forces."

"I'm sure the whole quarter's making the same calculation, master."

"Mm." He polished the spectacles. Put them back on. "The ledgers, Isandro. There are entries in the older books that the Crown would find... interesting. Debts owed by beasts. Contracts witnessed by them. One account — I won't say which — records a wolf who owed the guild a favor and repaid it in a manner the historians of three kingdoms would commit crimes to read about." He smiled his stamp-smile. "If the hunters came here, they would find a library of evidence that beast-speaking is not merely real but *commercially established*. They would burn the guildhouse, and worse, they would audit it."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because you are my senior auditor, and I am informing you, as a matter of professional courtesy, that the archives are to be reviewed. Certain pages may need to become... illegible." He returned to his solitaire. "Fire is traditional. Do wear gloves; vellum smuts terribly."

Isandro stood there for a moment, doing rapid, silent arithmetic of her own. Corvel knew. She couldn't prove he knew, and he would deny it to the grave with perfect bookkeeping sincerity, but the whole conversation had been conducted in the language they shared best: figures, margins, things said sideways. He had just told her, in the dialect of market forces and smutty gloves, that he knew what she was, that the proclamation endangered her specifically, and that the guild's ledgers contained her name written in more places than one.

"Good night, master," she said.

"Good night, Isandro. Mind how you go home. The streets are full of informants this season, and most of them can't even read."

* * *

She went down to the archive instead of going home.

The archive occupied the guildhouse cellar, behind a door with three locks, and it held the true wealth of the Ember Guild: not the bottled storms or the ghost-debts or the famous last salt of drowned Vessany, but the *paper*. Forty years of impossible transactions, recorded in Corvel's crabbed hand and the hands of auditors before him, shelved in the dark like wine.

Isandro lit one lamp, low, and found the volume she was looking for by memory alone. The current-year ledger. Her ledger. The one with her name on the flyleaf in her own writing — *Isandro Brightwater, Auditor* — and beneath it, in the margin of the third page, in her own hand, in a moment of private foolishness she had never been able to explain even to herself:

*Also speaks to mules, cats, gulls, one offended goose (see p. 41), and the river, though the river mostly complains.*

She had written it as a joke. A private joke, in a locked archive, in a book only she and Corvel touched. Five years ago it had seemed safe as a secret kept in a bank vault.

Tonight it read like a confession awaiting only a signature from the Crown.

She carried the ledger to the lamp and stood over it for a long time. This was the strange cruelty of it: the page was hers. Her handwriting, her joke, her one small vanity in a life otherwise spent being invisible — the invisible armor she wore so well that sometimes she forgot she was wearing it, and wrote things like this, little proofs that she existed, that someone in the world knew her all the way down. Burning it felt like burning a diary. Burning it felt like burning a piece of herself, the piece that had believed secrets kept in cellars stayed kept.

*Don't be stupid,* Duchess had said.

Isandro took off her glove, as instructed, and fed the corner of the page to the lamp.

Vellum burns reluctantly. It curls, browns, goes black at the edges with a smell like a snuffed candle multiplied by regret. She held the burning page over the cold hearth and watched her own handwriting blister and vanish — the goose, the complaining river, the mules — and felt the armor close around her again, plate by plate, colder and smoother than before. Safe. Invisible. Alone, which was the price of the second thing and had always been.

When it was ash she ground the ash fine between her fingers and scattered it in the hearth, and washed her hands thoroughly, twice, and put the ledger back on its shelf, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the building settle around forty years of other people's secrets.

That was when she heard the knocking.

Not at the guildhouse door — at the *cellar* door, the trapdoor to the coal chute, the one that hadn't opened in years. Three slow knocks. A pause. Three more.

Isandro went very still, lamp turned low, one hand finding the poker.

Through the chute's iron grate, in the dark, in a voice roughened by burlap and fear and the particular hoarseness of a creature who had spent the day in a sack, came words no human ear in Vessany's successor-kingdoms would have believed:

*Ink-girl,* said Duchess. *They're asking me questions. And ink-girl — the ones asking aren't from the Crown at all.*
