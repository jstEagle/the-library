## 3. Master Corvel's Offer

The morning after the proclamation, the Ember Guild smelled of ink and fear.

Isandro noticed it first in the ledgers themselves. She had worked for Master Corvel long enough to know that paper held moods the way cloth held smoke. The accounts came up from the vault that morning with a dampness to them that had nothing to do with weather, and when she ran her thumb along the edge of the storm-bottling inventory, the pages shivered.

"You're imagining things," she told them quietly, and then, because no one was listening but a shelf of account books and one very opinionated housecat asleep on the window ledge, she added, "We're all imagining things."

The cat opened one eye. *You're not,* it said, in the way cats say things—half contempt, half invitation. *Your hands smell of burnt page.*

Isandro tucked her fingers into her sleeves. "Good morning to you too."

*They took the cooper's daughter off Wren Street yesterday,* the cat said, stretching until every rib showed. *I saw it from the wall. She screamed like a gull.*

"I know."

*Do you?* The cat sat up and began washing one paw with terrible deliberation. *Because you've been auditing since dawn as though arithmetic could save anyone.*

Isandro did not answer. Arithmetic, in fact, was the only thing that had ever saved her. Numbers did not care what you were. A column of figures summed the same whether you could speak to beasts or not, and Master Corvel had hired her six years ago because her sums were flawless and her silences were longer than most people's conversations. He had never once asked why she flinched at the fishmonger's cart, or why she always knew, before anyone else, which of the delivery animals would bolt.

She had built her whole life inside those unasked questions. And now a herald in crown colors had stood at the Salt Gate and read out, in a voice like a tax assessment, that the questions were over.

*The cooper's girl couldn't even talk to anything,* the cat went on. *She just *understood* them. Understood a goat once, publicly, at market. That was enough.* It licked its paw. *What does it take to be enough for you, ledger-keeper?*

"Quiet," said Isandro, who was very good at saying quiet to animals and very bad at saying it to herself.

The door to the counting room opened without a knock. That alone made her spine go straight; everyone knocked for Master Corvel. Everyone except Master Corvel.

He was not a large man. He had the particular smallness of someone who had spent decades making himself dense instead—compact as a locked strongbox, gray at the temples, with reading spectacles pushed up into his hair and a signet ring he wore on his thumb, which Isandro had always privately considered either an eccentricity or a threat display. This morning he carried two cups of tea, which he had never once done in six years.

"Master Corvel," she said, rising.

"Sit down, Brightwater." He set one cup before her. "Drink it while it's hot. I'm only going to say this once, and you'll want your hands occupied so they don't shake where I can see them."

Her hands, she realized, were shaking exactly where he could see them. She wrapped them around the cup.

Corvel lowered himself into the chair across the desk—the client's chair, the debtor's chair—and folded his small clever hands atop his knee. For a long moment he simply looked at her, and Isandro understood, with the sinking clarity of a figure refusing to sum, that everything was about to change.

"Page forty-one," he said. "Of the general ledger. Third entry down."

"I—" Her mouth was dry. "That's the entry for the miller's ghost-debt, sir. Settled four years ago, in grain—"

"Brightwater." His voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. Gentle was not in his repertoire. "I am not testing your memory of the ledger. I am telling you that I have known about you since your second week in my employ, and I would like us both to stop pretending, because pretending is about to become fatal."

The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere below, in the salt cellar, a barrel settled with a groan.

"How," Isandro managed.

"The horses." He said it matter-of-factly, the way he might report an exchange rate. "Every carter in this city knows you can settle any animal in the guild's yard just by standing near it. They think you have a way with beasts. Carters are sentimental idiots. But I watched you the day the black mare foaled early, in the ice, and the foal was turned wrong, and you knelt down in that straw and *talked to her*, Brightwater—not *at* her, not the soothing nonsense people do—and the mare *listened*. Rolled. Did what you asked. And afterward you looked at me across the yard with such terror that I knew you'd rather die than be caught at it." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve. "So I decided never to catch you at it. Six years of not catching you at it. Do you know how much discipline that takes from a man whose entire trade is looking closely at things?"

Isandro's tea trembled against her lip. She set it down. "Why?"

"Because a gift that walks through my door is an asset or a liability, and I don't sell liabilities, and I don't waste assets." He put the spectacles back on. "Also—though you'll find this harder to believe—because you were nineteen and alone and looked like a rabbit in a snare, and I have a weakness for things in snares. It's why I run this guild. Half our inventory is things in snares."

"The bottled storms," she said faintly.

"The bottled storms. Exactly." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat checked. "Now. Yesterday a herald read a proclamation at the Salt Gate. Today there are beast-hunters billeted at the Salt Barracks under a Captain Ferrow, and by week's end there will be more of them, and they will come to every counting-house, chandlery, stable, and slaughter-yard in Vessany City asking after anyone who talks to animals. Including, eventually, this one." He leaned forward. "And here is what you need to understand about me, Isandro: I do not intend to let them take you."

The use of her given name landed like a dropped coin.

"They took the cooper's daughter," Isandro said. "She wasn't even—she only *understood*—"

"I know what she could do. I also know what happens to the taken. The proclamations call it 'assessment.' Assessment means cages, and handlers, and being made to testify against other gift-bearers until you're used up." His jaw tightened. "The crown has decided that people like you are a resource. I have spent thirty years building a guild on the principle that nothing with a will of its own should belong to the crown. So we are going to make you expensive instead."

"Expensive?"

"Protected. Indispensable. The kind of asset that gets men hanged for touching." He spread his hands. "Here is the shape of it. You know our books better than I do—you keep them. So tell me: what does the Ember Guild hold debts *on*, besides storms and ghosts and salt?"

Isandro blinked. The question was so far outside the shape of her terror that it took her a moment to climb into it. Then, because she was who she was, she answered it properly.

"Livestock," she said slowly. "Delivery contracts. Carthorses, mules, the ferry-ponies. We hold surety on... on perhaps two hundred animals across the city and the river roads. Beasts pledged against their owners' debts. If a debtor defaults, the beast passes to the guild."

"Precisely." Corvel's eyes had gone bright and hard, the way they did over a profitable contract. "Two hundred animals. Every one of them with ears, and memory, and a tongue—if only someone could ask them what they'd seen. Think about it, Brightwater. A carter vanishes on the river road. His widow swears he was robbed; the magistrates shrug; the case dies. But the mule was *there*. The mule saw the faces, heard the voices, remembers the road. In this city, half the crimes ever committed happened in front of some beast or other, and every one of those crimes is sitting in our ledgers as collateral." He tapped the desk. "Beasts can testify, Isandro. No one has ever thought to collect on that debt. Because no one could hear it. Until you."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Isandro gripped her cup.

"You want me to take testimony," she said. "From the collateral."

"I want you to audit it," Corvel corrected. "In writing. Properly witnessed, properly sealed, entered in a new book I'll have bound tonight—a testimony ledger, separate from the general accounts, kept in the vault. What the beasts tell you becomes evidence. Evidence becomes leverage. Leverage becomes protection, because the day the guild can hand the magistrates proof of who really killed the Harrow Street moneylender—or who's been selling grain short to the workhouses, or which alderman took crown silver to look away from smugglers—that day, every powerful man in this city decides the Ember Guild is more useful intact than raided. And a guild that's useful intact is a guild the hunters cannot touch. Not its master." He met her eyes. "Not its ledger-keeper."

It was brilliant. It was horrifying. It was, Isandro thought with a lurch, exactly the kind of scheme she would have invented herself if she'd had the courage—which was precisely why she'd never let herself imagine it.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Corvel sighed. He rose, went to the window, and stood looking down into the yard, where the morning deliveries were coming in through the salt gate below.

"If you refuse," he said, "you keep hiding. You get very good at it, quieter than you already are, and you last maybe a year. Maybe three. The hunt doesn't stop, Brightwater—it widens. First it's speakers. Then it's understanders. Then it's anyone the neighbors ever saw talking kindly to a dog." He turned back. "Or you take my offer, and you stop hiding. Which is its own kind of danger—I won't lie to you. The moment you start working openly, even secretly-openly, you exist. You become a name in a book somewhere besides mine." He came back to the desk and sat, and for a moment all the merchant's polish fell away and he just looked tired, and old, and like a man weighing something on scales only he could see. "But existing is what living things do. Hiding is what prey does. I've watched you be prey for six years, and it has made you careful and lonely and very, very good at sums, and I think you deserve better than that. I think the city deserves better than that, frankly. There are wrongs in these ledgers that only you can right."

Silence. The fire ticked. Down in the yard, a carter swore cheerfully at a stubborn pony, and Isandro heard the pony's flat, patient reply—*he kicks with the left, not the right, the great fool, how many years*—and had to close her eyes against the sudden sting of it. All those years of hearing and saying nothing. All those witnesses in the stalls and paddocks, carrying their knowledge around like water they couldn't pour.

"What would it cost me," she asked quietly, "to say yes?"

Corvel studied her. "Exposure," he said honestly. "That's the price. Safety purchased with visibility. There's no way around it—if you take testimony, you use the gift, and using it means someone, someday, sees. Maybe a handler. Maybe worse. I can buy you time and cover and a legal fortress made of paper, but I cannot buy you back your invisibility. That's the coin, Brightwater. That's the whole coin." He slid a fresh sheet of paper across the desk, and his pen, and turned the inkwell toward her. "So. Employment terms, amended. Same wage, plus a tithe on recovered debts. One condition of mine: you never testify on a beast's word without entering it true, even when it's ugly, even when it costs us a client. The guild trades in truth; that's the entire reason it works. Agreed?"

Isandro looked at the pen. At the blank page. At the man who had known her for six years and kept her secret in a strongbox of silence, and who was now asking her to spend that silence like capital.

She thought of the cooper's daughter screaming like a gull. She thought of the mare in the ice. She thought of a lark in a cage downstairs, sold to the guild last month in settlement of a hatmaker's debt, which sang to her every morning in a language of light and hunger, and which she had been carefully, cowardly not listening to.

"Yes," she said, and picked up the pen.

Corvel exhaled—she realized he'd been holding it—and for one unguarded moment he looked enormously relieved, and then the merchant armor came back up and he was all briskness, pulling ledgers toward him, naming dates and seals. "We begin tomorrow. Tonight I'll have the testimony book bound—green leather, so it never gets shelved with the others by mistake. And Brightwater—" he paused, pen hovering, not looking at her, "—the first entry should be something simple. Close to home. Build your nerve before you build your enemies."

"There's a mule," Isandro heard herself say. "Old Bayard. Pledged against a carter's debt—Tomas Wick, river road contract. He missed his payment last month and nobody's seen him since. His sister's been coming round asking after him." Her heart was beating strangely. "Bayard was with him."

Corvel looked up sharply. "You think something happened to Wick."

"I think," said Isandro, "that somebody owes somebody an answer, and for once I can collect it."

For a long moment Corvel regarded her with an expression she had never seen on him before—something between pride and alarm, the face of a man watching a small investment turn into something with teeth.

"Well," he said finally. "Gods help the guilty, then. Tomorrow morning, the paddock. Wear your oldest cloak, and if anyone asks, you're conducting a routine valuation of the collateral." He gathered his cups, then stopped at the door, his back to her. "Brightwater. One more thing. The hunters filed their warrant with the harbor office this morning. Standard procedure—they'll tour the counting-houses within the fortnight." He glanced back over his shoulder, and his voice was dry as ledger-paper. "So whatever you learn from that mule, I'd suggest you learn it quickly. Before the men who might be in its testimony come knocking on ours."

* * *

That night Isandro could not sleep. Near midnight she gave up, took a lamp, and went down through the cold corridors to the vault, where the ledgers slept in their oiled shelves like roosting birds.

She hadn't meant to open the general ledger. But her hand found page forty-one on its own—the third entry down, the miller's ghost-debt—and beneath it, in the margin, in a cramped hand she had never noticed in six years of faithful auditing, someone had written:

*The keeper hears what the goods say. Handle accordingly. — C.*

Dated the week she was hired.

Isandro stood in the lamplight with her heart hammering, reading it again and again. *Handle accordingly.* Six years of *handle accordingly*. Every kindness she'd assumed was indifference. Every time he'd sent her to the stables instead of the docks, cleared her schedule on market days, looked away—

"He knew," she whispered to the empty vault, and the vault, being full of paper, said nothing, but somewhere above her, in the dark rafters, the caged lark stirred and sang one soft note into the blackness—*yes, yes, little sister, yes*—like a bell confirming a bargain already struck.

Tomorrow, the mule. Isandro closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and climbed back toward the light with the green leather book waiting to be born, and the first true entry already forming in her mind like frost on glass:

*Tomas Wick, carter. Where are you, and who was with you?*

She did not yet know that the answer would wear a hunter's badge.
