## 4. What the Mule Remembered

The mule's name was Duchess, and she was the worst witness in the history of the Ember Guild.

"Again," Isandro said, keeping her voice low and her lips nearly still—the trick of it was to speak without appearing to speak, a skill she'd perfected over years of pretending to mutter over ledgers. "From the beginning. Slowly."

*Slowly,* Duchess agreed, with the air of one granting an enormous favor. *The cart was heavy. The man smelled of onions. I walked.*

"You said that already. What happened *after* you walked?"

*I stopped walking.*

Isandro pressed her fingertips to her temples and breathed through her nose. Around her, the guild's stable yard went about its morning: Ostrec the under-clerk hauling a barrel of pickled eels toward the counting room, two apprentices arguing over whether a bottled thunderhead counted as perishable inventory, the stable cat—whom Isandro had long ago learned not to talk to, because cats were gossips of the first water—watching from atop the fence with yellow-eyed judgment.

To everyone else, she was simply the ledger-keeper standing in front of a mule with her eyes closed, which was odd but not alarming. Master Corvel's people were all odd in some way or another. Oddness was practically a job requirement.

*You're frowning at me,* Duchess observed.

"I'm not frowning at you. I'm thinking."

*It looks like frowning. The carter frowned like that before he died.*

That landed in Isandro's chest like a dropped coin. "Tell me about that," she said gently. "The night he died. Take your time."

Duchess took her time. Duchess took so much time that Isandro had time to notice things: the way the mule's ears swiveled toward the street whenever a cart rattled past; the raw place on her flank where a harness had rubbed; the deep patience of an animal who had been worked hard and spoken to little, and who had therefore stored up every word she'd ever heard, the way a miser stores coins.

*He loaded me wrong,* she said at last. *Too much on the left side. He always did that. He said I leaned. I don't lean. The road leans.*

"The road leans," Isandro agreed solemnly, writing nothing down, because you could not write down what a mule said—not in any ledger anyone else could read—and because half of testimony was making the witness feel heard.

*We took the river road. It was dark. There was a man on the road ahead, standing where no man should stand, and the carter said a bad word and reached under the seat for his knife, only he'd sold his knife in the autumn to pay his mother's doctor, so there was just his hand, closing on nothing.* A pause. *I remember the sound of his hand closing on nothing. It was very loud.*

Isandro's pen hovered over her private notebook—the small one, the one she kept locked in the third drawer with a false bottom, the one that would get them both killed if anyone found it. "What did the man look like?"

*Tall. Boots that clicked when he moved. He smelled of metal and oil and something bitter, like the inside of a lock. And he wore a badge.*

"A badge? What kind of badge?"

*A sun with a spear through it. Shiny. New. He held it up like it was supposed to mean something to us. The carter laughed at him. Then the man hit him with a short black stick, twice, and the carter didn't laugh anymore, and then the man went through the cart and took the strongbox and left me standing there with my master dead in the road, and nobody came until morning, and by then the crows had opinions.*

Isandro sat down slowly on an overturned feed bucket.

A sun with a spear through it. She knew that device. Everyone in Vessany's capital knew it, though most pretended they didn't: the mark of the Crown's Office of Beasts and Aberrations. The beast-hunters. They were supposed to be a rumor out of the provinces, a story told to frighten gift-bearers into silence. They were not supposed to be standing on river roads in the dark, robbing and murdering carters like common bandits while wearing the crown's own shine.

And they were certainly not supposed to be doing it three streets from the Ember Guild.

"Duchess," Isandro said carefully, "did the man say anything? Words?"

*He said,* and here Duchess's voice changed, flattening into a cold imitation, *'In the name of the Office, surrender any beasts in your charge for inspection.' And the carter said, 'She's a mule, you peacock, inspect away,' and the man did not laugh, and then came the stick.*

A mule. Surrender any beasts. Isandro looked at Duchess—at the long gray face, the patient brown eyes, the ears that heard everything—and felt the floor of her ordinary life tilt beneath her.

Because the Office of Beasts and Aberrations did not care about mules. Not really. Not as mules.

They cared about what mules might have *heard.*

* *

Master Corvel received the news in his office above the counting room, a room famous throughout the guild for containing exactly one good chair (his), one indifferent fire, and a taxidermied pike wearing a tiny velvet hat, whose purpose no one had ever dared ask about.

"A hunter," Corvel said. He was a narrow man, dry as August paper, with spectacles he polished when he was thinking and removed when he was frightened. At present he was polishing them with great intensity. "On the river road. Killing a licensed carter carrying guild freight."

"So it would appear, sir."

"And the mule saw it."

"Mules see everything, sir. That's rather their profession."

Corvel set down his spectacles. "You spoke to it."

It was not a question, and Isandro's stomach performed a slow, deliberate somersault. Three years she had worked for this man. Three years of careful muttering, of talking to animals only in empty rooms, of being the quiet ledger-keeper whom nobody noticed because noticing her might lead to noticing *more*. She had built her invisibility brick by brick, and now—

"I listened, sir," she said. "The mule talked. There's a difference. Legally speaking."

Something flickered across Corvel's face—too fast to name, gone before she could read it. He turned to the window, hands clasped behind his back, looking down at the yard where Duchess stood dozing in the sun.

"Do you know what I traded for last spring, Brightwater?" he said. "A debt owed by a storm. An actual storm—a squall off the Graywater coast that sank a fishing fleet and then, through some failure of its nature, failed to sink one particular boat. The boat's captain swore a debt to the sea, and the sea, being unable to collect, sold the account to us. We hold it still." He tapped the windowsill. "Impossible things, Brightwater. That is our trade. And yet somehow a clerk who converses with mules surprises me more than a storm with a conscience."

"Sir—"

"Hold." He raised one dry finger. "I am not dismissing you. I am not reporting you. I am telling you that as of this morning, your talent is no longer a secret from me, and that you should think very carefully about why I have chosen to keep it that way." He picked up a bell from his desk and rang it once. "Send in Ostrec," he called through the door, and then, to Isandro: "The murdered carter was hauling guild freight. Which means the guild has a claim against his killer. Which means—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile touched his mouth— "the guild is owed a murder, Brightwater. And we collect our debts."

Ostrec arrived, breathless and smelling faintly of pickled brine. "Sir?"

"Fetch the evidence box from the river road. The constables released it this morning—guild seal, guild claim. Everything the killer left behind. Bring it to the stable yard."

"The stable yard, sir?"

"Where else?" said Corvel. "Our witness is stabled there."

* *

The evidence box contained: one broken lantern. One torn sleeve of good wool, gray. One boot heel, wrenched off in the mud. And one badge.

Isandro lifted it out with both hands, though it weighed almost nothing. Sun with a spear through it. Stamped silver, new enough to still have burrs on the edge. She turned it over. On the back, in tiny official engraving:

**OFFICE OF BEASTS AND ABERRATIONS — WARDEN 3RD CLASS — L. FERRANT**

"He left it behind," she murmured. "Why would he leave his own badge?"

*Because it isn't his,* said Duchess, who had craned her neck around to inspect the box with professional interest. *His badge is older. Scratched. He keeps it wrapped in cloth in his left boot because it bites his skin. This one is borrowed. Or stolen. Or given.*

Isandro stared at her. "How do you know what's in his boot?"

*I carried him,* Duchess said simply. *Last month. Twice. From the Office house on Candlemaker's Row to the river gate and back. He rides badly and smells of the bitter lock-oil. Wardens don't ride guild stock, ledger-woman. Someone paid us. Someone inside the guild hired a mule to carry a beast-hunter around the city, and I remembered, because remembering is what I'm for.*

The yard seemed to go very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, a fishwife was shouting about herring prices; somewhere above, the apprentices had begun their noon argument about the thunderhead again. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that did not yet know what was walking through it.

"Candlemaker's Row," Isandro repeated. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "That's four streets from here."

*Five,* said Duchess. *But the roads lean.*

She looked down at the badge in her hands—at the bright, new, official sun with its spear—and understood, with the particular clarity that comes just before everything changes, what she was holding. Not evidence. Not merely evidence. A thread. Pull it, and the whole hunt came unraveling into the light: wardens riding guild mules, hunting something—or someone—in the capital itself, months before any proclamation, any herald, any public fear. They weren't coming.

They were already here.

And they had ridden a mule who remembered everything, past a ledger-keeper who could hear her, and neither of them had known.

"Master Corvel will want this," she said aloud, and then paused, because Duchess was giving her a look—the flat, ancient, unimpressed look that mules have perfected over five thousand years of carrying fools.

*Will he?* said Duchess. *Or will he want you?*

Isandro opened her mouth to say something reassuring and found she had nothing. Because the truth was that she had asked herself the same question since the office upstairs—that flicker on Corvel's face, too fast to read; the careful way he'd said *your talent is no longer a secret from me*; the sudden, uncharacteristic urgency of a murder investigation convened within the hour. Master Corvel collected impossible things. It was the family trade, three generations deep.

And this morning, quite by accident, she had become one.

*Duchess,* she said silently, *if anyone asks—you kicked me. You were startled by a chicken. You're a mule; no one will question it.*

*I would never kick you,* Duchess replied with dignity. *But I'll say I did. That's what witnesses are for.*

Isandro closed the evidence box, tucked the badge into her apron pocket where it lay against her hip like a small cold sun, and started for the stairs to Corvel's office.

She never got there. She made it as far as the counting room door when Ostrec came pelting down from the street, hatless, white-faced, brine dripping forgotten from his sleeves.

"Brightwater—" He caught the doorframe to steady himself. "There are men at the Salt Gate. In silver. Reading a proclamation. They've got a list of names, and they're taking people—" His voice cracked. "They took Mistress Anselm. The candle-dipper. Right off her own step. She didn't even finish sweeping."

Behind him, through the open door, Isandro could hear it beginning: bells, boots, the rising murmur of a city learning to be afraid.

And in her pocket, cold against her hip, the badge of Warden Ferrant, 3rd Class, who had ridden a guild mule past this very door—and who, she now understood, had not been hunting the city at all.

He'd been scouting it.

For her.
