## 5. The Debt of Wren Halloway

Wren Halloway had been in the Ember Guild's counting room exactly once before, three years ago, and she had sworn on her mother's grave—and her mother was not dead, which made the oath doubly binding—that she would never stand in it again.

Yet here she was, standing in it, watching dust motes perform their slow ballet in the light from the high windows while Master Corvel read her file with the expression of a man sampling a wine he suspected of being vinegar.

"Three years," Corvel said.

"Three years, one month, and eleven days," Wren said. "But who's counting."

"I am." He turned a page. "That is rather the point of this room."

The counting room of the Ember Guild did not count money. Money was the least of what they counted. Along the walls stood shelves of ledgers bound in leather the color of old bruises, and each one held debts that no honest court would recognize and no honest bank would touch. Wren knew, because she'd spent a profitable decade making sure she was never on the wrong side of one, and then, in a single spectacular night involving a stolen reliquary, a flooded canal, and a man she was still too angry at to name, had ended up on the very worst side of the very largest.

Her debt was listed in a ledger with a clasp of black iron. She'd seen it once. It said, in the tidy hand of some clerk who had never met her: *Wren Halloway owes the Guild one miracle, rendered.*

Nobody had ever explained to her satisfaction how a person was supposed to render a miracle on schedule. She'd asked. Corvel had smiled the way a lock smiles at a key it hasn't decided to accept yet.

"You'll be pleased to know," Corvel said, closing the file, "that an opportunity has arisen."

"I don't like the sound of that."

"You rarely like the sound of anything. It's one of your more consistent qualities." He laced his fingers together on the desk. "I need someone escorted. Someone important. Someone the Guild cannot afford to lose."

"And you want me to do it because—"

"Because you are the only person in my employ who is simultaneously expendable and competent. It's a rare combination. Most people are one or the other, and the ones who are both tend to be dead already."

"Flattery," Wren said, "from a man who owns my soul's paperwork."

"Your soul's paperwork is in excellent order, which is more than I can say for most." Corvel rose, and that was when Wren noticed the second person in the room, tucked into the corner behind a screen of shelving, so quiet she'd taken them for furniture.

A young woman. Dark hair pinned back with the severity of someone who considered beauty a clerical error. Ink on her fingers, ink on her cuff, ink—Wren would swear to it later—on her very expression. She was holding a ledger against her chest like a shield.

"This is Isandro Brightwater," Corvel said. "She is the someone important. You will take her where she needs to go, when she needs to go there, and you will ask no questions."

"I always ask questions. It's how I find out where things need to go."

"Then ask them of her, not of me." Corvel gathered his coat. "I have an appointment with the harbormaster. Isandro—the Salt Gate accounts, if you please. And Wren—" he paused at the door, and for one moment the genial mask slipped and something older and wearier looked out, "—keep her safe. That is not a request. It is a line item, and you are in arrears."

The door shut behind him.

Wren turned to look at her charge properly.

Isandro Brightwater looked back at her with the flat, patient stare of a cat being introduced to a dog it has already decided to despise.

"Well," Wren said. "Aren't you a bundle of laughs."

"I could say the same," Isandro said, "if you'd said anything worth laughing at."

Oh, Wren thought. *Oh, this is going to be a long escort.*

* * *

They spent the afternoon circling each other in the guildhouse like two storms sharing a sky and refusing to touch.

Wren learned the geography of her by observation, which was her preferred method of learning anything: Isandro worked in the scriptorium off the counting room, a narrow chamber smelling of lamp oil and salt—salt, of all things, in little dishes on every shelf, which Wren decided was either superstition or the world's most boring snack. The girl audited accounts with a speed that made Wren's eyes water. Bottled storms, graded by ferocity. Debts owed by ghosts, with interest accruing posthumously. A jar labeled *LAST SALT OF THE DROWNED KINGDOM* that Isandro handled with both hands and a bowed head, like a relic, which Wren supposed it was.

"You're staring," Isandro said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing. It's my job. If I'm to keep you alive, I need to know what you are."

"I'm a clerk."

"You're a clerk the way I'm a seamstress. Technically true, deeply misleading." Wren dropped into the chair opposite, tipping it onto its back legs. "Corvel doesn't assign escorts to clerks. Corvel doesn't assign escorts at all. Corvel lets people drown and writes it down as depreciation. So what are you?"

Isandro set down her pen with great care, the way one sets down a thing one is tempted to throw.

"I'm a clerk," she said, "who is going to finish this page. You may assess the page too, if it helps."

Wren looked at the page. It was an inventory of moth-wing lanterns, each one annotated with what appeared to be the *mood* of the moth. One entry read: *No. 44 — sullen. Recommend not lighting until it apologizes.*

"You're making that up," Wren said.

"I'm transcribing it. There's a difference. The moths make it up; I just write it down honestly." Isandro's mouth twitched, almost against her will, and for half a heartbeat the severity cracked and something young and tired showed through. Then the shutters came down again, neat as a shopfront at closing.

And Wren, who had spent her whole life reading the space between what people said and what they meant, felt the ground shift under her.

Because she knew that shutter. She'd built the same one. It was the look of a person who had learned, early and expensively, that the thing that made them strange was the thing that made them hunted, and that the only safe place to keep a self was behind a counter, behind a ledger, behind a face like a locked door.

*You too,* she thought, and hated her for it, and hated herself more for the thinking.

"I need air," she announced, standing so fast the chair legs cracked against the floor.

"The door is that way," Isandro said. "It has always been that way. You may find that reassuring."

* * *

Wren found the guild's yard, and the guild's mule, and the mule turned out to be better company than either her charge or her conscience.

She was sharing a heel of bread with it—technically the mule's bread, but the mule had a gambling problem and had lost it fair and square in a staring contest—when Isandro came out into the yard with a lantern and a look on her face that Wren's assessment skills immediately filed under *trouble.*

"You're talking to the mule," Isandro said.

"I'm negotiating with the mule. There's a difference." Wren offered the last of the bread. "He's agreed to lower his rates."

"The mule doesn't have rates."

"Everyone has rates. His just happen to be paid in bread and flattery. Mostly flattery. He's a vain animal. Look at him."

The mule, who was in fact preening, looked insufferable about it. And Isandro laughed—a short, startled sound, like a bird startled out of a hedge—and then looked appalled at herself for laughing, and Wren felt that same ground shift again, worse this time, because the laugh had been *warm*, and warmth was the one currency Wren had never learned to carry without dropping it.

"You should eat," Isandro said, recovering. "There's a kitchen. Corvel's cook makes a stew that has been described as *edible adjacent*."

"Did you just make a joke?"

"No."

"You did. That was a joke. The clerk makes jokes."

"The clerk is going inside." Isandro paused at the door, lantern held high, and for a moment the light caught her face and Wren saw the exhaustion underneath the severity, the fear underneath the exhaustion, the whole buried architecture of a person carrying something enormous and pretending it was a satchel. "Wren. Why did you take this job? Truly. You could have refused."

Wren could have lied. Lying was her native tongue; she spoke it in her sleep.

"Corvel holds my paper," she said instead. "He holds it, and I hold a debt I can't pay, and when a man holds both ends of the rope, you do what he asks until you find a knife. That's all this is. Rope management."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment.

"That's the most honest thing anyone in this building has said to me in four years," she said, and went inside.

Wren stood in the yard with the mule and the cold and the strange, unwelcome feeling of having been *seen*, and decided she hated it, and decided she hated her, and fed the mule the rest of the bread anyway because flattery was cheaper than thinking.

* * *

The trouble announced itself two days later, as trouble in Vessany always did: with paperwork.

Wren was in the yard again—she was spending a great deal of time in the yard; the yard asked nothing of her—when the bell above the guildhouse door rang wrong. Not the customer's ring, the bright double note. This was a single flat toll, and every person in the yard stopped what they were doing at once, the way birds stop before a storm.

Isandro appeared at the scriptorium window, and her face had gone the color of the ledger she'd been holding.

"What is it?" Wren was moving before she'd decided to, which she noted with professional annoyance.

"A proclamation." Isandro's voice was level in the way that ice is level. "At the Salt Gate. They're reading it in every square. The crown is offering a bounty." She swallowed. "For beast-speakers."

The word dropped into the yard and lay there.

"Beast-speakers," Wren repeated.

"People who talk to animals. It's a new crime. It's—" Isandro's hand was pressed flat against the window frame, and Wren watched the knuckles whiten, watched the girl perform calm the way a drowning man performs swimming, and every piece Wren had been quietly assembling for two days—the severity, the shutters, the salt dishes, the way she'd looked at the mule not with amusement but with *recognition*—clicked together at once.

Wren did not say it. Saying it would be a death sentence, here, in a yard full of ears. But she looked at Isandro Brightwater, clerk, and Isandro looked back at her, and for one naked moment neither of them pretended, and the space between them filled with everything unsaid.

*You too,* the look said.

*Me too,* Wren's look answered. *God help us both.*

"Right," Wren said briskly, loudly, for the benefit of the yard. "Well. If the crown's paying bounties on people who chat with animals, I'm in no danger. The only thing I talk to is this mule, and he's never once answered."

The mule, with impeccable comic timing, brayed.

The yard laughed. Isandro didn't. She came down into the yard, and she took Wren's sleeve, and her voice dropped to a register meant for one person only.

"There's a page in one of Corvel's ledgers," she said. "A census. It names me. It names what I am. If the hunters get into this building—"

"Hunters." Wren's mouth had gone dry. "Plural. They're sending hunters. To Vessany."

"To everywhere." Isandro's grip tightened. "Wren. I need that page to not exist."

And here was the thing, the thing Wren would turn over in her mind for years afterward, on cold roads, in worse company: she could have said no. She could have named her price, negotiated, done what she always did, turned everything into a ledger entry with her on the winning side.

Instead she heard herself say: "Corvel's study. Third shelf. The census ledger has a green spine and a bad temper. I'll get the page. You get us out of the city, because after I steal from the Ember Guild, *neither of us* is going to be popular."

"You'd do that?" Isandro said. "You'd burn your own—"

"I'd burn the whole shelf." Wren was already walking, coat on, knives checked, heart doing something complicated she refused to examine. "But let's start with the page."

Behind them, in the yard, the mule watched them go with an expression of profound equine satisfaction, as if he had arranged the whole thing and was waiting for his bread.

* * *

The page burned well. Green-spined ledgers, it turned out, were full of the driest, most flammable paper in the world, which Wren chose to interpret as the universe's one small apology.

They stood at the scriptorium hearth together, watching the census entry curl and blacken—*Brightwater, Isandro. Gift confirmed. Disposition: retain*—until it was ash, and Isandro's shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time in two days.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the mule. He gave me the idea." Wren dusted off her hands. "Now. About getting you out of the city. I know a smuggler's gate under the Salt Gate itself, and I know a road south that the crown's maps don't bother with. But I want terms."

"Terms."

"I'm a professional. I don't escort clerks out of burning countries for free." Wren folded her arms. "When this is done, Corvel marks my debt *paid*. In writing. In the black ledger. You witness it. That's my price."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment, and Wren braced for the haggling, the suspicion, the counteroffer.

"That's it?" Isandro said. "Your freedom? That's what you'd ask?"

"That's *everything*," Wren said, and heard, too late, how it sounded, how it was true in a way she hadn't meant to say out loud. "That's—look, it's a fair rate. Ask anyone."

"Anyone," Isandro said softly, "or the mule?"

And despite everything—despite the proclamation, the hunters, the ash still warm on the hearth—Wren Halloway laughed, a real one, startled out of her like a bird from a hedge.

Which was, of course, exactly when the knocking started at the guildhouse front door. Heavy. Official. The knock of people who did not need to be let in, because they had papers that opened everything.

Isandro went white. "They're here," she whispered. "They're already here."

Wren's hand found her knife. "Back door," she said. "Now. And clerk—whatever you're hiding, whatever it is they want—"

"I know," Isandro said. "Run first. Explain never."

"See? You *do* make jokes."

They ran.
