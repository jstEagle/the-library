## 6. Ash on the Wind

The trouble with a life built on quiet competence, Isandro thought, was that it taught you nothing at all about how to be loud in an emergency.

She had been up since the fourth bell, which was not unusual. The Ember Guild's ledgers did not balance themselves, and Master Corvel held the firm conviction—stated often enough that it had hardened into scripture—that any account left unattended overnight grew interest of a supernatural variety. "Debts are like cats," he liked to say. "Turn your back and they multiply." So she sat in the counting room with her sleeves pushed up and her ink-stained fingers flying, working through the quarterly reconciliation while dawn came grey and thin through the shutters.

That was when the lark started screaming.

Not singing—screaming, in the high bright language only she could hear, the one she had spent thirty years pretending not to understand. *Fire, fire, iron and fire, they're coming up the Salt Road, two carts, six horses, dogs, DOGS—*

Isandro's pen stopped mid-stroke, leaving a blot like a small dark wound on the page.

The lark belonged to Mistress Hetta three doors down, who kept it for its voice and let it out at dawn to sit in the guild's gutter and insult the pigeons. It had never once screamed. Birds did not scream about weather. Birds screamed about hawks.

*How many dogs?*

*Four,* said the lark, and then, with the terrible precision of small creatures, *They smell you.*

She was out of her chair before the thought finished forming. Down the stairs, taking them two at a time, past the sleeping apprentices' alcove where Tam and Pell snored in innocent tandem, through the hall with its shelves of bottled storms glinting like dirty jewels.

Corvel was already awake. He was always awake; she suspected he dissolved at night into ledger-dust and reassembled at dawn. He stood at the front window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width, and when he turned to look at her his face wore an expression she had never seen on it before.

He looked old.

"Isandro," he said quietly. "Go down to the salt cellar. Now. Take nothing."

"Master Corvel—"

"*Now*, girl." And then, because he was Corvel, and even terror could not entirely strip the accountant out of him: "Take one thing."

She heard the knock at the door a heartbeat later. Not a knock, really—a pounding, official and rhythmic, the sound of paperwork made flesh. Through the door came a voice, flat as a ruled line:

"By order of the Crown Assize on Ungodly Beasts. Open in the name of the hunt."

* * *

The salt cellar ran beneath the whole guildhouse, dug into the old foundations a hundred years before anyone now living had been born. It was cold and dim and smelled of the sea's ghost, and the sacks stood in ranks like patient soldiers, each one stamped with the guild's mark: a circle with a flame inside it. Isandro crouched behind the third rank, heart slamming, and listened to her home die above her.

Boots. Many boots. Corvel's voice, pitched in its driest register—the one he used on creditors and tax men. "Sirs, I must insist you present a writ. The Ember Guild is chartered under—"

"The charter is revoked," said the flat voice. "Sealed yesterday. Your papers are forfeit. Your person is forfeit. Where is your ledger-keeper?"

Ah, Isandro thought, with a strange floating clarity. There it was.

"My ledger-keeper," Corvel repeated, in the tone of a man handed an arithmetic error by an inferior. "I keep the ledgers. That has been the arrangement for forty years. You may verify—"

"You have a clerk," the voice said. "Young. Female. Seen at the Salt Gate market talking to livestock. Seen again at the fish stalls. Seen—" a pause, the sound of paper unfolding, and Isandro realized with a lurch that somewhere in this city there was an actual document, with actual observations written on it in an actual hand, describing *her*. "Seen standing very still in a field while a cart horse grew calm. We have been watching for some months, Master Corvel."

"I'm sure I don't know what you imagine a girl does with a horse—"

"Seal the back rooms. Bring the books. All of them."

Then the noise began in earnest, and Isandro pressed her hands over her ears anyway, though it did no good, because the worst of it wasn't loud. It was the sound of drawers being emptied. Of shelf after shelf being swept. She knew exactly what those hands were finding: the storm in the blue bottle from 3rd of Harvestmonth; the debt owed by the ghost of Alderman Vetch, seven years outstanding; the phial of last salt from the drowned kingdom, priced beyond crowns and therefore priceless. Forty years of Corvel's careful impossibilities, being crated up like turnips.

And somewhere overhead, Corvel's voice kept going—dry, pedantic, quoting charter clauses to men who had already burned the charter—and Isandro understood, with a pain that felt like a pulled tooth, that he was doing it on purpose. Every clause he cited was a minute bought. He was spending himself at the rate of one regulation per breath.

*I am sorry,* she whispered into the dark, to a man who could not hear her, in the language of animals because it was the only honest tongue she had. *I hear you. I will remember this.*

The trapdoor to the cellar opened.

Isandro nearly stopped breathing. Then she saw it was Tam—the youngest apprentice, fifteen, freckled, terrified—with Pell's white face behind him, and a sack of bread rolls clutched to his chest like a baby.

"They didn't see us come down," Tam breathed. "We were in the coal chute. Isandro, they took Master Corvel, they put him in the cart, he didn't even—he just straightened his cuffs—"

"He would." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Listen to me. Both of you. Go out through the coal chute and separate. Don't go home—go anywhere but home. Tam, your aunt in Tanner's Row. Pell, the widow Osgood owes us favors, tell her I said so. In a week, maybe two, if it's safe, someone will find you. If nobody comes—" She swallowed. "If nobody comes, start new lives. That's an order from management."

"But what about you?" Pell whispered.

Isandro looked around the cellar—at the salt, at the cold stone, at the little hatch in the corner that opened onto the drainage run and the canal beyond, which she had cleaned out herself every spring and had never once, until this moment, been glad of.

"I have one book to take care of," she said.

* * *

Here is the thing about the ledger of Vessany, which she retrieved from its hiding place inside the false bottom of the saltpeter chest, wrapped in oilcloth, with hands that shook only a little:

It was not valuable. Not in the way the guild measured value. It was a plain book, water-swollen at one corner, its leather scuffed, its pages filled in a hand that was elegant and increasingly desperate as it went on. It was the account of a country that had been sold, and drowned, and struck from every map in the world, and it recorded—in sums, in tithes, in columns of figures that broke her heart precisely because they were so orderly—everything Vessany had ever paid out, and everything it had never gotten back.

The final entry was short. She had read it perhaps two hundred times in the years since Corvel had shown it to her, once, drunk on plum wine, and then never spoken of it again.

> **Item: the door out. One (1). Condition: sealed. Payable only in person, in salt, before winter's end. No proxies. No credit extended.**

She had always assumed it was a curiosity. A piece of dead-country poetry, filed between a bottled fog and a mermaid's IOU.

Crouched in the drainage run with canal water soaking through her skirts and the guildhouse burning—not burning, she corrected herself fiercely, *being inventoried*, which was somehow worse—she no longer assumed anything. The hunters had taken every book. They had taken the storms and the salts and the debts of ghosts. But they had not taken this, because Corvel had hidden it so well that even she had needed three years to find it, and if a man hid one book above all others—

If a man hid one book above all others, it was because it mattered more than all the rest together.

She put it inside her shirt, against her heart, and crawled toward the canal.

* * *

The city, when she came up out of it, was doing what cities do: pretending. Market stalls opening. Bread baking. A woman arguing about the price of eels with the fervor of a woman defending her homeland. Nobody looked at a soaked, mud-hemmed clerk limping along the canal path, and Isandro discovered that thirty years of being invisible had prepared her for exactly this, and that the discovery tasted like ash.

She got as far as the Fishmonger's Stair before her legs gave out and she sat down on the wet steps among the gulls, and it was there that grief finally caught her.

It ambushed her sideways, the way grief does. Not at the memory of the boots and the broken seal on the door. At a smell. Someone nearby was frying onions, and the guild kitchen had fried onions every single morning of her twelve years there, and Corvel had complained about it every single morning—"the entire establishment smells like a tavern's regret"—and had eaten three helpings every time, and now he was in a cart in irons, having straightened his cuffs, and she had not thanked him. Not once. Not for the onions, not for the ledgers, not for whatever strange, crooked, unspoken thing it was that he felt for her, which she had spent twelve years refusing to examine because examining things was her gift and she had never once applied it to her own life.

A gull landed near her and eyed the oilcloth bundle hopefully.

"It's a book," she told it. "You'd hate it. It's mostly arithmetic."

*Ledges,* said the gull, which had a poor vocabulary even for a gull.

"Ledgers. Yes." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, businesslike, the way Corvel would have. "Ledgers full of impossible things. And one impossible thing that might possibly be a door."

The gull tilted its head. From the harbor below came the ordinary sounds of the morning: water slapping pilings, rope creaking, someone swearing cheerfully at a crate.

And one sound that was not ordinary at all. A woman's voice, low and furious, coming from under the stair itself—which, Isandro now noticed, had a gap beneath it, curtained by old fishing net, currently occupied by a pair of muddy boots, a coil of rope, and a smuggler.

Isandro knew her by sight the way everyone on the docks knew her by sight: Wren Halloway, the best runner on the coast until the incident, the details of which changed with every telling except for its two fixed points—that it had involved the Ember Guild, and that Wren had come out of it owing a debt the guild's own ledgers described, in Corvel's neat hand, as *unquantifiable; see attached correspondence; do NOT extend further credit.* She was compact and sharp-faced and currently pressing one finger to her lips with an expression suggesting that if Isandro made a sound, she would be sorry in ways not yet invented.

Isandro considered her options. The hunters wanted her. The city was crawling with them by now. She had no money, no home, one waterlogged book, and a set of skills that were suddenly worth a cell or a gallows.

She also had, she realized, looking at the woman under the stair, something better than money.

"You owe the guild," Isandro said.

Wren's eyes narrowed to knives. "Keep your voice—"

"The guild owes *me*," Isandro said, louder, because fear had finally burned down far enough to leave anger, and anger was warm and useful. "Everything it has, it took with it this morning, except me. Which means every debt on those books is now collectible by the only clerk who walked out through the cellar." She held up the oilcloth bundle. "Including yours."

There was a silence. Somewhere above, the onion-frying continued, oblivious. The gull, sensing drama, settled in like theatergoer.

"That's blackmail," Wren said at last, with something that might have been admiration fighting its way up through the fury.

"That's accounting," said Isandro. "I need to leave the city today. You know every road, every gate, every hole in every wall that isn't watched. Get me out, and we'll call the ledger square."

"And if I say no?"

Isandro thought about Corvel straightening his cuffs. She thought about the lark, still screaming somewhere in her memory. She found, to her mild surprise, that her voice did not shake at all.

"Then I'll stand on this stair and describe, at volume, to everyone who passes, exactly how a certain smuggling run went wrong, and what exactly was in the crates, and why exactly a certain someone couldn't deliver it. It's all in the attached correspondence. I've read it. Twice. It's very educational."

For a long moment Wren Halloway stared at her from under the stair with the expression of a woman recalculating a map and disliking every route on it. Then she swore, fluently and at length, in at least three languages and one dialect that appeared to consist entirely of nautical profanity, and jerked her head at the space beside her.

"Get down here before someone sees you, you terrifying little inkpot," she muttered. "There's a coal barge leaves on the morning tide. And for the record—" her jaw tightened, and for half a second something moved behind her eyes, something raw and familiar, the look of a person carrying a wound in the same place Isandro carried hers— "for the record, I'm not doing this for your guild. Your guild can drown."

"It already did," Isandro said quietly, and crawled in under the stair beside her.

Above them the city went on pretending. Below the city, in a cell, a dry old man was presumably quoting charter clauses at his jailers. And in the oilcloth bundle against Isandro's heart, in a dead hand on drowned paper, an entry waited with the patience of all unpaid accounts:

*Payable only in person, in salt, before winter's end.*

Winter, Isandro thought, watching the first snow of the year drift down onto the harbor like ash.

Winter had already begun.
