## 7. Three Debts and a Door

Wren Halloway had stolen from kings, lied to priests, and once talked her way out of a hanging by convincing the magistrate that the rope was cursed. She was not, therefore, easily impressed.

But she was impressed by how long Isandro Brightwater could sit still.

Three days in the smuggler's loft above the tannery on Cordwainer's Row — three days of tallow-stink and the wet-sheep smell of hides curing below — and the ledger-keeper had barely moved from her corner by the one window that didn't face the street. She sat cross-legged with the Vessany book open across her knees like something holy, or something dangerous, which Wren supposed amounted to the same thing when you were talking about guild property.

"You're going to ruin your eyes," Wren said, for the fourth time that morning. "Then who'll read your precious numbers? I can't. Numbers swim off the page when I look at them. Deliberately, I think."

"They don't swim," Isandro said, without looking up. "You just don't hold them still."

"Same thing."

"It isn't." A pause. Her finger moved down a column. "Also, this is salt accounts, not numbers. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

Isandro looked up then, and Wren got the full effect of those grey eyes again — the ones that made her feel like a ledger entry herself, some old debt being quietly assessed. It was an uncomfortable sensation. Wren dealt with it the way she dealt with most discomfort: she talked.

"Here's what I don't understand," she said, dropping onto the crate opposite, which groaned in protest. She'd been told the crate held tanning knives. She chose to believe it. "Corvel gets arrested, the guildhouse gets cracked open like an egg, hunters are crawling through Vessany City sniffing for beast-speakers — and your plan is to *read*."

"My plan is to find out where we're going before we go there," Isandro said. "You'd be amazed how many people skip that step. Most of them end up in rivers."

"I've ended up in rivers. It builds character."

"You ended up in the Mirle because you were drunk and trying to race a barge."

"The barge started it."

Below them, someone began scraping a hide, a long rhythmic sound like a giant sharpening its nails. Isandro flinched — she flinched at everything now, doors, boots, bells — and went back to the page.

Wren watched her for a while. This was another thing she did instead of discomfort: watching. She was good at it. You had to be, in her line of work, which had been smuggling until two days ago and was now, apparently, *babysitting a fugitive accountant*, a career change she had not agreed to so much as failed to escape from.

The girl's hands were stained to the wrist with ink. There was a smudge of it on her cheekbone where she'd pushed her hair back. She read the way other people prayed — lips moving slightly, whole body leaned into the page as if the words might bolt if she gave them room.

And every hour or so, she would stop, close her eyes, and press two fingers against the cover of the book, like taking a pulse.

Wren had decided not to ask about that. Yet.

* * *

The trouble with hiding in a tannery, Wren reflected later that afternoon, was that it smelled like a tannery. The trouble with hiding with Isandro Brightwater was worse: the girl kept *talking to things*.

Not people. Things. The loft cat, mostly — a scarred orange tom with half an ear missing who had adopted them on the first night and whom Wren had named Turncoat, on principle. Wren had come up the ladder twice now to find Isandro murmuring to him, low and conversational, the way you'd talk to a nervous client. And both times, the cat had *answered*. Not meowing. Answering. A particular chirrup, a tilt of the head, a slow blink that landed exactly where a sentence should land.

It was the most unsettling thing Wren had seen since the ferryman with the backwards feet, and she had decided, firmly, not to think about it.

She was thinking about it constantly.

"You could ask, you know," Isandro said that evening, without looking up from the ledger. Turncoat was draped across her shoulders like a stole, and Wren would swear the animal was smirking.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever it is you keep almost asking."

"I don't *almost* ask things. I ask things outright. It's one of my flaws."

"Mm." Isandro turned a page. "Then ask why you keep staring at my hands."

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "Your hands are filthy with ink. Anyone would stare."

"Anyone would stare at the ink," Isandro agreed. "You stare at the way I touch the book."

The scraping below had stopped. The silence felt very large.

"He talks to me," Isandro said, scratching Turncoat under the chin. "The cat. He's been telling me which windows the watch uses, and which neighbor reports foot traffic, and that the man downstairs waters his beer. He thinks we're stupid. He's mostly right about you."

"He is *not*—"

"And before you say anything," she went on, calm as a column of figures, "no, I'm not going to do it to you. I don't listen in on people unless they're animals, and I don't use it against friends."

"We're not friends."

"No," Isandro said. "We're not. But you haven't sold me to the hunters yet, and it's been three days, and you sleep with a knife under your pillow and your boots on, which means you expect to run too. So whatever you are, you're running from the same thing I am. That's not friendship. It's arithmetic."

Wren stared at her. Then, despite everything — despite the hunters and the burned guildhouse and the fact that her own name was probably on a list somewhere between *smuggler* and *accessory* — she laughed. It came out rusty, like a hinge.

"Arithmetic," she said. "Gods help me. Corvel trained you well."

Something crossed Isandro's face at the name — quick, deep, gone. She bent back over the ledger.

That was when Wren saw it. The moment the lamplight shifted, the moment the girl's fingers stilled on the page and stopped moving entirely, and her breath went strange — caught, like a fish on a line.

"What," Wren said. "What is it."

Isandro didn't answer. She turned the book around and pushed it across the floorboards toward Wren, and her hand was shaking.

"Read it," she said. "Out loud. I need to hear it out loud. I need to know I'm not making it up."

* * *

The page was old. Older than the rest of the ledger — the paper thicker, softer, browned at the edges like a leaf kept too long. The ink had gone the color of dried blood. At the top, in a hand far more formal than the cramped accounting script filling the rest of the book, someone had written:

**ENTRY THE LAST. THE DOOR OUT OF VESSANY.**

Wren cleared her throat and read.

"*Item: one door, standing, location per annex. Purpose: egress entire. Conditions of service as follows.*"

She glanced up. Isandro nodded, eyes closed, listening.

"*Payable in person only. No proxy, no agent, no heir may tender payment. Payable in salt — the last salt of the kingdom, held in trust per prior entries. And payable before winter's end, after which the account closes and the door closes with it, and no further claims shall be honored, in this world or any other.*"

"*Note, in the clerk's own hand:* the door does not take coin. It does not take goods, nor years, nor blood, though it has been offered all three. It takes what the payer loves most, weighed at the threshold, and it is not cheated, and it does not give change.*"

"*Final note:* let whoever reads this understand that the door was never meant to save Vessany. It was meant to save what Vessany loved. We were always the salt. What mattered was always going to be carried through.*"

The loft was silent except for Turncoat purring, a sound like a small distant storm.

"Well," Wren said, because someone had to. "That's cheerful."

"Winter's end." Isandro opened her eyes. They were wet, but her voice was steady, and Wren thought, absurdly, *she's checking the figures even while she cries.* "When does winter end here?"

"When the river gods get bored. Some years March, some years May. Why?"

"Because if the account closes—" Isandro pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "If it closes, that's it. There's no second reading. Ledgers don't reopen, Wren. Not these ledgers. I've spent six years learning that. A closed account is a grave."

"So we go before winter's end." Wren heard herself say it and was annoyed at how reasonable it sounded. "Where's the annex? The location?"

Isandro flipped pages — forward, back, forward again — hunting. Wren watched her do it and felt the cold arrive slowly, the way it does in a room when someone leaves a door open somewhere you can't see.

"It's not here." Page. Page. "It's not— they wouldn't have. They wouldn't have left it out, it's the *last entry*, it's the whole point of the—" She stopped. Went still. Then, carefully, she lifted the ledger and held it up to the lamp, tilting it, and Wren saw it too: a rectangle on the inside back cover, faintly paler than the leather around it, the ghost-shape of a page pasted in and removed.

Someone had cut out the annex.

"Somebody took it," Isandro whispered. "Somebody cut out the only map to the door."

"Or somebody moved it," Wren said slowly, "to somewhere safer than a book that hunters might seize." She was already pulling her coat on. "Which clerk kept the Vessany accounts before the guild consolidated? Before Corvel?"

Isandro blinked at her. "Why?"

"Because books get copied, Brightwater. Before they get bound. Somebody made a fair copy once, and fair copies live in desks and attics and the pockets of dead men, and I have spent eleven years knowing where dead men keep their pockets." She jammed her knife into her belt. "So. Do you want to stand here grieving a missing page, or do you want to come steal it back?"

For the first time in three days, Isandro Brightwater smiled. It was small and crooked and it changed her whole face, and Wren looked away from it quickly, the way you look away from a fire you've decided not to warm your hands at.

* * *

They didn't go anywhere that night, because the city had teeth now. Watch patrols doubled on Cordwainer's Row; a herald's drum passed at midnight reciting names, and Wren lay awake counting the intervals between drums and doing sums she hated — how long, how far, how fast, whether a woman and a ledger-keeper and a traitor cat could outrun a crown.

Near dawn, Isandro sat up in her blanket nest and said, "I'll pay you."

Wren, who had been pretending to sleep, opened one eye. "Pay me for what?"

"For guiding me. When we find the annex. Out of the city, to the border, wherever the door stands." The girl's voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone reciting terms she'd rehearsed. "I have nothing. The guild's assets are seized. But I have authority over its debts — Corvel signed it to me, the transfer's real, it's in my pocket along with everything else I own. So I'm cancelling yours."

Wren sat up. "My what?"

"Your debt." Isandro pulled the main ledger — the guild's true ledger, the one she'd carried out of the burning house strapped to her ribs like a second heart — and opened it to a page near the front, and turned it toward the lamp.

Wren Halloway. Smuggler. The entry was short, because impossible debts apparently required little paperwork:

***Owes the Ember Guild one passage, unpayable by any route known. Debt incurred [date struck through]. Terms: to be collected when the guild finds the road it was owed upon.***

"Do you know what that means?" Isandro asked quietly. "I didn't, at first. I audited this entry four times over six years and I never understood it. But I understand it now. Someone in this guild once promised you a road that doesn't exist. A way out that can't be bought or bribed or sailed to. And you've been carrying that promise for — how long?"

"Nine years," Wren said. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Nine years. And it's the reason you never left Vessany City, isn't it? However bad it got, however close the watch came — you stayed, because the one thing you wanted more than escape was the *real* escape, the one you were owed, and you couldn't leave in case they came to collect." Isandro closed the book gently. "I'm cancelling it. Tonight, if you want. Full discharge, sealed in my own hand. And then you can walk away free, and I'll find the door myself."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, Turncoat yowled once at nothing.

"That's a stupid offer," Wren said.

"It's a fair one."

"It's a *stupid* offer, because if you cancel it, I have no reason to help you, and you'll die in a ditch by the second milestone." Wren dragged a hand through her hair. "And also — also — because you don't cancel a debt like that. Nine years, Brightwater. Do you know what a person does with a debt like that? She builds her whole life around the hole it leaves. You don't just—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "You don't just fill in the hole and hand the page back."

"No," Isandro agreed. "But you can spend it."

"Spend it."

"A debt is worth exactly what it can buy. Yours can buy a guide who knows every road out of this country and half the roads that aren't roads. Mine can buy you the only thing you've wanted for nine years." She held out her hand, ink-stained, steady now. "One passage, payable in full, upon arrival at the door out of Vessany. Those are my terms. Take them or leave them, but decide tonight, because the drum comes around again at dawn and I'd rather know which side of it you're standing on."

Wren looked at the offered hand for a long time.

She thought about nine years of staying. Of turning down three good runs south because the run south wasn't *the* run. Of waking up in safe houses with her boots on, waiting for a knock that never came, telling herself the wait was loyalty when it was really just hope wearing a cheaper dress.

She thought: *this girl audits souls the way I case houses.*

She spat in her palm — old custom, older than either of them — and shook.

"One passage," Wren said. "Full discharge on arrival. And Brightwater? If the door turns out to be a story, I'm taking the cat."

Turncoat, from the shadows, made a sound that was unmistakably a laugh.

* * *

They left at first light, before the drum, wrapped in tanner's smocks that Wren had stolen from the drying line with a clear conscience. Isandro carried both ledgers against her ribs. Wren carried the knives, the lies, and the growing, unwelcome suspicion that she had just been out-negotiated by a twenty-year-old accountant, and that she hadn't minded.

At the top of Cordwainer's Row, where the street bent toward the Salt Gate, Isandro stopped.

"What?" Wren said.

"The annex." Isandro was looking at the gate — the old gate, salt-white stone, worn hollow at the base by centuries of hands touching it for luck. "Everyone keeps saying *the door out of Vessany* like it's a thing in a story. But look at it. Look at the name of the gate we'd have to pass to leave the city at all."

Wren looked. The Salt Gate. Of course. Salt in the foundations, salt in the name, salt in the last entry of a drowned kingdom's book. The country erased from maps had been leaving itself clues in plain sight for two hundred years, and only a ledger-keeper would ever think to read them.

"Oh," Wren said. "Oh, that's *obscene*. Somebody built the riddle into the architecture."

"Somebody built it into everything," Isandro said softly. "Come on. We have until winter's end."

They walked toward the gate together, a smuggler and a keeper of impossible accounts, and behind them the drum came around the corner, beating out names.

Neither of them looked back.
