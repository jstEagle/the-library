## 8. The Ferryman's Tithe

The river had refused Ossa Merrow for eleven years, and she had stopped taking it personally somewhere around the seventh.

It wasn't that the Sennet ran shallow where she stood. It wasn't that her pole found no purchase, or her punt no current. The river simply *declined*—a polite, total refusal, the way a door closes not slammed but swung shut by a careful hand. She would push off from the south bank with a paying passenger aboard, and the water would part around her hull like a crowd around a leper, and she would drift—drift, always drift, sideways and backward and nowhere—until some other ferrywoman rowed out and took her fare off her hands for half price, which stung worse than anything.

So Ossa kept the ferry house at Tarn's Bend and mended ropes and tarred hulls for the women who could cross, and at night she sat on the dock with her boots off and her feet in the water, and said to the river: *I'm still here.*

And the river said nothing.

She was saying nothing back on the evening the two strangers came down the north road—one tall and scowling like weather coming in, one small and gray-cloaked and looking at the water the way other people looked at church—and Ossa knew before they reached the dock that this was going to cost her something. She'd learned to recognize that look. It was the look of someone who had come a long way to ask for an impossible thing, and Ossa had a professional relationship with impossible things, mostly as their victim.

"Ferry's closed till morning," she called, without turning around. "And even open, I don't cross after dark. The eels get ideas."

"We're not looking to cross," said the small one. Her voice was quiet but it carried over the water the way a stone skips—light, touching many places. "We're looking for someone who knows where the old border used to run."

Ossa pulled her feet out of the river slowly, as if withdrawing from a conversation.

"Old border," she repeated. "There's no old border. There's just the marsh, past the ford, where the maps go shy."

"The maps go shy," the tall one muttered. "That's one way to put drowning a whole country."

"Wren." The small one said it like a hand on a shoulder. Then, to Ossa: "My name is Isandro Brightwater. This is Wren Halloway. We've come from Corvel's guild in Vastermoor—the Ember Guild."

Something went cold down Ossa's spine, and it wasn't the wind off the water. Everyone who worked a river knew the name Ember Guild the way farmers knew the name of the tax man: with respect, resentment, and a ledger entry of their own.

"I know the guild," she said. "I owe them a debt."

"I know," Isandro said. "That's why we're here."

* * *

They sat in the ferry house while Ossa made tea out of willow bark and river mint, because hospitality was hospitality even when you suspected your guests were about to ruin your life. The tall one—Wren—had claimed the chair nearest the door and sat in it like a cat pretending not to watch the mouse hole. The small one held her cup in both hands and waited, which Ossa found more unnerving than talking.

"You'll want the story first," Ossa said finally. "Everyone wants the story before they tell me what they want. Fine. Here's the story. My grandmother crossed the Sennet for forty years. My mother crossed it for thirty. I was born in that punt, more or less—Mother went into labor mid-river during the spring melt, and the ferry house was closer than the midwife. First thing I ever saw was water. First thing I ever heard was my mother swearing at it." She sipped her tea. "I could pole before I could walk properly. The river knew me. I used to swear it did—it carried my punt like a hand under a hull. Passengers said crossing with me was smoother than sleeping."

"And then?" Isandro asked gently.

"And then when I was nineteen, there was a flood year, and a man came to the bank at night with a box he wanted carried across, no questions, triple fare." Ossa turned her cup in her fingers. "No questions is the oldest rule of the ferry trade. Older than fares. So I poled him across. And the box—" she shrugged, and found the shrug didn't want to come, so she set the cup down instead. "The box went into the river. He went in after it. And I went in after him, because that's the other rule: you don't let anyone drown on your crossing. Not on *your* crossing. That's yours forever."

Wren had gone very still. "You pulled him out."

"I pulled *him* out. The box stayed down. What was in the box—I never learned. But three days later the river stopped carrying me. Not capsized me. Refused me. I'd take passengers on and we'd sit in the middle of the channel like a stone with opinions. I lost the family trade in a season. The guild bought up Mother's debt that winter when she got sick—bought it fair, mind, they always say it's fair—but fair isn't kind, and kind isn't paid, and here I am, eleven years later, still owing them a sum I couldn't earn in thirty." She looked up. "So. You know where I stand. Now tell me why two guild travelers are sitting in my kitchen asking after a drowned country."

Isandro set down her cup and opened the satchel she'd been holding like a child holds a doll, and took out a book.

Ossa knew books. She'd seen ledgers, hymnals, the magistrate's big tax folio that came through every autumn. This was none of those. The cover was salt-stained leather gone soft as cloth, and when Isandro opened it, the pages exhaled a smell that hit Ossa somewhere below thinking—brine and cedar smoke and, absurdly, her grandmother's kitchen, though her grandmother had been dead twenty years and had lived nowhere near any kitchen with brine in it.

"This is the ledger of Vessany," Isandro said. "The country that isn't on the maps anymore."

"There was never a country called Vessany," Ossa said automatically. Every child in the river country learned the sentence before they learned to swim: *there was never a country called Vessany.* You learned it the same way you learned not to eat the white berries. It kept you alive. It kept everyone alive. Because the alternative to the sentence was the question underneath it, and nobody asked the question anymore, because asking had consequences, and the consequences had names, and the names were still in office.

"No," Isandro agreed. "There wasn't. And yet here's its ledger, and it lists a customs house at the eastern marsh, and tolls collected, and a ferry route—" she turned a page toward Ossa, and Ossa's breath caught, because the handwriting was fine and slanted and utterly familiar, the hand of a woman who had written her daughter's name on a christening cloth, "—and a ferry route along the Sennet bend here, marked *Merrow*, licensed under crown charter of Vessany."

"My grandmother," Ossa said. Her voice came out strange. "That's my grandmother's writing. I'd know it anywhere. She taught me my letters with that hand." She stared at the page. The numbers marched down it in tidy columns. A working woman's numbers. Ferry receipts. Tolls paid. A life recorded, a life that officially had never happened, in a country that officially had never existed, and here it all was, real as rope, real as tea, real as the ache behind her sternum.

"They didn't erase everything," Isandro said quietly. "They erased the *name*. The rest is still out there. The customs house is still standing—I've seen the entry, and Wren has seen the marsh. The border is still where it was. It just needs people who remember where that is." She met Ossa's eyes. "You know where the old border runs. The guild's records say so. Your debt entry says so—*knowledge of the drowned march*, it says, listed among your assets like a bolt of cloth."

"It would," Ossa said bitterly. "They inventory everything."

"They do," Isandro said, and for a moment something flickered across her face that wasn't guild at all—something tired and angry and very young. "Which is why I'm not here on the guild's behalf. I'm here on mine. I need to reach that customs house before winter closes the marsh. The hunters are behind us. You may have heard—"

"I've heard." Everyone had heard. Heralds at every market square, reading the proclamation in that flat official voice: *persons gifted with the speech of beasts are abominations against natural order and are to be surrendered.* They'd taken the tanner's boy in Millford three weeks ago. Ossa hadn't known him well, but she'd known his dog, and the dog had howled at the ferry landing for nine days afterward, and Ossa had sat with it, because dogs and rivers were the only two things that talked to her anymore, and neither of them said much worth hearing.

"So you want me to guide you through the marsh," Ossa said. "Past the ford, along the old toll road, to a customs house that serves a country that doesn't exist. In winter. With hunters behind you." She laughed, short and hard. "And what do I get out of it? Because if the answer is *adventure*, I should tell you now that I find adventure overrated and damp."

"Cancellation of your debt," Wren said. "Full and final. I'm owed cancellation of mine; the guild master signed terms before—" she glanced at Isandro, "—before circumstances changed. Your entry gets the same treatment. Paper's already drawn. We just need your mark."

Ossa looked at the paper Wren produced. It was real guild paper, sealed in wax the color of embers. Eleven years of arithmetic, cancelled in one signature. She thought about what she'd buy first. New rope. A new punt, maybe—a good one, ash and pine pitch. She thought about standing on her own dock owing nobody anything.

Then she thought about her grandmother's handwriting on a toll receipt for a crossing that had never legally happened, and something in her chest shifted like ballast.

"What happens to the marsh folk?" she asked. "If I go."

"Nothing, if we don't lead hunters there," Isandro said. "Everything, if we do. Which is why we need someone who knows the paths the patrols can't follow. Someone the marsh trusts."

"The marsh doesn't trust anyone."

"The marsh trusted your grandmother."

Ossa stood up, because sitting felt wrong all of a sudden, and walked to the window. Night had come down fully now, and the river was a long black ribbon with the stars stuck to it like sequins. Somewhere out there, under that water, was the box. Under the marsh, past the ford, was the customs house. Under everything, under the sentence every child recited, was the country. Her grandmother had poled a licensed ferry for a nation that the world had agreed to forget, and the world had forgotten it so thoroughly that even the river had joined in—had refused her, Ossa, the last of a line of Vessany ferrymen, the way a man refuses to speak a dead friend's name.

Maybe that was it. Maybe that was the whole ugly truth of it. The river hadn't refused *her*. The river had been refusing the name. And she'd spent eleven years taking it personally.

"You'll need to leave at slack water tomorrow," she said, without turning around. "The marsh channels run backward on the flood tide, and if you don't know them you'll spend a day going the wrong direction at speed, which is the most efficient way to be miserable I know of." She turned. "I'll take you as far as the old toll road. After that we'll see. I'm not promising past the ford."

"Understood," Isandro said.

"I'm also not promising I won't turn around halfway and come home. I have a ferry house. I have obligations."

"Of course," Isandro said, and Ossa narrowed her eyes at her, because the girl was agreeing too easily, smoothly, the way water agrees with a hull right up until it doesn't.

"And I want it in the paper," Ossa added. "Cancellation. In ink. Before we leave the dock, not after."

"Already written," Wren said, tapping the seal. "Corvel drew it generous. Apparently he liked to keep his creditors happy." Something in her voice suggested she did not extend the same sentiment to Corvel herself.

Ossa nodded slowly. Then she looked at the ledger again, lying open on her table, her grandmother's letters marching across the years, and heard herself say the thing she'd sworn she wouldn't say:

"There's a toll booth on the old road. Third marker past the ford. When I was small, my grandmother told me stories about a clerk there who never slept, and I thought it was a story to keep me from wandering. It wasn't a story, was it?"

Isandro and Wren exchanged a glance.

"No," Isandro said. "I don't think it was."

"Well then." Ossa began stacking cups, briskly, because her hands needed work or they were going to shake. "Slack water's at dawn. Sleep. And if either of you snores, you're sleeping in the punt."

* * *

She dreamed about the river that night, and for the first time in eleven years, the dream was different.

In the old dream she was always poling the night crossing, the man and his box, the black water rising. But tonight she stood on the dock instead, and the river spoke—not in words, rivers don't use words, they use *pressure*, they use pull—but she understood it anyway, the way she'd understood it as a child, before she'd decided understanding was a thing adults gave up.

*I never refused you,* the river said. *I refused the name they drowned. Come back to the water, daughter of the Merrows, and carry what must be carried.*

Ossa woke before dawn with her face wet and couldn't have said whether she'd been crying or whether the river had gotten into the dream itself. Outside, the sky was the color of unpolished pewter, and the first flakes of the year were coming down over the water, fat and slow, like the world shaking out a blanket.

Winter. Early. Hard, if the wind had any say, and the wind always had a say.

She dressed, loaded the punt with rope and tar and the good lantern, and woke her guests with a boot heel on the floorboards. Wren surfaced like a soldier, knife somehow already in hand, then pretended it hadn't happened. Isandro woke the way water wakes—gradually, everywhere at once—and was dressed and helping before Ossa finished telling her to.

At the dock, Ossa paused with her boot on the gunwale and looked back once at the ferry house. Her whole inheritance: a building, a boat, a debt, and a name that meant *ferrywoman* in a language nobody spoke anymore.

"Last chance," she said, mostly to herself. "Turn around. Mend ropes. Die comfortable."

Then she pushed off, and the river—her river, the traitor, the mourner, the keeper of her grandmother's crossings—took the hull under it like a hand, and carried her.

Ossa stood very straight in the stern and did not let herself feel it, not yet, not fully. There would be time for feeling later, if they lived. For now there was slack water and a falling tide and two strangers with a dead country in a bag, and snow coming down on the water like salt.

"Hold on," she told them. "The marsh bites."
