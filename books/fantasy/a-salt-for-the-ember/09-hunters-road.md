## 9. Hunters' Road

The high road out of the ferry country was, according to Ossa, "the only road worth the name between here and the border," which Isandro took to mean it was also the most dangerous. Roads worth the name attracted carts, and carts attracted tolls, and tolls attracted men with badges.

They had been walking three days since Ossa joined them at the river, and Isandro had learned several things about her new companions. Wren Halloway complained about her boots the way other people prayed—frequently, rhythmically, and with evident sincerity. Ossa navigated by a private geography of grief, pointing at hillsides and saying things like *the water used to come up to there* about places that had never seen a flood in living memory. And both of them watched Isandro when they thought she wasn't looking, the way people watch a stove they suspect is still hot.

"You're doing it again," Wren said on the morning of the third day.

"Doing what?"

"The lips." Wren tapped her own mouth. "You move them when you think nobody's watching. Like you're tasting something."

Isandro kept her eyes on the road ahead, where it climbed through gorse toward a ridgeline stitched with pines. "I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Debts."

Wren snorted. "You guild folk count debts in your sleep. I've seen the ledgers. There's one entry in your master's book that's just *the moon, arrears*."

"That's a real account," Isandro said, with more dignity than the statement deserved. "The moon was collateralized against a storm we bottled in 411. It has never once paid interest."

To her surprise—and she suspected to Wren's—the smuggler laughed. It was a short, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years, but it was a laugh. Ossa, walking behind with the mule (a placid creature named Turnip who had taken an immediate and vocal liking to Isandro), raised an eyebrow at the both of them and said nothing, which from Ossa was practically applause.

It was almost noon when Turnip stopped walking.

Not balked—stopped. The mule planted all four hooves and turned her long gray head toward the trees below the ridge, and her ears went flat, and she said, in the low rumbling voice that only Isandro heard: *Iron. Dogs. Coming up the switchbacks.*

Isandro's stomach dropped through the road and into the valley below.

"What is it?" Ossa asked, because whatever else mules were, they were honest barometers, and Ossa had spent a lifetime reading weather.

"Hunters," Isandro said. "Coming fast."

Wren's hand went to the knife at her belt, then—showing more sense—to the scrub at the roadside. "Off the road. Now. There's a drainage cut back a hundred yards; we can lie flat and let them pass."

"They won't pass," Isandro said. "Turnip says four riders and six hounds. They're not patrolling. They're tracking."

"Tracking who? This road sees a hundred travelers a week."

"It sees a hundred travelers a week," Isandro agreed, already moving toward the cut, "but how many of them are traveling with a fugitive beast-speaker, a smuggler with a guild debt, and a ferrywoman whose river won't carry her?"

Ossa made a small wounded noise. "My river situation is *private*."

"*Down*," said Wren.

The drainage cut was a shallow trench where spring meltwater had sawed into the hillside, now dry and floored with last year's bracken. They crouched in it with Turnip standing over them like a gray roof, which was either excellent camouflage or the single worst idea anyone had ever had, depending entirely on whether the hunters' hounds were stupid.

They were not stupid.

Isandro heard them before she saw them—not the riders, but the dogs, a music of claws and breath and low conversational woofs drifting down through the gorse. And beneath the woofs, words. Hounds spoke a language like water running over stones, all current and no reflection, and what this pack was saying was:

*Scent-scent-scent, the gray one, the tall one, the soft-handed one, here-here-here.*

*The soft-handed one,* Isandro thought. *That's me.* She looked at her hands, ink-stained, ledger-callused, and felt a wild urge to laugh. Three days of careful anonymity, of keeping her mouth shut and her gift locked behind her teeth, and she'd been betrayed by the way she petted a mule.

The riders came into view along the ridgeline. Four of them, crown green and silver, and the hounds fanned out ahead of them in a practiced crescent—six lean brindle shapes moving with the terrible efficiency of animals that had never once been told *no*.

One hound broke from the crescent and came straight down the slope toward the drainage cut, nose working, and Isandro understood with cold clarity that in perhaps ten seconds everything would be over. Wren's knife hand flexed. Ossa went very still, the stillness of someone deciding what to grab first if they had to run.

And the hound arrived at the edge of the cut, looked down at them—

—and Isandro did the thing she had sworn, for twenty-nine years, never to do in front of another human being.

She spoke.

*Sit,* she said, in the running-water language, and poured every ounce of ledger-clerk authority she possessed into it—the voice she used on debtors, on Corvel's creditors, on the one time a ghost had tried to renegotiate its terms. *Sit, and be quiet, and listen.*

The hound sat. Its haunches hit the bracken with an audible thump, and its head tilted, and its brown eyes went wide with the particular astonishment of a soldier who has been addressed directly by a general.

The other five hounds caught up, took one look at their seated colleague, and sat too, in a neat brindle row along the lip of the cut, tails sweeping the ground in slow, confused arcs.

*Good,* Isandro said, before she could stop herself, and three of the six wagged harder.

Above, the lead rider reined in. "What's got into them?" he called down to his fellows. "Brammel's bitch hasn't sat since the day she was whelped."

"Maybe they've struck scent," said a second voice, and Isandro's blood went to ice, because she knew that voice. Not from the raid on the guildhouse—from the mule's testimony, weeks ago, delivered in a stable that smelled of hay and old violence. *The man with the badge,* Turnip had said. *The one whose hands were red when he came back to the yard.* Handler Vesek. Here, on this road, close enough that she could hear him breathe.

"Strike or no strike, get them moving," said the first rider. "We've lost two days to this ferry country already."

Vesek whistled—the sharp double note of a handler calling a pack home—and six hounds did not move.

Six hounds looked at Isandro.

*Oh no,* she thought.

*Go on,* she told them, as gently as she could while her heart hammered like a debtor at the door. *Back to your man. Good dogs. Very good dogs. Go.*

The nearest hound—a young one, all legs and apology—whined. *But you smell kind,* it said, in the simple grammar of hounds. *Kind smells are rare.*

*I know,* Isandro said. *Go anyway. Please. If you don't go, bad things happen to everyone, including me, and I have been nothing but polite to you.*

The young hound sighed the sigh of the put-upon throughout history, stood, and trotted back up the slope. One by one the others followed, though the oldest bitch paused at the crest of the ridge, looked back down at Isandro with an expression Isandro could only describe as *we will discuss this later*, and then went.

The riders wheeled and moved off along the ridgeline, westward, away from them. The sound of hoofbeats faded. Somewhere a lark started up, oblivious, singing about absolutely nothing.

Isandro exhaled.

Then she realized that Wren and Ossa were both staring at her.

Not at the hounds. At her. At her mouth, specifically, which—as Wren had so helpfully pointed out that very morning—had been moving.

The silence lasted four full seconds. Isandro counted them. It was a habit.

"Well," said Ossa at last, into the bracken. "That explains the mule."

"The mule?" Isandro's voice came out an octave too high.

"Turnip," Ossa said. "Follows you around like you're made of apples. Stares at you when you talk. I've been ferrying livestock for thirty years, girl. Animals don't stare at people unless the people are saying something worth staring at."

"I thought it was the apples," Isandro said weakly.

"Nobody is made of apples," Wren said flatly. She hadn't moved. Her eyes were narrow and bright and fixed on Isandro with an expression that wasn't fear, exactly, and wasn't wonder, exactly, and wasn't anything Isandro had a name for. "How long?"

"How long what?"

"*Halloway.*"

Isandro looked down at her ink-stained hands. Above the cut, the lark sang on. "Since I was six," she said. "A cart horse told me where its owner had dropped his purse. I gave it back. The owner asked how I knew. I said the horse told me. He laughed. Everyone laughed. So I learned to stop saying it out loud." She swallowed. "Twenty-three years of not saying it out loud. Until just now. In front of six government hounds and two people I've known for less than a week."

Another silence. Then Wren did something entirely unexpected: she sat down in the bracken, pulled off her left boot, shook a pebble out of it, and put the boot back on, all with the air of a woman clearing the floor for a serious conversation.

"My brother talked to gulls," she said.

Isandro blinked. "*What?*"

"Gulls. Filthy things, terrible conversationalists, all shouting and stolen chips. But he'd sit on the breakwater for hours, chatting away. Our mother beat him for it. Called it devil-work. So he learned to do it quiet, same as you." Wren laced the boot with unnecessary force. "He's dead now. Fever, not the hunt. But I'll tell you what I learned watching him: the ones who hide it longest are the ones who hide it best, and the ones who hide it best are the ones the hunt never catches." She looked up. "Until today. Today you caught yourself."

"I turned their dogs," Isandro said. "Their *handler* was ten feet away. If he'd looked down instead of up—"

"He didn't."

"This time."

Wren opened her mouth—presumably to say something reassuring in her characteristic style, which was to say something true and unhelpful—but Ossa raised a hand.

"Listen."

Hoofbeats. Behind them. Coming back.

They flattened into the bracken as the riders reappeared on the ridgeline—but wrong, somehow, moving in circles, the hounds cast wide and casting again, quartering the slope below the road with increasing agitation. And cutting across the wind, faint but unmistakable, came Vesek's voice, tight with something worse than anger:

"She was here. The speaker was *here*. The pack sat for her—Brammel doesn't *sit*—" A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone of a man reporting a wound: "Sir, with respect, ask your questions of the dogs' behavior, not of my handling. I know what I know. Lips moving. Words I couldn't catch. A woman with soft hands and ledger ink on her fingers."

The second rider laughed, short and ugly. "Ledger ink. Next you'll tell me the clerk of the Ember Guild is a beast-speaker. The Guildmaster's already in chains, Vesek. His little bookkeeper ran off with a smuggler and a madwoman. That's not a conspiracy, that's a Tuesday."

"But the dogs—"

"The dogs are tired. We make for the border crossing by nightfall. Move out."

This time the hoofbeats truly faded, westward, and did not return. But Isandro lay in the bracken long after they'd gone, staring at the pine needles overhead, feeling the shape of her life rearrange itself around a single fact: *he saw my lips move.* Twenty-three years of armor, and it had failed in under a minute, on an open road, because a young hound had smelled kindness and she had been too frightened to be careful.

"We can't take the high road anymore," Ossa said quietly. "He'll remember this stretch. He'll watch it."

"Then we don't take roads," Wren said. She was already on her feet, brushing off her knees, and there was something new in her face—something that might, in better light, have been resolve. "There's a drover's path over the fells. Sheep tracks, mostly. Slow, wet, and ugly."

"Ugly is fine," Isandro said. "Ugly is *safe*."

They gathered the packs. Turnip, who had stood through the entire ambush with the composure of a professional, leaned her warm gray weight briefly against Isandro's shoulder—an unmistakable gesture, in any language—and Isandro scratched the place behind her jaw that made her lip curl.

*Thank you,* Isandro murmured, too low for human ears.

*You smelled afraid,* Turnip said, unbothered. *Afraid smells are common. Kind smells are rare. I told you.*

They cut north off the road and began to climb. The fells rose around them, brown and trackless, and somewhere behind, growing smaller with every step, the high road ran on toward the border crossing where four riders would report, in writing, in triplicate, that the Ember Guild's bookkeeper had spoken to dogs.

By dusk Isandro's feet hurt, Wren's boot had developed a squeak of apocalyptic proportions, and Ossa had identified eleven hillsides where the water used to come up to there. They camped in a hollow without a fire, sharing cold bread and colder silence, and it was Wren who finally broke it.

"That handler," she said, to the darkness. "Vesek. He didn't want to report you. Did you notice? He argued with his own officer. *Ask the dogs' behavior.*" She snorted softly. "Man loves those hounds more than his commission. That's a lever, if we ever need one."

"He saw my lips move," Isandro said. "That's not a lever. That's a noose."

"Nooses can be cut," Wren said. "I've cut a few." A pause. "Metaphorically. Mostly."

In the dark, Isandro found herself thinking not of the hunters but of the young hound, all legs and apology, whining *you smell kind*—and of the old bitch looking back from the ridgeline as if to say *we will discuss this later.*

Later, it turned out, would come sooner than any of them wanted. Because three days later, on the ugliest and safest stretch of sheep track in the fells, with rain coming down in ropes and the whole world reduced to gray wool and gray stone, they crested a rise and found the pack waiting for them.

Six brindle hounds, sitting in a row in the rain, tails neatly wrapped around their feet. No riders in sight. And the old bitch—Brammel, Vesek had called her—rose to her feet, shook the water from her coat, and looked up at Isandro with an expression of vast canine patience.

*We need to talk,* she said.
