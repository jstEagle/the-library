## 10. The Village That Paid Its Taxes to Nobody

Brammel had been elder of Hollowmere for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had kept one rule above all others: never let the tax collector leave hungry.

It was a strange rule for a strange office, because the tax collector never came. No one came. Hollowmere sat at the ragged edge of three maps that all agreed, in their different ways, that nothing was there. The crown's surveyors had ridden through once, sixty years ago, measured the well, counted the goats, written *unproductive moorland* in a fine clerk's hand, and gone home. And yet every autumn, on the first frost, Brammel walked to the old boundary stone at the edge of the frozen marsh and left a basket there: a quarter of the year's grain, a jar of honey, a knitted thing his wife had made before her hands forgot how.

The basket was always empty by morning.

He did not know who took it. He had stopped asking around year twelve, when it became clear that nobody in the village would admit to it either, and that everyone checked the stone anyway, and that the whole arrangement worked precisely because no one looked too hard at it. Taxes paid to nobody, collected by nobody, owed by everybody. His grandmother had called it *the tithe of the forgotten*, and she had said it the way other people said grace.

"You're brooding," said his granddaughter Tansy, who was eleven and considered brooding a personal failing of his generation.

"I'm considering," Brammel corrected. "Brooding is free. Considering has a purpose."

"What's the purpose?"

"That someone's coming up the moor road."

Tansy squinted out the window at the grey morning. Four figures, small with distance, moving slow. One limped. One had a mule — no, wait, that was just a very large woman with a pack frame, which happened more often than you'd think on the moor. "Travelers?"

"Travelers." Brammel reached for his stick and his good coat, the one without the burn hole. "Put the kettle on, love. And hide the good spoons."

"Why?"

"Because travelers who look like that are either running from something or toward something, and either way they'll want to take a piece of this village home with them as proof they were kind once. Spoons go first."

* * *

They arrived at his door within the hour, and Brammel saw at once that he'd been right about the spoons, because the young man among them — sharp-faced, road-worn, with the particular swagger of someone who had decided swagger was safer than fear — was already cataloguing the room the way a magpie catalogues a kitchen.

The others were stranger. A tall woman with river-mud still dried in the creases of her knuckles, though it hadn't rained in a week. A broad-shouldered man with a soldier's posture and a hound's eyes, who flinched when Tansy's cat walked past him, which told Brammel more than any introduction could have. And in the middle, a young person with ink-stained fingers and a book clutched against their chest like a shield, or a child, or both.

"We're looking for the old border," the ink-stained one said. "Of Vessany."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Brammel heard it fall all the way down.

He was ninety percent sure his face did nothing at all. He had practiced doing nothing at all for most of his life; it was the village's second-favorite skill after cheese. But the tall ferrywoman — Ossa, the soldier called her, in a voice that suggested they'd been arguing about it for days — went white as the frost on his window, and the ink-stained one's arms tightened around the book.

"Vessany," Brammel repeated mildly. "Don't know it. Never heard of such a place. Tansy, have we got anything in the larder for four hungry liars and a cat-fearing soldier?"

"Brammel—" started the sharp-faced one, Wren.

"Eat first," Brammel said. "Lie after. Lies sit better on bread, my mother always said, and my mother lied professionally, so she knew."

* * *

Over barley soup and the last of the autumn honey, the story came out in pieces, the way stories do when people aren't sure yet how much of themselves to spend.

Brammel learned that the ink-stained one was named Isandro, and was a ledger-keeper, and carried a book that made Ossa press her palm flat against the table when it was set down, as if steadying herself against weather. He learned that Wren Halloway owed debts she described in the tone of someone describing an old injury. He learned that Vesek had been a beast-hunter for the crown and was now, by his own flat account, "between employments," and that Isandro could speak to animals, and that this gift had turned them from travelers into fugitives somewhere back down the high road.

"And you want the door out," Brammel said, when the soup was gone. "The Vessany door."

Isandro's chin lifted. "You do know it."

"I know a door that takes payment in what you love most," Brammel said, "and I know that saying so out loud is the fastest way to make sure nobody ever finds it. So let's start smaller. Let's start with taxes."

He told them about the basket. He told them about the boundary stone, and the empty mornings, and the surveyors who had written *unproductive moorland* over four hundred years of graves and weddings and excellent cheese. He told them how Hollowmere still sang the Vessany harvest song but only the verses that didn't name the king, because the king's name had been scrubbed from every hymnal south of the marsh and nobody could quite remember the tune of the missing lines, only that there had been lines, and that their absence ached like a tooth.

"Every year," Brammel said, "we pay our taxes to a country that doesn't exist. And every year somebody collects them. Sixty years, and the crown has never once noticed, because the crown cannot see a debt unless it's written in its own hand." He tapped the table. "But a ledger-keeper might."

Isandro opened the book.

Brammel had seen old books before. He had buried three of them with parishioners who couldn't bear to be parted from them even underground. But this one made the fire lean sideways, just slightly, the way a dog leans toward a familiar hand. The pages were salt-stiff. The ink had gone brown as old blood. And when Isandro turned to a page near the middle and read aloud — *Received of the Parish of Hollowmere, one quarter-share, in perpetuity, against the day of return* — Tansy burst into tears, which embarrassed her terribly, and Vesek looked away, and Ossa put her face in her hands.

"That's us," Brammel said quietly. "That's our entry. My grandmother signed that. She had beautiful handwriting and terrible opinions about honey."

"It's real," Ossa whispered. "People kept paying. All this time, people kept—"

"Somebody kept collecting," Wren said, and her voice had lost its swagger entirely. "Who? Who's been taking the baskets?"

Brammel spread his hands. "That, friends, is the question I've been not-asking for thirty-one years. And I'll tell you what I've worked out. Whoever it is, they're still staff. Whatever else burned when Vessany burned, the customs service is apparently immortal. Bureaucracy always is. It's the one thing no fire has ever successfully killed."

* * *

He took them to the stone himself, because the afternoon was clear and because he wanted to see their faces when they stood on the old line.

The marsh lay beyond it, frozen into a silence so complete you could hear your own heartbeat apologize for interrupting. Out in the white distance, half-sunk, stood a building. Brammel had known it was there since childhood — everyone did, the way you know about a mole on your own back, without ever properly looking.

"The drowned customs house," he said. "Vessany's last official structure. The marsh took the road and the villages on the far side, but that building refuses to sink. Refuses to fall down, too. I sent Tansy's father out to it once, on a dare, when we were boys. He got within fifty yards and swore the chimney was smoking."

"Was it?" Isandro asked.

"He didn't stay to check. He came home, joined the church choir, and never took another dare in his life. Some buildings you don't knock on." Brammel turned to face them, and here he made himself stand straighter than his back liked, because what came next deserved a straight back. "Now I'll tell you the rest of it. The part I shouldn't."

Wren's hand drifted toward her knife, and Brammel raised a placid palm. "Not a trap. A warning. There's a difference, though I grant it's mostly timing."

He told them about Old Maren, who had been the village's memory before Brammel inherited the job. Maren had been a girl when Vessany fell, old enough to remember the country, and she had spent her whole long life trying to get back to it. She found the door — or it found her; Maren was never clear on which, and neither, Brammel suspected, was the door. She stood before it with sixty years of longing in her chest, and the door asked its price, and she paid it.

"She loved Vessany most," Ossa said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "She'd have paid gladly."

"No," Brammel said. "That's the cruel joke of it, child. If it wanted what you loved best in the abstract, it would be a cheap door. Grief is easy to spend; we're all rich in it. No. It takes what you love most *now*. What you'd fight for tomorrow morning. What makes you check the lock twice." He looked at each of them in turn. "Maren had a daughter by then. Born in exile, named for a river that no longer appeared on maps. The door took the daughter — not her life, understand, nothing so clean. It took her *from her*. Maren walked through alone into a country of ghosts, and lived there forty years, and wrote us one letter, and the letter said: *I got everything I wanted and it tastes of nothing.*"

The wind moved through the frozen reeds, and for once Brammel wished it wouldn't, because silence would have been kinder.

"So when you find your door," he finished, "and it asks — and it will ask — don't answer quickly. Don't answer bravely. Bravery is how it gets you. Answer honestly, and then ask yourselves whether what you keep is worth what you gave." He shrugged, an old man's shrug, carrying sixty years of unasked questions. "Or don't listen to me. I pay taxes to nobody. What would I know?"

* * *

They stayed the night, because the snow had begun coming down in earnest and because Brammel had made the soup stretch and felt entitled to company in return.

It was, he thought later, one of the better evenings of his old age. Wren taught Tansy three card tricks, two of which worked. Vesek sat by the fire with the cat eventually installed on his knee, wearing the expression of a man who had been conquered and found it merciful. Ossa asked Brammel for the harvest song, the full version, and when he admitted he only knew the scrubbed verses, she sang the missing lines herself, low, in a dialect the marsh had kept alive the way amber keeps an insect — and the whole room went quiet, and Tansy's tears came again, and this time nobody was embarrassed.

And Isandro sat beside him at the table with the ledger open between them, making notes in a small precise hand.

"You're writing it down," Brammel observed. "Maren's story."

"Someone should keep accounts," Isandro said. "Even of warnings. Especially of warnings." They glanced up. "Your grandmother's signature is in this book, Elder. Your village has been in credit with Vessany for sixty years. When I find the door — if I find the door — that's a debt I intend to see honored."

Brammel studied the young ledger-keeper for a long moment: the ink-stained fingers, the stubborn jaw, the book held like something both owned and owning.

"You know what worries me about you?" he said. "Nothing. That's what worries me. People with nothing to hide usually have something they haven't been asked about yet." He closed the ledger gently under Isandro's hand. "Ask yourself tonight, before sleep. Not what you'd pay. You won't know until the door names it, and it will name it true. Ask instead who you'd refuse to walk through without. Hold onto that. It's the only currency the door can't counterfeit."

In the morning the snow had stopped and the world was white and new, and the four of them stood at his gate buckling on packs, bound for the marsh and the smoking chimney and whatever waited behind the customs house door.

Brammel pressed a parcel into Isandro's hands: bread, honey, and, wrapped separately at the bottom, a small cloth bag that clinked.

"Salt," he said. "From our pans. It's poor stuff compared to what Vessany used to make, but it's ours, and it's paid for." He fixed the young ledger-keeper with a look. "And if you happen across whoever's been collecting our taxes all these years — you tell them Hollowmere says the accounts are in order, and we'd like a receipt."

Isandro laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of them — and then went solemn, and touched two fingers to the bag of salt as if swearing on it.

"They'll have a receipt," Isandro said. "I'll see to it personally."

They went down toward the marsh, four small figures on the white road, and Brammel watched until the cold made his eyes water, and then watched a while longer, telling himself it was the cold.

Behind him, Tansy tugged his sleeve. "Grandda. Their footprints."

He looked. The snow lay unbroken behind the four travelers — except that where they had walked, the prints were filling not with snowmelt but with something faintly glittering, as if the ground itself remembered salt.

"Well," said Elder Brammel, and reached for his stick. "Fetch my coat, love. And the good spoons. Looks like we're finally going to meet the tax collector."
