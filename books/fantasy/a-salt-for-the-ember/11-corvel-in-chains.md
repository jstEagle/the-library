## 11. Corvel in Chains

Wren Halloway had a personal rule, honed over eleven years of smuggling and three years of regretting it: never stop for anything on the road that wasn't yours.

Not carts with broken axles. Not crying children, who were usually bait. Not soldiers, not priests, not old women selling charms against exactly the kind of trouble you were carrying. Every one of them was a door, and doors opened onto other people's problems, and other people's problems had a way of moving into your house without paying rent.

So when Ossa pulled the ferry-horse to a halt at the crest of the pass and said, "Smoke," Wren's whole body said *no* before her mouth could.

"Where?" Isandro stood up in the cart bed, one hand on the rail, the ledger of Vessany wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across her back like a second spine. She'd taken to wearing it that way even to sleep. Wren understood the impulse. You didn't set down the thing everyone wanted to kill you for.

"East bend." Ossa shaded her eyes with a hand scarred from thirty years of rope-work. "Cookfire smoke. A lot of it. And—" She went still in the particular way she did when the river-sense in her bones woke up, which was ridiculous, because there was no river within five miles of this pass, only a frozen stream and a lot of very smug crows. "Wagons. Six. Caged wagons."

"Cages," Vesek repeated flatly, from the back of the cart where he'd spent two days being the least popular cargo they'd ever carried, himself included.

"Prisoner convoy," Wren said. Her stomach had already done the arithmetic before her head caught up. Six wagons, crown colors, heading north toward the capital's cells. And four fugitives standing on an open ridge like a family portrait. "Everybody down. Now."

They got down. Mostly. Isandro stayed up a heartbeat longer, staring east, and Wren saw her lips move — just slightly, just the ghost of a shape — the way they moved when she was listening to something no one else could hear.

"What is it?" Wren hissed.

"Nothing," Isandro said. Which was how Wren knew it was something. "Crows. They're just — excited about something past the bend."

"Great. Delighted. In the cart."

They took the cart off the road and into a fold of hillside where a shepherd long dead had built a drystone wall, and Wren told herself firmly that whatever happened at the east bend was none of their business. The convoy would roll north. They would roll south. Two lines on a map that never needed to touch.

She believed this for approximately four hours, until dusk came down blue and cold and Ossa, who had gone ahead on foot to scout the ford, came back at a pace that meant bad news walking.

"It's Corvel," she said.

The fire they'd allowed themselves — small, screened, more warmth than light — suddenly felt very loud. Wren looked at Isandro. Isandro had gone the color of the ash at the bottom of a burned page.

"The guildmaster," Vesek said slowly. "Your master. He was arrested in the raid."

"He was," Isandro said.

"He's at the ford," Ossa said. "In the last wagon. The others are gift-bearers — a boy who whistles storms down, a woman with moss growing under her skin, gods know what else. They're chained in pairs. But Corvel's alone, in his own cage, and he's sick. Fevered. The guards were complaining about it — they don't want him dying on the road, some clerk in the capital wants him alive for questioning." She paused. "There are eight guards. Two hounds."

"Hounds," Wren said, and put her face in her hands.

"I could talk to them," Isandro said quietly.

"You could talk to *anything*, that's the entire problem with you," Wren said into her palms. She lifted her head. "No. No, no, no. I want everyone to hear me say this clearly, so that later, when we're running through a frozen marsh pursued by the entire crown, you remember that I said it: we are not doing this."

"Wren—"

"Eight guards, Isandro. Armed ones. For one man who sold your name to save his own neck. Who *did* save his own neck, by the way, while yours had a price on it and mine had a debt on it and Ossa's whole country has been underwater for a generation. That man made a bargain and the bargain was *you*, and now you want to stroll down to the ford and unmake it out of the goodness of your—"

"Yes," Isandro said.

And that was the thing about Isandro Brightwater that Wren had never learned to defend against: she didn't argue. She didn't plead her case or dress it up in reasons. She just said *yes*, in that mild ledger-keeper's voice, as though mercy were simply an entry waiting to be posted, and left everyone else to do the arguing against it.

Ossa was looking at the fire. Vesek was looking at his boots. Traitors, both of them.

"Fine," Wren said, because apparently she was also a traitor. "*Fine.* But I'm saying for the record—"

"For the record," Isandro said, "he taught me my letters. All of them. Even the ones in dead languages." She fed a stick to the fire. "He knew what I was since the day I arrived, and he never once sold me until selling me was the only coin he had left. I'm not saying that makes it right. I'm saying I'd like to look at him before I decide what it makes him."

Which was, Wren thought grimly, the most Isandro answer possible, and also completely unfair, because how was anyone supposed to argue with that?

"We scout first," she said. "We plan. Nobody does anything brave. Bravery is how smugglers die, and I have *plans*, and those plans do not include a heroic rescue at a ford in the middle of winter."

"Understood," said Isandro, in the tone of someone agreeing to a plan fully intending to be brave anyway.

* * *

The ford was worse than scouting promised, because scouting promised eight guards and delivered nine; a tenth had ridden ahead to the village to buy warm fodder and hadn't come back, but might at any moment, and Wren hated a variable she couldn't see.

They lay in the reeds on the far bank, the four of them, close enough to hear the guards grumbling. The hounds were staked near the fire, big black-and-tan things with the bored misery of animals made to stand watch over people instead of chase them. One of them lifted its head, tested the wind, and then — deliberately, Wren would have sworn to it later — turned its back and scratched its ear.

Isandro's lips were moving again.

"Don't," Wren breathed.

"I'm asking them to be tired," Isandro breathed back. "It's not lying. They *are* tired. I'm just suggesting they lean into it."

The plan — such as it was, and Wren wanted it noted that she had built it in twenty minutes under duress — leaned on three facts. First: the guards were cold, wet, underfed, and deeply uninterested in dying for a prisoner convoy in the middle of nowhere. Second: the fevered man in the last cage was worth more to them alive than awake. Third: Wren Halloway could pick a lock in the dark faster than most people could find the keyhole sober.

The hard part was everything else.

They waited for the moon to go behind the hills. Then Isandro spoke to the hounds — properly, aloud, in that low voice she used for animals, half command and half apology — and the hounds lay down by the fire like sacks of laundry and did not get up. Then Vesek walked openly into the camp, in his hunter's coat, with his hunter's badge still on it, and told the guard captain that a relief detail had come up from the garrison with orders to transfer the fevered prisoner immediately, quietly, before he died on crown hands and became a paperwork problem.

It should not have worked. It worked, mostly because the guard captain was nineteen, exhausted, and visibly relieved to hand the problem to someone wearing the right badge. Vesek had a talent Wren was only beginning to appreciate: he looked like authority the way a stone looks like a mountain. He didn't smile. He didn't explain twice. He held out his hand for the keys as if they had always been his.

The last part was Wren's. The cage lock was good work — guild-grade, actually, which stung — but good work has patterns, and Wren had spent her life learning them. She had the shackle open in ninety seconds, and then she was face to face with Master Corvel Ashgrave for the first time since the night the guildhouse burned.

He was worse than the fever. The fever was just the top layer. Underneath it he was thinner, grayer, folded small in the corner of his own cage like a ledger entry crossed out. His eyes found her in the dark and for one moment there was nothing in them at all — no recognition, no cunning, nothing — and Wren thought, *oh, there's nobody home.*

Then he recognized her, and flinched.

"Wren Halloway," he rasped. "Of all the — you shouldn't be here. This is a trap, obviously. I told them you'd come. I told them—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, smaller. "I told them everything."

"I know," Wren said. "I read the report. Well. I heard the report. From the woman whose river you helped drown, incidentally, so do try to keep up." She jerked her chin toward the dark. "Come on. Up. Can you walk?"

"Why?" It came out cracked, almost angry. "Why would you — I gave them your name, girl. Your name, your gift, your habits. I sat in a cell and I *sold* you, and it bought me six weeks and a wagon with a roof on it, and I'd do it again tomorrow, do you understand? That's what I am now. I checked. I checked every day to see if I'd become someone else overnight, and every morning it was still me." His breath rattled. "So ask yourself what you're buying, Halloway, because I promise you it isn't worth the lock-picking."

Behind him, at the edge of the firelight, Isandro stepped out of the shadows.

Corvel saw her and made a sound Wren had only ever heard from men watching ships go down.

"Isandro." Her name came out of him like something being extracted. "You shouldn't — I signed the guild to you. Before they took me. Did you find the paper? It's real, it's witnessed, it's yours, I did it so there'd be — so something of it would survive, and then I gave them your name anyway, because I'm a coward, and you should leave me here, you should *leave me*—"

"Master Corvel," Isandro said, in the voice she used for frightened animals and frightened accountants, which Wren suspected was the same voice, "you're feverish, you're freezing, and you're wrong about at least two of those things. Come out of the cage."

"I betrayed you."

"Yes," Isandro said. "I know. I've read the entry. I've had time to think about it." She held out her hand. "Here's what I decided. You don't get to decide what you're worth anymore. That's not how ledgers work. Someone else reads them and finds the truth. So you'll come with us, and you'll live, and someday — maybe a long someday — you'll do one thing that costs you something, and we'll call it a start. Those are the terms. Take them or freeze."

For a moment Corvel just stared at her, and Wren watched something move behind his eyes that she recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors: the specific humiliation of being forgiven by someone you'd decided had every right not to be.

He took her hand.

* * *

Getting him out was the easy part, in the end. Getting away was the part where Wren earned her reputation.

The nineteen-year-old captain figured it out alarmingly fast — Wren granted him that much — and raised a shout that brought six guards stumbling out of their tents with swords and no plan. What stopped them wasn't steel. It was Isandro, standing in the open with snow starting to fall around her, speaking to the hounds in a clear carrying voice:

"*Home. Go home. There's nothing here for you.*"

And the hounds went. Both of them, straight through the camp, past the guards, up the south road at a lope, and six armed men watched their own dogs desert them and lost, visibly, all faith in the evening. By the time the captain remembered he had a crossbow, they were in the trees, and by the time he organized a search, the snow was coming down thick enough to erase a cart, a horse, and four criminals plus one traitor, which Wren privately considered the single useful thing Corvel had ever contributed to an operation: his fever ran hot enough that they didn't need a lantern.

They didn't stop until dawn, in a hollow under a rock shelf, and Corvel collapsed off the cart bench into Isandro's arms like a puppet with cut strings.

"He needs a healer," Ossa said, feeling his forehead with the back of her wrist. "Or a miracle. Preferably both, and soon."

"There's a shepherd's bothy half a day south," Vesek said. "I marked it on the route. Stone walls, turf roof, usually stocked. We can hold there through the worst of the weather."

"And after the weather?" Wren asked.

Vesek looked at the sky, which had gone the color of dirty wool, and said nothing, which was answer enough.

Wren sat down hard on a stone and let herself, for exactly sixty seconds, feel the full weight of what she'd agreed to. Eight guards evaded. A prisoner stolen from the crown. A dying man in the cart who had sold them all and would probably sell them again if the price got low enough and his fear got high enough. Snow coming in like a debt collector.

"You did a stupid thing last night," she said to Isandro, who was wrapping Corvel in every blanket they owned.

"I know."

"It's going to cost us. Winter, hunters, all of it. He's dead weight and worse."

"I know," Isandro said again. She tucked the blanket under Corvel's chin with a tenderness that made Wren's chest hurt in a way she refused to examine. Then she looked up, and her eyes were steady and clear and absolutely certain. "But Wren — if we'd left him, I'd have known, every day for the rest of my life, exactly what I was. And I didn't want to know that. I'd rather carry him."

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Thought of a warehouse in Saltgate, eleven years ago, and a debt she'd sworn she'd never let anyone collect, and the strange discovery of the last few weeks that debts could be carried together and weigh less.

"Well," she said finally. "Someone has to teach him to be better, and it's clearly not going to be me. I haven't got the patience. I'd charge him."

Isandro smiled — tired, real, the first proper smile Wren had seen from her in days. "You'd itemize it."

"Hourly," Wren confirmed. "With interest."

Corvel stirred, moaned, and opened his eyes a slit. "Halloway," he whispered. "The ledger. The Vessany ledger. Page forty-one. There's an entry I never showed you. Read it before—" His eyes rolled, fixed on Isandro, desperate. "Before winter closes the passes. Promise me you'll read page forty-one."

Isandro and Wren exchanged a look over his feverish body.

"What's on page forty-one?" Isandro asked.

But Corvel had already gone under again, breathing in shallow rattles, and the snow kept falling, and somewhere south of them a shepherd's bothy waited with stone walls and, Wren sincerely hoped, a locked chest of brandy — because the sky had the look of a winter that intended to arrive early, and mean it, and stay.
