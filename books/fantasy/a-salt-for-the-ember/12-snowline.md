## 12. Snowline

The snow came in the night like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy, and no interest in anyone's plans.

Ossa knew snow. She had grown up on a river that froze in patterns the old women could read like scripture: *thin ice with a white bloom means the current's gone shy; black ice with a singing edge means stay home.* The Vessany river had been her whole education, her church, and later, when it drowned along with everything else, her grief. So she was the first one awake in the grey dark of the bothy, listening to the wind change its mind against the door, and she knew before she opened her eyes that they were not walking anywhere today.

"Snow," she announced to the room.

"Thank you, Ossa," Wren said from under three blankets, her voice muffled and murderous. "I had wondered what that white stuff was."

"It's early snow," Ossa said. "Hard snow. The kind that comes down meaning something." She said this with the particular gravity of a woman whose entire homeland had been erased by weather and bad treaties, and who therefore felt entitled to pronounce on meteorology.

From the corner where he'd been sleeping sitting up—a habit Isandro had noticed days ago and wisely never mentioned—Vesek opened his eyes. Even defected, even hollowed out, he still woke like a soldier: instantly, silently, hand drifting toward a knife that wasn't there anymore because Wren had confiscated it at the border village and refused to give it back. "Roads?"

"Roads are done," Ossa said. "For two days, maybe three. The high passes will take a week to fill and another week to become passable again. We're here until the sky gets bored."

Isandro sat up slowly, hair full of straw, looking like an accountant who had been dragged through a hedge backward—which was, Ossa reflected, essentially accurate. "Here being?"

Ossa gestured around the bothy with the air of a woman presenting a palace.

It was not a palace. It was a shepherd's shelter, dry-stone walled, half sunk into the hillside like something trying to hide. One room. One hearth. A roof of turf and old slate that groaned musically whenever the wind remembered it. There were three stools, a table scarred by a century of knife-sharpening, a stack of peat cut by some shepherd who presumably wouldn't come up until spring thaw, and a smell of ancient sheep that had soaked so deep into the stone it had practically achieved citizenship.

"There are worse places," Vesek offered.

"You would say that," Wren said. "You've slept in ditches for a living."

"I've slept in ditches *professionally*," Vesek corrected, with the ghost of dignity. "There's a difference."

* * *

They spent the morning making the bothy livable, because that was the unspoken law of their little company now: everyone worked, no one asked why you knew how to do the thing you were doing.

Wren, naturally, claimed the fire. She built it the way she did everything—with a smuggler's economy, three moves ahead, coaxing flame out of damp peat and a handful of pine cones she'd been hoarding in her pack like a dragon with poor taste in treasure. Ossa watched her crouch over the hearth, sleeves pushed up, swearing gently at the smoke until it obeyed, and thought—as she had thought several times now, always with a small internal flinch—that Wren Halloway looked most like herself when she was tending something. Not guarding. Not scheming. Tending.

Isandro took inventory, which was Isandro's version of prayer. She laid out their provisions on the scarred table with the solemnity of a guild audit: hard bread, dried fish, a knot of salt pork, a bag of oats, and—produced last, held up to the light like evidence—the better part of a wheel of cheese that Brammel's village had pressed on them at parting.

"This is four days of food," Isandro said. "If we're careful."

"If we're careful and nobody has second helpings," Wren said pointedly, looking at Vesek.

"I have a soldier's appetite."

"You have a soldier's *everything*," Wren said. "Appetite, snoring, opinions about knots—"

"My opinions about knots are correct."

"They're pedantic. There's a difference."

Ossa, meanwhile, did what she always did when landlocked: she went looking for water. Every place had it, if you knew how to ask. Behind the bothy, half buried in drift, she found the spring—a stone-rimmed eye of water still running dark and patient under the new snow, and when she knelt beside it and put her palm flat on the surface, the cold climbed her arm like a cat greeting someone it vaguely remembered.

*Hello,* said the water. Water always said hello first. Rivers said hello the way crowds do, all at once, a thousand voices braided into one long sentence. Springs said hello quietly, like shy children.

*Hello,* Ossa answered, as she always did, and as always the water had nothing else to say, because water only ever wanted to talk about itself, and this spring had led a very short life. But it told her the depth of the frost coming, the way the wind ran along this hillside, where the drifts would build—and when she went back inside and reported all this, arranging stones around the hearth to reflect heat toward the sleeping places, positioning them against the draft under the door, nobody remarked on it. That was the other law of their company: nobody remarked on anything. Wren talked to locks until they gave up. Isandro read animals' faces like ledger entries. Vesek could tell you the weight of a man by his footfalls. And Ossa listened to water.

They were four people who should never have found each other, bound together by debts none of them could pay, pretending very hard that this was a business arrangement.

* * *

By afternoon the storm had committed fully. Snow came sideways off the ridge in white ropes, erasing the world past ten paces, and the temperature dropped the way a trap drops. They sealed the door as best they could, fed the fire, ate carefully, and then faced the oldest problem known to travelers stuck indoors:

Nothing to do.

Wren solved it the Wren way, by producing a set of bone dice from somewhere in her clothing that Ossa chose not to investigate, and proposing stakes.

"We don't gamble," Isandro said.

"You gamble constantly. You gamble every time you open one of those ledgers and hope the entry makes sense. I'm merely formalizing it."

"With what stakes? We own nothing."

"Debts," Wren said, and grinned, and Ossa saw Isandro's expression flicker—the look of a woman who had just heard a word she loved spoken by a mouth she distrusted. "We each owe the Ember Guild something impossible, yes? So we play for IOUs. Small ones. Loser owes winner a truth."

"A truth," Isandro repeated flatly.

"One true thing, spoken aloud, no dodging. Cheapest currency there is, and the rarest." Wren rattled the dice. "Or are you frightened of a fair trade, Brightwater?"

Ossa watched Isandro weigh it—watched the accountant behind those grey eyes run the numbers and find, reluctantly, that refusing would cost more than playing. "Fine," Isandro said. "But I want it noted that this is extortion."

"Noted," said everyone, including, to his own visible surprise, Vesek.

They played through the afternoon while the storm sang against the walls. Ossa was terrible at dice—she suspected, correctly, that Wren cheated, though proving it was beyond her—but she won twice anyway, by accident, through the sheer chaotic grace of a woman who had spent years arguing with rivers and therefore understood that the universe sometimes simply gave you things out of spite.

Her first win was off Vesek. He paid his debt immediately and without drama, the way soldiers pay: he told them about his brother. A younger brother, a farm boy, who had been taken by the hunt fifteen years ago—not as quarry but as bait, stationed in a wood where a beast-speaker was rumored to pass, and forgotten there overnight in winter, and found frozen. "They wrote it up as exposure during training," Vesek said, staring into the fire. "Which was technically true. I signed the report myself. My first signature on a report that lied." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if checking whether they still belonged to him. "That's my debt. Not money. I owed that boy a report that told the truth, and I never filed it."

The fire popped. Nobody said anything kind, because Vesek would have broken apart under kindness, so instead Wren dealt the next hand briskly and said, "Your dice-throwing is ugly and superstitious," and Vesek said, "So is your shuffling," and the moment passed without breaking.

Ossa lost to Isandro next, and paid her truth more slowly, because hers was heavier in the mouth.

"You know about Vessany," she said. "Everyone does, sort of. Everyone knows it drowned." She turned her cup in her hands. The ferrywoman's cup, chipped at the lip, carried from a country with no name. "What nobody knows is that I was supposed to be on the last boat. My father held the ferry at the customs crossing—he was the last ferryman of the Sere, our river. When the waters came up—the floods, the breaking of the levees after the crown pulled the maintenance crews—we got everyone we could across. Boatload after boatload. Old women, children, goats, a piano once, which I still think about." Someone made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth; Ossa suspected Wren. "And then there was one boat left and two of us on the bank, and my father put me in it and stayed. He said somebody had to hold the rope. He held the rope." She looked at the fire. "The debt I owe the guild isn't coin either. I paid Corvel everything I had to buy passage south afterward, and what's left unpaid is—" her hands moved, helpless, describing a shape that had no edges, "—it's the rest of my life, owing a man a favor equal to a father. You can't balance that book. I've tried. The arithmetic doesn't close."

She hadn't meant to say all of it. She'd meant to give them one clean sentence and sit back down. But the storm outside and the fire inside and the sheer accumulated exhaustion of weeks on the road had loosened something, and the words had come out the way floodwater comes out—past any gate you thought you'd built.

When she finished, Isandro reached across and put one hand flat on the table between them, palm down, the way Ossa herself greeted springs. An accountant's gesture, and somehow also a river-woman's. Ossa covered it with her own hand and let go of her cup.

* * *

The ritual, once started, would not stop.

It happened gradually, the way snow happens—one flake, then the sense of it everywhere. Wren lost a hand to Isandro and grumbled her truth out sideways, not looking at anyone: that she'd had a crew once, a real crew, before the guild's debt made her a solo operator, and that she'd gotten them caught to save a shipment she could have abandoned, and that the debt she owed the Ember Guild wasn't the fine—it was what she'd promised her imprisoned quartermaster she'd do with her freedom once she earned it back, and hadn't yet, because earning freedom back and spending it on promises were different skills. "So there," she said, aggressive, daring anyone to make it tender. "Now you all know I'm soft. Use it against me, see if I care."

"Nobody's going to use it against you," Isandro said.

"That's worse," said Wren, and threw the dice with unnecessary violence.

Then Isandro lost—to Vesek, of all people, who threw the bones with a soldier's luck and collected his truth with a soldier's directness: "A true thing. Go."

Isandro sat with it longer than Ossa expected. The firelight moved on her face. When she spoke, her voice was quiet in the way of someone reading a difficult entry aloud.

"I can hear them," she said. "All of them. Always. Birds, horses, dogs, the mule we bought and sold, the oxen in every field we pass. They talk constantly, mostly about food and fear, and I cannot stop hearing it, and I have never once told anyone the whole of it, because the whole of it is this: it's lonely. Being the only person in every conversation. You're surrounded by voices and none of them are for you." She folded her hands. "Until recently."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the others. Warmer. More dangerous.

"Well," said Wren, roughly, into it. "This has been the worst game of dice in recorded history."

"Yes," said Isandro, with feeling.

"Same time tomorrow?" said Ossa.

* * *

Night settled in properly. Vesek took first watch out of pure habit, though there was nothing to watch except snow, and Ossa lay in her blanket near the fire listening to the storm work the hillside and thinking about doors.

Brammel's warning sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: *the door takes payment in what you love most.* She had turned it over and over on the road since, testing it for flaws, the way she'd test ice. And tonight, listening to the breathing of these three improbable people—the accountant, the smuggler, the hunter-turned-deserter—she understood, with the clarity of exhausted honesty, exactly what the warning meant and exactly whom it threatened.

Because Ossa's love was already paid. Her father had spent it holding a rope. What remained in her heart was smaller and stranger: a river that no longer existed, a grief she carried like ballast, a chipped cup. Whatever the door took from her, she could survive the taking. She had practice.

But Wren's whole face changed when she talked about her quartermaster, about freedom, about the promise she hadn't kept. And Isandro—Isandro had a ledger entry in her pocket, Ossa had seen it, the transfer papers Corvel had signed over before his arrest, the Ember Guild itself folded into inheritance, and Isandro touched that fold of paper the way other people touch amulets. And Vesek had nothing left in the world except whatever honor he was trying to rebuild, plank by plank, out here in the snow.

*What you love most.*

Ossa stared at the fire and did the arithmetic she was best at: not numbers, but weights. Four travelers. Four loves. One door.

Across the room, Wren shifted in her sleep and muttered something that sounded suspiciously like *loaded dice*, and Isandro, half-asleep against her pack, murmured back, "Noted," and Vesek stood his watch and pretended not to smile.

Outside, the snow kept falling, filling the passes, closing the world behind them and the world ahead, leaving only the bothy and the fire and the four of them suspended between countries like a held breath.

Ossa closed her eyes. Tomorrow, or the day after, the sky would get bored and the road would open again, and they would walk north toward a frozen marsh and a customs house that demanded papers, and one of them—maybe all of them—would have to learn what their hearts actually weighed.

In the last hour of her watch, trading places with Vesek near dawn, Ossa heard it: far off under the wind, a sound she knew from a lifetime of listening to moving water.

Ice cracking. Long and slow, out on the marshes to the north.

Something down there was waking up—or letting people in.
