## 13. The Hound Who Chose

Vesek had trained eleven hounds in his years with the Crown Hunt, and he could read each of them the way a priest reads scripture. Bracken told him weather by the set of her ears. Old Sorrel announced rivers a mile before they appeared, sneezing at the damp like a fussy dowager. And Ash—the lead hound, the best nose in three provinces, the animal they'd named after embers because he seemed to carry fire under his skin—Ash spoke to him in the small grammar of shoulders and tail.

For six days, that grammar had been saying *no*.

Not laziness. Not illness. Ash ate well, slept well, ran the trails with vigor. But every time the scent line bent toward the four fugitives—the beast-speaker and her ragged escort of debtors—Ash would cast once, twice, and then sit down in the snow as if he were a statue someone had commissioned for exactly this spot.

"Up," Vesek said, for perhaps the hundredth time. "Up, boy. Work."

Ash looked at him. It was not a dog's look. Dogs looked at their handlers the way children look at parents: with love and a certain hopeful stupidity. This was something else. This was the look of a colleague who had been overruled in a meeting and intended to say so.

The other five hounds milled about, whining, torn between the trail and their leader's judgment. The trail itself was plain enough—a churned line through the snow where four sets of boots had passed, heading north along the old shepherd tracks above the tree line. Plain, but strange too. Vesek had hunted smugglers, poachers, one memorable tax-dodger who had disguised himself as his own wife for eleven months. Fugitives left chaos behind them: dropped things, doubled paths, panic written in footprints.

These four left almost nothing. Once, someone had paused to right a fallen cairn. Once—and this had troubled him for two days—someone had buried a dead crow properly, with a little mound of stones, before moving on. Who buries a crow while fleeing the Crown's riders?

He knew who. The same person whose voice, on the high road nine days ago, had turned his pack from wolves into puppies.

Vesek hadn't seen it happen. He'd been thrown from his horse when the ambush sprang—or rather, when it failed to spring; the hounds had simply stopped, all six of them at once, mid-lunge, like dancers missing a beat. By the time he'd gotten his feet under him, the fugitives were gone into the rocks, and his hounds were sitting in a neat row, tails sweeping snow, looking up at him with the expressions of animals waiting to be told a secret.

But Rennet, his junior handler, had seen. Rennet swore on his mother's grave that the woman's lips had moved, and that she'd spoken no language Rennet knew, and that the hounds had understood anyway. Rennet reported it in writing, as protocol demanded. The report went south. Somewhere in a warm office in the capital, a clerk filed it, and somewhere above that clerk, a decision was made, and now Vesek carried new orders in his breast pocket:

*On confirmation of vocal command of beasts, subject is to be taken alive. Handlers are reminded that the subject's gift is transferable only through prolonged contact. Do not permit the hounds extended proximity to the subject.*

*Extended proximity.* As if the gift were a fever you caught by sitting near someone. As if his hounds might come home talking.

He thought about that order more than he wanted to, especially at night, especially since Ash had started refusing.

* * *

They cornered the fugitives on the fourth morning, at a place where the shepherd track narrowed between a rock face and a long drop into white nothing. Vesek saw them first from a ridge below: four figures moving single file, slow with cold. The tall smuggler woman in front, knife out of habit even though her hands were blue. A broad-shouldered ferrywoman carrying most of everyone's supplies without complaint. A gaunt man in ruined fine clothes—Corvel, the guildmaster himself, walking like a man made of wet paper. And at the rear, small and hooded, the one the whole machinery of the Crown was grinding toward: Isandro Brightwater, keeper of ledgers, speaker to beasts.

Vesek signaled his team wide. Six hounds, two handlers besides himself, and the high ground. Textbook. He'd run this exact maneuver forty times. In his head, he heard the closing words of every report he'd ever filed: *subject secured without serious injury.*

Then he gave the release signal, and Ash did not move.

"Ash." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "Ash, *heel*—"

The lead hound stood at the crest of the ridge, snow smoking off his back, and looked down at the four figures below. And then—he had never done this, not once in seven years, not in training, not ever—Ash sat down facing Vesek, planted like a monument, and *barked*.

One bark. Deep, deliberate, unmistakable. The bark that meant, in the old grammar every handler learned first: *no.*

Rennet, coming up the slope on the flank, released his own pair. They took three strides and stopped, circling back to sit beside Ash, flanking him like honor guards. Five hounds now, sitting in the snow, refusing. Only Sorrel, old and half-deaf and loyal past the point of sense, bounded down the slope toward the fugitives—and then, halfway there, pulled up short, sneezed violently, and trotted back as if embarrassed.

"What in the Crown's name—" Rennet began.

"Hold!" Vesek shouted, too loud, and his own voice frightened him.

Below, the fugitives had stopped. The smuggler had her blade up. The ferrywoman had stepped in front of the guildmaster. And the small hooded figure—Isandro—had turned around and was looking straight up at the ridge. Straight at the dogs. Straight, Vesek was nearly sure, at him.

The wind carried her voice up. It wasn't loud. It wasn't a command or a spell or anything Rennet could put in a report. It was just a woman speaking clearly into the cold air, the way you speak to an old friend across a street.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I know what they're making you do. He's telling me."

*He.* Ash.

And Ash—for the first time in his working life—left his post. He walked down the slope, unhurried, past the frozen tableau of handlers, past Rennet's open mouth, straight down to the narrow track, and sat in front of Isandro Brightwater like a pilgrim arriving at last at a shrine he'd walked his whole life to find.

She knelt. She pushed back her hood. She said something to him that none of them could hear, low, just for the dog. Ash leaned into her, eyes closing, tail giving one slow sweep against the snow.

Vesek stood on the ridge with six hounds who would not hunt and orders in his pocket he suddenly could not read, as if the ink had run, as if the words had always been in a language he'd only pretended to know.

* * *

What happened next happened slowly, which Vesek would remember later as the strangest part of it. No swords came out. Nobody screamed. The smuggler kept her knife up but didn't advance; the ferrywoman watched the handlers with the flat patience of someone who had drowned once already and found it instructive. Corvel sat down on a rock because standing had become optional for him.

Isandro stayed kneeling in the snow with the lead hound of the Crown Hunt resting his great ember-colored head on her knee, and she talked to him, and the dog answered in his way, and whatever passed between them, it took a long time, long enough for the cold to work into everyone's bones, long enough for Vesek to walk down the slope because standing on the ridge had become unbearable.

He stopped ten paces from her. Protocol said twenty. Protocol also said a lot of things.

"You're the handler," she said, looking up. She had snow in her lashes and tiredness all the way down to something underneath tiredness, some older stratum of it. "Vesek. She told me your name—Bracken did, when you called her in from the flank on the second day. You call all of them by name, and softly, even when you're angry. She likes that. She says you're the only one who never called her 'the brindle.'"

Vesek's throat worked. "Hounds don't talk."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "They don't."

"They can't—what did she tell you? About me?"

"That you sleep badly. That you check on them in the night even when you're off duty. That when Sorrel went lame two winters ago and the Guildmaster wanted him put down, you carried him home yourself and argued for a month until they let him retire." She tilted her head. "That you taught them all their names like they were children learning letters."

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's just handling. Good handling. You learn their names so commands land clean."

"Is that why?" she asked, and there was nothing cruel in it, which was somehow worse. Just honest curiosity, the question of someone auditing an account.

Below them, Rennet was shouting something about reinforcements being a half-day behind, about the reward, about duty. The words slid off the mountainside. Vesek heard himself say, "Why is my lead hound committing treason?"

"He isn't." She scratched Ash behind the ear, and the dog sighed like a bellows. "He's doing the thing you taught him. You told me—no. Bracken told me. You say it every time you turn them onto a trail: *only if it's right.* You've said it ten thousand times. You thought it was just words. Words are never just words. Not to them. They keep accounts too, handler. Every word you've ever spent with them, it's all still on the books."

"The order says—" His hand went to his breast pocket and stopped. "The order says you're to be taken alive. For study."

"I know. I heard you reading it to yourself last night by the fire. I'm sorry. I wasn't trying to listen. He was listening, and he tells me things, and I don't always know how to make him stop." She rose, brushing snow from her knees, and looked at him with an expression he would think about for the rest of his life—not pity, not contempt, but the look of a bookkeeper finding an entry she wished weren't there. "You're a good man, Handler Vesek. That's the worst position to be in right now."

Behind him, Rennet drew steel. "Master Vesek! Are we taking them or not? There's a bounty—"

"There's a bounty," Vesek repeated. The words tasted like coins left too long in a mouth. He turned. Rennet stood with his sword out and his two hounds at his heels, except his two hounds were not at his heels; they had drifted, subtly, unmistakably, to stand nearer to Ash. Near the woman. Rennet hadn't noticed yet. Rennet had never learned their names like letters.

"Put it away, Rennet."

"She's worth more than both our pensions. She's worth a house, Master Vesek. A house with a garden."

"I know what she's worth." Vesek looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. "That's not the same as what she costs."

* * *

It ended, in the end, the way most things end: badly for the ambitious and quietly for everyone else. Rennet, seeing which way his hounds' shoulders pointed, made the calculation young men with gardens in their eyes make, and went down the mountain alone to meet the reinforcements and claim sole credit. Vesek watched him go and felt nothing so much as relief, thin and cold as the air.

Which left him standing on a frozen track with the four most wanted people in Vessany's memory, six hounds, and no orders he was willing to follow.

"So," said the smuggler, lowering her knife at last. She had a face like weather—everything moving underneath, nothing wasted on top. "Handler. What happens now? Because I'll be honest, we've got a schedule, and it doesn't include being arrested, and Ossa here has been looking at your rations since you crested that ridge."

"I have not," said the ferrywoman, who had absolutely been looking at the rations.

Vesek opened his mouth. Closed it. Here was the thing he had not let himself think on the ridge, the thing that had been circling him for nine days like a bird too cautious to land: he had read the reports from the capital. He knew what happened to the gift-bearers they took alive. Everyone in the Hunt knew; they just used the word *study* and let the word do its work. He had signed the transport orders himself, twice. He had told himself both times that the alternative was worse, that the law was the leash and he was the hand on it, and a leash in kind hands was mercy—

Ash bumped his leg. Looking up at him. Waiting, as he had waited ten thousand times, for the words: *only if it's right.*

"I can't go back," Vesek said. It came out smaller than he meant. He tried again. "If I go back, I report that we lost you in the storm. If I stay—" He laughed, and it was not a happy sound. "I don't know how to stay. I've been the hand on the leash for nineteen years. I don't know what I am without it."

"You're the man who buried a crow," said the small hooded figure, who was apparently keeping closer track of them than any ledger. Wait—that was Wren's observation, offered dryly. Isandro just smiled. "We noticed. Someone buried a crow back on the pass, stones and all. We argued about which of us did it. Turns out it was you?"

"It was bad luck, a dead crow on a trail," Vesek said stiffly. "Bad for the dogs' morale."

"Of course," said Wren.

"He gave it proper words, too," Isandro added solemnly. "According to Bracken."

"*Bracken*—"

"Come with us," Isandro said. Simply. The way she seemed to do everything—no theater, no persuasion, just an entry made plainly in the book. "Not because you owe us anything. Because you're owed. Nineteen years, and the Crown never once paid you in truth. Come with us as far as the border, at least, and see what you're walking toward before you decide. That's fair dealing. That's accounting."

"And the dogs?"

Isandro looked at the six of them, sitting in the snow in a loose crescent, watching the negotiation with the attentive patience of creditors. "The dogs," she said, "decided some time ago. I'd stop asking them."

Vesek stood a long moment. The wind moved. Somewhere far to the south, small and getting smaller, Rennet was running toward a reward and a house with a garden, and the machinery of the hunt was grinding northward behind him, patient as winter.

"All right," Vesek said finally, and his voice steadied as he said it, surprising him again. "All right. But I'm leading. None of you can navigate in snow, I've watched you for four days, you walk like soup."

"We walk like *soup*—" Wren began.

"And the hounds stay off-leash," he went on, over her. "All of them. Whatever this is—" he gestured vaguely at Isandro, at Ash, at the whole impossible arrangement, "—they chose. I won't put them back on the rope. That's my terms."

Isandro smiled, and it was the smile of someone closing a ledger with satisfaction. "Accepted. Witnessed?"

"Witnessed," said Ossa, already lifting the first sack of confiscated rations onto her shoulder.

"Witnessed," said Corvel, from his rock, sounding for the first time in days like a man rather than a confession.

Ash pressed his warm side against Vesek's leg, the old greeting, the oldest word they had between them, and Vesek put his hand on the ember-colored head and did not say *only if it's right*, because for the first time in nineteen years, he didn't need to ask.

They had made it perhaps a mile north, the seven of them and the six hounds threading up the white track, when Bracken stopped dead at the ridgeline, ears forward, and looked back the way they'd come.

Vesek turned. Far down the valley, dark against the snow, riders. Many riders. Moving fast.

"Well," said Wren, unshouldering her knife with the cheerfulness of a woman greeting an old acquaintance. "That'll be Rennet's reinforcements. Anyone have a plan, or shall we continue walking like soup?"
