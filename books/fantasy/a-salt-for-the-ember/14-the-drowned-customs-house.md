## 14. The Drowned Customs House

The marsh announced itself three days before we reached it.

First it was the smell—cold water and old stone, like a cellar that had decided to become a landscape. Then the birds changed. I couldn't name half of them, but they spoke to each other in short, clipped phrases that sounded less like birdsong and more like sentries passing along the word *strangers*.

By the second day, Ossa had stopped talking almost entirely. She walked at the front of our ragged line with her ferry pole over her shoulder like a soldier's pike, and when Wren asked her if she was all right, she said, "I know this smell," in a voice that closed the subject like a ledger cover snapping shut.

Vesek walked at the rear with his hounds—three of them now, since Bracken had gone lame crossing the last ridge and rode on the pack mule like a furry dignitary reviewing his subjects. The hounds had not tried to hunt us since the morning their lead, Sallow, had sat down in the road and refused to move one more step toward me. Vesek still flinched whenever they looked at me, as if expecting them to remember their training and be ashamed of themselves.

"They keep looking at you," he said on the third morning, low, so only I could hear. "Like you're a sermon."

"I'm not a sermon," I said. "I'm an accountant."

"Most sermons are shorter."

It was the closest thing to humor he'd produced since abandoning the crown's service, so I counted it as progress and wrote it mentally in the margin of the day: *Handler Vesek — improving, slowly. See also: still carries a knife he doesn't need.*

Wren brought up the middle because she refused to walk anywhere except where she could watch everyone. She'd been a smuggler long enough to distrust open ground, treeline, weather, other people's mules, and—on principle—the concept of destinations. But even she quickened her pace on the third afternoon, when the marsh opened ahead of us and we saw what stood in it.

"There," said Ossa. And her voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring.

* * *

The customs house of Vessany stood on a rise that had once been dry land and was now an island in a frozen marsh. The ice held the reeds upright like a congregation frozen mid-prayer. The building itself was stone, squat, and stubborn—a customs house is by nature a building that refuses to acknowledge its own unimportance—and it wore its drowning with dignity. Water stained its walls in dark tide-marks, a history of every flood written on it like entries in a book no one had bothered to close.

A flagpole rose from its roof. No flag. Just the rope, knocking against the pole in the wind, patient as a clerk waiting to be acknowledged.

"It should be under ten feet of water," Ossa said. Her hand was on the ferry pole so hard her knuckles had gone white. "When the kingdom drowned, everything on the border went under. My grandmother told me. She was born on the Vessany side of the river, before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before there wasn't a side."

"Then why is it standing?" Vesek asked.

"Because," said Wren, in the flat tone of someone who has learned to expect the worst from buildings, "it hasn't finished with us yet."

We crossed the ice carefully. It creaked but held; the cold had been hard enough these last weeks to make a floor of the whole marsh, though here and there black water showed through thin places, watching us go by. As we neared the island, I noticed the path. A causeway of flat stones ran from the ice up to the customhouse door, each stone swept clean of snow.

Swept. Recently.

Nobody said anything. We are, by now, a family of people who have learned that the scariest thing in any landscape is evidence of housekeeping.

The door itself was tall and narrow, painted a green that might once have been official and had faded into something closer to memory. Above it, carved into the lintel in letters worn soft by salt and time:

**VESSENY CUSTOMS — ALL THINGS CROSSING MUST BE DECLARED**

Below that, smaller, someone had added at some point, in a different hand:

***including grief***

"Well," said Wren. "That's cheerful."

I touched the lintel. The stone was warm.

Not sun-warm—it was the shadowed side, and the afternoon was grey enough that the sun had given up on the region entirely. Warm the way a hand is warm. Warm the way the lark's cage had been warm, back in the guildhouse, the first time I'd heard it speak. I pulled my fingers back and looked at them, and then at the door, and felt the particular dread of a person who has spent her life around impossible things and knows that they are rarely *finished* being impossible just because you've arrived.

"We could go around," Vesek offered. "There must be another way across the old border."

"There isn't," Ossa said quietly. "There never was. This was the way. Everything else was smugglers' tracks, and the smugglers' tracks went through the marsh, and the marsh ate them." She looked at the door with an expression I couldn't fully read—grief wearing anger's coat. "My debt," she said. "The river that refuses to carry me. That river died when Vessany died. It's under this ice somewhere. I've been looking for it my whole life without knowing it."

She stepped forward and knocked.

The knock echoed far longer than a building that size had any right to permit—as if the sound were traveling through corridors that hadn't existed since before the drowning, past offices full of dust and patience.

Then bolts moved. Many bolts. Somewhere inside, something cleared its throat.

* * *

The door opened onto a hallway lit by lamps that burned without oil—or rather, burned oil that had run out some decades ago and simply declined to let that be an obstacle. A counter ran across the hall at chest height, scarred and ink-stained, exactly like Master Corvel's front desk at the guild, if Corvel's desk had been dead a hundred years and kept working anyway.

Behind the counter stood the customs officer.

I say *stood*. It had the shape of a person—a tall, thin shape in a uniform whose colors had washed out of the world along with the country they served. Its face was the problem. Not horrifying, precisely. Worse than horrifying: *unfinished*, the way a word looks when you've stared at it too long. Features suggested rather than committed to. Eyes like two smudges of ink deciding whether to be eyes.

It looked at us. All five of us—counting the hounds, who pressed against Vesek's legs and made small unhappy sounds.

"Good afternoon," it said. Its voice was dry paper, and polite, and utterly exhausted in the way of someone who has been waiting a very long time for anyone to declare anything. "Welcome to the border of Vessany. Please have your documents ready."

Silence.

"Documents," Wren repeated.

"Papers. Declarations. Manifests of goods, persons, and intentions." The officer produced, from somewhere beneath the counter, a rubber stamp and an inkpad that was somehow still wet. It set them down with the tenderness of a woman laying out a child's christening gown. "Nothing crosses the border of Vessany undeclared. That is the law. It remains the law." A pause. "*Especially* now."

Ossa made a sound beside me, very small. I took her hand without looking away from the officer.

"We don't have papers," Wren said. "We have—" she gestured at herself, at all of us, at the general catastrophe of our situation, "—this."

"Then I regret to inform you," said the officer, and its unfinished face somehow managed to convey genuine sorrow, "that you cannot cross. You may wait in the queue."

"Where's the queue?"

"Behind you. It is currently empty. It has been empty for ninety-one years, four months, and eleven days. But it is a *queue*, and queues are hope, and hope is the last thing Vessany lost." It straightened its collar, which was more collar than shirt at this point. "Next."

"You can't just—" Wren began.

"Next," said the officer, to an empty room, with dignity.

It was at this point that I did the thing I probably should have done sooner, and certainly would regret doing later, and did anyway, because I am who I am.

I opened my pack, drew out the ledger of Vessany, and set it on the counter.

* * *

The change in the officer was immediate and terrible to watch.

Its ink-smudge eyes fixed on the book. The whole room seemed to lean toward it—the lamps brightened, the walls exhaled a breath of dust, and somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked like a joint remembering movement. The officer reached out with both hands, hesitated just above the cover, trembling.

"May I?" it whispered.

"It's yours," I said. "Or—it was your country's. We found it. It belongs to whoever kept the accounts."

The officer touched the ledger the way Ossa touched her ferry pole. The way Corvel touched the guild seal. The way, I suppose, I touch ledgers myself, which is to say like a person touching the only solid thing in a flooding world.

It opened the book. Turned pages with fingers that had no business having fingerprints and did anyway. Read. And read. And then it stopped, on a page near the end, and went very still.

"This entry," it said. "Who presented this entry to you?"

"A man named Corvel," I said. "Master of the Ember Guild. He signed the guild over to me before he was arrested. The ledger came with it."

"The Ember Guild." The officer looked up. For the first time its face seemed to come slightly more into focus, as if attention were a kind of light. "The guild that trades in impossible things." Something that might have been a smile moved across the unfinished features and got about halfway there before losing its nerve. "Of course it does. Of course the guild ended up holding Vessany's accounts. Who else would take them? Who else would *believe* them?"

"What does the entry say?" Ossa asked. Her voice shook. "Please. What does it say?"

The officer turned the book toward us.

The entry was old—older than the others, the ink browned, the handwriting cramped with urgency:

*Item: One (1) door out of Vessany.*
*Condition: functional. Location: sealed pending payment.*
*Terms of settlement: payable IN PERSON, IN SALT, BEFORE WINTER'S END.*
*Payable by: the heir of the account holder, or their assignee.*
*Note: the door takes its fee. It always has. Declare this plainly to any claimant. We do not sell what we do not name.*

Beneath it, in fresher ink—Corvel's hand, unmistakable, the little hooked letters he made when writing quickly:

*Declared plainly, per instructions. Let her decide with her eyes open.*

I read it twice. Three times. My eyes kept snagging on *the door takes its fee* the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"That's not terms," Wren said flatly. "That's a threat with a stamp on it."

"That," said the officer, closing the ledger gently, "is a declaration. Which brings me to the matter of your documents." It squared the book on the counter between us, and its voice shifted into something formal and final, the voice of a law that had outlived its country out of sheer stubbornness. "This ledger is a valid instrument of passage. It was Vessany's master account. Whoever holds it may present it as their passport."

Relief started to bloom in my chest. I want to record, honestly, that for one whole moment I thought we had won—that the accounting gods, for once, had smiled.

"But," said the officer.

Everyone in the room said "but" with it, in spirit.

"A passport," the officer continued, "bears one name. One traveler only. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic. The door's fee is paid in what the payer loves most—one love, one passage, one soul carried through. It cannot be split. It cannot be shared. It cannot be *argued with*." The ink-smudge eyes moved from face to face: Wren, jaw set; Ossa, hand white-knuckled on the pole; Vesek, standing very straight the way men do when they're afraid; me, presumably looking how I felt, which was like a ledger with the sums refusing to balance. "You may present the book. One of you will pass. The rest will remain on this side of the border, which is to say"—and here its voice gentled into something horribly kind—"in the world that erased us."

The lamp above the counter flickered. Outside, the wind moved through the frozen reeds, and the flag rope knocked against the empty pole, and somewhere beneath the ice, a drowned river waited under a hundred years of winter with one week left on its clock.

Winter's end. Before winter's end. I looked at the officer.

"How many days," I asked, "until winter ends?"

The officer consulted something internal—an almanac of a country that no longer had seasons, or perhaps just its own long patience.

"Seven," it said. "And the door, I should mention, keeps its own hours." It folded its hands on the counter, the picture of bureaucratic calm, and delivered the sentence that would follow me into my sleep for every night remaining:

"So. Who among you will declare?"

* * *

We didn't answer it that evening. We retreated to the ice's edge and camped in sight of the customhouse windows, where lamplight glowed in rooms that had no business glowing, and argued in the fierce whispers of people who love each other too much to shout.

"One traveler," Wren said. "One. So it's simple. None of us pays. We find another way or we die trying, and nobody feeds themselves to a door."

"There is no other way," Ossa said. "I told you. There was never another way."

"Then we die trying *creatively*."

"You can't solve everything with smuggling, Wren."

"I've gotten remarkably far on it."

Vesek said nothing for a long while. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man setting a knife on a table: "It would take mine. Honor's the thing I love most, and I haven't got any left worth keeping. It'd hardly notice."

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare volunteer like it's nothing. It's not nothing. That's the entire point of the fee."

And they all looked at me, then—Wren with her arms crossed, Ossa with tears she refused to acknowledge, Vesek with his hollowed-out honesty—and I realized, sitting there on a frozen marsh with seven days on the clock, that I already knew the answer, and had known it since I read the entry, and that the entry's last line had been addressed to me all along.

*Let her decide with her eyes open.*

Above us, in the customhouse window, the officer appeared briefly, silhouetted against the lamp. It raised one hand—not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. The way a clerk acknowledges the next name on a list.

Then it turned down the lamp, and the dark came on like a stamp coming down on wet ink.
