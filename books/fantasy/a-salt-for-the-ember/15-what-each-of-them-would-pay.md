## 15. What Each of Them Would Pay

Wren Halloway had spent her whole life learning what things cost. Not the prices chalked on market slates—those were lies told in comfortable handwriting—but the real costs. The cost of a forged seal was a year of her life if she was caught. The cost of a friend was roughly a year of her life even if she wasn't. The cost of a night's sleep on frozen ground was a morning of walking like a puppet with half its strings cut.

So she was, she felt, uniquely qualified to do the arithmetic that nobody else in their miserable little company seemed willing to do.

They had reached the customs house the previous evening, and now they sat in the frozen marsh at its edge, in the lee of a drowned boathouse whose roof poked out of the ice like the rib of some enormous patient animal. Ossa had a small fire going, fed on marsh-reeds that burned fast and bitter. Vesek was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Isandro sat with the Vessany ledger open on her knees, and she had been staring at the same page for the better part of an hour.

Wren counted it up while she chewed on dried plum that had gone the texture of leather with ambitions.

The customs officer—clerk, thing, whatever it was—had been very clear, in the way that only creatures who have waited a hundred years to say a sentence can be clear. *The door takes a tithe. Each traveler pays. Not coin. Not blood. What you love most.*

Wren had heard a lot of fine print in her day. She had written some of it. This was the worst clause she'd ever encountered, and she once signed a contract with a man who owned the river he drowned people in.

"So," she said, to break the silence before it froze solid around them like everything else. "Let's say the obvious out loud, shall we? Since we're all thinking it and pretending we aren't, which is the most exhausting game in the world."

Isandro looked up. Her eyes had that ledger-keeper's stillness that Wren had learned to distrust and then, against all her better judgment, to rely on.

"Say it, then," Isandro said.

"It's a trap." Wren held up fingers. "One: it wants what we love most, which means it wants the things we *can't* give, which means either we don't pay and we don't go, or we pay and we walk through as hollowed-out versions of ourselves. Two: there are four of us, and the customs house said the ledger works as a passport for one traveler. So even if we all pay, three of us might be standing on this side of a door in the middle of a marsh in winter with the crown's riders coming. Three—" she hesitated, because the third point was the one she'd been circling all night, "—three, I don't think it's lying. That's the worst part. I've been lied to by professionals. That clerk doesn't have it in him. He's just *telling us the rules.*"

"Thank you for that cheerful summary," Vesek said, without looking up from his knife. "I was worried we might get through the morning without despair."

"I don't do despair before noon," Wren said. "It's a rule. Despair is a night activity, like burglary."

Ossa laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody. She was sitting closest to the fire, her hands out, and Wren noticed she'd been staring at her own palms the way people stare at old scars.

"It's not a trap," Ossa said quietly. "It's a toll. There's a difference. A trap wants you to fail. A toll just wants to be paid." She flexed her fingers. "My river used to be like that. You paid it respect or you paid it everything, and it didn't care which, so long as you paid."

"Your river drowned," Wren said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Ossa's face did something complicated and closed, like a ledger snapping shut.

"Yes," Ossa said. "It did."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the marsh, ice settled with a groan, the sound of a country shifting in its sleep.

* * *

It was Vesek, of all people, who said the next thing, and he said it in the flat voice of a man reading out charges.

"The hounds will have our scent by midmorning. The relief riders will have reached the garrison by now. We have, at the outside, a day. Perhaps two if the snow comes back." He sheathed the knife. "So we should decide now. Who pays, and who goes through, and who stays."

"And who stays does what?" Wren asked. "Stands here and waves?"

"Whoever stays buys time," Vesek said. "The way I would have bought it, if you'd let me do it properly at the customs house the first time."

"No," Isandro said.

It was the first thing she'd said in an hour, and she said it the way she wrote in the ledger—no flourish, no argument, just the entry itself. *No.*

"You don't get to decide that," Vesek said. "You're the one the ledger names. You're the one the door is for."

"Which is exactly why I don't get to decide it," Isandro said, and there it was—the thing under the thing, the way her voice went careful when she was holding something heavy. "Don't you see? That's what it's *for.* It opens for the one who pays. And it takes what you love most. So before anyone starts volunteering to stand in the snow and be heroic, we should all be very honest about what it would take from each of us. Because I've been doing the arithmetic too, Wren. I just didn't want to say it out loud."

"Then say it now," Ossa said. "We're past the point where honesty is optional. Honesty is the last supply we've got."

Isandro closed the ledger. She looked at each of them in turn, and Wren had the strange sensation of being *read*—of being an entry in somebody's book, with all her debts laid out in a fair hand.

"Vesek," Isandro said. "You love your honor. You gave up your career, your name, your pack, everything you were trained to be, because you couldn't stomach what the hunt was. If the door takes what you love most, it takes the one clean thing you have left. The thing you built out of the wreckage. You'd walk through as a man with nothing left to be."

Vesek didn't move. But the knife-hand, the one resting on his knee, went very still.

"Ossa," Isandro went on, gentler. "Your river. It's gone, but you love it anyway. You love it more than anything—the water that wouldn't carry you, the banks that aren't there anymore. If you pay, you don't just give up a memory. You give up the grief. And your grief is—" she paused, searching, "—it's the shape of your whole heart. It's how you love everything else. Through it."

Ossa's hands closed slowly, like something drowning.

"And Wren." Isandro turned last, and Wren braced herself, because she already knew. Of course she knew. She'd known since the customs house, since the clerk said *what you love most* and Wren's stomach had dropped through the ice of her own body. "You love your freedom. You've paid for it your whole life. You've never belonged to anyone, never owed anyone, never stayed anywhere longer than the law required. If you pay—"

"—then I'm free of nothing and bound to everything," Wren finished. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was her one consistent talent. "Yes. I did the sums. I always do the sums."

"And you," Vesek said quietly, turning it back on Isandro. "What does the ledger-keeper love most?"

And there was the silence.

Wren watched Isandro's hands on the closed ledger and thought: *she's known since before any of us.* Because Wren had seen the paper Corvel had pressed into Isandro's hands before the hunters took him—the transfer of deed, the guild itself, signed and sealed, the whole impossible inheritance of the Ember Guild with its bottled storms and its ghost-debts and its last salt of a drowned kingdom, made over to a clerk's assistant who kept the books and talked to larks. Corvel had signed it away to save it, or to save her, or both, and then he'd been dragged out of his own guildhouse in chains, and now the guild lived in Isandro like a second heart.

"The guild," Isandro said. "Corvel gave it to me. It's mine. And it's the only home I've ever—" She stopped. Started again, in the ledger voice. "It's the only thing that was ever *mine.* Not assigned to me. Not borrowed. Mine."

"So the door wants the guild," Ossa said.

"The door wants the guild," Isandro agreed. "And here's the arithmetic none of you want to hear: I'm the only one who can pay without it destroying me. Because the guild was never meant to be *kept.* It's a ledger of debts. It's meant to move. And the rest of you—" her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it, "—the rest of you would be paying with the *self* you'd need on the other side. I'd be paying with a thing. A beautiful, impossible, precious thing, but a thing."

"That's not how love works," Wren said.

"Excuse me?"

"That's not how love *works,*" Wren repeated, standing up, because she couldn't argue properly sitting down; her whole body had to be in it. "You can't rank it. You can't say *mine's a thing so mine's cheaper.* The door doesn't take things, it takes what you *love,* and you love that guild like it's your own pulse, and if you hand it over you'll walk through that door with a hole where your heart used to file its paperwork. I've seen people give up the thing they loved. I've *been* the reason people gave up the thing they loved. It doesn't leave a hole. It leaves a *wound,* and wounds don't close just because you're standing in a magic valley."

"Then what's your proposal?" Vesek asked. "That nobody pays and we all freeze here philosophically?"

"My proposal," Wren said, "is that only one of us needs to go through."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"The ledger works as a passport for one traveler," Wren said. "One. So one of us goes through, finds whatever's in that valley, and comes back—or sends help, or opens the door wider, I don't know, I'm a smuggler, not a theologian. And the one who goes should be the one whose price is *bearable.*" She jabbed a thumb at her own chest. "Me. My freedom. You want to know a secret? I'd give it up anyway. I've been giving it up in installments since the day I met you lot. I stay when I should run. I come back when I'm paid to leave. I slept on frozen ground to keep a ledger-keeper's ears from freezing off, and nobody was *paying* me." She laughed, and it came out ragged. "My freedom's already half-spent. The door can have the rest. It's getting a bargain."

"No," said three voices at once, and the unanimity of it was so startling that Wren actually stepped back.

"Oh, that's rich," she said. "That is *rich.* You'll all refuse on my behalf but Isandro can march in and hand over her birthright because it's 'just a thing'? You're hypocrites with excellent manners."

"Yes," Vesek said, with the terrible calm of a man who had spent his life following orders and had finally found orders worth disobeying. "We are. That's what love is. It's hypocrisy with excellent manners. You taught me that, actually, when you came back for Corvel."

"I came back for Corvel because leaving a man in chains is bad for business."

"You came back," Vesek said, "because you couldn't not. And now you can't not. So we're at an impasse of everyone loving everyone too much to let them pay, which I believe is the definition of a family, and also the definition of a disaster."

Ossa made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. "My grandmother used to say that. That family is just a debt you can't settle and wouldn't want to."

"Your grandmother," Wren said, "sounds like she'd have loved the Ember Guild."

"She'd have burned it down," Ossa said, "with love."

* * *

They argued until the light went long and gold across the ice, and then they argued in the dark with the fire for a witness, and the argument went around and around like a cart with a broken wheel—Isandro insisting the guild was the only payable price, Wren insisting on her own candidacy with the fervor of a woman auctioning off her own heart and calling it a bargain, Vesek quietly restating his claim every quarter hour like a tax collector, Ossa saying almost nothing until, near midnight, she said the thing that stopped the wheel.

"I know why my river wouldn't carry me."

They all looked at her. She was staring into the fire, and her face was lit from below, and she looked, Wren thought, like a figurehead on a ship that had finally been allowed to speak.

"I never told anyone. Not even you two, that night in the bothy. I told you about the flood, about Vessany going under, about my family. But I never told you why the river *refused* me afterward. Every ferry I tried to work, every boat—the water went dead under me. Flat. Silent. Like a room full of people who've decided not to answer." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Because I was the one who opened the sluice gates."

Wren felt the cold come off the marsh in a new way.

"There was a storm. The levees were failing. The elders said open the relief channels to save the town, and I was the ferrywoman, and everyone trusted me with the water, so I opened them. And the relief channels took the wrong branch. Or I opened the wrong ones—I've never known, I've never let myself know—and the flood came through the low quarter first. My family's quarter." Her voice didn't break. It went *quiet,* which was worse. "The river didn't refuse me out of cruelty. It refused me because I couldn't stand to be carried by it. Every time it held me up, I remembered what it took. So it stopped holding me up, because I asked it to, without ever saying the words."

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say. Wren, who had a lie for every occasion, found her entire inventory useless.

"So," Ossa said at last, "when you all sit there doing your arithmetic—Wren's freedom, Vesek's honor, Isandro's guild—I want you to understand what mine would be. Not a memory. Not grief in the abstract. It would be my *forgiveness.* The one thing I have never been able to give myself. And if I pay it to that door—" she looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious, "—then I walk into Vessany's remnant forgiven, and my family's ghosts will be standing on the other side, and I will have to look at them *without* my guilt, and I don't know who that woman is. I don't know if she's me."

"That's not a reason not to pay," Vesek said softly. "That's the reason the door asks."

"Oh, don't you *dare* be wise," Ossa said, and threw a reed at him, and he caught it, and something in the four of them cracked—not breaking, Wren thought, but cracking the way ice cracks when the water underneath decides to move.

* * *

It was long past midnight when Isandro finally spoke the sentence that ended the argument, or rather, that revealed the argument had never had an ending, only a pause.

"I've been reading the ledger," she said. "The Vessany ledger. All night. And there's something none of you noticed, because you were all busy volunteering to die nobly, which I appreciate, by the way, it's the nicest thing anyone's ever done for me."

She turned the book around so the firelight fell across the page. Her finger rested on an entry near the back, in the old clerk's hand, the ink gone brown as old blood. Wren leaned in and read it, and read it again, because the first reading refused to make sense.

*Item: one door, outbound. Payable: in person, in salt, before winter's end. Note appended, later hand:* the tithe is not fixed. The door weighs what is offered against what is withheld. He who offers all and withholds nothing pays less than he who offers much and keeps a corner. *Signed:* the last clerk of Vessany, in perpetuity.

"The tithe is not fixed," Wren said slowly.

"It weighs what's withheld," Isandro said. "Which means the door doesn't want our most-loved thing the way a thief wants a purse. It wants our *whole* offering. It's not a price, it's a—a test of whether we can let go. Which means—" and here her voice did something Wren had never heard it do, which was to *hope,* recklessly, in public— "which means maybe it doesn't take the whole thing. Maybe it takes the *grip.* The holding-on. Maybe what it costs is not the guild but my belief that I'd be nothing without it. Not Wren's freedom, but her belief that freedom is the only thing keeping her alive. Not Ossa's forgiveness, but—"

"Her certainty that she deserves neither," Ossa finished, in a whisper.

"Or," said Vesek, in the voice of a man who had learned the hard way how hope gets used as bait, "or it's a clerk's note written a hundred years ago by a desperate man trying to comfort travelers he couldn't save, and we're reading tea leaves in a ledger because we're afraid."

"Yes," Isandro said. "Both. It's both. That's what every ledger entry ever written is—someone's best guess at the truth, in ink that outlives the guesser." She closed the book and held it against her chest, and Wren saw that her hands were shaking, and saw also that she was smiling. "But it's the only clause we haven't tried. And winter ends soon. And the riders are coming. So tomorrow we go to that customs house, and we offer—not one of us. *All* of us. And we offer with open hands, and we find out what the door weighs."

"And if it takes everything anyway?" Wren asked. "If the old clerk was wrong?"

Isandro looked at her, and Ossa, and Vesek, and Wren watched her do the thing she did best, the thing that had nothing to do with ledgers: she looked at her family and counted them, and found them worth any entry in any book.

"Then we'll have lost the things we love most," she said, "together. Which is more than most people manage."

It should have been a comfort. It almost was. Wren was actually, briefly, warm—there by the bitter fire, with the marsh groaning and the snow holding off and the door waiting in its drowned customs house like a debt about to come due.

Which was, of course, when the hound began to bark.

Not one of Vesek's old pack—those were leagues behind, and loyal to a different master now. This was a fresh voice, young and eager, out on the dark road, and it was joined by a second, and a third, and under the barking, faint but unmistakable, came the jingle of harness and the creak of many saddles.

Vesek was on his feet with his knife out before the first echo died. Ossa was killing the fire with her boot. Isandro had the ledger inside her coat.

"How many?" Wren breathed.

Vesek tilted his head, reading the dark the way Isandro read ink. "Too many," he said. "They didn't wait for morning. And Wren—" his voice dropped, urgent and strange, "—they're not coming for us."

"Then who—"

The barking crested, and beneath it, cutting through the night like a bell through fog, came a human voice, cracked and thin and unmistakably, horribly familiar, raised in the old guildhouse cadence of a man reading out an account:

"—I know the way! I know the door! I can *show* you where it is!"

Corvel.

Wren looked at Isandro and saw her face go white as the marsh ice, and thought, with the clarity of the truly doomed: *the darkest hour isn't when they find you. It's when they bring your heart along as a guide.*
