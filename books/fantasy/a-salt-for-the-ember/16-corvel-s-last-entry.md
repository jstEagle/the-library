## 16. Corvel's Last Entry

The snow came down like the sky was settling an old account, and Isandro sat in the shepherd's bothy with her hands wrapped around a clay cup of something Ossa insisted was tea and everyone else politely called *hot*.

Outside, the wind worried at the bothy's turf roof. Inside, Vesek was mending a harness strap with the grim concentration of a man who had spent twenty years doing terrible things efficiently and had recently discovered mending. Wren was asleep sitting up, which she claimed was a skill smugglers developed out of professional necessity, her boots crossed at the ankle and one hand resting near her knife even in dreams. The hound—Vesek's lead hound, who had chosen them over his handler before his handler chose them over his crown—lay across the doorway like a furry portcullis.

And Corvel's page lay on the table between Isandro and the firelight.

It had arrived that morning, which should have been impossible, and arrived anyway, because the Ember Guild had never much respected impossibility. A gull had brought it. A gull, at this altitude, in this weather, looking deeply put upon about the whole affair. It had dropped the oilskin packet in the snow outside the bothy, fixed Isandro with one yellow eye, and said—in the flat, transactional tone of a bird being paid by the delivery—

*"Guild business. Sign here."*

She had signed. She always signed. Then she'd fed it half their remaining salt pork, which it accepted as a gratuity rather than thanks, and gone back inside to open the packet with fingers that shook more than she wanted them to.

One page. Ledger paper, guild stock, the faint smell of ink and lamp oil that she would know anywhere, that she had breathed for eleven years in the counting room above the salt cellar. And on it, in Master Corvel's hand—the small, precise, miserly script of a man who counted every letter the way he counted every coin—a single entry.

She had read it perhaps forty times since dawn. She read it again now.

*Entry 4,411. Account: Brightwater, I. Asset transferred: the Ember Guild, whole and entire, per instrument signed and witnessed. Status: held in trust.*

*Correction.*

*Status: mislabeled.*

*The Guild is not an asset. The Guild is an expenditure. It was founded by people who had lost a country and needed somewhere to put what remained—the debts, the names, the grief—and they built it to be SPENT. Every coin, every favor, every impossible thing in those ledgers exists to be given away. That is the whole of it. That was always the whole of it.*

*I kept it instead. Eleven years I kept it, and taught you to keep it, and called the keeping stewardship. It was hoarding. I hoarded you too, girl. Forgive an old man his ledgers; they were the only children I let myself have.*

*They are breaking me slowly in the Saltcells. They ask questions. I have answered some. You know which ones. I will not insult you by asking forgiveness for that either—I asked the clerk who brought me my ink whether forgiveness could be entered as a debt, and he said no, and he is right. Some things are not payable. They can only be carried.*

*But hear the last of it, because I paid a great deal to have this page delivered and I refuse to die having wasted the postage:*

*An inheritance is not a thing you own. It is permission. The founders left you permission—to spend it, to spend YOURSELF, to walk through whatever door is waiting for you and not look back at the books. Close the account, Isandro. Spend the Guild. Spend it all. That is what it was FOR.*

*Yours in arrears,*
*C. Corvel, Master (retired, forcibly)*

*P.S. The lark in the third-floor cage. I never told you why we kept it. It was the first creature you ever spoke to, the day your mother brought you in from the rain. You were four. You told it its song was "very good but a bit sad." We kept it so there would be one witness in the building who knew what you were and loved you anyway. Set it free if you ever get the chance. It has earned retirement.*

Isandro set the page down very carefully, as though it might bruise.

"Well?" Wren asked. She hadn't been asleep after all. She was never actually asleep; Isandro was beginning to suspect sleep was simply another country Wren smuggled goods into and out of on her own schedule. "Bad news?"

"It's from Corvel."

Wren sat up fully at that, all pretense abandoned. Even Vesek looked over. The hound lifted his head off his paws.

"The man who sold your name to the hunters," Wren said, in the tone of someone reciting a legal document, "to save his own skin."

"Yes."

"The man whose arrest got the guildhouse burned and the ledgers seized."

"Those ledgers," Isandro said, "which he apparently considered a form of hoarding."

"The man," Wren pressed on, with the relentlessness of weather, "who took you in at fourteen—or whenever it was—taught you numbers instead of teaching anyone you were safe, worked you like a clerk-shaped ox, and never once, in eleven years, told you that he knew exactly what you were until it became useful to him."

"He also," Isandro said, "kept a lark for eleven years because I once told it its song was sad."

A silence. Ossa, who had been pretending to stir the tea, stopped stirring. "You *what*?"

Isandro hadn't meant to say it aloud. But the page had cracked something open in her chest, the way ice cracks on a river—not breaking, just admitting that spring was coming whether the river liked it or not. "When I was four. Before my mother died. She brought me to the guildhouse out of the rain and I talked to a caged lark in the counting room. I don't remember it. He remembered it for thirty years."

Ossa sat down slowly. The ferrywoman had a face made for rivers—all long lines and patient weathering—and right now it did something complicated. "My grandmother used to say," she offered, "that Vessany people couldn't throw anything away. Not a button, not a boat, not a grudge. Everything got kept, and everything got *entered*, and nothing ever got forgiven because forgiveness wasn't a line item."

"That sounds like him," Isandro said.

"And yet," Vesek said quietly, from his corner. He didn't finish the sentence. He'd been a hunter long enough to know how to leave a thought where others could pick it up or step around it.

*And yet,* Isandro thought. *He sent the page. He spent his last coinage, his last favor, his last postage, on telling me to give everything away.*

* * *

The trouble with reconciliation, Isandro decided that afternoon, while she split kindling with more force than the kindling deserved, was that it refused to behave like arithmetic.

She understood betrayal. Betrayal balanced. Corvel had traded her name for his comfort; the columns matched; the sum was ugly but complete. She could hate that cleanly. She had been hating it cleanly for weeks, carrying it like a stone in her boot, and the stone had a satisfying weight and shape and she knew exactly where it hurt.

What she did not understand was a man who betrayed her *and* kept a lark. Who sold her *and* spent his dying strength to tell her the truth about the guild. Who broke her heart *and* then, with the same precise hand, wrote her the only letter that had ever made her feel less alone in it.

"You're splitting that wood like it owes you money," Wren observed, appearing beside her with two cups of the tea-substance. She handed one over. "Which, granted, everything does, but the wood hasn't been formally billed."

"He knew," Isandro said. She drove the hatchet into the block and left it standing there, quivering. "All along. Since I was four. He watched me hide it for eleven years—watched me flinch every time an animal came near, watched me lie, watched me make myself small and quiet and *invisible*, because I thought if anyone knew, I'd be taken away or worse. And he never said. Never once said *you're safe here*. He just... filed it. Entered it in some ledger in his head and locked the drawer."

Wren drank her tea. Waited. Wren was very good at waiting; it was one of the few things about her that wasn't sharp.

"And now he writes me that the guild was always meant to be spent," Isandro went on, "as if that fixes it. As if *permission* makes up for all the years he let me believe I had none. I'm supposed to feel—" She gestured helplessly at the snow, the mountains, the entire concept of emotion. "*Warm.* About a dying man. Who I also want to shake until his teeth rattle. Both at once. How does that work? Where's the column for that?"

"There isn't one," Wren said.

"Then how do you balance it?"

"You don't." Wren crouched, pulled the hatchet free, and offered it back to her handle-first—an odd courtesy from a woman who guarded her own knives like state secrets. "My mother sold me to a ship's captain when I was nine. Best price on the docks, she said. Got a good deal, too—she haggled him up from eight crowns to eleven." She said it lightly, the way she said most unbearable things. "I hated her for years. Clean, tidy hatred. Kept it polished. And then, maybe five years ago, I found out she'd used the money to buy passage for my little brother out of Vessany before the water came. Eleven crowns. My price. Spent on saving someone else."

Isandro stared at her. In all the weeks of bickering and bad roads and shared blankets in ditches, Wren had never once spoken of Vessany as anything but geography.

"So what did you do?" Isandro asked. "With the hatred. When it stopped balancing."

Wren turned the hatchet over in her hands, considering. "Carried it," she said finally. "Lighter than I expected, once I stopped trying to put it down or pick it up. Just... carried it. Some accounts you don't close. You just stop letting them decide what you buy next." She handed the hatchet back. "Your old master's dying in a cell knowing you'll carry him the same way. I think that's the closest thing to forgiveness a man like him knows how to ask for. He wouldn't even recognize the word. He'd call it *carrying forward an unrecoverable loss*."

Despite everything—a crack of laughter escaped Isandro, startled out of her, steaming in the cold air. "That's exactly what he'd call it."

"He'd footnote it," Wren agreed. "Twice."

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold. Ossa cooked—actual food, a miracle of barley and salt fish and one onion she defended like a fortress—and Vesek told a story about a customs raid gone wrong that was, against all odds, funny, mostly because the customs officer in question had been a goat. The hound got the onion ends and pretended this was his due as a war hero.

Afterward, when the others had settled into their bedrolls and the fire had gone down to a red contemplation, Isandro sat with the page again. She had brought a stub of pencil and the edge of her own notebook, because some habits were load-bearing.

Beneath Corvel's entry, in her own hand, she wrote:

*Entry 4,412. Account: C. Corvel. Asset received: truth, one measure, delivered late but undiluted. Debt status: unpayable. Disposition: carried.*

She looked at it a long time. Then she added:

*Note: carried gladly. Note to note: he would hate that phrasing. Good.*

It didn't fix anything. That was the discovery of the day, the one she folded up and put in her pocket next to the page: reconciliation wasn't a door that opened onto a room where everything was fine. It was a smaller door than that. It opened onto a corridor where you could walk past the wound without bleeding on everything, where you could take what a broken man had to give without pretending the giving canceled the taking. He had failed her monstrously. He had loved her clumsily, in the only currency he'd ever trusted, which was paper. Both entries stood. The book stayed open.

*Permission, not possession.*

She thought about the customs house waiting at the drowned border. She thought about the terms the elder Brammel had warned them of—the door takes payment in what you love most—and about the instrument of transfer sewn into her coat lining, the paper that made her, absurdly, legally, impossibly, the owner of the Ember Guild entire: the bottled storms, the ghost-debts, the last salt of a drowned kingdom, the lark.

Corvel had signed it over before the hunters came. She'd assumed it was protection—if the guild fell to the crown, it would fall to *her*, and she'd be beyond their reach. Now she saw the other reading, the one written between his cramped lines: he hadn't been protecting her. He'd been *arming* her. Handing her the one thing heavy enough to pay for a door, and trusting her—hoarder, miser, keeper of eleven years' worth of locked drawers—to have learned, at the end, what it was for.

*Spend it, girl. That is what it was FOR.*

"I'm going to," she murmured to the fire. "You insufferable old man. I'm going to spend every last coin of it."

From the doorway, the hound's tail thumped twice against the floor, which was either agreement or a request for more onion.

* * *

She woke near midnight to Wren shaking her shoulder, and knew before her eyes opened that something was wrong—not danger-wrong, the bothy was quiet and the watch-schedule intact, but the specific wrongness of Ossa standing at the window with her face lit silver by snowglow, absolutely still.

"What is it?" Isandro whispered.

"Listen," Ossa said.

At first there was only wind. Then, beneath it, threading through the dark like something remembered rather than heard: water. Moving water, deep and wide and wrong for a mountain bothy, wrong for winter, wrong for anywhere within fifty miles of frozen marsh.

Ossa's river. The river that had refused to carry her, that she had followed and mourned and chased across half a country—it was calling, and it sounded close, and it sounded like it was running toward the border ahead of them.

Or, Isandro thought, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, like something else was using its voice.

Because under the water-sound, faint but unmistakable, came bells. Many bells. Down-valley, on the hunters' road, ringing the way bells ring when a hunt has found what it's looking for—

—and beginning, deliberately, to close the distance.
