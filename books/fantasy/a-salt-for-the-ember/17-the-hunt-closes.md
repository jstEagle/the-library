## 17. The Hunt Closes

Vesek had spent eleven years learning to read a hunt the way other men read weather, and what he read from the customs house window that morning made his stomach settle into a cold, professional calm.

They were not rushing. That was the first thing. A green captain rushes; a captain who has done this before takes his time, posts his riders in the treeline where the light won't find them, sends two men wide along the frozen marsh to close the back of the building. Vesek counted nineteen horses visible and guessed at six more in the alders, which meant Captain Rennick himself had come down from the garrison at Harrow Ford, which meant someone in the capital had finally decided this matter was worth a name on it.

"They're not even hiding well," Wren said beside him. She was crouched under the customs house's one intact window, whittling something she refused to show anyone. "That fellow by the birch has been standing in the same spot since dawn. His horse has eaten half the tree."

"That's Sergeant Mallow," Vesek said. "He's not stupid. He's bored. There's a difference, but it looks similar from the outside."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"No," Vesek said. "Nothing I say today is going to comfort anyone. I'd just like it noted for the record that I said so."

From below came the sound of Isandro's voice, low and steady, talking to the customs officer about papers they did not have and terms they could not meet. Ossa was with her, and beneath their words Vesek could hear the hound — his hound, or formerly his hound — Bracken, tied up outside among the hunters' pack, making the small restless sounds of an animal being asked to do something he no longer believed in.

Vesek had been thinking about Bracken all morning. He suspected he would be thinking about him for the rest of his life, however much of that remained.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had lasted four hours, and its entire content had been: *stay inside, stay quiet, let Isandro finish whatever she was doing in the cellar.*

Plans like that worked fine until somebody's horse shifted its weight and snapped a dead branch, and then you had perhaps a quarter hour before Rennick stopped being patient. Vesek knew the quarter hour when he saw it: the moment Mallow turned from the birch and walked toward the front door with his hand resting easy on his sword hilt, the way a man walks when he's been told to knock but expects to be knocking on a locked door.

"I'm going out," Vesek said.

Wren looked up from her whittling. For once she didn't have anything clever ready, and he watched her scramble for some and come up empty, which was, in its way, the most honest thing she'd ever shown him.

"You're going to surrender," she said finally. "To them. To *us*, effectively. To the machinery."

"To the machinery," Vesek agreed. "You've been listening."

"Someone has to." Her jaw was tight. "Vesek. They'll hang you. Or worse — they'll take you apart slowly to find out why the hounds wouldn't hunt. You know what the questioners do with a man who can't be explained."

"I know exactly what they do," Vesek said. "I've delivered men to them." He picked up his badge — the hunter's badge he'd carried for eleven years, the one with the crown's stag stamped into it, the one he'd polished every morning because a dull badge meant a sloppy hunter and a sloppy hunter got people hurt. He set it on the windowsill. It looked very small there, and slightly ridiculous, like a coin someone had dropped.

"Then don't go out there," Wren said. "Let me go out there. I'm owed nothing. I'm a smuggler with a debt and no country; nobody writes ballads about what happens to me."

"And that," Vesek said, "is precisely why it has to be me. They're not here for you. They're here for a beast-speaker, and they'll tear this marsh down to the peat looking for one. But if the beast-speaker walks out and puts his hands up — if he confesses clean, in proper form, with his badge surrendered and his testimony offered freely — then Rennick has what he came for. Reports get written. Orders get countermanded while everyone argues over custody. And the four of you get hours. Maybe most of a day."

"Hours," Wren repeated flatly.

"Rennick is careful. Careful men are slow. I'm going to make myself so interesting to him that he forgets to hurry." Vesek found, to his mild surprise, that he was almost smiling. "Besides. Someone in that pack still answers to my whistle, and I'd rather he heard my voice than my screaming."

Wren stood there a long moment. Then she held out her hand, palm up, and in it lay the thing she'd been whittling all morning: a small wooden hound, rough but unmistakable, ears up, tail mid-wag.

"For luck," she muttered. "Not sentiment. Luck. There's a difference, but it looks similar from the outside."

Vesek took it and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where the badge used to sit. "I'm stealing your line," he said.

"It was always mine. Everything is. Ask anyone."

* * *

He went out through the front door with his hands open and empty, and the cold hit him like a slap from an old friend.

Mallow stopped three paces away, and behind him the whole treeline seemed to lean forward — nineteen horses, twenty-five men, and thirty-odd hounds, and every eye of every species fixed on Vesek as though he'd walked out of a story they'd only half believed.

"Sergeant," Vesek said. "Fetch the captain, would you? Tell him Hunter Vesek is surrendering under the confession rite. Full form. He'll want his clerks."

Mallow blinked. "You're — " He glanced past Vesek at the customs house, then back. "Alone?"

"As a Sunday sermon."

It took Rennick ten minutes to come down from the ridge, which told Vesek the man had been waiting for exactly this, or something near it. Rennick was grey at the temples and grey in the eyes and moved like a man who had never once in his life been surprised and resented the suggestion that he might be. He dismounted, walked up until propriety required him to stop, and studied Vesek the way a clerk studies a ledger with an entry that doesn't balance.

"Hunter Vesek," Rennick said. "Your pack reported you missing nine days ago. Your second reported you insubordinate eleven days ago. And this morning my lead hound sat down at the edge of this marsh and refused to move until I threatened him, which is a thing hounds do not do." He folded his hands. "So. Confession rite. Speak it, then, and we will see what shape your truth comes in."

There it was. The machinery, laid out in the open: the reports, the cross-checks, the suspicion already filed and waiting. Vesek had spent eleven years believing the machinery was strong because it was merciless. Standing in front of it now, he saw the other thing — the true thing — which was that the machinery was strong because it was heavy, and heavy things are slow, and slow things can be outrun by anyone willing to drop what they're carrying.

He dropped it.

"I confess," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "that I am the beast-speaker you seek. That I concealed it from my superiors for the term of my service. That I used the gift to turn my own pack on the ninth day of the pursuit, and again yesterday, and that the refusal of your lead hound this morning is my doing and no fault of the animal's. He is a good hound. Better than the man who trained him." He knelt, because the rite required it, and because kneeling felt less like defeat than he'd feared — it felt like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. "I offer full testimony under seal. Names, dates, methods. Everything I know about how the gift moves through bloodlines, and everything I know about how the hunt has been mishandling it."

A murmur ran down the treeline. Somewhere in it, Bracken began to bark — joyous, furious, unmistakable — until a handler cuffed him quiet.

Rennick didn't move for a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice pitched only for Vesek: "Why?"

Because the machinery is fallible, Vesek thought. Because I watched it grind a carter's widow into the mud over a mule's memory. Because I listened to a woman speak to animals and heard nothing monstrous in her voice, only tiredness and kindness, and realized I had spent my life hunting the wrong thing. Because a smuggler carved me a wooden dog.

"Because I finally read my own orders closely enough to see what they were for," he said aloud.

Rennick's face did something complicated and small. Then he straightened, and the machinery closed around Vesek like water around a stone: ropes, questions, the clerks summoned forward with their ink and their forms. Confession rite, properly observed, meant written record, meant witnesses, meant argument — and argument was time. Vesek gave them testimony the way a miser gives coins: slowly, precisely, each fact weighed and priced. He buried the true items — the road through the marsh, the cellar door, the names of the people inside — under a flood of accurate irrelevancies. Every word true. None of it useful. Let them spend their hours parsing him.

By midday the sun had climbed and the frost was sweating off the reeds, and Rennick had posted only four men on the customs house instead of storming it, because a careful man does not storm a building while he holds, as he believes, the prize already. Vesek sat bound against a supply wagon with Mallow standing guard over him, and Mallow — bored, decent Mallow — kept glancing at him with an expression caught between disgust and grief.

"The wooden dog," Mallow said finally. "In your pocket. You want me to throw it away for you?"

"No," Vesek said. "I want you to lose it somewhere you'll find it later, and feel bad about losing it, and keep it anyway."

Mallow stared at him. "That's the strangest order I've ever been given."

"Get used to it. You're guarding me till the wagons come. It's going to be a strange day."

* * *

The wagons were the flaw in it, and Vesek had known they would be. A confession rite bought hours, but hours end, and when the prison wagon rolled up the marsh track in the late afternoon, Rennick's patience ended with it. Vesek heard the captain shouting orders — sweep the building, drain the cellar, bring me everyone inside — and understood that his arithmetic had run out maybe an hour sooner than he'd hoped. Careful men are slow, but they are also thorough, and thoroughness catches up.

He was loaded into the wagon. The last thing he saw before the doors shut was Bracken breaking loose from the pack line and galloping after the wheels, barking, until a rider ran him down and dragged him back by the collar. The hound fought like a creature possessed, and Vesek pressed the little wooden dog against his chest through his shirt and thought: *hold, old friend. Hold just a while longer.*

But the doors didn't shut.

A hand caught them — Mallow's hand — and Mallow leaned in, wearing the expression of a man about to commit the first crime of his career and astonished to find how easy it felt. "Sergeant Ollin says the axle pin on this wagon's cracked," he said loudly, for the benefit of anyone listening. "Says it needs swapping before it carries weight. Twenty minutes, maybe forty."

"Mallow," Vesek said.

"Don't thank me." Mallow's ears had gone red. "Thank the dog. He hasn't stopped staring at me since noon and I can't stand it." He slammed the doors to — gently, gently — and the wagon sat there on the frozen track with its cracked axle pin and its cargo of inconvenient truths, and across the marsh Vesek could hear, faintly, the sound of the customs house cellar door opening, and voices rising, and running feet on old boards.

Forty minutes. Perhaps.

He began, in the darkness, to count.

* * *

What happened next, Vesek reconstructed afterward from noise alone, and the noise was considerable.

There was a crash from within the customs house — glass, timber, something heavy and official falling over. There was Rennick's voice cracking like a whip: *the cellar, the cellar, they're in the cellar.* There was a sound Vesek had never heard in eleven years of hunting and recognized instantly anyway: the whole pack of hounds, thirty-odd animals, breaking into song at once — not the chase-bay, not the kill-cry, but something older and stranger, a rolling chorus of it, joyful and defiant, drowning out the horns.

And through it, thin and clear, a woman's voice speaking a language no one at the guild had ever taught her, and the ground itself seeming to answer.

The wagon lurched. Not the axle — hands. From underneath. And Wren Halloway's voice, low and furious, coming up through the floorboards:

"If you say one sentimental word about this, I'm putting you back."

"Noted," Vesek said, and rolled out through the cut floor panel into the arms of a smuggler, a ferrywoman, and the best hound in the king's service, while behind them the customs house of a drowned kingdom burned its last papers and the hunt's machinery — heavy, slow, fallible — floundered in the marsh trying to remember which way it had been pointed.

"Bracken followed the wagon," Ossa said, hauling him upright. "Wouldn't be shaken. Kept circling back to us like he was reading sign."

"He was," Vesek said. His legs were shaking. He found he didn't mind who saw. "Where's Isandro?"

"Cellar," Wren said. "With the ledger. She said—" and for once Wren's voice faltered, just slightly, just enough that Vesek understood before she finished, "—she said the door wants paying. She's paying it. Now. We're holding the entrance and you—" she shoved the reins of a stolen hunter's horse into his bound hands, "—are going to ride the perimeter and give us every minute you've got left in you."

Vesek swung up into the saddle. His wrists were still roped; he'd worry about that later. Ahead of him the evening was coming down blue and cold over the marsh, and behind him the cellar door of a country that didn't exist stood open, and somewhere inside it a woman he'd hunted was buying all their lives with the only coin she had.

"Every minute," he said, and put his heels to the horse, and rode at the closing dark.
