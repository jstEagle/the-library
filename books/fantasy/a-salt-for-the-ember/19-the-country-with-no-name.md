## 19. The Country With No Name

Ossa had spent her whole life learning the language of rivers, and so she knew, before anyone else, that the water on the other side of the door was speaking to her.

It came up through the floorboards of the customs house cellar like a whisper through a keyhole—*come up, come up, come and see*—and it was not the river she had lost. That was the first thing she understood, standing with her hand still pressed against the cold wood of the door, her heart going like a ferryman's pole driven hard into the current. The river of her childhood had been broad and brown and patient, carrying barges of salt fish and smuggled lace, and it had refused her, in the end, with a cruelty she had never managed to explain to anyone. This water was young. This water was glad.

"Ossa." Wren's voice, roughened from holding the door against the hunters' shoulders. "Are you going to stand there all day, or are you going to let the rest of us through?"

She hadn't realized she was blocking the stair. She moved, and Isandro came up out of the cellar with the ledger clutched to her chest like a child, her face streaked with salt dust and something that might have been tears, though Ossa had learned by now that Isandro Brightwater would deny crying the way other people denied theft. Vesek came last, limping, one wrist still ringed with the bruise of the manacle he'd worn as his confession. Behind them, below them, the door was already closing—not slamming, but folding itself shut with the unhurried dignity of a ledger being closed at the end of a long day's accounting.

The last thing Ossa heard from the far side was a boot kicking wood, and Wren's name shouted once, in a voice that would not be denied.

Wren did not look back at the door. Wren never looked back at anything; it was, Ossa had decided somewhere on the hunters' road, a policy rather than a temperament. But her hand went to her knife hilt and stayed there, and Ossa saw that her knuckles were white, and said nothing, because that was the currency between them.

They climbed the cellar stairs.

* * *

The customs house above was the same one they had entered—Ossa recognized the smell of old paper and older dust, the counter worn smooth by two centuries of elbows—but the windows had changed. The windows that had looked out on a frozen marsh now looked out on morning.

Not the gray, grudging morning of the marshlands, either. This was a morning with intention. Light came down the valley in a long gold spill, catching the frost on a hillside of terraced fields, catching the smoke rising from chimneys—chimneys, plural, dozens of them, a whole town folded into the valley's elbow—and catching the river.

The river ran through the middle of everything, bright as a drawn blade, and it was singing.

Ossa had to put her hand on the counter to keep her feet. She had heard rivers sing before; every ferrywoman had, on certain nights, in certain moods of the water. But she had never heard one sing *welcome*. Rivers sang to themselves. Rivers sang warnings. This one was singing to her, by name, in the old Vessany dialect her grandmother had used for lullabies—a dialect Ossa had thought lived only in her own skull, unshared, unshareable, like everything else from the drowned country.

"Isandro," she said, and her voice came out strange. "The water here knows my grandmother's songs."

Isandro looked up from the ledger. Of all of them, she was the one who understood, because she had always understood what it was to carry a thing in you that no one else could hear. She didn't ask questions. She just came and stood at the window beside Ossa, and together they watched the valley.

"Is that—" Vesek began.

"A town," Wren said flatly. "There's a town. There's a whole *town*, and fields, and—" She stopped, and Ossa watched her do the arithmetic she could not avoid: roads, patrols, a population. A country does not need to exist on maps to exist on the ground. "How many people?"

The answer came from behind them.

"Four thousand, at the last census. Though the census-taker counts himself twice, so perhaps three thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine."

They turned. The customs officer stood in the doorway of his back room, and he was not wearing his official coat anymore. He had exchanged it for a plain wool jacket, and without the coat he seemed to shrink—not smaller, exactly, but *unbuttoned*, a man stepping out of a uniform he'd worn so long it had grown into his shoulders. He was holding a stamp in one hand and a small ink pot in the other, and he looked, Ossa thought, like a man who had just set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"You're not a customs officer," Isandro said.

"I am the customs officer." He set the stamp on the counter with a small, ceremonial click. "I am also Ferro Dannek, junior clerk of the Third Registry of Vessany, appointed forty-one years ago, never relieved of his post." He smiled, and it was a crooked, tired, entirely human smile. "The crown erased the country. Nobody thought to erase the paperwork."

* * *

He fed them first. This, Ossa decided, was the mark of a true clerk of Vessany: whatever else the world did to you, you fed people before you explained anything. The back room held a stove, a kettle, a wheel of hard cheese, a jar of pickled onions that made Wren's eyes water, and bread that was still warm, which meant there was an oven, which meant there was a baker, which meant—

"There's a whole town," Ossa said again, because she still couldn't make the sentence sit still in her head.

"There's a whole valley," Ferro said. He poured tea into mismatched cups, and Ossa noticed that every cup was chipped in the same way, at the same spot on the rim, as though they had all come from the same wreck. "The valley has a name, but we don't use it. Names get written down. We learned that lesson." He sat down across from them, and for the first time Ossa saw how old he was—not the brisk middle age of the customs house but something older, a man who had spent four decades keeping a door open on the chance that someone would come through it. "When the crown struck Vessany from the maps, they thought that was the end of it. Maps are how kings think. But people are not on maps. People are on *roads*. And the roads here—" he tapped the floor, and beneath it Ossa felt the young river running, singing, "—the roads here were never the crown's to close."

"The door," Isandro said. Her hands were wrapped around her tea, and she was looking at Ferro with the expression Ossa had learned to read as her thinking-face: total attention, total suspicion, total hope, all at once. "It opened for the ledger. The ledger was the passport. But the payment—"

"Was yours to make, and you made it." Ferro's eyes were kind. "The guild, yes? We felt it come through. The old accounts settling. There are bells in the valley that ring when a debt is paid in full. They rang this morning, and half the town turned out thinking the war had ended all over again." He chuckled. "Old Marta Kess threw her hat in the river. She does that when the bells ring. It's her one extravagance."

Wren snorted into her tea. "A woman who throws her hat in the river every time a debt gets paid. I like her already."

"You'll like her less when she's interrogating you about your lineage," Ferro said. "Everyone gets interrogated. It's not malice. It's bookkeeping." He leaned back. "Now. The four of you came through a door that opens once a generation at best, and you came through *together*, which has never happened, and I would very much like to know how, because the terms of the tithe are—"

"We each paid," Isandro said quietly. "What we loved most."

The room went still. Ossa looked at her own hands, at the calluses the pole had worn into her palms, calluses she had thought were hers forever, part of her, the shape of the life the river had refused her. And she realized—slowly, the way frost forms, crystal by crystal—that her palms were smooth.

She turned her hands over. Turned them back. The calluses were gone. Not faded. *Gone*, as if the river had reached through the door and taken them as its tithe, the one thing she had loved most: the work, the craft, the identity built out of being the ferrywoman the water would not carry.

"I can't—" She stood up so fast her chair went over. "My hands. What did I—"

"Ossa." Isandro was on her feet too, steady, warm. "Show me."

Ossa held out her hands like a woman presenting evidence. Isandro took them, turned them over, and Ossa watched her face do something complicated—grief and wonder fighting for the same ground.

"The river took them," Ossa said. "The river I grew up on, the one that wouldn't carry me—it took the only thing it ever gave me. The work. I can't pole a ferry anymore. I can't—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated the crack, hated the tears that were absolutely not forming, because she was Ossa, and Ossa did not cry about rivers, Ossa only *fought* them. "Forty years I wanted to be rid of that river and I never wanted to be rid of *this*."

"Ossa." Isandro's voice was very quiet. "Look at your hands again. Not what's gone. What's there."

Ossa looked. The calluses were gone, yes. But underneath them, where a lifetime of the pole had pressed the skin flat and hard, her palms were new. Soft. The hands of a woman who had not yet decided what she would do with them.

"The door didn't take the river from you," Isandro said. "It took the *debt*. The river refused you. That refusal was the thing you loved most, in the end—the fight with it, the wanting it, the whole shape of a life built around a door that wouldn't open. You paid it, and what's left is—" she turned Ossa's hand over once more, gently, "—a river that's already carrying you. Listen."

And Ossa listened, really listened, the way she had not let herself listen since she was a girl, and the river under the floorboards was singing her name, and there was no refusal in it at all. Only *come down, come down, the water is fine, we have been waiting for you since before you were born.*

She sat down. She picked up her tea. Her hands shook, and the tea trembled in the chipped cup, and nobody said anything about it, which was the kindest thing any of them had ever done for her.

* * *

Ferro walked them into the town that afternoon, because there was no keeping four strangers indoors once the windows were open, and because, he admitted, he wanted to see the look on Marta Kess's face.

The valley in full light was more than the window had shown. Terraced fields climbed the slopes, fallow now under early snow but marked out with the care of people who intended to keep farming until the world ended and then farm the ruins. The town itself was built of stone and timber and, here and there, of things that had clearly been salvaged from the drowning—door lintels with old Vessany script, a fountain whose basin was the prow of a barge, a church—no, not a church, Ossa realized, a *registry*, because of course it was—whose bell tower still bore the crown's tally-marks, scratched out, every one, scratched out so thoroughly the stone had gone pale.

People came out of their doors as they passed. They didn't gawk, exactly. They *assessed*. Ossa felt it the way she felt a change in current: a hundred pairs of eyes taking the measure of four mud-caked travelers, weighing them, entering them into some invisible ledger.

A boy of about ten ran alongside them for a full hundred paces before working up his courage. "Are you from Outside?"

"We are," Wren said.

"Is it true the crown hunts people who talk to animals?"

Wren glanced at Isandro. Isandro glanced at the ground. Vesek, who had spent his career doing exactly that hunting, developed a sudden fascination with the fountain.

"It's true," Wren said at last. "It's also true that the hunt is going to have a very bad winter."

The boy nodded gravely, as though this confirmed everything, and ran off shouting something in the old dialect, and three more children joined him, and by the time they reached the registry square there were perhaps forty people pretending not to follow them, which in a town of four thousand was the equivalent of a state reception.

Marta Kess was waiting by the fountain. She was small and round and fierce, with a hat that Ossa suspected was her spare. She looked them over once, top to bottom, the way Ferro had said she would.

"Four of them," she said to Ferro. "Through the door. *Four*."

"The tithe was paid four times over."

"Four times." Marta's eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Forty-one years I've kept my grandmother's recipe for salt bread on the chance someone would come through, and now four come through at once and I've only got the one oven." She seized Ossa's arm. "You. You have river hands. I can always tell."

"I *had* river hands," Ossa said, and heard her own voice break on the past tense, and Marta Kess looked at her palms, and then at her face, and understanding passed between them like a current.

"Child," Marta said, "the river here has been asking about you for forty years. It asks every spring. *When is she coming, when is she coming, the one who was refused.*" She patted Ossa's arm, hard, which Ossa would learn was how Marta hugged. "You didn't lose your river. You were traded *up*."

* * *

They were given a house that evening—given, not lent; Marta Kess did not lend, Marta Kess *gave*, with the terrifying generosity of a woman who had waited four decades to give anything—and Ossa lay awake in a bed with a feather mattress and listened to the river through the window.

It sang all night. It never stopped singing. And somewhere past midnight, half-dreaming, Ossa realized she was humming back, harmonizing with the water the way her grandmother had harmonized with the old brown river before it turned on them all, and the sound of her own voice in the dark of a country with no name made her cry in a way that was not grief, or not only grief, or grief that had finally, after all these years, finished its long journey and arrived somewhere else.

She got up. She dressed. She went down the stairs, out the door, through streets silvered with frost, down to the bank.

The river ran fast and clear under the stars. She knelt at the edge, and the water came up over her smooth, new palms, and it was cold, and it was glad, and it said her name the way her grandmother used to say it, and Ossa said, out loud, in the old dialect, in the language of rivers:

"I'm here. I'm sorry it took me so long. What do you need?"

The river laughed—that bubbling, breaking laugh that rivers have when they are pleased with themselves—and it said:

*A ferry. We have been forty years without a ferry, and there is a woman downstream who has been rowing her grandmother's route by hand, badly, and she is getting old, and the water has been waiting for someone to teach her daughter properly. Not with your hands, ferrywoman. Your hands are new. Teach her what the river taught you. That is a thing no tithe can take.*

Ossa knelt there a long time, laughing and crying into the current, and the current held her the way it had never held her before.

* * *

In the morning, the bells rang again.

Not the settling-of-accounts bells—Ossa knew their voice now, deep and satisfied. These were the registry bells, quick and urgent, and she came out of the house to find the whole street already awake, and Isandro standing in the square with a piece of paper in her hand, her face gone white as the frost.

"What is it?" Wren was already there, already armed, because Wren was always already armed. "Hunters? Did they find the door?"

"No." Isandro's voice was strange. "It's a letter. It came through the customs house—through the *door*, the way Corvel's last page came. It's addressed to me." She looked up, and Ossa saw that her hands were shaking. "It's from a woman named Wren Halloway."

Every head turned to Wren.

"That's not possible," Wren said. "I'm standing right here."

"It's dated next spring," Isandro said. "And it says—" she stopped, swallowed, started again. "It says the hunt collapsed. It says the prisoners went free. And it says, *Wren, if you're reading this, the door opens both ways now, and there is a valley waiting for you on the other side, and you should know that you make it.*"

The square was very quiet. The river, down below, had stopped singing to listen.

Wren stared at the letter for a long, long moment. Then she laughed—one short, disbelieving bark—and ran both hands through her hair, and said, in a voice that shook only slightly:

"Well. I suppose I'd better not die between now and spring, then. I've got an appointment with myself."
