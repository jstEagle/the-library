## 20. Ledgers Kept by Hand

The valley had a way of making mornings feel like they were being handed to you personally.

Isandro woke in the house the exiles called the Counting Room—though nothing had been counted there for sixty years—and found that someone had left a cup of barley tea on her windowsill while she slept. It was still warm. That was the thing about Vessany's remnant: people did small kindnesses the way other places did gossip, constantly and without being asked.

She drank the tea standing at the window, watching frost burn off the terraced fields below, and thought about ledgers.

She had been thinking about them for eleven days now, ever since the customs officer—Vessany's last clerk, a dry, patient man named Ferren who stamped their papers through as though sixty years of waiting were merely an administrative delay—had shown her the archive beneath his office. Shelves of it. The whole drowned country's bookkeeping, preserved in wax-sealed boxes: births, harvests, tithes, grievances. A nation that no longer existed on any map, still keeping its accounts.

"They never stopped," Ferren had said, running one finger along a spine. "That was the whole trick of surviving erasure. You keep records. As long as someone writes you down, you exist."

Isandro had spent her life believing the opposite—that invisibility was armor, that the safest place was the margin no one read. But she had walked through a door that cost her everything she loved most, and she had come out the other side with more than she'd paid. So perhaps it was time to revise her accounting.

The old Ember Guild ledger sat on the table by the hearth, the one she'd carried out of the salt cellar while hunters kicked in Corvel's door. She opened it to the last page, where the final entry waited in her own hand: *paid in full.* Below it, blank paper. Blank paper was a kind of invitation, if you were the sort of person who listened to books.

She went looking for ink.

* * *

Wren Halloway was in the smithy, ostensibly helping and actually supervising, which was Wren's native mode of existence. The valley's smith, a broad woman named Ket with forearms like mooring rope, tolerated this with the serenity of someone who knew exactly how much actual work was happening.

"You're up early," Wren said, not looking up from the horseshoe she was holding at a critical angle and doing nothing with.

"I need your help."

"That's my favorite sentence. Go on."

"I'm starting a new ledger."

Wren set down the horseshoe. "You gave up the guild."

"I gave up owning it." Isandro turned the phrase over, testing its weight. It still fit, mostly. "But the guild did things, Wren. Sixty years of things. Bottled storms sold to men who flooded valleys. Debts collected from ghosts whose families never saw a coin. The last salt of a drowned kingdom—" she stopped, because Ossa was coming up the path and some sentences deserved to wait for their full audience. "Corvel kept honest books about dishonest business. Every wrong the guild ever did is written down somewhere in those pages. Names. Amounts. Who owed what, and who was made to owe."

Ket grunted. "And you want to make it right."

"I want to *start* making it right. I can't fix it from here. Most of it I can't fix at all—the people are scattered or dead or living under new names in countries that took them in. But names can be written down. What was taken can be recorded. And someday, when the borders are less—" she gestured vaguely at the concept of crowns, "—less themselves, restitution can begin. If somebody keeps the record."

Wren leaned against the anvil, arms crossed, and studied her with the expression she wore when she was deciding whether to be moved or difficult. Historically, she chose difficult roughly seventy percent of the time.

"You realize," she said, "that you're proposing to rebuild the exact institution you just spent a winter fleeing. Ledgers. Debts. Restitution schedules. Isandro Brightwater, you are going to become Master Corvel with better intentions."

The words landed harder than Wren probably intended, because Wren always aimed true even when she didn't aim cruel. Isandro felt the old flinch—the fear of becoming the thing that raised her—and then, surprisingly, let it go.

"Corvel's last entry said the guild was meant to be spent, not kept," she said. "I spent mine on a door. What's left is the part that was always good: the part that wrote things down so they couldn't be denied later. That part doesn't belong to anyone. It belongs to whoever does the writing."

Ket looked at Wren. Wren looked at the ceiling of the smithy for a long moment, jaw working.

"Fine," she said finally. "Fine. But I'm not doing sums. I do smuggling arithmetic, which means I round generously in my own favor."

"You'll do what you're good at," Isandro said. "Finding people. You were the best finder the guild ever had, and you hated every minute of it, which means you did it honestly. We'll need someone who knows how the old networks ran—who got squeezed, who got erased, who's still out there using a name the guild gave them."

Wren muttered something that might have been agreement or might have been a curse on all record-keeping institutions. With Wren, the two sounded identical.

* * *

They set up in the Counting Room because it seemed fitting and because it had the best light, three tall windows facing east over the valley. Vesek carried the table up from the customs house on his back, refusing help with the grim competence of a man who had spent thirty years carrying things he didn't choose and was determined to enjoy choosing this one.

He arranged the room like a garrison. Ink in the northeast corner. Paper stacked by weight. A stool positioned so no one could approach the writer from behind—a habit he apologized for twice and then stopped apologizing for, because Isandro told him it made her feel protected rather than policed.

"It's strange," he said, on the third morning, watching her rule lines across a fresh page. "I spent my career hunting people who could do what you do. Now I spend my mornings grinding ink for her."

"How does it sit?"

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which had initially been unnerving and had become, over the weeks, oddly comforting. Vesek did nothing casually, including change.

"Like a coat turned inside out," he said. "Same weight. Different lining." He set the inkwell down with a small precise click. "The hounds send their regards, by the way. Bracken caught a rabbit this morning and left it on Ossa's doorstep. He's never once brought me anything but trouble."

"He says you talk too much during tracking."

"I don't talk at all during tracking."

"Well," Isandro said, dipping her pen, "he says you breathe judgmentally."

The first name went onto the page that same hour. A woman named Sefa Marrowgate, recorded in the guild's oldest surviving ledger as having sold her shadow to settle a debt of nine crowns—a debt the ledger itself admitted had been inflated by a factor of four. Isandro wrote: *Sefa Marrowgate. Owed: five crowns and one shadow, returned unspent. Status: whereabouts unknown. Last seen: Grayharbor, eleven years past.*

It wasn't much. It was a line of ink about a woman who had probably died believing herself thief-ridden and shadowless. But it was true, and it was written, and Ferren's whole argument stood behind it like a wall: as long as someone writes you down, you exist.

By midday they had seven entries. By the end of the week, forty-one. Wren turned out to have a memory for names that bordered on the supernatural—she could recall the face, voice, and preferred swindle of every person she'd ever run cargo for or from—and she dictated while Isandro wrote, pacing the Counting Room like a general reviewing troops.

"—and then there's the Pellowe family, whole village of them, the guild bought their water rights in '41 through a shell company called Meridian Trade, which was me, I was the shell company, gods, I was so young—"

"You defrauded an entire village?"

"I *carried goods* for the defrauding of an entire village. There's a distinction. It's a thin distinction. Write it down anyway."

Isandro wrote it down. Under *wrong done*, she put: *water rights purchased below value via false company; company operated by W. Halloway, who has since confessed and volunteers testimony.*

"There," she said. "Now it's on record that you confessed."

Wren stopped pacing. "That's—you can't just—"

"It's how restitution works. The record shows the harm and the repair together. Otherwise the ledger's just a list of wounds." Isandro looked up. "You've been paying that debt since before I met you. Carrying water to villages that wouldn't thank you. I watched you do it on the ferry crossing, remember? You complained the entire time."

"I complain professionally. It's load-bearing."

"Mm." Isandro turned back to the page. "Complaint noted. Also load-bearing."

* * *

Ossa came on the second week, in the late afternoon, with river-light still in her manner even here, forty miles from any water wide enough to matter. She stood in the doorway of the Counting Room a long time before speaking, looking at the shelves, the ruled pages, the careful columns.

"My mother's name will be in there somewhere," she said at last. "The guild held the tolls on the Vessa before the drowning. Afterward they held the salvage claims. They charged widows to retrieve their own wedding rings off the riverbed." Her voice stayed level the way ice stays level—smooth, and bearing weight underneath. "I used to think if I ever found the person who signed those receipts, I'd—I don't know what. Something with fire in it."

"And now?"

"Now I find I mostly want it written down properly. The right dates. The right amounts. My mother's name spelled correctly—it's Tamsin, with an i, everyone gets it wrong." She came into the room and pulled a stool to the table without asking, which was very Ossa. "Ferren says the original receipts are in box fourteen. I'll fetch them tomorrow. And Isandro—" she paused, hand flat on the table, "—when we get to the salvage claims, I'll dictate. You'll write. That's the order of things. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Because I know you'll want to soften it. You do that. You hold the pen like it might hurt someone."

"It might," Isandro said. "That's rather the point of pens."

Ossa almost smiled. "Then we understand each other."

They worked until the light failed. When Ossa finally rose to go, she stopped at the door and said, without turning around: "My mother would have liked this room. She kept accounts for the ferry cooperative. She said a ledger was just a promise you could hold." Then she was gone into the blue evening, and Isandro sat with the candle and thought: *a promise you could hold.* She would put that on the flyleaf.

* * *

Winter loosened its grip by degrees, the way old men surrender arguments—one concession at a time, each accompanied by visible grievance. Snow slid from the roofs. The terraces showed green teeth. Children reappeared outdoors in numbers, shrieking, and Isandro discovered that the valley's foxes had opinions about the shrieking that they shared with her freely whenever she passed the woodpile.

"The little loud ones," a vixen told her, "have stolen my best sunning rock again. This is why I eat children in stories."

"Noted," Isandro said gravely. "For the record, I'm on the side of the rock."

The ledger grew. It grew past restitution and into something wider: a register of the guild's debts, yes, but also of gifts given and never acknowledged, of favors done by the small people the big books ignored. Wren insisted on a section for "things owed TO us, because fairness cuts both ways and I am owed a truly staggering quantity of money." Vesek built a second shelf. Then a third. He began bringing the hounds to the Counting Room in the afternoons, where they slept in a heap by the cold hearth and occasionally twitched through dreams that Isandro pretended not to overhear, out of politeness.

One gray afternoon, near the end of thaw, she looked up from her writing and realized the room had quietly filled. Wren was mending a strap in the window seat. Vesek was re-inking a faded page from box fourteen, his big hands absurdly delicate around the pen. Ossa sat cross-legged on the floor reading aloud from the old receipts in slow, steady tones, correcting each one against the new ledger, her mother's name spelled correctly in triplicate now. Sunlight lay across the table like a struck match.

Nobody had assigned anyone these places. They had simply taken them, the way water finds its level.

"Wren," Isandro said.

"Hm."

"When you first met me, you called me 'the accountant' like it was an insult."

"I called lots of people lots of things. I was going through a period."

"What would you call this?" Isandro tapped the open ledger. "What we're doing."

Wren considered, needle paused mid-stitch. "Bookkeeping," she said at last. "Reckoning. The long tedious work of putting things back where they belong." She bit the thread. "Why? Fishing for something grander?"

"No. Just checking it sounds different from the outside than it used to."

"It sounds," Wren said, "like the same word with the meanness boiled out of it. Which, for the record, took you walking through a magic door and giving away an entire criminal empire to accomplish, so I hope you're satisfied."

Immensely, Isandro did not say. She bent back over the page instead, and wrote the day's last entry, and blew it dry, and felt the strange specific peace of work that would outlast her.

* * *

The rider came up the valley road three days later, at an hour when the light was doing something golden and shameless over the eastern ridge.

Isandro saw him first from the window—a single horseman, moving tired, leading a second horse that carried no rider but bulged with saddlebags. Her stomach dropped with old reflexes before her mind caught up: riders meant crowns, crowns meant hunts, hunts meant—

But the horse was lame in the near hind, favoring it gently, and the man rode slumped, and no hunter of the crown rode into exile country alone unless he came as something else.

By the time he reached the Counting Room's gate, half the valley had materialized with the silent, total efficiency of people who remembered what soldiers looked like. Vesek stood closest, arms folded, wearing the particular stillness he reserved for assessing threats. Wren had appeared from absolutely nowhere, which was her gift.

The man dismounted badly. Up close he was younger than his posture—gray-faced, chapped, eyes rimmed red from road dust. He fumbled at his collar and produced, of all things, a letter, sealed with plain wax, and held it out toward Isandro with both hands, the way you offer bread.

"Isandro Brightwater?" His voice cracked on the second syllable. "I was told—there's a network, smugglers' roads, they said if I followed the marks I'd find—" He swallowed. "I have letters. From the prison at Cormorant Reach. From a dying man who paid my whole village's debts twenty years ago and never told anyone, and when I heard what happened to him I—" He stopped, gathered himself. "There are others coming. Gift-bearers, freed when the hunt collapsed. Dozens. They'll keep coming. Someone said—someone said there was a valley, and a ledger, and a woman who writes names down."

Behind her, Isandro heard Wren exhale slowly. Heard Ossa's breath catch. Felt Vesek step, very slightly, to stand at her shoulder—not guarding her, she realized. Standing *with* her, which was different, and better.

The man was still holding out the letter. On the back, in a hand she knew—shaky now, blotted, but unmistakably his—were the words: *For my heir, with the last of my accounts. Spend it well. —C.*

Isandro took the letter. Her hands were steady. Outside, the valley waited, and the road behind the messenger stretched away toward the world she'd left, and it occurred to her, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that doors apparently opened in both directions now.

"Well," she said, breaking the seal. "Someone get this man some tea. And bring me the new ledger—we're going to need more pages."
