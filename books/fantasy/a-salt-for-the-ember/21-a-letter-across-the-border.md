## 21. A Letter Across the Border

Wren Halloway had stolen things from kings, smuggled contraband past three separate customs regimes, and once talked her way out of a hanging by convincing the magistrate that the rope was cursed. She considered herself, on the whole, difficult to surprise.

So it was a mark against the morning when she came down the valley path and found Ossa standing at the old ferry landing, holding a piece of paper as though it might bite her.

"It's from across," Ossa said, without turning around.

"From across what? The river? The marsh? Be specific, I'm barely awake."

"Across the border." Ossa's voice had that flat careful quality it got whenever Vessany came up, which was to say, whenever anything came up, because everything in this valley was Vessany whether it admitted it or not. "A bird brought it. A gull."

"A gull carried mail."

"A gull carried mail," Ossa confirmed, "and Isandro says that's normal, and I have decided not to argue with her about birds anymore because I always lose."

Wren took the paper. It was good paper—too good, the kind of heavy cream stock that cost more per sheet than most people earned in a week—and it was sealed with wax stamped with the sigil of the Ember Guild: an ember in a salt circle. Wren knew that seal better than she knew her own mother's face, which was not saying much, since her mother had never once looked at her directly if she could help it.

The letter inside was written in a cramped, furious hand she recognized instantly.

*To whoever is still alive over there,* it began.

"Charming," Wren said aloud.

*This is Master Corvel of the Ember Guild, writing under conditions I will describe as adequate only because my lawyer is present.*

"He doesn't have a lawyer," Wren told Ossa. "He has a clerk who flatters him."

*You will be pleased—or at any rate relieved, which for you I understand is the same thing—to learn that the hunt is finished. Collapsed. Dissolved like sugar in bad tea. Corvel's confession did what confessions do when they are made loudly enough and in front of enough important people: it embarrassed the crown. It turns out that arresting gift-bearers makes excellent theater until someone points out that half the court's own grandmothers spoke to cats. The beast-hunters have been disbanded. Their records burned. Their commander reassigned to counting harbor taxes, which I am told he finds humiliating, and which I find delightful.*

Wren read that part twice. Then she sat down on the ferry bench, because her legs had opinions about standing.

Ossa watched her. "Bad news?"

"No." Wren cleared her throat. "Good news. Extremely good news. The kind of good news that makes you want to punch something, apparently."

*The imprisoned gift-bearers are free,* the letter went on. *All of them. The lark you freed from the guildhouse cellar—the one whose testimony started all of this—was released the same day. It sang outside my window for a week out of what I can only assume was spite. Vesek's confession was entered into the trial record; his name has been struck from every warrant in the country. He is, officially, a man who never existed, which I believe was already his ambition.*

Wren laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh. Vesek was going to be insufferable about this. Vesek was going to preen around the valley for a month, polishing his new legal nonexistence like a medal.

*As for me:* the hand grew smaller here, tighter, as though the writer were pressing hard to keep himself honest. *I am dying. The treatment they gave me in custody was meant to loosen tongues. It loosened mine, and it is also, slowly, stopping my heart. The physicians give me until spring. I tell you this not for sympathy—I have earned none—but so that you understand the terms of what follows.*

*I have spent the winter settling the guild's accounts. Every debt forgiven that could be forgiven. Every wronged party compensated where compensation was possible. And one final entry, which I enclose a copy of below, because I believe you are the sort of person who will want to check my arithmetic.*

Below, in the same furious hand:

*Entry the last: To Wren Halloway, smuggler, debtor, and the single most exasperating employee the Ember Guild ever failed to fire—debt of four hundred crowns, one favor unnamed, and one apology long overdue: PAID IN FULL. Do not write to thank me. You'll only make it sentimental.*

Wren stared at the words *paid in full* until they blurred.

Four hundred crowns. She'd owed the guild four hundred crowns since she was nineteen years old, since the night her first crew went down in the shallows off the Salt Gate and Corvel fished her out of the water along with two crates of contraband brandy and told her, drying her hair with a ledger page because there was nothing else to hand, that the rescue would go on her account. She had spent eleven years paying it down. She had assumed she would die owing it. There was a strange, vertiginous freedom in being done—a lightness, and underneath the lightness, annoyingly, grief.

"You're crying," Ossa observed.

"I'm not."

"There's a tear on your face."

"That's river spray."

"We're nowhere near the river."

"The valley is damp," Wren said with dignity, and wiped her face with her sleeve.

*One matter remains.* The letter's tone shifted again, softer now, though it seemed to resent going soft. *Isandro. She will not hear from me directly—she made her position clear the last time we spoke, and she was right to. So I am writing to you instead, because you are rude enough to deliver an unwelcome message honestly. Tell her the guildhouse stands empty. Tell her the ledgers are hers by right, though she has already found better use for them. And tell her—*

Here the handwriting stopped entirely. There was a long gap, then a different line, written slower, in ink that had gone slightly watery, though Wren chose not to speculate about why.

*Tell her the door was never locked from my side. Tell her I kept the lamp lit in the salt cellar until I couldn't climb the stairs anymore. Tell her that of everything the guild ever traded in, she was the only thing I got right.*

Wren folded the letter carefully along its original creases.

"Well?" said Ossa.

"Well what?"

"What does it say?"

"It says," Wren said, "that we're going to need more paper."

* * *

They convened in the long house that evening—which was to say, everyone crowded into Isandro's kitchen, because Isandro's kitchen had the best fire and the only teapot that didn't taste of marsh water, and because Isandro had never once managed to say no to anyone who showed up hungry.

Vesek arrived first, as he always did, carrying firewood he had clearly chopped himself and clearly wanted credit for chopping. He was a different man from the hollowed-out handler who had walked into the hunters' camp to give himself up as the beast-speaker. Winter had filled him back in. He'd taken to teaching the valley children how to whistle hound-calls, which the parents tolerated and the dogs found hilarious.

Brammel came next, the elder of the border village, walking the ridge path down with his stick and his opinions. Then a dozen others drifted in behind him, because word traveled fast in a valley of exiles and the words tonight were *letter* and *across* and *Corvel*, and nobody in this valley had ever successfully resisted gossip about the world they'd left.

Isandro read the letter aloud.

She read it well—steadily, without performance, pausing only at the last lines, the ones addressed to her, which she read quietly and then set down on the table and smoothed flat with both hands, the way she smoothed everything, ledgers and tempers and frightened animals alike.

Nobody spoke for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the fox that lived under the woodpile—it had a name now, Fennick, bestowed by the children over Wren's objections that naming wildlife was how you ended up feeding it forever—scratched at the door and was ignored on principle.

"The hunt is over," Isandro said at last. "Everyone's free. The ones who were taken, the ones who ran. They can go home."

"And us?" asked Brammel. His village still sent its taxes to a country that didn't exist; he had strong views on home. "What about the ones who can't go back to what was?"

Isandro looked at the letter. Then, slowly, she looked up—at Ossa, who had lost a river; at Vesek, who had lost a name; at Wren, who had lost whatever it was Wren had lost, which Wren declined to specify even to herself.

"I've been thinking about this all winter," Isandro said. "Since the door opened. Since we saw what's actually over here." She gestured at the kitchen, the firelight, the crowded room full of people who six months ago had been strangers bound together by debts and desperation and were now bound together by something with considerably better interest rates. "We keep saying we escaped. We fled. We got out. But look around. This isn't an escape. This is a place. It has a forge and a granary and forty-one people and a fox with a name, and last week Marta's baby was born here, healthy, in sunlight, in a country that isn't on any map but exists anyway."

She picked up the letter again.

"Corvel freed them, but freedom isn't the same as somewhere to go. Half those gift-bearers spent years hiding what they are. Some of them don't have homes to return to. Some of them have homes that will never feel safe again—not because of laws, laws change, but because of what their neighbors remember." Her voice was steady, but Wren, who had spent months learning to read Isandro Brightwater the way other people read weather, saw the effort underneath it. "There's a door. It opens both ways. I paid for it once. I think—" she stopped, and started again, quieter. "I think it should stay open."

"You're proposing," Brammel said slowly, "an open border."

"I'm proposing a door with a welcome mat."

"A door," said Ossa, "in the middle of a frozen marsh, guarded by a customs officer who demands papers."

"The papers are a formality," Isandro said. "He stamped a goose through last month. The goose had no papers whatsoever. He just liked the goose."

"That does sound like Aldous," Brammel admitted.

"So we write back," Isandro said. "That's why I called everyone here. Not to decide for them—for anyone. But the letter asks for an answer, and I want the answer to come from all of us, because it's all of us it concerns." She turned to Wren, and there it was, the thing she'd been building toward all evening, visible now like a reef under clear water. "And I want you to write it."

Wren blinked. "Me?"

"You knew him longest. You hated him best."

"That's not a sentence anyone's ever said to me before."

"You're also the only person in this valley who can write an insult that sounds like a compliment," said Vesek. "It's a genuine talent. I've watched you do it to me at least forty times."

"Forty-two," Wren corrected. "But fine. Yes. I'll write it. On one condition."

"Name it."

"That we send it by the gull, and that somebody explains to me how a gull finds a specific guildhouse in a city of two hundred thousand people, because I refuse to believe it's magic, I've built a whole personality on believing it's not magic."

"It's magic," said Isandro, Ossa, Vesek, and Brammel, in unison.

"I hate this valley," said Wren, and reached for a pen.

* * *

She wrote three drafts.

The first was too angry—all the old grievances lined up like cargo awaiting inspection, four hundred crowns and eleven years and a rope she'd once nearly swung from, and she burned it in the stove because Isandro was right about one thing: the man was dying, and there was no percentage in shipping a dead man ballast.

The second was too kind, and made her feel like she was wearing someone else's coat, so that one went into the fire too, though not before Ossa read a line over her shoulder and said, "Oh, that's lovely," which settled the matter permanently.

The third draft she wrote slowly, by firelight, while the kitchen emptied around her and Isandro pretended very badly not to watch.

*Master Corvel,* she began. *You wrote that I shouldn't thank you, so I won't. Instead I'm going to do something worse. I'm going to answer you honestly, which I know from experience you hate.*

*The valley sends its regards. All forty-two of us now, counting the baby, who is named Sela and has her mother's lungs and your talent for making everyone around her do exactly what she wants. Vesek says hello. He's teaching the children to whistle. He's happy, Corvel. I know you'll want to mock that, so let me save you the trouble: yes, Vesek, happy, the word means what it always meant, you just never stayed anywhere long enough to learn it.*

*Ossa says hello too. She stood on the old ferry landing yesterday looking east for a long time, and when I asked her what she was doing she said, "Saying goodbye properly this time," and then she made me stand there and say goodbye to something too, and I still don't know what, and I've decided not to ask. That's the kind of valley this is. People make you do rituals. It's infuriating. You'd love it.*

*Isandro read your letter to the whole valley by firelight. She read the last part quietly. I won't tell you what her face did, because some things aren't yours anymore, and because you'd only put it in a ledger.*

*Here is our answer, and it comes from all of us: the door stays open. Send us your gift-bearers, your hunted, your tired, your people who speak to cats and crows and mules and who have nowhere left that doesn't make them small. Send the ones who were taken and the ones who hid and the ones who did neither and are ashamed of it. The valley has room. The valley has a forge, a granary, a customs officer who likes geese, and a fox named Fennick who has strong opinions about newcomers and weak ones about sausages.*

*We are not rebuilding the guild. Isandro keeps a new book—not debts, names—and she says restitution starts with knowing who was wronged. But there's work here for hands that want it, and silence here for people who need it, and nobody in this valley will ever again ask anyone to be less than what they are.*

*You wrote that she was the only thing you got right. For the record, you got one other thing right: you fished a drowning smuggler out of the Salt Gate and put her on your books instead of in your cells. Everything else I am, everything else I built, started there. I'm not thanking you. I'm just entering it into the record.*

*Come see us, if spring allows. The door takes payment in what you love most, and I know for a fact you love the guild, and I know for a fact it's already spent, so as far as I can tell you're traveling free.*

*Paid in full,*

*W. Halloway*

She read it over twice, added nothing, crossed out nothing, and sealed it with the stub of green wax Ossa kept for marking jars of pickled eels, which felt appropriately undignified.

Then she held it out to Isandro, because it wasn't really hers to send.

Isandro read it standing by the fire, her lips moving slightly, and when she finished she didn't say anything for a long moment. Then she pressed her thumb to the seal, the way she used to press the guild's stamp to its ledgers, and handed it back.

"Send it," she said. "Before I add a postscript and ruin it."

* * *

The gull took the letter at dawn from the ferry landing, from Ossa's hands, because Ossa insisted that anything crossing water ought to cross it properly. It wheeled once over the valley—over the forge smoke and the granary roof and the frozen marsh where the customs house stood patient as a stone—and then it turned east, toward the world with maps, and shrank to a fleck, and was gone.

Wren watched it go with her arms crossed and her heart doing something complicated that she intended to examine later, or possibly never.

Beside her, Isandro said, "Do you think he'll come? In spring. If he lasts."

Wren considered. She thought of the furious hand, the watery ink, the lamp lit in the salt cellar until the stairs got too steep. She thought of a dying man spending his last winter settling accounts, and of the one account he'd left deliberately unsettled—the one that could only be closed in person, at a door, in salt.

"Oh, he'll come," she said. "It's the last entry in his ledger. And Corvel never in his life left an entry unfinished."

Far to the east, past the edge of sight, the gull beat on toward the coast, carrying a green wax seal and forty-two names' worth of welcome—and behind them, in the customs house at the drowned border, Aldous the last clerk sharpened his pen, dusted off a stamp that hadn't been used in a hundred years, and waited for spring traffic.
