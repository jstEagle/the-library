## 22. First Thaw

The fox arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing in the valley because nobody kept Tuesdays anymore, but Isandro had never quite managed to give up the habit of naming her days, and so Tuesday it was.

She was mending the south fence when the fox came trotting up the frost-softened lane with the particular swagger of an animal who has recently eaten something it shouldn't have and regrets nothing. Isandro set down her mallet and waited.

"You're not from here," she said.

*Neither are you,* said the fox, sitting down at a respectful distance and wrapping its tail over its paws like a merchant settling in for negotiation. *But I've heard about you. Down past the birches they say there's a woman who talks. Properly. Not like the old ones who only mutter at crows.*

"The old ones mutter at crows because crows gossip," Isandro said. "It's a reasonable arrangement."

*I want to learn,* the fox said simply.

Isandro looked at it for a long moment. Behind her, up the slope, smoke rose from three chimneys — Ossa's house nearest the water, Vesek's stubborn little cottage that he'd built twice because the first attempt had leaned like a drunk, and the long low hall where Wren held court over what she insisted on calling "the warehouse" and everyone else called "Wren's pile of things she refuses to throw away."

A year ago, this conversation would have been conducted in a cellar, in whispers, with a hand on the latch.

"Learning to talk," Isandro said, "is the easy part. The hard part is learning to listen. Foxes are terrible listeners."

*We are excellent listeners,* the fox said with dignity. *We simply reserve our attention for things worth hearing.*

"Well," said Isandro, "you've come to the right valley. We're mostly things worth hearing."

She held out her hand, palm down, the way you offer trust to something with teeth. The fox touched its nose to her knuckles, once, formally, as if sealing a contract. Somewhere in her chest, the old reflex flared — *look away, hide it, no one must see* — and then, as it did more often these days, faded into nothing at all.

The sun was out. Anyone might walk by. Let them see.

* * *

The child's name was Sefa, and she was eight years old, and she had been born in the valley, which made her one of perhaps a dozen people alive who had never known any country at all. Her grandmother had come through the door in the last winter of old Vessany, carrying Sefa's mother wrapped in a shawl, and the family had never once spoken of the world on the other side except to say, in the tone reserved for weather, *it is gone.*

Sefa found Isandro and the fox by the mill stream two days later, because children in the valley had a genius for finding exactly the thing adults were trying to keep quiet.

"You're teaching it," Sefa said accusingly.

"I'm learning with it," Isandro corrected. "That's different. That's respectable."

The fox opened one eye. *It's the same thing.*

"It is not the same thing," Isandro said. "One of us is being paid."

"I could pay," Sefa said immediately. She dug in her pocket and produced, with great ceremony, a river-smoothed stone, a bent nail, and half a honey cake wrapped in a leaf. "I saved the cake special."

Isandro looked at the honey cake. The honey cake, frankly, looked better than most fees the Ember Guild had ever collected, and she had once accepted a debt payable in ghost-grudges.

"That's a fair wage," she said gravely. "First lesson: sit still. Animals can hear your heart, and yours is currently shouting."

Sefa sat down so fast she nearly fell in the stream. The fox regarded her with the weary patience of a tutor presented with a promising but exhausting pupil.

*Her heart says,* the fox reported, *that she has wanted this since she was six, when she asked the goats why they were sad and nobody believed her.*

"Were the goats sad?" Isandro asked.

*Goats are always sad. It's structural.* The fox lowered its head toward Sefa, and its voice gentled into something Isandro had rarely heard from fox-kind. *Ask me your question, small one. The real one. You've been carrying it since the lane.*

Sefa's hands twisted in her lap. When she spoke, it came out in a rush, the way confessions do.

"When I talk to animals, my gran goes into the other room. Not angry. Just — she goes. And I wanted to know if talking is bad. If it makes people leave."

The stream went on chuckling over its stones. Isandro found, to her surprise, that her own throat had gone tight.

"No," she said. "Listen carefully, because this is the whole curriculum, and I'm giving it to you free even though you paid in advance." She took Sefa's hands. "Talking isn't what makes people leave. Fear is what makes people leave — their fear, not yours. My whole life, I hid this gift because I thought hiding would keep me safe, and do you know what hiding got me? It got me alone in a cellar with a lark, being careful where no one needed me to be careful. The gift was never the wound. The hiding was."

Sefa considered this with the ferocious seriousness of the young.

"And then you stopped hiding?"

"Then the world stopped letting me," Isandro said, "and it turned out to be the best thing that ever happened to me. Because when I finally spoke where people could see—" she nodded up the slope, at the chimneys, at the whole improbable settlement— "they came and stood next to me. That's the second lesson, and it's the important one. The gift finds your family. You just have to let it be seen doing it."

The fox put its nose against Sefa's ear and murmured something too low for Isandro to catch. Whatever it was, Sefa laughed — a wet, hiccupping laugh, the kind that follows crying without admitting it — and threw her arms around the fox's neck, and the fox bore it with the expression of a creature renegotiating its fee.

From the path above, Wren's voice drifted down: "If the beast-speaker's finished holding school, Ossa says the ice is off the ferry crossing and she wants witnesses when she tells the river she's back."

* * *

Ossa had waited a year and a season to say it, so nobody rushed her.

They all walked down together — Isandro with mud on her knees, Wren carrying a coil of rope she'd brought for no practical reason, Vesek walking with the careful gait of a man whose honor was still knitting itself back together, and Sefa running ahead and then doubling back and then running ahead again, bursting with a secret she'd been sworn to keep until after lunch.

The river ran high and bright with snowmelt. Where the ferry had been dragged ashore and left to rot through the winter, Ossa's boat sat fresh-tarred, ropes coiled, oars shipped. She stood at the bank a long time without speaking. The wind moved her hair. Somewhere upstream, water fell over stones with the sound of someone clearing their throat.

"You drowned my family's trade," Ossa told the river at last, conversational, almost fond. "You took the customs house. You took the border. You took forty years of my mother's people thinking they were ferrymen when really they were just standing on the edge of a country pretending to be a lake." She rolled up her sleeves. "And then you sent me a debt I couldn't pay and a door I couldn't open, and I hated you for it, and I'm done hating you, because look at you. Look at you running. You're beautiful when you run."

She stepped into the shallows, boots and all.

"So here's the arrangement," she said. "I ferry again. Spring to thaw to spring. And you carry me — actually carry me, none of your sulking, none of that business where the current goes strange around my hull. In exchange, I stop calling you *the river that refused me*, and start calling you by your name, which is Aldwater, which my grandmother knew, and which I will teach to every child in this valley until it sticks."

The river said nothing, because rivers don't, but the current around her ankles went suddenly, unmistakably gentle, curling affectionate circles in the silt, and Ossa laughed — a short, startled laugh, like a woman discovering she'd been forgiven before she finished apologizing.

"Well," she said briskly, wading back, eyes suspiciously bright. "That's settled. First crossing at noon. Fares negotiable. Wren, you're paying double, you owe me from the bothy."

"I absolutely do not—"

"You sang. All night. About a tax collector."

"It was a *ballad*—"

"Double," said Ossa, and Vesek snorted, and Sefa shrieked with laughter, and Isandro stood a little apart with the spring sun on her face and thought: *this is what the ledger was for. Not the debts. This.*

* * *

That evening, in the long hall, with the fire lit against the last cold snap of the season, Isandro opened the old ledger of Vessany for what she intended to be the final time.

It lay on the table between the bread and the lamp, swollen with damp from its year of travel, its salt-stained cover soft as cloth now. She had read it a hundred times. She knew every entry: the bottled storms, the ghost-debts, the last salt of the drowned kingdom, the door out, payable in person, in salt, before winter's end. She knew the page where Corvel's final letter had been pressed — she kept it there still, though she no longer needed the words. *The guild was always meant to be spent, not kept.*

Tonight she turned past all of it, to the end. To the last line.

She had written it herself, the night after the customs officer — Vessany's last clerk, his stamp worn nearly smooth — had pressed ink to their passage and said, in a voice like a man setting down a weight carried for sixty years: *the account closes when someone records that it closed.*

Someone had to record it. That was the whole religion of ledgers, and Isandro Brightwater was its last devout.

She dipped her pen. Beneath the entry for the door out of Vessany — beneath *paid: one guild entire, one inheritance, surrendered freely* — she wrote:

**Paid in full.**

She looked at it for a while. Then, because a ledger should be honest about its arithmetic, she added a second line beneath it, smaller:

**Received in exchange: everything not listed.**

Wren leaned over her shoulder, smelling of tar and honey cake theft. "That's not standard accounting practice."

"No," Isandro agreed. "It's better."

Vesek, mending a net by the fire — the man had discovered nets and would not be parted from them — grunted the grunt he used instead of laughing. Ossa was already halfway asleep in her chair with Sefa curled against her side, the child having extracted a promise of a fox lesson tomorrow and a goat lesson the day after, "because if goats are structurally sad somebody should ask them about it."

Isandro closed the old ledger and did not feel the grief she'd braced for. What she felt was lighter and stranger: the sensation of a book finishing, and another one waiting.

Because there was a new book. There had been for months. It sat in her room on the shelf where the old one used to live in Vessany's guildhouse, and it was not a ledger of debts at all. It was a ledger of names — every soul the old guild had wronged, every debt unjustly held, every impossible thing extracted from someone who couldn't refuse — and beside each name, in her careful hand, either *restored*, or *in progress*, or, in one case that made Wren furious every single time she saw it, *pending, correspondent uncooperative.*

Restitution was slow work. It was also, Isandro had discovered, the only work that had ever felt like hers.

She blew out the lamp. Through the window, the valley lay silvered under a rising moon, the river loud with meltwater, the ice going out of the high passes one crack at a time. Somewhere on the slope, a fox was teaching itself patience, and somewhere below the birches, a child was dreaming in a language older than any border.

The door out of Vessany still stood in its frozen marsh, and the clerk still stamped travelers through, and lately — word came down the ferry road in fragments, in letters, in the testimony of exhausted people who had walked out of the dark with nothing — lately the travelers were coming more often. Gift-bearers. The hunted. People whose countries had erased them. They came to the customs house with no papers at all, and the old clerk looked at them, and stamped the air in front of their hearts, and pointed them up the valley road.

Isandro had started keeping a bed ready. Then two. Then four.

Tomorrow, she thought, drowsing in her chair, she would write to the world they'd left — the letter Wren kept insisting they owed the other side, the one that began *to anyone still hiding: the door opens both ways now.* Tomorrow she would take Sefa to meet the goats. Tomorrow the ferry would cross at noon with fares negotiable and Wren paying double.

Tonight, the last line of the old ledger read *paid in full*, and the first line of the new one was already dry:

**Name: unknown girl, age six, heard the goats weeping and was not believed. Status: believed.**

Outside, the thaw went on all night, water talking to water in a hundred small voices, and Isandro slept without locking anything, and understood, at last, that this too was accounting: everything given away, and somehow, impossibly, more remaining than when she'd begun.
