## 5. What the Dying Man Owed

The map did not sleep.

Vesper had pinned it open above his worktable in the rented room above the chandler's shop, and all night it seemed to breathe — the ink shifting like something alive beneath vellum, roads rearranging themselves when he wasn't looking directly at them. He had tried to copy it three times. Three times his hand had refused, or perhaps the map had refused him; the lines came out wrong, trembling, as though he were attempting to transcribe a scream into sheet music.

So instead he studied it. He was good at studying. It was the one thing the guild had ever truly given him — not the title, not the ledger-desk with its brass fittings and its view of nothing, but the discipline of looking. *Look longer than is comfortable,* Seraphine used to say in her lecture voice. *The truth of a place lives in what you keep wanting to look away from.*

He looked.

The map was drawn on skin. He had known this since the second hour, when the candlelight caught the fine grain of pores along one margin and he had to set down his magnifier and breathe for a while with his head between his knees. Human skin, cured and stretched thin as paper, and upon it a country that matched no atlas in the guild's collection: a mountain rendered in strokes so fine they might have been made with a single hair, descending in concentric rings like the growth-lines of a tree cut open. Roads spiraled inward toward a black point at the heart of the rings, and every road was annotated in a hand small enough to require the loupe — annotations not of distance but of *price*. *Two memories.* *One name, freely given.* *A year, unspoiled.* *A firstborn's breath.*

And at the outermost ring, where the mountain's foothills met a border of stitched scar-tissue, one annotation was written larger than the rest, underlined twice, in ink that had gone brown with age:

*What the dying man owed.*

Below it, a symbol Vesper recognized from his guild training and had never once seen used outside an examination hall: the cartographic mark for a *debtor's door* — a threshold drawn only on maps of places that kept accounts. Places that remembered what you carried into them.

He had spent the night cross-referencing. That was the comfort of habit, the warm bath of procedure: pull the old surveys, check the marginalia, compare hands. The stranger's handwriting appeared nowhere else in the guild archives. But the *symbol* appeared eleven times across four centuries of restricted ledgers — the same ledgers Vesper had fled from two nights ago, the ones that recorded souls as line items, debits and credits in a currency no honest bank would touch. Eleven debtor's doors. Eleven entries. And beside each entry, in the column where a clerk would normally record the settlement date, the same word, repeated like a refrain:

*Outstanding.*

Vesper lit his fourth candle of the morning and told himself the shaking in his hands was the cold.

* * *

The clue took him east out of the city before noon, through streets still slick with last night's rain, past the guildhall — he crossed to the far side of the avenue rather than walk beneath its eaves, and hated himself for the flinch, and kept walking. The map's annotation gave no address. It gave a *bearing*: the debtor's door faced the rising sun, and stood "where the salt-seller's cart once overturned." Which was madness, because carts overturned everywhere and always had, and no survey could pin a location to a spilled sack of salt from a century ago.

But the map was not a survey. The map was a *deed*, and deeds did not describe the world; they described *claims upon it*.

He followed the bearing through the tanners' quarter, where the smell of lime and hides made his eyes water, and up a street so narrow the upper storeys leaned together overhead like conspirators. Here the city grew older and poorer by degrees, the way sediment grows older as you dig. And here — wedged between a coffin-maker's and a house whose windows had been bricked over with such care that it looked less abandoned than *embalmed* — he found the door.

It was ordinary. That was the horror of it. A plain door of grey wood, paint peeling, a knocker shaped like a leaping fish worn smooth by centuries of hands. No sign, no number. But the doorstep was salt — a crust of it, white and crystalline, spilling from the threshold like snow that refused to melt, and when Vesper crouched and touched it, the crystals were warm.

Salt preserved. Salt killed. He had learned both uses in the same guild lecture, long ago, and had thought it merely an interesting fact.

He knocked.

For a long moment, nothing. Then, from within, a voice — a girl's voice, young but frayed at the edges, like cloth washed too many times: "If you've come about the debt, we're paid. We're paid, we're *paid*, go away."

"I haven't come about—" Vesper stopped. Lied badly. Started again. "I knew your father."

Silence, long enough that he counted his own heartbeats against the dripping of a gutter somewhere above. Then bolts — three of them, drawn in sequence, top to bottom, a ritual of fear performed so often it had worn grooves in the wood — and the door opened the width of a hand.

One eye regarded him. Dark, sharp, and ringed with exhaustion, set in a face that was — Vesper's breath caught — *wrong*. Not ill. Wrong. The girl's skin had the translucence of very fine paper held to a flame; the lamp behind her glowed faintly *through* her cheekbone, through the edge of her jaw, as though she were becoming a lantern of herself. Where the light passed through her, he could see the ghost of veins like rivers on a map.

"You didn't know my father," she said. "Nobody knew my father. People *owed* my father, and people were owed *by* him, and now he's dead on a guild floor somewhere and I can't even bury him because they won't release the body until—" She stopped herself. Swallowed. The light moved inside her throat. "Who are you?"

"Vesper Villalobos." The name felt strange in his mouth now, heavier, like a coin he'd discovered was clipped. "I'm a cartographer. Your father died in the guild's map room. He gave me this."

He held up the map.

The eye widened. The door opened another hand's width, then another, and then the girl stood before him entire, and Vesper saw the full extent of what the lamplight had only suggested. She was fading. Not metaphorically — *literally*, the way a drawing fades, the way ink goes grey and then to nothing. Her left hand was worst; he could see the doorframe through her fingers, could see the street beyond it, slightly bent, as though viewed through old glass. She wore a man's coat buttoned to the throat despite the mild day, and around her neck, on a cord, hung a small brass token stamped with the same symbol he'd found in the ledgers: the debtor's door.

"You're the one he gave it to," she whispered. "He said — he said if anything happened, if he didn't come back, someone would come. Someone who could read it." Her eyes searched his face with a desperate, terrible hope. "Can you read it? Can you get her back?"

"Get *who* back?"

"My soul." She said it the way other people said *my hat*, flatly, already tired of the absurdity of having to say it at all. "They took most of it. They left me enough to walk and talk and pay the interest, which is all I am now, mostly — a walking interest payment." She laughed, and the laugh had no floor under it. "I'm Ilsa. Ilsa Brandt. Were you followed?"

"No."

"Then you're the first honest person I've met in a year. Come in before the street notices you."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of salt and lamp oil and something else — something mineral and cold, like the air off a glacier. Every window was shuttered. Every mirror was turned to the wall. On the kitchen table, amid the debris of a life half-lived: cold tea, a birdcage with its door open and no bird, and stacks upon stacks of paper. Letters. Petitions. Copies of contracts, each one annotated in a firm, careful hand Vesper recognized from the map's margins — the dead man's hand.

"He wrote to them for eleven months," Ilsa said, following his gaze. She sat across from him, folding her faded hands on the table so he wouldn't have to watch the light pass through them, though he watched anyway; he couldn't help it. It was like watching a coastline erode in fast motion. "Petitions, appeals, formal requests for renegotiation. My father believed in paperwork the way other men believe in saints." A bitter smile. "He was a toll-keeper on the eastern causeway. Thirty years counting coins and keeping books. When mother died, there wasn't money for the physician, and there was this — a broker who came through town, very respectable, very sympathetic, offering a loan against 'future assets.' Father signed. He thought *future assets* meant the harvest. Or the house." She turned the brass token over and over. "It meant me."

"The court beneath the mountain."

"So you *have* read the ledgers." Something flickered in her face — not surprise; recognition. The look of one drowning swimmer sighting another. "Then you know how it works. The debt compounds. Interest accrues in soul-weight. They took a portion at signing — that's standard, they call it the *earnest* — and they take more every tithe-night, and the portions don't grow back. You can't earn a soul back the way you earn coin. There's no wage for it." She looked up at him, and her eyes were the clearest part of her, dark and furious and alive in a face going transparent. "Father tried to buy me back. Did you know that? He spent eleven months petitioning, and when they refused the petitions, he tried to *trade*. He went to the market under the hill — the bazaar — and sold years. His own years. One after another. I watched him age a decade in a season. And at the end, when he'd sold almost everything, they told him the truth: that a bought soul isn't merchandise you can redeem. It's *collateral*. And collateral isn't sold back. Collateral is *held* until the debt is discharged, and the debt never discharges, because that's how it was designed."

Her voice cracked on the last words, and she pressed her lips together and mastered it, and Vesper understood suddenly that she had mastered it many times before, alone, in this shuttered house, and that the mastery itself was a kind of fading.

"He came to the city to steal it back," she said quietly. "That's what the map was for. He drew it himself, from memory, from everything he'd learned in eleven months of asking questions that got people hanged. He said a deed could be stolen just like any other document. He said if he could reach the archive, he could find our entries and amend them." A pause. "You found him dying. What did he say?"

"'The tithe is due.'"

"When?" Her whole body went rigid.

"Three nights from now. Tithe-night. The new moon."

Ilsa closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the fury in them had been forged into something harder and colder and more useful than grief. "Then hear me, cartographer, because I will only say this once. In three nights, they take the rest of me. Whatever portion remains — whatever's still enough to be *me* — it goes into their ledger, and I become what my father became at the end: a body that walks and pays and cannot remember why it bothers. I have seen it. I watched the neighbors' boy go through it. He smiled all the time afterward. He smiled *all the time*, and there was nobody home behind the smiling." She leaned forward, and where her shadow should have fallen on the table there was only a smudge, a watermark of a shadow, faint as breath on glass. "My father gave you that map because he ran out of time to use it himself. So either you came here to tell me to make my peace, in which case you can see yourself out — or you came because some part of you has read those ledgers and knows exactly what it feels like to be a line item in someone else's book."

Vesper said nothing. He thought of his own name in the asset column. *Sold by an ancestor named Villalobos.* He thought of Seraphine's voice, cool as ledger-paper: *you'll be erased, not saved.*

"I thought so," Ilsa said.

"It's not that simple." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "The map leads to the mountain. Getting in is one thing. The archive is defended by — I don't know what. The ledgers *scream*, Ilsa. I opened one and it screamed my name. Everything in that court knows what it owns, and it knows when its property moves. If I go in there, I'm not a thief walking into a vault. I'm a runaway horse walking back into the stable, and every groom in the building knows my brand."

"Then you understand me perfectly." She smiled, and it was the first real smile he'd seen from her, crooked and exhausted and utterly without self-pity. "We're both collateral, cartographer. We're both outstanding debts. The only question is whether we spend what's left of ourselves hiding in shuttered houses, or whether we spend it *walking*." She stood, and the lamplight shone through her shoulder blades like sun through a curtain, and she was, Vesper thought, the most solid person he had ever met. "Tithe-night is in three days. The mountain is four days' walk. So we leave tonight, and we walk fast, and you spend the whole journey teaching me to read that map, because my father drew it and I'm the only one left who thinks the way he thought."

"And if we fail?"

"Then we fail loudly, in the middle of their own court, and make them explain to *themselves* why a pair of line items was worth all the fuss." She was already moving, pulling a satchel from under the bed, filling it with the economy of someone accustomed to leaving quickly. "There's one more thing. The road to the mountain — the real road, the one the map shows — it doesn't start at any gate or crossroads. It starts where you stop being able to turn back. My father marked it with a symbol. Show me."

Vesper unrolled the map on the kitchen table, weighting the corners with the teacup and the birdcage. Together they bent over it — the cartographer and the collateral, the man with no claim on his own name and the girl with no claim on her own soul — and the candle between them burned straight and steady, as if it, too, understood that from here on, every hour was being spent, not saved.

"There," Vesper said, and put his finger on the mark. "The debtor's door. We're standing on it."

Isla looked down at the salt-crusted threshold beneath the floorboards, and then at him, and nodded once.

"Of course we are," she said. "He always did keep the important things close to home."

Outside, unseen by either of them, the last of the afternoon light slid off the bricked-over windows across the street, and somewhere far beneath the earth, in a hall of obsidian and grain-filed souls, a bell that no surface ear could hear began, very softly, to count.
