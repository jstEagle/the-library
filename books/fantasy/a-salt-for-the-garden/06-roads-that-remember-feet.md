## 6. Roads That Remember Feet

The road began where the last map ended, which Ilsa decided was either a very clever joke or a very cruel one.

They had walked out of the low country at dawn, when the fog still lay in the ditches like spilled milk, and Vesper had consulted his instruments three times before noon — the brass compass that pointed not north but *toward obligation*, the folding rule that measured distance in years rather than miles, the little book of blank pages that was, he insisted, the only truly honest map in the guild's possession. Ilsa had watched him do it all with the solemn precision of a priest performing rites, and she had thought, not unkindly, that she had never seen a man so afraid of being wrong.

By afternoon, the road had stopped cooperating.

It was a farmer's road, or it had been once: rutted clay, hedged with hawthorn gone feral, a stone wall on the left that ran on and on with the patient stubbornness of things built by dead men. The sky above it was the color of pewter, and the wind smelled faintly of woodsmoke and wet wool — smells that Ilsa knew, that sat in her chest like a hand pressed flat against her sternum. *Home* smells, though home was a burned farmhouse two hundred miles behind them and a father two days dead in a cartographer's guildhall.

"The map says the mountain is northeast," Vesper said, for the fourth time. He held the dead man's map open against his knee, and the ink on it moved when he wasn't looking — Ilsa had seen it, little silver threads crawling like frost across glass, rearranging themselves the way a sleeping person rearranges their limbs.

"The map is a liar," Ilsa said. "Or a teacher. My father said the roads past the low country remember feet. Every foot that ever walked them."

"Roads don't remember."

"Roads here do. That's the whole trouble with this country, Master Villalobos. Everything remembers, and nothing forgives."

She said it lightly, but the truth of it sat in her mouth like a stone. She was still fading. She could feel it most in the mornings, when she held her hands up against the gray light and the light came *through* them, faintly, the way it comes through a curtain. Her edges had gone soft. Yesterday she had dropped a cup in the inn at Harrow's Ford and it had passed through her fingers and broken on the floor, and the innkeeper's wife had looked at her with that particular pity reserved for the dying and the mad, and Ilsa had laughed, because what else was there to do.

Vesper had not laughed. Vesper had picked up every shard of the cup with his bare hands, wrapped them in his handkerchief, and put the bundle in his satchel as though broken crockery were evidence.

He was like that. She was beginning to understand that about him. He catalogued grief. He filed it. He could not fix a single thing that had happened to either of them, and so he was fixing the *record* of it, as if the record were the thing that mattered.

Maybe, she thought, watching him fold the map along its silvered creases, maybe for him it was.

* * *

The road changed in the last hour of daylight.

It did not announce the change. That was the unsettling part. One moment Ilsa's boots were crunching over clay and the hawthorn was scratching at her sleeves, and the next the clay was cobbles — worn, round-shouldered cobbles, slick with old rain — and the hawthorn was a garden wall, and the wall was hung with roses that had no business blooming in this season or this country.

Ilsa stopped walking. Vesper walked four more steps before he noticed he was walking alone, which told her something about how much attention he paid to the world when his attention was promised to a page.

"Stop," she said.

He stopped. He looked back at her, then at the road, and she watched him do the thing he did — the rapid inventory, the cataloguing of the impossible. His eyes went wall, roses, cobbles, wall again, and his hand drifted to the compass in his pocket, and she could see the exact moment he understood that the compass was going to be useless.

"This isn't on the map," he said.

"No."

"Roads don't do this."

"You keep saying that." She touched the garden wall. The stone was warm under her palm, warm as sun-baked stone at evening, though the day was cold and dying. "It's the test. My father told me about it. The road to the Undermountain doesn't just carry you, it *asks* you. It shows you home. And if you can't — " she searched for the words her father had used, half-drunk by a fire years ago, words she had taken for a fable — "if you can't give it something true, it stops carrying you. You walk forever on a road that goes nowhere, remembering."

"Remembering what?"

"Whatever it gives you." She looked down the cobbled lane, at the roses, at the warm stone. Her throat tightened. "It's showing me mine already. Look — that's the wall from the house. My mother painted the gate blue and my father complained about it for eleven years and never once repainted it."

There it was, ahead of them: a blue gate, paint peeling in exactly the way she remembered, in exactly the places she remembered, because she had peeled it herself as a child, one flake at a time, sitting on the step with her knees up.

Vesper looked at the gate for a long moment. Then he looked at her, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — something that wasn't pity and wasn't envy and wasn't quite either of those things.

"Then we turn back," he said.

"We can't turn back. The road doesn't allow it. You know it doesn't — look behind us."

He looked. So did she. The cobbles behind them ran on into a fog that had not been there a moment ago, a fog with no wall in it, no hawthorn, no low country, no anything. Just gray, and the faint suggestion that the road continued, and the certain knowledge that if they walked into it they would walk until they forgot why they were walking.

Vesper's jaw did the thing it did when he was reorganizing his fear into categories. Ilsa was getting good at reading him. Fear, she had learned, sat in the man like water in a well — deep, cold, and carefully covered over.

"Then we go forward," he said. "Through your — through the memory. And we keep walking until it asks me something, and then I'll answer it, and we'll be through."

She wanted to tell him. She wanted to say, *it's not a question you answer, it's a question you pay, and I don't know what you have to pay with.* But the light was going, and the blue gate stood open, and behind it she could see the kitchen garden, the bean rows, the crooked apple tree her father had sworn he would cut down and had defended from every axe for twenty years.

"I have to do this alone," she said. "The road doesn't share memories. It tests one traveler at a time. You'll have to wait at the gate."

"And if it takes you?"

"Then it takes me." She made her voice light, and hated how thin the lightness was. "I'm already half theirs, Master Villalobos. There's not that much left to take."

He flinched. She hadn't meant it as a flinch-word. But he flinched, the way he always did when she spoke about her fading plainly, as though plainness itself were a discourtesy.

"Vesper," he said.

She blinked. "What?"

"My name. You keep saying Master Villalobos. It's — " he stopped, and she watched him struggle with it, this man who could measure the world down to its hairs and could not measure out a sentence of feeling without visible labor. "It's a name they gave me. A name that's owed to someone else. While we're on a road that remembers, I'd rather — I'd rather not owe it anything."

Something in Ilsa's chest turned over. She thought of her father, who had spent the last years of his life trying to buy back the one thing that couldn't be bought, and who had called her *little wren* to the very end, because her true name was a debt too, and he had wanted her to have one word that was free.

"All right," she said. "Vesper."

She walked through the blue gate.

* * *

The memory took her the way sleep takes a child — gently, and all at once.

She was nine years old and it was harvest and the kitchen smelled of roasted apples and her mother was singing, badly, the way she always sang, and her father was sharpening a scythe on the step with that slow rhythmic shhhk, shhhk that Ilsa had slept to for whole summers. The light was the light of late September, gold and low and full of floating dust, and everything was so exactly, so mercilessly itself that Ilsa stood in the middle of her own kitchen and wept.

Because she knew what it was. She knew it was the road wearing her home like a dress. She knew her mother had been four years dead and her father two days, and that this kitchen existed now only in the holding-places of things, in the memory of roads and the ledgers of courts, filed and catalogued and *owned*.

And the road — the road asked her. She felt it as a pressure, a hand laid flat against her sternum where the home-smells lived. *What will you give?* it asked, in the voice of the scythe and the singing and the apples. *What is true?*

She thought about what she had. Not much. A fading girl's memories, a dead man's debt, a stranger's stubbornness. She reached into the middle of it, into the truest truest thing, and she gave the road the smell of the apples. Not the whole memory — she kept the singing, she kept the scythe, she kept her mother's face. But the smell, the warm cinnamon-and-smoke smell of roasted apples in September, she pulled out of herself like a splinter and held out in her two soft-edged hands.

The road took it.

The kitchen went dim by degrees, the way a fire dies, and the singing faded mid-verse, and her mother's face was the last thing to go, turning toward her with an expression that was not reproach, because it was not anything, because it was only memory doing what memory does when it is spent.

Ilsa came out the far side of the gate into the ordinary gray evening, onto ordinary clay, and the road went on ahead of them, patient as ever, and she was crying without any sound, because the kitchen was gone now — not dead, not lost, but *spent*, and there was a difference, and the difference was that she had done it herself.

Vesper was standing where she'd left him. He had taken off his coat, she saw, and folded it, and was holding it out.

"You're cold," he said.

"I'm always cold now."

"Then take it anyway."

She took it. It was too big and it smelled of ink and lamp-oil and the particular clean dustiness of map drawers, and she wrapped it around herself and said nothing, because he had said nothing, because between the two of them there had grown a language made entirely of things not said, and it was, she suspected, the only language either of them was fluent in.

"The road will ask you next," she said, when she could speak.

"Yes."

"Vesper. Listen to me. It doesn't ask questions. It asks for *payment*. Something true. Something warm. A memory of home, or the smell of one, or — I don't know. Something you'd rather keep." She looked up at him. "Do you have that? Something like that?"

And she watched him consider the question with the same terrible honesty he brought to everything, watched him actually *search*, methodically, the way he'd search a drawer — and watched him come up empty, and watched him understand that he'd come up empty, and watched him decide, in the space of a single breath, that he was going to walk forward anyway.

"I have the guild," he said.

"Vesper — "

"I have the map room at closing, when the light comes through the west windows. I have the smell of the ink. I have the ledger-room at dawn." His voice was level, but she could hear the labor in it, the sound of a man carrying something heavy up many stairs. "That's home. It's the only home I've had. If the road wants it, it can have it."

"That's not — " She stopped. Because what was she going to say? *That's not warm enough? That's not true enough?* It was true. It was the truest thing he had, and that was the whole tragedy of him, folded up small enough to fit in a sentence: his warmest memory was a room where he worked, and his family was an institution that had sold him.

"It will have to do," he said, "because it's what I have. Come on. The light's going."

* * *

The road asked him at moonrise.

Ilsa knew it the moment it happened, though nothing visible changed. The clay lane simply *stilled*, the way a held breath is still, and the wind dropped, and the hawthorn hedges leaned in — actually leaned, she saw, their thorned branches curving inward like cupped hands — and the moonlight pooled on the road in front of Vesper in a wide silver circle, like a coin laid down for payment.

Vesper stepped into the circle.

Ilsa stood at its edge, inside his folded coat, and watched.

She saw the road show him things. She couldn't see what they were — the circle was private, that was the mercy of it and the cruelty — but she saw his face by moonlight, and his face was an open book to her now, she realized, this man who thought himself illegible. She saw him look at something with an expression of such naked, unguarded longing that her heart broke a little, right there in the hedge-shadow. Whatever the road had shown him, he had wanted it. He had wanted it the way the starving want bread — without art, without dignity, entirely.

Then his expression changed. It went from longing to confusion to a kind of bleak, methodical despair, and she understood, before he said a word, before anything happened at all: the road was asking, and he was searching, and he was finding nothing.

The circle waited. The moon waited. The hedges waited, their thorns curved like patient fingers.

"Come on," Ilsa whispered. "Give it something. Give it anything. Lie to it if you have to, give it my kitchen, give it my apples, give it — "

Vesper knelt down in the silver circle. She saw him take out the little book of blank pages — the honest map, the only honest one — and hold it out flat on his palms, the way you'd hold out bread, or a bird.

"This," he said, and his voice carried strangely in the still air, thin and clear. "This is the only thing I ever made that wasn't owed to anyone. It's blank. It's nothing. It's the only honest thing I own. It's not a memory. It's not warm. I know it isn't what you ask for. But it's true, and it's mine, and I'm offering it."

The road did not move.

The silence stretched so long Ilsa's heart began to hurt. She saw the moonlight flicker over the blank pages, once, twice, like a reader turning down a corner. She saw Vesper kneel there with his offering — a book of nothing, a home of nowhere, the whole poverty of his life held out in two steady hands — and she felt the pity rise in her again, the pity that had been in her since the gate, pity for this man with no kitchen and no singing and no scythe-sounds, this man whose warmest thing was a room that had betrayed him.

But under the pity, something else was rising. Something with more spine in it.

Because he wasn't leaving the circle. She could see it in his shoulders, in the set of his knees. The road was refusing him, and the refusal must have felt like the whole world refusing him, the way it had always refused him — the clerks with their jokes about his lack of family name, the guild that kept him like a tool, the court that owned his soul like a sack of grain — and he was *kneeling there anyway*, offering his nothing with both hands, too stubborn to be ashamed of his poverty, too stubborn even to stand up.

The moonlight flickered a third time. And Ilsa saw — she was almost sure she saw, in the silver shimmer of it — the faintest impression of a small room with one west-facing window, and a boy at a desk with a pen, and the boy was alone, and the boy was *working*, and the work was the warmest thing in the picture, and the road looked at it the way a strict teacher looks at a wrong answer that is, nonetheless, honest.

The circle dimmed.

Not accepted. Not refused. Dimmed — the way a lamp dims when someone considers it, when someone turns the wick down to see if the flame is real.

And then the road moved.

It did not open. It *rearranged* — Ilsa felt it in her teeth, a deep grating shudder through the ground, and the hawthorn hedges swung back, and the clay lane unspooled ahead of them into the darkness, northeast now, unmistakably northeast, toward a smudge on the horizon that might have been cloud and might have been a mountain.

Vesper stood up. He put the blank book away with hands that were not quite steady. He did not look at her.

"It let us pass," he said.

"It considered you," Ilsa said. "That's not the same thing. It let you through because you were too stubborn to leave."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough, and she understood that he was not rough with grief, exactly, or not only grief.

"It showed me something," he said. "In the circle. It showed me — I think it was supposed to be a memory of home. But it wasn't mine. It was a *guild* memory. A hall of clerks, and everyone had a family name, and there was a boy in the corner with no name, and the road couldn't find anything else, so it showed me that." He looked down the dark road, at the mountain-smudge. "That's what it thinks my home is. That's what *I* think my home is. A corner in a room full of people who had what I didn't."

"Vesper — "

"It's fine." He started walking, and she walked with him, his folded coat still around her shoulders. "It's a fair accounting. I've always kept fair accounts."

They walked in silence for a while. The road was carrying them now — Ilsa could feel it, a faint tilt underfoot, a helpfulness in the gradient, the road *wanting* them forward. It had taken her apples and it had considered his nothing and it had decided, in its slow vegetable way, that they were worth carrying.

"Vesper," she said at last. "What you did in there. Kneeling. Offering a blank book to a road that wanted your heart."

"It was all I had."

"I know." She looked at his profile against the moonlight, this meticulous, stubborn, unfriended man, and the pity in her chest had turned into something else entirely, something that sat straighter. "Most men would have lied. Most men would have stolen one of my memories to feed it. You gave it the truest thing you own, and your truest thing is a blank page, and you weren't even ashamed."

"I was ashamed."

"I know," she said again. "That's what I mean."

Ahead of them, the mountain-smudge had resolved itself into something with actual weight, a blackness against the lesser blackness of the sky, and the road ran toward it straight and unswerving now, done with games, done with memories, as if the road itself knew that the country ahead had no use for warmth.

And then Ilsa saw it — off the road's shoulder, half-sunk in the moorland, a light. Not a house-light. Not a hearth. A cold, greenish glow, the color of foxfire, the color of deep water, and beside it, where no shrine should be on a road that ran through nothing, stood a low stone structure with a dark doorway, and something in the doorway was moving.

Vesper stopped. So did she. The road under their feet had gone very still, the stillness of a thing that has delivered a traveler to a border and will go no further.

"What is that?" Vesper asked.

Ilsa's father's voice came back to her from some fireside, some half-drunk fable, and she heard her own voice answer him as if from a long way off:

"That's the shrine," she said. "The one at the border of the Undermountain's country. My father said you don't pass it for free." She swallowed. "He said the thing that lives there doesn't want your memories."

The green light pulsed, slow as breathing.

"It wants your name."
