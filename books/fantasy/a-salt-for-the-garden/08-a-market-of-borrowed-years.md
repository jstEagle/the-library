## 8. A Market of Borrowed Years

I had thought, until that night on the ferry, that I understood what it meant to be fading. I had lived with it for three years — the way my reflection lagged a half-second behind me in shop windows, the way candles burned straight through my outline as if I were made of cheap glass. Fading was subtraction. It was being erased line by line from a drawing, gently, by someone patient.

But when the ferry-creature spoke Vesper's name across the black water — his *guild* name, the one he'd paid away at the shrine like a coin bitten between teeth — I watched something worse than subtraction happen to him. I watched him be *unwritten*.

He did not cry out. That was the first thing I noticed, and I have thought about it every day since. The name left him the way blood leaves a man who has decided not to bleed: quietly, thoroughly, and without his permission but very much with his consent. The letters that had been stitched into the creature's skin — all thousand borrowed voices — shivered once, and then somewhere among them a new thread appeared, written small and neat in cartographer's ink: *Vesper Villalobos, Third Assistant to the Office of Boundaries.*

And then even that was gone.

He sat back down beside me in the boat and put his hands flat on his knees, and he looked at the water, and I realized I was watching a man discover the shape of a wound only after the knife was out.

"You didn't have to do that," I said.

"Yes, I did." His voice sounded strange to both of us, I think. Lighter. Like a room after furniture has been carried out. "It's done."

The ferryman began to row.

---

We came ashore at dawn — or what the underworld offered instead of dawn, which was a slow brightening of the cavern ceiling far above us, like embers deciding to remember fire. And there, spread along the shore of the last dark river, stood the market.

I had heard my father speak of it once, late, after wine, in the voice he saved for things he'd sworn never to discuss. He called it the Bazaar of the Borrowed. He said you could buy anything there except something new. Everything for sale had belonged to someone first: years, regrets, names, the memory of a mother's kitchen, the specific weight of a sleeping child on your chest. Nothing was created beneath the mountain. Everything was *resold*.

I hadn't believed him. I had been nine, and he had been crying, and I had chosen not to believe him because believing would have meant asking why he knew so much about it.

Now I walked into it, and I believed him.

The bazaar filled a hollow between two ribs of the mountain like water filling a cupped hand. There was no sky, so the merchants had hung lanterns of bottled dusk — and yes, I recognized them, the same jars Vesper had catalogued in that guild warehouse a lifetime ago, or however long ago it was; time down here is a rumor and we'd stopped checking it against our pulses — and the light they gave off was the color of an evening you can't get back. Stalls crowded the shore in crooked rows. Some were tents. Some were just blankets. One was an open coffin, and the merchant inside it sat up to hawk her wares, which were, she announced in a voice like dry paper, "secondhand sunsets, barely used, owner deceased."

The crowd was worse than the stalls. It moved like weather. There were things in it wearing robes, and things in it wearing skin, and things whose robes and skins I could not distinguish. A tall figure with scales where its face should have been weighed out spoonfuls of sleep. Children — or the shapes of children, translucent at the edges, the way I now was too — ran between the stalls stealing glimpses of themselves from mirrors that screamed when touched.

"Stay close," Vesper said.

"I'm not going anywhere," I said. "Look at me. I'm going everywhere *slowly*."

That was supposed to be funny. He didn't laugh. He looked down at my hands — I followed his gaze — and saw what I had been hiding since the shrine: that my fingertips no longer quite ended. They went soft at the edges, like wet paper, like the last inch of a candle's wick. The claim on my soul wasn't waiting anymore. It was *feeding*.

"We find the galleries," he said. "The map said the archive sits above the tithe-hall, up through the upper galleries. We buy a way in tonight, and tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow is tithe-eve," I said. "Your map says so. The dates don't move, Vesper. Only the roads do."

"Then we move faster than the dates." He said it the way he wrote his ledger entries: flat, certain, as though certainty were simply a matter of good penmanship. And then he stopped walking, because directly in front of us, occupying the space where the road widened toward the market's heart, was a stall selling exactly one thing.

Years.

Not metaphorically. Not as trinkets. There was a shelf, and on the shelf were stoppered vials of every size, and inside them moved lights of every color, and each light was a year of somebody's life. A fat amber one glowed like honey held to a window — someone's golden summer, probably, some farmer's best harvest. A thin gray one flickered badly, the year of a fever, maybe, or a war. Near the end of the row, almost hidden, was a vial so pale it was nearly clear, and the merchant caught me looking and said, "That one's a year nobody wanted. Cost you nothing. Costs the taker everything."

Vesper stepped past it without slowing. But I saw his eyes catch on the shelf the way a thirsty man's eyes catch on a well. And I understood, with a lurch, what I had not let myself understand before:

He was paying for this journey out of himself. First his title. Now, if he needed to, his time. He had come down here with nothing but his skill and his stubbornness, and he was spending both like a man who has finally learned that hoarding gets you nothing but a tidy grave.

* *

The stall we needed found us before we found it. That's how the bazaar works, my father should have warned me; it smells want.

We were pushing through the crowd near the market's center when a voice slid out of the press of bodies, low and pleasant as oil poured into water:

"Cartographer."

Vesper stopped. So did I. In a place like this, being recognized is not flattering. Being recognized means you are *inventory*.

The stall was narrow and deep, hung with maps — real ones, old ones, their edges browned and soft as moths' wings. The merchant was a woman who might have been sixty or six hundred; her face was lined like a well-used chart, and her eyes had the particular shine of someone who has read everything and forgiven nothing. She was sorting a tray of compass needles that all pointed, I noticed, at Vesper.

"You've the look of the Guild," she said. "Or you *had* it. Someone's been peeling you like fruit." She smiled with too many teeth, kindly. "Sit anyway. You'll want what I have."

"We're not buying," I said.

"Everyone's buying." She turned the tray of needles idly. All of them swung together, tracking Vesper as he shifted his weight. "Question is what, and whether you can afford it. You want the upper galleries, boy. Don't insult me by pretending otherwise. Every soul that walks into my market wanting the Court looks at my stall within the hour. Want is a road here. It leads to me."

"How much?" Vesper said.

I could have hit him.

"*Don't*—" I started, and he raised one hand, not looking at me, and something in the gesture — the absolute, unremarkable authority of it, a man halting traffic over his own life — shut me up better than any argument.

"Name your price," he said to the merchant.

She leaned back. Behind her, hanging from the tent-pole like a trophy or a threat, was a rolled map sealed in lead-black wax, and even folded I could see the workmanship of it — the fine ruled lines, the careful hachures marking depth, the little precise lettering of someone trained the same way Vesper had been trained. A map of the Undermountain's upper galleries. The way in. The way through. Maybe — if the archivist's rumors my father had whispered held any truth — a way *out*.

"The usual price," the woman said, "is memory. A good one. Warm ones hold value down here; the cold courts can't counterfeit them. Or regret, if you're short on warmth — those trade heavy, but they change hands sour." Her eyes glittered. "But you've neither to spare, have you? I watched you at the shrine, cartographer. You gave up your name like a man taking off a coat he never liked. What's left of you is... spare parts." She tapped the counter. "So. One year."

The word landed on the shore of the market and lay there between us.

"One year of what?" I asked, though I knew. Everyone in that crowd knew. You could feel the whole bazaar lean in around us, hundreds of ears, thousands of lanterns guttering with interest.

"One year of his life," the merchant said pleasantly. "Taken off the end, mind — I'm not a butcher. He won't miss it till he misses it." She smiled again. "Standard rate. The Guild pays it all the time, for lesser goods. Ask your friend. Oh — forgive me. He can't be asked anymore, can he? No records." Her smile widened a fraction. "*Unrecorded men pay cash.*"

The crowd murmured, delighted, the way crowds are delighted by other people's wounds.

Vesper was quiet for a long moment. I watched his hands — those meticulous, ink-stained, beautiful hands — resting flat on the counter, and I watched the smallest tremor go through them and get suppressed, the way he suppressed everything, filing it neatly away in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes.

"It's a fair price," he said finally.

"It's a *terrible* price," I said. "You've already paid your name. You'll walk out of these galleries with nothing left of yourself to—" My voice cracked, which humiliated me, which made me angrier, which made me say the rest too fast: "I know what it is to be spent down to nothing by a bargain, Vesper. I *am* a bargain. Half-faded, remember? Collateral? I am what happens at the end of every deal struck under this mountain, and I am telling you not to sign."

"And I'm telling you," he said, still not raising his voice, still not looking away from the merchant, "that I intend to leave this mountain with you alive, and I cannot do that on empty pockets." He flexed his fingers once, like a man testing a rope before trusting it with his weight. "A year off the end. I'd have traded forty for it, if asked. This is the cheapest thing anyone's offered me since I came below."

"That's what they *want* you to think it is," I whispered.

"I know." And then he did look at me, and what was in his face stopped my breath — not fear, not recklessness, but something calmer and stranger than either. Something that had not been there in the guild hall when Seraphine Quill had offered to erase him politely. Something that had perhaps been born the moment his name dissolved in black water. "That's why it's mine to spend."

The merchant produced a scale. Old brass, worn smooth, its two pans hanging like cupped hands. Into one pan she set a single small vial, empty, plain glass, waiting.

"Blood or breath?" she asked Vesper. "For the measure."

"Breath," he said. "I'll need the blood later."

I didn't ask what for. Some questions you save like coins.

He breathed into the vial. She stoppered it and set it in the pan, and the balance dipped — one year, weighed and found sufficient — and she took up the lead-sealed map from the tent-pole and set it in the other pan, and the beam swung, and settled, and hung level.

"Even," she said. "Fancy that. A year of a man's life for the floor plan of his own prison. The mountain does love symmetry."

Vesper took the map. He broke the seal with his thumbnail, and unrolled it just enough to confirm what he needed, and I saw his eyes do the thing they always did — the flicker, the *cataloguing*, the whole world briefly becoming lines and distances and bearings — and then he rolled it closed and tucked it inside his coat, against his heart, where he kept the dying man's map and the fragment of page proving an ancestor's coercion.

"Pleasure doing business," the merchant said, pocketing the vial of his breath-year. Already the light in it was changing, turning gold at the edges, becoming *someone else's* summer.

We walked away. The crowd parted for us now with something like deference — or appetite, it was hard to tell the difference down there. Behind us I heard the merchant call out in her dry-paper voice, cheerful as a carrion bird:

"A bargain freely struck! Witnessed! *Witnessed!*"

And the whole bazaar took it up softly, hundreds of voices, a tide-lap of witnesses, sealing it into the mountain's memory forever: *witnessed, witnessed, witnessed.*

---

We walked a long way before either of us spoke. The lanterns thinned. The crowd thinned. Ahead, the galleries opened their first dark mouths in the mountainside, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and old ink.

Finally I said, "How did it feel?"

He considered the question honestly, which is more than most people would have done. "Lighter," he admitted. "Which frightens me more than heavier would."

"Why?"

"Because everything I've lost down here has made me lighter." He touched his coat, over the maps. "My name. My title. Now a year. I keep expecting to reach the point where there's nothing left to carry, and instead I keep finding—" he searched for the word, frowning at the dark like a proofreader at a misprint, "—*room*. As if I were a house being emptied for someone to move in."

"Into *what*?"

"I don't know yet." And then, quieter, in the voice of a man reading his own handwriting and not recognizing it: "But whoever moves in, Ilsa — I think they might be *me*. The real one. The one before the ledgers."

I looked at him — this stubborn, meticulous, half-unmade man who had given up everything a court and a guild had told him he was, and who walked lighter with every loss, toward the place designed to consume him — and for the first time since my father died whispering of tithes, I felt something that was not fear.

Hope is a dangerous thing to feel in the Undermountain. It shows, down here. Hope glows. I felt it anyway, faint as the palest vial on a merchant's shelf.

Ahead of us, the first gallery swallowed the lantern-light and gave nothing back. And somewhere above us — high in the dark, in the archive where souls sit filed like grain — something vast and patient turned a page, and waited to see what the unrecorded man would write next.
