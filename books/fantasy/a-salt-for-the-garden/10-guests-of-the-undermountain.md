## 10. Guests of the Undermountain

They gave us rooms in the guesting wing, which Ilsa knew — because her father had told her once, when she was small and he was drunk on cheap wine and cheaper memories — was not a kindness at all. *Guests*, in the Undermountain, were simply debts that still had feet.

Her chamber was beautiful. That was the first cruelty of it, and the court knew exactly what it was doing. The bed was hung in grey silk that moved without wind; the washbasin was carved from a single geode, amethysts glittering at its bottom like drowned stars; and the window — a real window, glass and all — looked out over a city of lanterns that never went dark because there was no morning to put them out. Someone had laid a fire. Someone had left a carafe of wine the color of old bruises. Someone had turned down the covers as if expecting her, and Ilsa understood with a cold sliding feeling in her stomach that they had been expecting her for a very long time. Since before her father died. Perhaps since before she was born.

She could see her own hands resting on the sill, and she made herself look at them properly, the way you look at a wound you're trying to decide whether to scream about. Her little finger had gone translucent. Not pale — *translucent*, like the tip of a candle flame seen through paper. When she pressed it against the stone it left no shadow.

*Two days*, she thought. *Tithe-night is two days away, and I am running out of self.*

She dressed in the clothes provided — of course there were clothes provided, grey wool and silver clasps, sized perfectly, which frightened her more than anything else so far — and went to find Vesper.

He was already in the corridor, standing perfectly straight with his hands folded behind his back like a man inspecting an inn he did not intend to stay in. But she had learned to read him in the weeks on the road, in the way you learn to read weather: the stillness of his shoulders meant calculation, not calm. His eyes were moving. Corridor, ceiling, torch brackets, the pattern of the black stone floor — left to right, right to left, again, again.

He's measuring, she realized. He can't help it. He's counting the steps between the doors.

"Good morning," she said, because under the mountain no one could say whether it was morning or not, and she had decided early that this would be her small rebellion: to insist on mornings.

"It isn't one," Vesper said mildly, "but good morning anyway." Then, quieter, without turning his head: "Fourteen paces from your door to mine. Sixty-two to the stair. Two guards at the landing, changed every hour, and they change *early* — by about forty heartbeats."

Ilsa blinked at him. "You've been awake all night counting."

"I slept badly." A pause. "The bed was too soft. It kept apologizing."

Despite everything — despite the translucency of her own finger, despite the wine-colored city outside — Ilsa laughed. It came out of her sideways, startled, and died quickly in the corridor's hush, but Vesper almost smiled, which for Vesper was roughly equivalent to weeping with joy.

"They're being polite," she said.

"They're being *precise*," he corrected. "Politeness is what precision looks like when it wants something." He offered his arm, formal as a dance instructor, and together they went down to be hosted.

* * *

Breakfast was served in a hall called the Long Courtesy, and Ilsa understood immediately why it bore that name: the courtesy went on and on, course after course, hour after hour, a procession of dishes she could not name — fish that tasted of honey and regret, bread warm as skin, fruits that burst with the exact flavor of some childhood summer none of them had actually lived. The court sat them at a table of polished obsidian beneath chandeliers of bone-white crystal, and their hosts drifted around them like smoke given manners.

The herald came first. He had recognized Vesper at the gates as "the asset," and he had not stopped smiling since, and his smile had too many corners to it, like a folded map creased wrong. This morning he wore grey and silver, matching their borrowed clothes exactly, which Ilsa suspected was meant to be funny.

"Our guests rest well?" the herald asked, pouring wine that steamed faintly, though the hall was warm.

"Well enough," Vesper said.

"The mountain is pleased. The mountain speaks often of you both." The herald's eyes slid to Ilsa, lingered on her fading hand where it rested beside her cup, and gleamed. "It has been so long since a claim came home. There will be a hearing, of course, before the tithe — the court honors its forms. And tonight, if you wish it, you may walk the galleries. The archive especially. We know how cartographers love archives."

He said it lightly. Too lightly. Ilsa watched Vesper's face do nothing at all, which was how she knew the offer mattered enormously.

"We would be honored," Vesper said, with a bow so smooth it could have been oiled.

When the herald drifted off to torment some other corner of the hall, Ilsa leaned close. "Why would they give us the archive?"

"Because they think it's a trap I'll spring on myself," Vesper murmured into his cup. "Everything in that place will be watched. Every shelf I touch will tell them what I want. They're inviting me to show my hand." He took a slow sip. "So I'll show them a hand. Just not the one they're dealing for."

* * *

The archive of the Undermountain occupied a space that should not have existed inside a mountain, and Vesper walked into it the way a starving man walks into a banquet — slowly, reverently, and with a plan for everything he intended to take.

Ilsa watched him do it, and watching him became, over the hours, the strangest education of her life.

The archive was vast in a way that argued with geometry. Shelves rose out of sight into a darkness that glowed faintly, as though the mountain itself had a dim idea burning behind its eyes. The files were not books. They were *sheaves* — bundles of thin grey leaves bound in salt-stiffened cord, each one humming very softly, a sound felt in the teeth more than heard. Souls, filed. Thousands upon thousands of them, sorted by debt, by date, by the cold arithmetic of who had sold whom and when. Somewhere in all of it was a sheaf with Vesper's name knotted into its cord. Somewhere else, one with hers.

And the archivist — the ancient clerk with dust in the seams of her face and centuries stacked behind her eyes — trailed them through the stacks like a courteous jailer, answering questions.

That was the part Ilsa could not puzzle out. The archivist *answered*. Freely, thoroughly, even warmly, whenever the herald wasn't near.

"The eastern galleries," Vester asked, in the tone of a man making pleasant scholarly conversation, "how are they organized? Chronologically?"

"By water," the archivist said. "There is a river down here, or what serves for one. It runs through the lowest halls, past the tithe-vault. Sound carries along it strangely. Anything spoken near the water can be heard three galleries away, sometimes four. The founders thought it efficient. We think of it as the mountain listening to itself."

"And the tithe-vault itself?"

"Directly below us, roughly. Nine levels down. But the stairs don't go straight — the galleries shift on tithe-night. The mountain rearranges itself for the ceremony. Paths that exist today will not exist tomorrow night, and paths that have never existed will open." She smiled, and there was something underneath the smile, something worn and bitter and old. "The court believes this makes the vault secure. In truth it makes the whole mountain a lock with one key, and the key is knowing the pattern."

"And does anyone know the pattern?" Vesper asked lightly.

"No one has ever needed to," the archivist said, and looked at him a moment too long.

They walked on. Ilsa kept half a step behind them both, playing the part she'd chosen — the fading girl, docile, overwhelmed, trailing after the scholar because she had nowhere else to go. It was not entirely a performance. She *was* fading; she could feel it now, a lightness in her limbs like the first stage of sleep, her edges going soft. But she kept her eyes open, and she had spent her life watching her father move through rooms full of dangerous men, so she knew how to look without appearing to.

What she saw was this: Vesper was not reading the files. He was reading the *rooms*.

At every junction he paused, ostensibly admiring the stonework, and his fingers rested briefly on the wall — and she saw the chalk-grey ghost of a line appear and vanish under his fingertip, so faint it might have been imagination. At every stairwell he glanced up and down, twice, unhurried. When the archivist mentioned the shifting galleries, he asked her to describe last tithe-night's arrangement in detail, "purely for scholarly interest," and listened with his whole body while she spoke — the order of openings, the sequence of closings, which galleries sealed first and which held longest.

He was memorizing a map that had never been drawn. No, Ilsa corrected herself, watching his eyes flicker left-right-left along a corridor of filed souls: he was memorizing a map that *could not* be drawn, because it only existed for one night, and no cartographer had ever been fool enough or brave enough to stand in this place with his eyes open when it happened.

Until now.

* * *

They broke for a meal at what the court called evening, served on a balcony overlooking the deep gallery — a vertiginous shaft where the shelves spiraled down into darkness like the inside of a shell. The food was exquisite. Ilsa ate almost none of it. Her stomach had developed opinions about Undermountain hospitality.

Below them, preparations were underway. She could hear it before she saw it: a low rhythmic sound, metal on stone, patient as tide. Workers — she could not call them people, though they had once perhaps been people — moved along the spiral paths, sweeping, arranging, carrying urns of salt to line the ceremonial ways. Salt, everywhere salt, poured in white ribbons along the gallery floors, marking boundaries she didn't yet understand. Lanterns were being hung in a pattern. A great bell, green with age, was being lowered on chains toward the vault below.

"Tithe-night," the herald said, materializing beside them with his folded-map smile. "You'll want to see it, I think. Both of you. Such occasions bring clarity." He refilled their cups unasked. "Your father attended once, Miss Ilsa. Did he ever tell you? Years ago. Before you were born. He stood on that very balcony."

Ilsa's hand stopped halfway to her cup.

"He watched the souls come down," the herald continued pleasantly, "and he met certain parties, and he made certain arrangements, and nine months later you were born already promised. He was a man who loved bargains, your father. He simply was never very good at them." The herald's gaze moved to her fading hand with the tenderness of a butcher appraising meat. "Do finish your wine. You'll need your strength, fading as you are. The court so dislikes waste."

He bowed and drifted away.

Ilsa sat very still. The balcony rail was cool under her translucent fingers, and somewhere below, the bell swung on its chains and did not ring.

"He's lying," she said finally. "Or he isn't. I don't know which is worse."

"Neither do I," Vesper said quietly. "But I noticed something he didn't intend to show us." He nodded downward, subtle. "Look at the workers laying the salt. Look at the pattern."

She looked. Ribbons of white, spiraling inward, crossing at intervals — and at each crossing, a knot-work of lines that tugged at something in her memory, something she couldn't place until suddenly she could: her father's maps. The old ones, the ones he burned. She had seen that lattice once, as a child, in a page half-consumed by fire, and her father had snatched it away and drunk for three days afterward.

"That's a claim-line," she whispered. "Like on a deed."

"Exactly like on a deed." Vesper's voice had gone very soft, the voice he used when the world resolved into lines and bearings. "The entire tithe-ceremony is drawn in salt. The galleries, the spiral, the vault below — it's one enormous document, Ilsa. The mountain doesn't *host* the tithe. The mountain *is written*, every tithe-night, fresh. Claim-lines renewed. Ownership redrawn." He paused, and she heard him swallow. "Which means it can be erased. Amended. Redrawn."

Ilsa turned to look at him fully. In the lantern-light his face was pale and absolutely steady, and his eyes reflected the salt-patterns below like a man seeing not a ceremony but a blank page waiting for a pen.

"You're going to rewrite it," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try." He tore a piece of bread he didn't eat. "Tomorrow night, while the galleries shift, while every path in this mountain opens and closes according to a pattern I am spending today learning by heart. One night. One chance. The map exists only during the tithe, and so does the moment to change it."

"The archivist," Ilsa said. "She told you the arrangement of last year's shift. Will it repeat?"

"She said no one has ever needed to know the pattern." Vesper smiled then, thin and sharp as a compass needle. "Which means someone *could* know it. Which means there is a pattern. Patterns repeat, Ilsa. Even here. Especially here — a court built on ledgers worships repetition. It's the only prayer they know."

* * *

That night — that court-designated night, grey silk and apologizing beds — Ilsa could not sleep, and around the hour when the lantern-city outside dimmed to embers, she gave up and padded barefoot to Vesper's door, intending only to sit somewhere with another heartbeat in it.

The door was ajar. Inside, Vesper sat cross-legged on the floor with his eyes closed, and he was drawing.

Not on paper. On the floor itself, in the air above the stones, his finger tracing lines over and over — the galleries, the stairs, the spiral, the crossings of salt — again and again and again, faster, until his hand blurred, until he was walking the whole underworld in miniature with nothing but a fingertip, sealing it into himself the way other men seal prayers into their bones. His lips moved silently. *Sixty-two paces. Nine levels. Water carries voices. Galleries seal east-first.*

Ilsa stood in the doorway and watched a man build a weapon out of memory, and she understood, with a tenderness that startled her, that this was what he had instead of sleep, instead of family, instead of a name anyone wanted. This was his whole self, poured into lines.

Then his eyes opened and found her, and he didn't stop drawing, and he didn't apologize.

"There's something you should hear before tomorrow," he said quietly. "Come away from the door. The walls in this wing listen." He waited until she sat across from him, her fading hands folded in her lap, and then he lowered his voice to almost nothing. "The archivist sent me a message. Hidden in the way she described the water."

Ilsa leaned in. "What did she say?"

Vesper's finger stilled, mid-stroke, over the invisible map of the vault where two souls lay filed like grain.

"She said: *"Ask the salt why it kills as well as keeps."*" He looked up at Ilsa, and in the dark his eyes were the color of the lantern-city at ember-hour, burning low and stubborn against an enormous dark. "And then she said the second thing. The thing that changes everything."

Outside, far below, the great bell swung silent on its chains, waiting for tithe-night.

"What second thing?" Ilsa whispered.

"The original ledger of my ancestor's sale," Vesper said, "is not kept in the vault."
