## 12. Tithe-Night Eve

Ilsa had learned to measure her fading in small things.

This morning it was the comb. She held it before the polished obsidian mirror the court had provided — everything here was provided, graciously, poisonously, as if they were honored guests and not livestock awaiting slaughter — and she could see the wall through the teeth of it. Not around it. Through it. A faint gray shimmer where horn should have been, like breath on cold glass that refused to fade.

She set the comb down carefully, because breaking things was one of the last proofs she had weight.

The guest chamber they'd been given was beautiful in the way a spider's web is beautiful, if you were small enough to mistake silk for shelter. Walls of veined black stone. A window that looked out not on sky but on the vast hollow of the mountain's throat, where lanterns of green flame floated like jellyfish and the sound of preparation rose ceaselessly from below: the ringing of bells with no clappers, the dragging of something enormous across stone, the singing — always the singing, hundreds of voices in unison, rehearsing a hymn whose words made her teeth ache if she listened too long.

Tithe-night. Tomorrow's moonrise.

She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum and felt, beneath the skin, the wrongness. Like an itch located somewhere no hand could reach. Her father had described it once, near the end, when his own fading had gone too far to hide: *It feels like being remembered by someone who is forgetting you.*

Down in the mountain, they were forgetting her on purpose. Filing her away. Somewhere below there was a drawer with her name inked on its rim, and every hour the clerk's pen moved, another thread between Ilsa and Ilsa came loose.

*They will come for you,* her father had said, pressing the map into Vesper's hands with the last strength he had. He hadn't said who *they* would be. He hadn't promised they would succeed. Dying men make generous promises; it costs them nothing.

Ilsa straightened her spine, tested her reflection — still solid enough to cast a shadow, though the shadow had gone thin at the edges, frayed like old cloth — and went to find Vesper.

* * *

She found him in the gallery outside their chambers, sitting on the floor with his back against a pillar, surrounded by paper.

That was the thing about Vesper Villalobos: give him a room in hell and he would treat it as a survey site. He had been at it for three days now — since the herald had welcomed them with those oiled words, *the asset returns home*, since Ilsa had watched him go very still and very pale and then, remarkably, go *to work*. He'd charmed or bribed or simply bewildered the servants into bringing him supplies. He'd walked the halls with a measuring cord coiled over his shoulder like a garrote, counting paces under his breath, sketching columns and stairwells and the strange impossible geometry of the archive levels, where corridors bent back on themselves like intestines.

Now the papers fanned across the floor around him, weighted at the corners with candle stubs and a salt cellar he'd stolen from dinner. His fingers moved without pausing, quick and certain, and there was ink on his chin and on the heel of his left hand, and he was muttering.

"—seventeen landings between the hearing hall and the archive gate, but only nine going down, which means the stair eats eight coming up—"

"Or you counted wrong."

He didn't jump. She'd noticed that about him too. Nothing startled Vesper; things merely arrived, and were catalogued. He glanced up at her, and for a moment his eyes did the thing she hated most — the flicker downward, checking whether she was more transparent than yesterday. Checking, without meaning to, how much of her remained.

"I don't count wrong," he said. "It's the mountain that counts wrong. Deliberately." He tapped a page. "Look. The galleries rearrange after each bell. But the rearrangement has a pattern — it's slow, glacial, like a tide. If I chart tonight's sequence, tomorrow during the tithe I'll know where the archive will be before it arrives."

"And if your chart is wrong?"

"Then we'll be standing inside a wall when it finishes moving." He said it mildly, the way other men discussed weather. Then, quieter: "How are you?"

There it was again, that flicker. She sat down across from him, careful to fold her legs so her skirt didn't pass through her own ankle — it had happened twice today, and each time felt like a small door closing somewhere inside her.

"Solid," she lied.

Vesper looked at her for a long moment. He had a face built for stillness — sharp-planed, watchful, a cartographer's face, made for observing rather than being observed — but she was learning to read it anyway. The way the muscle beside his jaw tightened when he was afraid. The way his gaze went soft and unfocused when he was grieving, as though he'd stepped inside himself to look at something.

"You're lying," he said. "But I appreciate the craftsmanship."

Despite everything — despite the mountain and the drawer with her name on it and the comb she could see through — Ilsa laughed. It escaped her like a bird startled off a wire, brief and bright and immediately swallowed by the singing from below.

"It's bad, then," he said.

"It's bad." She held up her right hand and turned it in the green lantern-light. He leaned close, genuinely close, and she watched him study it the way he studied maps — with total attention, as though she were terrain worth knowing precisely. The tips of her fingers had gone translucent. When she flexed them, the light passed through the nails and laid faint glowing crescents on the floor.

"When my father started fading," she heard herself say, "he stopped writing letters. Said his handwriting was the first thing to go — the words wouldn't hold ink anymore. Ink needs something to grip." She curled her translucent fingers closed. "Yesterday I signed my name on one of your spare charts. To see."

"And?"

"It looked like someone else's handwriting. Shaky. Wrong." She swallowed. "Vesper. If we fail tomorrow—"

"We won't."

"If we *fail*—" She reached out and took his wrist, and was absurdly relieved to feel her fingers press actual flesh. "Then I want to have said things. While I still have a mouth to say them with. Do you understand? I spent three years watching my father fade and neither of us ever said anything real. We were polite about it. We rationed our honesty like it was salt and the war would last forever. And then he bled out in your map room and the last thing he said to me was about a debt, and I—" Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I am done rationing."

Below them, the singing swelled. Somewhere in the dark of the mountain's throat, the enormous dragged thing was set down, and the whole floor hummed like a struck bell.

Vesper turned his wrist gently until his hand covered hers. His palm was warm and ink-stained and slightly trembling — she felt it, that fine tremor, and understood it cost him something to let her feel it.

"Say them, then," he said.

* * *

They talked until the bells rang the third hour past midnight, sitting among his charts with the stolen salt cellar between them like a centerpiece neither wanted to explain.

She learned that he had been left at the guild's ink door in winter, wrapped in a surveyor's oilcloth, with nothing but a first name stitched into the hem. That Seraphine Quill had taken him in the way one acquires a useful instrument — cleaned, calibrated, kept. That his earliest memory was of the ledger room's smell: tallow and iron-gall ink and the particular dust that collects on vellum no one is allowed to touch. That he had loved it there. That was the terrible part, he said, staring at the ceiling of the underworld as though it might confess something. *I loved it. A cage you love is still a cage, but you'll defend it with your life.*

"I used to think belonging was a place," he said. "A desk. A chair with wear-marks shaped like you. I thought if I just worked precisely enough, mapped accurately enough, they'd have to keep me. Permanence through usefulness." A short, airless laugh. "And all the while there was a file downstairs with my soul in it, and my name in the margin in some ancestor's hand. Turns out I was never applying for membership. I was inventory."

"My family wasn't a guild," Ilsa said. "It was loud. My mother sang off-key on purpose to make us laugh. My father told stories with his hands — you had to duck. We had a garden, do you know that? A real one, above ground, with beans and a plum tree that fruited badly. Everyone laughed at the plum tree. It made maybe six plums a year and we treated them like coronation gifts." Her chest ached in the old way, the human way, and she was grateful for the ache. "When the debt collectors took me — when I began to fade — the worst part wasn't fear. It was that the garden didn't notice. Beans kept growing. The plum tree dropped its six plums on the grass and nobody picked them up. I used to dream about those plums rotting in the rows." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Belonging isn't a place, Vesper. I had the place. Belonging is being missed. It's someone walking past an empty chair and feeling it."

The silence that followed wasn't empty. It was the loaded kind, the kind that comes before a confession or a leap.

"I miss you already," Vesper said quietly. "In advance. I've been doing it for days. Every time your hand goes through your sleeve, I catalogue it — *lost, this much, at this hour* — the way I'd record coastline erosion. Because it's the only way I know how to grieve, and because—" He stopped. Started again, slower, like a man crossing ice. "Because I never had anyone to lose before. So I'm practicing."

Ilsa reached across the salt cellar and took both his hands. Hers fit inside his like pages inside a binding.

"Then here's what we do," she said. "Tomorrow night, when the tithe begins, you're going to walk into that archive and redraw the world, or whatever impossible thing you've planned — and I'm going to stand next to you and be stubbornly, inconveniently *present*, for as long as I have edges. And afterward, whoever we are when it's finished, we are going to plant something. Anything. A bean. I refuse to end this story without a garden."

"A garden," Vesper repeated, as though committing coordinates to memory.

"On the surface. Where the sun can bully it. And you'll complain about the dirt under your nails and I'll pretend to listen, and every year the plum tree will fail beautifully, and we will pick up every single plum." Her voice steadied as she spoke, gathering itself like a wind gathering rain. "That's the bargain. That's what we're fighting for tomorrow. Not freedom in the abstract. *Plums.*"

For the first time since she'd known him, Vesper Villalobos smiled with his whole face. It transformed him — made him young, made him real, made him look briefly like a man who had never been filed anywhere.

"Plums," he agreed. "Witnessed and sealed."

He took the salt cellar and shook a few grains into his palm, and she understood, with a chill that was also a warmth, that he was showing her the shape of his plan without saying it aloud. Salt, in his open hand, catching the green light.

"One more thing," she said, as the fourth bell began to toll. "If the mountain takes me tomorrow — if it goes wrong —"

"It won't."

"*If.*" Her grip tightened on his ink-stained fingers. "Then don't you dare let them file you away too. Whatever they offer, whatever name they write you down under — you get up, and you walk out, and you stay *unclaimed*. Promise me. Promise me you'll be nobody's asset."

The bell tolled through the stone, once, twice, three times. And far below them, in the heart of the archive, something answered it — a sound like a thousand pages turning at once, like ledgers opening, like the mountain clearing its throat to call its debts due.

Vesper bowed his head over their joined hands, and Ilsa saw his lips move, sealing the promise the old guild way — witnessed, written, true.

Above the mountain, unseen behind miles of stone, the moon climbed toward fullness, pulling the tide of tithe-night after it like a rope.

And in the gallery outside their chambers, on one of the scattered charts, a single grain of salt that had fallen from Vesper's palm lay gleaming — and, as the last bell faded, began very softly to glow.
