## 16. The Court Wakes

The first sign was the salt.

Ilsa felt it before she heard anything — a prickling along her arms like the memory of gooseflesh, though her skin had long since forgotten how to rise to a chill. She was sitting in the guest chamber the court had given them, a room of velvet and veined marble that pretended warmth the way a corpse pretends sleep, when every grain of salt in the little dish beside her bed stood up on end.

She had been fading for months now, but fading had taught her things. The less of her there was, the more she could feel — as though the world's small currents no longer bothered to go around her and simply passed through, carrying their news into whatever remained. The salt's alarm passed through her now, and she understood it the way one understands a scream through a wall: not its words, but its terror.

Then the bells began.

They were not bells, exactly. They were more like the sound a mountain would make if it cleared its throat — a deep, grinding resonance that came up through the floor and out of the walls, out of the veins of obsidian itself, until the wine in Ilsa's untouched glass shivered into rings. Somewhere far below, or perhaps far above — distance worked strangely here, and direction worked not at all — something enormous had noticed an absence.

*He's done it,* she thought. *Or he's tried.*

Vesper had kissed her forehead three nights past — a quick, dry press of lips, the kiss of a man who did not know how to be kissed at and had decided to practice on someone who could barely feel it — and said only, *If I'm not back by the second bell after midnight, don't wait. Don't be brave.* And she had promised, because it was easier than arguing with a man holding a stolen page against his chest like a relic.

Now the mountain's throat was ringing, and she thought: *he told me not to be brave, which means he knew I would have to choose.*

The chamber doors were black oak banded with iron gone green as old bruises. When she pulled at them they opened without resistance, which frightened her more than locked doors would have. Locked doors meant walls. Open doors meant invitation, and invitations beneath the mountain were never free.

* * *

The galleries were waking.

Ilsa moved through corridors she half-remembered from the walk down with Vesper, except the corridors no longer agreed to be where she remembered them. A hallway that had run straight toward the archive now curved gently left, then sharply right, then ended in a wall of seamless stone polished to a mirror. In the mirror her reflection lagged — a half-second behind her movements, a ghost keeping polite distance from its body. She raised a hand; the girl in the glass raised hers too late. Through the girl's shoulder Ilsa could see the stone behind her, faintly, the way you can see a candle through a closed fist.

*Nearly done,* she thought, with a strange calm. Her father had sold her soul as collateral for a debt he'd hoped to buy back, and the court had begun collecting by installments. Every week there was less of her. She had stopped looking at mirrors some time ago. This one she looked at deliberately, because she wanted to remember what courage looked like when it was almost transparent.

The corridor rearranged itself again. She turned, and found herself facing a servant of the court — one of the hollow-eyed stewards whose livery seemed woven from shadow and formal apology.

"Guest Villalobos," it said. Its voice was dry paper folding. "You are requested to remain in your chambers."

"I'm lost," Ilsa said, which was true, and also a shield. Lost guests could be pitied. Pitied guests could be escorted rather than seized.

"The galleries are unsettled this evening," the steward said. "For your safety."

"For my safety," she repeated. "Has something happened?"

Something moved behind the steward's eyes — or rather, something *stopped* moving, the way water stops when a stone drops into it. "The archive has experienced a disturbance," it said at last. "Please return to your chambers, Guest Villalobos."

"Is Vesper hurt?"

"The guest Villalobos is accounted for." A pause, thin as a blade. "Mostly."

She let them escort her back. It was what they expected, and expectation was a door left ajar. But at the threshold of her chamber she stumbled — genuinely, mostly — and caught herself on the steward's arm, and felt through the sleeve how little was inside the livery: a frame of cold, a suggestion of bones, a patience. "Forgive me," she whispered. "The stairs. My head." And while the steward steadied her with hands careful as a librarian handling a damaged book, she looked past its shoulder down the service corridor it had come from, and memorized: third turning, a stair descending, a lantern burning blue where lanterns should burn gold.

Blue light meant the archive. Vesper had told her that much, in the fragments of plan he'd allowed himself to share. *Everything else down here lies about its color,* he'd said. *But the archive is honest about its light. Old clerks get sentimental about honesty eventually. It's the last thing they surrender.*

* * *

They locked her in, of course. The lock made a sound like a promise being signed.

Ilsa sat on the edge of the bed that was too soft, in the room that was too warm, and pressed her fingertips together and watched the candlelight pass between her fingers instead of around them. There was a version of her — the girl from the farm roads, the girl who had wept over her father's body and buried him with river stones because the ground was too hard — who would have sat in this velvet cage and waited to be saved, or waited to be collected, whichever came first.

That girl had faded too, in her way. What remained was harder and thinner and, she suspected, meaner in the useful sense.

Her father had taught her cards. Not gambling — he'd disapproved of gambling — but the arithmetic of it, the reading of a table. *The house always wins, Ilsa-girl,* he'd say, dealing. *So you don't play the house's game. You change the game. You make the house play yours.* She'd thought it was about cards. Standing now in the court beneath the mountain, watching her own hands go translucent, she understood he had been teaching her the only lesson that mattered, the one he'd failed to learn in time himself: never sit down at a table where the other side writes the rules.

The tithe-night preparations had been going on for days. She had seen them from the gallery windows: processions of hooded clerks bearing ledger-chains; the great brass scales being scoured with vinegar and ash; the tithe-bell itself, vast and green, hung in the central shaft where it could drink the sound of everything that happened beneath the mountain. Tomorrow night — or whatever tomorrow meant down here, where the hours were kept like prisoners — the court would call in every debt, seal every bargain, and file two more souls among its grain. Hers. His.

Unless the game changed.

She thought about the steward's phrase. *Accounted for. Mostly.* Which meant they had him, or believed they had him, or wanted her to believe they had him. And it meant the theft — his theft, their theft, the fragment of coerced deed he'd carried out of the archive under his coat like a coal — had been discovered. The mountain's throat-bells had confirmed that. The court was awake, and the court was angry, and anger in a place this old did not shout. It reorganized corridors. It locked doors. It waited with the patience of geology.

*I can't wait back,* she decided. *Waiting is their game.*

There was one thing the court could not ignore, one move that forced every piece on its board to respond. She had learned it in the market of borrowed years, listening to the traders haggle, hearing the old woman with the scale-eyes explain the law of debts as casually as weather: *A claim may be contested by the claimant's blood, publicly voiced, before the full court. No debt sealed in silence stands. That's why they hate noise down here, girl. Noise is expensive for creditors.*

Her father's debt. Her collateral. Her voice — thinning, fading, nearly spent, and therefore worth exactly nothing to lose.

* * *

Getting out was easier than staying in, which told her everything about how courts think. They guard against force. They never quite believe in audacity dressed as confusion.

She knocked on her own door and called out in a wavering voice — not entirely performed — that the room was spinning, that she was ill, that something was *happening to her*, please, please, someone come. The lock considered. The door opened onto two stewards, and Ilsa collapsed forward into their cold arms, and while they fumbled with a weight that weighed less each day, she went limp and counted their footsteps and marked every turning by the smell of the stone: wet iron, dry ash, old incense, and then — faint, honest, blue — the mineral tang of the archive galleries.

She ran when they set her down to summon a physician-of-record. She ran badly, barefoot, her breath making no impression on air that had stopped fully believing in her, but she ran, down the stair with the blue lantern, along a corridor lined with filing-doors that murmured in their sleep, names like insects in amber.

Behind her, the mountain changed its mind about its own shape. She heard stone grind against stone, corridors unmaking themselves, the whole architecture turning its attention — its enormous, mineral attention — toward finding one fading girl and one thief of pages. The floor tilted gently, trying to slope her backward. The air thickened, trying to slow her. She put her hand against the wall and the wall pulsed under her palm like the flank of some sleeping animal beginning to dream of her.

And ahead, at the end of the gallery, stood the herald.

It was tall in the way that legal documents are tall — all verticality and authority, robed in black shot with silver thread that spelled clauses if you stared too long. Its face was smooth as a wax seal. When it spoke, its voice arrived already notarized.

"Guest Ilsa," it said. "Your presence outside sanctioned chambers constitutes breach of guest-right. You will return—"

"No," Ilsa said.

The word fell into the gallery and rang there. She felt it ring — felt it the way the salt had felt the alarm — because a refusal, spoken aloud beneath the mountain, was a legal event. The walls went still. Even the hunting pressure of the shifting stone paused, the way a hound pauses when the fox turns to face it.

"My father," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked, and she let it crack, because a crack is louder than smoothness, "sold a debt to this court, and pledged my soul as collateral. That pledge was made in silence — filed in silence — sealed in silence. And I am told—" she stepped forward, barefoot, transparent, terrifying to look at and making sure they looked — "that this court honors its own laws above all things. So I invoke them. I claim my father's debt. Publicly. Before the full court. I contest the claim on my soul, and I demand the hearing the law grants me."

The herald did not move for a long moment. Silver clauses crawled across its robes like ants discovering sugar. Somewhere in its sealed face, she imagined, calculations were being made — the cost of refusing her against the cost of granting her, silence against noise, and the terrible arithmetic of a fading girl who had nothing left to bargain with and therefore could not be threatened.

"You are nearly collected," the herald said at last. "What remains of you cannot stand before the court."

"Then the hearing will be brief," Ilsa said. "Unless you're afraid of what I'll say."

She didn't know what she would say. She knew only that her father had died owing, and that owing men leave papers, and that somewhere in the grain-halls of this mountain was the record of how her father had been pressed — the same coercion Vesper held proof of, the same old rot at the root of the ledger-tree. If the court gave her a platform, she would find the truth or die transparent on it. If the court refused her — refused her *publicly*, before witnesses — then the law it worshipped became a knife in its own hand.

The herald understood this. She watched it understand, the clauses on its robes crawling faster, faster, and then going still.

"A hearing," it said, and the words tasted bitter even to her ears, "will be convened. Before the full bench. On the eve of the tithe." A pause, delicate as a trap springing. "Both claims will be heard together. Yours. And the asset Villalobos's."

So they *had* taken him. Or caught him, or cornered him. Her stomach — the part of her still solid enough to dread — dropped through floors the mountain no longer needed.

"And if I refuse those terms?" she asked, though her voice shook.

"Then the claim proceeds uncontested," said the herald, "and the silence resumes."

The silence resumed. She thought of her father's face in his last winter, the way he'd stopped speaking near the end, stopped asking, stopped contesting — worn down by silences into a man who signed whatever was put in front of him. She thought of Vesper saying *don't be brave*, and of the salt standing on end, and of the blue honest light of the archive where he had gone alone to steal back a soul the world had sold before he was born.

"No," Ilsa said again, quieter this time, and it rang just as far. "I'll be there. Both of us will."

* * *

They escorted her back — politely now, which was worse — and locked the door with a softer sound, the sound of a promise being countersigned.

Alone, in the too-warm room, Ilsa sat down on the edge of the bed and finally permitted herself to shake. Her hands were almost gone. When she held them to the candle she could count her fingerbones by their shadows alone.

Tomorrow — tithe-eve, hearing-day — she would stand before the full court of the Undermountain and speak for a debt older than her grief. And beside her, guarded or chained or smiling that terrible careful smile of his, would be Vesper, who had told her not to be brave, and whom she intended to forgive for that the moment she saw him.

Through the window slit, far down the central shaft, the great green tithe-bell hung motionless, waiting for its night.

Beneath it — small, distant, unmistakable — a lantern burned blue.

Someone was still moving in the dark. Someone was still counting turnings, learning the shape of the hunt, refusing to be either caught or quiet.

Ilsa pressed her fading hand flat against the cold stone sill and smiled at it, at the bell, at the whole listening mountain.

*Change the game,* Papa. *I'm changing the game.*

Far below, the blue light flickered once — twice — and began to climb.
