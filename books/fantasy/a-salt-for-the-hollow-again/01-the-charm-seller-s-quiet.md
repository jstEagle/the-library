## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

There are three rules to selling charms in Corren, and I have followed all of them for twenty years without exception.

One: never sell a charm that works.

Two: never let them see you look at the sky.

Three: never, under any circumstances, hum while you work. Warders are trained to hear resonance the way hounds are trained to smell fear, and humming is how careless hedge-witches end up in the gray wagons on their way to the Salt Cells.

I broke rule three this morning, which is why I was already in a foul temper when the inspection came.

It was Tam's fault, really. Tam is eight, gap-toothed, and convinced that my stall on Weft Street sells actual magic, because I once told him—stupidly, sentimentally—that the little knotted cords I sell for two coppers each will keep nightmares away. He'd been humming at me all week, some tuneless thing he learned from his mother, and it had lodged in my skull like a burr. So there I was, arranging my display of dried lavender bundles and river-stone pendants at dawn, humming along like a woman with nothing to hide, when I caught myself and bit down on the sound so hard I nearly cracked a tooth.

Twenty years. Twenty years of perfect quiet, undone by an eight-year-old's earworm.

"Morning, Mistress Vane," Tam said, appearing at the stall's edge the way he always did, materializing out of the crowd like a small accusatory ghost. "Did you knot the new ones yet?"

"I did."

"Do they work?"

"They work exactly as well as they're supposed to," I said, which is the truest sentence I've spoken in years. The knots are just knots. The lavender is just lavender. The river stones are just stones I spent an entire fraudulent afternoon collecting from a creek bed while pretending to pray over them. What I sell is comfort, and comfort is not against the law in Corren. Not yet.

What's against the law is what I do after dark, three miles north, where the land folds down into a hollow that doesn't appear on any map worth trusting. But we'll get to that.

The morning went the way mornings go: slow, gray, smelling of coal smoke and the fish carts coming up from the harbor. Corren in autumn is a city holding its breath. You can feel it in the way people walk—heads down, shoulders drawn, everyone performing the same careful theater of being nobody. The Edict has been law for sixty years now, long enough that most folk have forgotten magic was ever real, and the ones who remember have gotten very good at forgetting louder.

I sold a nightmare-knot to a dockhand with shadowed eyes. I sold a luck-braid to a woman whose husband was shipping out on a whaler, and I didn't tell her that no knot of mine would reach that far, and she didn't ask me to. That's the unspoken contract of my trade. People don't buy charms because they believe. They buy them because believing costs two coppers and not believing costs everything.

Around midmorning, the light changed.

Not dramatically. Nothing about inspections is ever dramatic until the very last moment, and then it's too dramatic entirely. It was more that the quality of the shadows along Weft Street shifted—the particular flatness that means someone upstream is wearing a warder's gray, and every lamp-glass and windowpane in the district is drinking the light differently because of the detection lattice woven into their badges.

I kept my hands moving. Tied a knot. Straightened a bundle. Counted my coppers into their box with the bored precision of a woman who has done this ten thousand times.

Two of them came down the street. I knew before I looked. You always know; the crowd parts around warders the way water parts around a keel, without anyone deciding to move.

The first one was young, all jaw and certainty, the kind who joined the Order because it made him somebody. He glanced at my stall the way you glance at a fruit cart, and I felt the familiar little flicker of the lattice pass over me—a cold fingertip dragged across the back of my neck—and felt it find nothing, because there was nothing to find. My stall is warded, but the warding isn't magic. It's craft. Misdirection, mislabeled jars, a false bottom in the copper box that reads as a dead zone to any lattice. Twenty years of engineering my own dullness. I am, if I may say so, magnificently boring.

The second warder stopped.

He was older. Gray at the temples, a face like a folded map, and he didn't glance—he *looked*, the way you look at a page when you suspect the ledger's been cooked. His badge caught the light and held it wrong.

"Mistress." He picked up one of my nightmare-knots and turned it over in his fingers. "These are popular."

"Popular enough," I said. "Coppers don't lie."

"No," he agreed. "They don't." He turned the knot again, and I watched his thumb trace the cord, and I thought, very calmly, *he knows knots*. Not charm-knots—warder's knots, the kind used in lattices and bindings, the kind that share maybe thirty percent of their structure with what I sell, which is enough for a trained eye to see the family resemblance. "Where did you learn to tie these?"

"My grandmother," I said. "She was a net-mender. Down at the harbor."

"A net-mender." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "They make good hands, net-menders. Precise." He set the knot down, aligning its edges with the others so neatly it was almost a threat. "You've got precise hands, Mistress Vane."

"You flatter me, Warden."

"Do I know your name?" He tilted his head. "Have we met?"

"I don't believe so."

"Hm." He looked at me a moment longer—one heartbeat, two, three, long enough for me to inventory the four exits from my stall and calculate which of my tools I could destroy fastest—and then something behind his eyes shifted, some internal arithmetic resolving to zero, or near enough. "Mind the Edict, mistress. Coppers don't lie, but neither do knots. They always tell you who tied them."

He moved on. The young one followed. The cold fingertip of the lattice dragged across my neck once more and found, as always, nothing.

I sold three more charms with steady hands. Then I sat down on my stool and discovered that twenty years of discipline will keep your face smooth but does absolutely nothing for your knees.

*They always tell you who tied them.*

He'd been fishing. I was nearly sure he'd been fishing. Warders linger sometimes; it's a tactic, make the mark sweat and watch for the tells. And I hadn't sweated. I hadn't done anything but be precisely, perfectly boring.

But I wrote the encounter down anyway, that evening, in the cipher I keep in the back of my ledger, because the other thing twenty years teaches you is that *nearly sure* is a luxury you can't afford twice.

* * *

Night in Corren belongs to different traffic than day. The curfew bell rings at ninth hour, and after that the streets empty of honest people and fill with the other kind—smugglers moving salt and worse through the Warrens, lovers taking the long way home, and one charm-seller with a satchel full of tools walking north along the drainage road with the unhurried gait of a woman visiting a sick relative.

The Hollow is three miles out, past the tanneries where the stink keeps casual wanderers away, past the old quarry road, down into a fold of land that the maps call a surveying error. There's a stream there that runs backward on foggy nights, and a stand of alders that lean away from something in the center of the hollow, and in the center of the hollow, under a skin of turf and root and twenty years of my maintenance, there is the Gate.

I won't describe it fully. Describing things gives them weight, and the Gate has quite enough weight already. I'll say only this: it is a door that was built to stay shut, and it is the reason the stars over Corren still come in the standard number, and the reason the border wards hold, and the reason magic is a rumor instead of a weather. Somebody, a very long time ago, decided the world was safer with that door barred, and a chain of keepers agreed, and I am the last link of that chain, and I have been alone with the job since I was nineteen years old.

Every third night, I check the wards. Every seventh night, I renew them. On the great feast-days I do the deep work, the binding-sigils, the slow patient stitching that keeps the door dreaming instead of waking. It takes hours. It takes everything I have, quietly, invisibly, and then I walk home and open my stall in the morning and sell lies to people who need them, and that is the whole of my life, and I have told myself for two decades that it is enough.

That night was a seventh night. Renewal work.

I climbed down into the Hollow with my satchel and my hood up, and the alders leaned away from me the way they always do, politely, like courtiers making room. The stream ran backward through the fog. I knelt at the first anchor-stone and put my palms flat against it and let my senses sink down through the turf into the deep structure of the binding, the way a physician lays hands on a chest and listens.

And for a moment—just a moment, just a breath—I heard something I had never heard before.

A sound like ice on a pond. A single high note, thin as wire, coming from somewhere far down in the Gate's architecture. Not breaking. Not yet. But *straining*.

I pulled back and checked the stone. Checked the sigils. Checked the secondary anchors, the tertiary weave, the whole litany of it, working faster than I should have, because fast work is sloppy work and sloppy work kills warders. Everything read true. Everything read *tired*, but tired is normal; the Gate is old and old things tire, and part of my job for twenty years has been compensating for its fatigue, feeding strength into the binding the way you feed broth to a convalescent.

But that note. That thin, high, straining note.

I told myself it was the wind in the quarry. I told myself I was rattled from the warder, hearing ghosts in the architecture. I finished the renewal work with extra care, doubling the stitches at the northern anchors, and I sat back on my heels in the wet grass and listened to the Hollow breathe, and underneath the breathing, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the note was still there.

Here is the thing about hairline cracks. They don't announce themselves. They don't split the world open on the first night. They sit there, invisible, doing their patient arithmetic, and the only person who ever notices them is the person who has been listening to the porcelain sing for twenty years.

Nobody else would have heard it. I'm certain of that. The lattice-wards in the city, the Order's monitoring stations, the whole vast apparatus of Corren's vigilance—all of it pointed outward, watching for smugglers and hedge-witches and the usual small crimes. Nobody watches the Gate. Nobody alive even remembers why it would need watching, except me.

Which meant that whatever was straining that note, whatever slow pressure was building down in the deep structure—it wasn't coming from outside.

Something inside was pushing.

I packed my tools with hands that wanted to shake and didn't let them. I walked back along the drainage road under a sky full of stars—every one of them present and accounted for, I counted, I always count—and I let myself into my rooms above the stall and sat down at my table and did not sleep.

Toward dawn, I took out my ledger and turned to the cipher pages, and beneath my notes on the warder with the folded-map face, I wrote a new line:

*The Gate made a sound tonight. Gates that are kept should not make sounds.*

Then I closed the book, and banked my lamp, and went down to open the stall, because the world doesn't stop for cracks, and coppers don't lie, and I had nightmare-knots to sell.

I told myself I'd give it three nights. Three more listenings, three more checks, and if the note was still there—if it grew—I'd decide what came next.

I want to state, for the record, that I intended to be careful. I intended to be slow, and methodical, and boring, the way I have been for twenty years.

The stars lasted two more nights.
