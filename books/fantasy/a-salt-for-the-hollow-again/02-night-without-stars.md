## 2. Night Without Stars

I was re-salting the threshold of my stall when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out*—all of them at once, like a hand snuffing a thousand candles in a single motion, and the silence that followed had a texture to it, a pressure against the ears, the way a room feels when someone has just stopped screaming.

The salt spilled from my fingers onto the cobbles. I stood there in the dark of Corren's Charmers' Row with my heart doing something unprofessional in my chest, and I did what I always do when the world breaks: I checked my hands for blame.

Twenty years of practice. It takes about three seconds now.

* * *

Let me back up, because if you're going to judge me—and you will—you should at least have the full inventory.

My name is August Vane, and by day I sell charms that don't work. Not most charms—all of them. Knotted red thread for safe travel. Dried lavender sewn into linen for restful sleep. Little clay beads fired with a prayer to nobody in particular for luck in love, luck in labor, luck in staying off the warders' lists. Corren outlawed magic two generations ago, but it never outlawed the *wanting* of it, and wanting is a market. I sell people the shape of hope at two coppers apiece, and they go home feeling watched-over, and I go home feeling like a fraud with excellent margins.

By night, I do the other thing.

There is a gate in the hills east of Corren. The Hollow Gate, the old maps call it, though there's nothing hollow about it—it's a seam in the world stitched shut with wards so old that half their grammar has been forgotten by everyone except me and one dead man whose notes I inherited along with his debts. The gate doesn't open. That's rather the point of me. Every night for twenty years I've walked up through the pines and pressed my palms to the binding stones and fed them just enough of myself to keep the stitching tight, and every morning I've come back down and sold lucky beads to people who'd burn me at a stake if they knew what my hands could do.

It's not heroism. I want to be clear about that. Heroes choose between things. I chose stillness, which is what cowardice calls itself when it can afford good boots.

So: the stars went out, and I checked my hands, because the last time something this large happened near the Hollow Gate, someone paid for it, and in my experience the paying is usually done by people who weren't consulted.

* * *

Corren handled the dark the way Corren handles everything: badly, loudly, and with an eye toward profit.

Within the hour the taverns were full of theories. The gods had blinked. The fae had turned the sky's face to the wall. The northern kingdoms had finally built that engine the pamphlets kept threatening us with. A fishwife on Tallow Street swore her grandmother had seen it before, in 'the year of the peeled heavens,' which sounded invented until three other grandmothers confirmed it independently, at which point it became history.

I closed the stall early, which I never do, and went home to my narrow rooms above the chandler's, and I did not light a lamp, because I didn't need one, and because old habits are the only inheritance I've ever trusted.

Here is what I know about wards, knowledge bought at the price of two decades of solitude: wards are agreements. They are bargains struck between a place and a power, sealed in salt and blood-ink and repetition. The border wards ringing the northern reaches—the great ones, the ones laid down before Corren had walls—are anchored to the sky itself. To the stars. Not poetically. *Literally.* Each wardline terminates in a star-anchor, a fixed point of heaven's light, because a thing that moves can be followed but a thing that *is* cannot be argued with.

If the stars went out, the border wards didn't weaken.

They died.

I sat on the edge of my bed in the dark and felt it happen—not the dying, that was too far away, but the *aftermath*, the way you feel a door slam in a house you thought you were alone in. The Hollow Gate's binding shivered. Not cracked. Shivered. Like a dog hearing its name called from very far away by a voice it shouldn't recognize.

And here is the thought that arrived then, unwelcome as a tax collector, and I want you to appreciate how long I fought it:

Nobody unmakes star-anchors. Nobody *can*. The craft is lost, scattered, burned with the warders' libraries when the Purges came through. Except—

Except it isn't entirely lost. Except there is exactly one kind of person alive who would know where the anchors were set and how they were tied and what it would take to cut them, and that person is someone who has stood where I have stood, who has read what I have read, who has kept—or broken—a gate.

Someone with gate-knowledge had reached up into the sky and picked the locks.

I told myself it might be a scholar. I told myself it might be an accident, some cascade failure, some natural rot in the old works. I told myself several comforting lies with the fluency of a woman who sells hope for a living, and then I got up, lit no lamp, and pulled the loose floorboard under the bed.

* * *

Under the floorboard is my other life, packed for flight these twenty years and never used. You'd be amazed what becomes routine. A satchel of tools: bone styluses, a mortar worn smooth as a knuckle, vials of blood-ink mixed to the old recipe (mine, not anyone else's—I'm not a monster). Three ward-stones I carved myself, each keyed to a different escape route out of the city, none ever triggered. A knife. A change of clothes that don't smell like lavender and commerce. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a letter I wrote nineteen years ago to a brother who was already gone, because grief needs somewhere to put its luggage.

Tomas. Fourteen years old, last seen walking north behind our mother's cart, promised he'd be back by harvest. That was thirty years ago now, if we're counting, and I am always counting; it's the arithmetic of the abandoned. For the first decade I looked for him. For the second decade I told myself looking was a luxury, and that keeping the gate sealed was protecting him too, wherever he was, because a sealed gate means a contained world, and contained worlds don't eat children.

That's the doctrine. I learned it young. *A keeper's first duty is containment; everything outside the seal is either protected or irrelevant.* I've had twenty years to notice how convenient that doctrine is for keepers, and how rarely it's convenient for anyone else.

I sat on the floor with the satchel open and the letter in my lap and I had the argument. You know the argument. Everyone has a version of it. *Stay. Stay hidden, stay small, stay alive; the dark will pass or it won't, and either way a dead charm-seller helps no one.* Against: *the wards are down, the gate shivered, and whatever comes through the hole in the world will eventually come down the salt road to Corren, and you'll be standing at your stall selling knotted thread to people the dark is eating.*

Stillness had been my religion for twenty years. But religions fail their adherents at exactly the moment they're needed most, and I felt mine failing now, felt the whole architecture of my careful invisible life groan like a bridge under weight it was never rated for.

Because—and this is the part I could not argue with, try as I did—if someone with gate-knowledge had unmade the border wards, then they might next turn to the gates themselves. Plural. Including mine. Including the one I had spent half my life holding shut with nothing but stubbornness and a nightly tithe of my own strength.

And if they opened my gate, they would find what I'd left there.

Which is nothing, you understand. Nothing but a binding, and the residue of twenty years of tithes, and—no. No, I'm getting ahead of myself, and a narrator who gets ahead of herself is just a gossip with better syntax. Let me say only this: when I imagined a stranger's hands on my gate, reading it the way I read it, learning what I had kept and what I had *cost*, something in me went cold and certain, and certainty is the most dangerous thing I own.

I packed the satchel. I left the letter—sentiment is heavy, and I've carried it long enough to know its weight by heart. I banked the cold hearth, left rent under the chandler's door with a note saying *family business in the country*, which was even true, depending on your definition of family, and slipped out into a city gone strange under a starless sky.

* * *

Corren without stars is a different city. I mean that precisely. The streets looked wrong the way a face looks wrong when you meet it outside its usual context. Lamplight that had always seemed sufficient suddenly seemed like a rumor. People stood in doorways staring straight up, mouths open, and the collective upward gaze of a whole city has a sound to it, a hush like snowfall, like held breath.

At the Salt Gate—the southern gate, the trade gate—they were letting refugees in.

I saw them by lamplight as I worked my way along the wall toward the postern I actually intended to use: families with carts, carters with families, a whole river of the northern poor flowing south with the particular blankness of people who have outrun something and haven't yet decided whether to believe it. They weren't wounded. That was the eerie part. No burns, no blood. Just dust, and that stare, and children asking questions in the exhausted loop of children who have asked too many times.

I should have kept moving. I want that noted for the record: I *should* have kept moving. But a woman near the gate was saying the thing, the phrase, the one I'd hear a dozen more times that night and would dream about for months after, and I stopped to listen because a charm-seller learns to collect words the way other collectors collect coins.

"The sky's peeling," she said. She was talking to a gate-sergeant who clearly wished she were talking to anyone else. "North of the Sere foothills. My man saw it before we ran. Like—like skin coming off a burn, but it was the *sky*, ma'am, the black of it, lifting at the edges, and behind it—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, and this is the part that made my spine file a formal complaint. "Behind it there wasn't more sky. There was *light*. Wrong light. Greenish. Like the underside of a leaf."

Peeling. The black of the sky lifting at the edges, and behind it, the glow of whatever the stars had been nailed down to hide.

I have kept the Hollow Gate for twenty years and I will tell you honestly: I did not know the sky was a ward. I knew the border wards anchored to the stars; I did not know the *dark between* them was load-bearing. Which meant whoever did this understood the deep structure, the foundation under the foundation, the craft beneath the craft. That is not scholar's knowledge. That is not accident. That is keeper's knowledge, wielded in reverse—a lockpick made from a key.

Somewhere out there was a person who had learned everything I had learned and drawn the opposite conclusion. Where I had seen a door to hold shut, they had seen a door to open. And they were better at it than me, or faster, or braver, or simply less tired, and they had started with the sky.

"Move along," the sergeant said, to the woman, to me, to the century. "Move along, no charity inside the walls tonight."

I moved along. To the postern, where a bribe I'd been aging like wine for fifteen years opened the little door in the wall, and then I was outside Corren for the first time in four years, standing on the salt road with the satchel on my shoulder and no stars above me and the hills eastward lying black against a horizon that glowed faintly, faintly green, like something bruising.

* * *

I want to describe the walk, because the walk is where I changed, and changes deserve witnesses even when the only witness available is yourself.

The road east climbs through farmland gone fallow since the tolls rose, and then into pine, and the pines without starlight were a solid black mass, a wall of *not*, and I walked into it with my hood up and my wards strung around me like a woman wearing all her jewelry at once. Every sense I owned was open. This is the secret shame of the hidden life: hiding is exhausting not because of the effort of concealment but because of the vigilance. Twenty years of listening for footsteps. And now, walking toward the thing instead of away from it, I felt something I hadn't felt since I was a girl apprenticed to a man whose name I will get to, eventually, when I'm ready, which is not yet.

I felt *useful*. Terrible, doomed, probably fatal usefulness—but useful, and I was ashamed of how warm it made me, the way you're ashamed of enjoying soup during a famine.

Twice on the climb I passed shrines. Old ones, pre-Purge, waystones with the sigils chiseled off and only the shapes of the chiseling left, scars in the pattern of prayers. In the starless dark, my wards brushed against those mutilated stones and the stones *answered*—a faint hum, a resonance, like plucked strings remembering music. Wild magic pooling in the ruins of the old faith, gathering where the wards had fallen, seeping up through the world's wounds.

The gate, when I reached it near dawn-that-would-not-dawn properly, was intact.

I want to be exact about this, because precision is the only penance I know: the Hollow Gate was intact. The binding stones stood in their ring. The sigils I have renewed nightly for seven thousand nights glowed their familiar dull amber, steady as banked coals. Nothing had touched it. Nothing had broken it.

But it was *singing*.

Low, below hearing, felt in the teeth and the sternum: the gate resonating with the fallen sky like a tuning fork laid against a bell. And threaded through the song, so faint I might have invented it, might yet insist I invented it, was something else. A signature. A flavor in the working that my hands knew before my head did, the way you know a footstep on the stairs, a cough in another room.

Familiar. Achingly, impossibly familiar, and I stood in the ring of stones with dawn refusing to arrive and my breath smoking in air that smelled of ozone and pine, and I said aloud, to no one, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine:

"Oh, no. No, no—whoever you are—"

Because the signature on the wind, the residue of the working that had peeled the sky, was written in a hand I knew.

I had taught it its letters myself, a lifetime ago, on a kitchen table, in a house that no longer stands, to a boy who was supposed to come home by harvest.

The gate sang. The empty sky pressed down. And August Vane, who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of standing still, took one step forward—which is, I now understand, the only kind of step that has ever mattered.
