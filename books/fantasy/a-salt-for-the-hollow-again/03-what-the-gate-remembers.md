## 3. What the Gate Remembers

The Hollow Gate sat three days' walk north of Corren, which was exactly far enough that nobody went there and exactly close enough that I could pretend I hadn't spent twenty years commuting to it like a woman with the world's worst job.

I'd packed for grief without knowing it. That's the thing about habits: they dress themselves in whatever clothes are hanging nearby. My satchel held chalk, salt, iron filings, a whetstone I never used on anything but my thoughts, and a jar of plum preserves my neighbor Marta had pressed on me at the door with the fierce tenderness of a woman who had decided I was too thin and would not be argued with. I took the preserves because refusing Marta required more energy than I had left.

The road north was wrong in small ways first. Wildflowers blooming out of season along the ditches, their colors just slightly too loud, like a painting hung crooked. A crow with too many eyes—I counted four, then decided I'd counted wrong, then counted again from a respectful distance and got six. The wards were down. The world knew it before I did. Magic doesn't flood back through a broken border all at once; it seeps, like water finding the cracks in a cellar wall, and everything it touches gets a little drunk.

By the second day the sky had stopped pretending. No stars, no moon, just a flat gray ceiling that made noon feel like a lie told badly. Refugees came south in knots of five and ten, carting children and chickens and, in one memorable case, an entire loom. They talked about the northern sky peeling. One old man described it as "the world showing its underpainting," which was either poetry or madness or both, and I gave him my bread for it because it was better than anything I could have written.

I didn't tell any of them where I was going. I'd gotten very good, over two decades, at being a charm-seller with a bad hip and a fondness for solitude. The bad hip was even real now. Twenty years of crouching over sigils will do that.

* * *

I smelled the gate before I saw it.

Not rot—nothing so honest. It smelled like a struck match that never finished striking, sulfur held forever on the edge of becoming flame. When I was young, the Hollow Gate had smelled of cold stone and river water and, faintly, of Tomas, who used to dare me to touch the standing stones and then dared himself harder and got his hand slapped by a ward for his trouble. He'd cried about it and sworn me to secrecy, and I had kept that secret the way I kept every secret afterward: completely, and to no one's benefit.

The gate itself was a ring of nine stones in a hollow between two hills, older than the city, older than the law against magic, older probably than the concept of law. The warders' guild had maintained it for centuries until the guild was dissolved and its members scattered or worse, and then there was only me, unlicensed, unpaid, and stubborn enough to keep doing the work because someone had to and because I had convinced myself that keeping something safe was the same as keeping something good.

I crested the hill and looked down into the hollow, and every plan I'd made on the road—the careful survey, the methodical assessment, the tidy mental checklist I'd been composing to keep myself calm—went out of me like breath through a cracked window.

The sigils were rewritten.

Every binding mark on every stone, the work of generations, carved deep and maintained in chalk and blood-ink by hands going back further than records—the whole litany of them had been overwritten. Not erased. Overwritten. Someone had laid new script directly atop the old, line over line, in ink that gleamed wet-dark even in the starless gray light, and I knew what it was before I knelt and touched it, because some knowledge lives below memory, in the body, in the place where you keep the things you learned young and hoped to never use.

Blood-ink. And not animal blood. The script was fed, sustained, *alive*, and it had been alive for years, drinking slow and steady from a source that—

That I recognized.

I want to tell you I figured it out gradually. That I examined the residual signature with professional detachment, noted its qualities, drew conclusions. What actually happened is that I put my fingers into the groove of the nearest stone and the signature rose up to meet me the way a scent rises from a coat you haven't worn in years, and I was fourteen again, and my brother was laughing at me across the hollow with a stolen apple in each fist, and I said his name out loud to an empty field like a madwoman.

"Tomas."

The word didn't echo. The gate drank it.

* * *

Here is what I understood, kneeling there with blood-ink drying tacky under my fingertips, and here is what I understood too late:

A gate needs feeding. Every warder knows this. A binding is a hunger dressed as a lock; it eats stray magic, ambient power, the low hum of the world, and as long as its diet stays thin and diffuse, it stays docile. For twenty years I had tended the Hollow Gate's hunger myself. I had brought it offerings measured to the grain. I had been so *careful*. Careful was my religion, my craft, my entire personality. I had starved the gate precisely, lovingly, the way you ration food to something you're afraid will grow.

And someone had found another table to set beside mine.

The soul-sigil wasn't new. That was the horror of it. Under my fingertips, reading the layers the way you'd read tree rings, I found years of it. Five years at least. Maybe seven. A slow drip of essence, siphoned in quantities so small that the gate's own appetite masked them, threaded through channels that ran—my stomach turned as I traced them—through the very maintenance lines I had renewed every season. My work. My careful, faithful, invisible work. I had been the irrigation system. I had kept the channels clear and flowing and never once thought to ask where the water went, because asking questions meant admitting the work existed, and admitting the work existed meant the gallows, and so for twenty years I had chosen blindness and called it devotion.

The signature in the ink was Tomas's. Compressed, thinned, stretched across years like butter over too much bread—but him. His particular frequency, the one I'd have known blindfolded, the one I'd slept next to for eleven years before he walked north one autumn morning to see the gate and never came home, and everyone said runaways go to the coast, and I believed them because believing was easier than looking, and I was sixteen and already learning the family trade of not-looking.

He hadn't run away. He'd been taken. Fed to the gate in teaspoons while I dusted its edges and called myself its keeper.

I don't remember sitting down. I remember the plum preserves jar rolling out of my satchel when I finally moved, rolling in a lazy arc across the grass, and stopping against a stone, unbroken, and I remember thinking with insane clarity: *Marta will want that back.*

Grief is a strange country. You cross its borders expecting weeping and find instead a terrible administrative clarity, a clerk inside your chest stamping documents: *Cause of death: theft. Perpetrator: unknown. Accessory: the victim's sister.* I sat in the grass and let the clerk work, because if she stopped working I was going to scream loud enough to bring every feral thing in these blighted hills down on me, and I still had hands, and hands could be given jobs.

So I gave them one. I read the rest.

* * *

The script was Voss's school. I recognized the hand the way you recognize a teacher's corrections on your own essays—the economy of it, the arrogance of strokes that never doubled back. Malachai Voss had been the finest warder of his generation, my mentor for one blazing year before the guild fell, before he vanished and the world decided he was dead. He taught me that control justified anything. He taught me that a keeper's mercy was a gate's weakness. He taught me, though he'd have hated the phrasing, that love was a leak in the vessel.

And he had built his masterpiece on top of my diligence. That was the part that made me laugh, actually laugh, alone in a hollow full of my brother's ghost-signature—a short, ugly sound with nothing funny in it. All those years I'd congratulated myself on my invisibility. The charm stall, the limp, the harmless little luck-knots sold to farmers' wives. I'd thought I was hiding from the world. I hadn't been hiding. I'd been *working*. Unwitting. Unpaid. The perfect accomplice: loyal, silent, and grateful for the chance.

The gate's hunger had been redirected, drop by drop, into a reservoir somewhere—no, not somewhere. I pressed deeper into the script, past the feeding lines, and found the destination etched in a cipher I half-knew and fully dreaded. North. Deep. A mountain. A court that bought souls as coin, if the oldest rumors were true, and Voss had always loved the oldest rumors, had collected them the way other men collected wine.

He wasn't feeding Tomas's soul to the gate. He was *laundering* it. Aging it. Preparing it, the way you prepare a key blank in a lock, until it fit the one door in the world worth opening.

The Hollow Gate. Which I had kept sealed. Which I had kept *safe*. Which I had polished and primed and maintained, year after year, so that when its moment came, it would be in perfect working order for the man who intended to throw it wide.

I was still holding the stone when the understanding finished arriving, all of it, the whole shipment, and the clerk in my chest stamped the final document and stepped away from her desk, and what was left underneath was not grief anymore.

It was fuel.

* * *

I did the small things first, because the small things are how you keep from shattering. I retrieved the preserves jar. I ate, because the body is a stupid loyal animal and demands feeding even at the end of the world, and the plums tasted like nothing and I made myself finish them anyway. I cleaned my fingers of blood-ink with sand and spit, ritually, thoroughly, the way Voss taught me—*respect the medium, August, respect it even when it disrespects you*—and hated him with a clean, clarifying hatred that felt almost like health.

Then I stood in the center of the ring, in the place where Tomas had last stood on the morning he walked north, because I knew—he'd told me once, grinning, conspiratorial—that he liked to stand there and imagine the gate opening onto somewhere better. Somewhere magic was allowed. Somewhere a boy with quick hands and quicker laughter could be more than a smuggler's errand-runner in a city that feared him.

"I'm coming," I said to the stones. To him. To whatever thin compressed thread of him was wound through this hungry dark machinery, five years deep in someone else's ledger. "I don't know how yet. But I'm coming, and I'm bringing company, and none of us are polite."

The gate, being a gate, said nothing. But the blood-ink pulsed once under the starless sky, slow as a sleeping pulse, and I chose—with the full deliberate madness of a woman past the point of caution—to take it as acknowledgment.

I had spent twenty years being careful, and careful had fed my brother to a mountain. So much for care. So much for the quiet life, the charm stall, the limp, the invisibility I'd mistaken for virtue. There was a court beneath Mount Sere that traded in souls, and a dead man's student walking toward it with his own doctrine loaded in her hands like a weapon pointed the wrong way round, and I was going to need help I couldn't buy with luck-charms and couldn't ask for without telling the truth, most of it, eventually, to strangers.

There was, I remembered, a smuggler captain who ran the salt roads north. Wren Calloway. Rumor said she'd carry anything for anyone if the price was interesting. I had one forbidden luck-charm in my pocket, sewn into my sleeve against every law I'd spent two decades obeying, and I suddenly wanted very much to break it out and spend it.

I took one last look at the gate—at the overwritten stones, at the wet-dark script drinking its slow meal—and I did not kneel to repair it. Let it flicker. Let it crack. Let Voss wonder, wherever he was, why his perfect machine had started ticking wrong. A keeper keeps. But I was done being a keeper, and the first act of my retirement was to walk away downhill, toward Corren, toward the salt roads, carrying my brother's name like a coal cupped in both hands.

Behind me, faint as a struck match finally finishing its strike, the Hollow Gate began, very softly, to sing.
