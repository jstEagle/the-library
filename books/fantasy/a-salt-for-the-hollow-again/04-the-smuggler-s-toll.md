## 4. The Smuggler's Toll

The Kestrel was moored at the Saltgate, which was the kind of joke Corren liked to make about itself: a gate named for salt, guarded by men who'd never seen the sea they taxed. I found her at the third pier down, wedged between a grain barge and something that smelled like it had died twice, and she was the ugliest ship in the harbor.

I mean that as a compliment. Beautiful ships get boarded. Ugly ones get ignored, and there is no charm in my whole repertoire stronger than being ignored.

She was low in the water, black-hulled, her rails scarred white where cargo had chewed them for years. Her name had been painted over so many times the letters sat on top of each other like sediment—KESTREL on top, and beneath it, if you squinted, the ghost of some other name, and beneath that another. A ship with a history of pretending to be other ships. I felt an immediate kinship.

The woman at the gangplank was sharpening a knife and watching me before I'd decided to watch her. That's the thing about smugglers and warders: we share a profession. We both survive by noticing what doesn't fit. And a woman in a charm-seller's gray cloak, walking the Saltgate piers at dawn with salt-stained hems and no cargo, did not fit.

"You're lost," she said. Not unkindly. The way you'd tell a deer it was lost, while resting your hand on the bow.

"I'm looking for Captain Calloway."

"You're looking at her. What's the cargo?"

"No cargo."

"Then what's the fare?"

"Passage north. Past the Marches. As far as Mount Sere's shadow, if you'll go that far."

Wren Calloway looked me over the way a horse-trader looks at a lame mare—thoroughly, without apology, cataloguing every flaw. She was shorter than me by a head, wiry, with a sunburn that had clearly stopped negotiating with her skin decades ago and one earring where custom said there should be two. Her knife never stopped moving against the whetstone. I got the sense it never did.

"Mount Sere," she repeated. "Where the sky's peeling. Where Voss's soldiers are asking questions with thumbscrews." The knife paused. "You want me to sail toward that."

"I want you to sail toward the edge of it. I walk the last part."

"Alone?"

"That's my business."

"It's my business whether you come back with soldiers behind you." She stepped aside from the gangplank, which I understood was not an invitation but a repositioning. "Come aboard. We'll see how much of your business survives daylight."

* * *

Below decks, the Kestrel smelled of tar, brine, and someone's breakfast, and it was full of people who all stopped talking when I came down the ladder. There is a particular silence a crew makes when a stranger enters—a silence with weight distributed through it, everyone waiting to see who moves first. Twenty years of keeping my head down had made me fluent in silences. This one said: *we are deciding about you.*

"This is my crew," Wren said, gesturing broadly, as though presenting a museum exhibit titled *Reasons to Turn Back.* "Fen, who steers better than he listens. Sable, who owns everything sharp on this boat. Harl, who owes me money and will therefore be polite. Petra, who does the math, and if you lie to her, she'll find the lie in the ledger before you finish telling it."

Each of them nodded or didn't, according to temperament. Fen was young, freckled, openly curious in the way of people who hadn't yet learned curiosity gets people killed. Sable was older, gray-braided, and looked at me the way I imagined I looked at warders. Petra didn't look up from her ledger at all, which frightened me more than Sable's stare.

"And you are?" Petra asked, still writing.

"August. I sell charms."

"In Corren?" Fen laughed. "Charms are illegal in Corren."

"They're *licensed* in Corren," I corrected. "Love knots. Traveler's blessings. Little brass hands against the evil eye. Nothing that works."

That got a real laugh out of most of them, because it's the oldest joke in the trade: the licensed charms are the ones that don't work, and everyone knows it, and the law knows everyone knows it, and that's the point. The law doesn't fear magic. It fears magic that works.

Petra's pen stopped. "And the ones you sell privately?"

"Captain," I said, turning back to Wren, "you asked what the fare is. Let me answer properly."

I set my satchel on the table between us and opened it just enough. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a luck-charm—not the brass nonsense I sold at my stall, but the real article. A knotted thread of storm-silk around a sliver of heart-oak, bound with a working I'd built over three nights and broken my own rules to make. It turned probability the way a rudder turns a ship. Not dramatically. Just enough. Enough that the reef shifts two feet, the patrol looks left instead of right, the rope frays on the second haul instead of the first.

In Corren, possessing it was a hanging offense. Selling it was worse.

Wren didn't reach for it. That impressed me. Most people reach.

"That's a working," she said quietly. The crew's silence changed key—from *deciding* to *listening*.

"It's a working."

"You're a mage."

"I'm a charm-seller," I said, "who occasionally misplaces things into his inventory. You can call it whatever keeps your conscience comfortable."

"My conscience is extremely comfortable," Wren said. "It's my neck I worry about. Mages draw warders like blood draws sharks. If they take you off my deck, they take my ship apart looking for more of you. Do you understand what you're asking? You're not buying passage. You're buying the risk of *you*."

"I know. That's why I'm paying in this." I nudged the satchel an inch toward her. "A charm like that is worth more than your hull. With it, the Kestrel doesn't run the Marches. She *threads* them."

Sable made a low sound that might have been a whistle or a curse. Petra wrote something down. Fen leaned forward until Harl pulled him back by the collar like a cat with a kitten.

Wren picked up the charm at last. Turned it over. Held it to the lantern light—and here's the thing about testing a luck-charm: you can't test it directly, because it's already working on the test. Any honest charm-seller will tell you that. So what Wren did instead was cleverer.

She flipped the charm into the lantern flame.

The oilcloth wrapper caught; the knot didn't burn. Storm-silk doesn't. But the binding flared—a brief blue shimmer, the working flexing, bending the fire's attention elsewhere so the thread passed through untouched. Probability, doing its quiet work. The flame had simply decided, overwhelmingly, not to touch it.

The crew exhaled as one body.

"Right," Wren said, and dropped the charm into her shirt. "Fare accepted. One condition."

"Name it."

"You eat with us, you work with us, and you sleep where we sleep. No cabin, no privacy, no secrets above decks." Her eyes were flat and serious. "I don't care what you are, August-who-sells-charms. But I care very much what you do when the warders board us at the Gray checkpoint, and I intend to be standing next to you when you decide. Understood?"

Understood. Though I noted, with the small cold part of my mind that had kept me alive for twenty years, that *no secrets above decks* was a promise I could not actually keep. Below decks, then. I was already arranging my lies into their traveling shapes.

"Understood," I said.

* * *

We sailed on the evening tide, and I learned the Kestrel the way you learn anything worth knowing: by being useless at it first.

I coiled rope wrong. Fen showed me how, laughing, not meanly. I took the tiller during a calm stretch and held us straight for nearly an hour before Sable pronounced me "less than hopeless." I learned that Harl talked constantly when he was nervous and that he was nervous whenever land was in sight, and that Petra kept the true ledger hidden inside a false ledger inside a prayer book, because of course she did. I learned that Wren ate last, always, and that nobody commented on it, and that this was its own kind of law.

And somewhere past the harbor lights, with the city shrinking behind us and the first true dark I'd stood in since the stars went out pressing down around the sails, I realized I was humming.

I stopped. Listened to myself. When had I last hummed? At my stall I made myself small and pleasant and forgettable; forgettable people don't hum. In the twenty years of nights at the Hollow Gate, I had been alone with the wards and the wind, and the wind does not count as company.

But here I was, elbow-deep in a bucket of salt fish with a freckled boy chattering at me about currents, and I was humming an old tune my mother used to sing, and nobody was watching me the way you watch a suspect. They were watching me the way you watch the new hand. Which is to say: critically, but with the assumption that I'd still be here tomorrow.

It terrified me more than the warders ever had.

Because here is what twenty years of hiding teaches you, and it took me three days on that ship to say it honestly: hiding isn't safe. Hiding is just a slower way of being caught. Every year I kept the gate alone, I told myself I was protecting the world from what the gate held—and maybe I was—but I was also protecting myself from this. From belonging to people who could be taken away. From giving anyone else a reason to hang.

Voss had taught me control. I'd thought I'd rejected him. Standing in the galley of an ugly ship, fish scales on my sleeves, I began to wonder if I'd only ever copied him in smaller handwriting.

"You've gone somewhere," Fen said.

"Just counting fish."

"You count them wrong. There's eleven. You said nine."

"Then I'm a generous counter."

He grinned. Above us, Wren's boots crossed the deck, steady as a metronome, and the Kestrel ran north through water gone strangely pale—the first stain of the blight bleeding down from the Marches.

* * *

The checkpoint came on the third day, because checkpoints always come on the worst possible day; that's practically their charter.

Gray Rock Reach: a burned tower on a spit of stone, rebuilt in Voss's black-and-white livery, with a chain across the channel and soldiers on the parapet and a signal mirror flashing lazy interrogations at every hull that passed. Wren brought us to a stop half a mile out, and we waited, and the waiting was its own torture, and I spent it memorizing exits I couldn't use.

"They board every third ship," she murmured beside me at the rail. "They boarded us last run. They'll remember the Kestrel."

"So we turn back?"

"We pay the toll." She glanced sideways at me. "Which brings me to the part of the voyage where I learn whether you're worth what you paid."

The boarding party came out in a longboat: six soldiers, a sergeant with a ledger, and—my stomach dropped—a warder. Young, but real, with the gray cloak and the iron pin and the faint oily pressure of active senses sweeping over our deck like a hand feeling for a pulse.

I knew that pressure intimately. I had been that pressure, once, in another life, before I'd chosen the stall and the smallness. And I knew exactly what it would find if it brushed against me: a working buried under my skin like a splinter, twenty years deep, too large to hide and too old to remove.

So I did the only thing I could. I reached for the oldest trick I knew—not magic, but craft. I let the warder's senses find the ship instead of me. A vessel that has carried cargo for thirty years soaks up residue the way a wall soaks up smoke: tar-charms in the hold, salt-workings in the bilge, the ghost of a hundred small smugglers' spells soaked into the wood. The Kestrel was, magically speaking, a confession. All I had to do was stand very still and be furniture.

The sergeant came up the ladder with his ledger. "Ship's name?"

"Kestrel. Out of Corren, salt run, papers current." Wren handed them over with the bored competence of a woman who had done this forty times and been impressed by none of them.

"Cargo?"

"Salt, tar, and disappointment."

The sergeant almost smiled, hated himself for it, and didn't. He checked the manifest against the hold while the warder walked the deck. I stacked crates. My hands were steady. My heartbeat was a war drum, and I kept my face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman thinking about nothing but crates.

The warder stopped near me. Of course he did. The universe has a sense of humor and it is not a kind one.

"You," he said. "Name and trade."

"August. Deckhand. Newly signed."

"Newly signed." His senses pressed against me like weather. I fed him the ship: the tar-charms, the bilge-workings, the honest criminal residue of a smuggling hull. Stand still. Be furniture. Be the least interesting object on this entire—

"She's new," Wren said, appearing at my shoulder with the timing of a woman who had been counting the same seconds I had. "Picked her up at Saltgate. Cheap, strong back, can't coil rope. You want her? Take her. I'll throw in the rope."

A beat. Two. The warder's gaze moved from me to Wren and back, weighing, and I understood suddenly that this was the test Wren had meant—not the charm in the lantern, not the toll at the chain, but this: whether I would panic, and whether she would cover, and what either of those things would cost.

"Manifest's clean," the sergeant said finally. "Toll's standard. Pay the man, Captain."

Wren paid. The longboat pulled away. The chain dropped. And the Kestrel ran on north into graying water while I stood at the rail and discovered that my knees had opinions about the last five minutes that they'd been politely withholding.

"You lied to them," I said quietly, when the soldiers were specks. "You don't even know why I'm going north."

"No," Wren agreed. "I don't." She turned, and her eyes were not friendly. They were the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Which means tonight, below decks, in front of my crew, you're going to tell me enough of the truth that I can decide whether covering for you was seamanship or suicide. That's the rest of the fare, August. The charm bought the ship. The truth buys the captain."

I looked north, toward the pale stain on the horizon where the Marches began, toward the mountain underneath whose roots my brother's soul had been sold like a sack of grain. Toward the gate I had kept alone for twenty years, and the ruin that solitude had made of everything.

"All right," I said. "But you should sit down first."

Wren Calloway, who feared nothing on any sea, folded her arms and did not sit down.

So I told her about Tomas.
