## 5. Salt and Ash Roads

There are three kinds of passengers a smuggler learns to dread. The first is the one who talks too much. The second is the one who won't talk at all. The third is the one who pays in magic.

August Vane was all three, and I'd known her for four days.

She'd come aboard the *Kestrel* at Corren's salt-docks with a leather case she held like it contained a sleeping child and a face like a woman attending her own funeral out of politeness. Paid me in a luck-charm — a little braided thing of copper wire and something that wasn't quite glass — which I should have thrown into the harbor on principle, because luck-charms are how warders catch you, everyone knows that, and because I don't believe in luck. I believe in ballast, tide tables, and knowing which checkpoints have been burned this week.

I kept the charm. That should have been my first warning about myself.

* * *

The checkpoint at Grayhaven had been burned three days before we reached it, and you could smell it from a mile off — that sweet-rot stink of wards dying angry, like meat left in the sun. Voss's soldiers hadn't bothered rebuilding. They'd done something worse. They'd set up a table in the middle of the ash road, pitched a canopy over it against the drizzle, and started asking questions.

We came around the headland at dawn with the tide doing us one favor and the wind none, and I counted six soldiers, a sergeant with a voice like gravel in a bucket, and a line of travelers backed up half a mile: farmers, refugees, a man pulling a handcart of turnips like turnips were going to save anyone. Two soldiers worked the line. A third stood apart, holding a slate, and every few minutes he'd look up from it and study the faces coming through, slow as a man reading scripture.

The slate had a sketch on it. I couldn't see it from the water, but Fen — my youngest, my sharpest eyes — read it through the spyglass and went quiet in a way Fen never goes quiet.

"Captain," he said. "It's a woman. Middle-aged. They're calling her—" he squinted— "*a keeper of gates.*"

Beside me at the rail, August Vane went very still. Not frightened-still. Still the way water goes still right before it decides to be ice.

"Anyone can keep a gate," she said. "It's a door. You push it."

"Sure," I said. "And anyone can smuggle salt."

Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Yours is a respectable crime, Captain Calloway. Mine would not be."

I didn't ask what hers was. Not then. That was the arrangement we'd struck at Corren — passage to Dellamere, no questions past the ones that kept my crew alive, payment in charms I pretended were trinkets. I've run enough strangers to know when a story has a locked room in it. August Vane was mostly locked rooms with a person standing in the doorway saying *nothing to see here* so politely you almost apologized for looking.

The problem was that the soldiers on the ash road very much wanted to see.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about running a burned checkpoint: the burning isn't the danger. The burning is the opportunity. A dead ward-tower means no scrying, no alarm-bells wired into the border net, no record of your hull beyond whatever a bored clerk writes down. What it means instead is men with slates and sketches and time to kill, because their officer wants a capture and their pay depends on it, and there is nothing more dangerous than a soldier with a quota.

I put us in at the old fish landing, a mile south, where the pilings were rotten enough that no one official bothers. Rode the cart road north with Fen driving, August beside him under a fishwife's shawl I got off Marta Tull for the price of two casks of decent brandy and an apology. August looked, I'll grant her, convincingly miserable. Some of it might even have been real.

The line moved like a funeral. We shuffled forward through the drizzle while the soldier with the slate asked his questions. *Name. Business. Where from. Where bound.* And then, to every woman past thirty, a new one, delivered flat as a ledger entry:

"You seen anyone keeping gates? Anyone mend wards? Anyone sell charms that aren't charms?"

Most people said no. One old boy said *my wife mends socks, does that count,* and got a rifle-butt across the shoulder for his humor, and after that everybody said no very quickly.

Then it was our turn, and the soldier with the slate looked up, and I watched his eyes do a thing I've seen a hundred times and never learned to stop: they snagged on August and stayed.

"Name," he said.

"Marta Tull," said August, in Marta Tull's own accent, which was either an impressive piece of work or evidence that she'd spent more time listening to fishwives than any charm-seller should. "Fishwife. Bound for my sister's at Dellamere. Got herring." She lifted the basket. It had herring in it. When had she gotten herring?

The soldier turned the slate around. The sketch on it was good. Too good — drawn by someone who'd studied the face a long time, or studied a description written by someone who had. It was August. Older around the eyes than the drawing, hair different, but it was her, the way a storm is still the same storm whatever name the fishermen give it.

"That you?" the soldier asked the sketch, and then, catching himself, "You know her?"

"No," said August.

"She looks like you," he said.

"Everyone looks like someone," said August. "My sister says I look like our mother, and our mother looked like trouble." Somewhere behind me Fen made a sound he converted into a cough.

The soldier stared. And here is the part I still think about, lying awake at night, in the way you think about a rope that held when it had no business holding: I stepped between them.

Not smoothly. Nothing I did that morning was smooth. I just moved — shoulder in, voice out, the full weight of a captain's boredom deployed like a weapon. "Look, friend, we've been on this road since before light, I've got forty barrels of salt going soft in this rain, and my girl here hasn't slept. If you want to compare her to every drawing in the Marches, fine, but do it down the line, because there's forty people behind us and one of them's got a handcart full of turnips and a temper."

He blinked at me. Soldiers are like gulls; they go for whoever makes the most noise.

"The sketch," he persisted, "is of a woman who keeps gates. Ward-craft. Illegal since the Edict. There's a reward."

"There's a reward," I agreed, "and there's also my cargo spoiling, and only one of those things is my problem. Is she your woman?" I jerked a thumb at August. "Does she look like she could keep anything but herring fresh?"

And August — gods help me — August held up the basket and said, "They're very fresh," and the soldier laughed. Just a short bark of it, surprised out of him, and the laugh broke the spell the way a laugh will, because suspicion is a serious thing and seriousness is fragile.

He waved us through. Wrote something on his slate. I didn't breathe properly until the checkpoint was a smudge of smoke behind us and the road bent away toward the coast hills.

Fen waited exactly until it was a smudge and not a second longer. "Captain," he said, "that was her."

"I know," I said.

"That was the woman on the slate."

"Fen."

"She paid you in *magic*, Captain."

"Fen," I said again, and he shut up, because Fen's a good kid under all the noticing, and because he knew — they all knew — that I'd stepped in front of that slate without thinking, without deciding, the way you grab someone falling. And I didn't have a reason. That was the part that sat wrong in my chest like a swallowed hook. I cover for my crew because they're mine. I cover for paying passengers because they're coin. August Vane was neither, and I'd lied to a man with a rifle for her anyway, and lying to armed men for unexplained reasons is how smugglers end up in ditches with their throats opened and their cargo confiscated.

So that evening, in the lee of a ruined kiln with the crew fed and the watch set, I went and found her sitting on the seawall with her leather case in her lap, watching the water like it owed her money.

* * *

"Let's skip the part where I pretend I haven't been thinking," I said, sitting down next to her. "I'm bad at pretending. It's why I'm a smuggler and not a diplomat."

August didn't look at me. "You want your charm back."

"I want the truth, and if the truth comes with the charm back, I'll take both." I let that sit. Rain came in off the water, fine as sifted flour. "That soldier had your face drawn by someone who's seen it close. Someone talked, or someone watched, and either way there's a reward on you with the word *gates* in it. I ran a burned checkpoint today on your behalf and I did it blind. On my ship, blind gets people killed. Usually me."

"You covered for me," she said, "without knowing why."

"Don't flatter yourself. I cover for lots of people without knowing why. It's called being a bad businessman." I pulled the luck-charm out of my coat and turned it over in my fingers. In the dark it did something subtle and wrong, the way deep water does. "But here's what I know. Four days aboard, you've fixed our compass needle that's been drunk for a year. You re-sealed the aft hatch that leaked. Yesterday you told Harl the storm was turning two hours before the glass did, and you were right, and you covered it by claiming you smelled it. Nobody smells weather turning. So either you're the luckiest woman on the coast, or everything you touch, you touch with craft. And there's a man on an ash road with a drawing of your face and a word on his slate that starts with *ward*."

The waves worked the pilings below us. She was quiet long enough that I started composing her eulogy for the conversation, and then she spoke.

"My brother is dead," she said. "Or worse than dead. His soul was sold — sold the way you'd sell a cask, weighed and priced and passed across a table — to the court beneath Mount Sere. Twenty years ago. As payment." Her hands flattened on the leather case. "I'm going to get it back."

I'll say this for myself: I didn't laugh. Plenty of captains would have. Souls aren't cargo. Souls aren't anything. You can't hold them, weigh them, sell them — except that everything I'd heard since the stars went out suggested the world had recently renegotiated its terms, and the new terms were ugly.

"Sold to who?"

"People who buy." She finally looked at me, and her eyes in the dark were steady in a way that cost her something; I recognized the price because I've paid it, telling crews about cargoes that don't exist and routes that shouldn't. "A man named Malachai Voss is collecting what the court holds. He needs my brother's soul to open something called the Hollow Gate. I need to reach it first."

"And the sketch? The *keeper of gates*?"

Something moved behind her face — a door closing, gently, on a room she wasn't ready to show me. "Voss thinks I know things about gates," she said. "He's wrong about what I am. But he's right that I'm in his way."

It was a lie. Not a whole lie — a sailor's lie, true along the waterline and hollow underneath. I've told enough of them to hear the join. She was giving me the soul because the soul was safe to give, grief being a currency people don't counterfeit, and she was keeping the gate because the gate was the thing that would make me put her off at the next port and sail hard in the other direction.

I could have pushed. I want that recorded somewhere: I could have pushed. I had her alone, at night, on my deck, with a crew at my back and a charm in my fist that hummed like a trapped fly. One captain's suspicion, properly applied, and I'd have known everything.

Instead I heard myself say: "Mount Sere's a month's hard sailing this time of year, and Dellamere's on the way, and you're paying for the whole route, not half of it. So congratulations. You're stuck with us until the job's done."

She blinked. "Just like that?"

"Not just like that." I stood up, joints announcing themselves the way they do past forty. "Like this: you saved Fen's cousin's boat in Corren harbor — don't look surprised, Marta Tull talks. You fixed my compass. You lied to that soldier with better nerve than half my crew. And when the stars went out, you didn't run inland like everyone sensible. You booked passage *toward* the thing everyone's fleeing." I tucked the charm back in my coat, because apparently I was keeping it, gods help me. "People run from trouble or toward it, and the ones who run toward it are usually either fools or the only ones who can help. I've got a full complement of fools already. I could use the other kind."

For a moment — just a moment — something in her face came unlocked, some expression twenty years out of practice, rusty and blinking in the light. Gratitude, maybe. Or the memory of gratitude. Then it shut again, polite as a shopkeeper's smile.

"Thank you, Captain," she said, and meant it, and withheld something while meaning it, and I let her, because that's the bargain you make with people who've survived something: you take the truth they can carry and you wait for the rest.

I was halfway to my cabin when Fen fell into step beside me, because Fen always knows.

"Well?" he said.

"We're carrying a soul-thief's sister to Mount Sere," I said.

He considered that. "Is that the truth?"

"It's the truth she's selling," I said. "And Fen — I bought."

He grinned, and I didn't, because under the grin and the purchase and the strange warm feeling of having chosen loyalty over sense, one thought kept circling back like a gull over offal:

She'd said *he's wrong about what I am.*

Which meant there was a right answer. And I intended, before this voyage ended, to know exactly what it was.

Ahead of us, past the gray line of the coast, lay Dellamere — the free city, the archive city, the place where forbidden books went to grow old in company. And August Vane, I noticed, had gone very quiet at the rail, staring at its distant lights like a woman reading a letter she'd written to herself years ago and hoped would never arrive.
