## 6. The Archivist Below

Dellamere smelled of ink and brine and old arguments.

That last one took me a moment to place, standing at the Kestrel's rail while Wren negotiated our berth with a harbor official who had the weary look of a man paid to be offended. But that was the smell — the whole city smelled like a debate that had been going on for three hundred years and showed no signs of concluding. Corren smelled like fear kept under a lid. Dellamere smelled like fear that had been opened, examined, annotated, and shelved.

"Free city," Fen said beside me, spitting over the rail with the reverence of a man reciting scripture. "No warders. No registries. You can say the word *magic* out loud here and nobody arrests you."

"What do they do instead?"

He considered. "Argue about it."

The Kestrel slid into a berth between a grain hauler and something low and black that I decided not to look at too closely. Wren came down the gangplank last, counting coins into her palm without looking at them, which is either a sign of great wealth or great practice at pretending.

"Rules," she said, to me specifically, because apparently I required rules. "We're here two days. Resupply, refit, and you find your archivist. You don't tell anyone what we're carrying." She paused. "You don't tell anyone what *you* are."

"I don't know what I am."

"Then you've got nothing to gossip about. Perfect."

* * *

Ilex Threnody worked below ground, which I learned should have told me everything.

Finding them was its own small odyssey. The archive district sat on the seaward side of the city, a warren of stone buildings sunk half their height into the cliff face, so that the upper floors caught the light and the lower ones caught the damp. Every door bore a plaque naming what died inside: Histories, Herbals, Cartographies, Music (Disputed). One door simply said NO, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *we know why you're asking.*

Wren refused to come. "Last time I set foot in an archive, a librarian fined me for breathing on a map. I'll be drinking somewhere defensible." She gave me a knife I hadn't asked for and instructions I hadn't requested — *if you're not back by moonrise, I'm assuming you're dead and selling your charm stock* — and left me at the top of a stair that went down farther than stairs have any business going.

The door at the bottom was iron, unmarked except for a chalked symbol I recognized from my mother's letters: the old sigil for *that which must not be catalogued*, drawn here as a joke, or a dare, or a welcome mat.

I knocked.

"State your business and your blood fraction," said a voice through the iron. It was a pleasant voice, unhurried, with the particular smoothness of someone who has never once been interrupted successfully.

"My business is gate-lore. My blood fraction is none of your — "

The door opened.

The person in the doorway was tall in the way that suggested they'd been asked more than once to stop growing out of politeness. Silver hair cropped short, skin the pale gray-gold of moonlight on water, ears that came to subtle points the owner had clearly made no effort to hide. Half-fae, then, and not bothering with glamour — which in most cities would be a death sentence, and in Dellamere was merely a conversation starter.

"You're the one who's been asking after me in the taverns," they said. "Badly. Your cover story was 'a scholar interested in coastal folklore.' No one believes a woman with ward-scarred hands cares about coastal folklore."

I looked down at my hands. Twenty years of drawing sigils leaves marks — fine white lines along the fingers, like the ghost of strings. I'd stopped seeing them years ago.

"August Vane," I said.

"Yes," said Ilex Threnody, stepping aside. "I know. Come in before someone sees you being known."

* * *

Their rooms were what I imagine a dragon's hoard looks like if the dragon were organized and slightly ashamed of itself. Shelves cut into the living rock, stacked with books, scrolls, tablets, and objects that had no business existing: a compass that pointed at lies, a jar of preserved thunder, a child's alphabet primer with every letter subtly wrong. Candles burned without dripping. A kettle poured itself.

"You're exiled," I said, taking the chair they indicated. It was the only chair. They sat on a stack of atlases.

"I'm *liberated*," Ilex corrected. "Exile implies someone wanted me back. The Concordance Archive expelled me for cataloguing forbidden materials. Specifically—" they gestured at a shelf near the ceiling, where perhaps forty volumes sat behind a wire mesh like dangerous animals, "—gate-lore. All of it. Every account, every fragment, every mad pilgrim's diary. I spent eleven years proving the Hollow Gate wasn't unique."

My spine went cold in a way I'd learned to recognize as information. "It isn't?"

"There were others. Nine, by my count, though two are contested. Gates — thresholds between what magic was and what it's allowed to be. Each one bound, each one sealed, each one *fed*." They watched me carefully as they said the word. "You knew that part already. Don't perform surprise; it's insulting to us both."

I didn't answer. In the dark of the Hollow, on nights when the wards hummed under my hands, I had felt something on the other side of the binding. A pull. A hunger, patient as geology. I'd told myself it was the gate's nature. Sealed things strain. That's what sealing means.

"How does one feed a gate?" I asked, and my voice came out level, which cost me.

"Soul-stuff. Distilled. The old texts call it *resonance* — a soul compressed past the point of memory into pure... currency." Ilex rose, pulled down a slim volume, and opened it across their knees without reading it. They'd clearly memorized the page. "And here we arrive at Mount Sere. The court beneath it — the Gilded Court, the Pale Court, whatever flattering name the fae nobility are using this century — has run the only functioning soul-economy in the world for four hundred years. They buy. They sell. They lend at interest that would make a Corren moneylender blush. And for the last twenty years, their largest single customer has been a man buying resonance in quantities that suggest he's fueling something very large."

"Voss."

"Malachai Voss." Ilex said the name the way you'd set down something heavy. "Usurper. Self-declared Lord Warden of the Northern Reaches. And before all that — " they tilted their head, studying me with those fae eyes that catch light like still water, " — shall I continue? You've gone very still. Stillness is either guilt or preparation. With warders it's usually both."

"He was never a warder," I said. "Warders are licensed. Registered. He's — "

"He was the finest gate-warder of his generation. Trained at the Hollow crossing itself. He held the northern wards for six years before the Purge, and when the order fell, he didn't burn his credentials like the others. He *kept* them. Kept everything. The rites, the resonances, the binding-geometry." Ilex leaned forward. "You of all people should know that, August. He was your teacher."

The candle flames all bent sideways at once, though there was no draft.

I want to say I laughed. I want to report some dignified refusal, some sharp denial. What actually happened was smaller and worse: I heard myself say, "No," in a voice like a girl's, and then I heard the silence afterward, in which twenty years rearranged themselves around a new center of gravity.

Because it was true. Of course it was true. Malachai Voss — Master Voss, who taught me that a ward is a promise you make to strangers; who could read a binding sigil faster than I could read a sentence; who stood with me on the wall above the Hollow Gate when I was nineteen and said, *someday this will be yours to keep, and keeping is the highest calling there is.* Who vanished the year after, in the chaos of the Purge, and whom I had mourned quietly and privately ever since, the way you mourn a lighthouse that goes dark.

"He told me the gates were dying," I said finally. "In his last lesson. He said the bindings were failing everywhere, that magic was draining out of the world, and that someday someone would have to choose between letting it go and — " I stopped.

"And holding it," Ilex finished. "Yes. That doctrine appears throughout his early writings. Control justifies anything. A gate is a wound; a warden is a tourniquet; a tourniquet may tighten as far as necessary." They closed the book. "He wrote beautifully. That's the trap of him. I've read usurpers' manifestos by the shelf-full, and Voss is the only one who made me *want* to agree."

"The souls he's buying. For the ritual. To reopen the gate." My hands had found each other in my lap and were gripping hard enough to hurt. "One soul in particular."

"Tomas Vane." Ilex said it gently, which from a fae-tongued archivist is a deliberate act of skill. "Sold to the court beneath Sere twenty years ago. Age fourteen. The transaction records list the payment as 'binding maintenance, indefinite term.' Which is archivist language for: they've been feeding him to the Hollow Gate since before you started keeping it."

There it was. The thing I'd carried down those stairs without knowing its weight, now set on the table between us, ticking.

Twenty years I had tended those wards. Twenty years of midnight work, of careful invisibility, of telling myself that secrecy was sacrifice, that my quiet vigil was the only thing holding the dark back. And all the while the wards I maintained had been *drinking*. Being fed. Being kept fat and functional on a boy's soul so that the gate would stand ready — ready for whom?

Ready for him. Ready for his student's perfect, obedient, invisible keeping to deliver the gate to him intact.

"I did his work for him," I said. "For twenty years. For free."

"You did what you thought was right with incomplete information," Ilex said. "Which describes nearly every tragedy I've catalogued." They stood, crossed to the meshed shelf, and began working a lock with fingers that moved like water finding its level. "Here's what matters now. The court sells to Voss, but courts also keep ledgers, and ledgers have gaps. If you mean to steal a soul out from under the Gilded Court, you'll need three things: the servant-tunnels under Sere, which exist and which I have mapped; a distraction of genuinely historic quality; and me."

I looked up. "Why? You're safe here. Exiled, but safe. Why hand yourself back to the world that burned your life's work?"

Ilex held the locked volume against their chest and considered me with an expression I couldn't parse — something older than amusement, sadder than curiosity.

"Because I spent eleven years proving the gates weren't unique," they said, "and in all that time I never met a single keeper. Warders, yes. Fanatics, certainly. But a keeper — someone who chose the vigil, who paid the price in secret, who is now standing in my archive deciding whether to commit treason against the most dangerous man alive — " they smiled, and it transformed their face into something younger and more reckless. "You're a primary source, August Vane. And I have *so many questions*."

* * *

We argued terms until the candles burned themselves out of spite. Ilex would come aboard the Kestrel with their maps and their ledger-knowledge and their insufferable composure; in exchange, passage to wherever the road ended, protection of their person and their books, and — insisted upon with unexpected ferocity — the right to record everything. "Someone has to write down what actually happened," they said. "The victors will lie and the vanquished will lie better. An archivist owes the truth to whoever survives to read it."

I climbed back up the long stair into Dellamere's evening with the volume under my arm — Ilex's masterwork, *A Catalogue of Thresholds*, banned in nine jurisdictions — and the name Malachai Voss sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Master Voss. Whose hands had guided mine on the first sigil I ever drew. Who used to say *again, August, again* until my fingers bled, and then healed them himself, murmuring that a warden's body was a tool and tools must be maintained. Who believed, with his whole magnificent ruined heart, that control was love's sternest form.

I had built my whole life inside his lessons without noticing the walls were his.

At the harbor, Wren was waiting exactly where she'd said she'd be, leaning against a bollard with the boneless confidence of a cat that owns the street. She took one look at my face.

"That bad?"

"Worse. We've got an archivist. And I need to tell you something about the man we're stealing from, and you're going to wish I hadn't."

She pushed off the bollard and fell into step beside me, matching my pace without asking it. Behind us, Dellamere's lamps were coming on floor by floor, city by city, all the way down into the rock.

"Well," she said. "Start with his name. Names first. Everything else goes easier after names."

"Voss," I said. "His name is Voss. And twenty years ago, he taught me everything I know."

Wren was quiet for three full steps — a personal record — and then she whistled, long and low, at the darkening sea.

"Oh," she said, "this voyage is going to be *interesting*."
