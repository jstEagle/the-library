## 8. The Usurper's Summons

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a mountain, and I have spent thirty years learning to conduct it.

Not the silence of tombs—tombs are merely rooms that have given up. I mean the silence of a held breath. The silence of a court that has been waiting so long for something to happen that it has forgotten how to make noise of its own. Mount Sere breathes that silence through its gilded corridors, down the stairways carved by hands older than any kingdom, into the chamber at its root where I have built my life's work out of bone-ink and patience.

I stood in that chamber this morning, as I have stood every morning for six years, and listened to the gate hum.

It hums low now. Barely a thread of sound. Twenty years ago—when I first came beneath the mountain, when the fae court still pretended I was a guest rather than an instrument—it sang loud enough to ache in the teeth. A sealed gate does not go quiet because it is satisfied. It goes quiet because it is starving.

And I intend to feed it.

* * *

Let me tell you what they will say about me, afterward, whoever writes the histories—and someone always writes the histories; it is the one industry no war has ever managed to kill.

They will call me Malachai Voss, the Usurper. They will say I betrayed my order, sold my oath to the under-court, murdered the Wardens' Conclave in their own hall. All true, incidentally, though "murdered" is doing generous work there—the Conclave was already dead. It simply hadn't stopped moving yet. There is a difference between killing a man and closing his eyes, and I did the latter for eleven men who had long since stopped seeing.

They will not say—or if they say it, they will not believe it—that I am trying to save the world.

But I am. That's the joke of it, if you like jokes. Every terrible thing I have done has been an act of faith, and faith, done thoroughly enough, looks identical to atrocity from the outside.

Here is the truth that kept me awake in my years as a warder, the truth I found scrawled in the margins of ledgers no one else bothered to read: magic is dying. Not fleeing, not hiding—dying. Slowly, the way a language dies when children stop speaking it. Each generation the wells run a little shallower. Each decade the wild places grow a little tamer, the spells a little harder, the mages a little fewer and a little duller. I have measured it. I was, once, a very good measurer. The Hollow Gate was built to hold power *in*—a reservoir against the long drought—and then some frightened generation of keepers sealed it shut instead, hoarding the world's strength behind a wall of wards while everything outside withered.

A sealed gate isn't protection. It's a miser starving in a house full of bread.

So: open it. Pour the stored centuries back into the soil. Let magic run in the world's veins again, wild and ungovernable and everywhere. Yes, there will be convulsions. Yes, the law-abiding cities of men—who have outlawed the very thing that once saved them—will suffer. But a fever before health is still a cure, and I have never met a surgeon who apologized for the knife.

The Conclave called this heresy. The Conclave, mind you, whose grand solution to magical decay was to write increasingly elegant ward-circles around the problem until the problem was entirely enclosed in notation and nothing whatsoever had improved. I loved them. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever margin survives me. I loved those stubborn, cowardly, brilliant old men, and I closed their eyes gently, and I have regretted the gentleness more than the deed.

* * *

This morning I inspected the vessel.

It sits at the center of the ritual floor, on a plinth of black glass that took the under-court's glasswrights four months to grow. To look at, it is unremarkable—a stoppered jar of smoked crystal, no larger than a lantern, faintly warm to the touch like a stone left too near a fire. Inside is a soul.

Tomas Vane's soul, to be precise. Or rather, what fourteen years of boyhood distilled down to when the rest was pressed away: a compressed constellation of memory, a handful of light that remembers being a person. The fae do not waste. When the gate's original binding demanded a soul as anchor-price, the court paid with currency it had been collecting for centuries—souls bought, traded, won at cards from mortals who didn't read the fine print. This one had sat in the treasury for twenty years, catalogued and shelved, until I went looking for a key.

Because here is the arithmetic of gates, which I learned at the Conclave's knee and have never stopped resenting: you cannot unmake a binding from outside. You can only satisfy it. The Hollow Gate was bound with a soul, and so it must be opened with one—the same one, resonant, willing or otherwise. For twenty years the gate has been sipping at Tomas Vane through hairline channels in the seal, feeding itself the interest on its principal. What remains in the jar is nearly enough. Nearly.

I lifted the vessel and held it up to the light of the lumen-globes, the way a jeweler holds a stone, and I confess I spoke to it. I am not a sentimental man—I have audited my own sentiment the way I once audited wards—but there is something in a soul that demands acknowledgment, even compressed. Even shelved.

"Soon," I told it. "You'll be part of something enormous."

The light inside turned over slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice. I choose to believe that was recognition. The alternative—that it was only resonance answering proximity—is accurate, but accuracy is often the less merciful reading, and I have decided to spend my mercy where it costs least.

* * *

Now. The itch.

I felt it first three days ago, and I have felt it every day since, and it is driving me half to distraction, which at my age and station is saying something.

It began as a wrongness in the resonance readings—the gate's hunger-note shifting pitch by fractions no instrument caught but my own scarred ears registered instantly. Then the border wards fell, all along the north, in a single night, in a pattern that was not decay. Decay frays from the edges inward. This unraveled from the heart outward, deliberately, expertly, like a knot being picked by fingers that knew exactly which strand to pull.

Someone with gate-knowledge unmade my work.

And here is the thing that keeps me pacing the long corridor outside the ritual chamber at hours when even the fae court sleeps: I know that hand. The wards didn't fail randomly—they failed *clean*, each collapse sequenced to protect the population centers, routed away from the villages, threaded through the gaps in my own lattice with a precision that could only come from someone who had studied my lattice. Studied it for years. Maintained it, even.

There were exactly two people alive who knew my ward-work that intimately. One of them I buried myself, with honors, and I checked the grave.

Which leaves August.

Little August Vane, who sat in the third row of my lecture hall at seventeen with her chin propped on her fist, pretending boredom so convincingly that I nearly believed it myself. Who asked questions shaped like objections but aimed like arrows. Whose ward-drafts were so clean I used to hold them up to the class and lie that they were mine, just to watch her jaw tighten—because praise would have made her preen, and I wanted her hungry. She had the finest instinct for structural sympathy I have ever taught. Structural sympathy: the gift of feeling where a ward *wants* to break. You cannot train it. You can only recognize it and pray it never turns on you.

She disappeared the year after the sealing. Vanished into Corren's sprawl like a dropped coin into deep water, and I confess I let her go. Sentiment again, badly audited. I told myself she'd burn out, marry a cooper, die of ordinary life. And all these years, while I built toward this, she was apparently crouched behind a charm-seller's stall keeping my gate patched with her two clever hands—maintaining the very seal she should have helped me break, holding the world's medicine locked in its bottle out of—what? Duty? Fear? Love of quiet?

Twenty years of stillness, spent feeding the gate through the cracks. And she doesn't know. That's the exquisite cruelty of it, the part I turn over at night like a stone with something shining underneath: her ward-work, every careful invisible repair, every night she spent kneeling at that gate pouring herself into the seal—all of it siphoned stray power down the channels I laid. Her diligence fed my ritual. Her secrecy was my subsidy. If she ever learns it, and she will, it will break her more thoroughly than any soldier of mine could.

I find I don't relish that. Note it down: the Usurper does not relish breaking August Vane. He wishes, obscurely, that she had come to him years ago, that he could have shown her the ledgers, the measurements, the slow dying of the light—and watched her argue, because she would have argued magnificently, and perhaps eventually she'd have seen it. Perhaps together—

No. Sentiment. Flagged and filed. She is an interference pattern in my resonance and I will treat her as such.

* * *

The court, naturally, wants her dead.

I stood this evening in the Amber Gallery—so named because the entire ceiling is fossilized resin, and the fae lords like to point out the ancient insects trapped in it, which tells you most of what you need to know about their humor—while Lady Serevanthe unfolded herself from her throne-chair and made her request in the manner of her kind, which is to say as a compliment wrapped around a knife.

"How marvelous," she said, "that your little keeper stirs at last. Send us her name and we will send you her hands. The court so enjoys new acquisitions." Her smile has too many angles in it. All their smiles do. "Grief makes such interesting flavors."

I declined. Politely. In the under-court, declining a favor is itself a debt, and I now owe Serevanthe a favor of unspecified size, payable after the opening, which suits me fine because after the opening her entire economy of souls becomes obsolete and favors will be worth exactly what they've always been worth to the fae: nothing they can't take by force.

But privately—here, in the ledger of my own skull—I note the real reason I refused, and it is not strategy. It is that August Vane standing on the wall of the Hollow Gate, alive and arguing with me across the distance, is the last conversation I have left with the man I was before all this. Kill her, and I close my own eyes next.

* * *

Tonight I gave the summons.

The ritual requires a final resonance—the vessel brought to the gate itself, matched to the seal's living frequency at the moment of contact. Distance blurs the tuning. So the vessel travels to the Hollow, carried in a litter of iron and silk, escorted by a column of the under-court's honor guard and three regiments of mortal soldiers whose loyalty I purchased with the only coin that never devalues: the promise that when magic returns, their children will have some.

I stood on the approach road at dusk and watched them assemble, torchlight running down the column like water down a chain, and I thought: this is either the beginning of the world or the end of my understanding of it, and there is no longer any way to know which until it is done.

That is the honest terror of conviction. Doubters get to hedge. Believers walk out onto the ice and the ice either holds or it doesn't, and everyone watching gets to say afterward that they saw the cracks.

The itch has moved closer. I can feel it now almost continuously—a faint wrongness at the edge of my resonance sense, like a familiar tune played a half-step flat somewhere beyond the hills. She's coming. Of course she's coming. I trained her; I know precisely how stubborn I made her. Somewhere south of the Gray Marches, a smuggler's ship or a salt wagon or a stolen horse is carrying August Vane toward me, and she is bringing with her either the last obstacle to the world's salvation or the final piece of it, and I genuinely cannot tell which.

There is a version of this story in which I let her reach me. In which we stand on the wall of the Hollow Gate with the vessel between us, and I show her the numbers, the dying curves, the margins of dead men's ledgers, and she argues—and loses, or wins, and either way I finally hear my own doctrine said back to me in a voice younger and less tired than mine, and learn whether I ever believed it at all.

There is another version in which I use her. Her blood opened a ward in my vault-readings once; bloodline keys cut both directions, and the gate's original binding was written by her ancestors' hands. Resonance seeks resonance. A keeper's blood at the moment of opening might tune the seal better than any litter-borne jar—might make the opening cleaner, faster, kinder to the world's thin skin.

I have not decided which version I am living in. I have decided, however, that she arrives alive. Whatever else happens on that wall, August Vane reaches the Hollow Gate breathing, because I have waited thirty years for one conversation, and I am done being patient with everything except that.

Behind me, deep in the mountain, the gate hummed its thin starving note, and in the iron-and-silk litter the small warm light of a drowned boy turned over in its sleep, turning toward home without knowing it.

Three days. Let her come.
