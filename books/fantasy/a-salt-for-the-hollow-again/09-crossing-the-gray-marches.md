## 9. Crossing the Gray Marches

There are three kinds of trouble on a ship, and I've learned to price them all. There's the kind you can pay off with coin, which is cheap. There's the kind you pay off with blood, which is expensive but at least honest. And then there's the kind that climbs aboard wearing a charm-seller's face and pays its passage in luck-charms that shouldn't work, and you spend the whole voyage telling yourself you'll sort out what it cost you later.

August Vane was the third kind.

We'd been running north along the coast for six days when the Kestrel's bow-wave turned the wrong color. Not dirty—wrong. Green water going gray like old dishwater, and then grayer, until the sea beneath us looked less like water and more like the memory of water. Fen spat over the rail and said what we were all thinking.

"That's not the sea doing that."

"No," I said. "That's the Marches."

The Gray Marches sit between the last honest harbor and the salt road to Mount Sere—a hundred square miles of drowned meadowland where the border wards used to run thick as hedge-rows before they fell. Everyone knew the wards had come down when the stars went out. What nobody said out loud, because saying it made it real, was what the wards had been *holding back*. Twenty years of wild magic, penned up with nowhere to go, festering like a wound under a bandage nobody changed.

Now the bandage was off, and the wound had opinions.

"We could go around," Harl offered from the tiller. He said it the way he offered most things—like he already knew the answer and was just checking whether I'd gone soft.

"Going around adds nine days." I spread my chart on the deck box and weighted the corners with coins. "Nine days is nine more days of Voss's soldiers asking travelers about women who keep gates. Nine more days of August here standing on my deck looking like exactly that woman." I didn't look at her when I said it. That was deliberate. "We go through."

"Through the fester," Fen said.

"Through the fester."

Nobody cheered. Good crews don't cheer bad plans; they just start working them. That's how I knew I had a good crew, even then, even with Harl's eyes doing that thing they did when he thought no one was watching—flicking shoreward, flicking back, weighing something I couldn't see yet.

August stood at the rail with her hood down for once, studying the gray water like she was reading it. Maybe she was. I'd stopped pretending I knew what she was by then, but I hadn't yet stopped pretending it didn't matter.

"You've crossed this before," she said. Not a question.

"Twice. Before the stars went out." I came to stand beside her, because a captain who won't stand at the ugly end of her own orders isn't worth the salt on her decks. "It was marshland then. Ugly, but honest ugly. Now—" I gestured at the water, the color of unwashed wool. "The wards kept things sorted. Wild magic doesn't sort well on its own. It pools. It grows teeth."

"And if it bites?"

"Then we learn something new about ourselves." I clapped her shoulder and immediately regretted the familiarity of it, but she didn't flinch, so I let it stand. "Rig the lines. Double-watch. Anything glows, anything sings, anything looks at you sideways—you call it loud."

Fen grinned his gap-toothed grin. "And if it looks at us frontways?"

"Then it's lying."

* * *

The Marches got worse the way bad weather does—not all at once but in increments, each one small enough to explain away until you looked back and couldn't find where reasonable ended.

First it was the birds. Or the shapes that used to be birds, wheeling overhead in formations too regular, too patient, like punctuation marks waiting for a sentence. Then it was the reeds, which had grown through the hulls of sunken boats and come up flowering in colors I don't have names for and don't want any. Then it was the sound. A low hum under everything, felt in the fillings of your teeth, that Ilex—the half-fae archivist we'd picked up in Dellamere, all elbows and footnotes—identified with the enthusiasm of a man who had never once been punched for being right.

"Resonance bleed," he said, crouched at the bow with his long fingers trailing above the water without touching it. "The wards didn't just suppress the wild magic. They gave it structure. Rules. Now it's making up its own." He glanced back at August, and there was something careful in the glance, some private arithmetic. "Spells left unattended don't die, you know. They go feral. Like dogs."

"Feral spells," Fen repeated. "You mean spells that bite."

"I mean spells that hunt."

I put Fen on the lead skiff anyway, because Fen was the best hand I had in shallow water and because fear shared is fear halved, or so my mother claimed before the sea took her argument up personally. We poled the Kestrel herself through the deeper channels—she drew too much for the open fen—and the gray water closed behind our hulls without wake, which is wrong, water should always argue with a boat, and every hour of that silence sat on my chest like a debt collector.

August worked the pole beside me. She'd taken to the work fast, the way she took to everything—quietly, competently, like someone who'd spent twenty years practicing at being useful without being noticed. I'd started cataloguing those habits the way you catalogue a cargo you suspect is mislabeled. The way she never let anyone walk behind her. The way she touched the hull each morning, two fingers, low on the starboard side, like checking a pulse. The way her charms *worked*, damn them, when charm-craft was supposed to be dead wood and superstition in a world with the magic outlawed out of it.

"You're staring, Captain," she said, without turning.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring at the back of my head and navigating."

"Multitasking."

Almost, she smiled. It was the almost ones that got under my skin. The full ones I could discount as salesmanship. The almost ones were the ones that looked like a person deciding, again, not to be a person.

The channel narrowed toward midafternoon, walls of reed closing in, the hum rising until Ilex pressed his palms flat against the gunwale and said, very quietly, "Captain. Stop the boat."

I stopped the boat.

Ahead, maybe forty yards, the reeds had died. Not browned, not withered—died, gone white as bone in a perfect circle thirty feet across, and in the center of the circle the water hung in the air. Just hung. A sphere of gray water the size of a wagon, turning slowly, holding inside it—I squinted, and wished I hadn't—shapes. Little swimming lights, darting, chasing each other. Playing.

"What is it?" Harl whispered.

"A spell," Ilex breathed. "An old one. Warding-work, maybe, or a binding that slipped its anchor when the wards fell. It's been out here alone for months." His voice did the thing it did when he forgot other people were listening, going soft and wondering. "It's *lonely*. Look at it. It's been playing with itself for months."

"Well," said Fen, "that's the saddest thing I ever heard, and I once heard Harl sing."

"It's dangerous," August said. Her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of someone reading a room full of armed men. "Unstructured resonance builds charge. If it touches the boat—"

"If it touches the boat, what?" I asked.

She hesitated one beat too long. "Nothing good."

So of course that was the moment Fen decided to be Fen. He leaned out over the skiff's bow with the boat-hook, meaning—I will charitably assume—to nudge the floating sphere away from the channel, and the sphere turned, all at once, like an eye.

Every light inside it stopped playing.

"Oh," said Fen.

The spell hit him before he finished saying it.

I've seen men take arrows, blades, booms, bullets, and one memorable musket-ball through the hat. None of it prepared me for watching a boy get hit by *weather*. The sphere collapsed into a streak of gray light and took Fen in the chest, and he went backward off the skiff into the reeds, and the sound he made wasn't a scream—it was the sound a rope makes when it parts under load, a snap and a sigh together, and then he was down in the shallows and the gray light was crawling over him like frost growing in fast-forward.

"FEN!" Harl was over the side before I could shout, which would have been brave if it weren't stupid, and I was halfway to shouting it when August moved.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of it. She didn't run to him. She *arrived*. One moment she was at the pole and the next she was kneeling in the shallows with her sleeves pushed back and both hands buried to the wrist in the gray light crawling on Fen's chest, and her hands were *glowing*—not the pretty glow of festival-lanterns, but a hard white workman's light, the light of a forge, of a lighthouse, of a thing built to hold back the dark on purpose. And she was speaking. Words I didn't know, in a cadence I almost recognized, the cadence of ward-chants I'd heard sung by the old warders before the order was broken and scattered, except hers were rougher, faster, improvised, a sailor's version of a scholar's prayer.

The gray light fought her. I saw it fight her—saw it rear up off Fen's chest like a snake striking at her wrists, saw her jaw set and her voice climb and the white light in her hands flare until I couldn't look at it, and the whole marsh went silent, the hum itself flinching, everything in a hundred miles of fester holding its breath to watch.

Then the gray light broke. It came apart like smoke in wind, streaming off Fen's body in threads, and August caught the threads—one, two, five, ten, her hands moving faster than I could follow, braiding them, folding them smaller and smaller until she held a knot of dull gray light the size of an apple. She stood, walked three steps to open water, and drove the knot down into the fen like planting a bulb. The water swallowed it. The hum dropped an octave and went quiet.

Fen coughed.

That was the sound that broke the spell on the rest of us. Harl hauled him upright, and Fen coughed again and swore, which was the healthiest thing I'd heard in a week, and then everyone started talking at once, and under all of it ran the question nobody was asking, the question sitting in the middle of the boat like a third passenger:

*What did she just do.*

August knelt in the shallows with Fen's head in her lap, checking his eyes, his pulse, her hands still faintly shining, and she knew—we all watched her know—that the hiding was over. I watched her decide what to do about it. It took her maybe two seconds. I've thought about those two seconds since, more than I think about most things.

She lifted her chin and looked at each of us in turn, captain first, and said, "Ask."

* * *

We made camp that night on a shelf of dry ground the charts called Widow's Rest, which the charts had named for reasons that seemed, after today, unhelpfully specific. Fen was asleep by the fire with color in his face and a headache he described as "a hangover with ambitions." Ilex hovered over him taking notes until Fen threatened to haunt him preemptively. And the rest of us sat in a ring around August Vane, who had not run, had not lied, had not done any of the hundred things I'd have done in her place, and were trying to work out why that made it worse instead of better.

"Magic," Harl said finally, because someone had to say the word out loud. "You did magic. Open magic. In front of the marsh, in front of the sky, in front of *us*."

"Yes."

"Do you know what they do to mages now? Do you know what Voss's people do?" He was up on his feet, pacing, and I let him pace because Harl pacing was safer than Harl thinking. "They don't arrest you. Arresting's for smugglers. For mages they—"

"I know what they do," August said quietly. "I've spent twenty years making sure they'd never do it to anyone near me."

"Twenty years." Harl laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "Twenty years on my boat. Sleeping an arm's reach from my sister's son. And the whole time—"

"The whole time I kept the wards on your hull patched," she said, and the words landed in the campfire silence like stones in a well. "The luck-charm I paid Wren with. The fair-weather knots. The reason the Kestrel runs customs lines that catch everyone else. You thought you had a lucky ship, Harl. You had a *kept* one."

I thought about that. I thought about six years of impossible passages, of inspections that somehow always found the other hold, of storms that split around us like we owed them money. I thought about the charm she'd paid me with, which I'd worn at my throat ever since and which had, I realized with a slow cold turn in my gut, probably been doing considerably more than luck.

"You've been warding my ship," I said. "Since Dellamere. Since before."

"Since the night I boarded."

"Why?"

"Because you were carrying me into the worst place in the world," she said, "and you deserved to arrive alive."

Harl opened his mouth. Closed it. Sat down heavily on a crate, deflated, a man whose anger had gone looking for footing and found none. "That's not fair," he muttered. "You can't just—be decent at people. It makes it very hard to stay angry."

"I know," August said. "It's the worst part."

Ilex, from across the fire, had his head tilted in that way he did when he was listening to something underneath the conversation. "The working you did on Fen," he said. "That was gate-craft. Old gate-craft. I've only read about it."

August's face did something complicated. "It's ward-craft."

"It's the root of ward-craft. Where do you think wards come from?" He leaned forward, firelight catching the fine bones of his fae-blooded face. "Someone taught you at a gate."

The silence stretched. Fen, feigning sleep with his eyes open a suspicious slit, was fooling no one. Harl looked at me. I looked at the fire, buying time, and then I looked at August, and I did the thing I'd been putting off since the checkpoint, since the burned road, since the first lie she told me that I chose to swallow.

"Here's how this goes," I said. "One truth tonight. Not the whole truth—I'm not a fool, and you're not ready—but one true thing, freely given, and we build on it. That's how crews work. That's how *this* crew works, if it works." I held her gaze. "Your true thing, Vane. Spend it well."

She looked around the fire—at Harl, sulking and listening; at Fen, failing to sleep; at Ilex, waiting with his terrible patience; at me, presumably, wearing whatever my face does when I care, which I'm told is nothing good.

"My brother is dead," she said. "Or worse than dead. His soul was sold, twenty years ago, to pay for a binding I've been keeping secret ever since. The man who bought it means to use it to reopen the Hollow Gate. I am going to Mount Sere to steal him back." A breath. "That's the true thing. It's not all of it. But it's true."

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, the marsh hummed its one long note, softer now, like it was listening too.

It was Fen, of course, who broke it. He sat up, wincing, hair full of reeds, and said: "So when do we leave?"

"Fen—"

"No, hear me out." He counted on his fingers. "Magic lady saved my life. Magic lady's been saving ours for weeks. Magic lady's brother got robbed by the same bastard who's burning checkpoints and asking after *us* now, thanks to Harl's big mouth at the toll—" Harl made an indignant noise; Fen talked over it—"and tomorrow we sail into the grayest ugliest hole in the world to go poke the biggest thief in history. Captain, I've been on this crew six years. This is the first job that's ever *meant* anything. I'm in."

He looked at Harl. Harl looked at the fire for a long time, jaw working, and I watched him lose the argument with himself in stages, and finally he said, "If she turns on us, I get first swing," and that was Harl for *aye*.

Ilex simply nodded, as if the outcome had been footnoted somewhere in advance.

Which left me. Five faces turned my way, and the sixth—hers—careful, braced, a woman who had clearly rehearsed being alone again and was trying not to hope in front of witnesses.

I took the luck-charm from around my neck. Held it up to the firelight. It gleamed like it was showing off.

"This stays on the ship," I said, "where it belongs. And you—" I pointed it at August, then tucked it back under my shirt, over my heart, where apparently it had been doing its real work all along. "You tell me the rest of it. All of it. When you're ready. Not because I'm owed it. Because a crew that keeps secrets sinks slower than a crew that lies, and I intend to sink very slowly indeed."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch. It was the smallest surrender I ever witnessed, and the loudest.

"Get some sleep," I said, standing. "Tomorrow we cross the deep fester, and I want everyone fresh enough to be terrified properly."

They bedded down by degrees. Ilex first, curled like a comma. Then Harl, still muttering. Then Fen, actually sleeping this time, breathing easy. August sat last at the fire, feeding it sticks, and I paused behind her on my way to the stern watch.

"For what it's worth," I said, "the magic was the least surprising thing about you. You've been doing impossible things since you came aboard. At least now I know what to blame."

She smiled up at me—the full one, this time, rare as stars lately—and said, "Get some sleep, Captain."

I was three steps gone when Ilex's voice stopped me, low and urgent, from the edge of the firelight. He was standing rigid, facing the dark water, one hand raised for silence, and the look on his face made the hair on my arms stand up.

"Captain," he whispered. "The hum. Listen to it."

I listened. The marsh's long note had changed—risen, quickened, and underneath it now, far off across the black water, something was answering. Rhythmic. Patient. Getting closer.

Like footsteps. Like a great many footsteps, wading.

"Wake them all," I said. "Quietly. Something's crossing the Marches toward us—and I'd bet salt it knows we're here."
