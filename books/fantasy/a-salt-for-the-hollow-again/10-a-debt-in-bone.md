## 10. A Debt in Bone

The waystation at Cinder's Rest was the kind of place that had stopped trying sometime before I was born. Its sign hung by one nail and had clearly lost the argument about what the establishment was called—half the letters had rotted, leaving something that read either *CINDER'S REST* or *SIN DERS T*, depending on your theology. The innkeeper was a woman built like a barrel with opinions, and she charged us triple the going rate because she could smell desperation the way Wren could smell a customs patrol.

We'd been three days out of the Gray Marches. Fen's shoulder was wrapped and healing, the feral spell's damage knitting under Ilex's salves and my own rather more effective—though quieter—work. The crew had, remarkably, not sold me to the first bounty man we passed after I'd lit up like a festival lantern saving Fen's life. I kept waiting for the other shoe. In my experience shoes were always dropping; I'd built a twenty-year life on dodging them.

"Two rooms," Wren told the innkeeper, "and nobody asks anybody anything."

"That'll be extra," the innkeeper said.

"Of course it will."

Ilex drifted toward the common room's one bookshelf—a water-damaged shelf holding three volumes, two of them cookbooks—with the expression of a scholar entering a tomb. Fen went to drink. Harl went to brood, which was his full-time occupation now, and he'd gotten very good at it. And I stood in the doorway with my pack on my shoulder and felt the pull.

I can't explain it better than that. Magic, real magic, has a texture, and old magic has a *grain*, like wood, like bone. Something in Cinder's Rest was old, and it was calling me the way a loose tooth calls the tongue.

"I'm going to have a look around," I said.

Wren didn't look up from counting coin. "If you get arrested, I'm telling them you're Harl's problem."

"That's cold."

"That's business." But she said it with the corner of her mouth doing the thing it did when it wasn't quite a smile, and I'd learned by now that this was Wren Calloway's version of *be careful*.

* * *

The thing I was pulled toward stood behind the waystation, past the stinking privy trench and the lean-to where a mule regarded me with the deep indifference of its kind. It was a hut. Not a cottage—a hut, made of stacked stone and driftwood and what I genuinely hoped was not bone, though I was lying to myself and we both knew it, because the whole structure had the pale, faintly porous look of something that had once lived inside a large animal.

A sign nailed to the door read: **ORACLE. PAY FIRST. NO REFUNDS. NO CURSES REMOVED THAT YOU YOURSELF EARNED.**

I stood there a long moment, wind pulling at my coat, the mule watching, the hut watching back in a way huts shouldn't.

Every instinct I had—twenty years of them, all polished smooth by use—said *walk away*. Curiosity is how warders find you. Curiosity is how stalls get searched. Curiosity is the first crack in any wall.

But I'd spent the last two weeks watching walls crack, hadn't I. Border wards. Stars. Myself.

I knocked.

"Pay first," said a voice like gravel settling in a sack.

"I haven't even asked a question."

"Doesn't matter. You will. Everyone does. They come up that road with their little faces all pinched, telling themselves they're only curious, and within the hour they're in here asking about love or death or whether their husband's really at sea." A pause. "Pay. First."

I fished out coin. Then, because I'd been a charm-seller for two decades and knew a fellow professional when I heard one, I added the thing I'd been carrying since Corren: a luck-charm, the last of the batch I'd made before the stars went out. Braided horsehair and river-iron, knotted nine times under a dark moon. The kind of thing that would get a woman hanged in Corren and was worth more than the coin I'd just handed over.

The door opened before I could knock again.

She was small. That was my first thought—she was *small*, a bird-boned woman in a robe made of what was definitely, unmistakably, bone: little polished plates strung together like scale mail, clacking softly when she moved. Her face was brown and seamed and ordinary as bread, except for her eyes, which were the color of milk and had no whites to speak of, and which looked at me the way a well looks at a dropped coin.

"Well," she said. "The gate-keeper."

My blood went to ice water. "I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't. You never do. That's the whole trouble with you people." She stepped back to let me in. "Come in, August Vane. Mind the cat."

There was no cat. There was, however, a hearth, a table, two stools, and hanging from every rafter, every nail, every available surface—bones. Chicken bones, ox bones, and others I chose not to inventory. Some were strung on threads. Some were arranged on the table in patterns that hurt my eyes if I looked at them too directly, the way a ward-sigil does when it's live.

"How do you know my name?"

"Your name's the cheapest thing about you." She lowered herself onto a stool with the care of the very old or the very wise, and with her it was hard to tell which. "Sit. You paid, so I'll tell you true, and I'll tell you all of it, because you paid *extra*, and because—" the milk eyes fixed on me, "—because I've been waiting twenty years for you to walk up that road, and I'm tired of waiting."

"Twenty years," I said carefully.

"Give or take. Time's slippery out here since the wards went. Sit *down*, girl."

I sat.

* * *

"I'm going to show you something," she said. "And before I do, you need to understand the rules of bone. Bone doesn't lie, but it doesn't explain, either. It shows what *is*, and what *will be if the wind doesn't change*, and it's up to you to tell the difference. Can you live with that?"

"I've lived with worse."

"I know," she said, and something in her voice made me look at her sharply. "That's rather the point."

She swept the table clean of the old arrangements, took up a handful of bones from a pouch at her belt—small ones, finger-bones, though whose fingers I didn't ask—and cast them.

They didn't scatter. That was the first wrong thing. Bones should scatter. These *arranged*, clicking into place like a lock finding its tumblers, forming a shape on the table: a gate. Two posts, a lintel, and between them a darkness that wasn't the table showing through, because the table was right there, I could see the wood grain around the edges, but inside the gate's outline there was only black, and cold came off it like the door of a winter cellar.

"Touch it," the oracle said.

"Everything in me is screaming no."

"Good. That means it's real. Touch it anyway."

I touched it.

* * *

The world went away.

I stood on the salt plain before the Hollow Gate, and the gate was *open*—not broken, not shattered, *open*, the way a door opens when a hand that owns it pulls it wide. And through it came light. Not the clean light of stars, but a light like a furnace seen through a keyhole, and it poured into the world and into *people*—I could see them, thousands of them, kneeling on the plain, mages and hedge-witches and charm-sellers, everyone who'd ever hidden a spark under a floorboard, and the light went into them like water into cracked earth, and they *glowed*.

And they were bound. I could see the threads. Fine as spider silk, running from each glowing chest, each throat, each mind, back through the gate, and the threads converged—converged on a figure standing before the gate with his hands raised, and I knew him before I saw his face. I knew the set of his shoulders. I'd watched those shoulders for six formative years, waiting for approval.

Voss.

He wasn't gloating. That was the horror of it. He looked *serene*, the way he'd looked when a ward came right, when the lesson was learned, when the world arranged itself into the shape he'd told it to. He was saving them. You could see it in his face. Every mage bound to his gate was, in his arithmetic, a mage *saved*—from the decay, from the scattering, from the slow leak of magic out of the world that he'd preached about in my girlhood until I'd believed it like scripture. *Concentrate it or lose it*, he'd said. *A well or a flood. Choose the well.*

The vision turned, the way visions do, and I was looking at the threads again, and I found mine. Of course I found mine. It was the brightest one, the oldest, because I'd been feeding the gate for twenty years without knowing, my quiet little nightly maintenance, my careful invisible ward-work, all of it siphoning down the channel he'd dug, and my thread ran straight and thick into his hand, and when he closed his fist—

I was on my knees in the hut. The oracle's hand was on my shoulder, and her grip was much stronger than her bird bones suggested.

"There's the first half," she said.

"*First*—"

"Sit up. You paid for all of it."

* * *

She cast again. The bones arranged themselves into the same gate—but this one was *closed*, sealed, wrapped in chains of sigil-light so dense it looked like a knot of white fire, and I knew that knot. I'd tied versions of it every night for twenty years. This was the seal perfected. This was what I'd been building toward my whole careful life: the Hollow Gate locked so completely that nothing passed, ever again, in either direction.

And the gate was *burning* something to stay shut.

I could see it the second time because I knew what to look for—the seal drew its strength from a source, and the source was a small bright thing at the gate's heart, a compressed constellation of memory, and it was being spent, thread by thread, year by year, to feed the binding. Not tortured. *Used*. The way you burn a candle. The way you spend a coin. And the constellation had a shape I knew, a tilt of the head, a gap-toothed grin I'd last seen on a fourteen-year-old boy on a dock, waving at me until the ship took him out of my life and into whatever dark had swallowed him.

"Tomas," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

"The seal needs a soul," the oracle said, into the silence. "A gate that big doesn't shut for free. It never did. The old binding took one too—your brother's, sold by the court beneath Sere, fed to the gate a drop at a time all these years. And if you seal it *forever*—truly forever, the way you've been dreaming of forever since you were a girl—it won't sip anymore. It'll drink. All of him, all at once, and then the seal holds for a thousand years and your brother is a smear of spent light in the dark, and he will have been *aware* the whole time. Bone doesn't lie. I'm sorry. Bone doesn't lie."

The hearth cracked. Somewhere outside, the mule said something dismissive.

"So that's the choice," I said. "Voss opens it and chains every mage alive to it—me included. Or I seal it and burn my brother as fuel. Those are the options."

"Those are the two roads everyone can see," the oracle said. "Yes."

"And you're the kind of woman who puts *NO REFUNDS* on her door and then tells people there's a third road."

The milk eyes crinkled. "I'm the kind of woman who tells people what the bone says. And the bone says something *strange* about you, August Vane. It's said it for twenty years, and I never understood it, and now you've walked up my road and I'm starting to."

She swept the bones up and cast them a third time, and this time they didn't make a gate at all. They made—nothing. A circle. A ring of small bones with a great empty space in the middle, and the empty space wasn't cold. It was just *empty*, open, ordinary, the way a doorway is ordinary when there's no door in it.

"What's that?" I whispered.

"That's the riddle. Bone speaks plain when it can." She tapped the ring of bones. "A gate dies when its keeper stops being one."

I stared at it. "That's not plain. That's the opposite of plain. That's plain's evil twin."

"Bone's not a teacher. Bone's a mirror." She leaned forward, and the bone-plates of her robe clicked like distant dice. "So think, gate-keeper. You've kept your gate for twenty years. Alone. In secret. In *shame*, if I'm any judge, and I am. One woman, one wall, one weight. What happens to a wall when a thousand hands hold it up?"

"It—" I stopped.

I thought about my wards. I thought about the nightly work, the careful solo labor, the whole architecture of *mine*—my stall, my secrets, my gate, my guilt, my brother, my penance. Twenty years of *mine*, and every ounce of it had funneled, without my knowledge, into a tyrant's ritual. Power hoarded is power *channeled*. Ilex had said something like it once, in Dellamere, in that archivist's drone that made even horror sound like a footnote: *gates don't imprison power, they concentrate it.* And I'd heard him, and I hadn't understood that he was describing me.

But a thousand hands. A wall held by a thousand hands isn't a wall anymore. It's a *people*.

"It stops being a wall," I said slowly. "It becomes... the thing the wall was for. Protection. Without the wall."

"And a keeper with no gate to keep—"

"—is just a woman," I said. My throat was tight. "Just a person. With nothing to hide behind."

"Mm." The oracle began gathering her bones, one by one, with the tenderness of a woman putting children to bed. "The riddle's not a spell, you understand. Bone doesn't do spells. It's a *shape*. What you build inside that shape is your own business, and it'll cost you what everything costs—everything you've got."

"That's a terrible price tag."

"It's the only honest kind." She looked up at me, and for a moment the milk eyes weren't eerie at all, just old, just tired, just kind. "You want to know the worst of it, gate-keeper? The vision of the open gate, the one where Voss binds the world? It comes true *if the wind doesn't change*. And the vision of the sealed gate, the one that burns your brother? Same. Both of them are true if you keep walking the roads you can see. The third road—" she shrugged, and her bones rattled, "—the third road has no vision. That's how I know it's real. The bone goes quiet when a thing is truly undecided. It only speaks loudly when the ending's already written."

* * *

I walked back around to the front of the waystation in the blue dark, and the stars were still gone, and the sky was a smooth black lid over the world, and for the first time since they'd vanished I thought: *we can put them back.* Not by sealing. Not by winning. By something stranger—by *unclenching*. By taking the thing I'd held alone for twenty years and breaking it into pieces and handing it out like bread.

I found Wren in the common room, sharpening a knife with the particular aggression she used for thinking. She looked up.

"You've got a face," she said.

"I've always got a face."

"You've got a *new* face. The old one said 'I'm going to die tired.' This one says something I like even less." She set the knife down. "Talk."

"I know how to end this," I said. "And it's going to require me to do the one thing I've spent twenty years making sure I'd never have to do."

"Which is?"

"Trust people." I sat down across from her, and my hands were shaking, and I let them. "All of them. As many as I can find."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked the knife back up.

"Well," she said. "That *is* worse than dying tired."

Outside, far to the north, the black lid of the sky flickered—just once, just at the horizon, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. And every bone-oracle from Cinder's Rest to the coast would have felt it, I think, and every one of them would have gone quiet at the same moment, because the ending had stopped being written.

We had work to do.
