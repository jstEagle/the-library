## 11. The Court Beneath

The servant-tunnels of Mount Sere smelled like a candle that had been lit in a tomb and never put out—wax and old stone and something sweeter underneath that I chose not to name. Ilex led us through the dark with a folded page of their own archive held up like scripture, reading the tunnel map by the light of a glow-worm lantern they'd smuggled out of Dellamere sewn into the lining of a coat.

"Left at the third junction," Ilex murmured. "Then down. Always down. The court builds upward for show and downward for truth."

"Cheerful," Wren said behind me. Her voice was pitched low, but it carried the particular flatness she used when she was cataloguing exits. "How far down are we talking?"

"Half a mile of stair, then the under-halls." Ilex glanced back, and even in the wormlight their eyes had that fae shine that made people in Corren cross the street. "There will be servants' galleries above the court proper. We watch. We learn the rhythm of the place. Then we move."

"And if the rhythm doesn't suit us?" Fen asked. He was walking with his arm still bound from the Gray Marches, and he'd insisted on coming anyway, which told me something about this crew I wasn't sure I wanted to know yet.

"Then we improvise," Ilex said, "and improvisation is how half-fae end up exiled."

I said nothing. Twenty years of saying nothing is a hard habit to break, but lately my silences had been getting shorter, like a tide going out. I kept my hand on the charm-bag at my hip—the one real piece of my old life I'd brought—and counted stairs instead of thinking about Tomas.

Counting didn't work. It never does. By the two hundredth step I was thinking about him anyway: Tomas at nine, teaching himself to whistle through a blade of grass; Tomas at twelve, swearing he'd seen a star fall into the well behind our house; Tomas at fourteen, gone between one dusk and the next, and everyone telling me the border took him, as if the border were a wolf you could blame.

Except the border hadn't taken him. A gate had. And I'd spent twenty years oiling its hinges.

* * *

The stair ended at a door so thin I nearly walked past it. Ilex pressed an ear to the wood, then to the stone beside it, then—because they were Ilex—to the floor, which struck me as theatrical until I realized they were listening for vibration, not sound.

"The gallery runs along the eastern wall," they whispered. "Lattice screen. Old servant architecture—they built the court to be watched by the people who served it. A warning, maybe. Or a habit."

We filed through into a gallery that overlooked everything.

I have sold charms in Corren for twenty years. I have seen desperate people spend bread-money on knotted thread because hope was cheaper than medicine. I thought I understood what want looked like.

I did not understand anything.

The hall beneath us was carved from the mountain's own black bone and then gilded over it, every vein of stone traced in gold leaf until the rock looked embarrassed to be itself. Chandeliers hung from ceilings too high to see clearly, burning with a light that cast no shadows—or rather, cast them wrong, pooling where no bodies stood. Fae nobility moved across the floor in slow constellations of silk and bone-white skin, and around each of them drifted small lights, fist-sized, warm-colored, that bobbed and circled like tame birds.

Soul-lights. I knew what they were before Ilex's breath caught beside me and confirmed it. Compressed souls, decanted from their owners, worn as ornaments or spent as coin. One noble flicked a finger and her light drifted to another's hand in exchange for a slim black box, and the transaction had all the ceremony of someone passing a salt cellar.

"That one," Fen breathed, pointing. His voice cracked on the second word.

A light near the far wall, dimmer than the others, grayish at the center like a coal going out. It hung listless, barely circling, and when I looked at it something in my chest turned over and lay still.

Not because I recognized it. Because I recognized the *tiredness* in it. Twenty years of keeping a gate alone looks like that from the outside, I suppose—a light burning low and pretending it's a choice.

"Tomas," I said. Not loudly. Just to make the word exist in a place like this, where names were probably currency too.

"Not so fast," Wren murmured. She'd gone very still, the way she went still before boarding a ship she suspected was rigged. "That's a vault-light, or I'm a fishmonger. See the cage-work around it? The others float free. That one's anchored."

She was right. Around the gray light, almost invisible against the gold, ran a fine mesh of silver wire shaped like a birdcage the size of a fist. Anchored to a chain that descended from the chandelier above—and the chain, I saw with a lurch, ran up through the ceiling, deeper into the mountain. Toward the gate-side of things. Toward whatever Voss was building.

"They're keeping him charged," Ilex whispered. "A soul used as a key has to stay resonant. They feed it, water it, keep it fresh. Like livestock."

"Like a battery," I said, and hated how steady my voice came out. Somewhere in the last few weeks I'd learned to do that—put grief in a box and speak around it. Voss would have approved. That thought made me want to break something.

Below us, a herald made an announcement in a language that predated the language it was written in, and the court began to drift toward a dais where something large and veiled was being unveiled. Distraction. Movement. Every eye in the gilded dark turning toward spectacle.

"Now," Wren said.

* * *

Getting down was the easy part. There was a service stair behind the lattice, built for people whose job was to be unseen, and twenty years of being unseen had given me a certain professional respect for whoever had designed it. Wren went first with a knife in her sleeve. Fen followed. Ilex moved like smoke remembering it used to be solid. I came last, because I was the one who could feel wards, and feeling wards meant walking point whether anyone had ordered it or not.

The court's magic was everywhere and nowhere—ambient, diffuse, like heat off a forge. It didn't snag on us. Small mercies. But beneath the ambient hum, threaded through the floor, there were lines. Real lines. Old ones, laid before the court existed, running toward the mountain's heart.

Gate-lines. I knew their handwriting. I'd been tracing their cousin for twenty years in the dark outside Corren.

"This way," I breathed, and for once nobody argued, because I was the only one who could hear the mountain's grammar.

The vault was not marked. Of course it wasn't. It was marked by absence—a stretch of corridor where the ambient hum simply stopped, the way conversation stops when you walk into a room where people were just talking about you. A door of plain iron, unadorned, in a palace of gold. The most honest thing in the building.

Wren examined the lock, then the hinges, then the wall around it, and shook her head. "No mechanism. It's warded shut."

"I can open it," I said. "Give me a minute."

Here is what I did not say: I could open it because the ward was keyed to bloodline. I felt it the moment my fingers touched the iron—not a barrier but a question, the ward reaching into me the way a dog reaches into your pocket for a treat, checking scent, checking shape. Vane blood opened this door. Which meant Voss had keyed it to *us*. Which meant he had always known I might come. Or worse—he had never thought about me at all, and my family name was simply part of the lock's architecture, the way a keyhole is part of a door.

I couldn't decide which possibility was more insulting.

"August." Wren's hand closed on my shoulder. "You've gone white."

"It's keyed to my family," I said. "It knows me."

Silence behind me. Then Ilex, softly, with the terrible calm of an archivist watching a footnote become a chapter: "Then opening it announces you. Blood-keyed wards log their openings. Someone will know a Vane walked through this door."

"Someone already knows a Vane walks through every door in this story," I said. "Voss built the lock."

I opened it.

The ward parted around my hand like water around a stone, and for one heartbeat—one clean, silent heartbeat—I let myself believe we might actually do this. The door swung inward. Cold air spilled out, smelling of iron and snow and something older than both. Inside: a small round chamber, shelves of vessels, and there—third shelf, wrapped in gray silk, caged in that same fine silver mesh—a light the color of a coal going out.

Tomas.

He was smaller than I remembered. Souls compress, Ilex had told us, the way stars do, growing denser and stranger as they're squeezed. What hung in that cage wasn't my brother. It was everything my brother had ever been, folded down to a fist-sized ember, and I reached for it with both hands and no plan at all—

The ward snapped shut around my wrist.

Not the door-ward. A second one, nested inside, finer than the first, and it didn't check my blood. It *read* it. I felt it move through me like a cold finger down a ledger, line by line, and somewhere far above us—far above the gilded hall, far above the mountain's skin—a bell that was not a bell began to ring.

"What did you touch?" Wren was already moving, knife out, backing toward the door.

"Myself," I said. "Apparently I'm the alarm."

The soul-light flared. Just once—a pulse of gray-white, like a heartbeat, like recognition. And though I know what I know about compressed souls, though Ilex could write three treatises on why what happened next was impossible, I swear on my mother's grave that the light turned toward me. That it leaned, the way a person leans toward a door they recognize.

August? Something said, in a voice I hadn't heard in twenty years, thin as paper, stretched across the distance between fourteen and forever.

My knees nearly went. Wren caught my elbow and hauled me upright, and her face was very close and very fierce and entirely without pity, which was exactly what I needed.

"Later," she said. "Grieve later. Run now."

Because the ringing wasn't stopping. It was spreading—down through the vault walls, out through the gate-lines I'd felt in the floor, and along those lines came answering vibrations, dozens of them, waking up one after another like lamps being lit down a long road. The whole mountain was becoming a bell, and we were standing inside it.

Fen appeared in the doorway, breathing hard. "Gallery's emptying. Everyone's moving—soldiers, I think, or whatever passes for soldiers here. Ilex says there's a secondary route but it goes through the feeding halls."

"The feeding halls," Wren repeated flatly.

"Where they feed the lights," Ilex said from behind him, pale as their own archives. "It's the fastest way to the servant tunnels. It is also where the court keeps the things that do the feeding."

Above us, the chandelier-chain shivered. Somewhere in the gilded dark, a veiled thing on a dais finished being unveiled, and the applause, when it came, sounded like rain on a coffin lid.

I looked at the cage. At the ember inside it that had once whistled through a blade of grass and sworn a star fell into our well.

"Hold on," I told it—told him. "I'm coming back. I don't care what this mountain costs."

Then I ran, because the alternative was standing still while everything I'd spent twenty years protecting came apart around me—which, I was beginning to suspect, had been my family's signature move for a generation.

Behind us, the vault door sealed itself with a sound like a book closing.

Ahead, the feeding halls waited, full of teeth.

And somewhere overhead, patient as arithmetic, something that had been expecting a Vane finally got one.
