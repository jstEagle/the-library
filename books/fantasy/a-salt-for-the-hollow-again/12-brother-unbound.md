## 12. Brother, Unbound

The ward keyed to my bloodline was still screaming.

Not a sound, exactly—wards don't scream in any way ears can hear—but a pressure behind my eyes, a taste like copper and burnt sugar coating the back of my throat. Somewhere above us, in the gilded dark of the court beneath Mount Sere, bells I couldn't hear were ringing. Servants with too many joints were running. And somewhere in that machinery of alarm, someone very old and very rich had just been told that a thief with Vane blood was standing in the soul-vault.

I had minutes. Maybe fewer.

The vault itself was smaller than I'd expected. After the obscene grandeur of the court above—the chandeliers made of frozen laughter, the nobles wearing their centuries like jewelry—I'd imagined something vast. Instead: a low room of black stone, cold enough that my breath came out as frost, and rows upon rows of niches cut into the walls, each one holding a vessel.

Vessels is a generous word. Some were jars of blown glass, pretty as perfume bottles. Some were lanterns. One, near the door, was a child's shoe, and I decided very firmly not to think about what was inside it or who had worn it. The fae didn't store souls the way a miser stores coin. They stored them the way a collector stores butterflies—pinned, labeled, arranged for beauty.

Ilex's archive notes had given me a cataloguing system. Half-fae archivists, it turns out, are the only people alive who can read the court's inventory script, and Ilex had copied three pages of it into my notebook in a hand so precise it looked printed. *Vessels of recent acquisition, northern provenance, bound under the Sere compact.* Third row from the floor, fourth niche from the left, if the court hadn't reorganized since the ledger was written.

Third row. Fourth niche.

There it was: a small glass sphere, no bigger than an apple, sitting on a cushion of grey velvet like an egg in a nest. Inside it, light moved. Not glowing—*moving*, swirling slow and dense, the way snow moves inside a globe, except this wasn't snow. It was too structured. Too deliberate. Patterns folding into patterns, spirals nested inside spirals, and every few seconds the whole thing would pulse, gently, like something breathing in its sleep.

Twenty years of keeping the Hollow Gate had taught me to read essence the way other people read faces. I stood there with frost on my lips and read what was in that glass, and my knees nearly went.

It was Tomas.

Not the boy. I want to be honest about that, because honesty was the one thing I never gave him. Not the boy who used to catch frogs at the mill pond and put them down my collar. Not the boy who cried when our mother sold his wooden horse to buy bread and then forgave her by supper. What remained of my brother after twenty years of being drained, spoonful by spoonful, to feed a gate's binding—that was something else. A compressed constellation. Every memory he'd ever had, folded down and down and down into a space the size of a fist, packed so tight that the memories had begun to orbit each other. I could see them turning in there. Our mother's kitchen. The mill pond. A dog he'd loved named Bristle. The last thing he ever saw, which I had spent two decades not letting myself remember: the road north, and me, fourteen years old and terrified, promising I'd come back for him.

I hadn't come back. I'd stayed. I'd built a stall and a life and a wall of careful invisibility, and I'd told myself that keeping the gate sealed *was* coming back for him, somehow, eventually, in the way that cowards use the word *eventually*.

"Hello, Tom," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, which was fitting, because everything else was cracking too.

The light in the sphere pulsed. Faster, now. The spirals tightened, and I felt—there's no polite way to say this—I felt him *notice* me. Twenty years compressed into a fist-sized prison, and the first thing that constellation did upon registering my presence was reach. I felt it like a hook behind my sternum, gentle but insistent, a question asked in the oldest language there is: *you came.*

"I came," I said. "I'm late."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about souls: they're not quiet. Living ones are noisy, constant, a background hum you stop hearing the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But a stored one—a *kept* one—is silent, and the silence is worse. It's the silence of a held breath. Of a word swallowed. Of a person waiting twenty years for someone to open the door.

I knelt on the black stone, heedless of the cold seeping through my trousers, and set my palms against the glass. Ward-craft hummed up through my fingers unbidden, old habit, old training—Voss's voice in my head, young and sure: *A keeper reads before she touches, August. Reading is respect.* For once, the doctrine was useful. I read.

The vessel's binding was elegant. That's the word that kept rising, ugly and unwelcome: *elegant*. Whoever had crafted it—and I knew the hand, I'd known it since chapter one of my own life, since the man who taught me everything had signed his work the way he signed everything, in flourishes disguised as function—had woven the containment spell so fine that the soul inside could barely feel the walls. Tomas's constellation drifted in that glass believing itself free, or near enough to free that the difference didn't hurt. That was the cruelty of it. That was the *artistry*. Malachai Voss had built a cage so comfortable his prisoner might thank him for it.

And underneath the elegance, threaded through it like wire through wax: the drain. A hair-fine siphon running out of the sphere, down through the niche, into the stone, into the mountain's bones, toward the Hollow Gate. Toward the binding I had tended for twenty years like a garden, never once asking where the water came from.

My own wards had drunk from my brother. Every night for twenty years, I had knelt at the gate and poured my power into maintaining the seal, and the seal had quietly, efficiently, drawn the balance from the nearest source of bound essence. From him. My vigilance had been his consumption. My invisibility had been his appetite.

I'm going to be honest again, since I've started: in that moment, kneeling on the freezing stone with my brother's soul humming against my palms, I understood something that broke me more cleanly than grief ever could. Grief says *you lost something*. This said *you were the loss*. I hadn't failed to save Tomas. I had been the instrument of his spending, all these years, one careful invisible night at a time.

The light in the sphere pulsed again, softer. Reaching.

"Don't," I whispered. "Don't forgive me yet. Let me get you out first."

* * *

Extraction, per Ilex's notes, should have been simple. Break the vessel's binding, cradle the freed essence, carry it out in a warded lantern, done. Souls want to be free the way rivers want the sea; you mostly just have to stop holding them.

But Ilex's notes had been written by an archivist, and archivists describe systems, not sabotage. What the notes didn't say—what I only understood as I sank my craft into the vessel's binding and began, carefully, to unpick it—was that the drain didn't run one way.

It ran both.

The siphon feeding the Hollow Gate wasn't just drawing essence out of Tomas. It was *anchoring* him. Twenty years of continuous draw had grown roots. His constellation had grown around the pull the way a tree grows around a fence wire—not because it wanted to, but because that was the shape of the world it lived in. The binding and the soul had fused. He wasn't in the cage anymore.

He was the cage.

I sat back on my heels, hands shaking, and did the arithmetic every mage dreads. To extract him cleanly, I'd need to sever the siphon without shattering the fusion—which meant weeks of work, a fully equipped workshop, and probably Ilex, who could read the fusion's grammar in a way I never would. To extract him fast—to just *rip* him free, here, now, with alarms singing and soldiers coming—I'd have to break the vessel outright. And breaking the vessel while the siphon ran would do exactly what breaking a dam does to a valley. The sudden release of twenty years of pressurized essence would blow back through the drain line, up through the mountain, into—

Into the vault's structural wards. Into the court above. Into, if the resonance carried, the Hollow Gate itself, four hundred miles south, which was currently held together by nothing but my twenty years of maintenance and a prayer.

Collapse the vault. Possibly collapse the court. Very possibly crack the gate wide open ahead of schedule, handing Voss his reopening as a gift wrapped in my own desperation.

Or leave him. Walk away. Come back later, better prepared, with time and tools and help—and let him sit in his comfortable cage another week, another month, another year, while Voss's ritual crept closer to needing him as a key.

The alarms were getting louder. Or I was getting more afraid. Hard to tell the difference sometimes.

"Tom," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because nothing else was. "I found you. Do you understand? I found you, and I can't—" My throat closed. I pressed my forehead against the glass, cold enough to burn. "I can't take you home yet."

The constellation turned. Slowly. All those folded memories—kitchen, pond, dog, road—reorienting themselves around some center I couldn't see, until I realized the center was *me*. He was looking at me. However a soul looks, however a brother looks after twenty years of being a currency, he was looking at me, and what came off the glass in a wave so gentle it was almost patronizing was not anger.

It was recognition. And then, unmistakably, deliberately: forgiveness.

*You came,* the pulse said again. Slower now. Calmer. *That's the whole of it. You came.*

"That's not the whole of it," I said fiercely, and frost fell from my lashes. "That is nowhere near the whole of it. I left you on that road. I chose the gate over you for twenty years. I fed you to it without knowing and I *kept* it without asking, because asking would have meant knowing, and knowing would have meant—"

*August.*

My name. Not in words—he had no words left, words were among the first things they'd drained—but in the shape of my name, rendered in light and pulse, the way our mother used to call us in from the dark. I stopped talking. It seemed respectful.

*I know what you kept,* the pulses said, unfolding one memory at a time like a hand opening. *I've felt your work every night for twenty years. Your wards are the only warm thing in here.*

Which was, I want it noted, the single most devastating sentence anyone has ever said to me, and he said it without a mouth.

"You felt that?" I managed. "The maintenance?"

*Every night. Like weather. Like someone walking the fence line of a field I'm planted in.* A pause. The spirals turned. *I used to pretend it was you.*

"It was me."

*I know.* And then, so gently I nearly missed it: *I'm glad it was you.*

I put my arms around the niche and the sphere and the cold stone and held all of it, the way you hold a sleeping child, the way you hold anything you're about to lose, and I cried the way I hadn't cried since I was fourteen—ugly, graceless, alone in a vault full of other people's kept breath. Above us the alarms sang. Around us a hundred vessels held their hundred silences. And in my arms, my brother forgave me for the crime of surviving him.

When I finally pulled back, my face was wet and frozen and I'd made a decision, though I wouldn't know it was a decision until later. "I'm coming back," I told him. "Not eventually. Soon. With help. We're going to do this properly, and then I'm going to introduce you to the worst crew of smugglers on the salt coast, and Wren is going to pretend she doesn't like you, and Fen is going to teach you dice, and you are going to *hate* how much you love them."

The constellation pulsed. It might have been laughter. I choose to believe it was laughter.

* * *

They found me there, of course. I'd spent too long. Grief is a luxury priced in minutes, and I'd paid with all of mine.

I heard them before I saw them—the particular cadence of the court's guard, which is to say no footsteps at all, just a wrongness in the air, a pressure change, the sense of the room becoming *attended*. I was on my feet with a ward-shield up before conscious thought caught up, my back to the niche, my brother at my spine.

Three of them filled the doorway. Court-guard aren't soldiers, exactly. They're more like the idea of soldiers, worn by things that had agreed to look humanoid for the shift. Their armor was porcelain-white and seamless. Their faces were smooth where faces go. And between them, drifting slightly above the floor the way wealth drifts, came a court steward in robes the color of deep water, holding a ledger.

"Vane-blood," the steward said. Its voice was pleasant the way a trapdoor is pleasant. "The vault thanks you for your visit. The court regrets that the exit toll has... increased."

"I don't have anything you want," I said, which was a lie so large it deserved its own niche.

"Everything," the steward said, "is negotiable. That is rather the point of a court." It opened the ledger. "Though I confess we rarely get shoppers for row three."

Behind me, Tomas's light flared—fear, real fear, the first sharp emotion he'd shown—and I raised my shield higher and started doing the math on fighting my way out of a soul-vault, which is the kind of math that always comes out to zero.

Then the doorway did something doorways shouldn't.

It detonated inward.

Not fire—Wren doesn't believe in fire when precision will serve. The doorframe simply came apart along seams that had always been there, cut by a blade that had been warded, I'd learn later, with a charm I'd sold her for a luck-token and half a bottle of bad brandy. Through the wreckage stepped Wren Calloway, captain of the *Kestrel*, salt-smuggler, liar, and—at least for the duration of this particular disaster—my cavalry.

She looked terrible. Blood on her temple, a tear in her coat that suggested something with claws had opinions about her, and the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she resented. Behind her, filling the corridor with noise and smoke and what I sincerely hoped was a diversionary charm going off on schedule, came the sounds of the rest of the crew making a nuisance of themselves at maximum volume.

"Warden," she said—it was what the crew called me, half joke, half insurance—"you're late for your own funeral. Move."

"The vessel can't come with me," I said, fast, backing toward her with the sphere still pulsing behind me. "The binding's fused. If I break it here, the backlash goes up the siphon and cracks the gate. Maybe the whole court."

"Then we take the *vessel*," Wren said, like it was obvious, like it was nothing, like carrying a caged soul through an alarmed fae court was a Tuesday.

"If they catch us with it—"

"They won't." She grinned, and it was the grin of a woman who had already decided the odds were acceptable, which is either courage or brain damage, and with Wren it was usually both. "Fen's collapsing the tunnel entrance behind us. Ilex left a map. And Harl—" something flickered across her face, too quick to name, "—Harl's holding the boat end. So pick up your brother, Warden, and let's rob these bastards properly."

"I can't carry him and fight."

"You won't have to." She tossed me something underhand: a padded courier's satchel, lined with wool and, I realized as I caught it, with a lattice of cheap, ugly, *brilliant* smuggling wards—the kind Wren's crew used to move contraband past checkpoints. Shielded. Silent. Invisible to anything that wasn't looking for cheap, ugly, brilliant smuggling craft. "Cargo is cargo," Wren said. "Pack him."

For one absurd moment I almost laughed. Twenty years of high ward-craft, of elegant bindings and careful doctrine, and what saved my brother was smuggler's packing cloth.

I lifted the sphere from its velvet nest. Tomas's light surged against my palms—*now? now?*—and I hated myself with a completeness that felt almost holy as I settled him into the satchel instead of freedom.

"Soon," I whispered to the wool. "I meant it. Soon."

The light dimmed to a patient ember. Trusting me. *Forgiving* me. Which was infinitely worse than anger, and infinitely better, and I buckled the satchel across my chest like a heartbeat I'd finally agreed to carry.

The steward watched all of this with the serene entitlement of a creature that had never once been told no. "The court," it began, "will require—"

"Yeah," Wren said, and threw a charm I recognized—one of mine, bought fair, deployed better—straight into its ledger.

The resulting silence was total, absolute, and extremely temporary. In the ringing blank where the steward's next sentence had been, Wren grabbed my arm.

"Run now," she advised, "gloat later."

So we ran—through the wrecked door, down Ilex's servant-tunnels, past Fen swearing beautifully at a collapsing barricade, with alarms singing and the whole gilded horror of Mount Sere waking up above us and my brother's soul knocking gently against my ribs with every stride, saying, in the only language he had left:

*You came. You came. You came.*

Behind us, far below the mountain, something old heard the siphon stutter for the first time in twenty years—and began, very slowly, to wake.
