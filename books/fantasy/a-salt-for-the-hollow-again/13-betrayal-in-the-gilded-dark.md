## 13. Betrayal in the Gilded Dark

There are three rules to running a crew through hostile country: know your exits, count your people twice, and never trust anyone who's recently started being polite.

Harl had been polite for two days.

Not friendly—Harl was always friendly in the way of a man who'd learned that charm was cheaper than fighting—but *polite*. Asking after my sleep. Offering to take second watch. Complimenting the way I'd rigged the rope anchors down into the servant-tunnels like I hadn't watched him do it a hundred times himself and better.

I noticed. I told myself it was nerves. We were under a mountain full of gilded cannibals who bought souls by the ounce; everyone's manners were bound to go strange.

That's what I told myself. I want that on the record. Because later—when August asked me how long I'd known—I said "I didn't," and mostly that was even true, and mostly wasn't enough.

* * *

The court beneath Mount Sere smelled like honey and copper. That's the detail that stayed with me afterward, more than the vaults or the bone-chandeliers or the fae lords draped across their thrones like expensive rot. Honey and copper. Sweetness over blood. Somebody had built an entire civilization on the principle that if you gild the cage, nobody counts the bars.

We moved through the servant-tunnels Ilex had pulled from three centuries of archived floor-plans, and I'll say this for the half-fae: they navigated those passages like they'd been born in them, muttering annotations—"here the wall was rebuilt after the Sundering, so the mortar's newer than it looks"—in that dreamy lecturing voice that made Fen want to strangle them and made me, against every instinct, want to keep them.

August walked at the center of us with her hood back and her jaw set, and she looked like a woman walking toward her own funeral and intending to argue with the priest about the eulogy.

"Vessel chamber's past the treasury gallery," Ilex whispered. "Two turns east, then the stair down. The wards around the vault itself are keyed to—" they glanced sideways at August "—well. To you."

"To my bloodline."

"To Vane. Specifically." Ilex smiled apologetically. "The archivists before me catalogued the binding contracts. Your family name appears rather often. You were, ah, thorough."

"My family was *thorough*," August said flatly. "I was quiet."

"Quiet is a kind of thorough."

I put a hand up and the column stopped. Ahead, the tunnel opened onto the gallery—a long hall lit by lamps that burned without flame, all gold leaf and glass cases, and beyond it the double doors of the vault stair. Two guards. Tall, silver-skinned, bored. Bored guards worry me more than alert ones; alert ones can be distracted, but bored ones have already decided nothing here is worth dying over, which means they fight like men with nothing to prove, which means they don't stop.

"Right," I murmured. "Fen, left pillar. Harl, right. Ilex, you're our distraction—can you do whatever it is you do?"

"I can be extremely annoying," Ilex said. "It's a documented skill."

"It's a gift," Fen muttered.

We went.

Here's the thing about the plan: it worked. For about four minutes, it worked beautifully. Ilex strolled into that gallery like a tax collector, announced themselves as a courier from the Dellamere Archive bearing documents for the Lord of Coin, and was so insufferably pedantic about their forged credentials that both guards leaned in to squint at the seals—and Fen and Harl dropped them soft and quiet behind the pillars, and we crossed the gallery floor with our boots silent on marble worth more than the Kestrel, and I remember thinking: *this is going too well.*

Rule four of running a crew: when it goes too well, count your people again.

I counted four ahead of me. August, Ilex, Fen, Harl.

Should have been five. I was five.

I did the arithmetic wrong, then, standing there in a hall of stolen gold. What I should have counted was not bodies but *directions*. Who was watching the doors we'd come through. Who'd been watching them for the last hour while the rest of us studied the vault.

Harl had been watching them. Harl had insisted on rear guard. And Harl, polite Harl, generous Harl, had spent his watch doing exactly what a man does when he has sold something he cannot bear to look at: he kept his eyes on the merchandise.

* * *

The vault opened like a mouth.

Stairs down into cold, and then a round chamber walled in niches—hundreds of them, thousands, each holding a vessel of glass or iron or carved bone, each vessel holding a light. Not firelight. Soul-light. Some burned steady as candles. Some guttered. One near the door flickered in a rhythm I recognized before I understood why, and then I understood why, because it flickered the way lanterns do aboard a ship, in time with a swell that isn't there, and I thought: *that one used to live on water.*

Gods. They were all somebody. That was the whole horror of the place. Not the gilding upstairs, not the lords in their finery—just this. A library where every book screamed.

"Tomas." August's voice came out cracked down the middle. She'd stopped in front of a niche halfway around the chamber, and the vessel there was small—clay, unglazed, the kind of jar a farmwife keeps salt in—and inside it burned a light like a green-gold star having a nightmare.

She reached for it.

The ward caught her at arm's length like a wall of frozen air, and the whole room woke up. Light rippled outward through every niche, vessel to vessel, a shiver passing through a thousand sleeping souls, and somewhere above us a bell began to ring—one clean note, then another, patient as arithmetic.

"That's keyed to her name," Ilex said, very calm, very fast, which is how they sounded when things were worst. "Blood-ward. It will keep ringing until someone with authority comes to look."

"How long?" Fen had his knife out, facing the stairs.

"For a lord with a short staircase? Minutes."

"Then we take it and run," August said. Her hands were shaking but her voice wasn't, and I've seen brave women on my decks for twenty years and I knew the difference between brave and decided, and August Vane had passed clean through brave into decided somewhere on those stairs. "Help me break the ward."

"We can't break it," Ilex said. "But we might be able to *argue* with it. It's a contract-ward—it wants terms. Give me your hand."

And that's where I should have been watching the door.

* * *

They came down the stair in silence first—that was the tell, later, the thing I turned over a hundred times. Soldiers who mean to kill you come down loud, shouting, giving you the chance to surrender because corpses make paperwork. These came down quiet as tide, and I heard them only because Fen heard them, and Fen shouted "CONTACT" in a voice that cracked the chamber's hush like ice going out.

Then it was all noise.

Silver-skinned guards poured onto the stairs—not the bored pair from the gallery, these were house troops, armored in lacquered scale with curved blades, and behind them came something worse, a tall figure robed in ledger-gray with a face like folded paper, one of the court's *auditors*, and its voice when it spoke didn't so much sound as file itself into your skull:

"*Inventory is not permitted.*"

"Invoice *this*," Fen snarled, and threw a lamp.

I got August behind a buttress as blades filled the chamber. Ilex was chanting—actually chanting, hands blurring through contract-marks in the air, unpicking the blood-ward syllable by syllable while August fed it her own palm-blood because apparently that was the price of arguing with your own name—and I held the doorway with Fen, back to back, doing the oldest mathematics there is: how many, how fast, how long can we make minutes behave like hours.

Harl fought beside me.

That's the part I keep snagging on. He fought *well*. He killed two of them with the flat economy of a man who's done it since before I met him, and he shouted the right warnings, and when a blade got past me he took the cut across his forearm meant for my spine. Whatever deal he'd cut, whatever coin had changed hands—he was still, in that moment, bleeding for us, and I want you to understand that I don't think Harl ever once saw those two things as contradictions. That's what betrayal actually looks like, up close. It doesn't look like a knife in the dark. It looks like a man helping you carry the load, right up until the exact moment the load becomes someone else's problem.

"The ward's loosening!" Ilex called. "Thirty seconds—Wren, when it breaks, the whole vault will scream. We need to be moving!"

"Almost—" August had both hands pressed to the vessel now, blood-slick, and the clay jar was glowing like a forge heart, and inside it the green-gold light was *pressing outward*, pushing against the glass like a bird testing a window. "Tomas. Tomas, I'm here. It's August. It's—"

The light flared.

And the bell above us stopped ringing, and the silence that replaced it was worse, because it was the silence of something that no longer needed to announce us.

"*There you are,*" said a voice from the top of the stairs, warm and reasonable and rotten to the root, and I knew it from wanted-posters and dockside whispers and August's nightmares: Malachai Voss had come down personally to check his inventory. "*Keeper Vane. Twenty years, and you finally visit the office.*"

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I've replayed it slow so many times the slow version feels more honest, so let me give you the slow version.

Fen went down. A blade took him across the thigh and he dropped behind the buttress, alive, cursing, gripping his leg. That left me alone on the line.

Ilex broke the ward.

I heard it go—a sound like a sigh, like a lock giving up—and August tore the vessel free of the niche and the whole vault convulsed, every soul-light flaring at once, and half the guards staggered as if the floor had tilted. August clutched that little salt-jar to her chest with both arms, and the light inside it was thrashing, and she was saying his name, just his name, over and over, like a rope thrown into dark water.

"Exit," I barked. "Everyone to the tunnels, NOW—"

"Wren." Ilex had gone very still. Their eyes weren't on the exit. They were on the auditor—the paper-faced thing—which had produced, from the folds of its robe, a net of fine chain that gleamed with ward-script, and behind it, four more guards had sealed the upper gallery. I could hear them. Boots on marble, closing the doors we'd come through.

"They're cutting off the gallery," Ilex said. Conversationally. The way they reported everything, including their own death sentences, apparently. "If someone doesn't hold the stair, they'll close the ring and none of you leave. Someone has to stay and keep the stair open." They adjusted their spectacles. "Someone whose magic is mostly archival, and therefore nearly useless for carrying a soul-vessel through a running battle, and therefore—"

"No," I said.

"—there's really only one candidate—"

"*No.*"

"Wren." Ilex smiled at me. Half-fae smiles are a language unto themselves, and theirs said: *don't make this heavy.* "You have cargo that matters more than crew. That's not cruelty. That's seamanship. Even you know the order of operations."

"I don't abandon crew."

"You're not abandoning crew. You're choosing which crew member gets to matter most efficiently." They were already turning toward the stair, already pulling sigils out of the air like a street magician, laying down wards in a lattice across the steps. "Besides, I've read eleven thousand books. Statistically, I'm due to survive something."

The auditor raised its net.

I had maybe three seconds to choose, and I want to be precise about what the choice was, because afterward—after Dellamere, after everything—people talked about it like it was simple. It was not simple. Here is what was simple: August had the soul. August was the mission. If August fell, Tomas's soul went back in its niche and twenty years of grief and guilt and every mile of gray marsh became a joke the world told at our expense.

Here is what was not simple: Ilex, standing on a stair, buying our lives with their body, looking at me with those calm archive-keeper's eyes and *knowing* I would leave, having done the math themselves and handed me the answer, and the worst part—the part that sits in my chest like ballast even now—is that they were right, and they knew they were right, and they made it easy for me anyway, because that's what love looks like when it's wearing a librarian's patience.

"GO," Ilex said, and threw the lattice wide, and the stair lit up blue-white, and the first guard who touched it came apart at the seams like a bad knot.

I grabbed August by the collar and ran.

* * *

A running battle through those tunnels is a thing I could describe for an hour and you'd still never feel it, so I'll give you the pieces that stuck.

The soul-vessel glowed through August's coat like a coal in a pocket, lighting our way and marking us for every blade in the dark, and there was nothing to be done about it—she wouldn't cover it, wouldn't smother it, wouldn't do anything but hold it and talk to it in a low urgent voice, telling it about the farm, about the creek behind the house, about the dog whose name she'd almost forgotten and then remembered and laughed and cried at in the same breath, all while sprinting, all while parrying, because yes, she fought, she fought like someone who'd had twenty years to rehearse a war she'd sworn never to attend.

Fen limped between us, arm over my shoulder, leaving a blood-trail I hated because trails get people found.

And behind us—behind the blue flare of Ilex's last wards—we could hear it not ending. Fighting. Shouting. Then not-shouting. Then a sound I will not describe except to say it was brief, and bright, and final, and it was followed by the auditor's filed-glass voice pronouncing something in the cadence of a receipt being issued.

August stumbled. I hauled her up. Neither of us said Ilex's name, because saying it would have made it a thing that had already happened instead of a thing we were refusing to have happen yet, and I am very good at that particular refusal. I've been practicing it my whole life. It's why I run a crew instead of a household. Crews you can refuse to lose.

We hit the gallery. The double doors were barred—from outside.

For one heartbeat I stood there with my crew bleeding and my archivist dead-or-worse and my mage glowing like a signal fire, and I thought, with perfect clarity: *Harl.*

Because the bar was new. Because the guards on the far side of the doors weren't surprised by us—they were waiting, arranged, patient. Because Harl had taken rear guard and lost us in the first turn and been *found*, gently, by people who knew exactly where to find him, because someone had told them where we'd be, and there was exactly one someone who'd known.

I didn't rage. Rage takes time. I put my shoulder to the door hinge-side, where the bar has least leverage, and I hit it like the sea hits a seawall, twice, three times, and the wood gave, and we went through into the arms of six guards, and that's when Harl stepped out of the shadows of the gallery with his hands up and his sister's freedom-price presumably signed somewhere in triplicate, and he looked at me, and he said—

"I'm sorry, Captain. She's twelve. They had her in a cell downstairs. What was I supposed to—"

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare make it small."

And then the lights went out.

All of them—the flameless lamps, the gold glow, the vessels' soul-light bleeding through the walls—all at once, plunged into absolute black, and in the black I heard August breathe one word, not to me, not to any of us:

"*Tomas.*"

The vessel in her arms was blazing like a captive dawn, and every guard in that gallery turned toward it the way flowers turn toward sun, and I did the only thing captains are for.

"RUN."

* * *

We ran. Through the servant-tunnels, up the old stairs, out through the cistern gate into air that tasted like snow and stone, and behind us Mount Sere woke up proper—bells everywhere now, horns, the mountain howling its outrage down its own throat. We lost them in the scree fields by dark. We lost Fen's blood-loss by less. We made the tree line with the vessel burning and Harl stumbling behind us under guard because Fen had tied his wrists with rigging-line and I hadn't told him not to, and I still haven't decided whether that makes Fen crueler than me or just more honest.

Camp, such as it was: a hollow under a rock shelf, one cold campfire, four people breathing hard and one man tied to a tree trunk.

August sat apart with the vessel in her lap. The light inside had gone quiet—exhausted, I'd have said, if I believed souls could tire. Maybe they can. Maybe that's what twenty years in a jar does. She was talking to it still, low, endless, and I didn't go to her, because some vigils you keep alone even when someone's sitting right beside them.

Fen slumped against me, leg wrapped, grey-faced, alive. "Ilex," he said. Just that. Asking.

"Getting out," I said. "That's an order. Ilex is getting out."

Fen looked at me with the terrible clear eyes of a hurt young man who loves his captain, and he didn't believe me either, and neither of us said so.

And that's when August stood up. The vessel cradled against her chest, her face wet and set, and she looked at Harl tied to the tree, and then at me, and she said, in a voice scraped raw:

"He traded a boy's sister for a crew. And the court took the payment and sprang the trap anyway—or they'd have sprung it regardless, and he was the bait that made sure we walked in slow." She swallowed. "Wren. His sister's still down there. Twelve years old, in a cell beneath a mountain that buys children by the pound."

I knew what she was going to say before she said it. I knew because it was what I'd have said, once, before tonight, before the stair and the blue flare and the sound like a receipt being issued. And I sat there with Ilex's absence sitting on my chest like ballast, and I heard myself answer:

"I know. Gods help me, I know. First light, we go back in."

Because that's the truth I was left holding in the dark of that hillside, the truth that cost me an archivist to learn: loyalty isn't choosing who matters. Loyalty is refusing to finish the arithmetic. I'd chosen August over Ilex and I'd do it again and I'd hate myself cleaner each time, and the only penance worth the name was to make sure the ledger didn't end there.

Above the tree line, Mount Sere glittered like a crown on a corpse. Somewhere inside it, a paper-faced auditor was filing paperwork on a half-fae who'd died—or hadn't—buying seconds for people they'd known three weeks.

Three weeks. Long enough, apparently, to become the kind of loss that changes a ship's heading.

"First light," I said again, to all of them, to none of them. "And this time, nobody gets left to do the math alone."
