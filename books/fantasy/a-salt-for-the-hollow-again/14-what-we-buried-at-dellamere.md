## 14. What We Buried at Dellamere

The safehouse was a tanner's cellar two streets off the Dellamere canal, which meant it smelled like a tanner's cellar and cost us nothing because no one in their right mind would look for us there. Ilex had found it before the capture—of course he had; the man catalogued hiding places the way other people collected sins—and so we owed our shelter to the one of us currently sitting in a gilded cell beneath Mount Sere, reciting archive numbers at his captors out of spite.

We owed Harl nothing. That was the arithmetic everyone else was doing.

I sat on a salt barrel that had never once held salt—the smuggler's equivalent of a decoy book—and listened to the argument go around the cellar like water around a drain. Fen wanted him dead. Fen had a feral spell's scar across his ribs and a sister in Voss's territories and a very simple moral framework: you sell the crew, you die. Tammy, the Kestrel's cook, who had until this voyage seemed incapable of malice toward anything except undercooked eel, wanted him dead too, and said so with a cleaver in her hand, which I chose to read as emphasis rather than threat. Even quiet Odo, who communicated mostly in shrugs and rope knots, had shrugged in a way that clearly meant *dead*.

Harl sat against the far wall where Wren had put him after dragging him off the Kestrel at knifepoint, hands bound with the same line he'd used to tie off cargo for eleven years. He wasn't begging. I'll give him that. He sat like a man at his own trial who already knew the verdict and was mostly curious about the sentence, and every few minutes he'd look at me—not Wren, not Fen, me—with an expression I couldn't parse. Hope, maybe. Or the calculation of a man deciding whom to sell next if things went poorly.

Wren stood apart from all of it, arms crossed, watching her crew come apart at the seams she'd spent years stitching tight. She hadn't voted. Captains don't vote, she said, which is either a principle or a dodge depending on how much you trust captains, and I had learned to trust Wren Calloway exactly as far as I could throw the Kestrel, which is to say: not far, but further than I'd expected, and further every day.

"You want to know what he traded," Fen said. He was pacing, which made his scar pull white at the edges. "You want to know what Ilex bought with his freedom? A running battle through the servant-tunnels. You want to know what Ilex *is* now? Inventory. The court doesn't kill archivists, August. They *use* them. They'll have him cataloguing soul-ledgers by the end of the week, and every name in those ledgers will be another person we can't save, because the one man who knew where they were all filed decided his sister's breathing was worth more than ours."

"I know what he did," I said.

"Do you?" Fen rounded on me. "Because you weren't in the tunnels. You were ahead of us with the vessel. You didn't hear the ward-alarms change pitch when they came through the door we were supposed to have locked. You didn't watch Rell take a crossbow bolt meant for *you*."

Rell. I kept forgetting Rell, because Rell was dead, and the dead are easy to forget precisely because they can't stand in the room and remind you. Fen didn't forget. Fen carried his dead the way I carried Tomas—close, constant, corrosive.

"He sold us," Fen said, quieter now, which was worse. "He sold us for a ransom note and a promise. And you're going to stand there with your charm-seller's hands and tell us there's a version of this where he walks away."

* * *

Here is what nobody in that cellar wanted to hear, including me.

Twenty years ago, I made a choice that looked, from the inside, like virtue. Keep your head down. Keep the gate sealed. Keep your magic folded small enough to fit inside a charm-seller's stall. Don't make waves. Don't draw the warders' eyes. Be invisible, and the world's cruelty will flow around you like water around a stone.

It was cowardice wearing competence as a coat. I know that now. Every year I stayed quiet, the stray magic of Corren—the hedge-witches, the cunning-folk, the children who saw too much—got swept up, taxed, funneled, and finally fed into a ritual I was unknowingly maintaining. My invisibility had a price tag and someone else paid it. I just never saw the ledger.

Harl saw his ledger. His sister is in a Voss work-camp outside Grayhollow, and the court's brokers told him—one visit, one document, one signature—exactly what her continued breathing would cost. Not coin. Information. A location. A name. A woman who keeps gates.

And Harl, who has no magic, no archive, no gate, no cause—Harl, whose entire moral universe is the deck of the Kestrel and the people on it—did the arithmetic every person does when the world is built by men like Malachai Voss. He paid. Because the alternative was his sister paying, and the world had arranged itself so neatly that those were the only two options on the table.

That's what Voss builds. Not loyalty. Arithmetic. A world where betrayal is just the exchange rate between the people you love and the people you merely love slightly less.

I stood up off the salt barrel. My knees cracked—I'm fifty-three and twenty years of crouching over wards does that—and I said the thing I'd been turning over since the tunnels.

"We don't kill him."

The cleaver stopped moving. Fen stopped pacing. The cellar went quiet enough that I could hear the canal through the floor grate, black water moving without opinion.

"August," Wren said carefully, "before you say anything else, you should know that I'm inclined to let them vote."

"Then let them vote," I said. "But first they vote knowing what I know. All of it."

So I gave it to them. The ledger Ilex had escaped with—the one sitting in my pack, its pages soft as cloth from handling—that proved my twenty years of careful, lawful, invisible ward-work had siphoned power straight into Voss's ritual. Every charm I'd sold, every ward I'd maintained, every night I'd spent being *good* had been a tributary feeding his river. I had been, functionally, the most useful traitor in Corren, and I hadn't even known it. No one had recruited me. No one had threatened anyone I loved. I'd done it for free, out of caution, out of the belief that keeping myself safe was the same as keeping the gate safe.

"By Fen's rule," I said, "I should be dead three times over. By the court's arithmetic, I'm worth more than Harl ever was. If we start executing people for what Voss's world forced their hands into, the list starts with me and works down, and it ends with all of us, because every single person in this cellar has done something in the last month that Voss's law calls treason and something in the last twenty years that our own consciences should call complicity."

"That's not the same," Fen said. But his voice had lost its certainty, and I heard why: because it *was* the same, structurally, and he knew it, and hating Harl was easier than following the logic to the place where it pointed at himself. Fen had smuggled salt past checkpoints for years. Salt that fed free cities—but also, twice, he'd admitted, salt that fed Voss's garrisons, because the alternative was a search hold and a hold meant questions and questions meant the sister in Grayhollow. Everyone in this story had a Grayhollow.

"It's not the same," I agreed. "Harl knew. I didn't. That matters—it should matter, it's the whole difference between guilt and tragedy. But the punishment isn't the difference. Dead is dead whether you knew or not, and if mercy only extends to people who are innocent, then mercy is just another word for luck, and luck is exactly what Voss wants us believing in instead of each other."

Tammy put the cleaver down. Not in agreement—in exhaustion, I think, the way cooks do when the argument has gone on longer than the meal. She said, "The girl. Harl's girl. What happens to her if we cut his throat?"

Nobody had an answer. That was the point. Killing Harl wouldn't free his sister; it would just remove the one person with standing to bargain for her, and the court's brokers would close her file with a stamp and move on. We'd have executed a man for loving someone too much, and gained nothing but the clean feeling, which is the most addictive and least nutritious feeling there is.

Fen still wanted him dead. I watched him understand, slowly and painfully, that wanting it and justifying it were coming apart in his hands, and I didn't enjoy watching it. There's no pleasure in being right about something like this. Being right here felt like holding a knife myself.

* * *

The vote, such as it was, split the way rivers split around a stone. Fen and Odo for death—or at least for banishment, which on the Kestrel is death with extra steps, because the coast roads are Voss's roads now. Tammy abstained, saying she'd feed a dead man or a living one but she refused to choose which. Harl said nothing through any of it, which I finally understood wasn't stoicism. It was shame. He couldn't argue for himself because there was nothing to argue *with*. He'd sold the crew, and no speech un-sells a crew.

And Wren Calloway stood with her back against the cellar stairs, blocking the only exit, which is a captain's way of saying *nobody leaves until this is finished*, and looked at me for a long moment with an expression I'd seen once before, on the burned checkpoint road, when she covered for my lies without knowing what they were protecting.

"I don't agree with you," she said. Flat. Unapologetic. "For the record. I think you're wrong about the arithmetic. I think mercy is expensive and we're poor and Ilex is the one paying the bill in a cell right now while we philosophize over a man who sold him."

"Then why—" Fen started.

"Because." Wren turned to face the whole cellar, and her voice did the thing it did in storms, dropping low so you had to lean in to hear it, and everyone leaned in. "Voss wins by making us into him. Every execution, every betrayal, every time we do his math instead of ours, he gets a little more right about the world. I've run this crew eleven years on one rule: we are not the empire, and the difference has to be *visible*, or there's no reason for anyone to sail with us, hide us, bleed for us. Mercy is the thing worth building. Not because Harl deserves it. Because *we* deserve to be the kind of crew that gives it."

She looked at Harl then, and whatever passed between captain and traitor in that cellar was not forgiveness. I want to be clear about that, because forgiveness is warm and this was not. It was more like a debt entered into a ledger: *you owe, and the interest is that you stay, and you watch, and you remember.*

"Harl buys his way back," Wren said. "His sister's file. Her location, her broker, the terms. He knows the court's paperwork better than any of us now—he signed it. He gets us into Grayhollow's records or he spends the rest of his short natural life rowing the bilge watch. And when Ilex gets out—if he gets out—Harl stands in front of him and says what he did, out loud, to his face, and takes whatever Ilex says comes next. Those are the terms. Take them or take the canal."

Harl took them. He cried, a little, doing it—eleven years of dry-eyed smuggling undone in a tanner's cellar—and I found I couldn't decide if the crying made it better or worse, and decided finally that not being able to decide was the honest answer.

Fen spat on the floor, which in sailor is a full paragraph of dissent, and took the bilge-watch rotation himself, so that Harl would spend the next month under the eyes of the man who least forgave him. It wasn't peace. It was a truce with teeth. But truces with teeth are how families get built in hard country—you don't sand the edges off, you just learn which ones not to touch.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up to sleep in the loft above the vats, Wren and I sat by the grate where the canal light came through in broken lines, and she poured tea from a kettle Tammy had left, and it tasted like tanneries smell, and neither of us mentioned it.

"You didn't back me because you agreed," I said.

"No."

"Why, then? Really."

She wrapped both hands around the tin cup and looked at the light on the water. "Because I've buried crews before. Real graves, real names, back when I ran cargo for people who solved problems with knives. And every grave I dug taught me the same thing: the killing is never the last part. It's the first part of a long tail. You kill a man and then you're the crew that kills men, and everyone aboard starts doing the math on when it'll be their turn, and brave people get careful, and careful people get gone. I didn't spare Harl for Harl. I spared him for the version of this crew that exists in six months. The one I'm trying to build." A pause. "Also, you were right. But I'd have backed you wrong, so don't get proud about it."

I laughed, which surprised us both. It's been a thin year for laughing.

"The oracle's riddle," I said. "A gate dies when its keeper stops being one. I keep turning it over. I thought it meant stepping down. Handing off. But tonight, watching Fen and Harl and Wren and Tammy tear a hole in themselves and knit it shut again—I think it means something else. I don't think a gate can be held by one keeper at all. I think that was always the lie. Mine, and Voss's. One keeper means one throat to cut, one conscience to corrupt, one soul to spend. The gate dies when the keeping spreads. When it stops being a wall one person holds and becomes a thing a hundred hands carry."

Wren considered this over her terrible tea. "You're talking about teaching strangers ward-craft. In a country where it's a hanging offense."

"I'm talking about exactly that."

"Fishwives and dockhands and children."

"And soldiers, probably. And anyone else who'll listen."

She set the cup down. "Good," she said. "Because Voss's army crossed the Sere ford this morning. Three columns. They'll be on the salt road within the week, and everything between here and the Hollow is going to be refugees, panic, and fire. If you're going to hand the gate to a hundred hands, August, you'd better start handing fast."

Above us, through the floorboards, I could hear the crew settling—Fen's boots crossing the loft, Tammy banking the stove, Harl awake at this hour too, I noticed, listening to the same news we were, carrying his new debt like a stone in his pocket.

The family had fractured and re-formed, and it was smaller and uglier and stronger than before, the way mended things are. Now we had a war to introduce it to.
