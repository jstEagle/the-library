## 15. The Warder's Confession

The safehouse was a fish-curer's attic above a chandlery that hadn't sold honest rope in years, and it smelled like it. Salt brine and old tallow and the particular sourness of a building that had been lied about on its tax papers for three generations. I lay on a pallet under the eaves and listened to the harbor gulls argue and thought, with the detached clarity that comes from not having slept, that I had spent twenty years learning how to be invisible, and the lesson had worked so well that now even I couldn't find myself.

Three days since Dellamere. Three days since Harl had stood in the doorway with his sister's ransom note in his fist and we had all decided—messily, loudly, in Fen's case with a chair—not to kill him. Wren had backed me. Not because she agreed. Because mercy was the thing worth building. She'd said it like a woman quoting a price list she didn't trust.

I turned the thought over again, the way you worry a loose tooth. *Mercy is the thing worth building.* Fine words. I had built nothing for twenty years but silence, and I was starting to suspect what the silence had been *for*.

Above me, footsteps crossed the loft. Tammy, banking the stove. Harl awake at this hour too—I noticed, the way you notice a splinter you can't reach—listening to the same war news the rest of us were, carrying his new debt like a stone in his pocket.

And then, below, the shop bell rang.

At four in the morning. In a town where Voss's patrols had started stopping anyone who looked like they owned more than one shirt.

I was down the stairs with a knife in my sleeve before my second foot hit the boards, which is a sentence I would once have found impossible and now merely found exhausting.

* * *

It was Ilex.

They stood in the chandlery's doorway dripping seawater onto floorboards that had suffered worse, their half-fae ears pressed flat, their coat torn along one shoulder in a way that suggested teeth rather than blades. Behind them the harbor fog closed like a curtain. They looked, in short, exactly like an archivist who had swum out of captivity through a hole in the world's manners.

"Before you ask," Ilex said, "yes, it hurt, no, I won't be discussing it, and yes—" they pulled a book wrapped in oilcloth from inside their ruined coat, holding it out with both hands like something that might still be dangerous, "—it was worth it."

Wren came down the stairs behind me, knife already in hand, which is her version of a greeting. Fen crowded the stairwell. Tammy lit a lamp with hands that only shook a little.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

"A ledger," Ilex said. "Court-bonded, warded against theft by three separate oaths, one of which I have now broken and will be paying interest on for some time." Their voice was steady. Their eyes weren't. They were looking at me when they said the next part, and I understood, before they said it, that whatever was in that book was going to be about me.

"It's Voss's ledger. The ritual accounting. Twenty years of entries." A pause. "August. It has your name in it."

* * *

We sat around the chandlery's single table, and Ilex unwrapped the ledger the way you unwrap a body.

It was a fat thing, bound in gray hide, its pages dense with the cramped notation warders use—the same notation Voss had drilled into me at fourteen until I dreamed in it. Columns of resonance figures. Dates. Locations. And running down the left margin, in a hand I knew better than my own, a catalog of stray magic: every leak, every seep, every wild thread of power that had slipped loose in Corren and the border towns over two decades.

Every thread marked *collected*. And beside most of them, in the tally column, a small notation I recognized because I had written its cousin ten thousand times:

*Vane — maintained.*

"I don't understand," Tammy said.

"Let her," Ilex said gently, which was worse than shouting.

I read. My lips moved. Somewhere in the middle of it I stopped feeling the chair under me.

Here is what the ledger said, in the flat language of accounting, which is the cruelest language there is because it doesn't mean to wound:

When the Hollow Gate was sealed, the sealing wasn't clean. Gates that big never close without bleeding. Stray magic leaked out of the binding for years after—a trickle, then a habit, then a weather system. Every hedge-witch's failed hex, every child's accidental spark, every charm gone wrong in every back-alley stall in Corren: the gate's overflow, looking for somewhere to go.

And someone had been standing in that weather for twenty years, catching every drop and channeling it—carefully, invisibly, dutifully—into the wards. Into the Hollow Gate's maintenance lines. Because that was the doctrine. *Containment above all. Control justifies anything.*

Except the maintenance lines didn't lead to the gate anymore. They hadn't for years. Voss had rewired them, quietly, the way he did everything, so that the runoff of my diligence ran instead into reservoirs beneath Mount Sere. Into the ritual. Every night I knelt at the Hollow Gate and kept it sealed, I was also sweeping the streets of Corren for spare magic and delivering it to the man who meant to unseal it.

Twenty years. Two decades of entries. *Vane — maintained. Vane — maintained. Vane — maintained.* My signature, in effect, on every page.

"The worst part," Ilex said softly, "is that it wouldn't have worked any other way. He needed a keeper. Someone honest enough to do the work and frightened enough to never look past the work. You weren't his victim, August. You were his instrument. His best one."

The lamp guttered. Nobody moved to fix it.

"He rewired the lines," I said. My voice came out level, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening underneath it. "Under me. For years."

"Under everyone," Ilex said. "He was your mentor. He designed your training so you'd never question the routing. You checked the seals, not the conduits. That wasn't carelessness. That was architecture."

Fen said something low—"*Gods below*" or maybe just a sound—and Tammy put her hand flat on the table as if steadying herself against a swell.

And Wren looked at me. Captain of the *Kestrel*, woman who had covered for me at a burned checkpoint without knowing why, who had chosen me over Ilex in the dark under Mount Sere and hated herself for it. She looked at me the way you look at a hull breach: assessing whether the ship stays up.

"You didn't know," she said. It wasn't a question, but it wanted an answer.

"No," I said. And then, because rock bottom apparently has a trapdoor: "But I should have. Ilex told me weeks ago—gates don't imprison power, they concentrate it. I *felt* the routing was wrong. There were nights the current ran too smooth, like water finding a channel it liked. I told myself it was the wards settling. I told myself that for twenty years." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know how much magic flows through a city? Every fear, every fever-dream, every charm-seller's stock? I swept it all into those lines. I was *proud* of my thoroughness. He built a funnel and I called it a virtue."

"August—"

"Don't." I stood up. The room tilted politely and let me. "Don't tell me it isn't my fault. I'm not asking for absolution, Wren, I'm asking for a moment where nobody lies to me, including me."

Nobody lied to me. That was almost worse.

* * *

I made it as far as the harbor stairs before my legs quit negotiating.

I sat down on wet stone with the fog pressing in and the ledger's numbers printed on the inside of my eyelids—*Vane, maintained; Vane, maintained; Vane, maintained*, twenty years of my own diligence stamped across his campaign—and I did something I hadn't done since Tomas disappeared.

I broke.

Not quietly, either. Not the careful grief of a woman who keeps herself patched. This was the ugly kind, the kind with snot and shaking and sounds you'd deny later, the kind that comes out of the place where a fourteen-year-old boy's soul got sold to pay for a door and his sister swept the floors of that door every night for two decades and called it love.

Because that was the arithmetic underneath everything. I had stayed invisible to keep the gate safe. The gate's safety had been the excuse for every silence, every refusal, every year I didn't search for Tomas, didn't ask questions, didn't make a single wave. And the whole time, the invisibility itself was the crime. My caution had hands. My caution had been carrying buckets for a usurper while I congratulated myself on my discipline.

Tomas was in a vault because I was *good at my job*.

I heard boots on the stairs and knew the stride. Wren sat down next to me without asking, which is the single most courteous thing she has ever done. She didn't say anything for a long time. The harbor slapped at its pilings. Somewhere out in the fog, a patrol boat coughed past, hunting deserters.

"When I was nineteen," she said finally, "I ran cargo for a slaver out of Sere Approach. Didn't know. The manifests said salt." She picked at a splinter on the stair rail. "Found out in Carrow Bay. Watched a man unload crates that breathed."

"What did you do?"

"Threw the captain overboard and took his ship." A pause. "Didn't unmake the voyage, though. Those people still went where they went. I've captained twenty years trying to sail that debt down, and it doesn't shrink, August. It just gets carried." She glanced sideways at me. "You're not the first person to find out their honesty was somebody else's weapon. You're just the first one I've loved enough to sit in the rain for."

I stared at her. The word *loved* sat between us, enormous, unexamined, true.

"I fed him," I said. "Twenty years. Every night."

"Yes."

"And now he's marching on the free cities with an army funded by my housekeeping."

"Yes."

"And Tomas—"

"Is still in a vessel," Wren said, "which means he is still gettable. That's the part I'd hold onto, if I were you. The rest—" she gestured at the fog, the ledger, the whole ruined shape of things, "—the rest you carry. Like the rest of us. Welcome aboard, properly this time."

She put her arm around my shoulders, awkwardly, like a woman handling rigging she respected but hadn't rigged herself. And I let her. I cried into the shoulder of a smuggler on a fish-curer's stairs, and the world did not end, which I suppose should have taught me something about all the other catastrophes I'd been preventing.

* * *

We came back up together. Ilex had the ledger open to the last pages and was waiting with the particular stillness of someone who has saved the worst for last and knows it.

"There's a final entry," they said. "Dated nine days ago. The reservoirs under Sere are full, August. Twenty years of collected overflow, plus what the fallen border wards released when the stars went out. He doesn't need to gather anything anymore." They turned the page toward me. "He needs one thing. The final resonance. The soul-key."

"Tomas."

"Tomas." Ilex's ears dipped. "And there's a marginal note in his hand, the last thing he wrote. Do you want to read it?"

I read it. Of course I read it. I will read that line until I die.

*"She'll come. She always maintains."*

He was counting on me. My constancy, my guilt, my dutiful heart—he had priced it all into the ritual, the way you price in weather. The one variable he'd never audited was the crew around the table watching me read it: a smuggler captain cracking her knuckles, a fae archivist with a broken oath and a stolen book, a cook, a forgiven traitor, a boy with a healing feral-spell scar who owed me his life and paid his debts.

"Well," I said, and heard my own voice come back solid, which surprised both of us. "Then it's time I stopped maintaining."

Tammy looked up. "Meaning what?"

Ilex was already smiling—the sharp, delighted, slightly terrifying smile of an archivist who has just been handed a research question worth dying for. "Meaning," they said, "that a gate dies when its keeper stops being one. I've been thinking about that riddle since the bone-oracle gave it to you. And I think I know what it means. But you're not going to like the workload."

Outside the chandlery, far off down the coast road, the first outriders of Voss's army were lighting signal fires—one, then two, then a chain of them burning north to south like a fuse being lit.

Wren watched them through the window and said, very calmly, "Somebody teach these people ward-craft fast. We've got an army coming."
