## 17. The Many-Handed Seal

The first lesson I ever taught was to a fishwife named Berta who could gut a cod in four seconds flat and thought ward-craft would be about the same.

"It's knots," I told her, kneeling in the salt-stiff grass behind Wren's warehouse with a length of twine in my hands. "Everything is knots. You tie intention into a shape and you hold it there."

Berta squinted at me the way she probably squinted at fish that had been lying about their freshness. "That's it? Twenty years of outlawed magic and it's *knots*?"

"Twenty years of outlawed magic," I said, "and most of it was knots."

I'd spent two decades believing the opposite—that what I did was rare, delicate, a single thread only I could carry without snapping it. Voss had taught me that. *A gate has one keeper, August. One keeper, one key, one throat to cut if it goes wrong.* He'd said it like a comfort. It had taken me twenty years and a stolen ledger to hear it for what it was: a leash.

Now I sat in the dirt with twine and salt and forty people who smelled of fish and tar and fear, and I unspooled everything he'd taught me, one knot at a time.

* * *

It had been Wren's idea, though she'd deny it with her dying breath.

We'd been on the Kestrel's deck three nights after the ledger, after I'd finished crying into her shoulder like a woman wringing out a soaked coat, and I'd said something small and hopeless—I don't even remember what, something like *it's too much for one person*—and Wren had looked at me across the lantern light with that expression she wore when she was about to be insufferably right.

"So stop being one person," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"You heard me." She tossed a rope coil at my feet, which was how she expressed affection. "You've been keeping this thing alone because you thought alone was the price of admission. It isn't. It's just what Voss wanted you to think, because a hundred keepers can't be blackmailed and one can. So teach it. Teach it to everyone who'll stand still long enough. Make your precious binding so spread out and so ordinary that no army on this coast can dig it out."

"That's—" I started, and then stopped, because the objection I'd been reaching for wasn't there. Twenty years of doctrine, and the shelf where it should have stood was empty. "That might actually work."

"Don't sound so surprised. I'm full of good ideas. Ask anyone I've paid to say so."

So we started in Corren, because Corren was mine—the city whose wards I'd fed in secret, whose alleys I knew like the lines of my own palm—and because Corren was scared enough to listen. The stars were still gone. The northern sky still peeled. Refugees came down the salt road in numbers that made the word *refugee* start to feel inadequate, and every one of them carried the same question: *what do we do?*

For twenty years the answer had been *nothing, hide, be small.* I stood up in front of a crowd of fishwives and dockhands and two very confused soldiers and gave them a different one.

"My name is August Vane," I said, and my voice cracked on it, because I hadn't said it aloud to more than three living people since I was nineteen. "I am a mage. And I'm going to teach you."

* * *

They came, is the thing. That's what I hadn't expected—not the courage of it, but the sheer ordinariness. I'd imagined magic returning as revelation, as terror, as mobs with torches. Instead it came back as a Tuesday evening in a warehouse that smelled of herring, with children chasing each other between the barrels while their mothers learned to hold intention in a loop.

Berta was my best student. She had hands that understood material things, and once I showed her that a ward was just a promise made physical—a knot that says *not here, not today*—she took to it like she'd been born to it. Within a week she was teaching the other fishwives, standing on an overturned crate with twine wrapped around both fists, bellowing, "No, Marta, you're *gripping* it, you're not strangling a chicken, you're making a *promise*—"

Marta, for the record, never got better. But she got stubborn, and stubborn turned out to matter more than talent. The binding didn't need artists. It needed people who would show up.

The soldiers were harder. They'd spent their careers enforcing the law I was now breaking in front of them, and they knew it, and I knew it, and there was a long first evening where we all pretended not to notice. Then one of them—a gray-templed sergeant named Osk, who'd once nearly caught me at my stall back in chapter one of my quiet little life—cleared his throat and asked, very carefully, whether the ward-holding would count against him if the crown ever fell.

"If the crown falls," I said, "you'll want to have held one."

He nodded slowly, like a man doing arithmetic, and took the twine.

And the children—well. The children were the ones who frightened me, in the end, but not for the reason you'd think. They weren't frightened at all. A girl of maybe eight, all elbows and missing teeth, took to the first knot like breathing, and when I told her gently that magic was dangerous and needed respect, she looked at me with the infinite patience of childhood and said, "So is the sea. We live next to it anyway."

I didn't have an answer for that. I'm not sure the world does. Maybe that's the whole point of the world.

* * *

Here is how the many-handed seal actually worked, for those who like their miracles explained:

The Hollow Gate's binding had lived, for twenty years, in one mind. Mine. Every night I'd walked the wards, poured my own strength into them, held the whole architecture alone in the dark—which, as the ledger proved, meant every stray drop of power I gathered drained neatly toward Voss's ritual, funneled through the single point of me. One keeper, one key, one funnel.

But a binding doesn't have to live in one mind. That was the riddle's answer, the bone-oracle's terrible gift: *a gate dies when its keeper stops being one.* Not when the keeper dies. When the keeping stops being singular. If a hundred people each held a thread—if the binding were braided through a hundred ordinary minds, none of them able to command the whole, none of them able to be cut out—then the gate stopped being a fortress with one door and became something else entirely. Something with no door at all.

It was messy. Gods, it was messy. Ward-craft passed hand to hand degrades like a story whispered down a line of drunks. Berta's threads came out crooked. Osk's were rigid, over-tightened, military. The children's were wild and lopsided and occasionally, alarmingly, better than mine. There was no elegance in it anywhere. Ilex, when he arrived from Dellamere with his stolen maps and his half-fae eyes that saw the weave directly, surveyed the first week's work and pronounced it, in his precise archivist's voice, "the ugliest binding in recorded history."

"And?" I said.

"And it may also be the strongest," he admitted, looking faintly ill about it. "Elegance is a weakness, August. An elegant ward has a shape, and a shape can be studied, and a shape can be broken by someone who knows it. This—" he gestured at the warehouse, at Berta yelling about chickens, at a toddler gnawing on a ward-thread like a teething ring, "—this has no shape anyone can study. It has *people*. You've made a seal out of a town."

"That was the idea."

"Yes, well." He tucked his catalog under his arm. "I spent thirty years cataloguing gates. I'd have written a monograph on why this was impossible. Do try to fail quietly; I hate revising."

* * *

There were nights, though. I won't pretend there weren't nights when it all felt like sand running through my fingers.

The third week, a boy named Pip—ten years old, holding his first true thread of the gate's binding, his whole face lit up with the weight of it—asked me if his thread was the one that kept his mother safe. His mother had come north with the refugees. Her sister hadn't.

I knelt down next to him and lied, because some lies are load-bearing. "Every thread keeps everyone safe. That's how it works. That's the trick of it."

"But which one is mine?"

"All of them are yours. And yours is everyone's." I put my hand over his small fist around the twine. "You're not holding it alone, Pip. Nobody's holding it alone anymore. That's the entire point."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Good," he said finally. "Because my hands get tired."

Out of the mouths of babes, as the charm-sellers say. My hands had been tired for twenty years. I'd simply been too proud—or too afraid—to say so.

* * *

Word travels fast when it's illegal. By the fourth week we had watchers at the edges of the warehouse district—Wren's crew, mostly, Fen limping on his mended leg and pretending it didn't ache, Harl working the doors with the desperate diligence of a man paying off a debt he knew could never fully clear. Twice, soldiers came asking questions, and twice Osk himself met them at the gate and talked them into leaving, wearing his sergeant's face like armor. Whether he was protecting us or protecting his own investment, I couldn't say. I'd stopped needing to say. That was rather the point.

And on a gray morning in the fifth week, a scout of Voss's army—a lean man in travel-stained gray, posing badly as a tinker—stood on the ridge above the Hollow crossing and watched the old gate flicker.

I know this because Ilex's birds told us, and because later, after everything, we found the scout's report copied into a captured dispatch. I've read it a dozen times. There's something in seeing yourself through an enemy's eyes that no mirror manages.

*The gate is unstable,* the report read. *Not weakening—worsening. It does not respond to pressure. It responds to nothing. It appears and disappears along its whole length, as though the binding cannot decide where it lives. Recommend immediate action before the resonance window closes.*

Voss's margin note, in that careful scholar's hand I'd once copied letterforms from for hours, said only: *She's giving it away.*

Not fury. Not confusion. Just that—one line, in ink gone brown at the edges, and beneath it, pressed so hard the paper nearly tore, a second line:

*She never understood that some things must be held by one pair of hands or not at all.*

I read that line and laughed until Wren came to see what was wrong with me, and then I read it to her, and she laughed too, the sharp bark of it, and said, "He sounds like a man watching his life savings get distributed among the poor."

"He sounds like my teacher," I said. "That's the worst part. Every word of it is something he said to me when I was nineteen and believed him."

"Well." Wren leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, the lantern catching the scar along her jaw. "You were nineteen. Now you're a woman with an army of fishwives. Funny how time works."

"Funny," I agreed.

But I'll tell you the truth, since I've decided to be a person who tells it: part of me grieved, reading that margin note. Part of me understood him still, the way you understand a language you grew up speaking. He wasn't wrong that some things break when scattered. People do. Promises do. He'd just made the oldest mistake in the ward-warder's book—he'd confused the gate with the world, and decided the world was a thing to be locked.

* * *

On the last night before the armies came—and we knew they were coming; you can smell an army on the wind days before it arrives, a smell like iron and latrines and held breath—I walked the Hollow crossing one final time as its keeper.

Because that was the last night I was one.

The binding lived now in four hundred and six minds, from Berta to Pip to Osk to a Dellamere baker who'd joined for reasons involving a bet. Four hundred and six threads, braided crooked and strong. What remained in me was only my own strand, one among hundreds, no heavier than a fishwife's.

I stood at the crossing in the starless dark and reached for the gate the way I had every night for twenty years, out of habit older than grief.

And it wasn't there.

Not destroyed—*dispersed*. I could feel it everywhere and nowhere, a hum in four hundred throats, a promise kept by a town instead of a woman. For the first time since I was fourteen years old and lost my brother to the arithmetic of gates, I felt the specific, terrifying lightness of not carrying something alone.

I sat down in the grass at the edge of the old road, and I laughed, and then, because I am who I am, I cried a little too, and then I went back to the warehouse, because Berta had scheduled the evening lesson and Berta did not tolerate absence.

From the north, on the wind, came the smell of iron.

Three days, perhaps four. Voss was coming to take back a gate that no longer existed, from a woman who was no longer its keeper, guarded by an army of fishermen and children.

I hoped, with everything left in me, that he would understand what he was walking into.

I suspected, with the same everything, that he wouldn't.
