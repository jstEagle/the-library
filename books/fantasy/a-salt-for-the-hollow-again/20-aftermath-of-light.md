## 20. Aftermath of Light

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

I know because Fen asked me what day it was, and I told him Tuesday, and he said, "Good. I'd hate for the sky to fix itself on a day nobody keeps track of." Then he went back to sleep with his arm wrapped in poultice and his face the color of old candle wax, and I stood on the deck of my ship and looked up at a sky that had been empty for eleven days and was suddenly, absurdly, full.

I'm not a sentimental woman. Ask anyone. Ask Harl, who once watched me throw a man overboard for shorting me two coppers and then bill him for the rope. But when those stars came back — all at once, like someone striking a flint behind the whole black ceiling of the world — I sat down on the salt-crusted deck of the *Kestrel* and cried like a girl at her first funeral.

I'll deny that under oath.

* * *

Here's what the histories will say, if anyone ever writes them, which I doubt, since half the archivists in Dellamere are currently busy not being dead: that the Hollow Gate broke, and magic came home, and the stars returned because the gate had been eating their light for twenty years like a debt collector skimming off the top. That's the version Ilex tells, anyway, in that voice he uses when he's reciting something he read in a book written by a man who was wrong about everything else.

What the histories won't say is what it smelled like. Burnt sugar and hot iron and underneath it all something green, something growing, like the whole coast had been holding its breath and finally exhaled. The tide came in that morning carrying kelp from waters that hadn't seen kelp in a generation. Fishermen wept into their nets. One of them tried to kiss me, and I let him keep his teeth as a gesture of goodwill toward the new age.

Corren took it hardest. Corren had built its whole spine around the outlawry — the warders' guild, the inspection houses, the little brass badges men wore so they could kick in doors with a clear conscience. When the wards fell and the law didn't, there were three days where the city ate itself. Mages dragged out of cellars by neighbors they'd sold charms to for decades. A warder strung up from his own inspection scaffold with his badge stuffed in his mouth. And on the other side of it, just as ugly: a charm-seller's stall burned to the frame by a mob convinced that any magic at all was the thing that had starved their children, never mind that the charms had been keeping those children's fevers down for years.

Fear doesn't check its arithmetic. It never has.

I sailed the *Kestrel* up the coast doing what she does best, which is moving things people don't want official eyes on — except now the cargo was mages instead of salt, and instead of smuggling them out of Corren I was mostly smuggling them back in, or out of the reach of reprisal committees, or just away, if away was what they needed. Fen navigated from a cot belowdecks for the first week, shouting bearings through the floorboards with a fever, and we followed every one of them because Fen's bearings have never once been wrong and I wasn't going to start doubting him while he was sweating through his shirt.

Harl worked the rigging like nothing had happened. That was the arrangement, and everyone kept it, and nobody kept it comfortably.

* * *

Let me tell you about Harl, since apparently I'm the one who has to carry this.

He sold us. Sold our position beneath Mount Sere to the fae court for one thing only: his sister, Marta, who'd been held in the gilded dark as collateral against his debts. He didn't sell us for coin. He didn't sell us out of spite. He sold us because the court had her, and Ilex paid for that choice with three days in a cell that I don't ask him about because he goes quiet in a way that makes the whole room go quiet with him.

When we got back to the free cities, Fen wanted him marooned. Fen, who Harl had carried on his back through the Gray Marches when the feral spell had chewed his leg open. Fen, feverish, gray-faced, propped up on an anchor crate with a knife in his good hand, saying the word *maroon* like it was a mercy he was granting.

And August — August Vane, who had more reason than any of us to put a knife in a traitor, whose whole life had been ruined twice over by men making cold calculations about other people's souls — August said no.

She didn't say it gently. She stood there in the waystation's yellow lamplight, still smelling of Mount Sere's deep air, and she said, "Voss made a world where love is a lever. Every one of you would've done what Harl did, if the court had hold of someone you loved. Don't stand there pretending otherwise. I spent twenty years pretending otherwise, and look what it built."

Fen called her a fool. She said, "Probably." Wren, she said, turning to me, and I hated her for putting it on me, and I understood exactly why she did it, because a captain's word is the only kind that settles these things without leaving a scar shaped like a grudge.

So I backed her. Not because I forgave him. Because she was right, and because if we'd cut Harl loose or worse, we'd have become the kind of crew that solves its problems with a knife, and that kind of crew is just Voss's army with better sea legs.

Harl knows what he is. That's the thing. He doesn't perform contrition, doesn't grovel, doesn't make it easy on anyone. He just works, and works, and takes the worst watches, and when Fen's leg went bad in the second week Harl sat up with him three nights running changing poultices, and Fen let him, and neither of them said a word about it, and somehow that was the forgiveness — not spoken, just *allowed*, the way you allow water back into a hull you've patched but don't quite trust.

Forgiven but not forgotten. I wrote that phrase in the log book the night we left Dellamere, under the incident report, because Ilex insisted there be an incident report. He's drafting laws now. More on that horror shortly.

* * *

Dellamere declared itself the Free Conclave nine days after the gate broke, which is a very archivist way of saying "we're in charge now, please form an orderly line."

They did it because somebody had to. Magic was back — wild, unlicensed, falling out of the sky like weather — and every city on the coast was handling it the way coastal cities handle everything, which is badly and with committees. Corren had reprisals. Sallow Bay had a prophet. Dellamere had Ilex.

He sits in the old archive hall now, the one they exiled him from, at a table stacked with folios taller than Fen, drafting what he calls the Open Skies Accords. I heard him read the preamble aloud to a room of merchants, soldiers, and one extremely skeptical fishwife named Odile who now commands the harbor watch, and I'll give you the gist:

*"Magic is not property, and therefore cannot be owned, licensed, or seized. It is weather. It is tide. We do not punish the rain for falling; we build gutters. To those who fear it: your fear is honored, your safety will be guarded, and your neighbor who lights a lamp with a fingertip is not your enemy, she is your neighbor who lights lamps. To those who wield it: power hoarded is power rotted. What was done beneath Mount Sere was done by men who believed magic belonged to them. Let no one believe it again, least of all you."*

The fishwife voted yes. So did most of the room. Two warders walked out, and Ilex let them go without a word, and later I found him sitting alone among his folios looking like a man who'd just realized the map he'd drawn was of a country that didn't exist yet.

"Twenty years I catalogued forbidden things," he said to me, not looking up. "Gate-lore. Binding-lore. I thought I was preserving knowledge against the day it could be used. Turns out the day came and the knowledge was mostly *how to stop it*, and stopping it is what fed the monster." He turned a page with that careful half-fae grace of his, fingers long and precise even when they're shaking. "Now I'm writing laws for a world with open skies, Captain, and I confess I am making up approximately half of it as I go."

"Only half?" I said. "You're ahead of every lawmaker in history."

He laughed. It surprised both of us.

* * *

Which brings me to the count.

Every captain counts her crew. You do it at dawn, you do it before a run, you do it after a fight, and you do it in the aftermath, always, because the aftermath is where people go missing — overboard, over the side, gone to ground, gone to grief. Eleven days after the gate broke, standing in the archive hall with the smell of new ink and old paper around me, I counted.

Fen: present, mending, walking with a stick he pretends is a fashion choice and complaining about the food, which is how I know he's going to live. His leg is a mess of spell-scar that glows faintly blue when the weather turns, and he's decided this makes him a barometer, and honestly the forecasts have improved.

Harl: present, working, forgiven, not forgotten, taking the worst watches, teaching Marta knots on the dock of an evening now that the court released her and she's learning what daylight costs. She flinches at gilded things. We're working on it.

Ilex: present, drafting, drowning in his own legislation, occasionally surfacing to eat the bread Odile forces on him and to say alarming things like "clause fourteen may accidentally legalize dueling, should I care?"

Myself: present, obviously, counting.

August Vane: absent.

Not dead-absent. I want to be clear about that, because I've been over the ground and I know the difference between a body that isn't found and a person who isn't there. There was no body. There was barely a gate left to leave a body under — she unmade it, poured it out, and the last anyone saw of her she was standing in a storm of returning light with her brother's soul coming apart in her hands like a constellation finally allowed to set, and then the light took the hilltop, and when it cleared there was scorch-marked grass and a silence you could sail through, and no August.

But here's the thing about a woman who spent twenty years perfecting the art of not being noticed: when she doesn't want to be found, she isn't found. I've smuggled enough people to know the difference between lost and *hidden*. She's hidden. From us, maybe. From herself, probably. From whatever comes after you break the world open and have to walk around in it as the woman who did it.

Ilex says she survived — the gate's power went into the land, not into her, that was the whole design, the many-handed seal, the giving-away. He says the drain would have nearly killed her, but nearly killed is a condition with survivors. Fen says she's dead and says it flat, the way you say a hard thing so it can't sneak up on you. Harl says nothing, because Harl has learned when his opinions aren't wanted, though I catch him looking north sometimes, toward the salt road, with an expression I pretend not to see.

Me? I say she owes me a luck-charm. She paid me one for passage to Mount Sere, a little woven thing of salt-thread and river-stone that I carried against my heart through the worst weeks of my life, and I'll tell you what I haven't told the crew: it worked. Through the Gray Marches, through the tunnels, through the breaking — I did not die, and by every honest accounting I should have, several times. So either the charm worked, or the woman who wove it put something of herself in it beyond craft, and either way I intend to collect on the balance.

A captain counts her crew. My count comes up one short, and I don't accept short counts. Never have. Ask the man I threw overboard for two coppers.

* * *

We sailed south along the coast a week after the stars returned, and I saw the world trying on its new skin.

At Sallow Bay, a child lit the harbor lanterns by pointing at them, and the harbormaster — a granite-faced woman named Pell who once fined me for breathing too near her customs house — stood beside the child and *protected* her from the crowd, arms out, daring anyone to object. At Tarnholt, they burned the warders' registry in the square, and then, in the same fire, someone threw in a bundle of outlawed charm-work, and half the crowd cheered and half the crowd lunged to save it, and the two halves argued until dawn and mostly made peace, because that's what people do when the alternative is another twenty years of darkness.

There were reprisals too. I won't pretty it. A mage in Corren hanged from the customs bridge by men wearing the old brass badges under their coats, and the city guard looked elsewhere, and Ilex's accords were three days from being printed. Fear doesn't check its arithmetic. The work of the next ten years is teaching it to.

But I also saw a fishwife teach her whole street a warming-charm in an evening, no fee, no license, hands moving in the old pattern like she'd learned it from her grandmother before the outlawry and had kept it folded away all this time like linen saved for a wedding. I saw the northern sky, clean and starred, and refugees on the salt road turning their faces up at it and forgetting, briefly, that they were walking away from burned homes.

Tentative joy. That's the phrase Ilex used, and I mocked him for it, and I've stopped mocking him for it, because it's exactly right. Joy, but held carefully, the way you hold a cracked cup — aware of what it cost, aware it might not hold, drinking anyway.

* * *

On the fourteenth day, Odile came down to the dock where the *Kestrel* lay moored, and she handed me a package wrapped in oilcloth, and she said, "Found this pinned to the door of the burned charm-stall in Corren. Thought it might be yours."

It was a luck-charm. Salt-thread and river-stone, woven in a pattern I knew better than my own rigging — except this one was different. Newer. The knotwork was looser, less practiced, like the hands that made it had been shaking. And tucked inside the weave, where the old one had carried nothing, was a scrap of paper with six words on it in a small, careful hand:

*North road. Slowly. Tell no one.*

Well. *Tell no one.* From the woman who spent twenty years telling no one anything, and buried her brother's freedom and half a continent's magic under the weight of it.

I stood on my deck and laughed until Fen stuck his head out of the hatch and asked if I'd finally gone round the bend, and I said, "Get your stick, barometer. We're sailing north."

Because a captain counts her crew, and my count is one short, and the woman who taught me that mercy is the thing worth building is walking a salt road alone in a world she broke open for all of us, and I have never in my life accepted a short count.

The *Kestrel* would sail at dawn. Some debts you collect in person.
