## 21. Where Keepers Go

I died on the salt road, or something close enough to it that the distinction stopped mattering somewhere past the second milestone.

Here is what nobody tells you about shattering a gate: it does not go out like a candle. It goes out like a held breath finally released, and if you are standing at the center of twenty years of stored power when it happens—if you are, in fact, the person who spent those twenty years feeding it—then you learn very quickly what a wick feels like.

I woke face-down in a ditch with my hair gone white at the temples and my hands shaking so badly I had to watch them for a full minute before I trusted them to hold water. The sky above me was obscene with stars. Thousands of them, all the ones that had been missing and then some, crowded back into their places like guests arriving late to a wedding and finding all the good seats taken anyway.

"Good," I croaked at them. "Now you show up."

A shepherd found me eventually, or I found his fire—I honestly don't remember which way that transaction went. He fed me barley soup and asked no questions, which in this part of the country is either kindness or habit, and gave me a name to call him by that I'm fairly sure he invented on the spot. When I could walk, he pointed me south along the salt road and said, "Corren's that way, if you're fool enough."

"I'm always fool enough," I said, and meant it as a joke, and heard it land as a confession.

* * *

The strange thing about walking home anonymously is how easy it is. Twenty years of practice being no one, and it turns out the skill survives everything—even the end of the world, even having your face sketched on wanted broadsheets in three cities under the heading THE GATE-BREAKER, REWARD: ROYAL FAVOR. The sketches look nothing like me. In Dellamere's version I have a beard. I have decided to take this as a compliment to my craft rather than an insult to my chin.

So I walked. South through the Gray Marches, where the blight was already retreating like a tide going out, wild magic sinking back into the soil and leaving green shoots behind that glowed faintly at dusk, which would take some getting used to but was, on balance, better than the feral spells that had been nesting there before. Past the burned checkpoint where Wren had once lied to a soldier's face for me without knowing why—a debt I still hadn't found words for, and possibly never would, because some things you don't repay, you just carry forward. Through villages where people were nailing charms to their doors again, clumsy things, ugly things, made by hands that had never been taught, and every one of them working a little, humming with the loose magic of the new world like a cracked bell can still ring.

I could have fixed any of those charms in a minute. I made myself keep walking. There's a difference between teaching and doing-for, and I had spent two decades learning the wrong one.

Tomas's grave was where we'd buried him—or where we'd buried the empty box, rather, because there had never been a body, only a mother's certainty and a father's refusal to dig a grave they couldn't fill. Our parents were long dead now. The grave had kept itself, more or less; the headstone leaned like a tired man against a wall, and moss had eaten most of the letters, but I didn't need the letters. I'd carved them myself at fourteen, with a chisel and tears and the first flare of magic I ever let anyone see, and Voss had watched me do it and said nothing, and I had thought his silence was grief.

It wasn't grief. It was inventory. But that's a different chapter, and I've paid that bill.

I sat down in the grass beside the stone and took the broken vessel out of my pack.

It was a small thing, really, for what it had held. Fae-work, gilded once, now scorched black down one side where the gate's power had torn through it. A compressed constellation, Ilex had called what lived inside it—not a boy, not a soul in any shape the priests would recognize, but a knot of memory and self folded small enough to be carried, to be *spent*. For years it had sat in a vault beneath Mount Sere, weighed against other vessels in a court that traded children's souls like coin, and Malachai Voss had needed only one final resonance from it to reopen the Hollow Gate and bind every living mage to his great machine of salvation.

And when I shattered the gate, the vessel broke too, and what was inside it—

Well. Here is what I know. Here is what I felt, in the last instant before the power took me under: something unfolding. Something stretching after twenty years of being folded, like a body remembering it has limbs. It didn't speak. Constellations don't speak. But it turned toward me the way a plant turns toward light, and for one impossible moment I held all of it—all of Tomas, the scraped knees and the stolen plums and the way he laughed through his nose when he thought something was funny but didn't want to admit it—and then it came apart, gently, the way frost comes off a window in the sun, and it went everywhere, into everything, into the ground and the grass and the wide-open sky that no longer had holes in it.

He died free. That was the whole point. That was the terrible, non-negotiable arithmetic of the third path: the gate dies, the keeper stops being one, and the soul that fueled it gets to stop being fuel.

I set the broken vessel on the grave, in the little hollow where the stone met the earth, and weighted it with a flat rock so it wouldn't wander off in the next wind.

"Sorry I was late," I said. Twenty years late. Twenty years of warding a gate that was quietly eating him while I patted myself on the back for my vigilance. "You'd have hated the funeral. You always said funerals were just parties where nobody's allowed to laugh."

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere a lark was doing its lark business, entirely indifferent to the metaphysics of souls, and I found, to my surprise, that I could smile at that. Not the tight smile I'd worn across market stalls for two decades—the other kind, the kind that uses muscles you forget you own.

"I'm going to teach," I told him. "Out loud. Where people can hear me. You'd have laughed yourself sick."

I stayed until the light went gold, and then I stood up, brushed off my skirts, and left the vessel there with him, because he'd earned the right to be finished with containers.

* * *

Corren had changed, and hadn't.

The walls were the same gray-green granite, scarred in new places where the ward-collapse panic had turned briefly violent. The streets smelled the same—brine and coal smoke and the fishmonger's eternal war with the weather. The Charm Row was exactly where I'd left it, except that half the stalls were shuttered and the other half had sprouted hand-painted signs reading LICENSED PRACTITIONER in handwriting that suggested the licensing process involved a stamp and a shrug. Magic was legal now. Legal the way a river is legal: technically true, practically chaotic, and governed by rules that Ilex was drafting somewhere at a pace that could only be described as heroic.

My stall was still mine. The city had held it in trust, or more accurately nobody had wanted to rent a stall belonging to a woman who might come back from the dead, and superstition is cheaper than rent. The awning had rotted. The counter had warped. Someone had chalked a crude star on the doorframe—protection, or tribute, or just boredom—and I left it there.

I reopened on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were slow and I wanted a soft landing.

The first customer was a fishwife named Berta who used to buy luck-knots from me back when luck-knots were illegal and harmless. She looked at me, looked at my white-streaked hair, looked at the sign I'd hung—CHARMS, HONESTLY PRICED, QUESTIONS ANSWERED—and said, "They say you broke the sky open."

"They say a lot of things."

"You did, though." It wasn't a question. Nothing in Corren ever really was.

"Yes," I said. "I did."

She considered this with the gravity of a woman deciding whether a fish is fresh. Then she put a copper on the counter and said, "Then I'll have a charm that keeps eels from biting, and afterward you'll tell me how it works, because my girl's got the knack and I'll not have her fumbling blind."

And that—that was the moment the last twenty years finally ended. Not on the wall at the Hollow, not in the fire and the shattering, but here, over eels, with a copper on the counter. Because she hadn't asked me for magic. She'd asked me for the *teaching*, and I had spent so long believing those were opposites that it took a fishwife to show me they were the same thing wearing different coats.

So in the evenings, after the stall closed, I taught.

We started with six people in the back of Berta's smokehouse: her daughter Nell, who had the knack and hands like quicksilver; a retired soldier who wanted wards against nightmares and admitted it in a voice like a man confessing to crying at weddings; two brothers who ran the ferry and mostly wanted to know why their mother's old protection sigils had started glowing; a boy of eleven who'd followed the crowd in and refused to leave; and, to my enduring bewilderment, Harl, who had materialized in Corren a month after the battle with his sister settled safe in Dellamere and an expression that said he knew exactly what he owed everyone and intended to pay it in attendance. Nobody had forgiven him, quite. But Wren had backed mercy, and mercy, once chosen, turns out to be a thing you have to keep choosing, daily, like a fire.

Harl took notes. Harl was, infuriatingly, my best student.

I taught them the way I wished someone had taught me: that a ward is a promise you make to a place, not a cage you build around it. That control is a story power tells about itself. That the old doctrine—the one Voss preached and I practiced in secret for twenty years, the one that says magic must be hoarded by the few to protect the many—is how you get gates, and gates are how you get courts beneath mountains trading in children. I taught them the binding-threads, one strand each, the way I'd taught the volunteers before the battle, and I watched Nell hold hers up to the lamplight and say, wonderingly, "It's warm," and I thought: *yes. That's the whole secret. It was always supposed to be warm.*

Some nights I caught myself listening for the wards. Old habit—the nightly round, the quiet check, the keeper's vigilance that had been my identity and my prison both. But there was nothing to listen for anymore. The gate was gone. The crossing was just a road now, marked by a standing stone, and the binding lived in hundreds of scattered minds from Corren to the coast, each carrying their thread like a stitch in a coat no single person owned. If one of us faltered, the others held. That was the design. That was the riddle answered: *a gate dies when its keeper stops being one*—and a keeper stops being one the moment the keeping belongs to everyone.

Some nights I still woke at the hour I used to walk to the gate, heart pounding, certain I'd forgotten something crucial. On those nights I'd get up, light a lamp, and copy out lesson plans. It helps, having somewhere to put your hands.

Tonight the smokehouse was full—fourteen students, and Berta charging them a penny apiece for bench space, because commerce finds a way—and we were working on threshold-wards, the gentle kind, the welcome-and-warning kind, when the door opened on the smell of salt air and tar and a voice I'd have known in a crowd of thousands.

"Well," said Wren Calloway, captain of the *Kestrel*, leaning in the doorway with the wind still in her hair and that look on her face that meant she'd already won whatever argument she was about to start. "Word was the famous Gate-Breaker was hiding out in Corren selling trinkets. I said, no, she's teaching, because August never could resist telling people they're warding things wrong."

The room went quiet. Fourteen faces turned toward me, waiting to see what I'd do.

I wiped the chalk dust off my hands, and grinned at her like a woman with nothing left to hide—which, for the first time in my life, happened to be true.

"Class," I said, "this is Captain Calloway. She's going to tell us why she's here, and it's going to be trouble, and we're going to want to say yes anyway."

Wren's grin matched mine, sharp and bright as the returned stars. "North," she said. "There's a gate-ruin past the Gray Marches that's singing, Ilex has the maps, and Fen swears the crew's not sailing anywhere without you. So." She spread her hands. "How fast can you close a school?"

I looked around the smokehouse—at Nell already reaching for her notes, at Harl already calculating provisions, at the boy of eleven vibrating with the specific energy of a child about to stow away on something—and I laughed, out loud, nose and all, the way Tomas used to.

"Give me till morning," I said.
