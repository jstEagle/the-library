## 22. A Salt for the Hollow Again

A year is a strange unit of measurement. Long enough for a scar to go silver. Not long enough for a woman to stop flinching at the sound of her own name spoken aloud in a crowd.

I'd had twenty years of practice being no one, and it turned out that being someone again was harder work than any ward I'd ever woven. Being *August Vane*—out loud, in daylight, with witnesses—was the most demanding craft I had ever attempted, and I include in that count the time I shattered an ancient gate with my bare hands while an army screamed beneath me.

But here is what nobody tells you about the year after the world ends: the world does not end. It wakes up grumpy, confused, and hungry, and it demands breakfast.

That morning, breakfast was salt.

* * *

The Hollow crossing looked different without the gate, obviously. It looked different the way a face looks different after the fever breaks. The stone arch itself was gone—unmade, along with everything Voss had wanted to pour through it—but the land remembered its shape the way an amputee remembers a hand. Grass grew in a pale seam where the threshold had been, refusing to green properly, as if it were still waiting for instructions.

In place of the arch stood a standing stone. Fen's idea, originally, though he'd phrased it as "put something there before somebody puts something worse there," which is how most of the world's enduring monuments probably came to be. We'd hauled it from the quarry outside Corren on a borrowed cart, and it was nothing special to look at—gray granite, taller than a man, wider at the base, carved only with a single line of script down its face in Ilex's careful hand: **THE ROAD IS OPEN. WALK IT KINDLY.**

Fen had wanted something more martial. *Here we broke a tyrant.* Harl had wanted something more apologetic, which told you exactly where Harl's head lived these days. Ilex had split the difference and given us a sentence you could read either way, depending on how kind your walking was.

I crouched at the stone's base with a clay bowl of coarse salt between my knees and let the wind off the Hollow pull at my hair. Behind me, the road east carried the sounds of the gathering: carts, voices, someone's goat registering a formal complaint about the entire enterprise. The crew was assembling. My crew. The word still fit strangely in my mouth, like a coin from a country I'd never visited.

Salt in hand, I thought about the old tradition—the one I'd grown up on, back when magic was common enough to have traditions worth outlawing. You salted a threshold to keep things out. That was the whole grammar of it: salt as refusal, salt as *no*, salt as the line drawn between mine and not-mine. Every charm-seller in Corren sold salt wards by the sackful, and every one of them was a small, desperate *no* scratched against the dark.

I took a handful of salt and let it run through my fingers onto the grass at the stone's northern face.

"Wrong direction," said Wren Calloway, behind me. She'd come up quiet, the way she always did, boots silent despite the fact that she walked like a woman who owned the ground personally. "You're supposed to throw it over your shoulder. Superstition has rules."

"Superstition," I said, salting the eastern face now, an unbroken line of white against the green, "has never once survived contact with me."

"Fair."

The old rite barred. That was its entire purpose, and I had spent two decades being the living embodiment of its purpose—barring, sealing, refusing, hiding. So when I'd decided to mark the crossing's first anniversary with a ceremony, I'd spent three sleepless nights reworking the words, because I would not—I could not—salt another threshold shut. Not ever again. Not even symbolically. Especially not symbolically; symbols were where all my worst mistakes had started.

So this salt wasn't a wall. It was a welcome. An invitation written in the oldest material language I knew: *this road is kept, and everyone who walks it is kept too.*

I finished the circle, stood, and dusted off my knees. "There," I said. "Now it's a door with no hinges. Come through it or don't."

Wren considered the standing stone with the expression she reserved for charts showing bad weather ahead—which, on her, counted as deep approval. "It needs a name."

"It has a name. It says so right on it."

"That's a sentence, not a name." She squinted at it. "Callows' Rest."

"You cannot name a monument after yourself. That's—" I gestured at the general concept of Wren Calloway. "That's exactly the sort of thing Voss would do."

"Voss named a mountain after himself?"

"He named a *ritual* after himself. Close enough."

Wren grinned—that quick, crooked thing that had gotten half the free coast to underestimate her and the other half to buy her drinks—and slung an arm around my shoulders, smelling of tar and rope and the Kestrel's particular blend of salt-smoke. One year ago, an arm around my shoulders would have sent me calculating escape routes. Now I just leaned into it, briefly, like a fence post leaning into weather.

"Come on, keeper," she said. "Your people are waiting."

"They're not my people."

"They're absolutely your people. I've seen the paperwork."

"What paperwork?"

"The paperwork of me deciding so. It's binding."

And that, more or less, was how every decision of consequence got made in our family now.

* * *

The gathering at the Hollow had no official purpose, which was precisely why everyone came. A year of open magic had taught the free cities that if you waited for official purposes, you died waiting; everything worth doing happened in the unofficial spaces, at crossroads and kitchen tables and the decks of smuggler ships refitted for rescue work.

They came from everywhere. Fishwives from Dellamere who'd taken ward-threads at my first chaotic lessons and now ran their own circles, teaching girls who'd been taught their whole lives to make themselves small. Soldiers from the broken garrisons who'd chosen, after Voss fell, to carry salt instead of steel. Children—always children—who'd been born into a starless, warded, suffocating world and were now growing up under a sky full of impossible lights, and who treated wild magic the way children treat everything: as a toy they hadn't yet been told not to break.

Two of those children were currently attempting to teach a hedge-wisp to juggle. The hedge-wisp was losing. Wisps are made of stray intention and old light, and theirs had clearly been intended by someone with no talent whatsoever for catching.

"Hen!" bellowed Fen across the crowd, scooping up his daughter one-armed. His shoulder was mended now, though the Gray Marches feral-spell had left a mapwork of silver scarring that he showed off to anyone who'd hold still. "What did I say about enchanting livestock?"

"She's not livestock, she's a *light*—"

"Then enchant something that isn't about to be a *fire*—"

Harl appeared out of the crowd to take the wisp gently off the girl, cupping it in his big hands until it settled into a drowsy glow. A year ago he'd sold us to the court beneath Mount Sere to buy his sister out of a soul-debt. A year ago I'd refused to let the crew execute him for it, and the argument had cracked us nearly in half before Wren backed me—not because she agreed, but because, as she put it, *mercy is the only cargo that appreciates.*

His sister Maren was here today, alive and soul-whole, selling fried dough at a folding table and overcharging everyone cheerfully. Harl worked beside her most days now. He still didn't talk much. But he'd started meeting my eyes again around midwinter, and last month he'd silently fixed the wobbling leg on my market stall table without being asked, which in the currency of Harl was roughly a sonnet.

Ilex arrived the way Ilex always arrived: sideways, mid-story, already holding a document. They came threading through the crowd in a storm of layered robes—half-fae elegance dragged through an archivist's chronic disregard for mud—with satchels bulging and ink on their fingers and at least four other people's scarves on at once.

"—and *that* is why the northern courts will honor the Accord, provided," Ilex said, finishing a conversation with a Dellamere merchant as they reached us, "provided anyone actually enforces it, which is the question I've been asked eleven times this week, and the answer remains *probably*, which in legal terms is a scream."

"Ilex," I said. "You're supposed to be on holiday."

"This IS my holiday." They dropped their satchels with a thud that suggested several of them contained books, and books, in Ilex's case, meant weight. "I brought maps."

"You always bring maps."

"These maps are *recreational.*" They produced a rolled vellum and snapped it open across the standing stone's flat face with the flourish of a conjurer. It was, indeed, a map—a gorgeous, obsessive piece of work, inked in three colors, of the far north. Beyond the old border wards. Beyond, even, the places the refugees had come from, back when the sky peeled. "Nobody has surveyed the high latitudes since the gates fell. There are ruins up there, August. Court-sites. Old crossings nobody remembers. And—" they tapped a spot marked with a small, tantalizing question mark "—possibly a library. Possibly a *whole* library. Frozen. Preserved. Unguarded except by weather, and I don't scare anymore, I've met weather's lawyers."

"You were captive under a mountain eight months ago," Wren said.

"And I escaped, didn't I? Growth."

I looked at the map. The blank white spaces at its top edge didn't frighten me the way blank spaces used to. Once, a blank space on a map had meant *threat*, had meant *keep it warded, keep it unknown, keep it out*. Now it meant what it had always secretly meant: *there is more world.*

"We're not going anywhere," I said, "until the evening lesson's done. I promised the Dellamere circle I'd walk them through threshold-work myself."

"August." Wren's voice had gone quiet in the way it did when she was about to say something she'd been carrying a while. "That's actually what I wanted to talk to you about. The lessons. The circles. All of it."

Something old and cold stirred in my chest—the reflex of a woman who'd once heard *we need to talk about your keeping* and lost everything. Twenty years of habits don't die in twelve months; they just get quieter.

"Go on," I said carefully.

Wren looked at the standing stone. At the crowd. At the salt line gleaming faintly where the light caught it. "You've built something here. The teaching, the shared keeping—all of it. Three hundred people now hold threads of the old craft, and none of them can be cut out of anything, because there's no gate left to cut them out of. You did that." She paused. "And the Kestrel's been sitting in Corren harbor for six weeks because I couldn't figure out how to ask you to leave it."

The cold thing in my chest dissolved. What replaced it was harder to name—something with weight and warmth both, like bread fresh from an oven, like a hand in yours.

"The north," I said.

"The north," Wren agreed. "Ilex's library. The court-sites. Whatever else is up there now that the doors are all open and the sky's all lit." She shrugged with elaborate carelessness, which was how Wren said things that mattered most. "We'd be gone months. Maybe a year. And I know you've got the circles, and the stall, and—" she waved a hand at the gathering, at the whole improbable village that had sprouted around a dead gate "—all this. So it's not an order. It's a berth. There's a cabin with your name on it, if you want it. Always will be."

I looked at the map again. At the question mark in the white north.

Twenty years ago, I had chosen stillness. I had told myself it was duty—someone had to keep the gate, someone had to hold the line—and the truth, which took me two decades and a stolen ledger to learn, was that stillness was also fear wearing duty's coat. Stillness had fed a tyrant. Stillness had cost me my brother twice: once when I lost him, and once, worse, when I learned what my hiding had helped pay for.

Tomas's grave sat on a hillside outside Corren with a broken soul-vessel resting at its head, and I visited it every week, and every week I told him the same thing: *you're free, and I'm trying to spend what's left of my life better than I spent yours.*

Stillness had had its turn.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I expected, maybe because I wasn't lying, "the circles don't need me the way they used to. That was rather the point. Sera runs the Dellamere group better than I do—her students aren't scared of her, which the evidence suggests is pedagogically superior." I tapped the question mark. "And I've never seen the northern sky."

"Nobody has," Ilex said, delighted. "Not with stars on. It'll be entirely new constellations. I've prepared a naming schema."

"Prepared a—of course you have."

"There's a strong case for naming one after Wren purely so she'll stop complaining that nothing's named after her."

"There is NOT a strong case—" began Wren, and Ilex grinned, and Fen laughed from across the crowd where he'd been pretending not to eavesdrop, and Hen shouted something about naming a star after herself, and just like that the argument became the family's property, the way all our arguments did now, passed hand to hand and made into something warm.

I stood at the salted threshold of the Hollow and felt the year settle over me—not as a wound, but as a weight I was finally carrying correctly, with the load distributed the way loads should be. Across many backs. Among many hands.

"All right," I said. "North."

Wren whooped. Ilex began immediately annotating the map. And somewhere in the crowd, a hedge-wisp finally learned to juggle, briefly, gloriously, before setting fire to nothing at all.

* * *

We stayed until dusk. The evening lesson happened as promised, right there at the standing stone with the whole gathering for a classroom—three hundred people learning threshold-craft in the last light, clumsy and earnest and utterly ungovernable. I watched fishwives correct each other's forms. I watched a soldier help a child whose salt-line kept coming out crooked, telling her that crooked lines held fine if you believed in them, which was theologically dubious but practically true.

When the stars came out—they came out fast now, eager, as if making up for lost time—the crowd sang the old road-blessing, the one from before the outlawry, the one my mother had hummed while hanging washing. I sang it too. Out loud. In public. In my own voice.

Later, long past midnight, when the fires had burned to embers and the gathering slept in wagons and bedrolls all across the Hollow, I walked the salt line alone one last time. The white ring around the standing stone glimmered faintly under the stars—my craft in it, yes, but not only mine. Sera's thread. Fen's. Hen's crooked, perfect line. Three hundred hands' worth of *welcome* pressed into the ground.

At the northern face, where I'd begun, I knelt and added one final pinch of salt, and spoke the new words of the rite, the ones I'd written over three sleepless nights and never said in company, because some things you make for everyone and give to no one:

*"Old salt, new meaning. No more walls. Let everything through that means no harm, and harm nothing that comes through. Keep this road, all of you, together, always."*

The wind moved through the Hollow, gentle, carrying the smell of the sea from far off—carrying, perhaps, the smell of a smuggler ship at anchor in Corren harbor, sails furled, cabin waiting, north waiting beyond it.

I stood, brushed the salt from my hands, and turned toward home toward morning, toward the road, toward whatever the white spaces held.

For the first time in my life, I wasn't barring the door.

I was leaving it open behind me.
