## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet Life

The trouble with selling magic illegally for ten years, Sebastian Winterbourne had learned, was that you got very good at it, and being very good at something illegal made you careless, and being careless in Corvane got you hanged.

He reminded himself of this every evening as he lit the lamps in his little shop on Thimble Row and arranged the day's wares with the practiced hand of a man who had nothing to hide. Luck-knots, braided from river-grass and sold three coppers apiece. Fever-charms, which were — according to the sign in his window — *merely herbal sachets with a decorative knotwork tradition*. Wards against bad weather, bad luck, and bad husbands, all of them, per the sign, "strictly ornamental."

The Wardens' Edict had been law for a decade now. Magic was outlawed, magicians were outlawed, and the word *witch* itself had been struck from the city's ledgers so thoroughly that children born after the Edict thought it meant a kind of moth. Sebastian had been twenty-two when the Wardens came through the universities burning books, and he had done what his family had always done in hard times: he had gone quiet, gone small, and gone to work.

*We do not fight,* his grandmother used to say, her hands busy with a knot that could calm a colicky infant at forty paces. *We keep. The fighting families are remembered. The keeping families survive.*

She had been right, mostly. He survived. He kept. And every night he went down the stairs behind the false wall in his storeroom, past the pickling jars and the barrel of honest salted herring (an excellent cover; no one suspected a man who smelled permanently of brine), down into the cold dark beneath Thimble Row where the Hollow Gate waited under its shroud of salt.

But that was later. First there was the market.

* * *

Corvane's night market opened at dusk and ran until the watch-bells rang midnight, and it was Sebastian's favorite hour of the day, though he'd have denied it under torture. There was something restorative about the market's cheerful dishonesty — everyone there was breaking some small law or other, and everyone knew it, and nobody cared. The fishmonger shorted his weights. The fortune-teller in the purple tent told fortunes she didn't believe in. The pie-seller's meat was, by any honest accounting, mostly rat. It was a city within the city, built entirely out of winks.

Sebastian set up his stall between the candle-dipper and a man who sold singing birds that did not, in fact, sing (they were stuffed; the song came from a music box in the base; Sebastian respected the craftsmanship enormously).

"Evening, Winterbourne," said Marta the candle-dipper, who had forearms like ship rope and a laugh that could be heard from the docks. "Sold me a fever-charm last week. My boy's fever broke in a night."

"Herbs," said Sebastian piously. "Chamomile and willow bark. The knot is purely traditional."

"Course it is." She winked at him. "I'll take two more."

This was the arrangement, and it was a good one: half of Corvane knew perfectly well that Winterbourne's charms worked, and half of those knew better than to say so, and the whole edifice rested on the mutual agreement that everyone would go on pretending. It was, Sebastian sometimes thought, the most successful spell in the city — a glamour woven not of power but of politeness, cast over an entire market, renewed nightly by consent.

He was mid-sale — a luck-knot for a nervous bridegroom, who needed it, poor lamb, since his bride's family had come to the wedding armed — when he felt the change in the crowd.

It was subtle. A stillness rippling outward from the market's mouth, the way water goes quiet before a pike strikes. Conversations dipped. The fortune-teller's tent flap stopped swaying. Somewhere, a bird that couldn't sing was hastily covered with a cloth.

Wardens.

They came through the market in their grey coats and iron buttons, four of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of people whose authority required no hurry. At their head walked a woman Sebastian didn't recognize — young, sharp-faced, with a ledger-clerk's spectacles and a clerk's habit of writing everything down. Behind her, two men carried the detection engine.

Everyone in Corvane called it the Bell. It looked like a church bell that had been melted and reassembled by someone working from memory and spite, and it hummed — a low, sour note that made Sebastian's back teeth ache — whenever magic was near. Ten years of inspections had taught Sebastian its range, its quirks, its blind spots. He had spent a small fortune in bribes learning that the Bell disliked damp weather and large quantities of iron, and he had arranged his life accordingly.

"Routine inspection," announced the woman with the spectacles, in the tone of someone reading a grocery list. "Licenses and wares presented for examination."

Sebastian stepped forward with his best smile, the one that made him look agreeable and slightly dim. "Welcome, welcome. Winterbourne's Charms and Knotwork. Entirely decorative, I assure you. Would you like to see my license? Laminated it myself."

"Laminated?"

"With varnish. It's a craft technique."

The woman — her collar pin said *Inspector Dree* — looked at him the way one looks at a stain. But she took the license, and while her men began their slow circuit of the market with the Bell, she examined his wares with theatrical thoroughness, holding up luck-knots to the lamplight, sniffing fever-charms with the expression of a woman who has been promised excitement and is finding only chamomile.

"This one," she said, holding up a knot braided in the old style, seven strands, "is very fine work."

"My grandmother's pattern."

"Mm. And what does it *do*?"

"It looks nice on a mantelpiece."

Dree's eyes flicked up to his over the spectacles. For a moment — just a moment — Sebastian felt the look go through the agreeable-dim mask and take his measure underneath, the way a butcher's thumb finds the ribs of a beast. Then she set the knot down.

"The Bell will pass your stall shortly," she said. "I trust you have nothing it will object to."

"Only my herring," said Sebastian. "It's famously pungent."

She almost smiled. It was the worst moment of the evening.

Because here was the thing about the Bell, the thing Sebastian had learned in ten years of careful, suffocating, meticulous hiding: it did not detect magic, exactly. It detected *tension*. It sang loudest near strong workings, yes, but it also sang near old ones — near places where magic had soaked into stone and stayed, the way wine soaks into a table. And beneath Sebastian's shop, beneath ten feet of cellar and sixty feet of older stone, sat the oldest working in Corvane, a covenant laid down by his family nine generations ago, humming its slow patient note into the bones of the city.

The Bell had never sung for the gate. That was the miracle at the center of Sebastian's life, the fact upon which everything else balanced: the gate's magic was *kept* magic, sealed and sleeping, and it read to the Bell as silence. His grandmother had spent years teaching him why — the covenant turned inward, a lock turned upon itself, giving off nothing.

*As long as the seal holds,* she had said, *you are invisible to them.*

*And if it doesn't hold?*

*Then, dear heart, you will have larger problems than Wardens.*

The Bell drew nearer. Sebastian stood at his stall with his hands folded and his heartbeat steady through sheer professional discipline, and watched the two grey-coated men carry the sour-humming thing past the candle-dipper, past the fortune-teller's tent (a brief, wavering note — the woman had a real charm sewn into her hem, the fool), past the stuffed birds —

— and then it passed Sebastian's stall, and it hesitated.

Not sang. Not rang. *Hesitated.* The way a dog hesitates at a door where something once happened. The nearest Warden frowned and tapped the Bell's casing, and the machine gave its usual dull toneless hum and moved on, and Inspector Dree wrote something in her ledger without looking up, and the inspection rolled onward down Thimble Row like a cart with a squeaking wheel.

Sebastian smiled at his customers. He sold a fever-charm to a grandmother. He made change. He laughed at Marta's joke about the Wardens' trousers.

All the while, one thought turned over and over in him like a stone in a stream:

*It heard something.*

* * *

He closed the shop early, which he never did, and told himself it was because of the rain starting up outside, which was true but insufficient. He banked the fire, checked the ward-latches on the windows — purely decorative, of course — and then stood for a long moment in the storeroom, listening.

The house sounded like a house. Rain on the roof. The settling tick of beams. Herring, brining.

He pulled the false wall open anyway.

The stairs behind it were steep and narrow, cut into the older stone that predated Corvane's founding, and they went down farther than any cellar had a right to. Sebastian counted the steps out of habit — forty-one — and lit the lamp at the bottom, and there it was.

The Hollow Gate.

Most people imagined gates as doors, because most people had never seen one. This was a doorway-shaped absence in the living rock, tall as two men, its edges worn smooth as sea glass, and it was filled — not with darkness, not with stone, but with a slow grey shimmer, like light seen through falling snow, or the surface of deep water viewed from below. Around its frame, carved into the rock by hands nine generations dead, ran the covenant-script: the binding words, the keeper's names, the seal.

His family's work. His family's burden. *A Salt for the Hollow*, his grandmother had called the whole arrangement, with her grim little jokes, because the covenant was fed with salt and blood and vigilance, and it asked for all three forever.

Every week for ten years, Sebastian had come down these stairs and tended the seal. He checked the script for wear. He renewed the salt line at the threshold. He pressed his palm to the stone and let the covenant know, in whatever way such things know, that its keeper was alive and attentive and *still here*, thank you, despite everything.

Tonight, the salt line was intact. The script was unworn. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Except.

Except that as he stood there, lamp raised, rain dripping somewhere far above, Sebastian became aware of a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Coming not from the gate itself but from *inside* it, from beyond the grey shimmer, the way you might hear, standing on a shore, the drumming of surf you cannot yet see.

A hum. Low and patient and wrong.

The gate had never made a sound. In ten years of tending, through storms and inspections and one memorable earthquake, the Hollow Gate had been silent as a held breath. His grandmother had told him what its silence meant: *the seal holds*. She had also, in the last winter of her life, gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise and told him what sound would mean, if it ever came:

*If ever you hear it, Sebastian — if ever the door begins to hum like a throat clearing — the covenant is thinning. And a thinning covenant does not mend itself.*

He stood in the cold dark with the lamp shaking slightly in his hand — he noticed this with detached irritation, as though observing a stranger's hand — and listened to the gate hum, soft and slow, like something on the other side breathing in its sleep.

Or waking up.

The renewal, he thought. The covenant was forged to be renewed each decade, blood and choice, keeper's work — and the decade was nearly out. His grandmother had died eight years ago with the ritual half-taught, and Sebastian had told himself there was time, there was always time, the seal was strong, the family had kept it for nine generations, surely it could hold a few years more while he worked up the courage to attempt rites he only half remembered.

The gate hummed.

Above him, muffled by forty-one steps and a false wall and ten years of careful quiet, the shop bell over his front door chimed — absurdly loud in the dark, a small bright sound like a crack running through glass.

At this hour? In this rain?

Sebastian stood a moment longer, torn between the humming dark below and the lamplit ordinary world above, between the duty he had inherited and the life he had hidden inside it. Then he snuffed the lamp, climbed the stairs, and closed the false wall behind him, telling himself — the way he told himself things, the way the whole city agreed to tell itself things — that it was probably nothing.

Probably a customer.

Probably fine.

He unlocked the front door to find a man collapsed against his threshold, soaked in rain and something darker than rain, clutching a folded map to his chest with both hands as though it were the only warm thing left in the world.

"Winterbourne," the man whispered, and smiled with bloody teeth. "Thank the salt. I found you."
