## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

Sebastian Winterbourne had a theory about dawn, and the theory was this: dawn was a liar.

It came creeping through the shutters of his shop in long amber stripes, gilding the dust motes, warming the shelves of luck-knots and fever-charms until everything looked gentler than it was. It made even Corvane look kind—the crooked lanes, the soot-stained eaves, the Warden notices pasted over every third door like scabs. Dawn said, *all is well*. Dawn had been saying that for ten years, and Sebastian had learned to nod along, because what else could you do with a liar you couldn't afford to argue with?

He was counting knots when he heard the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. More of an argument between a fist and his door, conducted by someone who was losing. Sebastian set down the bundle of red thread, crossed the shop floor—past the shelf of dream-salves, past the jar of river-stones labeled *FOR LUCK, PROBABLY*—and eased the bolt back with the practiced caution of a man who had spent a decade being careful.

The man on his step was dying. There was no mistaking it. He stood the way a house stands after fire has been through it: upright out of habit only, everything essential already gone. His coat had once been courier's grey; now it was mostly blood, dark and stiff, blooming from a wound low in his side that Sebastian didn't need to touch to understand. His eyes were the worst part. They were bright. Not with fever—with purpose, the terrible clarity of a man who has decided exactly where his last steps will go.

"You're him," the man rasped. "Winterbourne."

"I'm a charm seller," Sebastian said automatically. "Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Very reasonable prices. I'm afraid we're not open until—"

The man fell forward into his arms.

Sebastian caught him more out of reflex than kindness and staggered back under the weight, kicking the door shut behind them with his heel. The lock clicked. Somewhere above, a shutter creaked—old Mrs. Tallow, awake too early, or perhaps not yet asleep. Sebastian held his breath until her footsteps shuffled away.

"Listen to me." The courier gripped his collar with fingers that left blood-print on the linen. "I ran from the coast road. Three days. They knew I carried it—they always know—" A wet cough bent him nearly double. "You have to take it. You have to *look* at it."

"Take what? Look at what?"

"The map." His hand went inside his coat, moving with the slow, grinding effort of a rusted hinge. "Your family's work. Your family's *failure*."

Every word landed somewhere old and unhealed. Sebastian opened his mouth to deny it—to say *my family sold buttons*, which was technically true of one branch of them and a lie he'd rehearsed for ten years—and then the courier pressed something into his hands and all the rehearsed words died.

A map. Folded small, soft as cloth from handling, stained across one corner with blood that had gone brown as old rust. And there, worked into the vellum itself in thread-of-gold lettering that no ordinary cartographer could afford, a sigil Sebastian knew better than his own heartbeat:

A gate beneath a crescent moon, flanked by two hands.

His family's mark. His father's mark. The mark Sebastian had burned off every document, every ledger, every memory he could reach, the night he'd buried the name Winterbourne and become simply *Sebastian, charm seller, harmless, invisible.*

"No," he heard himself say. It came out very quietly, the way denial does when it knows it's losing. "No, this isn't—I don't know this."

"You do." The courier smiled, and it was the kindest, cruelest thing Sebastian had seen in years. "That's why I came to you. There's no one else *left* to come to."

The shop felt suddenly smaller. The luck-knots on their pegs seemed to lean in and listen. Sebastian looked down at the map in his hands—at the sigil, at the blood—and felt ten years of careful, suffocating quiet crack straight down the middle like river ice in spring.

"Who did this to you?" he asked instead, because grief could wait but bleeding could not. "Wardens?"

"Worse." The courier slid down against the counter, and Sebastian knelt with him, pressing his palm flat over the wound. Warmth welled up between his fingers. Too much warmth. Far too fast. "Hollow things. Thin places. The roads are going soft, charm seller. Soft as bread left in rain. Something on the other side is pushing, and the door—" He seized Sebastian's wrist. His grip should have been weak. It wasn't. Grief and duty make strange strong men. "The anchor is failing."

The word *anchor* went through Sebastian like a nail through the palm.

He knew about anchors. He'd grown up knowing about anchors, kneeling on cold stone beside his father while salt-light poured up through the seams of a sealed door, learning words like *covenant* and *renewal* and *the price must be willing*. He had spent ten years pretending those words were a story told to frighten children, while every night descending the stairs beneath his shop to tend a door that hummed against his fingertips like a sleeping animal dreaming of waking.

"It can't be failing," Sebastian said. "The covenant holds. My family renewed it—we renewed it—it isn't due for years—"

"When was the last renewal?" the courier asked.

And Sebastian, who was good at many kinds of lying, found he could not lie to a dying man. "Ten years ago," he whispered. "The night my father died. He went down alone and came back gray as ash and died within the season, and I never—" His voice failed. He cleared his throat. "I never went down again except to tend it. To keep the seals clean. That's all. That's all I've done."

"Tending isn't renewing." The courier's breath was coming shorter now, each word rationed like water in a drought. "The covenant wants a keeper. A living one. Willing. Bound in blood and choice. Your father gave himself for ten years' grace. Ten years, charm seller. Count them."

Sebastian counted. He had been counting for ten years without knowing what the tally was for.

"And now?" His mouth was dry. "Now what happens?"

"Now the grace runs out." The courier fumbled at his own coat, at the map already in Sebastian's hands. "Nineteen days. Maybe fewer—the roads are thinning faster than the old books guessed. You have to reach the far anchor. Here—" His finger, trembling, traced a line across the vellum. Out of Corvane. Along the dead salt roads, the ones the Edict had emptied and the maps had stopped naming. East, toward the hills where the fog never lifted and the border towns had forgotten their own names. "The far anchor. Where the covenant was first forged. Blood and choice, freely given. Or the door opens, and everything your family kept back comes through."

"What comes through?"

The courier looked at him then with such pity that Sebastian understood the answer before it was spoken, the way you understand a storm by the smell of the air.

"You've felt it humming," the man said. "Under your shop. Don't pretend you haven't. It's been singing to you for months."

It was true. God help him, it was true. For months the gate beneath his floorboards had hummed at odd hours—a low, patient note, like a finger run around the rim of a glass—and Sebastian had told himself it was the pipes, the settling of old foundations, the thousand innocent explanations a frightened man invents at three in the morning. He had oiled the seals. He had recited the maintenance verses his father taught him. He had lied to himself with tremendous discipline.

"The anchor is failing," the courier repeated, softer now. "Say it back to me. Someone has to carry the words true."

"The anchor is failing," Sebastian said. The words tasted of salt.

"Good lad." The courier slumped, and for a moment Sebastian thought it was over—but the man rallied, dragged himself half upright, and gripped Sebastian's face in both cold hands. "One more thing. You won't be enough. Hear me? One keeper alone binds the door shut, and a door bound shut with a living keystone—that's a cage, and cages crack. The old covenant wanted pairs. Two hands on either side of the threshold. Remember the sigil. *Two hands.*"

"I remember."

"Find the other hand." His eyes were going distant, unfocused, though still fixed on Sebastian with terrible earnestness. "There's a witch. Hedge-blood. She's been asking after the map too—asking after her sister, who went through the wrong side of the door years back. If she finds you first, don't run. Running wastes time none of us have."

"A witch," Sebastian repeated faintly. "You want me to trust a witch."

"I want you to trust anyone who'll stand at that door with you," the courier said, "because I'm about to stop standing anywhere at all."

Then he smiled again—that same gentle, awful smile—and lay back against the counter leg, and said, "Thank you for opening the door, keeper," and began to come apart.

There was no other word for it. Sebastian watched, frozen, as the man's edges softened—not fading, not dissolving into smoke or shadow, but into *salt*. Fine white grains, lifting off his skin like frost reversing, drifting upward through the amber dawn light and scattering. His hands went first, then the line of his jaw, then the bright urgent eyes, and last of all his voice, which lingered a moment in the air like the ghost of a bell:

"Nineteen days."

Then a drift of white powder settled on Sebastian's floorboards, among the ordinary dust, indistinguishable from anything at all.

* * *

For a long time Sebastian did not move.

Dawn continued its lying. The light crept further across the floor, warm and gold and indifferent, touching the salt stain, touching the blood, touching the map still clutched in his hands hard enough to crease the vellum. Somewhere outside, Corvane woke up—a cart rattling past, a vendor calling the price of early bread, the whole vast machinery of a city pretending nothing had ever been otherwise.

Sebastian looked at the salt on his floor and thought, absurdly, *I'll need to sweep before Mrs. Tallow comes for her rheumatism charm.* Then he thought, less absurdly, *a man just died in my shop and left no body,* and the thought made him laugh—one short, cracked sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

He unfolded the map properly at last, kneeling in the light.

It was beautiful. That was the worst part. His father's hand was all over it—the precise linework, the little annotations in the margins (*beware the well at Kiln's Cross, it remembers*), the sigil rendered in gold thread that shimmered when he tilted it, as if the ink were alive and pleased to be home. The route wound east along the salt roads, through places Sebastian had only known as bedtime-story warnings: the Hollow Ford, the Glass Fen, Vesperhollow itself, the town that had traded its name away. And at the end of the line, drawn in his father's unmistakable hand, a small door beneath a hill, with two words beneath it in the old cipher that Sebastian could still read because some things refuse to stay buried:

*Far anchor.*

His chest hurt. Not the sharp pain of fear but the deep bruised ache of grief arriving ten years late, finding the door it had been told to wait at finally, finally unlocked. All those years of hiding—of selling luck to people who needed miracles, of descending nightly to wipe salt-scum from a door he pretended meant nothing, of keeping the name Winterbourne folded up small and hidden like a letter never sent—all of it, and the past had simply walked up to his shop at dawn and dissolved into his floorboards.

*Your family's failure,* the courier had called it.

Sebastian sat back on his heels. Below him, beneath the cellar stairs and the false wall and the long worn steps, the Hollow Gate hummed its low patient note. This morning, for the first time, he could swear the note had changed. Not a hum anymore.

Almost a knock.

"Nineteen days," he said aloud, to the empty shop, to the salt, to the liar-dawn. "Right. Well."

He got up, fetched the broom, and swept the courier into a plain clay jar, because whatever else a Winterbourne was, he was not a man who left the dead on the floor. Then he took down his traveling cloak from behind the dream-salves, where it had hung untouched for a decade, and shook off the dust.

He was reaching for his satchel when he heard the bell above his shop door chime—soft, deliberate, wrong. He had locked that door. He was certain he had locked that door.

In the doorway, backlit by the rising sun, stood a young woman with wind-chapped cheeks, hedge-witch eyes the color of storm-water, and a knife held low and easy at her side.

"You're the charm seller," she said. "The courier was supposed to reach me first. So before you decide to lie to me, Winterbourne—I've been following that map's scent since the coast road, and my sister is on the other side of that door. Start talking."

Sebastian looked at her, and at the map, and at the jar of salt, and thought: *nineteen days, and this is how it begins.*
