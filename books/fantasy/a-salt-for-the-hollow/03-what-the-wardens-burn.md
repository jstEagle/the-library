## 3. What the Wardens Burn

Wren had always said you could tell a lot about a fire by how it was lit.

A hearthfire crackled. A hedge-fire hissed and spat, all green and grudging. But a Warden's pyre burned clean and white and utterly silent, the way a lie burns—no smoke, no smell of anything but order. It didn't even bother to be cruel. That was the worst part. The Wardens had refined cruelty into procedure.

She crouched on the roof of the tannery across from Maren's apothecary, her skirts smelling of oak bark and old salt, and watched eleven years of dried herbs, bottled remedies, and Maren's terrible watercolor portraits of her cats go up in light that cast no shadows.

Six Wardens in the street below, grey cloaks, iron pins at their collars shaped like closed doors. One held the ledger open on his forearm, reading charges in a voice as flat as a ledger itself: *unlicensed compounding. Unlicensed divination. Unlicensed kindness.* He'd made that last one up, probably. Or maybe it really was on the list now. The Edict grew new teeth every year.

Maren herself stood in the street in her nightdress, hands bound with cold-iron wire, watching her life burn with an expression Wren knew well—the particular stillness of a woman deciding whether to be furious or practical, and landing, as always, on both.

*Don't look at the roof,* Wren willed her. *Don't you dare look at the roof.*

Maren looked at the roof.

Not directly—she was too good for that—but her chin lifted a half-inch, her eyes tracking past the chimney, past the rain barrel, to the exact spot where Wren's heart stopped and then started again out of spite. And then, because Maren had taught her everything she knew about surviving men with ledgers, the old woman smiled at the Warden captain and said, loudly enough for the whole street:

"You've missed a cupboard."

The captain turned. The Wardens turned. All six of them went back inside the burning building to check a cupboard, because procedure was procedure, and in those forty seconds Maren tilted her head toward the alley behind the tannery—a small, sharp motion, like a bird flicking a seed—and mouthed three words.

*The courier came.*

Then they put her in the wagon, and the white fire ate the rest, and Wren lay flat against the tannery roof with her cheek pressed to cold shingles and let herself shake, just for a moment, where no one could see.

* * *

The courier had come four nights ago.

Wren hadn't seen him—she'd been out gathering moonwort along the canal—but she'd come home to find Maren sitting very straight at the kitchen table with a stranger's wet cloak draped over a chair, and a teacup in front of an empty seat, and Maren's face wearing the expression she wore when she'd heard something she wished she hadn't.

"Sit down, love," Maren had said. "There was a man here looking for your sister's old road."

Nobody said Lark's name anymore. Not since the taking, seven years back—not since the night the Hollow Gate's shadow fell across Vesperhollow and Lark walked out to meet it the way she walked everywhere, too fast, without a coat. The Wardens called it a disappearance. The neighbors called it a tragedy. Maren alone had ever called it what Wren believed it was: a door, and someone taken through it.

"Her old road," Wren had repeated. "That's not a road. That's—" She'd stopped, because saying it aloud felt like handing the Edict a map. "Who was he?"

"A courier. Grey man, salt-burned boots, coughing like his lungs were full of beach." Maren had wrapped her knuckles around her tea, not drinking it. "He asked for the Winterbourne charm-seller in Corvane. Night market. Shop with the blue door. He said he'd been carrying something a long way and it wanted to go home."

"Something belonging to whom?"

"He wouldn't say. He said—" and here Maren had paused, and Wren had learned in that pause everything she needed to dread, "—he said the anchor is failing, and the family that set it has forgotten how to hold it. Then he paid for his tea with a coin from before the Edict and left before I could ask anything sensible."

Wren had gone after him. Of course she'd gone after him—out into the fog with a lantern and Lark's old compass, which pointed not north but *toward*, always toward, ever since the night Lark vanished. She'd tracked him through Corvane's dockside streets by the sound of that cough, that awful wet rattle, until the cough stopped behind a warehouse near the salt-stairs, and she'd found nothing but a smear of something pale on the cobbles that might have been frost or might have been—

Well. She'd told herself it was frost.

Now, lying on the tannery roof while Maren's apothecary collapsed inward with a sound like a sigh, Wren finally let herself think the word she'd been refusing for four days.

Salt. The man had dissolved into salt. She'd found the residue and told herself it was frost, because the alternative meant the stories were true, the ones Lark used to whisper when they were girls sharing a bed in Vesperhollow—the stories about thin places, and doors between realms, and people who crossed them coming apart at the seams like wet paper.

Lark had known things. Lark had always known things, right up until knowing things got her taken.

And now a courier who knew Lark's roads had crawled half-dead across the city to deliver something to a Winterbourne—a name Wren had never heard attached to magic before tonight, though she supposed that was the point of a name like Winterbourne. It sounded like weather. It sounded like nobody.

She sat up. Below, the Wardens were loading their wagon, and the captain—Vane, the neighbors whispered, Captain Ilsa Vane, who could smell a hedge-charm through a brick wall—was questioning Maren through the wagon bars. Wren couldn't hear the words, but she could read the shape of them: *Where did the courier go? Who did he see?*

Maren gave the captain a smile Wren recognized as the one reserved for customers who tried to haggle over fever-draughts, and the wagon rolled away south, toward the Wardenhouse, and Wren was alone on a rooftop with a dead woman's shop burning white below her and exactly one lead left in the world.

*A shop with the blue door,* she thought. *Night market. Corvane.*

"Well then," she said aloud, to the ashes, to Maren, to the ghost of a sister seven years gone. "I suppose I'm going shopping."

* * *

Corvane's night market smelled of fried onions, tallow, and the particular desperation of people trying to feel lucky under an Edict. Wren moved through it with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, doing the thing she did best, which was being forgettable. She'd bought a luck-knot from a stall near the fountain—an honest little thing, braided wrong in a way that told her the seller cared more than he was skilled—and asked, casually, where a person might find charms of better quality.

"Winterbourne's," the stall-keeper had said, jerking his chin down the lane. "Blue door. Bit dear. Bit odd. Harmless, though, if you take my meaning." He'd lowered his voice. "Between us, I think he believes in none of it. Sells the knots, mind, but there's no craft in him. You can tell."

*No craft in him.* Wren had thanked him and thought: *that's either the truth or the best hiding I've ever heard of.*

The blue door stood at the end of Candlewick Lane, wedged between a chandler and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, and it was exactly the kind of door a man would choose if he wanted to be overlooked—narrow, unremarkable, painted a blue so modest it barely committed to being blue. Above it hung a sign: **WINTERBOURNE CHARMS — KNOTS, KEYS, SMALL FORTUNES.**

Wren stood across the lane in the shadow of the button shop and watched the window.

Inside, lamplight moved. A man—tall, dark-haired, sleeves rolled, moving with the careful economy of someone who had practiced being ordinary for so long it had become its own kind of performance—was arranging charm-cords on a display board. He handled each one gently. That was the first thing she noticed. Even if there was no craft in him, he touched the work like it mattered.

The second thing she noticed was the compass.

It sat in her pocket, Lark's compass, brass-cased and needleless—or rather, needled only toward Lark, always, for seven years, steady as grief. Wren had checked it a hundred times a day for seven years, and in all that time it had never so much as trembled.

Standing across the lane from the blue door, it was spinning.

Slowly at first, then faster, the needle whirling like something frantic to be understood, and when Wren pulled it out into the lamplight the needle didn't point toward Vesperhollow at all. It pointed at the shop. At the man in the window. At the floor beneath him, if needles could mean *beneath*, and Wren's whole body went cold, because she knew what lay beneath cities in the old stories. Catacombs. Foundations. Doors.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you great hidden idiot. It's *you*. It's been under *you* the whole time."

The man in the window looked up.

Later, Wren would replay this moment many times, and in most versions she would handle it differently—approach the door openly, knock, say something disarming about luck-knots. In the actual version, the following happened: the man saw her standing in the shadows holding a wildly spinning compass, his face went through surprise, recognition, and naked fear in the space of a heartbeat, and he blew out his lamp.

Which confirmed everything, obviously. Honest charm-sellers did not blow out lamps when spotted by potential customers.

Wren was crossing the lane before she'd decided to, hammering on the blue door with the side of her fist. "I know you're in there! I know what the courier brought you—I know about the anchor!"

Silence. Then, muffled, cautious, a voice she would spend the rest of her life unable to describe without smiling despite herself: "I'm closed."

"You're not closed, your lamp just went out *very suddenly*—"

"I keep irregular hours."

"It's the night market, everyone keeps irregular hours!" She pressed her palm flat against the door, and through the wood she felt it—that hum, low and vast and wrong, like a hymn sung underwater. The same hum Lark had described once, wide-eyed, the week before she was taken. Wren's throat tightened. "Please," she said, and her rage cracked straight down the middle into something rawer. "My sister went through that door seven years ago. I think your family built it. I think a dying man just told you it's failing, and I think you're standing there deciding whether to run."

A long silence. Through the gap under the door, lamplight bloomed again—hesitant, then steadying.

"The courier," the voice said at last, quieter. "You knew him?"

"He came to my mentor. She's in a Warden wagon right now because of it, and her shop is ash." Wren leaned her forehead against the blue wood. "So yes. I knew him. And whatever he gave you, I'm coming in whether you open this door or I pick this charming, unremarkable lock."

Another pause. Then, with the weary tone of a man whose decade of careful hiding had just developed a leak:

"...The lock is warded."

"*Everything* is warded with you people, isn't it," Wren muttered, and reached for her knife.
