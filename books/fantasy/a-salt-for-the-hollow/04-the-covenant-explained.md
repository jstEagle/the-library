## 4. The Covenant Explained

The raid began, as all the worst nights of Sebastian's life did, with a knock that was far too polite.

"Shop's closed," he called through the door, already moving to snuff the lamp over the workbench. Below his feet, faint as a heartbeat, the Hollow Gate hummed. It had not stopped humming for three days.

"Open in the name of the Edict," said the door, in the voice of a Warden who did not believe in closed shops.

Sebastian doused the lamp, swept the last luck-knot into the drawer where he kept his sins, and was reaching for the loose flagstone behind the counter when the door came in anyway—wood splintering, boots first, then lanterns, then four grey coats of the Warden's Vigil filling his little shop like smoke.

"Sebastian Winterbourne," said the tall one, whose name Sebastian had spent a decade learning not to know. "Licensed purveyor of trinkets. We've had a report."

"A report of what? My prices?" He kept his voice light, his hands loose. Ten years of practice: be the dull man, the charm-seller, the one whose paperwork is always in order. He pulled the ledger from under the counter and offered it with the smile of a man who has nothing to hide and would very much like to discuss interest rates. "I'll warn you, my margins are tragic."

The tall Warden ignored the ledger. He was looking at the floor. At the flagstone behind the counter, which was, Sebastian realized with a slow, cold horror, *glowing*.

The gate beneath his shop had never glowed before.

"Step away from the counter, sir."

Sebastian did not step away from the counter. He stepped *through* the space behind it, yanked the flagstone, and dropped into the dark as the first binding-net came down where his head had been.

He fell farther than he meant to. He always did. Ten years of tending a door between worlds and he had still never gotten the hang of the ladder.

* * *

He landed in the catacombs in a shower of old dust and newer profanity, and found he was not alone.

A woman crouched at the tunnel's mouth with a knife in one hand and a fistful of something that smoked green in the other, and she looked, in the lantern-glow leaking down from the shop above, like every story his mother had ever told him about hedge-witches: sharp-eyed, sharp-elbowed, and dressed like the weather had personally wronged her.

"Don't," she said, before he could speak.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to say something charming. Charm-sellers always are." She nodded upward, at the boots stamping across his ceiling. "Wardens. Four?"

"Five. The polite one stays outside."

"They always send a polite one." She rose slowly, knife still out, and looked him over the way one looks over a horse one suspects of lying about its age. "You're Winterbourne."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped coin. "I'm the charm-seller. Sebastian. No family name, on the paperwork. The Edict's very keen on paperwork."

"The dying man in my mentor's shop had paperwork too. A map. With a sigil on it—a sun split by a door." Her eyes didn't leave his. "The same sigil that was branded into the box under your counter, which I saw through the window last Tuesday, because I've been watching this shop for a week."

"You've been—"

"Watching. Yes. You hum to the floorboards, did you know that? Every night, same hour. You press your hand flat against the stone like it's a horse that spooks." She tilted her head. "I grew up with a sister who did the same thing to the well-cover. I know what tending looks like."

Above them, something heavy went over. The Wardens were not being gentle with his inventory. Sebastian felt each crash like a personal insult—he'd *arranged* those shelves.

"They'll find the trapdoor," the witch said.

"They won't. It's warded to look like a wall to anyone with Edict-trained senses. Official magic-haters have very official blind spots." He hesitated, then held out his hand, palm up, the way you offer it to a dog you'd like to trust you. "Sebastian. Apparently Winterbourne. And you are?"

She looked at his hand for a long moment. Then she took it—her grip dry and strong, and a spark of green light jumping between their palms that made them both flinch.

"Wren," she said, sounding almost as surprised as he felt. "And I think your floor just introduced us."

* * *

They went deeper, because staying was dying and going up was worse. Sebastian lit no lamp; the catacombs under Corvane had been his second home for ten years, and the stone itself seemed to lend a grey half-light to anyone the gate considered friendly. Wren walked close behind him, knife sheathed but hand never far from it, and the green smoke-thing she'd held upstairs turned out to be a small jar of moths, which she released one at a time to check for warding-snares. It was, he had to admit, extremely practical, and he told her so.

"Charm-sellers flatter," she said.

"Hedge-witches bite."

"Only when provoked."

They came at last to the door.

Not the Hollow Gate itself—that lay deeper, behind three more doors and a corridor that only existed on certain days of the week—but the antechamber his family had built when Corvane was young and the Edict was a nightmare not yet dreamed. A round room of black stone, and set into its far wall, a relief carved the height of two men: a sun split down the middle by a standing door, and beneath it, in the old tongue, the words Sebastian had been reading since before he could read anything else.

*We held the door so you would not have to.*

Wren stopped dead. Her hand came off her knife, which frightened him more than the knife had.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's the sigil from the map. That's—" She turned, and in the grey light her face had gone very young. "My sister drew that. Lark. Before they took her. She drew it on everything, on her wrists, on the well-cover, and I thought it was just a game, some hedge-superstition, and she *knew*, she knew about this, and she never told me—"

"Wren."

"She went through a door, Sebastian. Seven years ago. A door in a barrow-mound that shouldn't have been there, and I was twelve and I *hid*, and the Wardens came and burned our camp and my mother said Lark was dead, and I believed her because believing was easier than—" She stopped. Breathed. He watched her put herself back together the way she'd probably been doing since she was twelve, piece by piece, alone.

"The man who died in my mentor's shop," she said, steadier now, "was carrying your family's map. He said the anchor was failing. So you're going to tell me what that means, charm-seller, and you're going to tell me all of it, because I have spent seven years not knowing, and I am *done* with not knowing."

So Sebastian told her.

He told it badly at first—the way you tell anything you've rehearsed in secret for ten years, too fast, too flat—and then he made himself slow down, because she deserved better than a recitation. He sat on the cold steps of the antechamber, and she sat one step above him, and the carved door watched them both with its split-sun patience.

"My family were keepers," he said. "Not wizards. The Edict never touched us because there was nothing to burn—no grimoires, no circles, just a door and a duty. Nine generations ago, the Winterbournes found the Hollow Gate already open, and things coming through it that unmade whatever they touched. Not monsters, exactly. *Absences*. And my many-times-great-grandmother—Maren, her name was—she brokered a covenant. Blood and choice, freely given, on both sides. It sealed the gate. It held."

"And the price?"

"The price is that it must be renewed. Every ten years, a Winterbourne comes down here and renews it. A drop of blood, a spoken vow, freely given. My father did it before me. My mother before him." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I did it four years ago. It should have held until next year. But the courier said the anchor is failing, and the gate's been humming for three days, and last night it *glowed*, Wren. Upstairs. In front of Wardens."

"Four years ago," Wren said quietly. "What happened four years ago?"

And there it was. The thing he had not said aloud in a decade, the stone in his boot he'd learned to walk on.

"My parents died. Both of them, the same winter. Fever, the healers said. And I came down here and I renewed the covenant alone, because I was the only Winterbourne left, and it took the blood, and it took the vow, and it—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "It took something else. I've never been able to name it. But I've been tired ever since. Tired in a way sleep doesn't touch. I thought that was grief."

"It wasn't grief," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"No. I don't think it was grief."

She was silent a long moment. Above them, muffled by ten feet of stone, the Wardens were still tearing apart his shop, his careful, harmless, ten-year life. He found he could almost laugh at it. He'd hidden so well. He'd hidden so *well*.

"Nineteen days," he said at last. "That's what the map says. The renewal date isn't next year. The covenant is unraveling, and it comes due in nineteen days, at the far anchor—the other end of the gate, wherever that is. And I have to get there, and I have to renew it, and I don't know what it will take from me this time, because the last time it took, it took *two* Winterbournes' worth and I only have one of me."

The moths had all been released now. They circled the carved door in a slow, green-tinged spiral, and Wren watched them with an expression he was beginning to recognize as her thinking-face, which was not reassuring for anyone.

"Freely given," she said. "Blood and choice."

"That's the old words, yes."

"Then we'd better make sure you're very free when you get there." She stood, and offered him her hand, and the green spark jumped between their palms again, warmer this time, like something being introduced twice. "Because I'm coming with you, Sebastian Winterbourne. Not for you. For Lark. If your door took my sister, your door is going to help me get her back."

"That's not how the covenant—"

"I don't care how the covenant works. I care how *I* work, and I work stubbornly." She pulled him to his feet. "Nineteen days. Salt roads, your map says? I know the salt roads. I ran them for two years after the Edict. There's a woman in Millbrook who owes me a favor, and a bridge that only exists at dusk, and at least three thin places where things with no faces like to wait for travelers." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, but it was an honest one, and after ten years of polite, hidden, careful living, honest felt like sunlight on a burn.

"You're enjoying this," he said.

"I'm *terrified*," she said cheerfully. "But my sister taught me that terror and purpose feel almost exactly the same in the dark, so you learn to pick the second one." She turned toward the deeper tunnels, where the catacombs ran out beneath the city walls toward the old salt roads and the world beyond them. "Come on, keeper. Nineteen days."

They had gone perhaps fifty paces when the humming beneath their feet changed.

It had been a hum for three days. Now it was a pulse. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried up through nine generations of stone, came a sound Sebastian had only ever heard in his nightmares:

A knock.

From the other side.

* * *

They stood in the dark, hand in hand, and did not let go.

"Nineteen days," Wren said again, but her voice had gone thin.

"Wren," Sebastian said, watching the carved door's split sun seem to brighten, "I don't think we have nineteen days anymore."
