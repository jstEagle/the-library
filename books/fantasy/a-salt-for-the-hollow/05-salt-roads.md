## 5. Salt Roads

The map, it turned out, was a liar of the most elegant kind.

Sebastian had known it within the first day. The courier's ink showed a straight road running west from Corvane's walls, neat as a ledger line, marked with waystations every few miles. The reality beneath their boots was a road of cracked white salt-pan, sun-bleached and abandoned, where the waystations had been ruins for longer than either of them had been alive. The road itself had a habit of curving when the map said it shouldn't, as though the land had grown since the ink dried and hadn't bothered to tell anyone.

"Your family made this map," Wren said on the second morning, holding it upside down, then right side up, then at an angle to the sun as if honesty might be a matter of perspective.

"My family made the *covenant*," Sebastian said. "The map is newer. My father's hand, I think. He always did draw what he wished the world looked like rather than what it did. It's why my mother handled the household accounts."

Wren snorted, and the sound surprised her—Sebastian could see it surprise her, the way a person is startled by their own laugh in a place where laughter has no business being. She folded the map away and walked on.

They walked. The salt crunched. The horizon stayed exactly where it was, the way horizons do, which Sebastian had always considered a small, reliable mercy of the world.

By the third day he had revised his opinion of horizons entirely.

* * *

It began with the birds. Or rather, with the absence of them, which is a subtler thing and easier to miss until it is complete. The salt flats had hosted a scruffy population of gulls and pipits who lived off whatever salt-flies bred in the pans. On the third morning there were none. On the third afternoon, the light went strange—too white, the shadows falling in directions Sebastian kept having to check twice.

"Thin place," Wren said. She had stopped walking without appearing to stop; one moment she was beside him, the next she was a statue with her hand out, fingers spread, reading the air the way a blind man reads a page.

"Thin meaning what, precisely?"

"Meaning the wall between here and there has been rubbed thin, like the elbow of an old coat." Her eyes moved without her head moving. "Something's been leaning on it from the other side."

"Leaning."

"Sebastian." She turned and looked at him, and there was no fear in her face, which frightened him more than fear would have. "You've spent ten years guarding a door. You must know what's on the other side of it."

"I know what the old books say is on the other side of it," he said. "Which is mostly diagrams."

"Diagrams," she repeated.

"My family were not poets, Wren. We were accountants of the uncanny. There are eleven volumes of *Observations on the Hollow*, and nine of them are tables."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—the almost-laugh again, briefer this time, gone before it could commit to anything. Then the light went wrong in a different way, and there was no room in the world for humor at all.

* * *

They came out of the ground the way frost comes through a floorboard: not arriving, but *revealing*—as if they had always been there and the salt had merely been politely ignoring them. Three shapes, or the memory of three shapes, man-high and wrong in the proportions, with faces like unfinished sculptures. Their eyes were the worst part. Not empty—Sebastian had expected empty, had braced for empty—but hollow, in the way a well is hollow: deep, and waiting for something to be dropped in.

"Hollow-eyed," Wren breathed. "The stories were true."

"Which stories?"

"All of them, apparently."

The first one lunged. Sebastian, who had spent a decade selling luck-knots and had never once been in a fight, did the only thing his body knew how to do: he threw his satchel at it. The satchel, containing fever-charms, two luck-knots, a tin of tea, and his entire remaining dignity, burst open midair in a small explosion of knotted string and dried herbs.

The hollow thing stopped. It looked down at the scattered charms. And then, horribly, it *reached* for them—fingers like candle-wax closing around a fever-charm, drawing it toward that well-deep face as if it meant to drink the little warmth out of it.

"Oh, you don't," Wren said.

She moved past Sebastian in a way that was not fast, exactly. It was more that the space between them decided to be smaller. Her hand came up, and wildness came off her like heat-shimmer—the air bent, the salt hissed, and every scattered charm in a six-foot circle stood up on its strings and turned to face the hollow things like a small knotted militia.

"*Go home,*" she said, and the words were not words. They were older than words. The salt road shivered.

The hollow things went home. There was no other way to describe it. They folded down into the ground like paper into water, and the salt closed over them, and the light straightened itself out, and the birds—Sebastian could have sworn—came back within the hour.

He sat down hard on the white road, next to his exploded satchel, and began, with slightly shaking hands, to gather up his charms.

"You threw your bag at it," Wren said.

"It seemed like the thing to do at the time."

"You threw your *bag*."

"It was either that or the tea," Sebastian said, holding up the tin, which had survived. "And frankly I wasn't prepared to lose the tea."

She laughed then. Properly, helplessly, sitting down beside him in the salt and laughing until she had to wipe her eyes, and Sebastian found that he was laughing too, and that somewhere in the last ten seconds the terror had turned into something he didn't have a name for yet but suspected the poets did, and his family's eleven volumes almost certainly did not.

* * *

The Wardens found them at dusk on the fifth day.

Sebastian smelled them first—lamp-oil and iron and the particular scorched smell of warden-cloaks, which were treated with something that made hedge-magic curdle in the air. He caught Wren's arm and pulled them both off the road into the ribs of a dead waystation, and they crouched in the shadow of its fallen arch while six riders drew up on the salt below.

"Two travelers, westbound," said the sergeant, dismounting. "Tracks say so. Captain wants anyone on the salt roads brought in. Special orders out of Corvane."

"Special orders," said another rider, and spat. "Whole city's gone mad since the charm-seller vanished. Vane's got half the garrison hunting one man and a hedge-witch."

Sebastian felt Wren go rigid beside him. He put two fingers gently against her wrist. *Not yet. Not for this.* She didn't relax, but she didn't move, and he counted that as one of the great victories of his life.

The problem was the horses. One of them—a gray mare with the deep unhappiness common to all government horses—kept turning her head toward the waystation. Wardens, Sebastian knew from a decade of careful observation, were trained to notice everything except the things they'd been ordered not to believe in, and a horse that wouldn't settle was exactly the kind of thing they'd been ordered not to believe in.

The sergeant was walking toward the arch.

Sebastian did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat: six armed men, one hedge-witch who could fight but whose magic would light her up like a festival lantern for every scrying-glass in a hundred miles, and a covenant with thirteen days left on it that could not be reforged from a Warden cell.

So he stood up, stepped out of the shadow, and said, in his best shopkeeper's voice, "Good evening. Can I interest anyone in a luck-knot?"

* * *

Later—much later, when the moon was up and the salt glowed like a second, paler moon beneath it—Wren demanded the full accounting.

"You *walked up to them*."

"I walked up to them."

"You, the man who threw his satchel at a hollow and hid from a *cat* in chapter three of your own life—"

"That cat was enormous."

"—you walked up to six Wardens and sold them *charms*."

"Outlawed charms," Sebastian corrected, with some pride. "I sold them outlawed charms. Fever-charms, specifically, because the sergeant had a cold, and luck-knots for the whole patrol, because they looked like they needed them. And then, while they were all examining their purchases and feeling vaguely criminal about it, you—" he gestured at her "—did whatever you did to the gray mare."

Wren looked away, but not before he saw the smile. "I told her there was a better stable two miles north. With oats."

"You *lied to a horse*."

"I *persuaded* a horse. There's a difference. It's a matter of professional ethics." She pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them, and for a while they just sat in the moonlight, two fugitives on a dead road, sharing the tea he hadn't lost.

"Sebastian." Her voice had changed—quieter, the humor set down carefully like a tool she meant to come back for. "Back there. When the sergeant was walking toward us. You stepped *out*. You made yourself the target. You didn't even hesitate."

"I hesitated enormously. I did all my hesitating in advance, in the archway. It's a technique."

"Sebastian."

He sighed, and looked out at the salt, because it was easier than looking at her. "Ten years I've been careful. Ten years of being small and harmless and selling string to people who needed string. And it turns out that when it mattered, the thing I'm actually for is standing between something dangerous and someone who matters." He shrugged, embarrassed. "It was a very efficient decade. I got all the cowardice done early."

Wren said nothing for a long moment. Then she reached over and took the tea tin from his hands, poured the last of it into his cup rather than her own, and pressed the cup back into his fingers with a look that said, in the language of shared tea, everything she was not prepared to say in the language of words.

"The far anchor," she said. "How far?"

"By the map? Three more days. By the map's father?" He turned the courier's bloodstained parchment toward the moonlight. "Could be anything."

Because there, in the corner where his father's careful hand had drawn the waystations, was a mark he had missed—a small circle, and inside it, the split sun of the Winterbourne sigil, and beneath it, in ink that was darker than the rest, newer than the rest, and not, he was now quite certain, his father's:

*The anchor is not where we left it. It walks.*

* * *

Sebastian read the line three times. Then he read it aloud, because some things are too absurd to be believed silently, and Wren took the map from him and read it a fourth time, as if the words might improve with repetition.

"Walks," she said.

"Walks."

"Sebastian. How does an anchor *walk*?"

He looked west, where the salt road ran on into the dark, and where—far off, at the very limit of sight, in a place where there should have been nothing at all—there was a light. Small. Steady. And, he realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the night air, *moving*.

Coming toward them.
