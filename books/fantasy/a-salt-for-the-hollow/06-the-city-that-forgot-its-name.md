## 6. The City That Forgot Its Name

The map said Vesperhollow was three days past the last salt road, and the map, Wren decided somewhere in the second day of walking through fog that tasted faintly of copper and old prayers, was a liar. It said nothing about the way the world went soft at the edges out here, or how the sun hung low and bruised-colored like it couldn't quite remember whether it was supposed to rise or set.

"Is it supposed to look like that?" Sebastian asked, for the fourth time.

"No," she said, for the fourth time, and kept walking.

He'd been surprisingly good company for a man who sold luck-knots for a living and had apparently spent ten years lying to everyone he met, including himself. He hummed when he was nervous, which was often. He carried the map against his chest like it might hatch. And he asked questions—endless questions—but they were good ones, the kind that made her think instead of making her want to throw something at him, which put him well ahead of most people she'd traveled with.

Now the questions stopped, because Vesperhollow rose out of the fog all at once, the way a thought rises when you've been trying not to think it.

It was a town, or it had been. Half of it stood as any border town might: crooked rooftops, a leaning bell tower, laundry strung between windows that flapped in a wind Wren couldn't feel on her own face. The other half—the far half, the half nearest where the horizon should have been—was *elsewhere*. Streets continued past the town's edge into a country of pale grass under a sky the color of milk gone off, and the houses there were still houses, still had doors and chimneys, but everything about them was slightly wrong, slightly faded, like a painting left too long in the sun. People moved along those streets. Or shapes that had been people.

"They're not dead," Wren said quietly. She didn't know how she knew, but she did, the way you know a song before you place it. "They're not dead and they're not alive. They're *remembered*."

Sebastian's hand found hers without either of them deciding it should. She let it stay.

"The taken souls," he murmured. "From the gate."

"Some of them." Her throat tightened. "Maybe one of them."

They walked in. The living half of Vesperhollow smelled of woodsmoke and bread, which seemed obscenely ordinary. A woman selling apples from a cart looked up at them with eyes gone gray at the rims and said, "You'll want the Saltrow Inn, loves, though I don't recall what street it's on, nor indeed what we called this street." She frowned, searching herself. "I don't recall what we called the town."

"You don't?" Sebastian asked gently.

"It had a name once." The woman handed them each an apple, unasked, unpaid. "Everyone here gave pieces of themselves away, over the years. Little bits. Names first. Then birthdays. Then faces, for some." She smiled, warm and vacant. "It keeps the fog from taking the rest. Mostly."

Wren ate the apple because refusing felt dangerous. It tasted like every apple she'd ever eaten, all at once, which was somehow worse than tasting like nothing.

* * *

The Saltrow Inn, when they found it by asking three people who each remembered a different part of its location, was run by a broad, cheerful man named Odo who remembered his own name perfectly and was enormously proud of it. "Odo," he told them twice, pressing bowls of stew on them. "Never given a bit of myself away. Stubborn as a mule about it. My wife says—" He paused. His smile flickered, just for a moment, like a lamp in wind. "My wife says. Someone says. There was someone who said things."

Wren's chest ached. She looked away and found Sebastian watching her with an expression she was beginning to know: the one where he wanted to fix something and knew he couldn't.

"We're looking for someone," she said. "A woman. Taken years ago. Lark. She'd have come through here—or stayed here. They say the taken linger."

Odo's face did something complicated. "Memory-ghosts don't come to the inn," he said. "But the church on the hill—what's left of it—they gather there. Where the two halves of the town touch." He leaned close, lowering his voice as if the fog itself might be listening. "Mind yourselves, loves. The ghosts remember more than we do. That's the cruelty of it. They remember *everything*, and none of it is theirs anymore."

* * *

The church on the hill had lost its roof and gained something better: sky, both skies at once, the bruised one and the milk-pale one meeting in a seam above the broken altar like two pages pressed together. In the pews sat the memory-ghosts.

They were beautiful, Wren thought, and hated herself for thinking it. Translucent figures woven from light and fog, each one flickering with fragments—a woman laughing at some joke no one else could hear, a boy tracing letters on a pew that wasn't there, an old man humming a lullaby in a language that predated Corvane. They weren't suffering, exactly. They were *echoing*. Living their brightest moments over and over, worn smooth as river stones.

And then, near the front, a figure turned around.

She was younger than Wren remembered, which was impossible, and older, which was also impossible. Red hair like their mother's. A smudge of ink on her jaw that Wren would have known anywhere, because Lark had always gotten ink on her jaw when she wrote letters, every letter, without fail, and Wren had teased her about it for twenty years.

"Lark," Wren said. It came out very small.

The ghost's whole body brightened. "*Ren.*" Lark crossed the church in three steps that didn't disturb a single speck of dust and stopped just short of touching her, hands hovering, trembling. "Oh, you absolute idiot. You found me. You actually—you shouldn't be here. Do you know what this place does? Do you know what it takes?"

"I know what it took." Wren reached out. Her fingers passed through her sister's wrist like reaching into cold smoke, and the grief of it nearly buckled her knees. "Seven years, Lark. Seven years I looked."

"I know. I watched you look. We see out, sometimes. When the fog thins." Lark's laugh cracked in the middle. "You got tall. You got *angry*. Look at you, you're all sharp edges and righteous fury, Mum would be so proud and so exhausted."

Behind them, Sebastian cleared his throat softly. Lark turned, and her expression shifted into something shrewd and knowing.

"And you're a Winterbourne." She said it like a diagnosis. "I can smell the covenant on you. Old blood, old oaths. You're the keeper's boy."

"I'm the keeper," Sebastian said, and there was steel under the mildness that surprised Wren, still, even now. "Or I'm trying to be. The anchor's failing. I've come to reforge it."

Lark went very still. Ghosts, Wren realized, could go stiller than the living; stillness was a language they spoke fluently.

"Reforge it," Lark repeated. "With what?"

"My blood. My choice. The old way."

"No." Lark said it flatly, immediately, and Wren heard in it seven years of watching from behind glass. "No, that's not—that's not how it works anymore, Ren, tell him. Tell him what the covenant costs."

"The price is a life freely given," Wren said slowly. "That's what the old texts—"

"That's what the old texts *say*." Lark's form flickered, brightening with emotion, and the nearest memory-ghosts turned toward her like flowers toward sun. "I've been on the other side of this door for seven years, Winterbourne. I've seen what comes through when it weakens. And I've heard the covenant speak—it speaks, down in the deep places, it has a voice like grinding salt—and the price isn't a death. Anyone can die. Dying is cheap. The price is a life. *Given.* All of it. Not your death offered at the threshold—your *living*, poured out year after year after year, until there's nothing left but a keeper-shaped hole wearing your name. That's what happened to your father. That's what happened to his father. You'd be giving it everything, forever, and calling it duty."

The silence rang. Somewhere below, the town bell tolled a hour that Wren suspected didn't exist.

Sebastian's face had gone white, but steady. "Then it will take my life freely given, and I'll pay it gladly, because the alternative is that door tearing open and everything on the other side pouring through into Corvane—"

"*Everything?*" Lark laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Winterbourne, you sweet, doomed fool. Things aren't trying to get *out*. The hollow tide doesn't want your world. It wants *us*. It collects. Every soul the old keepers fed it to seal the door, every soul it's pulled through thin places since—it keeps them. It *wears* them." She gestured at the pews, at the flickering congregation. "These aren't strays. These are its collection. And if you reforge the covenant the old way, you feed it again. One more soul added to the hoard. Maybe two, if you're both standing close enough when it bites."

Wren felt the ground tilt under certainties she'd been carrying for nineteen days. "Then what do we do? Let it fail?"

"I don't know." For a moment Lark looked simply like a tired girl with ink on her jaw. "But I know you can't pay a price you don't understand. And I know—" her voice dropped, urgent, ghost-thin, "—that the Wardens are coming. Three days behind you, maybe less. The captain. Vane. She's been to Vesperhollow before, Ren. She knows what's here. And whatever bargain she offers you, whatever truth she tells you—remember that the Wardens burned my teacher's shop and called it cleansing, and ask yourself what a woman like that wants with a door between worlds."

Fog was seeping through the broken walls now, thickening, and the memory-ghosts were dimming one by one like candles being pinched out. Lark's edges frayed.

"Go," she said quickly. "The inn. Stay at Odo's, he remembers enough to keep you safe till morning. And Ren—" She caught at Wren's sleeve, and this time, impossibly, her fingers held, cold and brief as frost. "Whatever you're to each other—I saw how he looks at you, and saints, girl, how you look at him—if the covenant demands a life, don't let it be a life *either* of you gives alone. Promise me. Promise me you'll be stupid about it together."

"I promise," Wren said, and meant it in the bone-deep way she meant almost nothing else.

Lark smiled—her sister's smile, whole and warm and infuriating—and dissolved into the fog like sugar into tea.

* * *

They walked back down through the town in silence. The living half had lit its lamps; the dead half glowed with its own pale light, and where the two halves met, the seam between worlds shimmered like heat-haze. Sebastian stopped there, at the boundary, and stared across at the pale country beyond.

"A life freely given," he said at last. "Not a death. A life."

"Yes."

"My father used to sing while he worked, you know. Down in the cellar, tending the gate. I thought he loved it." Sebastian's voice was quiet and terribly even. "He was singing so we wouldn't hear it taking him apart."

Wren took his hand. His fingers were cold.

"So we find another way," she said. "Lark's right—we don't pay prices we don't understand. We learn the covenant's true shape first. And we do it together, or not at all."

He looked at her then, and there was something raw and grateful in it that made her heart do an undignified sort of lurch, which she intended to examine never.

"Wren," he said. "There's something I haven't told you. About why Vane has hunted me for ten years. About what my family actually did to forge the first covenant, and what the Wardens' founders took from us in exchange for their silence. If we're going to stand in front of that door together—"

Down the hill, a bell began to ring—not the town's broken bell, but a hard, urgent clanging from the direction of the salt road. Lanterns bloomed in the fog, dozens of them, moving in formation.

Odo came puffing up the lane, breathless, his proud name clutched tight against the fog.

"Soldiers," he gasped. "Gray cloaks. Iron badges. Asking after a charm-seller and a hedge-witch—and their captain's already at my door."
