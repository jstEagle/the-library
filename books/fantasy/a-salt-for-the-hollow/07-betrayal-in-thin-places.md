## 7. Betrayal in Thin Places

The trouble with being hunted for ten years, Sebastian had learned, was that you eventually developed a sixth sense for it—a prickle at the nape of the neck, like a charm gone slightly wrong. He felt it now, in the ruins of a waystation inn on the salt road outside Vesperhollow, while Wren dozed against his shoulder with her boots still on and her fingers still curled around a knife.

He felt it, and he did not wake her fast enough.

"Evening, Winterbourne."

Ilsa Vane stood in the doorway where the door used to be, silhouetted against a sky the color of old pewter. She had not changed much in ten years—same iron-gray braid, same Warden's coat with the collar turned up against the salt wind, same expression of a woman who had read every book on duty ever written and found them all too sentimental. The only difference was the gray at her temples, which Sebastian suspected he had personally contributed to.

Behind her, four Wardens fanned out through the ruined common room, crossbows levelled with the bored competence of people who had done this many times.

Sebastian's heart performed a maneuver that would have gotten a lesser organ arrested. "Captain Vane," he said, keeping his voice pleasant, because panic was a currency he refused to spend in front of her. "You're a long way from Corvane. I'd offer you tea, but the kettle appears to have been looted. Possibly by you."

"Still making jokes." She stepped over a fallen beam, her boots crunching on salt-crusted plaster. "Ten years, and you still think charm is a substitute for honesty. It's why you were such an easy mark to track. A man who sells luck-knots that actually work doesn't stay invisible. He stays *memorable*."

Wren stirred against his shoulder. He felt her go from asleep to awake to *armed* in the space of one breath, the way hedge-witches apparently could, and her knife slid silently out of its sheath beneath the fold of her cloak.

"Don't," Sebastian murmured.

"I'm counting," she murmured back. "Four crossbows. Two of them are badly maintained. The leftmost man is favoring his knee."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Vane stopped six feet away and looked down at him, and here was the thing about Ilsa Vane that Sebastian had never managed to explain to himself: she didn't look like a zealot. The Wardens who burned apothecaries and smashed charm-stalls had a light in their eyes, a righteous fever. Vane's eyes were tired. They had been tired for as long as he'd known her, which was longer than either of them liked to admit.

"You've been busy," she said. "Corvane to the salt roads in eleven days, moving only at night, avoiding every checkpoint between here and the border. That takes either excellent tradecraft or excellent desperation. I've been assuming both." Her gaze flicked to Wren, then back. "And now you're headed for Vesperhollow, which no sane person enters, during a salt-storm season, which no sane person travels in. So let's skip the part where you lie to me, Sebastian. We're both too old and too tired."

"The kettle really is looted," Sebastian said.

Vane sighed. It was a genuine sigh, worn smooth by repetition, and something in it made the hair on Sebastian's arms stand up—not fear exactly, but the particular dread of hearing a door close that you hadn't known was open.

"Bind their wrists," she said to her Wardens. "Gently. If anyone marks them, I'll write it up as your incompetence and dock your pay."

* * *

They sat them on what remained of the inn's hearthstone, which was the warmest place in the ruin and therefore, Sebastian supposed, a courtesy. His wrists were bound in front of him with waxed cord, professional and painless. Wren's were bound behind her back, which she took as a personal insult and communicated via a stare that would have curdled milk at forty paces.

Vane dismissed her Wardens to the outer wall—"and if anything comes out of the fog, you shoot it, and then you come get me *before* you start celebrating"—and then she crouched in front of Sebastian, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the salt-burn chapping her knuckles.

"Do you know what I found in the courier's saddlebag?" she said quietly. "Three weeks ago. Before he died."

Sebastian kept his face still. Beside him, Wren had gone very carefully motionless—the stillness of someone listening with her whole skeleton.

"A map," Vane continued. "Bloodstained. Bearing a sigil I haven't seen since I burned the last Winterbourne ledger twenty years ago, back when I was a lieutenant following orders I didn't fully understand." She tilted her head. "A gate sigil, Sebastian. A *door*. And when I traced the courier's route backward, do you know what I found at the end of it? A charm shop. Run by a man whose family name appears in every sealed record my order keeps about the Hollow Gate."

"You could have arrested me any time in ten years," Sebastian said. "You inspected my shop twice. You bought a fever-charm off me once and complained it was overpriced."

"It *was* overpriced." Almost, almost, a smile. Then it was gone. "I didn't arrest you because I wanted to know what you knew. And now I know enough." She rose, paced two steps, turned back. Behind her, through the empty window frame, the fog off the salt flats was doing something fog should not do—it moved in slow spirals, like water finding a drain. "The Edict says magic is outlawed because magic is chaos. That's the sermon. But you and I both know the Edict was written by men who had seen what came out of the Hollow Gate in the last days of the old covenant, and who decided that the safest response to a monster behind a door was to pretend there was no door."

"So you *do* know."

"I know that three villages near the border stopped reporting last month. I know that my own Wardens are deserting rather than patrol the thin places. I know that whatever covenant held that gate shut is failing, and I know—" her voice dropped, and for one unguarded moment she sounded less like a captain than like a woman standing in a burning house, "—that the order I gave my life to has decided the answer is to seal it *permanently*, by any means, and that the means they've chosen requires a witch's blood freely given at the threshold."

The silence after that was very long, and very cold.

Sebastian became aware of Wren breathing beside him—slow, deliberate breaths, the kind you take so you don't scream. He thought of Lark. He thought of the memory-ghosts in Vesperhollow, and of the price the covenant demanded, and of how he had spent nineteen days telling himself he would pay it alone so that no one else would have to.

"They'll use Wren," he said flatly.

"They'll use whoever fits," Vane said. "A witch of the other bloodline, unbound, unprotected, walking straight toward the anchor with a Winterbourne. You two have been considerate enough to deliver yourselves." She crouched again, and now her voice went low and even, the voice of a woman laying cards on a table she intended to win. "So here is my bargain, Winterbourne, and I'll only make it once. Give the gate to us. Cooperate. Tell my order everything—the covenant, the anchors, the reforging rite—and let the Wardens control what happens at that threshold. In exchange, I keep the ritual away from her. They'll want a sacrifice; I'll give them a prisoner instead. Chains, not blood. She lives. You live. The gate gets sealed under Warden authority, properly, with an army at its back instead of two fugitives and a prayer."

"And the souls on the other side?" Wren said.

It was the first thing she'd said aloud, and her voice came out scraped raw. Vane looked at her for a long moment.

"Gone," she said simply. "Whatever's beyond the gate goes dark when it seals. That's the cost of the permanent solution. My order accepted it years ago. I'm offering you the chance to survive it."

"My sister is on the other side."

"I know." And there it was—that flicker in Vane's tired eyes, the ghost of a woman who had once, perhaps, before the coat and the braid and the orders, been someone who understood what it cost to say *gone* about a person you loved. "Which is why I'm making this offer to *you* and not to my superiors. Because I am about to commit treason by omission, and I'd like to feel it was worth something."

Sebastian stared at her. The fire-pit beside them had gone cold hours ago, but he felt heat crawling up his neck anyway—anger, and worse, the sick lurch of temptation. Because here was the arithmetic she was offering: Wren alive. Himself alive. An army holding the door. All it would cost was everyone already lost, everyone who could still be saved, and every promise he'd made to the dead courier and to his own reflection in the shop's dark windows for ten lonely years.

Duty, weaponized. She'd taken the shape of his own conscience and put a Warden's coat on it.

"You'd be betraying your order," he said slowly.

"I've been betraying it in small ways for twenty years," Vane said. "This would just be the first honest one." She stood, brushing salt dust from her knees. "You have until we reach Vesperhollow. One day. After that, my report writes itself, and neither of us gets to choose anything again." She turned to go, then paused, her back to them. "For what it's worth—I read your father's journals, the ones we seized. He wrote that the covenant was never meant to be a cage. He wrote that a door held shut by fear always fails, and a door held open by choice—" She shook her head. "Sentimental nonsense. Probably."

She walked out into the spiraling fog, and her Wardens fell in behind her, and the ruin was quiet except for the wind worrying at the broken beams.

* * *

Wren worked her wrists free in four minutes flat, which Sebastian chose not to find alarming, mostly because she immediately cut his bonds too.

"Well," she said, flexing her fingers. "That was cheerful."

"She offered you a cell instead of an altar and called it mercy."

"She offered us survival in exchange for everyone we're trying to save, and she did it while looking like she hated every word." Wren rubbed her wrists, watching the doorway where Vane had vanished. "That's the worst kind of enemy, Sebastian. The kind who's right about half of it."

"She's not right. Sealing the gate kills Lark. It kills all of them."

"I know." Wren was quiet a moment. The fog outside had begun to glow faintly, a pale wrong luminescence, and somewhere far off across the flats something made a sound like a bell underwater. "But here's what's bothering me. She said the order needs a witch's blood *freely given*. Freely given, Sebastian. Not taken. Which means the Wardens can't force it—they need a volunteer. And there are only two people in the world foolish enough to volunteer for that rite, and we're sitting in a ruin having this conversation."

Sebastian went cold. "She wasn't threatening us."

"No."

"She was *recruiting* us. Either we hand her the gate—or she waits for one of us to do exactly what we were already going to do, and calls it cooperation."

Wren smiled, and it was not a happy smile, but it was fierce, and he loved it, and he was beginning to stop pretending otherwise. "So the real question," she said, "is whether we run, or whether we get to that anchor first and reforge the covenant our way—before her order arrives, before the storm breaks, before the sky finishes tearing open. Look."

She pointed through the window frame. Above the distant smudge of Vesperhollow, the clouds had split along a seam of pale light, thin as a knife-cut, and the fog was pouring toward it like a tide going home.

Nineteen days had become one, and the night had noticed.

"We walk tonight," Sebastian said, and stood, and offered her his hand.
