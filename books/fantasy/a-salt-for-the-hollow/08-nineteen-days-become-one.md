## 8. Nineteen Days Become One

The storm came on the twelfth day, which was seven days too soon.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his eyes, the same ache he'd carried for ten years whenever the Hollow Gate stirred beneath his shopfloor, except now it came from every direction at once, as though the whole world had become a shopfloor and something underneath all of it wanted up.

"Wren," he said. "Wren, wake up."

She was already awake. She was always already awake; he suspected she'd been born mid-argument with the dawn and had never quite finished it. She stood at the mouth of the ruined waystation they'd sheltered in, her wild hair snapping in a wind that hadn't reached them yet.

"I know," she said. "The salt's singing."

"The salt is *what?*"

"Listen."

And he did listen, and gods help him, the salt was singing. A thin, high keening rolled across the dead road ahead of them, where the old salt flats stretched white as a shroud toward Vesperhollow. The crystals were vibrating, catching the grey pre-dawn light and throwing it back wrong—colors that didn't exist, colors Sebastian had no polite names for.

"That's not weather," he said.

"No," Wren agreed. "Weather has better manners."

Then the horizon tore.

There was no gentler word for it. The sky above the far hills split like old linen, and through the seam poured a wind full of salt and silence, and where the wind touched, things *stopped*. A dead tree fifty yards out simply ceased being a tree and became a suggestion of one. A milestone crumbled into powder that hung in the air, refusing to fall, as if even gravity had been asked to leave.

"The gate's opening early," Sebastian said. His voice came out flat and strange. He heard himself the way you hear yourself in dreams. "Vane said nineteen days. The covenant says nineteen days. It's written down. There are *documents*."

"Covenants are contracts," Wren said grimly, shouldering her pack. "Contracts get renegotiated. And I don't think we're going to like the new terms." She looked at him, and under all that fury there was fear, which frightened him more than the tearing sky. "Run now, grieve later."

They ran.

* * *

By noon Vesperhollow was dying around them, and by dying, Sebastian meant it was forgetting how to be real.

The border town had always been half-swallowed—the other realm bled into its edges, its streets ending in doorways that opened onto starlit nothing. But now the swallowing had become a tide. Houses faded like breath off glass. An entire street of chandlers' shops winked out between one step and the next, leaving only the smell of tallow and a cat sitting calmly where the street had been, regarding them with the supreme indifference of cats everywhere.

"It's pulling everything toward the threshold," Wren said. She'd gone pale, and not from the wind. "The taken souls, the buildings, the *roads*—it wants it all back on the other side before the door opens wide."

"Then we're swimming against the current."

"We've been swimming against the current since Corvane, darling. This is just the river admitting it."

He loved her. That was the thought that ambushed him, right there in the collapsing street, absurdly domestic amid the apocalypse: *I love her, and I have known it since she set my sleeve on fire to save my life on the salt roads, and I may die tomorrow without having said it.* He filed the thought away the way he filed everything—under *later*, in the great drawer marked *later*, which was, he was beginning to understand, where he kept his entire life.

A memory-ghost drifted past them then—one of Vesperhollow's lingering souls, translucent as smoke, an old woman carrying a basket that held nothing but light. She paused. She looked at Wren with terrible recognition.

"You're Lark's sister," the ghost said. "You have her chin. Her stubbornness lives in your whole face."

Wren went very still. "Is she here? Is Lark still—"

"Fading faster than the rest of us." The ghost's voice frayed at the edges. "The gate drinks deep when it opens, child. Whatever you mean to do—" She smiled, sad as dusk. "Do it tonight."

Then she was gone, and the street where she'd stood went with her, and they ran again.

* * *

They found shelter in the cellar of a chapel that had forgotten its god but remembered its architecture. Sebastian got a fire going from driftwood that might once have been pews. Wren sat with her knees drawn up, turning her mother's ring over and over on her finger—a witch's ring, other-bloodline, the thing that would matter so much in three days if three days still existed.

"Say it," she said finally. "Whatever you've been chewing on since the flats. You chew loudly."

"I do not—"

"You hum when you think. Like a kettle deciding whether to commit."

Sebastian fed the fire a splinter of pew and made himself look at her. The firelight did unfair things to her face, or fair ones; he'd lost the ability to be objective somewhere around the second ambush.

"Vane's bargain is dead," he said. "Both bargains. Hand the gate to the Wardens, or give them you—I'm choosing neither. I'm going to the far anchor, and I'm going to reforge the covenant the old way. The way my grandmother wrote it before the Edict, before anyone decided magic was a crime instead of a craft." He swallowed. "The old way costs a keeper. Freely given. On the threshold, forever. And I've decided it's me. It was always going to be me. My family swore it; I'm the last Winterbourne; the arithmetic is very simple."

"Arithmetic," Wren repeated, dangerously quiet. "You're going to walk into a door between worlds and let it close around your heart, and you're calling it *arithmetic*."

"Somebody has to hold the door, Wren. If no one does, both realms come apart, and everyone we've ever met comes apart with them, including a great many people I actively dislike, but also—including Lark. Including the souls who've spent years trapped halfway. Including, if I'm honest, a hedge-witch who deserves to go home to a world worth saving."

"Don't you dare make me the reason."

"You were never the reason. You were the reminder." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "I hid for ten years, Wren. Ten years of luck-knots and fever-charms and lying to every customer about who I was, and I told myself it was duty, but it was really just fear wearing duty's coat. Then a dying man handed me a map, and a furious woman broke into my shop, and for the first time in ten years I wasn't hiding anymore—I was walking *toward* something. That's yours. Whatever courage I have, you lit it."

The fire popped. Somewhere overhead, the chapel groaned, shedding another decade of itself.

"So that's your grand confession?" Wren said. Her eyes were bright and wet and furious. "You rehearse a martyrdom at me in a collapsing church?"

"There was some rehearsal, yes."

"Oh, Sebastian." She crossed the cellar in three strides and took his face in her hands, and her fingers smelled of ash and rosemary and home. "You absolute charm-selling idiot. I watched Wardens burn my mentor's life down to embers. I watched my sister get taken because I was too young and too slow. I have spent nine years sharpening myself into a weapon aimed at that gate, and somewhere on the salt roads I stopped aiming it and started—started *walking beside you*, which was never part of any plan, because plans keep me safe and you have never once kept me safe, you keep me *brave*, and those aren't the same thing, and I love you, and I am not letting you do this alone."

The words landed in him like sunrise.

"Wren—"

"I'm a witch of the other bloodline. My blood belongs to that side of the door as much as yours belongs to this one. If the covenant wants willing keepers, then it can have two, or it can have none, because I will stand on that threshold and argue with it until it surrenders, and I have argued with tax collectors, Sebastian. The gate doesn't stand a chance."

He laughed—really laughed, helpless and wet-eyed—and pulled her forehead against his.

"Together, then," he whispered.

"Together," she said. "But if you die on me, I'm resurrecting you specifically so I can kill you myself."

"That seems proportionate."

Outside, the sky tore wider, and the singing of the salt rose to a shriek, and the chapel above them lost its roof to a wind from nowhere—but inside, for one hour, in the small gold circle of a dying fire, two people chose each other over the arithmetic, and the choosing felt like the first honest thing either realm had seen in a decade.

* * *

They left for the far anchor before dawn, because there was barely any night left to leave before. The world had thinned to gauze; stars showed through the ground itself. Behind them, Vesperhollow was a memory of a town, and ahead, the salt flats blazed with impossible light, and at their center, visible now for the first time in ten generations, stood the far anchor—a door of black stone standing alone under the torn sky, humming the same note Sebastian had heard beneath his shopfloor for ten years.

Waiting for him. Waiting for them.

Halfway there, Wren stopped suddenly and grabbed his arm.

"Sebastian. Look at the door."

He looked. His heart, which had braced for horror, found something worse: hope. Because the black stone was glowing—not with the hungry light of the hollow tide, but softly, warmly, the exact color of the fire in a cellar, of a lamp in a shop window, of a welcome.

And carved above the lintel, in letters that had not existed an hour ago, in his grandmother's unmistakable hand:

*TWO HANDS HOLD WHAT ONE CANNOT.*

Wren's grip tightened on his arm. "It knows," she breathed. "It's been waiting for two."

The hollow tide crested behind them like a wave the size of the sky, and hand in hand, they ran for the door.
