## 9. The Reforging

The far anchor was not what Sebastian had expected.

He had imagined a tower, perhaps, or a ring of standing stones—something grand enough to justify ten generations of Winterbournes bleeding for it. Instead, at the end of the drowned salt road, in the white bowl of a valley where nothing grew and nothing rotted, there was only a door.

It stood alone, framed by nothing, hinged to nothing—a door of black iron gone green with age, set into an arch of salt-bleached stone that rose from the earth like the rib of some buried god. No wall behind it. No wall before it. Just the door, and the wind coming through its seams with a sound like slow breathing.

"It's smaller than I pictured," Wren said. Her voice came out thin. She had not slept since Vesperhollow began to fade behind them; none of them had. Even Captain Vane, who had followed them the last two miles with her Wardens' orders burned to ash in her pocket and her sword still on her hip because habit dies hard, said nothing at all.

"The gate isn't the door," Sebastian said. He dismounted—or rather, slid off the mule, which had carried them heroically and smelled accordingly. "The gate is the *agreement*. The door is just where the agreement touches the world."

"And the agreement is failing."

The hollow tide proved her right before he could answer.

It came across the valley floor the way fog comes off a marsh—but wrong, too thick, too deliberate, a grey-white flood that moved against the wind and made no sound at all. Where it touched the dead grass, the grass didn't wither. It simply forgot itself, going pale and weightless, drifting up like snow falling in reverse. Through it, Sebastian could see shapes. Faces, sometimes. Things that had been people once, or things that wanted very much to be.

Lark's warning echoed in his skull: *It doesn't want the door open. It wants the door GONE.*

"Nineteen days," Sebastian murmured. "It couldn't have waited nineteen more days."

"You sound personally offended," Wren said.

"I am, a little."

* * *

He knelt at the base of the arch while the tide gathered itself at the valley's rim like a held breath. There, worn nearly smooth, was the keystone—the old covenant-stone, carved with the Winterbourne sigil: a wren perched on a key.

Sebastian stared at it for a long moment, then laughed. It came out ragged.

"What?" Wren crouched beside him.

"My family's sigil. A bird on a key." He traced the carving with one finger. "I always thought it was a nightingale. Some noble little songbird. Ten years I've been selling luck-knots under a sign of a nightingale, feeling clever about the disguise." He looked at her sideways. "It was never a nightingale, Wren. It was you. Or someone like you. Someone from your line, on my family's stone, from the very beginning."

Wren went very still. Then she pulled up her sleeve, and there on her forearm, inked in hedge-witch green that had faded to the color of old sea-glass, was the same bird. A wren on a key.

"Gran said it was our hearth-mark," she whispered. "Said we'd always been *door-people*." Her laugh cracked in the middle. "I thought she meant we were good at finding lost things."

"Your bloodline helped forge this," Sebastian said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "Both sides of the door. That's why the courier came to me. That's why Lark was taken—"

"Don't." Wren's voice sharpened. "Don't make my sister a plot in your family history."

"He's not making anything up."

Vane had come to stand over them both, boots crunching salt. Behind her, the tide had begun to move again, closer now, patient as arithmetic. "I've read the original Wardens' charter. Older than the Edict, older than the burning times. The Hollow Gate wasn't sealed by one bloodline." She looked down at Sebastian's hand, then at Wren's marked arm. "It was sealed by two, holding hands across the threshold. One keeper on each side. The Wardens' founders were the second pair's children—and they got so frightened of what their parents had done that they outlawed the magic instead of maintaining it. Fools."

"Charming family legacy," Wren muttered.

"They all have one," Sebastian said.

* * *

He drew his knife while the sky went the color of dirty wool overhead.

The ritual was simple. That was the cruelty of it. His father's journal had been clear: *the anchor asks only for blood freely spilled and a will freely given.* No incantation longer than a vow. No circle. Just a hand opened over the stone and a heart that did not flinch.

*One life,* the old words said. *One keeper, given whole.*

Sebastian pressed the blade to his palm and cut—a clean line, deep enough to matter—and held his fist over the keystone as his blood fell in fat dark drops onto the salt.

Nothing happened.

The blood sat on the stone. The tide kept coming. And beneath him, through the soles of his boots, Sebastian felt the vast indifferent attention of the covenant turn toward him like an enormous eye opening, and then—gently, almost pityingly—look *away.*

"No," he said. "No, I'm offering. I'm *here.* Take it."

The silence of the stone was worse than refusal.

"It won't take you alone." Wren grabbed his wrist—not the cut one, the other—and hauled him around to face her. Her eyes were wet and furious. "You absolute mule. You were going to do this without telling me the terms. You've known since Vesperhollow. You've known since Lark told us, and you let me walk here believing—"

"Believing what? That I'd let you die?" His voice tore on the last word. "Yes! Gladly! That was the entire plan, Wren!"

"Well, it's a stupid plan." She shoved him, hard, and he staggered in the salt. "Because the covenant just told you, in front of God and Captain Vane and that *thing* eating the horizon—it doesn't want one keeper. It never wanted one keeper. Your family got it wrong. They've been getting it wrong for a hundred years, sending one poor soul at a time to bleed out on this rock, and the gate has been starving ever since!"

The tide crossed the first row of dead stones at the valley's edge.

"Willing pairs," Vane said quietly. She had drawn her sword, though against the tide it was about as useful as a spoon. "One from each side of the door. That's what the charter says. That's what it has *always* said. Your ancestors just preferred a corpse to sharing the job."

Sebastian looked at the door. At the grey flood. At Wren, whose jaw was set the way it set when she was about to do something magnificent and terrible, and whose sleeve still bore a bird on a key.

"No," he said.

"Yes," she said.

"Wren—"

"Sebastian Winterbourne." She stepped past him, toward the arch, toward the door that stood open on nothing. "Ten years you hid. Ten years you bled alone in a cellar under a charm shop, keeping watch over something nobody thanked you for, and you never once asked anyone to share it. So I'm asking *you.*"

She turned back, and her expression broke his heart cleanly in half.

"I'm not letting you be the door alone. Either we're both the door, or neither of us is, and if neither of us is, then everything on both sides of it drowns—including every soul it took, including Lark, including everyone either of us has ever loved. So." She held out her knife. "Choose fast."

* * *

He chose fast.

They knelt together at the keystone, knee to knee in the salt, while the tide came down the valley like the end of the world taking its time. Sebastian cut Wren's palm; she cut his again, deeper, reopening the wound the stone had refused. Their blood mingled on the covenant-stone, red on white, running together into the carved channels of the old sigil until the wren on the key seemed to fill with color.

And the eye beneath the world opened again—and this time it *saw* them.

The voice, when it came, did not come through the ears. It came up through the ground, through their knees, through the bones the salt had been quietly listening to all along:

***TWO WHO STAND AS ONE. ONE FROM EITHER SIDE OF THE DOOR. DO YOU GIVE YOURSELVES—NOT YOUR DEATHS, BUT YOUR SELVES—TO THE THRESHOLD?***

"Not our deaths," Sebastian repeated. Something in his chest unclenched that had been clenched for ten years. "It never wanted our deaths. My whole family died believing it wanted martyrs, and all it ever wanted was—"

"Keepers," Wren finished. "Company. It's been *lonely*, you enormous idiot."

Despite everything—the tide thirty yards away and closing, the air tasting of forgetting—Sebastian laughed.

"We give ourselves," he said aloud, and the words came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he was terror and wonder in equal measure. "Not as sacrifice. As *choice.*"

"Together," Wren said. "As choice."

***THEN SPEAK THE REFORGING, AND HOLD THE DOOR.***

They spoke it. The old words rose in them unbidden, half-remembered from a dead man's journal and half-invented on the spot, and it didn't matter, because the covenant was not particular about poetry—only about truth:

*"What was sealed, we hold. What was broken, we mend. What was stolen, we open wide. Let this door be neither prison nor wound but a threshold between willing hands, and let no power pass that love does not permit."*

The tide struck.

It hit them like a wave of cold iron and old grief, a thousand forgotten voices screaming *close it close it close it* and a thousand others screaming *open it open open*—and Sebastian felt himself begin to come apart, felt his edges dissolve into salt and memory—

—and then Wren's hand closed around his, cut palm to cut palm, blood to blood, and he stopped coming apart, because he was not one thing being erased. He was *two* things holding.

"Hold the door!" Vane shouted, braced against the flood with her sword planted like a lightning rod, doing whatever stubborn human thing she was doing to buy them seconds.

"We ARE the door!" Wren shouted back, laughing and sobbing at once, wild magic pouring off her in green sparks that the grey tide could not touch. "Hold ON, Sebastian—hold on to ME—"

The world went white.

Then green.

Then, impossibly, quiet.

* * *

When Sebastian could see again, the tide was receding—not defeated, but *answered*, folding back from the valley in long silver sheets, and through its parting came the souls. Hundreds of them. Memory-ghosts and taken ones, luminous and ragged, streaming toward the door that stood at last in balance—neither shut nor gaping, but held, exactly as wide as love permits.

At their head walked a girl with sea-glass eyes and a wren inked on her arm.

"Lark," Wren breathed.

Lark paused at the threshold—*their* threshold now, Sebastian felt it humming in his healed palm—and looked back at her sister. She smiled the way people smile when they are allowed, finally, to go home.

"Told you," she said, "that you'd find your way to the door eventually."

And then she stepped through, into light, and the light received her like a harbor receives a boat.

Wren made a sound that Sebastian would spend the rest of his life learning to answer, and he caught her as her knees gave, and they knelt together in the salt, holding on, while the last of the stolen passed home between their joined hands.

Behind them, Captain Ilsa Vane sheathed her sword, took out the charred remains of her orders, and scattered them to the wind.

"Well," she said. "Now what?"

Sebastian looked at Wren. Wren looked at Sebastian. The door between them hummed, warm as a hearth, patient as morning.

"Now," Wren said, wiping her face, "we figure out how two people share one doorway without driving each other mad. Starting with who sleeps on which side."

"That," said Sebastian, "is going to require negotiation."
