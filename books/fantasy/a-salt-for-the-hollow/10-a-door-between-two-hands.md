## 10. A Door Between Two Hands

*Sebastian*

The silence after the reforging was not an absence of sound. It was a held breath.

Sebastian knelt at the far anchor with Wren's hand clasped in his, both their palms cut and pressed together, their blood mingling in the hairline seam of the anchor-stone like ink finding its way into a page. The covenant had taken them—had *accepted* them—and now the Hollow Gate stood before them, no longer groaning on its ancient hinges but humming a note so low he felt it in his teeth, in the marrow of him, in the ten years of hiding that had led to this stone room at the edge of everything.

The gate did not close.

He had expected it to close. Every Winterbourne for nine generations had understood the covenant as a lock, a lid, a door slammed and barred against the hollow tide. But the gate stood open—a crack, a seam of silver-dark nothing no wider than a hand's breadth—and through it came light. Not the hungry, pale wrongness of the other realm, but something warmer. Souls, drifting through like embers rising from a banked fire.

"Wren," he said, and found his voice had gone to sand. "Wren, look."

"I'm looking." Her grip on his hand tightened. She was still trembling from the reforging—the hollow tide had broken against them like a wave against a seawall, and they were both salt-crusted, bleeding, half-deafened, and utterly alive. "Is it supposed to do that?"

"Do what?"

"That." She nodded toward the seam.

A soul came through. It resolved as it crossed—not a ghost exactly, not a body either, but a person-shaped warmth, translucent as breath on glass. It looked around the anchor chamber with wide, wondering eyes, and then it walked past them both, up the worn steps toward the world above, leaving a faint trail of sweetness in the air like a field someone had mowed in June.

Another followed. Then three more. A dozen.

"The stolen ones," Sebastian breathed. "The door isn't sealed. It's—open enough."

"Open just enough," Wren whispered. He heard the question underneath: *is Lark among them?*

They watched together, kneeling hand in hand like children at a keyhole, as the souls of Vesperhollow's taken passed through the threshold one by one. Some moved quickly, eager. Some lingered at the seam, pressing insubstantial hands to its edges as if saying goodbye to the country they'd been kept in for years. One paused directly in front of Sebastian and studied him with an expression he could only describe as *assessing*, the way his mother used to look at his schoolwork.

"Sorry," he told it. "For the decade of neglect. And the dust. There was rather a lot of dust."

The soul made a sound like wind through wheat—which he chose, generously, to interpret as forgiveness—and drifted upward after the others.

"You apologize to souls now," Wren said.

"It's a new decade. I'm turning over a new leaf."

* * *

*Wren*

She did not let go of his hand until the last soul had passed, because she was afraid—not of the gate, not anymore, but of what her own heart would do if she let go. So they knelt there while the hours turned, and the seam of the world breathed its slow silver light, and the stolen dead went home.

And then, near dawn by the water-clock she could not see but somehow felt in her changed blood, another soul came through. Small. Quick. Moving the way Lark had always moved—like the world might change its mind about letting her stay in any one place.

Wren was on her feet before she knew she'd stood.

"Lark."

Her sister stopped. Turned. And even translucent, even worn thin as morning mist, her face broke into exactly the grin Wren had carried through seven years of searching, six years of grief, and nineteen days of hell.

"There you are," Lark said. "You look terrible. Did you fall down a mountain?"

"Two," Wren said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle. "You should see the other guy."

Lark closed the distance between them, and when she embraced Wren it was like being hugged by sunlight—warmth without weight, arms without arms, love without any of the ordinary machinery of a body. It should have been less than a real embrace. It was more.

"I have to go the rest of the way myself," Lark murmured into her hair. "The door only opens so far. But I wanted—" She pulled back, and her ember-bright eyes flicked to Sebastian, who was standing with the exquisite awkwardness of a man trying to be furniture. "Him. Is he the one? From the shop? With the luck-knots?"

"He's the one," Wren said.

"Mother will be insufferable about this. 'My daughter married the gatekeeper.'" Lark grinned again, softer now. "Take care of each other. That's all. That's the whole wisdom of the dead, and honestly we get quite a lot of time to work on it."

Then she was gone—up the stairs, out into the grey beginning of morning, home—and Wren discovered that Sebastian had caught her elbow, and that she was crying, and that crying could feel like being emptied out and filled up in the same motion.

"She's going home," he said quietly.

"Yes."

"That joke about marrying the gatekeeper—"

"Don't," Wren said, laughing and sobbing at once, "get ideas. We've been bound to a cosmic threshold for approximately four hours. I'm not discussing marriage until at least a week has passed."

"A week," Sebastian agreed solemnly. "I'll put it in my ledger. Next to the dust apologies."

* * *

*Sebastian*

Captain Ilsa Vane was waiting for them at the top of the stairs.

Sebastian's heart performed a maneuver that would have concerned a physician. She stood in the ruined doorway of the anchor-house, morning light behind her, one hand resting on the warrant-case chained at her hip—the case containing the Wardens' sealed orders, the ones that read, in essence, *control the gate or destroy whatever stands in the way of controlling it.*

"Captain," he said. "If you've come to arrest us, I should warn you we're extremely bound to an interdimensional door. Pulling us away would be—you know. Catastrophic. Very embarrassing for the department."

"Quiet, Winterbourne." But there was no iron in it. She was looking past them, down the stairs, at the silver seam glowing in the dark below, and her face—the face that had haunted his decade of careful anonymity—was doing something complicated. "I watched it happen. From the ridge. The whole sky lit up like a struck bell. Do you know what my orders say?"

"We can guess."

"My orders say the gate is a weapon. That whoever holds the anchor holds the realm." She unhooked the warrant-case from her belt. Turned it over in her hands. "I've spent eleven years believing that. Burned eleven years of apothecaries and hedge-covens and frightened people with lucky charms, believing I was pulling thorns before they festered." Her jaw worked. "There was a girl in Corvane, years ago. Sold fever-charms outside the tannery. Nine years old. My sergeant said the law was the law."

Wren had gone very still beside him. Sebastian reached over and took her hand.

Vane opened the case. Inside lay the warrant, heavy paper, wax-sealed, stamped with the sigil of the Wardens' high command. She drew it out, and from her coat pocket a striker and a stub of candle, and there on the threshold of the anchor-house, in the grey light, with the souls of the stolen dead still faintly perfuming the air, Captain Ilsa Vane set her orders alight and let them burn down to her fingers.

"The gate is held," she said, dropping the last blackening scrap. "By two keepers, freely bound. That's what I'll write. If the high command disagrees, they can come argue with the door themselves."

"And if they do?" Wren asked.

Vane almost smiled. It was a rusty expression, clearly out of practice. "Then I imagine I'll be standing here next to you. I'm told the view improves." She settled her cap, turned on her heel, then paused. "Winterbourne. Your shop in Corvane. The luck-knots."

"What about them?"

"They worked. Mine's still on my pack strap. Ten years, never once frayed." She looked at him a moment longer—an apology in it, old and insufficient—and then she walked away down the salt road, toward the sunrise, a woman going home lighter than she'd come.

"Well," said Sebastian, when her footsteps had faded. "I believe that's the nicest thing a Warden has ever said to me. Admittedly the competition is nonexistent."

* * *

*Wren*

They built the cottage themselves, which took most of a season, because neither of them knew anything about building cottages and both of them were too stubborn to admit it. It stood a stone's throw from the anchor-house, with a crooked chimney (Sebastian's fault), a garden that grew things slightly out of season (Wren's fault), and a doorstep carved from a chip of the anchor-stone itself, so that the whole house hummed very faintly, day and night, like a cat deciding whether to purr.

Their days found a shape. At dawn one of them descended to the gate and listened to it, the way a shepherd listens to a flock—not guarding it, exactly. Keeping it company. The seam needed tending the way a fire needs breathing on: a word spoken into it each morning, a hand laid on the anchor-stone each night, two names said together at the turn of the month. *Freely given,* the covenant had whispered into their blood. *Freely kept.*

The souls still came through, sometimes—one or two a season, stragglers from the far side, wandering homeward. Wren learned their names. Sebastian learned to make tea that even the bodiless could taste, which he considered his finest professional achievement.

In autumn, the first letter arrived from Lark—or rather, it arrived the way letters from the other side arrive: written in frost on the kitchen window, in handwriting that slanted the wrong way and spelled *cottage* as *cotttage.*

*Am well. Told Mother about the gatekeeper. She was indeed insufferable. Send salt.*

Wren laughed until she had to sit down, and Sebastian read it over her shoulder, and then he fetched the good salt, the coarse grey kind from the evaporating pans, and scattered a pinch across the threshold, which was how you sent a parcel where they were. Probably. They were still working out the rules.

That evening they sat on the crooked doorstep together, watching the light fail over the salt flats, her head on his shoulder, his thumb tracing the thin white scar across her palm where the covenant had signed them both.

"Do you ever regret it?" she asked. "Ten years of hiding, and now this. Neither caged nor free. Keeper forever."

Sebastian considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty. "When I tended the gate alone, under the shop, it felt like a debt. Like something I owed a family I never met." He lifted their joined hands, scar to scar. "This doesn't feel like a debt. It feels like—" He searched for the word and found, annoyingly, that Lark's frost-letter had already written it. "Devotion. Duty remade. Pick your term; I sell charms, I'm good with words but only small ones."

"Duty remade," Wren said softly, and turned her face up to kiss him, and the door beneath them hummed its low contented note, and somewhere beyond the seam of the world, he liked to think, a soul on its long road home saw the glow of a small warm cottage and quickened its step.

Later, much later, in the dark before sleep, Wren stirred against his shoulder.

"Sebastian. The frost letter."

"Mm."

"She spelled *cotttage.* With two t's."

"I noticed."

"Do you think—" A pause, the particular pause of a witch assembling an alarming thought. "Do you think souls can make jokes in frost, or do you think there's a second letter coming?"

Outside, faint but unmistakable, the gate hummed.

It sounded, Sebastian thought, exactly like laughter.

*The End — or very nearly.*
