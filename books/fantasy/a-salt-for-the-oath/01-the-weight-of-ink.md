## 1. The Weight of Ink

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its hours by candlelight, because daylight was considered a form of optimism and optimism was bad for business.

Rafael Ravensworth had learned this in her first week, seven years ago, when Master Corvane caught her opening a shutter to air out the copying room and fined her three days' indenture for "exposure of inventory." The inventory in question had been a contract for a bottled storm — one of forty-one she would copy that year — and the storm inside its glass had sulked for a week afterward, pressing gray against the sides of its bottle like a cat denied a window.

She did not open shutters anymore.

Instead she sat at her desk in the long copying room with nine other scribes, all of them bent over their work like commas, and she did what she did every morning: she dipped her pen, she squared her paper, and before writing a single word of anyone else's business, she wrote her own.

*Day 2,547.*

*Days remaining: 1,093.*

Three years. One thousand and ninety-three mornings of this arithmetic, scratched small in the margin where no auditor would look, because auditors looked at everything except the margins, and Rafael had learned that too. A prisoner scratches walls. A scribe scratches margins. The difference was that a wall didn't make you count how much it was worth.

"Ravensworth." Master Hollis's voice came down the length of the room like something dropped from a height. "Storm contract, third renewal. The client wants clauses tightened. He claims his tempest has been *leaking*."

A ripple went through the other scribes — the particular ripple of people grateful it wasn't them. Storm renewals were tedious. Storms were, as a category, poor correspondents: they wanted nothing but to be let out, and every clause you drafted was another bar on a cage, and after seven years Rafael could feel the bars through the paper.

She rose, collected the original from Hollis's tray, and carried it back to her desk with both hands, the way you carry something sleeping.

The storm's bottle sat on the reading stand beside the contract, and she gave it the courtesy of a glance. It pressed back. Gray-green, restless, a thumb-width of weather doing its best behind curved glass. In the corner of the bottle, someone from the drafting department had affixed a small label: *Property of the Guild until redeemed. Do not name aloud near open windows.*

"They're tightening your terms," Rafael told it quietly. "I'm sorry. I only write what I'm told."

The storm dimmed, which she chose to read as understanding rather than sulking.

She began.

---

The trouble with copying contracts for impossible things, Rafael had decided somewhere around year four, was not the impossibility. It was the *specificity*. Anyone could promise a miracle. It took real labor to promise a miracle in triplicate, with witnesses, penalties, and a schedule of payments.

Today's storm belonged to a merchant in the salt trade who had bought it twenty years ago to sink a rival's fleet, then thought better of it, then discovered the Guild did not offer refunds on weather. So he'd kept it in his cellar, paying storage and interest, and now he wanted the renewal clauses amended so that if he died owing, the storm would pass to his heirs along with the debt — a family inheritance of unspent catastrophe.

Rafael copied the old language first, line by line, her hand moving with the automatic grace of long practice. Then the amendments. Then the clause that made her pause:

*Should the debtor default, the collateral named herein shall revert to the Guild entire, without appeal, redemption, or remittance.*

Collateral. She underlined it without thinking, the way she underlined it every time, because the word always snagged on something in her chest like a fishhook.

Every contract had collateral. That was the first law of the Guild of Impossible Things: nothing given, nothing lent, nothing bottled or borrowed without something held against failure. Years were borrowed against memories. Storms were sold against ships. Blessings were rented against firstborn sons, though the Guild had gotten subtler about that one after the scandal of '89.

And the ledgers — the great sealed ledgers down in the vault, which no junior scribe was permitted to touch — recorded what that collateral actually *was*. Everyone knew they existed. No one below the rank of master had seen one opened.

Rafael had asked about them exactly once, in her second year, and Master Hollis had looked at her with an expression she still couldn't classify — not anger, not warning, something closer to *pity wearing a mask of boredom* — and said, "The ledgers record what is owed. You don't want to know what you owe, Ravensworth. Nobody who asks ever does."

She hadn't asked again. But she underlined *collateral* every time, and last month she had started keeping a private list of the underlinings, folded into quarters and hidden beneath the false bottom of her ink box, and she did not examine too closely why.

*Day 2,547,* she reminded herself. *One thousand ninety-three.* Whatever the ledgers held, it couldn't reach her if she served out her term clean and walked out the door. Debt was a room. You left a room by finishing your sentence in it.

She believed this the way you believe a rope bridge: fully, and only while standing on it.

---

Midday brought the bell, and the bell brought bread, and the bread brought ten minutes in the courtyard where the scribes stood in a loose ring and spoke in the low, quick voices of people comparing sentences.

"Four years for me," said Petter, who was broad and cheerful and lied about his term constantly; it was six. "Then I'm going south. Open sky. You couldn't pay me to draft another weather clause."

"You'll draft whatever they put in front of you," said Maren, older, sharper, her knuckles stained permanently black. "That's the whole trick of this place. They don't need chains. They just need you hungry and holding a pen."

Rafael ate her bread and said little, as usual. Seven years had taught her the economy of the courtyard: everyone traded in time here, everyone counted, and the counting made them either kind or cruel depending on the day. Today Petter was kind. He broke his loaf and handed half over without being asked.

"You've got what, two years?"

"Three," Rafael said. "One thousand ninety-three days."

Maren snorted into her cup. "You count like a miser counts coins."

"A miser gets to spend his coins."

That landed harder than she'd meant it to, and the little circle went quiet in the way circles do when someone has accidentally told the truth. Across the courtyard, the guild masters' wing stood with its windows shuttered — always shuttered — and above it, far above, the mountain shouldered up into the clouds the way it always did, gray and enormous and patient, its peak lost in weather that never seemed to belong to any season.

The Undermountain. Everyone knew there were courts beneath it. Old stories, pre-kingdom stories, the kind nurses told and scholars dismissed. The Guild never mentioned it. But the Guild also never built anything facing the mountain if it could help it, and the copying room's one windowless wall was the wall that faced it, and Rafael had noticed that years ago and filed it away in the same drawer as her list of underlined words.

"Ravensworth." Hollis again, appearing at the courtyard door with the silent suddenness the masters cultivated. "After bread. Priority work. The night desk needs a hand with a translation."

"A translation?" Petter raised his eyebrows. Translation was rare work, and paid against indenture at double rate. "Since when do we translate?"

"Since clients bring us documents in languages we don't speak," Hollis said, "and since the Guild," and here his voice did something subtle, something Rafael's ear caught and held, "has always been able to find readers for anything. After bread, Ravensworth. Don't dawdle."

He turned and went. Maren leaned close.

"Double rate," she murmured. "Take it. And watch yourself — priority work means someone important wants it, and someone important wanting it means it's either very boring or very dangerous."

"Which do you hope?"

"I hope boring," Maren said. "Boring keeps you alive to finish your count."

---

The night desk occupied a narrow room on the guild's east side, lit by lamps that burned a greenish oil Rafael had never seen anywhere else. The document waiting for her lay under a cloth, and when Hollis lifted the cloth, Rafael forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

It was a page of vellum, old beyond sense — the edges crumbled like pastry, the surface scarred with water damage and something darker that she declined to identify. The script across it was dense, angular, beautiful in the way thorns are beautiful. It ran right to left, then left to right, then coiled into spirals at the margins, as if the words themselves were restless.

"Do you know it?" Hollis asked.

"No," Rafael said honestly.

"Read it anyway. Aloud, if you can manage. The client insists the translation must be performed, not merely written. Something about the words needing to pass through a living mouth."

"That's not how translation works."

"The client," Hollis said, with the flat patience of a man reciting scripture, "pays double rate."

Rafael bent over the vellum. The lamp oil smell thickened. And she began, haltingly, to read.

Except that *haltingly* was wrong, and the wrongness arrived slowly enough that she was three lines in before she understood it. Her tongue was finding the shapes. Not guessing them — *finding* them, the way your feet find stairs in the dark of your own house. The angular letters resolved into sounds, the sounds into rhythms, the rhythms into — not meaning, not quite, but the *shape* of meaning, like hearing speech through a wall in a house you lived in as a child.

She stopped. Her heart was going hard.

"Where did this come from?" she asked.

"A finder brought it out of the hill country. Why?"

"Because—" She looked at the page. The spirals in the margin seemed, in the green light, to have turned slightly, the way a sleeping man turns. "Because I don't know this language. Nobody knows this language. It's pre-kingdom. It's *deep-tongue*, it's from the—"

She didn't finish. Some instinct, old and cold, closed her mouth for her.

Hollis watched her with those eyes that were never quite bored, never quite pitying. "Continue," he said. "As far as you can."

She continued. The words came easier the longer she read, which was the most frightening part — not that she understood, but that she was *remembering*, syllable by syllable, something she had never learned. And buried in the middle of the page, in a passage her mind translated without asking permission, she heard her own voice say:

*...and the debt stands until the blood repays it, and the blood will come, for blood always comes home...*

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below them — floors down, or farther than floors — something vast turned over in its sleep.

Rafael set down the pen. Her hand was steady. She noted, distantly, that her hand was always steady, and wondered when she had become the kind of person whose hands stayed steady while everything else shook.

"It's a debt notice," she said. "An old one. It names a family."

"Which family?" Hollis asked, and his voice had gone soft in a way she would remember later, in the way you remember the exact sound of a trap closing.

"I can't make out the name. The vellum's damaged."

It was a lie. She could make out the name perfectly. It was written in spirals, in the margin, in the place where the writer had wanted it found by exactly one reader.

*Ravensworth.*

"One thousand ninety-three days," she said to herself, very quietly, under the sound of Hollis calling for a courier. "Just keep counting. Just keep the count."

Outside, far off over the hills, thunder gathered — though no storm was scheduled anywhere in the guild's books tonight — and the first fat drops of rain struck the windowless east wall, and began, softly, patiently, to fall.
