## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge.

Rafael heard it before she saw it—a low grinding in the dark, the sound the mountain made when it wanted to be remembered—and by the time she reached the scriptorium window with her candle, the rain had already turned sideways and the gutters of the Guild of Impossible Things were roaring like a drowned choir.

She had been awake because she was always awake at this hour. Three years of indenture had trained her body to rise before the bell, to sit in the dark with her ledger open, counting. Not words tonight. Days.

*Four hundred and eleven.*

She wrote the number small, in the margin where no one else would look, then scratched a line through the previous entry so the column wouldn't grow too long. A prisoner's arithmetic. She'd learned it from an old binder named Corvel who had served out his forty years and walked out the guild door without once looking back—or so the story went. Rafael suspected the not-looking-back was embellishment. She intended to look back. She intended to look back at every single stone of this place on her way out, and memorize them all, so that she could spend the rest of her life never thinking about them again.

Below her, the great door boomed.

Not the wind. The wind didn't knock.

She stood very still, candle tilted, listening. The Guild of Impossible Things kept strange hours—debtors arriving at midnight to sell their shadows, merchants at dawn wanting storms bottled before they spoiled—but there was a protocol for arrivals, a bell-pull, a speaking-tube, a warded slot for after-hours letters. Nobody knocked. Knocking was what people did when they didn't know the rules yet, or when they'd come from somewhere the rules hadn't reached.

The knock came again. Small. Almost polite. Three raps, a pause, three more.

Rafael took the stairs down two at a time, holding her candle high, telling herself it was duty and not dread. The entrance hall rose around her, all black oak and older stone, the guild's motto carved over the lintel in letters that had been re-cut so many times they'd lost their original meaning: *ALL DEBTS ARE POSSIBLE.* Somewhere above, the storm pressed its whole weight against the roof.

She unbarred the door and pulled it open six inches against the wind.

A child stood on the step.

Mud to the waist, mud to the hairline, barefoot despite the season, so caked in earth that Rafael couldn't have said at first whether the girl was eight years old or twelve or some impossible age between. Her eyes were the only clean thing about her—wide, dark, fixed on Rafael with an intensity that had nothing childlike in it. Rain sheeted off the doorstep behind her as if the sky itself were trying to follow her inside.

"You can't—" Rafael began. "There's a bell. There are forms. You can't just—"

The child spoke.

It was one word, maybe two, a low rising phrase that the wind nearly took, and it should have been nothing. It should have been gibberish, the way any half-drowned child's babble is gibberish. But the sound went through Rafael like a needle finding an old scar, and she heard—not understood, *heard*—the shape of it. *Come-in-from-the-cold* was what it meant, or something near that. *Shelter* was in it. And underneath, threaded through it like wire through wax: *I am owed.*

Her candle guttered. She steadied it with a hand that was not entirely steady itself.

"What did you say?"

The child said it again, patient, the way you repeat yourself to someone slow. And this time Rafael caught more—the second syllable carried a falling tone that meant *home*, or *hearth*, or possibly *grave*, the old tongue being economical that way—and the horror of it wasn't that she understood. The horror was that understanding felt like *recollection*. Like reaching into her own pocket and finding a stone she'd never picked up. The language sat in her mouth the way her grandmother's lullabies sat there: present, weighty, hers without her consent.

"Where did you learn that?" Rafael whispered. "What *is* that?"

The child tilted her head. Mud flaked from her jaw. Then she simply walked forward, past Rafael, into the hall, trailing water and dirt across forty years of polished floor, and stood in the center of the entrance chamber looking up at the carved motto with an expression Rafael would spend many sleepless nights trying to name. It wasn't wonder. It was recognition. The way you look at a face in a crowd that you were promised you'd never see again.

Then the girl shivered, once, violently, and became an ordinary freezing child again, and Rafael's training caught up with her outrage.

"Right," she said, mostly to herself. "Right. Blanket. Fire. Forms later."

* *

The night porter, Hesk, was supposed to be awake. Hesk was, in fact, asleep in his chair by the hall fire, which saved time and spared questions, and Rafael decided not to wake him until she absolutely had to. She got the girl in front of the hearth, got most of the mud off with a rough towel and less gentleness than the task deserved, and discovered beneath it a child of perhaps nine, sharp-boned, dark-haired, with a healing cut along one forearm that had been bound—with skill, with actual field-skill—in strips of woven grass.

"Who bandaged you?" Rafael asked.

The girl looked at her.

"Do you have a name? Where's your mother? Your—anyone?" Nothing. The dark eyes watched her, unblinking, cataloguing. Rafael had seen that look before, but only in the mirror, on the worst days of her first year here: the look of someone deciding how much of themselves to spend on the person in front of them.

She fetched bread and broth from the kitchens. The girl ate with terrible efficiency, no pleasure in it, fueling rather than dining, and when she finished she set the bowl down with a small precise click, centered exactly where it had been, and folded her hands. It was such an adult gesture, such a *clerk's* gesture, that Rafael laughed before she could stop herself.

"All right," she said. "You're clearly nobody's lost lamb. What are you?"

The girl opened her mouth. Closed it. Tried again, visibly working at it, the way a musician works a difficult passage—and produced a sentence in that same buried language, longer this time, flowing, effortless, the accent flawless in a way Rafael had no right to recognize and did anyway. Every syllable landed where it should. The cadence rose where grief rises and fell where oaths fall.

And Rafael understood perhaps one word in ten, and each word she understood arrived in her mind already translated, already warm, already *known*, like a room she'd been in before the furniture was rearranged.

*I have walked far,* the sentence said. Or: *They sent me far.* The grammar wouldn't resolve. But the last word rang clear as a struck coin, and it was a word Rafael knew with her whole body, the way you know *hunger* or *fire*:

*Due.*

The fire popped. Down the hall, Hesk snored on, blessedly oblivious.

"That word," Rafael said slowly. "Say that word again."

The girl said it again. *Due.* And Rafael's hands, resting in her lap, had begun—she noticed distantly, as if reporting on someone else—to shake.

Because she knew the word. Not from the pre-kingdom fragments in Master Aldery's locked cabinet, not from the cracked glossaries the antiquarians traded like relics. She knew it from the ledgers. From the margins of contracts she copied every day, the boilerplate clauses written in a hand older than the guild itself, the fine print no one read because no one could: *rendered due at first moonrise; rendered due upon demand of the court below; rendered due, rendered due, rendered due.* For three years she had inked those phrases without comprehension, a scribe copying shapes, and now a mud-caked child had walked out of a storm and pronounced one of them aloud like a sentence being served.

"How do you know that word?" Rafael asked, and heard her own voice go thin. "Who taught you? Who *sent* you?"

The girl considered her for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she reached out and touched Rafael's hand—two small fingers, cold as riverbed stones—on the exact spot where, since childhood, Rafael had carried a birthmark: a small brown crescent, left wrist, shaped like a downward-turning moon.

The touch lasted a heartbeat. It said everything and explained nothing.

* *

Master Aldery came down at dawn, because Aldery always came down at dawn, a grey precise man whose footsteps in the corridor sounded like pages turning. Rafael had spent the rest of the night in the chair beside the sleeping girl, telling herself she was keeping watch out of duty, knowing perfectly well she was keeping watch out of fear—that if she looked away, the child might dissolve back into the storm, or worse, might still be there.

"She appeared at the door during the worst of it," Rafael said, while Aldery circled the girl where she slept curled in the blanket like a comma. "No shoes. Grass binding on a wound. And she speaks—"

"I'm aware of what she speaks." Aldery had stopped moving. That alone frightened Rafael more than anything else that night, because Aldery never stopped moving; he believed stillness was where errors lived. He stood over the child with his head bowed slightly, listening—not to her breathing, Rafael realized, but as though waiting for her to talk in her sleep.

"It's the deep tongue," Rafael said. "Pre-kingdom. It's supposed to be dead. Scholar Vess says the last fluent speaker died two hundred years ago and even he was mostly guessing."

"Vess guesses for a living." Aldery crouched, unhurried, and studied the girl's face the way he studied contracts: for the clause everyone else had missed. "Where did you find her?"

"At the door. I told you."

"Before that."

"I don't know. She won't say. She barely speaks Common at all." Rafael hesitated, and then, because the alternative was carrying it alone: "Master, I understood her. Some of her. Fragments. I don't know how—I've never studied it, there's nothing *to* study—but the words come to me like memories. Like something I knew before I knew I knew it."

Something moved through Aldery's face then, quick and deep, a fish under ice. When he looked up at Rafael, his eyes had changed, and it took her a moment to identify the expression because it was so utterly wrong on him.

Hunger.

It was the look of a man hearing a number he liked. The look he got, Rafael realized with a cold turn of her stomach, when a debtor signed away something precious and the guild's percentage came due. She had seen him wear it across a bargaining table a hundred times and had never once thought to be afraid of it. Now it was aimed at a sleeping child, and Rafael understood, in the wordless way one understands a change in the weather, that the girl had stopped being a foundling the moment she'd opened her mouth.

"Fragments," Aldery repeated softly. "You understand fragments."

"A few words. Badly."

"Mm." He straightened, smoothed his coat, and was himself again, grey and precise, except that the hunger didn't leave his eyes; it just moved deeper in, the way debt moves deeper into a life. "She'll stay in the east room. Warded. No one speaks of her to anyone—not the wardens, not the other clerks, not Vess. I'll inform the council myself." He paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the storm-grey light, and added, almost as an afterthought, in a tone he probably believed was kind:

"You've done well, Ravensworth. Very well indeed. There may be something in this for you. Against your indenture, perhaps." A thin smile. "We'll see what the ledger says."

He left. Rafael sat by the dying fire and listened to his footsteps climb away, pages turning, pages turning, and tried to make sense of the wrongness settling over her like the storm's pressure.

Against her indenture. Four hundred and eleven days, dangled like meat. Aldery never offered discounts. Aldery never gave anything away—which meant he expected to receive something worth far more than four hundred days, and he expected to receive it soon.

Behind her, the girl stirred in her sleep and murmured—two syllables, soft, rising, the tone that meant *shelter* or *hearth* or *grave*.

And then a third word, clear as a bell through the quiet hall, spoken with the unmistakable diction of someone reading aloud from a page:

*Ravensworth.*

Rafael went cold from the scalp down. She had never told the child her name. No one had. The girl slept on, breath slow and even, mud dried in the creases of her knuckles, and outside the windows the storm finally broke apart against the mountain's shoulders, and the first pale light of morning came down the valley and lay across the floor between them like a path.

Like a road leading somewhere Rafael had no intention of going, and would go anyway.
