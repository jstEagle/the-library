## 3. What the Ledgers Owe

The sealed archives lived three floors below the copying hall, down a spiral stair that smelled of cold iron and older dust. Guild law said no scribe below the rank of Third Pen could enter without a countersigned writ. Rafael had been a Second for two years, which meant she could enter if she asked nicely and signed the visitor's ledger with her own blood-ink—a drop from the left thumb, pressed to the line beside her name.

She had never once needed to go down there.

Now she went at dusk, when the hall emptied and the guild master retired to his rooms above with his decanter of plum wine and his ledgers of other people's futures. She told the night clerk she was retrieving a template for a storm-bottling renewal. The clerk didn't look up. Nobody looked up in this place; looking up was how you noticed the cage.

The stair took her down in slow turns, lamplight guttering in the draft. Her thumb throbbed where the blood-ink needle had pricked it. Small price. The Guild of Impossible Things priced everything small until you owed something large—that was the whole architecture of the building, maybe the whole architecture of the world.

At the bottom, a door of black oak banded in silver, and set into the silver, a lock shaped like an open mouth.

"Rafael Ravensworth," she said to it, because the door liked to hear names. "Second Pen, indentured, four hundred eleven days remaining."

The mouth opened.

Inside, the archives ran longer than the guildhall above had any right to contain. The Impossible Things kept impossible geometry the way other houses kept cats—casually, and mostly out of sight. Shelves marched away into dark on all sides, some of them curving upward where no ceiling existed. Vellum, papyrus, bark-sheets, tablets of pressed salt. Contracts for every bargain the guild had ever brokered or inherited, going back further than the kingdom itself, back past the founding kings, back to a time when the word *king* hadn't yet been invented and the courts beneath the mountains still answered their own doors.

She'd been taught none of this. Scribes learned to copy forward, never backward. The guild trained its pens like it trained its horses: eyes ahead, blinders fitted.

Rafael lit a hand-lamp and started walking.

She wasn't sure what she was looking for. That was a lie she let herself keep for the first hour. She knew exactly what she was looking for: the child's language, the one that had come through the guild doors mud-caked and screaming two nights ago, syllables falling out of the girl like water out of a cracked jar—and Rafael, holding her, understanding fragments. Not translating. *Remembering.* A word for hunger. A word for *under*. A word that meant both *debt* and *gift*, because in whatever tongue the child spoke, they had apparently never seen the need to separate them.

The guild master's face when he heard it. She couldn't shake it. Hunger was too kind a word. It was the look of a man hearing a coin he'd buried ringing in someone else's pocket.

If the language was old enough, there would be records. If there were records, there would be contracts. And if there were contracts—

Her lamp found a shelf whose spines were bound not in leather but in slate, each one etched rather than written. The etchings were in no alphabet she knew, but beneath them, newer labels had been added in careful guild script: *Pre-Kingdom Instruments. Restricted. Do Not Copy.*

Do Not Copy might as well have been *Copy Me.*

She drew out the first slate. It was heavier than it should have been, cold as riverbed stone, and the moment her fingers closed on it the lamp flame leaned toward it like a plant toward sun. The contract inscribed there was between parties named only by titles: THE ONE WHO WAITS BELOW and THE ONE WHO CLIMBS. The terms were simple and obscene. Rain for harvests. Harvests for children. Children for time.

She put it back with hands that weren't quite steady and pulled another. And another. The guild hadn't always dealt in bottled storms and borrowed years, she realized. Those were the polite modern inventory, the shopfront goods. Beneath them, layer under layer like sediment, the guild had always been a broker for the deep courts. A middleman. An agent. A *factor*.

A factor keeps the books.

She stopped pulling slates at random and started hunting systematically, and that was when she found the master index.

It sat alone on a lectern of black stone at the archive's heart, chained at the corner—the chain long enough to allow reading, short enough to forbid removal. The index was a single enormous volume, its pages the grey-white of bone left in weather. Every debt the guild had ever carried or collected, entered in order of date. She turned pages. Centuries flicked past under her thumb like a gambler shuffling.

She told herself she was looking for the deep court entries generally. Any entry. Context. Due diligence.

Then she reached the section marked in faded red: *FAMILY INSTRUMENTS — RAVENSWORTH.*

There shouldn't have been a section. There shouldn't have been a name. Her family were wool merchants and failed farmers and one great-aunt who'd run off to sea; nobody in three generations had so much as visited the guild except her, and she'd been sold into it at fourteen by an uncle with gambling debts and a talent for paperwork.

But there it was. Ravensworth. Her own surname, in ink gone brown as dried blood.

Her hands moved before her mind agreed to it. She turned to the entry.

The page was old. Older than her indenture contract, older than her grandmother's marriage lines, older than the parish book in the village where Rafael had been born. The script was pre-kingdom formal, but someone had annotated it later in guild hand, and the annotations were what made her stomach drop through the floor:

*Instrument 44-Ravensworth. Party of the first part: Ravensworth, Maren, of the high farm above Coldbrook. Party of the second part: THE COURT BENEATH [designation per original].*

Maren. Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother, who had died when Rafael was six, who had sung her songs in a language everyone assumed was nonsense, who had held Rafael's hands and said, once, in that nonsense-tongue, a sentence Rafael had carried all her life without knowing why: *when the paper comes for you, don't sign it pretty.*

She read the terms three times. They didn't get better with repetition.

*Item pledged: one soul, of the line of Maren Ravensworth, generation unspecified, selection at the pleasure of the Court. Purpose of pledge: security against the debt herein described. Condition of release: payment in full of principal and interest, or presentation of substitute of equal weight. Term: due at first moonrise following the arrival of the token.*

*Token:* and here the old script gave way to a newer annotation, ink barely fifty years dry, in handwriting Rafael knew intimately because she had spent four years copying it, correcting it, being beaten across the knuckles for misreading it.

The guild master's handwriting. He had annotated her own family's soul-debt the way a merchant annotates a shipping manifest.

*Token dispatched,* his note read. *Arrival anticipated within the decade. Collateral to be received at the guild door. Do not inform the instrument's descendant until reception is confirmed.*

*Collateral.* The word sat beside her grandmother's name like a maggot in fruit. And beneath it, in the oldest layer of the page, in the original pre-kingdom script that Rafael now understood far too much of, the same word repeated in the old tongue. The word she'd heard the child scream on the doorstep. The word that meant *pledged thing*, *held thing*, *the gift that is owed back*.

The word Wren had used about herself. Rafael had named her already, privately, without deciding to—the girl was small and brown and quick-eyed as the bird—but the child had called herself something else, over and over, that first night, in the old language. Rafael had understood it then the way you understand a blow: not through study but through impact.

*Collateral.*

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if even fire was afraid to move.

She did the arithmetic because her mind demanded it the way a tongue demands a broken tooth. *Due at first moonrise following the arrival of the token.* The token had arrived two nights ago. The moon was a thin paring, three days from full, rising earlier each evening. Which meant first moonrise after arrival was—

Tomorrow. Tomorrow evening, when the moon came up over the mountain, the debt came due. Not the debt of coin or grain. The debt of one soul of the line of Maren Ravensworth, generation unspecified, selection at the pleasure of the Court.

Generation unspecified.

Selection at the pleasure of the Court.

Rafael gripped the lectern's edge. The stone was warm. She noticed that with distant, floating horror—the whole archive was ice-cold except this book, which was warm as a living thing, as if the ledger had a pulse, as if the debts themselves were alive down here in the dark, breathing slowly, waiting to be fed.

*Do not inform the instrument's descendant until reception is confirmed.*

He'd known. The guild master had known before the child ever arrived. Had perhaps known for years—for exactly as many years as it took to dispatch a token, to walk a feral girl out of the mountain and across the country in time for the moon. He hadn't taken her in out of charity when he'd set bread before Wren and posted a warden outside her room. He'd taken receipt of collateral.

And Rafael—Rafael was the spare. The backup. The substitute of equal weight, if the Court found her more convenient than a fading child. Generation unspecified cut both ways. She had thought she was digging into the guild's secrets. She had been reading her own invoice.

Somewhere above her, muffled through floors of stone, the guildhall clock began to strike the hour. Nine bells. In twenty-three hours, give or take, the moon would clear the eastern ridge, and somewhere beneath the mountain a court of old stone would look up from its table and say: *it is time. Send for ours.*

Unless someone got there first.

Rafael became aware she was crying only because a tear hit the ancient page and the vellum drank it instantly, greedily, the way everything in this house drank what you gave it. She wiped her face with her sleeve. Crying was a luxury itemized on no ledger she intended to balance tonight.

Think. She was a scribe. Thinking was her trade; ink was her weapon; and every contract ever written had a flaw, because contracts were written by hands, and hands were fallible, and fallibility was the only mercy the universe had ever extended to people like her.

*Condition of release: payment in full of principal and interest, or presentation of substitute of equal weight.*

Equal weight. The clause cut in every direction. Debts could be discharged, renegotiated, transferred—if you could get close enough to the ledger that held them. And somewhere under the mountain, presumably, the deep court kept a master ledger of its own. Every factor keeps the books. Even factors of souls.

She copied the entry. All of it, every annotation, every clause, in her own shorthand, fast, her pen scratching like a mouse in the walls. Then, because she was angry and frightened and those together made her stupid in the specific way that history rewarded, she did one thing more.

She signed her name beneath her copy. Full name. Blood-ink, from the pricked pad of her left thumb.

The ledger's page warmed another degree beneath her hand. She felt it distinctly now, like breath through paper, like something on the other side leaning closer to read along.

*Rafael Ravensworth,* the archive seemed to say. *We know. We've known since before you were born. Come down whenever you're ready. The door remembers your name.*

The lamp went out—not blown, just *out*, snuffed by nothing—and in the dark Rafael stood with her heart hammering and the copied pages crushed against her chest, listening to the vast archive breathe around her.

Above, through stone and floors and the ordinary wicked machinery of the guild, she heard footsteps cross the hall overhead. Slow. Unhurried. Stopping, she was almost certain, directly above the stair.

Then a voice, mild as plum wine, carrying down the spiral anyway, as if the building had been built to conduct it:

"Second Pen Ravensworth. Mind the red shelves. Some instruments bite."

Footsteps receding. A door closing. Silence.

Rafael stood in the dark a moment longer, then climbed toward the lamplight above, toward the child asleep behind a warded door, toward twenty-three hours and a mountain full of things that had her family's name in their mouths.

First moonrise. She had until first moonrise.

And she had just signed herself, in blood, into the middle of it.

* * *

She almost made it to Wren's door before the shaking started.

The corridor was empty, midnight-quiet, wardens posted at the outer stairs only—which meant someone wanted the child guarded from escape, not from intrusion. Rafael pressed her palm flat against the wood and felt, through it, the faint restless stir of a sleeping child, and that small warmth undid her.

Because now she knew the shape of the trap. Now she knew that the bread and the blanket and the warden's gruff kindness were all entries in a ledger, columns in a calculation two generations deep. The girl had walked out of the mountain as a message, a due-date stamped in flesh, and everyone who had fed her and sheltered her had been stamping the envelope.

Everyone except, possibly, Rafael. Who had held her and named her and understood her screaming without being taught. Who was, according to a page of brown ink, the alternate payment on her own grandmother's crime.

*I didn't know,* Rafael thought at the door, at the sleeping child behind it. *I swear I didn't know I was part of the price.*

From inside, faintly, in sleep, in the old tongue, the girl murmured a single word.

Rafael understood it perfectly.

*Mine.*

Not the guild's claim. Not the court's claim. The child, dreaming, claiming herself—or claiming Rafael; in that language the grammar refused to say which. The word that meant *debt* and *gift* both.

Rafael put her forehead against the door and breathed until her hands were steady, and began, silently, methodically, the way she drafted any difficult contract, to plan a theft.
