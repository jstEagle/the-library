## 4. The Man Who Counts for the Court

Tomas Vey counted things for a living. That was the joke he told himself on the bad nights—that a man could spend his whole life tallying other people's debts and never once audit his own.

He stood at the window of the counting room above the guild's eastern stair, where the ledgers lived behind iron grating and the air always smelled of tallow and cold stone. Below him, through rain-streaked glass, the courtyard lamps burned in their niches, and the storm that had dragged the child to their door still hammered the tiles like a creditor who wouldn't leave.

The child had been at the guild for two days now. Two days of fevered sleep in the infirmary loft, two days of Rafael sitting beside her with ink-stained fingers wrapped around one small muddy hand, two days of words spilling out of the girl in a language that had no business existing outside of sealed archives and older graves.

And Rafael had understood her.

Tomas had watched it happen from the doorway. He'd seen the moment the girl's voice cracked into that old tongue—the pre-kingdom speech, the deep court's language, the one the guild's own scholars called *dead as bedrock*—and he'd seen Rafael's face change. Not confusion. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing a song she'd forgotten she knew.

He should have reported it that night. He hadn't. He'd waited a day, telling himself he was waiting for confirmation, for corroboration, for anything that would let him put off the writing of the letter.

There was no more putting it off. The mirror in the corner of the counting room had begun to fog from the inside again, which meant they wanted to talk.

* * *

The mirrors were how the mountain spoke. Not every mirror—only certain ones, silvered with something darker than silver, hung in rooms the guild master pretended not to know about. Tomas had been given his first one eleven years ago, when he was nineteen and desperate and freshly indentured, his own debt written into the guild's books in figures he'd never be able to work off honestly. A man in grey robes had appeared at his lodging with an offer: carry messages, watch certain people, count certain things, and the debt would be managed. *Managed.* He'd been young enough to think that word meant forgiven.

Now he took down the mirror and breathed on the glass, the way he'd been taught, and waited for the frost to form letters.

It came slowly, the way it always did, each character blooming white like breath on a winter pane:

**THE CHILD. DESCRIBE HER.**

He picked up the stylus—a rod of bone capped with a sliver of slate—and wrote on the glass's frame, where the frost could read the vibration:

*Girl, seven or eight years. Found at the gate during the storm. No name offered. Speaks the old tongue.*

The answer came fast this time, faster than usual, and that speed itself was information. The mountain was interested. The mountain was *more* than interested.

**WHO ELSE HAS HEARD IT?**

*Rafael Ravensworth. She understood fragments.*

He wrote the name and felt it in his chest like a swallowed stone. There it was, then. Done. Eleven years of careful, quiet service, and all it took to break something was four words in his own handwriting.

The frost swirled, gathered, resolved:

**RAVENSWORTH. THE SCRIBE.**

Not a question. They knew her already. Of course they did. They'd known her longer than he had—he understood that now, had understood it for years in the way you understand a thing by refusing to look directly at it. Why else would a junior enforcer be assigned to shadow a copying clerk? Why else would his reports on her comings and goings be requested quarterly, meticulously, for a woman whose life consisted of ink, ledgers, and a rented room with a leaky roof?

Because her name was already in their books. Because somewhere beneath the mountain, in a vault he'd never seen, there was a page with *Ravensworth* written on it in a hand older than the kingdom.

**SHE IS NOT TO LEARN MORE. WATCH HER. REPORT EVERYTHING.**

Tomas set down the stylus. His hands were steady—they were always steady; that was what made him good at this work—but his pulse was loud in his ears, louder than the rain.

*Everything,* the frost had said. And everything included the way Rafael had held the girl's hand like she was afraid the world would take her. Everything included the fact that he loved her, though he'd never once written that, though he'd starved that truth in the dark for six years the way you starve anything you can't afford to feed.

He breathed on the glass one more time.

*Understood.*

The frost melted all at once, the way it did when the conversation was over, and the mirror showed him only himself: a broad, plain man of thirty with a scar through one eyebrow and the eyes of someone who counts.

* * *

He found Rafael in the scriptorium that evening, because he always found Rafael in the scriptorium, because some part of him had learned to arrange his patrols around the hours her lamp burned.

She was working late over a contract—a bottled storm, by the wax seal's color, bound for a shipping concern in the Salt Reaches—and she didn't look up when he came in. That was another thing about them: they had built a whole silent architecture out of ordinary moments. Him checking the ward-seals on the windows while she worked. Her sliding a cup of chicory to the edge of the desk without comment. Six years of almosts, stacked neat as folios.

"The child's fever broke," she said, still writing. "Master Corvane wants her moved to the undercroft tomorrow. 'For observation.'"

Tomas's stomach dropped. The undercroft was where the guild kept things it didn't want the city to know it had. "Observation," he repeated.

"That's the word he used." She set down her pen and finally looked at him, and her eyes had the flat shine he'd learned to read as fear wearing competence's clothes. "Tomas. She said something today. While I was sitting with her."

His heart began its slow, traitorous climb toward his throat. "What did she say?"

"Names." Rafael turned her pen over in her fingers, once, twice. "Old names. Court names. And—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, and he watched her decide, visibly, to trust him with the rest. "She said my family's name. Ravensworth. In the old grammar, the possessive form. Like she was reading it off a ledger."

The room got very small. Somewhere below them, in the dark under the mountain, he could almost feel pages turning.

"What did you tell Corvane?" he asked.

"Nothing yet." Her voice dropped. "That's why I'm telling you first. You're the one who knows how he works. If I report that the child knows my name—if I make myself part of whatever this is—"

"He'll use it." Tomas crossed to the window out of habit, checking the street below, the rooftops, the empty rain. "Corvane doesn't collect information, Rafe. He collects leverage. The moment your name is attached to that girl in his presence, you stop being a scribe and start being collateral."

The word was out of his mouth before he could catch it. *Collateral.* He saw it land—saw her flinch the way you flinch at a sound you don't recognize until after.

"Why that word?" she said quietly.

"It's guild vocabulary." It wasn't. Not in any contract he'd ever copied. It was mountain vocabulary, and he'd just handed her a splinter of the truth and called it nothing. He forced his shoulders loose, forced the enforcer's blandness back over his face like a hood. "I mean he'll bind you into something. Paper first, always paper."

Rafael studied him for a long moment. She was too sharp for him and always had been; the only reason his secrets had survived six years was that she trusted him enough not to sharpen herself against them.

"You'd tell me," she said. "If something was wrong. If I was walking into something."

"Yes." And God help him, he even believed it in that moment—believed that whatever the mountain ordered, he'd find a way to stand between her and it. That was the beautiful lie he'd been living inside, the one that let him sleep: *I'm watching her to protect her. The reports are just noise. They'll never act on them.*

The frost on his mirror tonight had said otherwise. *Watch her. Report everything.* Reports were never just noise. Reports were how debts got called in.

"I should get back," he said. "Ward rounds."

"Tomas." She caught his sleeve—not his hand, never his hand, they didn't do hands—and for a second her face was unguarded, and it undid him more than any interrogation ever had. "The girl. Wren—I've started calling her Wren, she makes a sound like one when she sleeps. Whatever Corvane intends for her... she's just a child. Whatever's owed here, she didn't sign anything."

*Didn't she,* Tomas thought, and said, "Get some sleep, Rafe."

* * *

The undercroft transfer happened at dawn, and Tomas made sure he was the one to oversee it, because if it had to happen at all, it would happen under his eye or not under anyone's.

Two porters carried the sleeping girl down on a litter. Rafael walked beside her the whole way, one hand resting on the blanket over the child's feet, and Master Corvane glided ahead of them all in his grey coat, keys singing softly on his belt.

The undercroft smelled of salt and old stone. Ward-lines crisscrossed the floor in patterns Tomas had never been permitted to study closely, and at the far end, behind a grate, sat the guild's sealed archive—the place where contracts went when they were too old, too strange, or too shameful for the open shelves.

Corvane paused at the archive door. Just paused. Looked at it the way a man looks at a strongbox.

Then he smiled at Rafael, all courtesy, and said, "You may return to your duties, Mistress Ravensworth. The child will want for nothing."

"She'll want for her name," Rafael said, "if nobody asks her for it."

"Names will come." Corvane's smile didn't move. "They always come due."

Tomas watched Rafael walk up the stairs, spine straight, hands fists inside her sleeves, and he marked the exact moment Corvane's gaze slid from her back to the archive door and stayed there, hungry as a lamp-flame.

* * *

That night, the mirror frosted without being summoned.

**THE SCRIBE DESCENDED TO THE UNDERCROFT UNINVITED. CONFIRM.**

He confirmed. What else was there? He described the exchange with Corvane, the archive door, the look. Every true thing, laid out in slate on silver, and each word a brick in the wall he was building between Rafael and himself, though she didn't know the wall existed.

**GOOD,** the frost wrote. **THE DEBT STIRS. WHEN IT COMES DUE, THE SCRIBE WILL BE NEEDED. SEE THAT SHE IS... AVAILABLE.**

*Available.* He stared at the word until the frost began to melt. Available the way a coin is available. Available the way collateral is available.

He thought of Rafael's hand on the child's blanket. He thought of the possessive grammar of an old name. He thought of eleven years of service measured out in small betrayals, none of which had ever cost him anything he couldn't bear to lose—until now, when the account had finally come due and the currency demanded was the only thing he'd ever owned outright.

Tomas breathed on the glass one last time.

*She will be available,* he wrote.

The frost accepted it. Somewhere far below, something turned a page.

Upstairs, a lamp still burned in the scriptorium window. He watched it for a long time, counting—as he counted everything—the hours left before the first moonrise, though he didn't yet know why that date mattered, only that the mountain had begun marking it in its margins, patient as ink.

Whatever was coming, it was coming for her. And he had just promised to open the door.
