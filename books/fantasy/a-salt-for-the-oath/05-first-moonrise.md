## 5. First Moonrise

The moon rose on schedule, and nothing happened.

Rafael had spent the whole day waiting for it the way you wait for a tooth to finish aching—half-dreading, half-hoping the dread was wrong. She'd checked the ledger three times. *Due at first moonrise.* The ink hadn't faded. The seal hadn't cracked. Somewhere in the deep archive, her grandmother's name still sat beside the word *collateral* like a stone at the bottom of a well.

And when the light came up silver over the guild's slate roofs and slid through the copying room's high windows, nothing in the building so much as shivered.

"Perhaps it means the first moonrise of the season," she told herself. "Or of the year. Or—" She stopped. She was doing what scribes did: looking for the loophole, the marginal note, the clause that would let everyone off. But debts like this didn't have loopholes. Debts like this had *terms*, and terms were patient.

The child—Wren, though no one had named her yet, not officially—spent the evening in the kitchen as she always did now, sitting on the hearthstone with her knees drawn up, watching the fire with an attention that seemed older than her small body. Cook had given up trying to make her eat with a spoon. She ate with her fingers, fast and neat, like something that had once known hunger in a place where hunger meant more than an empty stomach.

Rafael sat across from her with a ledger open on her knees, pretending to work.

"You understand me," Rafael said quietly, in the old tongue—the few words of it she'd recovered from her grandmother's letters, the fragments that had come back to her like water finding an old channel. *You are safe here.*

Wren looked up. Her eyes caught the firelight and held it a moment too long, the way a mirror does.

"I know," Rafael said, switching back to common speech because the old tongue exhausted her, each word costing something she couldn't name. "I know you know."

The girl tilted her head. Then she did the thing she'd been doing more often lately: she reached out and touched Rafael's wrist, two fingers, light as a moth landing, and held them there for exactly as long as it took Rafael's pulse to beat three times. Then she let go and went back to watching the fire.

Rafael had asked Master Corvane about it once. *She takes your pulse,* he'd said, smiling his dry smile. *Children do strange things.* He'd said it too smoothly. He'd been watching Wren too closely ever since the night she arrived, and Rafael had learned, over years of indenture, to read the guild master the way she read contracts—not for what he wrote but for what he left blank.

That night, Rafael walked Wren up to the little room under the eaves they'd given her, past the salt line Cook had laid across the threshold without being asked, without being told why. The girl stepped over it carefully, respectfully, the way you step over a sleeping dog.

In the morning, the salt was scattered. Not swept—scattered, flung outward from the door in a fine white spray, as if something inside the room had exhaled hard enough to move it.

Rafael told herself it was a draft.

*

It was on the third night after the moonrise that Rafael saw it, and once she'd seen it she could never unsee it again.

Wren was washing her hands at the scullery basin. It was late; the kitchen was down to embers; Rafael had come down for ink and found herself lingering, the way she lingered everywhere near the girl lately, unable to explain the pull except as debt—as if proximity were a payment plan.

The lamplight fell across the back of Wren's hands.

Her hands were translucent.

Not ghost-pale. Not the blue-white of cold. *Translucent*—the way breath fogs a window and then thins away from the edges first. Rafael could see the basin's rim through the girl's smallest finger, could see the curve of the ceramic where there should have been bone.

She stood very still. The water ran. Wren hummed a scrap of melody in the old tongue, something looping and minor, a lullaby or a counting song, and didn't seem to notice anything wrong with her own hands at all.

"Wren." Rafael's voice came out wrong, too thin. "Let me see your hands."

The girl turned, dripping, and offered them up like a child showing off clean fingernails. In full lamplight the effect was subtler—the translucence lived mostly at the edges, at the fingertips, at the outermost curve of each finger, as if she were a photograph whose borders were being eaten by light. But it was there. Rafael took one hand and pressed it flat against her own palm and felt—nothing. No warmth. No weight. A pressure like pressing on the surface of very cold water.

"How long," Rafael whispered, to herself, to the room, to whatever was listening. "How long has this been happening?"

Because here was the thing that made her stomach drop through the floor: she'd been looking at those hands every day. She'd held one of them just last night, during a nightmare, when Wren cried out in the old language and Rafael had sat with her until dawn. And she hadn't seen it. Which meant either it hadn't been there—or it had been there, growing, and Rafael had looked at a child every single day and seen only what she expected to see.

Scribes were trained to notice. That was the whole trade: notice the discrepancy, the altered figure, the name spelled one letter wrong. And she had missed this, night after night, because some part of her had needed not to see it.

The deadline hadn't passed unmarked after all. The deadline had passed, and the marking had begun—slowly, silently, in the only currency the mountain court dealt in. It was taking her back. Grain by grain. Edge by edge.

*

Master Corvane examined the girl's hands the next morning with the detached interest of a man appraising a shipment.

"Hm," he said. He turned Wren's fingers toward the window light, and the translucence bloomed visibly, the tips going glassy as lake ice. "Curious."

"It's not curious," Rafael said. Her voice shook. "It's a *collection*. Something is collecting her. Piece by piece. The debt came due and the court is—drawing it down. Like a creditor drawing on an account."

Corvane folded his hands behind his back. "You've been reading the sealed archives again."

"I've been reading the archives I'm entitled to read, which is none of them, which is why I read them." She was past caring about insubordination. "Master, look at her. Look at her *edges*. Tell me what happens when the fading reaches her heart. Tell me what happens when there's nothing left to fade."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. Then he crouched, which he almost never did, and spoke to Wren directly, in careful, accented common speech: "Do you hurt, little one?"

Wren considered him. Then she answered—and she answered in the old tongue, a full sentence, fluid and unhurried, and Rafael caught perhaps one word in five. Something about *cold*. Something about *coming*. Something about a word that recurred, that Rafael had heard before in her grandmother's letters and had never been able to translate: a word that sounded like *underneath* and *remembering* at once.

"What did she say?" Rafael demanded.

"She said," Corvane translated slowly, "that the ones below are counting. That they count well. That she is nearly counted." He straightened. His face did something complicated—something hungry quickly folded shut into something merely tired. "She also said that we should not worry, because worrying changes nothing below the mountain. They are very firm on that point."

"They're *taking her apart*—"

"They are taking back what was sold," Corvane said, and his voice had the flat authority of a man reading from a document he knew by heart. "Which is what courts do. Which is what *guilds* do, Ravensworth, if you want the truth of our trade. We sell impossible things. Someone, somewhere, always pays. Your family paid. Generations ago. The bill has simply arrived."

"Then void it. You're the Guild of Impossible Things. Unselling a soul should be within your charter."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—Rafael would remember that flicker later, would turn it over and over in the weeks to come, trying to decide whether it had been greed or fear or both wearing each other's clothes. "Perhaps," he said. "Perhaps there is a transaction to be discussed. When the time is right."

"When? When the fading reaches her heart? When there's less of her to bargain over every night?" Rafael's voice cracked against the stone walls. "Every hour we wait, she weighs less. Is that how you negotiate—wait until the collateral is thin enough to be cheap?"

Corvane looked at her for a long, measuring moment. "You care for her," he said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind. It was filed away. Everything with Corvane was filed away.

*

That night Rafael did not sleep. She sat in the copying room with every lamp lit, surrounded by paper, and tried to think like a scribe instead of like a frightened woman, because frightened women froze and scribes worked.

If the court was drawing the debt down nightly, then the process had rules. Everything below the mountain had rules—that much she'd gleaned from the archives, from her grandmother's letters, from the way the ledgers themselves behaved, all clauses and conditions and terrible precision. Rules meant mechanics. Mechanics meant a rate. A rate could be measured.

So she measured.

Each night, after Wren slept, Rafael brought her to the copying room's one good lamp and held the girl's hands flat against a sheet of heavy rag paper, and traced them. Outlines. She dated each one. She noted the degree of translucence in the margins using the only vocabulary she had—*faint, marked, severe*—knowing even as she wrote the words that she was inventing a taxonomy for a horror no guild handbook covered.

Night one: fingertips only. Night four: to the first knuckle. Night seven: past the second knuckle, and the left ear's outer rim gone glassy, and—a detail that made Rafael sit down hard on the stool—one small patch at Wren's temple, above the ear, where the skin had thinned so far that Rafael could see, faint and terrible, the shape of something beneath. Not bone. Bone she could have borne. It was a shape like script. Like writing. Curved lines, dense, deliberate, looping around itself.

Words. There were *words written inside the child*, visible now that she was thinning, as if the fading were stripping away everything she'd been made of since and revealing what lay underneath.

Rafael copied the shapes as best she could, freehand, trembling, onto a fresh sheet. Then she went to the one person in the guild who might know what they were—not Corvane, never Corvane—and found the old archivist, Brother Anselm, half-blind and half-mad and the only scholar alive who'd spent thirty years cataloguing pre-kingdom fragments before the guild sealed the collection.

He held her copy close to his good eye. He was quiet for a long time. Then his hands started shaking worse than usual.

"That's not decoration," he said. "That's a contract. That's a *contract text*, written in the old formal hand—the hand they use below for binding instruments. What you've copied is part of the body of an agreement. Terms. Conditions." He lowered the page. "Child, do you understand what I'm telling you? The girl isn't carrying a mark of the debt. The girl *is* the debt. Written out. Inscribed. She was drafted as a legal instrument before she was born, or she was made from one, or—" he rubbed his clouded eye—"or the soul that was sold was written into flesh, and what's fading away is everything that was added over her, and what's underneath is the original document."

"The original document," Rafael repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. "You mean my grandmother's contract."

"I mean a contract. Whether it's yours—" Anselm set the page down and looked at her with the awful gentleness of old men delivering bad news. "Ravensworth. Read the margin of your own tracing. You dated these outlines. Seven nights. Do you know what happens on the fourteenth? Because the instrument does. Contracts have completion dates. Whatever is written in that child will finish executing, and when a contract finishes executing, the parties settle."

"Settle."

"The court gets its due. The debtor's line gets—" he waved a hand, vague and dreadful. "Closure. Release. Whatever the drafters promised themselves closure meant. These old instruments weren't cruel, exactly. They were *thorough*. Thoroughness looks like cruelty from the outside."

Seven nights. Fourteen minus seven. A week.

Rafael walked back through the darkened guild halls holding her lantern low, and the corridors seemed longer than they'd ever been, and somewhere overhead the moon was rising again—second moonrise, third, she'd lost exact count, and the count was the only thing that mattered now. She passed the sealed archive door and put her palm flat against it, and the iron was cold, and beneath the cold she thought she felt—she would never be sure, she would doubt it every day for the rest of her life—a faint vibration. A rhythm. Slow and vast and patient.

Like counting.

*

Wren was awake when Rafael reached the eave-room. Sitting up in the narrow bed, knees drawn to her chest, watching the moonlight lie across the floorboards in a long silver bar. The salt line outside the door was scattered again. Third time. Rafael had stopped calling it a draft.

The girl saw her coming and didn't startle. She held up one hand—palm out, fingers spread, an offering or a request—and in the moonlight the translucence was unmistakable now, the whole hand gone to sea-glass, the inner script faintly luminous, and Rafael felt something in her chest tear along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"You can feel it happening," Rafael said. She sat on the edge of the bed. "Can't you. You've always been able to feel it. Even before the moonrise. Even before the guild. You've been fading your whole life, haven't you—so slowly no one ever noticed, and now they've stopped being slow."

Wren didn't answer in words. She did something worse. She took Rafael's hand and pressed it flat against her own sternum, over her heart, and held it there, and Rafael felt the heartbeat—quick, small, a bird's heartbeat—but she felt it *through* something, through a thickness like packed snow, like the sound of a bell heard from underwater. Fainter than it should have been. Fainter than it had been two weeks ago. She was certain of that. Certain the way a scribe is certain of a discrepancy: not because she could prove it, but because her trained mind refused, absolutely refused, to record the two readings as equal.

Then Wren spoke. One sentence, in the old tongue, slow enough that Rafael could hold each syllable up to the light of her grandmother's letters and match what she could.

She understood it. All of it. For the first time, completely, as if the meaning had been waiting in her blood for exactly this moment:

*"When I am counted full, they will come up to collect the rest."*

The rest. Not the girl alone. The *rest*.

Rafael pulled her hand back. The room was very cold. The moonlight lay on the floor between them like a blade laid down, waiting to be picked up.

"Rest of what," she whispered. But she already knew. She was a scribe. She'd spent six years learning what documents do when their terms complete—they don't end, they *expand*. They call in related obligations. They notify guarantors. Collateral covers the principal, and then the interest comes due, and the interest on a soul-debt generations old, compounded across a family line—

Her family line. Her name. *Ravensworth.*

The girl watched her with those fire-catching eyes and repeated the sentence, quieter, and this time Rafael heard the structure beneath it, the grammar of it, and the grammar was merciless: *when I am counted full.* Not *if*. The instrument was executing. The fourteen nights were not a warning. They were a countdown built into the text of a contract written before the kingdom, before the guild, before any of them had a choice about any of it.

Rafael got up. She lit every candle in the eave-room, because the dark had become an enemy, and then she went downstairs to the copying room and sat before a blank sheet of paper until the sky went grey.

She wrote one line at the top, in her best formal hand, the hand she used for instruments that mattered:

*Terms of settlement, proposed by the debtor's line.*

Below it, nothing yet. Just the heading, and the date, and the number of nights remaining, which she calculated twice and checked against the moon through the window, because errors in this document would cost more than ink.

Six nights.

Somewhere below the mountain, something was counting too. And it counted better.

She dipped her pen. Outside, the first bell of dawn rang, and beneath its bronze voice, faint and rhythmic and horribly patient, she could almost hear the other counter keeping time.
