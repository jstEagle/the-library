## 6. Terms of Service

The guild master sent for her at the hour when the lamps burned lowest and the clerks had all gone home to whatever passed for lives among people who sold years by the jar.

Rafael climbed the spiral stair to his office with her hands cold and her mind already drafting. That was the sickness of her trade: even walking toward what she feared, she composed. *Whereas the party of the first part... Whereas the party of the second part...* She could feel the shape of the document waiting for her on the other side of that door, because guild masters never summoned you to say anything kind. Kindness didn't require paper.

The door was oak older than the city, carved with the guild's sigil—a key turning inside a lock that had no door—and it opened before she knocked.

"Come in, Ravensworth." Master Corvane's voice carried the particular warmth of a man who had already decided how this evening would end and was simply being generous about the route.

His office smelled of tallow and old vellum and, beneath both, something mineral. Stone dust. The smell of deep places. Rafael catalogued it without meaning to—the way she catalogued everything now, since the ledgers had shown her their teeth.

Corvane sat behind a desk bare except for a single document, face down, weighted with a lump of mountain glass. He gestured to the chair across from him. It was a good chair. Comfortable. Positioned so its occupant sat slightly lower than he did, looking up into the light of his lamp rather than beside it. Rafael had sat in chairs like that her whole indenture. She'd only recently understood they were designed.

"Master."

"You've been in the sealed archives." Not a question. "Third vault. The pre-consolidation holdings."

She kept her face still. "I file where I'm told."

"You file where you're told," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "And yet three nights running, the archive seal has been broken and re-set by someone with excellent technique and a scribe's hand. Someone who then spent an entire day copying a single page from a ledger that hasn't been opened in sixty years." He leaned back. "I don't mind, you understand. I want to be clear about that. Curiosity is the raw material of our trade. A scribe without curiosity is just a machine with ink stains."

"But?"

"But curiosity unmanaged becomes liability." He turned the document over with one finger, unhurried, and slid it across the desk until it faced her. "And you, Ravensworth, have become very interesting to certain parties who do not distinguish between curiosity and liability."

She looked down.

Her own name looked back at her. *RAFAEL RAVENSWORTH*, in formal capitals, followed by a block of terms in the dense legal hand the guild used for instruments involving things that couldn't be bought any other way. Below the terms, a signature line waited, blank as an open grave.

She read. She made herself read slowly, the way she'd been trained—every clause twice, every conditional aloud in her head—because contracts like this one killed through speed. They counted on the reader's fear making them skim.

*Whereas the Guild of Impossible Things holds certain obligations outstanding against the Court Beneath the Mountain, being debts of long standing and original principal undisclosed;*

*Whereas the said Court has called upon the Guild to render payment in full at first moonrise following the autumn equinox, such payment being collateral held under bond of blood;*

*Whereas the party known as Wren, of no surname, arrived at these premises bearing the mark of the said collateral;*

*Now therefore the Guild proposes:*

She read the proposal three times. Then she sat back, and Corvane watched her sit back, and neither of them spoke for a moment, because they both knew exactly what the document said and pretending otherwise would have insulted them both.

"You want me to go down there," Rafael said. "Into the court. And steal it back."

"I want you to *recover* it," Corvane corrected gently. "Steal is such an ugly word for what will essentially be a negotiation conducted by other means. The guild does not steal. The guild acquires. There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork."

"And if I refuse?"

He spread his hands, a gesture of terrible openness. "Then nothing happens to you tonight. You go back to your desk tomorrow. You finish your indenture in—" he consulted a ledger open on the corner of his desk, though Rafael would have bet her last coin he knew the number to the hour— "four hundred and eleven days. You walk out of here a free woman, and the child dissolves over six nights like breath off glass, and the debt passes to the next of your line, which, as of the moment your grandmother's soul was taken, is you. And after you, whoever comes after you. The court is patient. Bloodlines are long. They can wait out a family the way water waits out stone."

"You'd let it happen."

"I would *witness* it happen," Corvane said. "There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork." He tapped the document once. "Read the consideration clause, Ravensworth. Read what we're offering."

She read it again, because he asked, and because some traitor part of her wanted to hear it in her own voice.

*In consideration of successful recovery of the collateral soul, the Guild shall void in full the indenture of the party Rafael Ravensworth, remit all outstanding debt, and release her name from the rolls of bonded labor, effective the hour of delivery.*

Four hundred and eleven days. Gone. Her freedom, the thing she'd counted in margins for nine years, scratched into the corners of every contract she'd ever copied like a prisoner counting walls—offered to her in exchange for a burglary in hell.

"It's too clean," she said.

"It's a fair offer."

"It's too clean," she repeated. "Nothing in this building is ever this clean. Where's the failure clause?"

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Respect, maybe, or the pleasure of a fisherman feeling the line go taut. He turned the document around, ran his finger down past the consideration, and there it was, in the smaller hand the guild used for clauses it hoped you wouldn't reach:

*Should the party fail to recover the collateral, or should she abandon the attempt, or should she reveal the terms herein to any authority outside the Guild, then the Guild shall retain custody of the child Wren in perpetuity, and shall further assume custody of the party Rafael Ravensworth, her indenture being extended without term, her person becoming guild property absolute, assignable at the Guild's discretion.*

*Assignable at the Guild's discretion.*

She looked up. "You'd sell me."

"The guild would honor its obligations," Corvane said. "To whomsoever held the paper." He folded his hands. "You should understand the position you're actually in, Ravensworth, because I respect you enough to say it plainly. The court below has already claimed the child. That claim is real, it is old, and it is enforceable in ways that make my wardens look like parish constables. In six nights, whether you sign or not, whether you fight or not, that girl goes to pieces like wet paper, and the piece of her that matters goes beneath the mountain, and your family's name stays nailed to the debt until your line runs dry. Those are the facts. My offer doesn't change them. My offer is simply the only door out of the room that doesn't lead through the wall."

"And if I run? Take her and go?"

"Then you're in breach, and everything I just described happens, plus the wardens." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "You've been watching us for weeks now. You know what the wardens are for."

She did. She'd copied their commissioning contracts.

Rafael looked at the signature line. Her hand, she noticed distantly, had gone very steady—that awful calm that came over her when the worst thing was also the clearest thing. Nine years of indenture had taught her one lesson above all others: *read the trap before you step in it.* But knowing a trap was a trap didn't help when the alternative was the floor opening underneath you.

"If I do this," she said slowly, "I set the terms of approach. I choose what I carry, who I speak to, what I tell the wardens. You give me access to the deep archives—all of them, not the ones you ration out. And I want it in the instrument. Not verbal. Paper."

"Done."

"And the child stays untouched while I'm gone. Fed, warm, unmarked. If I come back and find so much as a bruise on her—"

"Ravensworth." Corvane's voice lost its warmth entirely, and somehow that was more honest than anything else he'd said all night. "You have my word on the child, witnessed and sealed. I keep my paper. It's the only god I've ever worshipped, and I've never once found it false." He pushed the pen across the desk. It was a good pen. Guild-issue, goose-quill, cut for a heavy hand. She'd used a hundred like it.

She took it anyway.

One last time, she read the whole instrument top to bottom, because errors in this document would cost more than ink. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far below the floor, faint as a heartbeat through six stories of stone, she felt—or imagined she felt—the other counter keeping time.

*Rafael Ravensworth.*

She signed.

The ink caught the lamplight, black and glossy, and for a heartbeat it seemed to sink into the page faster than ink ought to, drinking downward, and Corvane drew the document back with the tenderness of a man collecting a debt he'd been owed for years.

"Wise," he said. "You'll want to begin preparations immediately. Salt, iron, a key—I'll see you provisioned from the stores. And Ravensworth?" She paused at the door. "The court will know your grandmother's name. It's written in their ledgers deeper than yours is written in mine. When the doors ask you who you are—and they will ask—don't flinch from it. Debts open doors down there. It's the one inheritance your family left you that appreciates in value."

She stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to.

"Why me?" she asked. "You have wardens. You have thieves on retainer—you licensed half of them yourself. Why send a scribe?"

Corvane considered her across the lamplight, and for once he seemed to weigh the answer honestly.

"Because the doors remember names," he said, "and yours is the name they remember. Because the court trusts blood the way my clerks trust countersigned paper. And because—" the warmth came back, thin and terrible— "because you'll try harder than any thief I could hire. Thieves want payment. You want your grandmother back, and your name, and that child breathing. Motive, Ravensworth, is the rarest commodity in my trade. I deal in impossible things. You, just now, are the most impossible thing in this building."

* * *

The corridor outside was dark, and she walked it slowly, one hand trailing the wall, letting the stone hold her up.

She had signed. The fact sat in her chest like swallowed ice. Nine years of careful, gray, survivable obedience, and in one evening she'd traded it for a suicide's errand with a bonus clause enslaving her forever if she stumbled. Any rational clerk would call it the worst bargain of the season.

But the alternative had been arithmetic too. Six nights. A girl going translucent at the edges, night by night, like breath off glass. Rafael had watched it begin two evenings ago—had seen the lamplight pass faintly through Wren's wrist where the bones should have stopped it—and she had understood, with the cold clarity of her trade, that there were only two kinds of people in any contract: those who signed, and those who were signed for.

Wren had been signed for before she was born. Before anyone alive had been born.

So Rafael had picked up the pen.

At the bottom of the stairs, a lamp still burned in the copying room, and she found Tomas there, alone, ostensibly auditing a stack of storm-bottle manifests. He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did—the small, controlled softening he thought nobody noticed, the way his shoulders came down a half-inch, as if she were the one room in the world where he could stand at ease.

"You were up there a long time," he said.

"He likes to talk."

"What did he want?"

Rafael crossed to her own desk, where her grandmother's stolen page lay hidden beneath a year of honest work, and rested her hand flat on the wood. She should lie to him. Every instinct of self-preservation said lie—he was the guild's enforcer, its counter, the man whose whole craft was knowing when numbers didn't add up. If Corvane had a spy in her life, it was the man currently watching her with those careful grey eyes.

But he had brought the child blankets the first night, without being asked. He had stood in the doorway of the sickroom with his hat in his hands like a man at a graveside. And in four days, maybe five, she would be beneath the mountain, and if she died there, the last true thing anyone knew about her would be whatever she chose to leave behind.

"He offered me my freedom," she said. "In exchange for going down and taking Wren's soul back from the court."

Tomas went very still. It was a professional stillness, total, the stillness of a man whose entire body had just become a locked room. "And?"

"And I signed."

"Rafael—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it's madness. I priced the madness. It was cheaper than the alternative." She pulled out the chair and sat, and only then did she let him see how much the signing had cost, because he was going to see it eventually anyway, and better now than in front of the wardens. "Six nights, Tomas. I watched her wrist go transparent. Whatever else is true, that part isn't negotiable. Nobody's coming to save her. So it's me or it's the mountain, and the mountain already has her."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp between them hissed and guttered. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and stripped of everything except the words.

"What do you need?"

It was not the question she'd expected. She'd braced for argument, for warning, for the smooth official concern of a guild officer—and instead he asked it like a man asking for orders, which frightened her more than any of it, because Tomas Vael did not take orders from her. Tomas Vael took orders from the guild. From *them*. And here he was, offering, before she'd even asked.

"Salt," she said. "A lot of it. White, coarse, unblessed. Iron nails, new-forged. And I need to know everything the guild's records hold about the roads under the mountain—which doors, which wards, which of them eat people." She met his eyes. "Can you get me that without Corvane knowing?"

Something moved behind his face—fast, buried, gone. Something that looked almost like relief, or like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Yes," Tomas said. "I can get you that."

Outside, the moon was rising over the guildhouse roof, one night closer to full, and Rafael thought of the clause she'd signed, and the number shrinking in the margin of her private ledger, and the small translucent girl asleep three floors above her, dreaming in a language older than kingdoms.

Five nights.

She dipped her pen and began to plan.
