## 7. Salt and Small Mercies

The guild kept its salt in the cellar, in barrels stenciled with the seal of the Office of Boundaries, and Rafael stole three fistfuls of it on a Tuesday while Master Corvane was upstairs explaining to a client why his bottled storm had developed opinions.

She measured it out that night on the floor of her cell—her room, she corrected herself; indentured scribes did not get cells, they got rooms with doors that locked from the outside—and drew the first ward in a circle around her cot. The old tongue had no word for *ward*, as far as she could tell. It had a word for *edge*, and a word for *keep*, and when she spoke them together over the line of salt, the grains shivered and stood up like tiny pale soldiers.

Wren, curled in the corner where Rafael had made her a nest of blankets, watched with enormous eyes.

"Does it hold?" Rafael asked.

The girl tilted her head. She was better now—color in her cheeks, mud scrubbed away, eating like a small wolf—but the fading still came at night. Every evening at moonrise her edges went soft, translucent as breath off glass, and every dawn she solidified again, a little less of herself each time. Rafael had taken to counting the freckles on Wren's left hand. There had been eleven. This morning there were nine.

"Does it hold?" Rafael asked again, slower, pointing at the circle.

Wren considered the salt. Then she said something in the old tongue—a short phrase, two words—and pointed at the window, where the moon was rising fat and white over the guild's slate roofs.

Rafael's skin prickled. "That's not an answer."

But it was. When the moon cleared the rooftops, the translucence crept up Wren's fingers and stopped dead at the salt line, like water meeting a dam. The girl looked down at her own fading hand inside the circle and then up at Rafael, and smiled—the first true smile Rafael had seen from her, gap-toothed and crooked and so sudden it hurt.

"Good," Rafael said, and her voice cracked on the word, and she pretended it hadn't.

* * *

She named her on Thursday.

It wasn't a decision so much as an accident. The child had been three days in the guild's care, and the other scribes had begun to ask questions Rafael couldn't answer—who is she, whose is she, what do we call her—and Rafael had been deflecting with the practiced ease of a woman who spent her days making impossible things sound contractual. But names were different. Names were load-bearing.

She found the answer in the margins of her own work. She'd been copying a contract for a fishmonger's borrowed luck—seven years of good catches in exchange for the seventh son's firstborn smile, standard terms, standard cruelty—and in the margin, in her own idle hand, she'd sketched a little bird. A wren. Small, brown, ordinary, loud far beyond its size.

She looked at the sketch. She looked at the child sitting cross-legged on the floor of the scriptorium, sorting discarded quills by length with fierce concentration, humming a tune in the old tongue that made the inkwells ripple.

"Wren," Rafael said.

The child looked up.

"It means nothing," Rafael said. "Or it means everything. Small bird. Loud voice. Survives winters." She tapped the sketch. "I knew a woman once who said wrens carry the souls of people who died owing something. They come back to finish their accounts."

Wren crawled across the floor, took Rafael's hand, and pressed it flat against her own chest. Under the ribs, her heart beat quick and light, exactly like a bird's.

"That's not reassuring," Rafael told her. "You understand that's not reassuring."

Wren smiled her crooked smile and repeated, carefully, in Rafael's own language: "*Wren.*"

Her accent was terrible. It was the best thing Rafael had heard in years.

* * *

The key was harder than the salt.

Master Corvane kept the archive keys on a ring at his belt, and the ring never left him—he slept with it, bathed with it, ate with it clinking against his knee like a second heartbeat. The door at the bottom of the east stair, the one marked with the sigil of the deep court, needed a key of black iron that Rafael had only ever seen once, the night Wren arrived, when Corvane had unlocked the sealed reading room and let the heralds' messenger inside.

But keys had shadows, and shadows had habits, and Rafael had spent six years learning the guild's habits because learning them was the only power an indentured scribe was allowed.

Corvane hung the ring on a hook behind his chair during meals. He took it down for his evening walk through the stacks. He wore it to chapel on rest-days, which meant that between the last bell of the workday and the walk, there was a window—narrow, maybe forty minutes—when the ring sat unattended in his chambers while he dined.

The problem was his clerk, Pell, who sat outside the door sharpening pens and minding everyone's business.

The solution, Rafael decided, was fire. Not real fire. Guild fire—the kind you reported.

On Friday evening she caught Pell in the corridor and told him, breathless, that she'd seen smoke seeping under the door of the map room, and Pell went at a dead run because the map room held three centuries of irreplaceable charts, and while he ran Rafael slipped into Corvane's chamber, lifted the black iron key from the ring, and pressed a lump of wax into its place on the hook. The wax was the same color as the iron. It would fool no one who looked closely. But nobody looked closely at hooks; they looked at fires.

There was no fire. By the time anyone checked the map room, the smoke smell Rafael had brewed from scorched paper and tallow had already faded, and Pell stood in an empty room feeling foolish, and Master Corvane docked his wages for leaving his post, and Rafael copied contracts all evening with steady hands and a heart going like a drum.

The key weighed almost nothing. It burned a hole in her pocket anyway.

* * *

They studied at night, after the guild slept.

Rafael would bar her door, light one candle stub, and lay out the pages she'd been hoarding: fragments of the old tongue copied from the sealed archives, from marginalia, from a half-burned letter some ancestor had hidden in a ledger binding two hundred years ago. The language had no alphabet she recognized—or rather, it had one, but it was written the way roots grow, branching downward instead of across, and every word was also a small drawing of the thing it meant. *Mountain* was a triangle with veins. *Debt* was a hand closing.

Wren taught her the sounds.

The girl had no patience for grammar and infinite patience for repetition. She would say a word, tap Rafael's lips until they shaped it right, say it again. *Edh.* Edge. *Kael.* Keep. *Vess.* The word for soul, which was drawn as a flame inside a cage inside a hand, and which made Wren go quiet and hard-eyed every time, so Rafael learned it fast, learned it first, learned to say it without flinching: *vess.*

"You were someone," Rafael said one night, in the middle of a lesson. "Before. You know that, don't you."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The candle guttered. Outside, wind worried the shutters the way a dog worries a bone.

"I remember," Wren said—in trade-tongue, halting, the words costing her effort she usually refused to spend. "Water. A garden. Someone singing." Her small hands closed. "Then dark. Then small." She looked up. "Being small is loud. Everything is too big and too bright and I can't—" She ran out of words, or courage, and finished in the old tongue: *vess kael edh.* Soul, keep, edge. My soul keeps its edges. Or perhaps: keep my soul at the edge. The language was slippery that way. Perhaps deliberately.

Rafael pulled the girl against her side. Wren fit under her arm the way a wren fits in a hedge—exactly, as if designed for it.

"When we go down," Rafael said, "I'm going to find whoever holds your vess, and I'm going to take it back, and I'm going to put it wherever it belongs. And then—" She stopped. She had been about to say *and then you'll be whole again,* but she was a contract-scribe, and she had learned never to promise terms she couldn't guarantee. "And then we'll see," she finished. "Whatever comes after, we'll see it together."

Wren nodded against her shoulder. Then, muffled: "Your accent is getting better."

"My accent is magnificent."

"It is getting better," Wren conceded, with the gravity of a magistrate, and Rafael laughed, and the laugh echoed strangely in the small stone room, and neither of them mentioned that it might be one of the last laughs her voice had left in it.

* * *

The nightmares came for both of them.

Wren's were the worst. She would wake screaming in the old tongue—words Rafael didn't have yet, words that sounded like stones grinding—and Rafael would gather her up and carry her to the window and point at the moon and say *edh, kael, edh* until the shaking stopped. Once, half-asleep, Wren whispered a name over and over: *Maren, Maren, Maren.*

Rafael's grandmother had been Maren Ravensworth.

She didn't tell the child that. Some knowledge was a weight you carried so others wouldn't have to.

Rafael's own dreams were quieter and worse. She dreamed of ledgers unrolling to the horizon, every page bearing her grandmother's name beside the word *collateral*, and at the end of the rolls a figure in grey who turned page after page and said, in Master Corvane's voice, *due at first moonrise, due at first moonrise, due at first moonrise.* She woke each morning with her jaw aching from clenching and her hands cold, and she got up, and she salted the thresholds, and she worked.

On the ninth night, Wren woke screaming a new word.

Rafael lit the candle and took the girl's face in her hands. "What is it? What did you see?"

Wren's eyes were huge and wet. "He knows," she said in trade-tongue. "The counting man. He knows you have the key."

Rafael went very still. "Tomas?"

"The counting man," Wren said again, and then, in the old tongue, a phrase Rafael had just learned that week, one that appeared in the archives beside the deep court's seal: *the one who counts for below.*

* * *

She found Tomas at dawn, as she'd known she would, standing on the guild's eastern wall where the mountain filled half the sky, grey and patient and vast enough to have weather.

"You've been avoiding me," he said.

"You've been letting me."

He almost smiled at that. He was a big man, Tomas, built like something the guild had commissioned rather than born, with a face that gave away nothing except, occasionally, to her, everything. Six years of stolen moments in stairwells and stack-rooms, six years of a love neither of them had ever named aloud because naming things made them real and real things could be leveraged. That was the first law of the guild, though it wasn't written anywhere: everything can be leveraged.

"Where did you go Tuesday night?" he asked. "Pell says there was a fire."

"There was no fire."

"No," Tomas agreed. "There wasn't." He turned to look at her fully, and his eyes were the worst thing she'd seen all week, because they weren't cold. They were grieving. "Rafael. Whatever you're planning—don't."

"You don't know what I'm planning."

"I know you." He said it simply, like a man stating the terms of a contract he'd signed long ago. "I know how you look when you're building something out of desperation. You look calm. You're looking calm right now."

Below them, in the yard, Wren was teaching one of the kitchen boys to skip rope, counting in the old tongue, her voice carrying thin and clear in the cold air. *Edh. Teth. Vael.* One. Two. Three.

Tomas flinched at the sound. Actually flinched, shoulders dropping a half-inch, like a man hearing a debt called in.

"She fades a little more every night," Rafael said quietly. "You've seen it. Everyone's seen it. In a few weeks there won't be enough of her to fade." She stepped closer. "I'm going down, Tomas. And you're either going to help me, or stand aside, or try to stop me. Those are the only terms on offer."

For a long moment he said nothing. The mountain watched them both. Somewhere beneath its roots, she thought, lords of old stone were turning pages.

"If you go down there," Tomas finally said, "they will know within the hour. They always know. And they will send for me, because I am the man who counts for them, and they will order me to bring you back." His jaw worked. "And I don't know what I'll say."

"Yes, you do," Rafael said.

He closed his eyes. "Yes," he admitted. "I do."

She left him there and went down to breakfast, and if her hands shook as she salted her door that evening, no one saw it, and if Wren noticed the extra line of grains around the windowsill, the child said nothing—only took Rafael's hand and taught her one more word before sleep.

The word was *miren*. It meant *come home*.

Rafael practiced it until she could say it perfectly. She had a feeling she was going to need it soon.
