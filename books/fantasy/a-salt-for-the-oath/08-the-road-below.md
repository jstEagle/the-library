## 8. The Road Below

The order came at the hour when even candles give up.

Tomas was in the counting room, as he always was after midnight, reconciling the guild's ledgers against the court's copies. It was work that required no thought and all of it—columns of names and sums that meant the same thing in two languages, *years* in one script and *souls* in the other. He had been doing it for eleven years. He had stopped tasting the numbers somewhere around year four.

The knock came once, then not again.

He knew what one knock meant. Two knocks meant a warden with routine business. Three meant the guild master wanted something witnessed. One knock meant the mountain was speaking, and the mountain did not speak often, and it never spoke kindly.

"Enter," he said, though the door was already opening.

The herald stood in the frame like something poured rather than born—a tall shape in grey, its face the smooth suggestion of a face, the way stone remembers water. Heralds were permitted to look almost human during daylight errands. At night they didn't bother.

*"The scribe moves before moonrise tomorrow,"* it said. Its voice was gravel settling in a sack. *"She carries salt and a stolen key. You will intercept her at the ferry crossing and return her to the guild. The child stays. The debt stands."*

Tomas kept his hands flat on the ledger. "She's under contract."

*"She is under contract."* The herald's mouth moved without disturbing the rest of its face. *"So are you."*

It left the way weather leaves—not through the door so much as away from the room—and Tomas sat alone with the lamps and the columns and the sound of his own pulse, which had gone strange and loud, like footsteps on stairs he hadn't climbed yet.

*Intercept her at the ferry crossing.*

He looked down at the ledger. His pen had stopped mid-figure, and the ink was pooling into a small black eye that stared up at him from the page. He blotted it. He blotted it twice. Then he closed the book, put out the lamps, and sat in the dark doing the only arithmetic that had ever mattered: eleven years of service against one woman, weighed on a scale that had never once tipped toward mercy.

The scale tipped.

---

He told himself, packing, that he would decide at the ferry. That was the lie he chose, and he packed it carefully between his spare shirt and his knife, because a man needs a lie to carry if he's going to walk toward a thing like this at a steady pace.

The Guild of Impossible Things had built him the way masons build arches: each stone shaped for its place, no piece asked to bear weight it wasn't cut for. Found at seven, nameless, half-feral, speaking a river dialect nobody wanted—he'd been exactly the raw material the guild prized. They had taught him accounts and anatomy, how to break a lock and how to break a promise gracefully, how to stand in a room and be furniture until furniture was useful. And beneath all of it, stitched in deeper than any of it, they had taught him the first law:

*The court's claims come due. The guild pays them. The guild is paid.*

He was the payment mechanism. He was the hand that carried other people's debts to their conclusion. For eleven years he had walked contracts to their endings and never once asked what it cost to be an ending.

Then Rafael Ravensworth had arrived at the guild with ink-stained cuffs and a way of reading that looked less like study than like argument, and Tomas had learned, over four slow years, what it was to be a man instead of a mechanism. She argued with books. She argued with the guild master, quietly, in margins. She argued with him the first time they met—over the proper dating of a bottled storm's lease—and he had fallen in love the way a roof falls in: suddenly, structurally, with a great deal of noise that only he could hear.

Now she was walking toward the mountain with stolen salt and a stolen key, and the mountain had sent him to stop her, and the arithmetic was done.

He left the guild by the coal chute, because wardens watched the doors, and took the low road north with the storm coming down behind him like a debt collector.

---

The ferry crossing was a rope-and-plank affair over a river the color of old pewter, and it was where the heralds expected him, so it was the last place he could afford to be seen being right.

He arrived an hour early and spent it doing the most difficult work of his life: nothing. He leaned against the ferry post in the rain and let the water flatten his hair and darken his coat and make him look exactly like a man who had trudged all night to do an ugly job. Because there would be witnesses. There were always witnesses. The court did nothing unobserved; observation was how it collected interest.

Rafael came down the hill road just past dawn, hooded, mud to the knee, Wren wrapped against her chest in a sling made from a guild blanket. The girl's eyes were open and fixed on the sky with the terrible patience of a child who has learned that looking at adults accomplishes nothing.

Tomas stepped onto the road.

Rafael stopped. Under the hood her face went through three expressions in a single breath—recognition, relief, and then the third one, the one that hurt: understanding. She knew why he was there. Of course she did. She'd had all night to know.

"Tomas."

"You're carrying stolen property." He kept his voice level, official, pitched for the treeline. "The key. The salt. Both guild assets."

"I'm carrying a child."

"The child is also guild property. Per the terms." He said it and hated every syllable and said it anyway, because the words weren't for her. They were for the dark places between the trees, where the air had begun to taste faintly of struck flint—the flavor of a listening court.

"So you're here to take us back." Her hand had drifted, very casually, to the salt bag at her hip.

"I'm here to assess the situation." He walked closer. Close enough that the rain between them was private. "Assessment follows. The ferry rope is frayed on the north anchor. It won't hold a crossing this morning. Anyone attempting it would have to go upstream—" he nodded, minutely, toward the shallows half a mile west, where the river spread thin over gravel, "—to the old ford. Which is slower. And exposed. And the only sane route."

A pause. Rain on wool. Wren blinked up at him from the sling, and her eyes were the gray-green of deep water, and they did not miss anything.

"And if I take the ford?" Rafael said softly.

"Then I'll be forced to pursue you there, where the footing is bad, where I might lose you in the mist, and where I will certainly report—at length, and with great regret—that the fugitive doubled back south along the mill road." He let one corner of his mouth move. "I'll need time to compose such a report. Hours, probably. I'm thorough."

Something broke open in her face—not a smile, nothing so safe as a smile. More like a crack in ice over moving water. "Tomas. If they find out—"

"They won't find out today." He reached out and straightened the blanket around Wren, an excuse to touch something of hers, and found the child's small hand close briefly around his finger with a grip far too deliberate for a child her size. "Go. Walk in the water where your tracks fill. Don't use the key until you're under the mountain; it warms when it's near its door, and warm iron can be tracked."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've tracked people by it." He met her eyes. "Eleven years, Rafe. I know every trick I ever used on anyone, which means I know every trick anyone could use on me. Use all of them."

She gripped his sleeve then, hard, rain running off both their chins. "Come with us."

And there it was—the door he'd been pretending wasn't in the wall. He looked at it. He wanted it more than he had ever wanted anything, and that was precisely why he couldn't walk through it yet. Not because of duty. Duty was dead already; he'd felt it die somewhere around the word *ford*. But because a pursuit needed a pursuer, and a false report needed a reporter, and every hour the court believed he was hunting her was an hour the heralds weren't flying ahead of her to warn the deep court she was coming.

"If I come with you, you arrive announced," he said. "If I stay, you arrive as a rumor. Rumors buy time. Time is the only thing down there that isn't owned."

"That's not—"

"It's the truth, and we don't have time for me to be brave properly." He pried her fingers gently from his sleeve and held them a moment longer than necessary. "Ford's that way. Go."

She went. He watched her until the mist took her—her, and the small gray-eyed weight of everything he'd chosen—and then he turned back up the hill road, toward the guild, toward the witnesses, toward the first lie of his life that mattered.

His hands were shaking. He noted it, professionally, the way he'd note a discrepancy in a ledger, and kept walking.

---

The report took him two days to deliver, because he made it take two days.

He gave the wardens a trail: a torn scrap of blue cloth on the mill road fence (his own shirt, sacrificed), a coin of the old mintage dropped at a crossroads inn where he bought a meal he didn't eat, a sighting sworn to by a drover who had seen someone, anyone, hooded, going south. He let himself be seen asking questions badly. He let himself be seen tired. A tired hunter reads as an honest one.

On the second evening, the herald came to him in the stable loft where he'd bedded down, materializing out of the straw-shadow with that flint-taste in the air.

*"You lost her."*

"I lost her." He kept his eyes down, submissive, a man bracing for punishment. "She doubled back. She's cleverer than her file suggested. I request additional resources."

A long silence. Rain on the stable roof. The herald's face inclined toward him, and Tomas felt it reading him the way he read ledgers—line by line, looking for the figure that didn't balance.

*"You are lying."*

Every muscle in Tomas's body went to stone. Eleven years of training screamed at him to blink, breathe, sweat on cue—to perform fear convincingly, because a liar caught was a liar broken. He raised his eyes slowly, let confusion and hurt into them, and said:

"I'm requesting resources. Would I do that if I were lying?"

The herald considered him. Somewhere below, a horse shifted and sighed.

*"Perhaps,"* it said at last. *"Lies are also a form of service, when they serve. We will watch you, counter. Watch closely."*

"I'd expect nothing else."

It dissolved. Tomas lay back in the straw and discovered that his heart was still working, which surprised him, and that his hands had stopped shaking, which frightened him more. Because the terror was gone now, and in its place was something calm and permanent, the way a scar is permanent.

They would watch him. Fine. Let them watch. Every watcher sees what he expects to see, and the court expected a mechanism, and mechanisms don't choose.

Except this one had. This one had stood at a ford in the rain and handed his whole constructed self over the side of a boat, and heard it hit the water, and felt lighter.

He got up, saddled his horse, and rode north—not fast, not slow, at the pace of a man following a trail he believed in. Behind him the guild's lights burned. Ahead, under the rain and the dark, the mountain waited with its doors that remembered names, and somewhere inside it a woman was walking toward a debt with his heart in her pocket.

There was one thing left he could give her that she didn't know she had: time. And he intended to spend every hour of it, buying her road with his own, lying to the dark in a voice it had trusted for eleven years—

—until the flint-taste filled the air again, closer now, and colder, and a voice that was not the herald's said, out of the trees ahead of him:

*"Stop there, counter. The court wishes to ask you about the ford."*
