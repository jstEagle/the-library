## 10. The Court Beneath

The doors that remembered her grandmother's name closed behind Rafael with a sound like a book snapping shut.

She stood in a hall carved from the mountain's living root, and the first thing she understood—before the light, before the lords, before the cold—was that everything here was *accounted for*. The vaulted ceiling was ribbed like the spine of some enormous ledger, each arch numbered in characters older than any alphabet the Guild of Impossible Things kept. The floor was polished stone worn smooth by centuries of feet, and every footfall echoed once, precisely, as if the mountain were tallying them.

The light came from nowhere and everywhere: a pale luminescence rising out of the stone itself, the color of moonrise through fog. It cast no shadows. That frightened her more than anything else so far. In the guild's archives, where she'd spent half her life, shadows were how you found the sealed things—the documents tucked behind shelves, the drawers that didn't match their cabinets. Here there was nothing to hide behind. Nothing to hide.

*You are expected,* the doors had seemed to say, in the shudder of stone and the taste of her grandmother's name still on her tongue.

Rafael adjusted the salt-ward satchel against her ribs—Wren had sewn the last seam herself, small clumsy stitches, and Rafael had sworn not to open it unless she meant to use it—and walked.

The hall opened into a court.

She had imagined, during the long descent, what the deep court might look like. She had imagined rot, maybe, or glitter—the two ways old power usually showed itself. What she found instead was order. Terraces of dark stone rose in concentric rings around a floor of black glass, and upon those terraces sat the lords of the court beneath the mountain, arranged with the terrible neatness of figures in an abacus.

There were nine of them. She counted twice. Nine never changed; Wren had told her that much, in the fragments of old tongue the girl still carried like river stones in her mouth. Nine lords, nine terraces, nine debts outstanding at any given time. When one was paid, another was written.

They were not what she expected either. Not monsters. Not beautiful in the way of the fair folk from the songs. They looked like *architecture*—like pillars given patience, like load-bearing walls that had learned to want things. Their skin was veined granite and pale quartz. Their eyes were seams of mica that caught the sourceless light and held it. The eldest, seated highest, had a crack running from brow to jaw that might have been age or might have been a smile.

None of them moved as she approached. That was the courtesy, she realized. Movement cost something here, and they did not spend it lightly.

A herald stood at the base of the lowest terrace—not a creature but a function, a figure robed in grey whose face was smooth as an unmarked page. When Rafael stopped at the boundary of the black glass floor, the herald spoke, and its voice was the voice of a contract being read aloud: level, complete, indifferent.

"Rafael Ravensworth. Scribe, third class, Guild of Impossible Things. Indentured. Debtor by inheritance." A pause, delicate as a turned page. "Granddaughter of Maren Ravensworth, who stands in our keeping."

Hearing her grandmother's name spoken aloud in this place hit her like cold water. She had said the name herself at the doors below—had used it as a key—but hearing it *returned*, catalogued, filed, made Maren real again in a way the archives never had. Maren was not a faded entry in a sealed ledger. Maren was a possession, listed, valued, kept.

"I've come about her," Rafael said. Her voice sounded thin against all that stone. "And about the other debt. The collateral."

"We know," said the eldest lord.

Of course they did. She felt the absurd urge to laugh. She had spent weeks preparing for this—memorizing clauses, rehearsing openings, packing salt and stolen keys and Wren's scraps of the old tongue—and the court had known since before she left the guild house. Probably since the night the child arrived at the door. Probably since the oath was written, generations ago, when her grandmother signed away a soul to save something the ledgers didn't record.

"Then you know I haven't come to beg," Rafael said.

"No," the eldest agreed. "Begging is for those who believe mercy exists outside a price. You are a scribe. You believe in terms." The mica eyes brightened fractionally. "We find scribes refreshing. So few of your kind understand that words are not decoration. They are *load*. Every sentence bears weight or collapses. You have come bearing weight."

It was a compliment. She made herself receive it like one, bowing her head the precise degree the guild taught for addressing a superior—enough to show respect, not enough to show submission. Somewhere in her chest, Tomas's voice murmured the training drill: *Never let them see which sentences cost you.*

"Your reputation preceded you down the mountain," said the lord on the second terrace—a female voice, or something shaped like one, resonant as a struck bell. "The one who found the flaw in the Vellum Accords. The one who rewrote a bottled storm's contract so cleanly that even the storm consented." A pause, and then, silk over granite: "We have wanted a mind like yours for a very long time."

*Every compliment is a hook.* Wren had taught her that too, without meaning to—in the nightmares, the girl would whimper phrases in the old tongue, and Rafael would piece together their shapes: *they praise the rope before they pull it.* She kept her hands still at her sides.

"I'm flattered," she said flatly.

"You should be," the bell-voiced lord said, and there was no irony in it at all, which was worse. "Flattery is honest here. We do not say what we do not mean. When we tell you that you are remarkable, it is because we have measured you. Everything in this court is measured, scribe. That is our nature and our law. We do not take what is not weighed. We do not keep what is not owed." The lord leaned forward, stone grinding softly against stone. "You will find us more honorable than your guild masters above. Ask yourself, when you return to the daylight: has the Guild of Impossible Things ever told you the true price of anything?"

Rafael thought of her indenture, ticking down in the margins of contracts for borrowed years. Thought of Master Corvane's hungry eyes when the child spoke the old tongue. Thought of the deal he'd offered her—her freedom for a theft, her body forfeit if she failed—and how carefully he'd left the true costs unstated.

She hated that the lord had found the soft place on the first strike.

"The debt," she said. "Maren Ravensworth's soul. And the collateral against my bloodline. I want both entries cleared."

"Both?" The eldest lord's crack-face shifted, and this time she was certain it was a smile. "You speak as though they were separate matters. They are one entry, child. Your grandmother sold a soul to seal an oath. The oath required collateral—assurance that the debt would be honored across generations. The court took a life unborn. A girl-child of the Ravensworth line, held in trust until the debt came due." The mica eyes fixed on her. "You knew her as a feral child at your door. We knew her as interest accruing."

Rafael's stomach turned over. *Interest.* Wren—small, mud-caked, screaming language no scholar knew—had been *interest*. A number growing quietly in a ledger while she grew up in the dark under the mountain, or wherever the court had kept her before the debt's term began.

"She has a name now," Rafael said. "Wren."

"Names are useful," the eldest said mildly. "Yours especially. Ravensworth. Raven's worth. Do you know why your family bore that name, scribe? It was not vanity. Ravens were the counters of the battlefield in the oldest days—they landed on the fallen and made tally of them. Your ancestors were counters too. Counters of souls." A pause. "It runs in the blood. Which is why the collateral was taken from it."

The hall was very quiet. The sourceless light did not flicker. Rafael became aware, distantly, that her heart was loud in all that silence, and that the lords could probably hear it, and that they were counting.

"You invited me to make an offer," she said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "I'm here to make one."

"Yes." The eldest lord settled back, stone sighing against stone. "But courtesy first. Courtesy is our currency, and we pay our debts in it as scrupulously as we collect them. You have descended far and fast, alone, through doors that do not open for strangers. You have earned refreshment." The herald gestured, and from somewhere beyond the terraces came servants—or things shaped like servants, grey and smooth-faced as the herald itself—bearing a low table of black glass. Upon it: bread, dark and dense. Water in cups of clouded crystal. Salt, in a small dish, white as teeth.

Rafael looked at the salt and understood the trap instantly. Eat the salt, bind yourself a guest, bound guests owe hospitality-debts. Refuse the salt, insult the court, insulted courts set terms from strength. She thought furiously, standing in the light that cast no shadows, and then she did what a scribe does.

She read the fine print.

"Before I accept your courtesy," she said, "I'll need the terms of guest-right stated aloud. Fully. In the old tongue, if your law requires it, but translated for my understanding. I don't eat under unexamined conditions."

Silence. Then, from the second terrace, the bell-voice laughed—a sound like a cathedral bell cracking, beautiful and wrong.

"Oh, *yes*," the lord breathed. "Yes, we were right to want her."

The eldest raised one hand, and the herald recited: guest-right, full and formal. Protection under the court's roof for the duration of negotiation. No harm offered by the court or its servants. No obligation incurred beyond civility. No claim upon the guest's name, voice, memory, or future—*unless freely traded.*

That last clause hung in the air like smoke.

"Unless freely traded," Rafael repeated.

"All trade is free or it is theft," the eldest said. "We are many things, scribe, but we are not thieves. Your guild masters above would simply have taken. We *ask*. It is slower. It is more profitable." The mica eyes gleamed. "Eat. Then make your offer. And know that whatever you offer, we will weigh it honestly. That is the only mercy this court keeps, and we keep it perfectly."

She ate. The bread tasted of stone dust and something sweeter underneath, like grain grown in candlelight. The water was cold and clean. She did not touch the salt—it had been part of the recitation, a test within the test, and leaving it untouched told them she'd caught it. The bell-voiced lord's expression, if stone could be said to hold one, suggested approval.

When the table was withdrawn, the eldest lord spoke again, and the courtesy in its voice had curdled into something else—still polite, still measured, but now she could hear the hooks beneath every word, barbed and patient.

"Now. The offer. But first, a gift of information, freely given, because we honor our guests with truth." The lord leaned forward, and the whole terrace seemed to lean with it, nine lords becoming one vast attention. "You should know that your companion below—the enforcer who falsified his reports to clear your road—he has been taken. He bargains even now, in a cell not far from where your grandmother waits. He is spending himself to buy you time. Pieces of himself. He believes you will never know." A pause, exquisite, cruel. "We tell you because truth is courtesy. And because grief makes negotiators generous."

The floor did not sway. The light did not dim. But something in Rafael went very quiet and very cold, the way a page goes still just before ink touches it.

Tomas. Down here. Spending himself in the dark for her, believing she'd never learn the price.

She thought of his hands steadying hers over contracts. Of the way he counted everything—steps, coins, days—because counting was how he loved things. Of the lie she'd last seen on his face, the one he wore for the guild masters, and the truth underneath it that he'd only ever shown her.

*Grief makes negotiators generous.* They were right. It did. And they had handed her the grief deliberately, the way a moneylender hands a desperate man the coin he needs most, knowing what the repayment will cost.

Rafael lifted her chin and met the mica eyes of the eldest lord of the court beneath the mountain.

"Thank you for your honesty," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she would remember later that it hadn't, and wonder what it had cost her to hold it steady. "Now let's talk about what can't be replaced."

Above them, far above, the mountain groaned once—a sound no one in the court remarked upon, a sound Rafael would think about for the rest of her life.

The eldest lord folded its hands of veined stone.

"Begin," it said.
