## 12. The Price of Looking Back

They did not chain him. That was the first insult.

Tomas Vane had spent eleven years as the Guild of Impossible Things' enforcer, and in that time he had learned that the worst prisons were the ones that trusted you. Chains meant you were worth restraining. A locked door meant you were worth guarding. But the deep court simply gave him a chair of black stone at the edge of a vault the size of a cathedral, and left him sitting there with nothing but his own thoughts for company—and his thoughts, he was discovering, had teeth.

The herald who'd taken him had been almost apologetic about it. *You'll be kept until we know what you are,* she'd said, which was a question he couldn't answer either. What was he? He'd been made for the court—raised on their coin, shaped by their agents, bound by oaths signed before he could read them. And then a mud-caked child had arrived at the guild door speaking the old tongue, and Rafael Ravensworth had looked at her like she was looking at her own grave, and Tomas had walked into the dark and lied to the first herald he met in thirty years of perfect service.

*She went east along the salt road.* Four words. The easiest lie of his life and the most expensive.

Now he sat in the vault where they kept things too valuable or too terrible to destroy, and he waited for someone to decide what he was worth.

He didn't have to wait long. The court's auditors came at what passed for dawn down here—a slow brightening of the lichen on the ceiling, pale as old milk. There were three of them, robed in gray so fine it seemed woven from fog, and they moved without sound across a floor that remembered every footstep ever set upon it. Tomas felt it under him now: a faint tremor of recognition each time one of them shifted weight, the stone cataloguing them the way he'd once catalogued debts.

"Tomas Vane," said the eldest auditor. Her voice was dry as a ledger page turning. "Counter of accounts. Keeper of the guild's arithmetic."

"That was the job description," he said.

"You have amended your own terms." She unfolded a scroll that hadn't existed a moment ago; the paper smelled of rain and iron. "Eleven years of faithful service. One act of treachery. The court is very interested in the mathematics of that exchange."

"Then you're going to be disappointed," Tomas said. "It doesn't balance. I knew that when I did it."

Something flickered behind the auditor's veil—not surprise exactly. Appetite. "Everything balances," she said. "Eventually. The only question is the currency." She let the scroll fall closed. "Yours, we think, will do nicely."

* * *

They took him deeper. Of course they did. Down past the seal-vaults where souls hung in jars of amber light like preserved fruit, down past doors that whispered names as he passed—the stone tasting him, finding him familiar, finding him *theirs*. He had been coming to this place since he was nine years old, delivered by an agent of the guild who'd told him it was a school for clever boys. It had been a school. It had also been a harvest.

The room they brought him to held no furniture except a table, and on the table lay a scale of the old kind, brass pans hanging from chains gone green with age. Beside the scale stood a decanter of water so clear it hurt to look at.

"Do you know what memory weighs?" the eldest auditor asked.

"I've weighed stranger things for this court," Tomas said. "Years. Names. A man's shadow, once—he'd gambled it away in the upper world and wanted it back before winter." He kept his voice flat, professional. It was the only armor he had left. "I never weighed memory."

"It weighs differently for every soul," she said. "For some it is nearly nothing—a feather, a sigh. For others—" she touched the scale's arm gently, and it groaned, "—it outweighs the body entire. We find that those who guard their memories most jealously are the ones carrying the most valuable ones."

"What do you want from me?"

"The truth of the salt road. Where you sent our heralds. How long the girl has. Every word exchanged between you and Rafael Ravensworth since the night the child arrived." The auditor folded her hands. "Give these freely and walk out with everything else intact. Refuse, and we will take what we need piece by piece, and you may not enjoy our selection."

Tomas looked at the decanter of water. "And if I bargain?"

Auditors did not smile. But something in the room leaned closer, the way a market leans toward a shout. "Everyone bargains," the eldest said. "That is why we built the mountain."

* * *

Here is what Tomas understood, standing at the scale while three gray-robed accountants waited with the patience of stone:

Rafael was climbing. Right now, tonight, while he sat in the belly of the mountain being priced, she was somewhere above him or below him—there was no real distinction down here—moving through doors that opened for names, spending her grandmother's shame like coin because it was the only coin she had. She was good at this. Better than good. He'd watched her unpick contracts for bottled storms, found the flaw in a hundred-year bond that three guild masters had missed. If anyone alive could steal a soul out from under the deep court, it was the woman whose family had sold it.

But she would need time. And time, in this place, was bought with weight.

So he put his hand flat on the table and said, "I'll give you the truth. All of it. Where I sent them, what Rafael knows, what the child is, what's coming due at the next moonrise. Everything you want."

"And in exchange?"

"You stop looking for her for three days."

The eldest auditor went very still. "Three days is not a currency. Three days is a delay."

"Delays are the only thing worth buying," Tomas said. "Ask any debtor."

The auditors conferred without speaking—some subterranean arithmetic passing between veils. Then the eldest turned back. "The court will grant your delay. In return, the court will require payment of equal weight to the information given. Truths about Ravensworth, the child, and the guild's defenses are heavy truths, Tomas Vane. You cannot pay them in promises."

"I know what I can pay with," he said, and hated how steady his voice came out. "Memories. Take them at the scale. Fair weights."

"Which memories?"

"Mine to choose," he said. "That's the price of my choosing them. You get the truths whole, and I choose what covers their cost."

Another silence. The lichen-light dimmed and brightened like breathing. At last the eldest nodded, once, and poured a measure of water into the left pan of the scale. "Then let us begin. Speak, and we will weigh."

* * *

He told them everything.

He told them he'd sent the heralds east along the salt road, toward the drowned villages, knowing the trail would go cold in the marsh fogs. He told them Rafael had learned her grandmother's name sat beside the word *collateral*, that she'd read the debt clause aloud to herself in the archive at midnight with her sleeves rolled up and ink to her elbows. He told them the guild master had offered her the theft-for-freedom bargain, that she'd signed it in her own blood-inked thumbprint because the guild master insisted on old forms. He told them Wren was fading at every moonrise, thinning like breath off glass, and that Rafael had started sleeping on the floor beside the girl's cot with a salt line drawn around them both.

The pan sank lower with every truth. The water in it didn't ripple—it *darkened*, taking the color and weight of what he surrendered. When he finished, the eldest auditor studied the scale the way a jeweler studies a stone.

"Heavy," she murmured, almost approvingly. "Heavier than expected. You loved her."

"That's not in my testimony."

"Love is never in the testimony," the auditor said. "It is in the weight." She gestured, and a second vessel appeared—an empty pan of black glass, waiting. "Now. Your payment."

Tomas had spent the whole testimony building the list in his head, sorting himself like a ledger. Some things he could not give up, or rather, some things he refused to: the smell of Rafael's hair when she fell asleep over her work; the sound Wren had made the first time Rafael fed her honey off a spoon, that astonished little gasp, like the girl had forgotten sweetness existed; the way the guild hall looked at dawn before anyone else was up, all that impossible quiet. Those he would defend with whatever was left of him.

But there was other stock. Older stock. Stock that had been rotting on his shelves for years anyway.

"My mother's face," he said.

The eldest tilted her head. "Explain the lot."

"She died when I was seven. I've carried her face for twenty-six years and I haven't been able to look at it straight in twenty. Every time I try, it comes with the coughing, the winter, the debt that got me sold to your agents in the first place. Take the whole parcel. The face, the voice, the funeral. All of it."

The scale drank it. He felt it leave—not pain, exactly, but a subtraction, a cold spot opening behind his eyes where a woman in a blue shawl used to live. He tested the edges of the absence and found, unsettlingly, that it fit him well. He had been hauling her for years. Now some other shelf held her, in a vault under a mountain, and he was lighter and worse for it.

"What else?"

"The year I was fourteen." He swallowed. "The training year. Master Corvo. Everything he taught me and everything he did to make it stick."

That one cost more coming out than going in. The memories peeled away in strips—the cellar, the drills, the nights he'd cried into his sleeve so no one would hear and mark it against him. He'd hated those memories and needed them; they were the reason he survived everything after. Without them he was a man with skills and no explanation for them. A lock with no key-shaped hole.

The pan sank. The auditors murmured among themselves.

"You are giving us ugly things," the eldest observed. "Grief. Shame. Pain. Do you imagine we don't notice the strategy? You are paying with coin you were glad to be rid of."

"I'm paying with legal tender," Tomas said. "You didn't specify quality."

"We specified *weight*." She leaned forward, and beneath the veil her eyes were the color of wet slate. "And the remaining balance is substantial. Your truths were heavy, enforcer. Heavier than two parcels of misery. Either dig deeper into your stock, or we reopen the terms and take our selection after all."

There it was. The hook inside the courtesy. He'd known it would come; he'd just hoped to die of old age before it did.

* * *

So he dug deeper.

He gave them the first time he'd seen Rafael—actually seen her, not catalogued her as the scribe with the ticking indenture but *seen* her—standing in the guild yard arguing with a storm-bottler about interest rates, rain plastering her hair to her forehead, winning. He gave them the night she'd laughed at something stupid he'd said and he'd understood, with the clean horror of a falling man, that he was done. He gave them every stolen hour since: the archive at midnight, the roof in summer, her hand finding his in the dark of the stairwell like it had always known the way.

He did it deliberately. That was the trick of it, the bitter craft of the bargain—if the court was going to hold his love for Rafael as evidence, as leverage, as *weight*, then better it sit in their vault than in his chest where they could reach for it again. Better he pay it out on his own terms, at his own scale, than have them carve it out later, mid-interrogation, mid-torture, and use the wound of its removal to break him open for the rest.

He would lose the memories either way if they took the court. This way, at least, he chose the order. This way he could look at each one once more before it went—turn it over in the light, the way you check a coin before spending it.

*Rafael laughing with rain in her hair.*

*Rafael asleep over a ledger, ink on her cheekbone.*

*Rafael saying his name like it was a door she was glad to open.*

The pan sank and sank. The water in it went dark as the space between stars. And Tomas stood at the table feeling the rooms of himself empty one by one, feeling the shape of his life go hollow where she had lived in it, and he kept his face still because auditors were watching and he was, whatever else, still a counter of accounts, and a good counter never lets them see the till running low.

When it was done, the eldest auditor consulted the scale, then the scroll, then something invisible above them all.

"Balance achieved," she said. "Barely. You cut it close, Vane."

"I'm told that's how honest accounting works."

"Three days," she said. "The search stands down until the third moonrise. After that, the court resumes, and the court does not tire." She rolled up her scroll. "One curiosity, before you're returned to your chair. You paid more than the balance required. The last few items were surplus—you gave us love you did not owe. Why?"

Tomas thought about lying and found he no longer had the material to build one that convincing. That was the joke of it, maybe the mercy of it: they'd stripped him so bare that the truth was the only thing left in his pockets.

"Because in three days," he said, "she's going to be standing in front of your lords, and everything I know about surviving this place is going to matter, and I couldn't afford to be carrying anything they could ransom back to me." He met the slate eyes through the veil. "You think you took my memories. You actually took my hostages. Now there's nothing in me you can trade against. I'm worthless to you, and free men are expensive."

The auditor regarded him for a long moment. Then, softly, in a tone he had never heard from an auditor before—something adjacent to respect—she said:

"The lords should be careful of you, Tomas Vane."

"They should be careful of her," he said. "I'm just the distraction."

* * *

They returned him to the chair of black stone at the edge of the seal-vaults, and left him alone with the amber lights and the whispering doors.

He sat in the quiet and took inventory, the way he always did after closing a hard account. Name: present, mostly. Skills: present, originless. Mother: gone, and the grief of her gone with it, which felt like losing her twice. Rafael—

He reached for Rafael and found a shape instead of a person. Warmth without features. An certainty of importance with no story attached. He knew—abstractly, the way you know a country you've never visited—that this hollow in him was the best thing he'd ever had, that he had paid away the best thing he'd ever had, and that somewhere above him a woman was risking her voice, her freedom, her name, for a child and a grandmother and a debt that was never hers, and that when she came back—if she came back—she would say his name and it would land on a man who no longer remembered what it meant to hear it.

She would never know. That was the design of it, the whole point. Sacrifice without witness. He had bought her three days and paid for them with the only currency she'd have refused to let him spend, and she would climb out of this mountain believing he'd betrayed her cleanly, or helped her cheaply, or simply gotten lucky—and she would never stand at this scale, never watch the water darken with everything he'd poured out of himself so that her climb would have one less hunter behind it.

Good, he told himself. Let it stay that way. Let it be a debt she never has to service.

He settled back in the chair, closed his eyes, and began, methodically, to rebuild. A man with holes in him could still count. Could still plan. Could still remember—without remembering how—that the vault doors on the eastern gallery answered to grief, and that grief was one thing they had not thought to take from him, because they'd assumed he had none left worth weighing.

Above him, faint as a heartbeat through miles of stone, the mountain shuddered.

Once. Twice.

Somewhere far below, a door had just heard a name it knew.

Tomas opened his eyes, stood, and walked—unhurried, unwatched, unpaid—toward the sound.
