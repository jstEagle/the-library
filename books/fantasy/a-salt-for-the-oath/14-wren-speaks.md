## 14. Wren Speaks

Wren knew the mountain was eating her.

She didn't have a word for it in the new tongue, the soft one Rafael used, all its sounds like water over stones. In the old tongue there was a word. There were many words for it, because the deep court had many ways of taking things, and they had named each one with care, the way a careful merchant labels his jars.

*Vess.* To be poured out slowly.

That was the one.

She lay on her pallet in the little room above the guild hall and watched her own hand in the lamplight. It was a good hand. Small, but clever. It had learned to hold a pen this week, and to fold paper birds, and to sign her name—*Wren*, which Rafael had given her like a coat against the cold. But tonight the lamp shone through her fingers as if they were made of the thin horn windows in the guild master's study, and she could see the grain of the blanket through her palm, and she thought: *vess.*

Not long now.

She wasn't afraid of not-long-now. That was the strange part, the part she kept folded up small inside herself where no one could see it, because she had learned that when people saw what was inside her they looked at her differently. The way Tomas looked at her sometimes, when he thought she was sleeping. Like a door he didn't want to open.

But tomorrow the lords would come up out of the mountain—or their voices would, riding the salt lines and the ledger seams—and there would be a weighing, and Rafael would stand before them with her ink-stained hands and her brave, doomed plan, and Wren had decided that enough was enough.

You could only be a secret for so long before the secret started doing damage.

She got up. Her feet made almost no sound on the floorboards, which should have frightened her too—they were becoming less real every night—but instead she felt the old patience settle over her, the patience of stone, of ledgers, of things that wait six thousand years for an appointment.

Rafael's room was at the end of the hall. Light under the door. Of course there was light under the door; Rafael never slept anymore, or if she slept it was sitting up with a pen in her hand like a soldier with a knife.

Wren knocked. Two soft raps, the way Rafael had taught her: *it's me, it's safe.*

"Come in."

The room smelled of ink and cold tea and salt. Salt everywhere now, in bowls on the sill, in lines across the threshold, in the little leather bag Rafael wore at her belt even indoors. Wren stepped over the threshold line carefully, out of habit, though the wards had never once stirred for her. She'd wondered about that. She wondered about a lot of things.

Rafael looked up from her papers, and Wren watched her do the thing she always did—the quick flick of her eyes down Wren's arms, checking the fading, measuring it, the way you check a wound. Tonight the flick stopped. Tonight Rafael's face did something complicated and then went very still.

"You can see the lamp through me," Wren said. "I know. I wanted to talk to you before it gets worse."

Rafael set down her pen. Very deliberately, the way she did everything that mattered. "Talk to me," she said. "Any way you like. Old tongue, new tongue, drawing in the salt. I'm listening."

And that—that was why Wren had come to her and not to anyone else. Because Rafael said *any way you like* and meant it, and because when Wren spoke fragments of the old language Rafael's face did something no scholar's face had ever done in all the years since the pre-kingdom drowned into story. It remembered.

Wren climbed onto the chair across the desk. She was tall enough now to rest her elbows on it, barely. Last month she hadn't been. She was growing and fading at the same time, being pulled in two directions, stretched between the world above and the court below like a page two scribes were fighting over.

"I need to tell you what I am," she said in the old tongue, and then, because Rafael's brow furrowed at the third word, she tried again in the new one, hunting for the shapes. Her mouth was clumsy with it still. "I need—I have to say. What I am. Before tomorrow."

"You're Wren." Rafael said it simply, firmly, like setting a seal on wax. "Whatever else you are, that comes first."

"That's the name you gave me. Names are coats." She had heard Rafael say that once to Tomas, arguing about something in the ledgers. "Underneath the coat I am—" She stopped. Started again. This was harder than she'd thought it would be. All those years of knowing, pressed down flat like flowers in a book, and now that she needed the words they crumbled.

"Take your time," Rafael said.

"I have less time than you think."

Rafael flinched. Wren was sorry immediately, but she didn't take it back, because it was true, and true things were the only currency the mountain respected. She'd learned that below. She'd learned everything below.

She held out her hand over the desk, palm up, and let the lamplight shine through it.

"When the lords weigh me tomorrow," she said, "they will call me collateral. They will say I am the payment on an oath, a soul sold by a woman named Ravensworth, four generations back. Your grandmother's grandmother's mother. They will speak of me like a jar of grain. A measure of weight. And you will argue with them, because you are brave, and you will lose, because they are older than arguing."

Rafael had gone white. "How do you know about tomorrow?"

"The salt told me. The salt is theirs first, ours second. Everything here is theirs first." Wren turned her hand over, watching the light pass through. "But that isn't what I came to say. I came to say the thing they don't know I know. The thing nobody knows."

She took a breath. Below, in the dark, breath had been optional. Here it helped. It gathered the words up and lined them ready.

"I remember," Wren said, "being grown."

*

The silence after she said it lasted so long that the lamp guttered twice.

Rafael didn't speak. She had gone very still in the way that Wren had learned meant *the ground just moved under me and I am deciding whether to admit it.* So Wren kept going, quietly, the way you keep walking across ice once you've heard it sing.

"Not grown like this." She gestured at herself, the small arms, the child's voice. "Grown like you. Hands like yours. I had a life. I had—" the words came slowly, dredged up from somewhere deep and dark and packed down hard, "—I had a garden. On a hillside. Above the snow line, where the old road used to run. I grew bitterroot and sky-onions and a flower whose name I only know in the old tongue." She said the flower's name. It sounded like wind moving through a cave mouth.

Rafael's lips moved, repeating it without sound.

"I had a husband. I don't remember his face. Isn't that strange? I remember his hands—he mended nets, no, that was someone else, he *carved*, he carved little animals from pine knots, and I remember our table, and I remember being angry at him once about something foolish, and I remember that the last thing I ever said to him was unkind, and I have had—" Wren paused, and found that her eyes were wet, and was surprised, because she hadn't cried in a very long time; crying was a thing bodies did, and hers was mostly borrowed, "—I have had sixty years to be sorry for it. Sixty years in the dark under the mountain. Do you understand? They didn't put me to sleep. They don't put anyone to sleep. Sleep would be a mercy, and the deep court does not deal in mercies, it deals in *principal and interest*."

"Wren." Rafael's voice cracked on the name. "Sixty years. But the debt was written generations ago—"

"Time below is a storeroom," Wren said. "They keep us the way you keep preserves. I was taken, and I was sealed, and I was aware, every hour, in the vault beneath the seal-stone, while the court above my head traded and feasted and weighed things. I heard the ledgers turn. I heard the lords argue about interest rates on a soul from the surface, and once—one time—a lord came down and stood in front of my shelf and read my label aloud, and laughed at something written there, and I have spent sixty years wondering what was funny." Her voice didn't shake. She had used up all her shaking decades ago. "Then the oath came due. First moonrise. And they needed the collateral walking and talking, so they poured me into—" she looked down at herself, "—this. A vessel sized for a child. A grown soul compressed into small bones. It fits badly. That's why I fade. I'm not dying, Rafael. I'm *leaking*. The vessel can't hold the weight of me, and every night a little more of what I was runs out through the seams, back down toward the mountain, home to the shelf."

Rafael stood up so fast the chair went over backward. She didn't seem to notice. She crossed to the window, stood with her back to Wren, and Wren watched her shoulders and understood that she was watching a woman hold herself together with her bare hands.

"All this time," Rafael said finally. Her voice was terrible and quiet. "All these weeks. You've been in my house, eating at my table, learning my letters—and you remembered everything. You remembered being—"

"A woman," Wren said. "With a garden. Who owed nothing to anyone." She slid off the chair and stood, small in the middle of the lamplight. "Why do you think I never cried at night? Why do you think I knew the old tongue? Why do you think the wards never woke for me?" She smiled, and it was an old woman's smile on a child's face, and it hurt to wear it. "I'm not a child who wandered out of the mountain, Rafael. I'm the mountain's property, escaped from its shelf, wearing the smallest shape it could pour me into. I ran because running was possible. I ran because the oath was coming due and I thought—if I get above the snow line, if I find the descendants of the woman who sold me, maybe they'll be sorry. Maybe they'll fix it."

"And instead you found me."

"And instead I found you." Wren tilted her head. "Are you sorry?"

Rafael turned around. Her eyes were bright and fierce and wet, and she looked, in that moment, exactly like the portraits in the sealed archives, the ones of the Ravensworth women going back four generations—all of them with that same jaw, that same stubborn set of the mouth, that same look of women who signed things they shouldn't and paid for it and signed anyway.

"I am sorry for all of it," Rafael said. "Every hour of your sixty years. Every hour they add tomorrow if we fail."

"Good," Wren said. "Sorry is useful. Sorry is fuel. Now sit down, because I haven't finished, and the last part is the most important part, and you're going to want to argue, and we don't have time for you to win."

Rafael sat.

*

"The lords think I'm just the coin," Wren said. "The weight on the scale. They've never asked me a question in sixty years except *are you intact* and *how much do you weigh*. That's their mistake, and it's a big one, and here's why."

She climbed back onto the chair and leaned forward, and lowered her voice even though there was no one in the hall below, even though the salt lines were quiet, even though the storm had passed days ago. Some habits were deeper than reason.

"Every debt has a clause of exchange. You taught me that—you thought you were teaching me letters, but I already knew, I just didn't know the *surface* words for it. Every contract the guild copies, every contract the court writes, they all have the same escape stitched into them, because the old law requires it. Balance must be possible. Even the deep court obeys the old law—it's older than they are, and they're afraid of it. That's your flaw in their ledger. I know you found it. I watch you sleep, sometimes. Not in a strange way. In the way you watch a fire."

Rafael made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"But here's what they don't know," Wren said. "Here's what only I know, because I was on the shelf, and I listened, and listening was the only thing I owned." She held up one finger, translucent at the tip, glowing faintly with lamplight behind it. "The exchange clause cuts both ways. Any soul held by the court may be exchanged for a thing of equal weight. Everyone knows that. Everyone assumes the exchange flows one direction—the court gives up the soul, the debtor pays the price. But the clause doesn't say *who* invokes it. It says *a party to the debt*. And Rafael—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, and the old tongue rose in it like water finding its level, and she let it, because some things could only be said properly in the language the mountain dreamed in, "—*a party to the debt is anyone the ledger names. And the ledger names me.*"

Rafael stared at her. "You're saying you could invoke it yourself."

"I'm saying I already know how. I've known for fifty-nine years. I memorized the clause in the dark, in the old script, letter by letter, because it was the only book I had." Wren sat back. "So tomorrow, when you stand before the lords with your unnamed thing in your hands, you won't be alone. When they laugh at you—and they will laugh, they laughed at everyone, I heard them laugh for sixty years—you will not be a lone debtor facing six thousand years of paper. You will be two parties to the debt, standing together, and one of us knows the exact wording that makes their own law bite them."

"Why would you help me?" Rafael asked, and it wasn't suspicion, it was wonder, raw and unguarded. "You could invoke it yourself. Name your price. Walk free. Why bind yourself to my plan, to my risk—"

"Because," Wren said, "an exchange needs a counterweight. Whatever you offer them, it has to balance against sixty years of a grown woman's life, plus interest, plus the vessel, plus the insult of the theft. Nothing you carry in your pockets weighs that much. Nothing in the guild weighs that much." She looked at her own fading hand, and then up, and met Rafael's eyes steadily. "But two offers together. Your thing, whatever it is, and my testimony—my witness, sworn before the old law, that the court held a conscious soul in the dark for sixty years and never once asked her name. That's a scandal, Rafael. That's a crack in their foundation. The old law punishes courts that deal dishonestly. If I testify, they can't simply refuse you. They have to weigh honestly. And honest scales are the only place in six thousand years where people like us have ever won."

The lamp burned steady between them. Somewhere below, the guild hall creaked, settling into night, full of sleeping clerks and bottled storms and borrowed years.

Rafael reached across the desk and took Wren's hand—the translucent one—and held it as if it were made of glass, which was close to true, and as if it were made of steel, which was truer.

"What was your name?" Rafael asked softly. "Your real one. The one from the garden."

Wren opened her mouth to answer—

and found, with a lurch like a floor tilting, that she couldn't. Not because it was forbidden. Because it was gone. Leaked out through the seams with everything else, dripped away night by night back down the mountain, and she sat there searching the shrinking country of herself for it and finding only the shape of the hole where it had been.

"I don't know," she whispered. "I don't know my own name. That's what they took first, when they shelved me. That's how you store a soul safely—you take the name, so it can't be invoked, so it can't be *exchanged*—" She stopped.

Looked up.

Rafael was already reaching for a pen.

"They can't refuse an exchange if I can't be named," Wren breathed, the horror and the hope arriving in the same instant, twins holding hands. "Unless—unless someone names me. Unless someone gives me a name worth weighing. Rafael. *Rafael.* The name you gave me. Is it real? Is it a true name, a naming-name, or just a coat?"

Rafael dipped the pen. Her hand was steady. Her eyes were not.

"It's real," she said. "I gave it in the old form, the binding form, the night you arrived. I didn't know why it felt right. Now I do." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her, and the pen touched down, and she began to write in the old script, letter by letter, the name *Wren* rendered the way the mountain spelled names—in iron and salt and law. "Tomorrow, when the scales come out, they're going to learn that the collateral has a name again. And a witness. And a party."

Outside, far below the city, beneath the roots of the world, something ancient shifted on its shelf—and felt, for the first time in sixty years, the ledger pull tight around its throat.
