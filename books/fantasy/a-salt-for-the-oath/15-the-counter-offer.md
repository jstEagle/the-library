## 15. The Counter-Offer

The hall of the deep court had no ceiling Rafael could see. Stone rose into darkness the way a well rises into sky, and somewhere above, water dripped in slow counts—she'd timed it against her pulse during the walk in and made it forty beats between drops. Forty beats. That was how long she had to hold her nerve each time one of the lords looked at her.

There were seven of them. They sat on thrones that might have been grown rather than carved, columns of pale rock furred with mineral bloom, and they did not move except to speak. Their faces were the faces of people who had been handsome so long ago that beauty had worn down to architecture. When they breathed—and she was not certain they did—the sound was like a door settling.

The master ledger lay open on a table of black glass between them. Her handwriting was already on it: *clause of exchange, invoked by Rafael Ravensworth, blood-line of the oath of salt.* She'd written it herself an hour ago, kneeling on cold stone with Tomas's stolen key warm in her pocket like a second heartbeat, and the ledger had accepted the ink without complaint. The rules cut both ways. She'd told herself that all through the vaults, all through the long corridors where doors whispered her grandmother's name as she passed. The rules cut both ways.

Now she stood before seven ancient lords with Wren's name written in the margin of their oldest debt, and waited for someone to laugh.

It didn't take long.

"An exchange clause," said the lord nearest her. His voice came out of him like wind through a chimney—hollow, and old, and faintly amused. "Invoked. Against us." He turned his head, and the motion took so long she could watch it happen, stone deciding to be flesh again for an afternoon. "Little scribe. Do you know how many have stood where you stand?"

"I imagine the number is small," Rafael said. "Since you keep them."

That earned her something—not laughter yet, but the attention sharpening, seven pairs of eyes going from dull to bright the way coals go when you lift the lid off them. She kept her hands still at her sides. In her left sleeve, pressed flat against the skin of her wrist, was the page she'd torn from the ledger's appendix: the terms, signed in her own blood because ink hadn't been enough and the mountain knew the difference. If they took her now, the page burned. She'd made sure of that. Salt-ink, Wren's recipe, learned in nightmares and copied out in a shaking hand. It would burn even wet, even here, even under stone.

"Small," agreed a second lord, a woman whose crown was a seam of white crystal growing straight out of her skull. "And none of them invoked anything. They begged. Begging is traditional. You've skipped the traditional part."

"The traditional part is why your ledger has clauses nobody uses," Rafael said. "You wrote rules that protect *you*. I've read them. All night, in your vaults, while your seal—" *your living seal, your grandmother, the woman you came here to steal* "—held the door. A clause that only ever works one direction isn't a clause. It's decoration."

The third lord spoke then, and this one she recognized from the ledgers: Vael-of-the-Root, whose signature appeared beside her grandmother's name, beside a hundred names, beside names so old the paper itself had been re-cut twice around them. His throne was the tallest. His face was the least finished.

"A scribe," he said, and the word came out tasting-tested, like he was deciding whether to eat it or spit it. "We have eaten scribes."

"You've *collected* them," Rafael corrected. "There's a difference, and it's in your own books. Page nine hundred and eleven. The entry is annotated in your hand: *asset retained, skill useful.*" She let that sit. "I'm useful too. That's the first thing you should know about me."

"And the second?" said the crystal-crowned woman.

"The second is that I'm not here to beg, and I'm not here to bargain blind. I'm here because your own law requires me to offer something of equal weight for the soul entered as collateral under the oath of salt, and I have found something of equal weight, and when I name it, you will stop smiling."

Silence. Forty heartbeats' worth. Somewhere far above, the drip counted on.

Then Vael-of-the-Root laughed.

It was not a kind sound. It was not even entirely a sound—it was a pressure, a change in the weight of the air, the way a landslide is technically a noise. The other lords joined him, and the laughter rolled around the hall and came back off the unseen dark overhead, and Rafael stood in the middle of it with her nails biting crescents into her palms and thought: *hold, hold, hold,* the way you hold a rope over a drop, the way you hold a pen steady when the contract matters more than the fear.

"They always say that," Vael said, when the echo died. "They always think they have found the thing we cannot replace. Child, do you know what cannot be replaced? Nothing. We are older than replacement. We held the first winter in a jar and traded it to the sea for a spring. We bought a river's memory and sold it back to it, and the river thanked us. There is nothing beneath this mountain—" and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than the laughing "—nothing beneath this mountain, and nothing above it, that we cannot weigh and match and own."

"Then you won't mind weighing what I brought," Rafael said.

She reached into her sleeve.

The crystal-crowned woman moved first—a flicker, faster than anything should move in this room of glacial gestures, and suddenly there were hands of pale stone an inch from Rafael's throat, and the smell of deep places, wet mineral and old dark. But the hands stopped. Because Rafael's fingers had come out of her sleeve holding not a weapon, not a page, but a small square of black glass—the same material as the master ledger's table—cut to the size of a ledger leaf, and blank.

"Terms of audience," she said. "Your own form. Article twelve: any party invoking the clause may present the offered weight upon a tablet of court-glass, and the offer must be heard entire before judgment. Heard *entire*." She looked up at the woman whose hand hovered at her throat. "Or are there amendments since the last recension? Because if your law can be amended mid-audience by whoever's closest, I'd want that noted in the record, and I promise you—" she smiled, and it cost her everything she had, and she spent it anyway "—I write very precise annotations."

The hands withdrew. Slowly. Like a tide going out that intended to come back.

Rafael set the black glass on the table beside the master ledger, and took out her pen, and dipped it in the small vial of her own blood she had carried down the mountain strapped against her ribs like a child.

"Do you know what Wren is worth?" she said, writing as she talked, keeping her voice level by feeding it work. "Not sentimentally. By your figures. I read the valuation last night. A soul entered as collateral under an active oath accrues interest. Hers has been accruing for sixty years—no, don't look surprised, I did the arithmetic, your annuities are elegant even if your ethics are medieval—sixty years at the rate your own tables specify, compounded against the oath-holder's line." She finished a line, blew it dry. "By your numbers, she is worth more today than my grandmother's whole life was when it was taken. Which means the exchange must exceed her. Equal weight, your law says. Not equal value. Weight. And weight compounds."

"She has read our tables," murmured the crystal-crowned woman, to the room, as though reporting weather.

"She has slept in our vaults," said another lord, and there was something new in the voices now. Not warmth. Recognition. The tone of merchants realizing the person across the counter has done the sums.

"So ask me what I'm offering," Rafael said. "Because I know you're dying to."

Vael leaned forward on his grown throne. Around them the hall seemed to lean with him, the whole chamber tilting its attention, and Rafael felt the mountain's regard settle onto her like snowfall—vast, silent, indifferent, and heavy past bearing.

"Name it," he said.

"First, hear the shape of it." She laid down the pen. "You need three things, all of which I can prove from your own records. One: an anchor. Your seal is failing—I've seen her, I've seen what sixty years of being a living lock does, and she is coming apart, lords, she is dust wearing a dress, and when she goes, everything your court is built on goes with her. Every debt, every jarred winter, every river's memory—all of it unanchored. Two: continuity. An anchor must share the nature of what it anchors. And three—" she met Vael's eyes and did not blink "—willingness. Your law, article thirty-one, the oldest recension, the one carved before your glass was cut: *no bond holds that was not freely given.* Even you obey that one. It's why you needed my grandmother's consent, however cruelly obtained. It's why you need Wren's line, not just Wren. And it's why—" her voice did not shake, she made certain of it, she would remember later that it had not shaken "—it's why you cannot simply take me. Taking voids the anchor. You've known that for six hundred years, and it's why you've never once replaced your seal, though you've certainly had candidates."

The silence now was different. It had texture. It had edges.

"What," Vael said slowly, "is your offer."

"My voice," Rafael said.

The word went out into the dark and did not echo. The mountain drank it.

"Not my hearing. Not my hands. My *voice*. The instrument of every contract I've ever written—I dictate as I draft, every clause, every term, spoken aloud to fix it in the world; that's guild-craft, that's the oldest craft there is, word made binding by breath. Take it and I never write another enforceable line. Never speak another oath. Never—" and here, for the first time, her control slipped, just a hairline crack, and she hated herself for it and pushed through anyway "—never say the words I'd want to say, to anyone, for the rest of my life. Silent. Permanently. A scribe who cannot speak is a bell that cannot ring. You know exactly what that costs, because you priced such things before the kingdoms above had names."

She turned the black glass toward them, and the words stood on it in her own hand, in her own blood:

*Offered weight: the voice of Rafael Ravensworth, whole and willing, given freely, to serve as anchor and seal of the deep court, in exchange for the soul entered as collateral under the oath of salt, released entire and unfading.*

"That's the shape of it," she said. "Equal weight. Willing. Freely given. Your law, your forms, your glass, your blood-ink. Now laugh again."

No one laughed.

The crystal-crowned woman rose halfway from her throne and settled back, like a wave reconsidering. Somewhere in the dark above, the dripping had stopped, and the absence of the count was somehow louder than the count had ever been.

"It is not enough," said Vael at last—but he said it carefully, testing the words, and Rafael had spent her life reading contracts and she read this one instantly: he was not refusing. He was opening negotiations.

"Run your valuations," she said. "I'll wait. I've gotten good at waiting. I scratched a calendar into my indenture margins for four years; I can scratch one into your floor."

"It is not enough," Vael repeated, "because a voice is a thing of the upper air. Thin. Borrowed. Breath is not stone, little scribe, and breath dies."

"Breath binds," Rafael said. "Ask your law. Ask the oath of salt, which a woman of the upper air spoke, and which has held this court's greatest asset for sixty years. You anchored your deepest treasure with a surface-woman's word. Don't tell me breath isn't strong enough. Tell me instead what else you have that does what my voice will do—because I've read your inventory, Vael-of-the-Root, all of it, and there is nothing in this mountain that speaks. Nothing down here makes oaths. You take them, you trade them, you hold them—but nothing beneath the mountain can *make* one. That's why you needed my grandmother's consent. That's why you need mine. And that is the one thing you cannot replace, and cannot make, and cannot take." She spread her hands. "You can buy a thousand years of winters. You can't buy a single new vow. I'm offering you the only mint in the world."

For a long moment, the hall did nothing at all. Then, one by one, the seven lords inclined their heads toward each other—not toward her—and conferred in a language that predated speech, a grinding subsonic conversation she felt in her teeth and in the soles of her feet and in the fillings of her bones. Rafael stood inside it and thought of Wren fading like breath off glass, edge by edge, night by night, and of Tomas somewhere above or below or between, and of her grandmother crumbling gratefully in the vaults, waiting for someone to come and finish the arithmetic.

The conference ended. The mountain exhaled.

"There will be conditions," Vael said.

"There are always conditions," Rafael said. "Write yours down. I'll read every word."

He gestured, and a fresh tablet of black glass slid from the table's surface like ice forming, and the first condition began to write itself, and the letters were wrong—too smooth, too eager, curling at the terminals like things that wanted to be swallowed whole—and Rafael watched them come and reached for her pen and thought, with a calm that terrified her more than any laughter had:

*Here is where it gets expensive.*

* * *

The first condition was simple, and she'd expected it: the exchange executed immediately, tonight, before witnesses, no adjournment. Fine. Every hour cost Wren edges; every hour cost her nerve.

The second condition was also expected: the voice surrendered whole, not shared, not leased, not revocable. She signed that line without letting herself feel it. Feeling was for later. Later was a country she might never get to visit.

The third condition stopped her pen.

"*The offered weight shall be proven willing,*" she read aloud, because reading aloud was the verification protocol and the irony of that was not lost on her, "*by the surrender of one true memory of the voice's choosing, to be held in trust alongside the seal.*"

"They're hedging," she said. "Willingness proven by memory. That's not in any recension I read."

"It is in the first one." Vael's voice had gone gentle again, and she understood now that gentleness was his hunting posture. "The recension written on skin. Some terms are not printed, little scribe. They are inherited."

She looked down at the clause, and she saw the trap in it, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and traps were apparently the family trade. *A memory of the voice's choosing.* Her choice. Which meant they would show her nothing, take whatever she named, and hold it forever in the dark next to her voice—and she knew, with the certainty of someone who had audited monsters, that once they held a memory of her, they would hold a hook in her. Debts accrued. Anchors dragged. Sixty years from now, some other descendant of hers would come down this mountain following a thread she'd spun tonight.

But there was no way around it. The clause was load-bearing. Refuse it and the willingness failed, and the willingness failing meant the offer failing, and the offer failing meant the guild kept Wren, and the guild kept Rafael, and the fading child dissolved in a warden's cellar while the paperwork called it justice.

So Rafael Ravensworth did the only thing left to a scribe with nothing but precision left to spend.

She amended.

"One change," she said, and dipped her pen in blood again, and the mountain leaned in to watch. "The memory surrendered shall be chosen by lot. Three memories, sealed, and your own law—article forty-four, dispute resolution, the oldest recension, the one even the skin doesn't override—shall select which is taken. Not me. Not you. The law. So that neither of us can rig it, and neither of us can be blamed, and your precious willingness is proven by chance, which is older than either of us."

Vael regarded her for a long time. Long enough for the drip to resume above them, forty beats, fifty, sixty.

"Clever," he said finally, and the word came out of him like a stone being pried loose. "Clever and costly. Do you understand what you may lose? Chance does not read hearts. Chance might reach into you and take the memory of the man who loves you. Or the child's face. Or your mother's kitchen. Or the sound—" and here his unfinished smile widened by a degree "—of your own name, spoken by someone who meant it."

"I understand exactly what I may lose," Rafael said. "That's what equal weight means. Sign it."

He signed. She signed. The tablets fused together with a sound like a kiss, and the master ledger drank the merged page, and high above them—or perhaps very far below; down here the directions had stopped agreeing with each other—a bell tolled once, and the court rose from its thrones to witness.

Rafael stood in the center of the assembling dark with her blood drying on her fingers and three sealed memories waiting behind her teeth like stones, and she thought of Wren's hand in hers, and Tomas saying her name in the stairwell with the lamps burning low, and her grandmother singing over a cradle sixty years gone, and she did not know which three the law would demand she seal, or which one the lot would devour.

She only knew that in a matter of minutes, she would open her mouth to give away her voice—

—and first, she had to choose what to remember losing.
