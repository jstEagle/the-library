## 19. Running Out of Mountain

*Rafael*

The mountain began to die the moment Wren's soul came loose from its seal.

She felt it first in her feet—a tremor through the vault floor, subtle as a heartbeat skipping. Then the salt in her pockets rattled. Then the great doors of the deep court groaned against their hinges like old men waking from a long sleep and not liking what they found.

Rafael did not stop to understand it. Understanding was a luxury for people with time.

Wren was in her arms, light as kindling now, her edges gone soft as wet paper. The soul had returned to her but her body hadn't learned yet how to hold it—like pouring wine into a cup that remembered being empty. Her eyes were open. That was something. Her eyes were open and they were looking at Rafael, and whatever lived behind them was still there, still hers, still the grown woman compressed into small bones who had waited under the mountain for sixty years to be remembered.

*Hold on,* Rafael wanted to say. *Hold on, little one.*

She couldn't say it. She would never say anything again. The court had taken her voice an hour ago, sealed it in a jar of black glass that some lord of old stone now carried like a trophy or a treasure—she'd never know which—and so she pressed her palm flat against Wren's chest instead, over the fluttering birdcage of her ribs, and signed against her skin: *I have you. Run with me.*

Wren's fingers curled around Rafael's wrist. Small. Cold. But gripping.

Then Tomas was there, skidding around the corner of the vault corridor with the look of a man who had run through hell and found it understaffed. His coat was torn at the shoulder. There was blood on his face that might have been his own; he didn't seem interested in checking.

"Take her," Rafael signed, fast, urgent, the way you sign when the building is falling down. "Carry her. I know the way."

"You don't—" Tomas started, and then the ceiling above them shed a slab of granite the size of a coffin lid, and he stopped arguing and picked up the child.

That was the thing about Tomas Vale. He argued like breathing. But when the world ended, he simply acted, and his arms were strong, and he took Wren from her as gently as if she were made of frost, because she nearly was.

They ran.

The corridors of the deep court had been carved before kingdoms, before writing, before the idea of a door that opened for anyone rather than everyone. They had been built to last forever. Forever, it turned out, was a contract term, and contracts could be broken, and the mountain was breaking them all at once—every oath ever sworn beneath its roots coming due in a single shrieking hour, stone unbinding from stone, the whole architecture of old bargains collapsing into the dark.

Doors that had opened for Rafael's grandmother's name now swung on their own, wild, some of them opening onto nothing at all—onto voids where corridors used to be, onto rooms that had already finished ceasing to exist. One door opened onto a hall filled entirely with falling water, black water, rising. Another opened onto a chamber where the walls were studded with jars—hundreds of them, thousands, each one holding a voice or a name or a year, the court's entire hoard shaking on their shelves like glass teeth.

Rafael looked at those jars for half a second. Half a second too long.

Tomas caught her arm. "Not now," he said—not cruelly, but with the flat certainty of a man who had spent his life among ledgers and knew exactly what a sunk cost looked like. "Not her. Not us. Go."

She signed *yes* and hated herself for looking, and they ran on.

Behind them, something followed. Not the collapse itself—the collapse made no choices—but something choosing to move through the collapse. A sound like a ledger closing. A sound like a pen scratching. She heard it in the walls, patient even now, even at the end: the court's heralds, the ones who kept the accounts, coming to see what had been taken and what remained to be collected.

They had taken a soul. The mountain did not yet know it was allowed to die without it.

*Tomas*

Tomas had carried dying things before. He'd been the Guild of Impossible Things' enforcer for eleven years, and people assumed the work was violence, and sometimes it was, but mostly it was arithmetic—the cold daily business of weighing what a person owed against what a person was, and finding the difference, and collecting it.

He had never once carried anything the way he carried Wren.

She weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it. Sixty years of a life, a whole woman's memories folded into a child's frame, and she weighed less than a sack of salt. Her head lolled against his collarbone and her breath came shallow and strange, like someone remembering how lungs worked after a long absence.

"Hold my neck," he told her quietly, between strides. "Just hold on. That's your only job."

Her fingers tightened. Good girl. Whatever she had been—whatever she still was, underneath the fading—she understood instructions. She had been collateral once. Collateral learns to obey.

The corridor ahead was buckling. He watched the floor rise in a slow wave, stone flexing like the skin of a drum, and he did the math he always did—distance, load, time—and decided the math was wrong and ran anyway, because lately all his math had been wrong in the same direction. Toward her. Toward Rafael. Every calculation he'd made since the night he lied to the heralds had come out the same way: *you will lose everything.* And every time, he'd signed his name to the loss and kept walking.

Ahead, Rafael stopped at a junction and held up three fingers, then pointed left, then down. Three turns, left, descend. He didn't ask how she knew. He'd seen her do this for hours now—navigate by memory and gesture through a labyrinth that was actively forgetting its own shape—and he had stopped questioning it somewhere around the third collapsed gallery, the way you stop questioning a rope while drowning.

But he did ask one thing. He slowed just enough to sign it badly, clumsily, his hands making shapes that would have embarrassed any proper speaker: *Which way is out?*

Rafael's answer was immediate and terrifying. She pointed *up*, then held up both hands, fingers spread, ten, then folded four down. Six. Six levels up. Through six floors of a mountain that was eating itself.

Tomas looked at the number and felt something in his chest go quiet and cold.

"We can't outrun it," he said aloud, knowing she'd read his lips if nothing else. "Six levels. The stairwells are going first—they're the oldest cuts, they fail first." He turned to face her fully, Wren shifting in his arms, and said the next part slowly, deliberately, so she could take it from his mouth alone: "So we don't outrun it. We get ahead of it. You lead. I'll watch behind. If I say *down*, we find cover and we let it pass over us. Agreed?"

Rafael nodded once. Then she did something he hadn't expected. She reached out and touched his jaw—briefly, hard, a strike more than a caress—and signed against his skin, fast, private, a language within the language: *You should have run the other way. Thank you for not running the other way.*

He wanted to answer. There wasn't time. There was never going to be time again; he understood that now, standing inside the death of a court older than language, holding a resurrected child, following a mute woman deeper into the ruin because her memory was the only map either of them trusted.

Trust. Eleven years he'd sold his judgment to the guild and his obedience to the court below, and no one—not his masters, not his handlers, not the lords of old stone themselves—had ever trusted him with anything that mattered. Trust was what other people gave. He counted. He collected. He reported.

And now a woman who had every reason to hate him was betting her life, and the child's life, on his back and his arms and his word.

His word. Which, until nine days ago, had been worth exactly what the court paid for it.

He adjusted Wren higher against his chest and ran.

*Rafael*

The stairs were worse than she'd feared.

Not because they were destroyed—some of them were intact, miraculously, spiraling up through the dark like the inner ears of some vast buried creature—but because they were *choosing*. The doors of the deep court had always recognized names. Now, unbound, they recognized nothing, and a door that recognizes nothing opens for no one, or opens for everyone, or opens onto hunger. Rafael learned the difference by touch. Safe doors breathed—cool air moving through them, air that had somewhere to go. Hungry doors exhaled warmth, and behind the warmth was a sound like pages turning, like the heralds reading aloud the inventory of everything the collapse had shaken loose.

She led them past four hungry doors in the first flight alone. At each one she put her palm flat to the stone, felt for breath, and moved on. Tomas watched her do it and said nothing, but he'd begun to mirror her without meaning to—when she stopped, he stopped; when she tilted her head listening, he went still and listened too. Two people learning each other's bodies as instruments, because words were gone and gestures were slow and the mountain was fast.

On the third level, the memory failed her.

She had walked these passages only once, years ago, in the archive of her own mind—her grandmother's stories, told to a seven-year-old across a kitchen table, the deep court described in fragments: *the hall of hanging lamps, the stair that sings, the door with no handle that hates liars.* Stories were maps with most of the lines rubbed out. She had navigated this far on grandmother's fragments and her own stubbornness, and now she stood at a junction where the fragments ran out, and behind her the sound of the heralds was getting louder, and Wren—

Wren lifted her head.

The child, fading, barely present, raised her face from Tomas's shoulder and looked down the right-hand passage with an expression Rafael had seen only once before: on the night she arrived at the guild door, mud-caked, storm-wrapped, speaking the pre-kingdom tongue. The expression of someone who remembers.

"She knows," Tomas murmured. "Look at her. She's pointing."

Wren's small hand rose, trembling, and indicated the passage on the right. Then she spoke—one word, in the old tongue, the deep tongue, the language beneath all languages. Rafael heard it and understood it the way she understood all of it now, as if remembering rather than learning:

*Down-then-up. The singing stair.*

The singing stair. Grandmother's fragment. The stair that sang—it hummed, the old woman had said, a note too low for most ears, a note that meant the way was true. Rafael had thought it a story. Everything she'd thought a story had turned out to be a clause.

She signed to Tomas: *Right. Trust her.*

He hesitated—one heartbeat, the hesitation of a man whose entire profession was verifying claims against evidence—and then he looked at Wren's pointing hand, at the child's absolute certainty, and he turned right without another word.

The singing stair hummed. Low, vast, a note felt in the teeth more than heard, and it rose with them, level by level, a thread of true pitch running up through the mountain's chaos. Rafael climbed with her hand on the wall and tears she refused to acknowledge running silently down her face, because the child who had been sold, the soul who had been held, the grown woman compressed into small bones—she was guiding them home. Collateral, remembering the way out of the vault where she'd been kept. Sixty years of captivity, and her first free act was to lead two strangers toward daylight.

*Tomas*

The heralds found them on the fifth level.

Of course they did. Heralds were the court's accounting department, and accounts, unlike mountains, do not collapse. They came out of a side gallery—three of them, tall, grey, their faces smooth as unfinished stone, their voices carrying the dry rustle of turning pages—and they did not run. Running was for debtors. They simply advanced, at the pace of inevitability, and their leader spoke.

"The exchange is recorded," it said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly. "The voice is received. The soul is released. These things are settled. But the seal is broken, scribe, and a broken seal must be mended, and you are the last piece of the old bargain still walking. Come back. The court is falling, yes—but courts rebuild. What is owed survives. It always survives. It is the only thing that does."

Tomas heard the words and felt his whole body go cold, because the herald was right, and he knew it was right, and rightness had been his trade for eleven years. Debts survived. That was the entire architecture of his life. He had enforced debts through fire, through flood, through the deaths of three guild masters, because the debt outlasted everything and someone had to keep the count.

But he looked down at Wren in his arms—fading, gripping his collar with fingers like frost, trusting him absolutely because she had no one else—and he looked at Rafael, standing between him and the heralds with her empty hands and her ruined throat and her spine straight as a stylus, and he understood that the herald had made an error. A bookkeeping error. The kind he himself had made a thousand times.

It had counted the debt and forgotten to count the debtor.

"Your court is dead," Tomas said. "You just haven't finished the paperwork."

The heralds flinched. All three of them. As if the sentence had struck them physically—as if a court that traded in souls had never once considered that it might be a soul itself, held, owned, bound by terms it had written for others.

Then the ceiling came down between them, half the gallery folding into rubble, and the argument was suspended by forces larger than either party, and Tomas turned and ran, and this time the heralds did not follow—because even inevitability, it turned out, could be buried.

*Rafael*

One level left.

The air changed first. Rafael tasted it on her split lips—mineral giving way to green, stone-smell thinning into something that contained rain, contained pine, contained the high thin cold of the world above. Daylight was close. She could feel it the way Wren had felt the singing stair: not knowledge but memory, the body remembering what it was for.

Tomas was slowing. She saw it and pretended not to—he'd been carrying Wren for hours, through ruin and darkness, and his legs were shaking now, visible tremors, and his breathing had gone ragged in a way that frightened her more than the heralds had. Strong men breaking quietly were the worst thing she knew.

So she did the only thing left in her inventory. She stopped, turned, and held out her arms.

He stared at her. *You want to carry her?* his face said plainly. *You're half-dead yourself.*

*I know,* she signed. *Give her to me anyway. You carry the salt. We trade weights. Neither of us makes the last stretch alone.*

He handed Wren over with the care of a man passing a flame through wind, and took the salt bags off Rafael's shoulders, and they went on—Rafael climbing with the child against her chest, feeling that terrible featherweight, feeling Wren's grip tighten and loosen and tighten again like a heart learning its rhythm; Tomas behind her with the salt and the stolen key and the last of his strength, watching the dark below for the shape of pursuit that never quite appeared again.

The final door was the oldest one. Grandmother had described it too: *the last door doesn't open. It gives up.* It had no hinges she could see, no seam, only a faint outline in the rock like a watermark, and when Rafael pressed her grandmother's name against it one more time—the name that had unlocked her path down, inheritance as key and curse both—the stone did not swing wide. It sighed. It softened. It let them through the way grief lets you through eventually: not because you've earned it, but because it's tired of holding you.

Daylight hit them like a blow.

They came out onto a mountainside steeped in late gold, real sun, real wind, grass bending in it, and below—far below, impossibly far, a day's descent away—the lights of the valley and the grey roofs of the guild. Rafael stood in the wind with Wren in her arms and Tomas beside her, three survivors on a slope of living grass, and for one full minute nobody moved, because for one full minute the world was simply, unbearably, ordinary.

Then Wren stirred against her chest and whispered, in the old tongue, one more word.

Rafael understood it instantly. It was not *home*. It was not *free*.

It was *they are coming*.

And far below, at the treeline, at the very edge of sight, she saw them: wardens. Guild wardens, mounted, riding hard for the mountain's foot—with, she would swear on every balanced ledger she'd ever kept, the guild master's banner snapping above them.

The mountain had let them go. The guild had not.

Tomas saw it too. He went very still, the stillness of a man recognizing his own employers at a distance, and then he said the only useful thing left in his voice:

"They'll reach the low road before we do. Unless we cut east, through the scree, and never stop."

Rafael looked at the scree field—a river of loose stone, sharp and unstable, sliding away downslope, the kind of ground that broke ankles and swallowed children. She looked at Wren, translucent as breath on glass. She looked at the banner below, and at the man beside her who had already given up everything he was built to be.

Then she pointed east, and started walking, and did not look back at the mountain that had taken her voice, or the guild that was about to demand its pound of flesh.

Some debts, she thought, signing the resolve into her own silence, are paid in motion.
