## 22. The First Word

Wren woke before the sun did, the way she always did now, and lay in her narrow bed listening to the guild wake up around her.

The Guild of Impossible Things had a sound in the morning that no other place in the world had. It was the sound of paper being squared against paper, of ink pots unstoppered, of Rafael's boots on the stairs two at a time because she never learned to walk slowly through a building full of work she loved. It was the sound of Tomas arguing with the kettle in the kitchen because the kettle had opinions and Tomas refused to lose to an appliance. It was the sound of salt being swept from the thresholds — not because they feared anything anymore, but because sweeping it had become a habit, and habits were how buildings learned to be homes.

Wren counted the sounds the way other children counted sheep.

*Boots. Kettle. Broom. Boots again.*

Then the door at the end of the hall opened, and light spilled across her floor, and Rafael leaned in with her hair still damp and her sleeves rolled wrong, one inside out, and looked at Wren the way she looked at her every morning: like checking. Like counting fingers and edges and making sure nothing had gone translucent in the night.

It hadn't. Not for a year. Not once.

Rafael couldn't say *good morning*. That was the shape of the thing they all lived around, the way you lived around a missing tooth — tongue finding the gap, always, but learning not to press. Rafael's voice lived under a mountain now, traded for a child's soul, and she had signed the trade with both hands and open eyes, and if she regretted it she had never once shown it, not even on the bad nights when words piled up behind her teeth and came out instead as ink, pages and pages of it, written fast and torn out and burned.

But this morning Rafael didn't just look. She pointed at Wren, then at the table downstairs, then made a gesture — flat palm, slow push — that meant *sit*, and another — finger to her own eyes, then to Wren — that meant *I'm watching you*, which was Rafael's way of saying *I love you* without saying anything at all.

Wren nodded solemnly, as befitted a girl with a secret.

Because today was the day. She had known it for three days, the way you know a storm is coming — not with your head but with your skin. The sentence had been living in her chest since midwinter, whole and finished, waiting for her hand to be steady enough and her courage to be big enough. She had practiced the letters in the dust of the archive floor when no one was looking. She had practiced them in steam on windows. Last night she had practiced them on the back of her own hand, in the dark, tracing each stroke until her finger knew the way without her mind's help.

Today her hand would be steady enough.

She got dressed. She went down to the table. She sat where the morning light came through the east window and turned everything the color of honey, and she waited, hands folded, heart going like a bird in a box, while the guild woke up around her piece by piece.

Tomas brought bread. He set a slice by her elbow without asking, the way he always did, and ruffled her hair, and she let him, because a year ago she would have bitten him and now she knew his hands were kind and that kindness was worth more than pride. He sat across from her with his tea and his ledgers — he kept the honest ones now, the new ones, the ones with no souls in them anywhere — and he worked in comfortable silence while the light crawled across the table.

Rafael came down last. She always came down last, because she stopped at every door on the way and checked every ward and read every seal, and Wren understood that too: some people carry their fear forward into carefulness, and carefulness is its own kind of love.

Rafael sat beside her. Opened her ledger. Dipped her pen.

And Wren took out the paper.

She had stolen it weeks ago from the scrap bin — a clean corner, good stock, too fine to waste — and she had kept it flat under her mattress, weighted with her one treasure, a river stone from the mountain's mouth that Tomas had given her the day they all came out into the daylight and did not die. She smoothed it now on the table's grain. She took Rafael's spare pen, the stubby one, the apprentice pen, because her hand was small and the good pens were too long.

And she wrote.

Her letters were crooked. Her lines wandered like a drunk walking home. But every stroke was true — the deep strokes and the shallow ones, the hooks and the running tails, the old pre-kingdom script that no living scholar could read except the dead woman who had taught it to her from inside her own bones, and the scribe sitting beside her who had learned it in a single terrible year because love is also a language and languages can be studied.

Wren wrote six words. It took her a long time. Tomas stopped working halfway through and watched without breathing. Rafael's pen hovered over her ledger and did not fall.

When it was done, Wren sat back and looked at what she had made, and her eyes stung, and she blinked the sting away because this was not a moment for crying. This was a moment for reading aloud.

That was the whole point. That was the promise she had made herself in the dark, practicing on her own skin: *aloud.* Because Rafael had given her voice away, and voices should not be given away in silence, and someone in this room owed the mountain a debt of noise.

Wren picked up the paper. Held it in both hands, the way Rafael held contracts when they mattered.

And she read.

"*What was given will be remembered.*"

Her voice cracked on the second word — she was still small, and the old tongue sat heavy in a small throat, consonants like stones, vowels like water over them. But she pushed through. She said it again, steadier. She said it a third time, and by then it wasn't stumbling anymore; it was ringing. The old language filled the kitchen the way it must have filled courts beneath mountains before there were kingdoms above them, before anyone thought to sell a soul or write a debt, and it sounded, Wren thought, the way the word *home* would sound if *home* were a place instead of people.

Silence after. The good kind. The kind that has weight and warmth.

Tomas had put his face in his hand. His shoulders were shaking, very slightly, and Wren pretended not to notice, because grown men deserved their dignity even when they were crying into their tea.

And Rafael —

Rafael was looking at the paper. Reading it with her eyes first, the way she read everything, twice, always twice. Then she looked up at Wren, and her face was doing something complicated, something with too many things in it to name, grief and joy and a year's worth of unsaid words all trying to get out the same door at once.

Then she put down her pen.

She raised her hands.

And she answered.

Wren had seen Rafael sign before — little things, household things, *pass the bread*, *time for bed*, the quick shorthand of people who live together. But this was different. This was slow. This was formal. This was the old tongue in its oldest form, the hand-speech that had come before the writing, the way the first speakers below the world had talked when their mouths were busy with oaths. Rafael's fingers moved like they had been waiting a year for permission: *what was given* — one hand closing, opening, giving itself away into empty air — *will be remembered* — the other hand pressing flat over her own heart, then turning outward, offering the memory to the room, to the light, to Wren.

Perfectly. Every sign exact. As if she had learned it not in a year but in a lifetime, as if it had been sleeping in her hands the way the old words had slept in Wren's bones, waiting for blood to call them awake.

*What was given will be remembered.*

Said in silence. Said better than silence usually can.

Wren launched herself across the table — papers scattering, Tomas's tea sacrificed, the stone clattering to the floor — and wrapped her arms around Rafael's neck, and Rafael held her back, hard, one hand cradling the back of her head, and neither of them needed any language at all for that part.

* * *

They hung the sentence above the door.

Not the original — that went into the new archive, in a frame, in the place of honor where the old soul-ledgers used to sit before Rafael burned them in the yard one bright afternoon while the whole guild watched and nobody stopped her. The original stayed under glass, the crooked letters and wandering lines preserved exactly as they were, because Rafael said — signed, rather, with dry precision — that perfection was for people who hadn't earned their words, and Wren had earned hers crooked.

But Tomas copied it out fair, in his neat enforcer's hand, onto a board, and Rafael painted it, and they hung it above the guild's front door where everyone who entered had to pass under it:

*WHAT WAS GIVEN WILL BE REMEMBERED.*

In the old tongue above, in the common below. So that the dead language would live in the doorway, and everyone who walked through it would carry a few of its shapes away with them without ever knowing they'd been taught.

People started coming to learn it. That was the strange part, the part Wren hadn't expected. First scholars, sniffing and skeptical, leaving quieter than they arrived. Then students. Then children from the town, dragging their parents by the sleeve — *the girl at the guild reads the mountain-language, Mama, the real one* — until the front room on teaching days was so full that Rafael had to stand on a chair to be seen, signing slowly, patiently, her hands translating a dead court into a living room.

Wren helped teach. She was, after all, the only native speaker left.

Some evenings, after the students went home, she and Rafael would sit in the kitchen and talk — really talk, the old way, hands flying, no paper needed. Rafael told her things in signs that she had never managed to write: about her grandmother, whose name opened doors in the dark; about the vault, and the dust, and the sound a soul makes when it finally stops holding up a ceiling. About the price, though never bitterly. About the price almost gratefully, which was the strangest thing of all, and which Wren understood anyway, because she had been the price, and here she was, alive, eating bread at a table, and some trades you thank the universe for even while you mourn what they cost.

One night Wren asked her, hands moving in the firelight: *Do you miss it? Your voice?*

Rafael considered for a long time. Firelight moved on her face.

Then: *I had a voice,* she signed. *It wrote contracts. Now I have hands. They write truer ones.*

And then, because Rafael was incapable of letting a tender moment stand unbargained: *Also, your handwriting is still terrible. We'll work on it.*

Wren threw a crust of bread at her. Rafael caught it and ate it, insufferable, beloved.

* * *

On the anniversary of the moonrise — the real one, the deadline that had nearly taken her — Wren climbed the hill behind the guild alone, as she did every year now, and stood where the mountain was visible on the horizon, a gray shoulder against the sky, quiet these days, its courts emptied, its doors sealed with salt and memory.

She spoke to it. Out loud. On purpose.

"I remember," she said in the old tongue, and the wind took the words and carried them toward the stone. "Everything. I remember being grown. I remember the seal, and the dark, and the long sleep inside a child's bones. And I remember her hands on my face when we ran, and his arms carrying me toward the light, and the way the mountain screamed when it lost us."

She paused. The wind paused with her, or seemed to.

"You thought a soul was a thing you could hold," she told the mountain. "You were wrong. A soul is a thing that remembers. And remembering gets out."

She turned her back on the peak and walked down the hill toward the lit windows of the guild, toward the smell of Tomas's bread and the scratch of Rafael's pen, toward the door with the sentence hanging over it, and as she passed beneath her own crooked letters she reached up and touched them, the way you touch a lintel for luck, the way you touch a scar to remind yourself it healed.

Inside, Rafael looked up from her ledger and signed without looking away from the page: *Wash your hands, dinner's soon.*

Wren grinned and signed back, fluent, easy, native: *Make me.*

And somewhere far below the world, in a court of old stone where no one ruled anymore, the words settled into the rock like seed into soil, and waited — not as a debt, not as an oath, not as collateral on anything — but as a gift, freely given, the first inheritance her family had ever passed down that cost the receiver nothing at all.

*What was given will be remembered.*

It was. It is. She made sure of it.
