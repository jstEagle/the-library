## 1. Charms for a Burning City

The fishwife's hands smelled of brine and copper, and they shook so badly the coin nearly slipped from them twice before it reached Yara's palm.

"Again," the woman whispered. "Please. The herring have not come in three days, and my Tam swears he saw something black on the water—something that *watched* him. I need a stronger charm than last time."

Yara kept her smile soft and her voice softer. "There is no such thing as a charm too weak, Mistress Odile. Only hearts too heavy to feel it working." She drew the little bundle from her tray: knotted thread, a dried sprig of yarrow—her namesake, her mother's joke made permanent—a pinch of salt from the eastern flats wrapped in waxed linen. Harmless things. Things that could hang above any door in Saltmere without drawing a second glance.

What she poured into them was another matter entirely.

She let the gift move through her fingers the way she'd taught herself over seven years: thin as a thread of spider silk, cool as well water, gone before it finished arriving. Enough to settle a frightened heart. Enough to turn one bad day aside. Never enough to ripple the air or warm the room or do anything the old stories called *magic*. The criers had a word for what she was now, and Yara had spent seven years making sure no one would ever use it about her.

Odile pressed the charm to her lips, then tucked it into her bodice with the tenderness of a woman hiding a love letter. "Bless you, girl. Bless your clever hands." Her eyes were red-rimmed, and not from the wind off the harbor. "They say the king's men burned a woman in Vell's Cross last week. A *healer*, they said. She'd pulled a fever out of a child with her bare hands, saved its life, and they—" The fishwife stopped herself, glanced at the crowd, at the gray sky, at nothing. "Forgive me. Old talk. Nobody believes old talk anymore."

"Nobody," Yara agreed gently, and took the coin.

It was then that the drums began.

* * *

Saltmere's market square had heard many drums in Yara's twenty-nine years: festival drums, war drums, once—terribly—the plague drums. These were none of those. These were slow, deliberate, spaced like footsteps, and every conversation in the square died between one beat and the next.

The royal criers came up from the customs house four abreast, in livery the color of wet slate, each carrying a scroll sealed with the sun-and-sword of House Maravel. The lead crier mounted the fountain steps, unrolled his scroll, and read in a voice trained to carry across harbors and battlefields alike:

*"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, of that name King of Carreth and its provinces: be it known that the crown declares a general hunt for practitioners of the forbidden gift, henceforth styled witchcraft. All persons possessing, concealing, or abetting such power are commanded to present themselves for assessment within thirty days. Those who come freely shall be marked and spared. Those who are discovered shall be tried. Those who flee shall be hunted to the borders of the realm and beyond them."*

A pause, long enough for gulls to cry and for someone near the fish stalls to drop a basket of mussels, the clatter enormous in the silence.

*"Informants presenting verified reports shall receive ten crowns per name. The crown thanks its loyal subjects, and reminds them: a neighbor's silence is a neighbor's kindness returned."*

Ten crowns per name. Yara did the arithmetic without wanting to. Ten crowns was half a winter's firewood. Ten crowns was a new net for a struggling boat. Ten crowns was enough to make kindness very expensive indeed.

The square did not erupt. That was the horror of it. The square simply *bent*—neighbors angling their bodies slightly away from one another, eyes dropping, smiles going stiff as painted masks. She watched a baker look too long at the herb-woman's stall. She watched two dockhands exchange a glance that had arithmetic in it. The whole market became a ledger, every person in it an entry waiting to be written down.

Yara packed her tray with hands that did not shake, because she had practiced this too. Seven years of practice. Seven years of being small and dull and harmless, of selling charms that were only thread and salt and superstition, of laughing at the right jokes and never, ever letting the gift rise past her wrists.

Invisibility had always been an illusion. She had simply never expected the illusion to shatter all at once, in front of everyone, with drums.

* * *

Her rooms above the chandler's shop on Tanner's Lane held everything she owned, which was not much, and everything she could not leave behind, which was less. She worked by the light of one shuttered lamp, sorting her stock into two piles: the dead stock and the living.

The dead stock went into the hearth first. Bundles of yarrow, knotwork amulets, bottles of "blessed rainwater" that was only rainwater—all the harmless theater she'd built around herself like a shell. It caught quickly; she'd dressed it in tallow. The smoke was bitter and sweet at once, and she stood watching it climb the flue, thinking of her mother doing exactly this, seventeen years ago, on a night much like this one.

Her mother had burned slower about it. Had saved the important things for last, standing at the hearth with her sleeves rolled back, explaining in her calm way: *The gift isn't in the charms, little wren. It never was. Burn the props and they'll believe there's nothing left to find.* Then she had opened the floorboard under the loom and taken out the thing she swore Yara would understand someday—the sigil drawn in salt and iron on a scrap of oilcloth—and put it in Yara's hands instead of the fire.

*If they ever come for us,* her mother had said, *you run east. Past where the maps stop pretending. Your grandmother will know why.*

Her grandmother had died before Yara ever thought to ask. But Yara still had the oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her coat these seven years, and she knew exactly what lay east of Saltmere, past where the maps stopped pretending, because her mother had shown her once, when she was nine, from the top of the chalk cliffs: a road that appeared only at dusk, running out over country that wasn't on any chart, toward a border that had been erased from every map in the kingdom by decree of a king three generations dead.

And she knew what stood at the end of it. She had heard it in her sleep for seventeen years: the sound the gate made when it closed, taking her father and her brother and most of her mother's gift with it, leaving her mother hollowed and quiet and dead within the year, leaving Yara with a sliver of inherited power and a lifetime of reasons never to use it.

The living stock fit in a satchel: three true charms, made strong, for emergencies she prayed would never come. A knife. Dried food. The oilcloth. And beneath it all, wrapped in wool, the one thing she had sworn never to touch again—a smooth river stone her brother had given her the summer before the gate, still faintly warm after seventeen years, though she had never once fed it power.

She looked at the stone a long time. Then she wrapped it tighter and buried it at the bottom of the satchel, and told herself the ache in her chest was only smoke.

Outside, the drums had stopped. In their place came the worse sound: knocking. Door to door down Tanner's Lane, unhurried, methodical. The census men had always knocked like that. These knockers would be asking different questions.

Yara pinched out the lamp, shouldered the satchel, and went down the back stairs into the dark.

* * *

She meant to cut through the market one last time—it was the fastest route to the east road, and the stalls were striking, the crowds thinned to stragglers and informants. She kept her hood up and her pace bored, the walk of a woman with nowhere to be and nothing to hide.

"You there. Charm-seller."

The voice stopped her heart before her feet.

She turned slowly. He stood by the shuttered fountain with a lantern raised, and the light found him in pieces: the slate-gray traveling coat of the crown's new order, the iron badge at his collar shaped like a sword through a sun, the lean face she had known since they were both children stealing apples off Deacon Merrit's wall.

Tomas Vael. Who had shared her bench at school. Who had once sworn, aged eleven, spitting apple seeds into the harbor, that he'd marry her if she'd have him, and laughed when she said she'd rather be a fish. Who had left Saltmere for the capital at nineteen and come back, apparently, wearing the hunt on his throat.

His lantern hand was steady. His eyes were not.

"Yara Yarrow," he said quietly. Not a question. He'd known her at fifty paces; he'd probably known her since she entered the square.

"Tomas." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice of a harmless woman. "They say you've done well for yourself."

"They say a great many things." He came closer, boots ringing on the cobbles, and lowered the lantern so its light didn't reach her face—an act of mercy or of cowardice, she couldn't tell. "I have to ask you questions. It's procedure. Where were you during the proclamation? What do you sell? Have you ever—" He stopped. Rubbed his jaw the way he used to before examinations. "Gods, Yara. Ten crowns a name. Do you know how many people in this town would sell you for ten crowns? Half of them have bought your charms."

"Then you'd best write me down," she said, "and collect your wages."

Something crossed his face—pain, or fury, or both wearing the same mask. "Is that what you think I came back to do?"

"I think you came back wearing that badge, and the badge does the deciding, whatever you came back to do." She held his gaze. Around them the square emptied, stall by stall, and somewhere down the lane the knocking continued, closer now. "You used to be the boy who lied to Deacon Merrit so I wouldn't get whipped. I'm asking you to remember him."

For a long moment Tomas Vael said nothing. The lantern guttered. When he spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "There's a list. Names go on it tonight, and riders go out at dawn. If your name isn't on it by morning bell..." He couldn't finish it. He looked away, jaw tight, and thrust something into her hand—folded, small, heavy. A purse. "The east road past the chalk cliffs. Don't take the coast path; it's being watched. And Yara—" His voice cracked, just barely, on her name. "Don't come back. Whatever happens. Whatever you find out there. *Don't come back.*"

He turned and walked away, lantern swinging, toward the next name on his list, and Yara stood alone in the emptying square with a purse of crowns she didn't want and a warning she hadn't asked for, understanding at last that invisibility had never been real—that she had been seen all along, by the one person whose seeing could save or damn her, and he had chosen, for tonight, to look away.

Dawn was three hours off. The riders would ride at first light.

Yara pulled her hood lower and walked east, toward the chalk cliffs, toward dusk, toward the road that wasn't there.
