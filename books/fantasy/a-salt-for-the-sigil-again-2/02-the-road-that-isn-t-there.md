## 2. The Road That Isn't There

The tide was going out of Saltmere the way everything else had been going out of it all year: slowly, and without any intention of coming back.

Yara found Dain Kestrel where she knew he would be—at the low tavern on the harbor's blind side, the one with no sign above its door because a sign invited questions, and questions were the one cargo Dain never carried. He sat in the corner with his back to the wall, as he always had, a cup of something brown and honest in front of him and his eyes doing their slow arithmetic of every person who came through the door.

He saw her before she reached the table. She watched it happen—the flicker, the stillness, the way his hand stopped halfway to the cup. Seven years, and grief still moved through him like weather.

"Yara Yarrow." He said her name quietly, as if it might be overheard by someone who could use it against her.

"Dain Kestrel." She sat down across from him without being asked. "I need passage."

"You need a lot more than that." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the wood. "There are criers in every square from here to the capital. *By order of the crown, all bearers of unlicensed gift shall surrender themselves for assessment.* Assessment." He laughed without any humor in it. "They've started calling the pyres assessments."

"I know what they're calling them. I sold charms in that square this morning while they read it out."

Dain looked at her then—really looked, the way he used to when they were young enough that looking cost nothing. "You always did have more nerve than sense."

"One of us had to have some nerve. You became a smuggler."

"I became a smuggler because I have exactly enough sense to know when a thing is worth the risk." His voice dropped. "And you're asking me to smuggle you toward the border that isn't on any map."

"The border that was erased from every map," she corrected. "Which means somewhere under the erasure, there's ink. Somewhere under the burning, there's a road."

* * *

Dain Kestrel had told himself, for seven years, that he had made his peace with what happened at the gate. He had told himself this in a hundred harbors, in a hundred dark rooms, usually after drinking enough to make the telling stick for an hour or two. It had never once stuck past dawn.

Now she sat across from him with soot still ghosting her knuckles—she'd burned her stock, then; burned seven years of harmless little charms, luck-knots and calm-salves and the thousand small mercies she'd hidden her gift inside—and she was asking him to take her to the one place in the world he had sworn never to go again.

"You know what's out there," he said. "Past the last village. The land goes strange. Roads forget where they were going."

"Not all of them. There's one road that only shows at dusk. My mother walked it. She wrote about it in—" Yara stopped. Her jaw tightened around whatever the rest of the sentence was.

"In the book the inquisitors burned," Dain finished for her.

"In the book some of which I remember anyway." She leaned forward. "I can pay. I have silver, and I have things worth more than silver."

"I don't want your silver."

"What do you want?"

The question hung there, dangerous, old. Dain looked at the window, at the gray light coming off the water, and answered honestly because lying to her had never once worked. "I want to not spend the rest of my life wondering if I could have gotten you out. So don't pay me. Just let me come."

* * *

They left Saltmere two nights later, in a cart of dried herring that smelled like a crime scene, driven north along the coast road until the coast itself grew uncertain—cliffs giving way to salt marsh, marsh giving way to a country of gray grass that bent all in one direction even when there was no wind.

The dusk-road revealed itself the way a fish reveals itself under dark water: not appearing, but becoming apparent. One moment there was only moorland and the bruised purple light dying in the west. The next, there was a track running straight as a ruled line through the grass, pale as bone, wide enough for the cart.

"There it is," Yara breathed.

"Don't say it like that," Dain muttered, but he turned the cart onto it anyway.

The road felt different underfoot than ordinary ground—a faint warmth, almost like walking on a sleeping animal. The grass on either side stood taller than it should, and the sky overhead deepened too quickly, dusk pooling around them like ink in water. And though they traveled through full night, the road stayed visible, glowing faintly, keeping its own small hour of evening wherever it went.

"It remembers dusk," Yara said softly. "That's what my mother wrote. The old roads were laid by walkers who worked at twilight, between the sun's gift and the stars'. They hold that hour forever. A threshold hour. Nothing licensed can see you clearly on a threshold."

"Nothing licensed," Dain repeated. "But the unlicensed can."

Behind them, far back along the way they'd come, a light flared. Not torchlight—torchlight wandered. This light moved with purpose, a cold white point like a falling star that refused to fall.

"They've found the road," Yara said.

"They've found *a* road. Whether it's ours—" But even as he said it, the light steadied, held its bearing, and began to gain.

* * *

Inquisitor Maren Voss rode with the white lantern held high and hated every mile of it.

She had known, since the moment she'd failed to speak Yara Yarrow's name in the market square, that this day would come. She had reported a hundred others since the hunts began—neighbors, a miller, a midwife whose hands had warmed fevered children for thirty years—and each report had cost her something she could not name and did not get back. But Yara's name she had swallowed, and a swallowed name does not dissolve. It sits in the chest like a stone and waits.

Her orders were simple: the charm-seller of Saltmere had fled north. Find her. Assess her.

Maren rode, and told herself she was following orders, and did not examine too closely why she had volunteered for a pursuit that led into the erased country, where the crown's writ ran thin as morning frost.

At her saddle rode a second passenger no one could see: a small brass locket, worn smooth, containing a curl of hair and a name she had never spoken aloud in seven years. *Lisbet.* Her daughter, who at age four had frozen a cup of water solid just by crying at it, and whom Maren had hidden ever since behind a screen of lies and distance and a husband who believed their child simply sickly.

If Yara Yarrow was running toward the old border, then perhaps the stories were true. Perhaps there was a door.

Maren spurred her horse and did not let herself hope. Hope was a gift too, and hers was long spent.

* * *

The sigil appeared at moonrise.

It was cut into a standing stone at the road's end—or rather, the road ended and the stone began, a single gray pillar in a circle of trampled earth where the grass refused to grow. And on its face, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather but unmistakable to a daughter's eye, was the mark Yara had grown up tracing in her mother's palm: a gate-shape, an arch within a circle, crossed by three lines like ripples on water.

Below it, in the old script, a single word.

"*Salt*," Yara read aloud, and her breath caught. "A salt for the sigil. That's what she called it. That's what the book was named for."

Dain set the lantern down and stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her cloak, and did not touch her, and the not-touching said everything the years had left unsaid.

"Is it real?" he asked. "The border. Is it really here?"

Yara pressed her palm flat against the stone. Beneath the cold surface, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a door remembering it was a door.

"It's here," she whispered. "It's been here the whole time, waiting for a keeper."

Behind them, over the dark moor, the white light of the inquisitors' lantern crested the hill—closer now, much closer—and Yara thought she could see, silhouetted at its edge, a rider she had once called friend.

"Maren," she breathed.

The pulse beneath her palm beat faster, as if the gate had heard the name too—and wanted something from it.
