## 3. What the Gate Remembers

The gate did not look like a door.

That was the first cruelty of it. Seven years of nightmares had built Yara a vision of black stone and burning script, a wound in the world she could point to and name. What she found instead was a hillside of grey granite veined with quartz, and in the hillside's face a shape so ordinary it hurt—an archway of fitted stone, knee-high bracken growing through its threshold, moss softening the keystone like a hand laid gently over a mouth.

Nothing came through it. Nothing went. But the air inside the arch was wrong the way still water is wrong, and when Yara stood before it, the charm at her throat—her last one, the luck-knot she'd braided for herself and never had the courage to wear until now—grew warm as a held bird.

"She's here," Yara said quietly. "Mama's sigil is on the keystone. Under the moss."

Dain crouched at the arch's base and brushed the bracken aside with a smuggler's care, the way he handled anything that might be trapped. The stone beneath was scored with a spiral of small marks, salt-eaten but legible: her mother's hand, unmistakable, the same looping cipher Yara had watched her trace in spilled flour, in ash, in the steam on windows. *A salt for the sigil.* The words had been a lullaby before they were an instruction.

"Does it open?" Dain asked.

"It was never closed." Yara touched the threshold and the world *leaned*—a pressure behind her ribs, a tide pulling at something deep in her chest. She snatched her hand back. "It's sleeping. It sleeps because it's starving."

Behind them, the road they'd traveled lay empty in the failing light, the dusk-road already forgetting itself, its edges fraying into ordinary moor. Somewhere back along that vanishing way were hoofbeats, or would be by morning. They had perhaps until dawn.

Yara laid both palms flat against the stone.

The gate remembered her.

It came up through her arms like cold water finding a crack in a wall—not pain, not yet, but recognition, vast and patient and terribly hungry. She saw in a single drowning instant what the gate was: not a door but a *throat*, an old agreement between two countries, and agreements of that age were kept in the only coin that could not be counterfeited. Gift. A keeper's whole gift, poured out, spent to the last bright drop.

She had wondered, all her life, why her mother had died on a night when nothing else had. Why there had been no fever, no fall, no blade—just a woman kneeling at a threshold with her hands on the stone and her daughter screaming in the bracken, and in the morning a body light as a shed skin and a gate sealed so well that every mapmaker in two kingdoms conspired to forget it.

"She paid everything," Yara whispered. "That's the price. That was always the price."

Dain rose slowly. She heard him come to stand behind her, heard the leather of his coat creak, and when he spoke his voice was the flat, careful voice he used for prices. "Then we don't open it. We find another way. There are passes east. There's always another way."

"There isn't." She kept her hands on the stone because if she lifted them she would run, and if she ran she would die in a country that was burning its own magic out, and so would every soul behind her. "The gate doesn't want a key, Dain. It wants a *keeper*. All of her. That's why it sealed. That's why my mother—"

"Yara."

"That's why my family—" Her voice cracked on it. "The gate didn't destroy my family. My mother fed it. Everything after—the hunts, the hiding, the burning—that was us. That was the crown. The gate only ever asked once, and she answered."

The dusk was going violet between the hills. Somewhere a curlew cried, three notes, falling. Yara took her hands from the stone and the hunger receded, sullen, waiting, and she understood that it could wait a hundred years more. Gates like this did not hurry. They simply remembered.

She sat down in the bracken inside the archway, in the threshold-wrong air, and began, with hands that only shook a little, to untie the charms from her hair, her wrists, the hem of her coat. Seven years of harmless magic: knots for luck, knots for calm, knots to make milk keep and fevers break and soldiers look elsewhere. She laid them in a row on the granite like a child laying out her treasures.

"If I pour it all in," she said, "it opens once. Wide, and for anyone standing on the threshold. Then it seals again, and it sleeps, and it forgets us." She looked up at Dain. "Everyone gets through. Everyone who runs. I get through too—on the other side of my own gift. Powerless. Just a woman, walking."

Dain said nothing for a long time. The light died out of the west by degrees, and in the last of it she watched him do the arithmetic he was always doing—routes and risks and weights—and watched it fail him for the first time.

"You'd be nothing," he said at last. "You'd be like me. Like everyone."

"Like everyone," she agreed, and found, to her astonishment, that the words tasted like the first swallow of water after a long thirst.

"That's not freedom, Yara. That's just a smaller cage."

"It's a cage with the door open."

* * *

They heard the horses an hour after full dark, when the moor had gone silver-black and the gate's arch held a faint luminescence, like the memory of moonlight. Not many riders. Three, perhaps four, moving fast, and Yara knew before Dain said it—knew by the rhythm, by the way her chest tightened with a grief older than this night—that one of them rode like Tam.

Tam Vess. Who had shared stolen plums with her on a church roof at nine years old. Who had held her hair back when the gift first woke in her at thirteen and she'd vomited light for an hour. Who had stood in the market square three days ago with a crier's proclamation in his fist and looked at her across a crowd of frightened fishwives, and *hesitated*, and let her go.

Now he came out of the dark with a lantern held high, and he was alone—he had made the last stretch alone, she saw, the others held back at the road's end, out of some courtesy or cowardice—and he dismounted badly, like a man whose body had stopped obeying him miles ago.

"Yara." Her name in his mouth was ruined. He'd been saying it to himself for three days, she could hear that. "I told them the trail went cold at the ford. I told them—God forgive me, I told them so many things."

"Tam." Dain's hand had gone to his knife. Yara touched his arm and he stilled, barely.

Tam stood at the edge of the threshold-light, and she saw that he was not armed as an inquisitor should be. No irons at his belt. No salt, no scripture, no brand-iron wrapped in oilcloth. Just a lantern, and under his coat, a shape she didn't understand until he drew it out and it uncoiled—a child's cloak, small, grey, smelling of woodsmoke and fear.

"Ilset," he said. "My daughter. She's six." His voice went to pieces on the number. He knelt in the bracken, in the dirt, and the lantern threw his shadow huge and broken against the gate. "She was born with it, Yara. The gift. We hid her from the midwives, from the priest, from everyone—Maren dyed her hair, we taught her to be *nothing*, we did everything right, everything you all did—and last week the tithe-collector saw her make a fallen bird get up and fly. He saw it, and he smiled, Yara. He *smiled*, and he wrote it down."

Yara could not speak. The gate hummed against her back like a second heartbeat.

"They muster at dawn. Four hundred soldiers and two inquisitors with real authority, not like me—I'm a joke to them, a soft one, they only keep me because I know the old roads." He looked up at her, and his face was the face from the church roof, from the plum-sticky summer, from every year before the gate took her family and duty took him. "I'm not here to arrest you. I'm here to beg. Open the gate. Put my daughter through it. I'll kneel here and you can take my gift instead of yours, I have one too, I've hidden it twenty years, I'll pour it in, I'll—"

"It doesn't work that way." Yara's voice came out steady, which surprised her. "It wants a keeper, Tam. A full one. Everything. It took my mother whole."

"Then it takes you whole," he whispered, "and Ilset lives."

"And every other hunted soul behind you lives. That's the shape of it. That's what I've already—" She stopped. Dain had gone very still beside her.

Tam bowed his head. "I can't ask that. God, I can't ask that of anyone." And then, because he was Tam, because he had never once in his life been able to stop himself from asking: "But I am. I'm asking."

The lantern hissed. Far off to the south, faint as a lie, came the sound of a horn—once, twice. Not dawn yet. But the dark was thinning at its edges, and somewhere behind that thinning, four hundred men were saddling horses.

Yara looked at the small grey cloak in Tam's hands. She looked at Dain, whose face in the lantern-light was an open wound he was pretending was a wall. She laid her palm flat on the gate one last time, and it remembered her, and it waited.

"The salt," she said. "Bring me the salt. We do this before the light."
