## 4. A Salt for the Sigil

The salt came from her mother's kitchen, carried seven years in a tin that had once held peppermints.

Yara knelt at the threshold of the gate and poured it out in a thin white line, grain by grain, the way she'd been taught before she was old enough to understand what she was being taught. The stones drank the light strangely here; dusk pooled in the gate's arch like water in a cupped hand, and where the salt fell, it glowed—a seam of pale fire against the black of the threshold stone. Behind her, the refugees she'd gathered along the road stood in a ragged half-moon: the fishwife Marta with her two children pressed against her skirts, the tanner's boy with his bandaged hands, a dozen others, silent as people who had learned silence was survival.

The sigil on the arch—her mother's sigil, the erased border, the door that had eaten her family—waited.

*It demands a keeper's full gift,* the gate had shown her, or she had understood it somehow, the way you understand a thing in dreams. *All of it. Every hoarded year.*

She had spent seven years pouring her power into charms no bigger than a thumbnail. Luck-knots. Warding-threads. A charm against fever, a charm against drowning, a hundred small mercies sold for coppers to frightened people in a city that was now burning its own magic out of itself with fire and rope. But she had never given everything. She had always kept a seed back, the way any sensible woman keeps a coin sewn into her hem. Seven years of seeds. Seven years of the part of herself she refused to spend.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake.

"Yara." Dain's voice, low, behind her left shoulder. He had not touched her since they'd made camp three nights ago—not since she'd told him what the gate would cost—but he was there, the way he had been there through all of it, stubborn as weather. "There's another way."

"There isn't."

"There's always another way. I've smuggled past worse doors than this one."

"Not this one." She looked up at him—at the scar splitting his eyebrow, at the eyes that had once been warm and were now only careful—and something in her chest cracked open like river ice in spring. "Dain. My mother stood where I'm standing. My father held her hand while she did it. They thought they were saving me." Her voice did not waver, though she wanted it to. "I am doing what they did. That's not tragedy. That's inheritance."

Behind them, down the slope of the valley, torches. The soldiers had found the road-that-wasn't-there at last—or perhaps her old friend Tam had finally stopped pretending he couldn't find it. She could hear horses. She could hear, faintly, a voice calling orders in the clipped cadence of the Inquisition.

Marta grabbed Yara's sleeve. "They'll kill us all."

"No," Yara said. "They won't. Not tonight."

She stood. She brushed the salt from her knees. And she began to speak her inheritance aloud.

* * *

Every keeper learns the words in childhood, and every keeper is made to forget them, because words of opening are dangerous things to carry in a country that searches children's mouths for spells. But the body remembers what the mind buries. Yara opened her mouth and her mother's voice came out of it, in the old tongue of a border no map remembered:

*I am the door's daughter. I am the salt and the seal and the keeping.*

The sigil flared. Light ran along the arch like flame along a fuse, and the stones beneath her feet hummed so deep she felt it in her teeth, in her ribs, in the roots of her hair.

*I was taught by the one who paid. I pay as she paid. I open as she opened.*

"Yara—" Tam's voice now, breaking across the hillside, closer than the soldiers should have been. He came running alone down the dark slope, torchless, his inquisitor's coat unbuttoned and flying open, and in his arms he carried a sleeping girl no older than six. His hidden daughter. The reason he'd begged passage instead of arrest. "Wait—please—we're here, let us through, don't close it—"

"I'm not closing it." She looked at him—at the man who had hesitated at the market, who had let her go twice when duty said burn—and gave him the truth like a gift. "No one closes it ever again. Come. Bring her. Bring everyone."

The soldiers crested the ridge then, a spill of torchlight and steel, and someone shouted a command, and someone else nocked an arrow—

And Yara poured out seven years.

* * *

It did not hurt the way she'd feared. It hurt the way birth hurts, or grief—the way anything does that empties you completely to make room for something new.

Power left her in a flood. Every luck-charm she'd ever hoarded, every warding-thread, every small hoarded mercy: it all rose out of her like heat off summer stone, silver-bright, streaming toward the arch in ribbons. She felt it go the way you feel blood leave a wound—first warmth, then cold, then a strange floating clarity. The years in the market square. The years of hiding. The fishwife's fear. The burned stock, the packed bags, the road that existed only at dusk. All of it, all of it, all of it, poured into the sigil her mother had carved and sealed with her own life.

The gate opened.

Not with thunder. With light. The darkness inside the arch thinned like mist at sunrise, and beyond it lay—not the other side of the hill, but somewhere else: green country under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, a river shining far below, a road going down into a world where no crown hunted gifts because gifts were simply what some people had, like brown eyes or a singing voice. Ordinary. Free.

"Go," Yara said, and her voice came out thin as paper, because most of her had gone through the door already. "Go now. All of you."

They went. Marta first, dragging her children, sobbing and laughing in the same breath. Then the tanner's boy. Then the others, stumbling and weeping and looking back over their shoulders at the soldiers who could not follow—who reined up at the salt line as if it were a cliff edge, because it was, because the gate would take anyone hunted but admit no hunter. Tam came last among them, his daughter over his shoulder, and he turned at the threshold and looked at Yara with an expression she would carry wherever she went, even without her gift: gratitude too large for words, worn openly on a face that had spent years learning to hide everything.

"You were better than your office," she told him.

"So were you," he said, and stepped through, and was gone.

The gate stayed open. It would stay open until dawn, she knew—she knew it the way she had known the price, the way keepers know things, with the knowledge that had been her gift and was now leaving her forever. One night. One door. Everyone behind her.

Which meant she was the last.

Dain took her hand.

His palm was rough, calloused from rope and rail, and it shook slightly, and he didn't pretend it didn't. "You'll be ordinary now," he said. "No charms. No wards. No luck to sell."

"I know."

"Frightened?"

"Terrified," she said, and laughed, and the laugh surprised them both—it rang out across the valley, bright and unconcealed, the first sound she'd made in seven years that hadn't been carefully measured for safety. "Dain. I've been terrified since I was fourteen years old. Do you know what I'll be tomorrow? Just tired. Just hungry. Just a woman on a road with a man she loves, in a country that doesn't care what she is." She squeezed his hand. "That's not nothing. That's everything they tried to burn out of the world."

Torches bobbed down the slope. Somewhere an officer was screaming about witchcraft and treason and doors that shouldn't exist. None of it mattered. None of it could cross the salt.

Yara Yarrow looked back once—at the burning country, at the road that wasn't there, at the whole shape of the life she had survived rather than lived—and then she did not look again.

Hand in Dain's hand, powerless, penniless, free, she stepped through the gate as the first grey light of morning touched the arch, and the sigil flared once, gently, like a lamp being shuttered for the night—

—and went dark, and stayed dark, and the door closed behind them forever.

Ahead, the river shone. Ahead, someone was shouting welcome in a language she didn't know yet. Ahead was the rest of her life: short, mortal, ungifted, hers.

She walked toward it.
