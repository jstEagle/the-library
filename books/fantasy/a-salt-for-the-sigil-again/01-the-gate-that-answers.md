## 1. The Gate That Answers

The fever came down the mountain ahead of the man who carried it.

Gideon Villalobos felt it first as a wrongness in the air, the way a sailor feels a storm in the set of the rigging—something in the pressure of the morning shifted, something leaning where nothing should lean. They had been mending the low wall at the edge of the watchpost yard, hands busy, mind empty, the way thirty years of habit had taught them to keep both. The stones were cold. The wind off Thornfell's shoulder smelled of pine and old snow and, faintly, of iron. Always iron, near the gate. The mountain bled it slow through its stone like a wound that had never quite closed.

Then the wrongness. Then, an hour later, the coughing.

They heard it before they saw him—a wet, rattling sound working its way up the shepherd's path, the track that wound from the high pastures down through the black pines to the saddle of rock where the gate stood. Gideon set down their trowel and their mortar and stood very still, listening the way they had learned to listen: not with the ears alone but with the skin, with the small hairs on the forearms, with the teeth. Thirty years a keeper. You learned that the gate had moods. You learned that some sounds were only sounds, and some sounds were bait.

The coughing resolved into a shape. A man, broad through the shoulders, narrow through the rest, wrapped in a shepherd's cloak gone grey with wool-grease and weather. He leaned on a crook worn smooth by two generations of hands. His face was the color of tallow. Sweat stood on his brow in beads that did not fall, frozen there by the cold, and each breath he drew sounded like a bucket hauled up a deep well on a fraying rope.

Gideon knew him. Tam Voss. Kept sheep on the high meadows east of the Thornfell spine, came down twice a year to sell wool and buy salt, had never once in thirty years looked at the gate with anything but the bored indifference of a man passing a wall. A good neighbor, in the distant way mountain folk were neighbors. A man who owed Gideon nothing and asked nothing, which was the only kind of relationship Gideon had let themselves cultivate since they had taken up the keeper's iron.

He was walking toward the gate.

"Tam," Gideon called, and their voice came out flat and hard, the keeper's voice, the one they used for weather and wolves and nothing softer. "Where are you going?"

Tam Voss stopped. Turned. His eyes were wrong—not fever-bright, not glassy with delirium, but *focused*, fixed on the gate behind Gideon's shoulder with an attention so total it seemed to drain the rest of his face of all expression. Like a man looking at a door he had only just realized was a door.

"It called me," he said. His voice was ruined, gravel in a sack. "In the night. It said—"

"It didn't say anything." Gideon crossed the yard in six strides and put themselves bodily between the shepherd and the path. Up close the fever stench rolled off him, sweetish and rotten, the smell of a body burning itself as fuel. "There's nothing up there but stone. You know that. Everyone knows that."

"It said my name." Tam's mouth worked. "It said, *Tam Voss, Tam Voss, come and be mended.*" A shudder went through him that was half chill and half something else, something that looked from the outside like longing. "Thirty years I've walked past it. It never said a word. And last night, in the fever, it said my name like my mother used to."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere above, along the saddle, the gate stood in its frame of living rock—no door, no arch, just a wall of grey stone some forty feet high and thirty wide, dressed with seven sigils cut deep and old, each one filled with lead and salt and the ashes of things Gideon preferred not to enumerate, even in the privacy of their own skull. The seal. Their life's work. Their life's prison, depending on the hour and the light.

"It said your name," Gideon repeated, "because fevers say names. I've had fevers. They called me by name, they called me by every name I've ever been ashamed of, and in the morning they were gone and so were the voices." They took Tam's arm—lean, trembling, furnace-hot through the wool—and turned him gently but with the immovability of a gatepost. "You're coming down to the watchpost. You'll have broth and willow bark and a warm stone at your back, and in three days you'll complain about my cooking, and that will be the whole of it."

Tam let himself be turned. But at the first step his head came around again, over his shoulder, toward the gate, and the look on his face—the naked, starving look of it—made Gideon's stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"It's warm," Tam whispered. "Can't you feel it? It's *warm*."

* * *

The fever broke at dawn.

Gideon sat up in the chair beside the hearth where they had not, by their own accounting, slept, and watched it happen. Tam Voss lay on the rope bed they kept for travelers and injured shepherds and their own worst nights, and at the moment the sun's first light came through the eastern window and lay across the floorboards like a laid-out blade, the sweating stopped. Not eased. *Stopped*, all at once, the way a clock stops, the way a man stops when a hand closes on the back of his neck.

The color came back into his face in the space of ten breaths. The rattle left his lungs. He drew one long, deep, easy breath, the breath of a man waking from a good sleep in a good year, and opened his eyes, and they were clear and brown and entirely his own.

"Keeper," he said, and his voice was whole. "Keeper, I—" He sat up, flexing his hands, staring at them as though they belonged to a stranger, which in a sense they did, being ten years younger than they had been the night before. "I feel *fine*. Better than fine. I feel—" He laughed, a short disbelieving bark. "I feel like I did when I was twenty."

"Fevers break," Gideon said. Their voice was level. Their hands, folded in their lap, were not. "It's what fevers do."

"Not like this." Tam swung his legs off the bed and stood, and stood *well*, and turned a slow circle in the middle of the room like a man rediscovering his own body. "Thirty years I've had the ache in my left knee. Gone. The tooth I lost to a bad fall, the gap where it was—it's *filled*, keeper, look—" He hooked a finger in his own mouth to show it, grinning, a man made suddenly, impossibly rich. "It's filled. What kind of fever grows a tooth?"

The kind that isn't a fever, Gideon thought. The kind that's a hook, and the hook has bait, and the bait is everything you've ever lost.

"Sit down," they said, "and eat your broth, and don't thank me, because I didn't do it."

"But who—"

"Nobody. Eat."

He ate. That was the worst part, Gideon thought later. He ate like a man at a wedding, with appetite and gratitude and no suspicion at all, and he thanked them anyway, and he talked about his sheep and his bad knee that wasn't bad anymore and his sister down in the valley who would weep to see him hale. And Gideon sat across from him and smiled the keeper's smile and felt, beneath the smile, the cold spreading through their chest like water finding the cracks in a wall.

Because they had watched his shadow.

It lay on the floorboards by the hearth, thrown long and thin by the firelight, and it had not moved with him. When he stood, it stayed. When he turned his circle, it stayed. It lay there in the shape of a man lying down, arms at its sides, patient as a seed, and Gideon had watched it with the fire popping and their heart going like a fist on a door, and Tam Voss had stepped over it twice without looking down, because no one looks down, no one ever looks down at the thing that has followed them faithfully their whole life and therefore cannot possibly have changed.

It was still there now, on the floor by the bed, in the morning light from the east window. It should have stretched and shortened with the sun. It did neither. It lay in the shape of a man lying down, and it was *waiting*.

* * *

Tam left at midmorning, against every protest Gideon could construct, walking tall and easy down the valley path toward his sister's farm, whistling—a man reborn, a miracle on legs, a debt walking away on two strong legs with no idea what had been lent to him or at what interest.

Gideon watched him go. Watched, at the edge of the trees where the light came slantwise through the pines, the moment his shadow rose up off the ground.

It did not rise the way a thing rises. It *peeled*, the way bark peels, the way a bandage peels from a half-healed wound, starting at the edges and lifting inward, and then it stood up—a flat black man-shape on the vertical air, thin as a knife blade seen edge-on—and it turned, once, toward the watchpost. Toward Gideon. Gideon would have sworn an oath on the keeper's iron that it looked at them, though it had no eyes, though looking was not a thing a shadow could do.

Then it went. Not walking. *Flowing*, up through the trees, along the ground and over it, north and up, along the shepherd's path, toward the saddle, toward the gate. Gideon watched it until the pines took it, and then they stood in their yard with the mortar drying on the wall they'd never finish and the wind coming down off Thornfell carrying its smell of pine and snow and iron, and they made themselves say it aloud, because some things must be said aloud or they will be denied forever:

"It's awake."

The words fell into the yard and lay there. Nothing answered. That, at least, was the same as it had been for thirty years.

* * *

They climbed to the gate that afternoon, and they did it properly, because thirty years of habit dies hard and habit was all that stood between them and screaming. They took the keeper's iron—the bell, small as a fist, black with age, that hummed faintly against their palm the whole way up like a held note. They took salt. They took the long iron probe with the silver inlay. They told no one, because there was no one to tell, which had always been the arrangement and had never, until today, felt like a wound.

The saddle of rock was as it always was: wind-scoured, bare, silent. The gate stood in its frame of living stone, grey and vast and dumb, seven sigils ranked in two rows down its face, each the size of a shield, each cut by hands that had been dust a thousand years before the first king of the first kingdom drew his first breath.

Gideon walked the line of them the way they walked it every season, solstice and equinox, and the way they walked it in the bad years more often than that. Sigil the first: cold. Stone-cold, lead-cold, dead as it had been for thirty years and a thousand years before that. They laid their bare palm against it out of ritual, and it gave back nothing, and they breathed.

Sigil the second: cold. The third: cold. The fourth, the fifth, the sixth—cold, cold, cold, each touch a small prayer, each silence an answer, and by the sixth Gideon had almost persuaded themselves. Fevers break. Shadows lie—or rather, shadows tell the truth, and the truth was only that miracles were like lightning, they struck where they would and no one could say why, and the old gods were *dead*, they had to be dead, because if they were not dead then thirty years of Gideon Villalobos's life had been a man standing on a beach telling the sea to stay back, and—

The seventh sigil was warm.

Not sun-warm. The stone around it was cold enough to bite; the sigil itself was warm as a cheek, warm as a hand that has just been holding another hand. Warm the way a thing is warm when something on the other side of it has been *breathing against it*.

Gideon stood before it for a long time. The wind moved. Far below, a hawk turned in the blue over the valley, riding the thermals above the shepherd's path where a man with a filled tooth and a young man's knees was walking down to tell his sister about his miracle.

The sigil was the seventh. The last. The one the old liturgies called the *Hunger's Mouth*, the one Maerin—the priestess, before she was defrocked, before Gideon had stopped letting themselves think of her—had told them, in another life, over cheap wine in another valley: *the seventh is the one that listens first and opens last. Watch the seventh. The rest are teeth. The seventh is the tongue.*

One sigil warm. One prayer answered. One shadow peeled from its owner and flowed home like a tithe.

Gideon knelt, and took out the salt, and began, with steady hands, to renew the packing in the sigil's grooves—lead and salt and ash, the old recipe, the only recipe. The iron bell in their pocket hummed against their hip like a wasp in a jar. And as they worked, as the salt ran white into the old cuts, they heard it—faint, beneath the wind, beneath the stone, or perhaps not beneath any of it but inside their own skull where no seal had ever been cut:

a voice, patient and vast and terribly, terribly gentle, saying their name.

Not *Tam Voss, Tam Voss, come and be mended.*

*Gideon Villalobos. Gideon Villalobos. I remember what you've lost.*

They did not answer. They finished the packing. They climbed down the mountain in the last light with the bell humming against them, and in the yard of the watchpost they took down the old chest, and opened it, and began, for the first time in thirty years, to sharpen the keeper's other tools.

By morning they would need a horse, and a road, and the one person in the valley who still knew the old liturgy—and who hated them for what Gideon had let happen to her.

The mountain, for now, said nothing.

But it was warm.
