## 2. Smuggler's Tithe

The mountain roads of Thornfell had three seasons: snow, thaw, and the brief green week when the whole range seemed to exhale. Gideon had walked them in all three for thirty years, and they had learned the roads the way a shepherd learns a flock—not by map, but by the small wrongnesses that meant something had strayed.

So when the road below the Widow's Stair went quiet on a morning in late thaw—no marmots whistling, no crows arguing over the scree—Gideon stopped walking and listened.

Silence on a mountain was never empty. It was held, like breath.

They left the trail and climbed the switchbacks above it, boots finding the old handholds worn into the rock by generations of keepers who had all learned the same lesson: watch from above, because whatever moved on these roads moved best where it could not be seen. From the shoulder of the Stair, Gideon looked down at the pass—and there it was.

A mule train. Six animals, seven people, moving at the pace of men trying not to look hurried. They were dressed as wool merchants, which was the oldest costume in the trade, and their mules were loaded wrong. Wool bales sat high and rode light. Whatever lay under them was heavy enough to make the animals plant their hooves like dancers.

Gideon counted the guards' spacing, marked the leader's stride, and felt something cold settle behind their ribs before they ever saw her face.

Because Gideon knew that stride. Seven years ago, on a night of freezing rain on the far side of the range, a woman with that stride had carried Gideon's half-dead body down out of the White Teeth on her back, fed them broth she could ill afford, asked nothing, and vanished before dawn. Ryn Sable. The name had come to Gideon later, in fragments—a smuggler who ran the high roads no crown patrol could hold, a woman who paid her debts even when it ruined her, a woman who, according to every story, did not exist anymore.

Gideon watched her walk beneath them, alive and breathing and leading cargo that made the mules afraid, and thought: *of course it's you.*

Then the cargo whispered.

It should have been wind. It was thaw season; the gullies ran loud with meltwater; the Widow's Stair channeled gusts like a throat. But this sound came from under the wool bales, low and wet and rhythmic, and when Gideon opened the trained ear of thirty years' keeping, they heard what the whisper actually was.

Prayers. Dozens of prayers, layered over each other, spoken too fast and too many at once—the sound of a hundred voices crammed into a space the size of a bread oven. A child asking for rain. An old man asking to see his son again. A woman asking, over and over, *please, please, please*, in the voice of someone whose prayer has already been answered and who cannot stop being grateful.

Gideon's hand went to the seal-iron at their belt—the short rod stamped with the gate's sigil, cold as river stone, the only tool their office had ever been given—and did not draw it. Not yet. Not here, above an open slope where a fight would end with mules bolting and crates cracking open on the rocks, spilling whatever was inside across half a mountainside.

*Follow,* said thirty years of instinct. *Find where it sleeps.*

* * *

She led them a merry dance, or would have, if she'd known they were there. Gideon kept to the high line all day while the mule train wound down through the pine shadow toward the valley road, and twice Ryn stopped, turned a slow circle, frowning at nothing—and both times Gideon was already flat against stone, still as lichen. She was good. Better than good. Once she threw a pebble down a side gully to listen to the echo, and Gideon understood she wasn't checking for pursuers behind her.

She was checking for something listening ahead.

By dusk the train reached a drover's inn at the meeting of two streams, the kind of place that existed to water livestock and ask no questions, and Gideon settled into the hayloft of its stable through the long blue evening, watching through a gap in the boards.

Ryn didn't drink. That was the first wrong thing. Smugglers drank—it loosened toll-takers, greased informants, made the road bearable. But Ryn Sable sat in the corner with one cup of small beer going warm and her eyes moving, always moving, to the stable door. When her people finally rolled into their blankets, she stayed up. She went out to the mules alone with a lantern, and Gideon slipped down from the loft and around the back wall in the dark between lantern-spill and moonrise, and stood close enough to hear her talk to the cargo.

"Hush." Her voice was rough with exhaustion. "I know. I know you're hungry. We're nearly there."

A pause. Then, quieter, almost tender, in the way of a person who hates themselves a little for the tenderness:

"No. You can't have mine. I told you. I *told* you."

Gideon stepped out of the dark into the edge of the lantern light. "Ryn Sable."

She moved faster than anyone Gideon had ever seen move. The lantern was down, the knife was up, and she was three feet away with the blade leveled at Gideon's throat before the echo of their own voice had finished bouncing off the rafters. Her eyes were wide and wild in the dark, and then they narrowed, and then—they were the only part of her that softened.

"You," she said slowly. "You're supposed to be dead. I carried you myself. I checked your breathing twice a day for four days."

"I got better."

"Nobody gets better from what you had." She didn't lower the knife. "Half your blood was frozen slush. I gave you the blanket off my own back, and my partner swore you'd be stiff as a plank by Greywater Ford. What are you?"

"Alive. Which is more than I expected to say to you." Gideon nodded past her, at the mules. At the wool bales, riding too low. "And you're hauling something you shouldn't be."

Something closed over her face like a shutter. "You're the keeper now. Aren't you. The Thornfell gate." She spat to the side, theatrical contempt that fooled neither of them. "Thirty years I've run these roads and nobody's touched me, and the one time I carry a load I'd give my teeth to drop, the mountain itself sends a keeper down my neck."

"Then drop it."

"You don't know what you're asking." The knife wavered—not from fear, Gideon realized, but from exhaustion so deep it had gone structural. "You don't know what they're paying me. Because it isn't coin, keeper. And before you arrest me or cut me down or whatever it is you do, you're going to stand there and listen, because I saved your life once and I am calling in the hearing of it. That's how it works. You know how it works."

Gideon knew. In the old law of the high roads, older than the crown and older than the guilds, a life debt was the one currency that never devalued. "I'm listening."

"Put the lantern light on the second mule," Ryn said. "Just the corner of the bale. And don't touch anything."

* * *

Under the canvas, under the false floor of the pannier, packed in straw that had been blessed by somebody—Gideon could smell the chrism even after years of telling themselves they'd forgotten church smells—lay clay jars. Nine of them. Each sealed with wax and a thumbprint pressed into the wax while soft, and each jar humming faintly against Gideon's palm when they brought the light near, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"They're prayers," Ryn said quietly. "That's what I was told when I took the job, and I laughed, and then I opened one, because I'm an idiot, and I heard—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a woman in the third jar. She prayed for her daughter's fever to break, and it broke, and something came to collect, and what it collected is in the jar. Not her body. Her *praying*. The part of her that asks. It's still in there asking for things. All night. Every night."

Gideon stood very still. Thirty years at a sealed gate taught a person many kinds of dread, but this was a new one: the dread of understanding something too quickly. "Who hired you?"

"Middleman. Never seen him twice. Pays in—" Ryn laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "You'll like this. He pays in answered prayers. Small ones. My roof doesn't leak anymore—I've been patching that leak eleven years, and one morning it just... wasn't leaking. My bad knee. My partner's cough, gone overnight. And every time, I wake up feeling lighter. Like something was taken *out*. And every time, the jars get heavier."

"Where are you taking them?"

"The old shrine at Coldbrook. The flooded one." She watched Gideon's face as she said it, and whatever she saw there made her go pale. "You know it."

Everyone who kept a gate knew Coldbrook. It had been a chapel of the old gods before the binding, drowned when the valley was dammed, and drowned things did not stay drowned when something on the other side of a seal grew hungry enough to reach for them.

"Ryn." Gideon set the lantern down, slowly, and turned to face her fully. "Whatever you think you're carrying, it isn't cargo. It's food. You're driving a herd of souls to a feeding trough, and the thing that eats is the same thing that's paying you. It answers your little prayers because it wants you comfortable. Comfortable couriers don't ask questions."

"I know." Her voice cracked on it. "You think I haven't worked that out? You think I sleep?" She sheathed the knife at last and dragged a hand down her face, and in the lantern light Gideon saw her properly for the first time in seven years—thinner, grayer, with a tremor in her left hand that hadn't been there before. "I tried to quit. Three loads ago. I told the middleman I was done, and that night my partner—who has never been sick a day in his life, keeper, not once—woke up choking. Something in his chest. The physician couldn't find anything wrong. And the middleman sent a boy to my door with a note that said, *the cough can come back worse.* So I loaded the jars. So here I am."

The silence of the stable pressed in. Somewhere in the rafters, a pigeon shifted. And underneath everything, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the jars hummed.

"Give me the job," Gideon said.

Ryn stared. "What?"

"The delivery. Give it to me. Tell your middleman it's done, tell him whatever keeps his threats quiet, and walk away."

"And he finds another courier. And another. And they don't know what's in the jars, and they don't have a keeper standing next to them, and they get eaten slower and stupider than me." She shook her head. "No. No, we do this my way. We deliver it—to Coldbrook, like the note says. And then you do whatever it is keepers do, and I take my payment, and I burn whatever's left of the arrangement to the ground. I want to look it in the face first, keeper. I want to see what's been eating my knee and my roof and my nights. I've earned that much."

"That's the stupidest plan I've ever heard."

"It's the only one I've got." She stuck out her hand, callused and steady despite the tremor, and waited. "Life debt, remember? You owe me a hearing, not obedience. But I'm asking anyway, keeper. Come with me. Because if what's at the bottom of Coldbrook is what I think it is, I'd rather have one person beside me who knows its name than ten who don't."

Gideon looked at the hand. Looked at the jars, humming their hundred tiny hungers. Thought about thirty years of solitary rounds, of talking to sheep because sheep were what talked back, of the empty chair across from every fire.

They shook it.

"One condition," Gideon said. "If it goes wrong—if it goes *wrong* wrong—you run. You don't argue, you don't come back for me. You run down the valley and you find the nearest warden post and you tell them nothing, absolutely nothing, because if the crown learns what's moving on these roads, they won't help. They'll take notes."

"Why wouldn't they help?"

"Because," Gideon said, and heard their own voice go flat and old, "I inspected the seal last month, and one of the sigils was warm. Something has slipped through, Ryn. Something small and patient, and it's buying friends with miracles. And I need to know whether it's working alone."

Ryn blew out the lantern. In the sudden dark, the jars hummed on, sweet and awful, like a choir learning a new song.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Coldbrook's two days down the valley. And keeper—when we're done, when this is done, you and me are square. Understood? After this I owe you nothing, and you owe me nothing, and we never have to see each other again."

"Understood," Gideon lied, and she probably lied back.

Neither of them slept. And near midnight, as Gideon stood the last watch by the stable door, they saw it: far down the black valley road, a string of torches climbing the pass from the lowlands, moving in formation, too disciplined for smugglers and too many for tax collectors.

Royal wardens. A full company, marching through the night toward the villages of the valley—toward the places where, all winter, prayers had been getting answered.

Gideon woke Ryn with a hand over her mouth, pointed down at the torches, and watched the color leave her face.

"They're not coming for us," Ryn breathed, when she could speak. "Are they?"

Gideon didn't answer. Down in the dark, the wardens' torches bent around the shoulder of the mountain, steady and unhurried, like fingers closing.
