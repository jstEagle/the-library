## 3. What the Wardens Want

The smoke came first.

Gideon had learned to read smoke the way other men read weather. Woodsmoke rose straight and lazy in the morning calm; hearthsmoke hung low and greasy over a village like a blanket pulled up to the chin. This was neither. This was black at its heart and white at its edges, thick enough to stand off the ridgeline like a pillar, and it did not drift with the wind so much as *climb*, as if something beneath it were hungry for the sky.

Gideon stood on the shoulder of Thornfell with their spyglass raised and counted the banners moving through the vale below. Six riders in royal blue. Then ten more. Then the wagons—cages on wheels, iron-barred, some of them already occupied by shapes that sat too still to be livestock and too upright to be corpses.

Wardens. A full tithe-company of them, which meant someone in the capital had signed papers, which meant this was not a raid but a *judgment*.

Below them, half-hidden in the fold of the valley, lay Hollowmere. Forty souls, give or take. A village that kept no shrine Gideon had ever found, sang no hymns, burned no incense. The people there were shepherds and turnip-farmers who believed in rain when it rained and frost when it frosted, and thanked nobody in particular for either. In thirty years of keeping the gate, Gideon had never once heard a prayer rise from Hollowmere that carried weight behind it. It was, as far as Gideon could tell, the most godless place in the whole of the Thornfell marches.

And the wardens had come for it with fire.

Gideon lowered the spyglass slowly, and the cold that moved through them had nothing to do with the altitude.

They should have gone back to the watchpost. That was the discipline of thirty years: see, mark, record, seal, and do not interfere. The keeper's first law, written in the old keeper's own hand on the inside of Gideon's skull: *You are not a soldier. You are a lock. A lock does not chase thieves.*

But the lock had felt one of its sigils go warm nine days ago, and the smoke below smelled wrong, and Gideon's boots were already carrying them down the scree before any law could catch up.

* * *

Hollowmere burned from the well outward.

That was the detail Gideon would carry afterward, the one that would not wash out: they had not fired the houses first, or the barns, or even the church-shaped building that wasn't a church but a granary with a carved lintel. They had started at the well—the center, the source, the place where a community's water lived—and worked outward in a spiral, so that the village died the way a body dies, from the heart to the fingers.

By the time Gideon reached the outskirts, moving low through a fallow barley field, the wardens had formed their herding lines. Gideon knew the drill from old reports, old decades: drive the villagers into the square, separate those marked from those merely present, burn the marked ones' homes while the crowd watched. Terror was the tool. Terror taught the survivors to fear the very idea of prayer.

But these were not prayerful people. So what, Gideon wondered, crawling through the stubble, were they marking?

The answer came in pieces, dragged out of the noise of burning.

A warden sergeant stood in the square with a ledger open on a lectern that had been carried up from somewhere, reading names in a flat voice. Beside him, an officer in a steel half-mask held a rod of pale metal—not iron, not silver, something with a faint oily sheen—and touched it to each villager's palm as they passed.

"Brannoc Hale." The rod touched a shepherd's hand. It stayed dark. The man passed through.

"Elswyth Hale. His wife." The rod touched. Dark. Through.

"Tam Osgar."

Gideon knew Tam Osgar. Everyone within twenty miles of Thornfell knew Tam Osgar, because Tam had been dying of a lung-rot all winter, coughing blood into his wool, and everyone had assumed he'd be under the turned earth by spring planting.

Tam stepped forward. The rod flared white.

The officer didn't shout or gloat. He simply said, "Marked," in the tone of a clerk noting a delivery received, and two wardens took Tam by the arms and moved him toward the cages.

His wife screamed. Of course she screamed. And the sergeant looked up from his ledger with genuine, bureaucratic patience and said, "Mistress Osgar, your husband was healed of a wasting sickness without physician, priest, or payment. You will be asked how. Answer honestly and the judgment may be lenient."

"I don't know," she sobbed. "He got better. He just got *better*. I thought—I thanked the morning, I thanked whatever—"

"You thanked," the officer said softly, "*something*."

"I didn't know what else to do! He was alive and I was glad!"

"Gratitude requires an object," the officer said, and Gideon felt the words land in their chest like a hook set deep. "Someone heard you, mistress. Someone answered. We are only here to learn who."

They put her in the cage next to her husband anyway.

Gideon lay in the barley stubble with their cheek against the cold ground and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade being drawn slowly, exactly what they were looking at. Not a suppression. Suppression would have been simpler, crueler in the ordinary way—burn the heretics, salt the field, done. But the wardens weren't asking the villagers to renounce anything. They were asking them to *identify* the thing that had answered. They wanted the name. They wanted the thread of connection, the line of gratitude running from a grateful heart to whatever waited on the other side of it.

Every answered prayer is a door opened a crack, Maerin had written once, back when Gideon had still been allowed to read her letters, back before the crown defrocked her for asking precisely the wrong question about the liturgy. *And every door remembers the hand that knocked.*

The crown wasn't hunting the doors shut.

It was tracing them back to their hinges.

* * *

The burning of the marked took until midafternoon, and Gideon watched all of it.

Not because they wanted to. Because a keeper who looks away stops being a keeper and starts being a coward, and cowardice, in Gideon's long experience of watching other people fail, was the first crack through which old gods poured.

They burned Tam Osgar in his own house. That was the method: the marked were not executed in the square but returned to their homes, and their homes were fired around them, and the reasoning—stated aloud by the masked officer to his men, in the carrying voice of a man who wanted the village to hear—was that "the residue of grace clings to walls and rafters, and fire is the crown's cheapest purification."

There was no residue. There was no grace. There was a man who'd been healed by something hungry, and the healing had almost certainly already taken its price—Gideon had seen the shadowless fever-break at the gate, had spent nine days documenting what walked away from a living man—and now the crown was going to burn the corpse and call it sanitation.

Three households. Seven marked souls. Gideon counted the flames, counted the screams, counted the wardens who stood at parade rest and did not flinch, and made a private ledger of their own: six officers, forty-one rankers, one masked commander whose sigil-ring caught the light with a gleam that Gideon's bones recognized before their mind did.

The ring bore a sigil. An old one. One of the nine that ran the length of the Thornfell gate, worn smooth by centuries of keeping.

Gideon had last seen it glowing warm.

* * *

They nearly died getting close to the cage-wagons, and would have called it worth it twice over.

Nightfall gave cover, and fire gave noise, and the wardens posted only three sentries on the prisoner line because the prisoners were peasants and peasants, in the crown's arithmetic, did not have friends willing to die for them. The crown was wrong about many things. Gideon intended to make it wrong about this one, though not tonight—not with forty-one blades and a masked commander whose presence made the air feel thin.

What Gideon wanted was small and enormous: a look inside the cages. Confirmation of what happened to the taken.

They found it through a knothole in the third wagon's wall, pressed eye to wood while a sentry's lantern swung twenty feet away.

Tam Osgar sat in the corner, alive, unburned—the marked who'd survived their house-fires had been collected rather than killed, Gideon realized; the executions were theater, the cages were the point—and he was praying.

Gideon saw his lips move. Saw his hands lift, trembling, into the posture of supplication that every human being seemed to know by instinct, the posture that predated every temple. And around Tam's wrists, faint as breath on glass, a sigil shimmered into visibility—a brand of pale light, the same oily sheen as the officer's testing rod—and where it glowed, Tam's skin beneath it had gone *smooth*. Young. Unlined. The hands of a man thirty years dead of age, if not yet of anything else.

And Tam was smiling as he prayed. Smiling with the serene, empty contentment of a man being fed through a straw.

*Payment in advance,* Gideon thought, and the cold went all the way down to the floor of them. *Or payment in kind. They heal you, you pray, the prayer feeds it, and the crown collects you like a tithe-animal because a hollowed worshipper is worth more than a corpse.*

The crown was not suppressing the old gods.

The crown was *farming* them.

Thirty years Gideon had stood at the gate believing the lie they'd helped build—that the kingdom above feared the old powers, policed them, kept the lowlands clean of them out of piety and prudence. Thirty years of feeling like a lonely lock on a forgotten door while the world got on with its sensible, godless business. And all the while, somewhere above, someone had been running the other direction along the same current: not sealing the flow of worship but channeling it, harvesting it, breeding miracles in the valleys and collecting the hollowed faithful like a crop that planted itself.

The gate hadn't been forgotten.

It had been left alone because it was the last piece of the mechanism the crown couldn't yet reach—and everything else, every village miracle, every answered prayer, every healed shepherd, was the crown teaching its subjects to knock on doors the keepers had bled to shut.

Gideon peeled back from the wagon, withdrew through the dark past the sentries, and did not breathe properly again until the ridge was under their feet.

Then they stood in the wind and let themselves shake, because there was no one to see it, and shaking was cheaper than breaking.

* * *

The report wrote itself in Gideon's head all the way home, in the terse cipher of the keeper's trade, and by the time the watchpost fire was rebuilt and the door barred, Gideon had already decided not to send it.

That decision frightened them more than the burning had.

For thirty years, the chain of command had been simple: observe, record, seal the sigil-report in lead, and pass it down-mountain to the archive at Greyford, where someone unseen filed it and nothing ever came back. Gideon had questioned it perhaps twice in three decades and always swallowed the question, because the previous keeper had warned that questions traveled down-mountain and answers came back wearing crowns.

But now Gideon sat with the lead-sealed report tube in their lap, firelight on the wax, and understood that sending it meant feeding it into the mouth of the thing they'd just watched eat a village. Every report Gideon had ever filed had gone somewhere. Somewhere had read about the gate's condition, the sigils' state, the health of the seal—and had done nothing, year after year, except leave the gate exactly as it was: unreachable, unwatched, *unharvested*.

The last unclaimed well in the kingdom.

And now the sigil had gone warm. Now prayers were being answered at the mountain's foot. Now the thing behind the seal had found some crack—or been handed one—and the machinery above had noticed, and sent rods and rings bearing the gate's own marks to test the water.

If Gideon sent the report, they would be reporting a leak to the people drilling for it.

Gideon fed the report to the fire instead, page by page, and watched thirty years of obedience curl and blacken, and felt less like a traitor than they had expected. More like a man who has finally read the contract he signed.

Outside, the wind came down off Thornfell carrying, very faintly, a smell that had no business on clean stone: incense. Old incense. The heavy amber-and-ash kind that Maerin's order had used, back before the order was broken.

Gideon went to the window.

Far below, in the black fold of the valley where no village stood, a light moved. Small, steady, unhurried. A lamp on the old pilgrim road, coming up the mountain through the dark, taking the switchbacks with the confidence of someone who had walked them before.

And behind it—Gideon narrowed their eyes, told themselves it was moon-shadow, knew better—behind the lamp, the darkness seemed to lean, the way a crowd leans toward a speaker.

The light stopped at the first switchback, directly below the watchpost, close enough that Gideon could see the figure beneath it: robed, hooded, alone, and holding something in both hands that caught the firelight from Gideon's own window and threw it back in a slow, deliberate flash.

A bell. Iron, small, dark with age.

Gideon's mouth went dry. There was only one bell in the world like that. Gideon had buried it with its owner, thirty years ago, at the flooded shrine below the glacier—and had stood at the graveside listening to the water close over it, certain that some things, at least, stay drowned.

The figure raised the lamp higher, so its glow fell on their face.

White hair. A face Gideon knew better than their own hands, older by thirty years than it had any right to be, smiling up through the dark.

"Keeper," said the dead woman, in a voice like ash settling, "you've let the fire get low. Come down and let me in."
