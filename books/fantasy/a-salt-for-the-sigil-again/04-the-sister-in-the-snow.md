## 4. The Sister in the Snow

The palace had taught Isolde three ways to leave a room unnoticed: the servant's stair behind the tapestry of the Hunt, the coal chute that smelled of the kitchens below, and the postern gate whose lock she had picked at eleven years old for the sheer sin of it. She used all three on the night her brother stopped knowing her face.

She did not cry until the mountains swallowed the city lights behind her.

Thornfell in winter was a country of white teeth. The road that carters called the Shepherd's Spine narrowed to a goat track after the second day, and by the third there was no road at all—only wind-carved ridges and the occasional cairn standing like a mourner who had forgotten what he mourned. Isolde had stolen a shepherd's cloak from a drying line in the last village, grey wool stiff with frost, and boots two sizes too large stuffed with straw. Her hands were cracked from cold. Her hair, which had once been brushed a hundred strokes each morning by a woman paid to be gentle, was a matted rope under the hood. She had never been so filthy, so hungry, or so free, and the freedom tasted like iron.

She thought about Aldric constantly. That was the cruelty of it: he was everywhere in her head, vivid as ever—the boy who had dared her to climb the plum tree and then cried when she fell, because he'd been crying for *her*; the young king who had waved off his chamberlain mid-lecture to walk her through the palace gardens and name every flower wrong on purpose just to make her laugh. And now—

*You are very like my sister,* he had said, three weeks ago, looking at her with the polite blankness of a man regarding a stranger's portrait. *She died, you know. Or went away. The court does not speak of it.*

*I am here,* she had said. *Aldric. It's me. It's Issa.*

Something had moved behind his eyes then—a fish turning under ice—and for one heartbeat she had seen him drowning inside his own face. Then the court physician had stepped forward with a cup of something steaming, and Aldric had drunk it like an obedient child, and the drowning look smoothed away into pleasant nothing.

*How strange,* he had said. *For a moment I thought—* He had smiled. It was the smile he used for ambassadors. *No matter. You must stay for supper, my lady. We will speak of my sister, if you like. I am told I loved her very much.*

She had stayed for supper. She had smiled and curtsied and eaten venison while her brother described her death to her face. Then she had gone to her rooms, packed nothing, taken only the letter hidden in the false bottom of her jewelry case, and left by the coal chute.

The letter. She touched the oilskin packet inside her shirt for the hundredth time, feeling its edges through the fabric. In Aldric's handwriting—his real handwriting, the slanted, impatient script he'd used since childhood, not the formal court hand—he had written:

*If you are reading this, Issa, then I have already lost too much of myself to say it aloud. There is something wearing me. It answers prayers. Do not pray. Whatever happens, do not pray. Go to Thornfell. Find the keeper of the gate. They will know what I mean even if you do not. Trust no one who offers you a miracle for free. — A.*

Dated eleven days before he had failed to recognize her. Written in a hand that shook only twice.

She had shown it to no one. She had run instead, because running was the only thing her body understood, and because the letter said *find the keeper of the gate*, and Isolde had grown up on nursemaid stories about the sealed gate in the Thornfell heights where the old gods were buried alive. Every child knew it. Every child also knew that keepers were a story too—grey wraiths who walked the high passes and turned trespassers to salt.

Well. She was about to become a trespasser.

* * *

The storm found her on the fourth afternoon, or perhaps she found the storm; in whiteout there was no telling which of them was hunting which. Snow came sideways, erasing the world in great wet strokes. Isolde had been following a cairn line that had, an hour ago, simply stopped existing. Her feet had gone past pain into a warm buzzing numbness that she knew—from the same nursemaid stories—was the last lie cold tells before it kills you.

And then, through the roar, she heard a bell.

One note. Low, iron-dark, struck once and allowed to die naturally, the way you might let a sentence trail off rather than end it. It should have been lost in the wind. Instead it cut through the wind as if the wind parted around it.

Isolde turned toward the sound and walked. She fell twice. The third time she fell, she did not get up immediately, and the snow began its gentle work of tucking her in, and some distant sensible part of her mind observed that this was how the stories always ended for fools on mountains.

The bell rang again. Closer. Or maybe the same distance, but louder, which amounted to the same thing when you were dying.

She got up. She put one boot in front of the other, and then another, and then a shape resolved out of the white—not a shape so much as a refusal of the storm, a dark vertical line that became a wall, a wall that became a door, and a door that opened before she reached it, spilling amber light across the snow like something spilled from a jug.

A figure stood in the doorway. Tall, wrapped in the same grey as the mountain, face shadowed under a hood, and for one absurd moment Isolde thought: *the stories were true, and I have walked into a wraith's kitchen.*

"Inside," the figure said. A woman's voice, low and rough as unpolished wood. "Before the storm changes its mind."

Isolde stumbled past her into warmth that hit like a blow. The door boomed shut behind her, cutting the wind off mid-howl, and the silence afterward was so total it rang.

She was in a round stone room, low-ceilinged, lit by a fire and a hanging lamp of green glass. Dried herbs hung from beams. A kettle sat in the coals. Shelves held jars of salt—jars and jars and jars of salt, more salt than any kitchen could use in a lifetime, stacked floor to ceiling along one curved wall, each jar labeled in a small precise hand. Salt in grey, salt in rose, salt black as ink. It looked less like a pantry than an armory.

"You're the keeper," Isolde said. Her voice came out a ruin, scraped raw by four days of wind.

"I'm the keeper." The woman pushed back her hood. She was perhaps fifty, weathered brown, with grey shot through dark hair cropped short and eyes the color of wet slate—eyes that looked at Isolde the way a smith looks at metal brought in off the street, weighing what it was and what it had been through. "Sit down before you fall down. There's broth on the hob. Drink it slow or you'll lose it again."

Isolde sat. The broth was mutton and barley and salt, of course, and it was the best thing she had ever tasted, though she suspected starvation was doing most of the flattering. The keeper watched her eat without speaking, feeding the fire, moving around the small room with the unhurried economy of someone who had lived alone long enough to stop performing for anyone.

When the bowl was empty, the keeper took it, set it down, and said: "You came up the Spine alone, in a killing storm, in boots stuffed with straw. Nobody walks out of the palace and over Thornfell for anything small. So either you're fleeing something, or you're carrying something, or both." She crouched by the fire, forearms on knees. "Which is it?"

Isolde's hand went to her chest without her permission. The keeper's slate eyes followed it and narrowed.

"Both," Isolde said.

* * *

It took her the rest of the evening to tell it, and she told it badly—out of order, doubling back, her voice cracking on the parts she hadn't yet said aloud to anyone. The keeper, whose name was Gideon Villalobos and who offered it grudgingly, as if names cost money, listened without interrupting. Once, when Isolde described the way Aldric had drunk the physician's cup, Gideon went very still, the way a deer goes still, and did not move again until Isolde moved first.

When she finally drew out the oilskin packet and laid the letter on the table between them, Gideon did not reach for it at once. She looked at it the way other people look at snakes.

"May I?"

"It's why I climbed your mountain," Isolde said. "Read it."

Gideon read it twice. Isolde watched her face do something complicated—the dread beneath routine, she would think later, the expression of a person watching thirty years of careful quiet develop its first crack straight down the middle.

"*Do not pray,*" Gideon said quietly. "He wrote that?"

"In his own hand. Before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before whatever is happening to him finished happening."

Gideon set the letter down flat, aligning its edges with the table's edge, a small compulsive neatness. "Tell me exactly what happened. Not what you've decided it means. What happened. Day by day."

So Isolde did. The first strangeness had been small: Aldric declining wine at his own birthday feast, saying he'd sworn an oath. Aldric had never sworn an oath in his life; he'd once joked that oaths were for men with worse memories. Then the prayers. A drought-blighted farm village east of the capital had woken to fields heavy with grain overnight, and the farmers had come to the palace gates weeping gratitude, and Aldric had received them personally, and Isolde had seen him touch the head of a farmer's sick daughter and the girl had stood up out of her litter whole. The court had called it a miracle of the Concord. The temples had filled overnight. And Aldric had begun to change—not cruelly, that was the terrible part, never cruelly. Kinder. Calmer. More generous. He forgave debts. He pardoned criminals. He remembered everything about statecraft and nothing about Sunday breakfasts, about the plum tree, about the song their mother used to hum off-key.

"He remembers the kingdom," Isolde said. "He's better at being king than he's ever been. That's how they got everyone to stop asking questions. He's the best Aldric has ever been, except he isn't Aldric." Her voice broke on the last word and she hated it and kept going anyway. "And the ones who ask questions—the old priest who asked why the miracles needed no offering, the chamberlain who checked the physician's ledgers—they're gone. Reassigned. One of them, the archivist, was found at the bottom of the palace well, and the coroner wrote *misadventure*, and everyone signed it, because signing it felt like grace. I signed it. I signed the mourning book with my own hand and I didn't understand why my hand was shaking until later."

Gideon had risen halfway through and begun pacing, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a caged animal measuring its cage. Now she stopped.

"The gate," she said, mostly to herself. "One sigil went warm last month. Just one. I told myself it was weather. Thirty years I've told myself things." She looked at Isolde, and whatever she saw in Isolde's face made her decide something. "There's a valley below this mountain. A woman named Maerin lives there—was a priestess, before the Concord burned her order. If your brother's letter says what I think it says, she'll need to see it. And we'll need to move soon, because—" She hesitated, and Isolde watched her decide to tell the truth, watched the truth cost her visibly. "Because the thirteenth new moon is coming. And there are things that can only be done, or undone, under a thirteenth moon."

"What things?"

"Things I swore I'd never need to remember." Gideon picked up the letter again, holding it toward the lamp, and her thumb paused over the signature. "*Trust no one who offers you a miracle for free.* Your brother wrote that while he still could. Which means he knew what was coming. Which means somewhere inside that palace, Aldric is still fighting." She folded the letter along its original creases and handed it back with both hands, formally, like returning a sword. "You crossed a mountain to find me. Why? You don't know me. By every story you've heard, I should have turned you to salt on the ridge."

Isolde looked into the fire. When she answered, her voice was steadier than she expected.

"Because everyone else who could help him is either dead or enchanted or pretending. Because I signed a mourning book for a living man and I will burn that book if it's the last thing I do." She met Gideon's slate eyes. "And because whoever wrote that letter trusted *you*—a stranger, a story, a wraith on a mountain—with the only true thing he had left to give. That's good enough for me."

For a long moment Gideon said nothing. The fire popped. Outside, the storm threw itself against the stone like a beggar at a locked cathedral door.

Then the keeper nodded, once, and began pulling jars of salt down from the shelves and packing them into a leather satchel with the brisk efficiency of a woman loading a weapon.

"Eat again," she said. "Sleep if you can. We leave for the valley at first light, ahead of the storm's tail." At the door, pausing with her hand on the latch, she added, quieter: "Your brother bought himself ninety seconds of lucidity once already, maybe. Letters are cheap compared to what he'll pay next. We should hurry."

Isolde pressed the letter back against her heart, beneath the wool, beneath the cold it had carried across a mountain range—and for the first time since the coal chute, since the supper where her brother described her death, the grief in her chest shifted its weight and became something with an edge.

Purpose. Cold, bright, and sharpening.
