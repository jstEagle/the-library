## 5. Thirteen Turns

The letter had been sitting on my table for two days before I worked up the nerve to touch it again.

Not because it frightened me—though it did—but because I'd spent thirty years learning that the things behind the Thornfell gate never announced themselves. They waited. They let you carry them around like a stone in your boot until the weight became so familiar you forgot it was there, and then they shifted.

The letter was a stone in my boot now. Aldric's handwriting, elegant and sure, describing a rite he could not have known and signing himself with words no living king should write: *I am the door held open.*

I set the candle closer, spread the page flat beneath my palm, and read it through for the ninth time. The script was Aldric's—I'd seen his proclamations nailed to the valley notice-boards, the looping *A*, the impatient crossbar on the *d*. But the letter's rhythm was wrong. A man's hand moves the way his breath moves, and this moved like something breathing through him. Long, patient strokes. Strokes that never hurried because nothing wearing that hand had ever needed to hurry.

*To the keeper of the gate,* it began. So it knew me. Of course it knew me. It had been reading my name off the seal for months, warm sigil by warm sigil, like a tongue testing a lock.

*I write with the king's fingers because yours are too far away. He dreams what I show him. He signs what I draft. By the thirteenth turn of the dark moon, the glove will fit so well he will not feel it, and neither will his kingdom. You remember how permanence is made, apprentice. You helped make it once. Come and unmake it—if you can find the rite, if you can find the will, if you can find anyone left who remembers the old liturgy well enough to chant it without laughing.*

Beneath the mockery, almost lost in the flourishes, was a line written smaller. Tighter. The letters cramped, as though the writer's hand had been fighting him:

*It lies about the price. —A*

I sat back. The candle guttered, righted itself.

There it was again—the crack in the thing's composure. Whatever wore Aldric had let its captive speak for half a line, which meant either arrogance or desperation, and I had learned long ago that gods were arrogant precisely when they were desperate.

I rolled the letter closed and went out into the cold.

* * *

Maerin lived at the bottom of the valley, past the terraced fields gone fallow since the wardens' last sweep, in a shepherd's hut with a slate roof patched in three colors of slate. She grew herbs she wasn't supposed to know the names of and kept bees she talked to like disgraced colleagues. When I knocked, she opened the door already frowning, as if she'd been frowning at the thought of me all morning.

"You've brought something," she said. "You have the face of a woman carrying something heavy in one hand and pretending it isn't there."

"I have the face of a woman who slept four hours."

"That too." She stepped aside to let me in. "Sit. Tea. You look like a fence post someone leaned theology against."

Maerin had been defrocked eleven years ago for asking questions during an inquest—specifically, for asking why the Church of the Ninefold Flame burned healers faster than heretics. She still wore the discipline of her order like a scar: precise hands, precise speech, and a habit of praying under her breath before doing anything difficult, then catching herself and stopping halfway through.

She read the letter twice. The second time, her lips moved, and I realized she wasn't reading—she was chanting, very quietly, fitting the letter's cadences against a liturgy she knew.

When she finished, she set the page down flat, the way you put down something that might be listening.

"This is addressed to you."

"It knows I'm here. It's known for a while."

"No." She tapped the first line. "*Keeper of the gate.* That's not a title, Gideon. That's an address in the old form. In the liturgy of the Sealed Ones, every prayer has a recipient, and the recipient's true name determines whether the prayer lands or falls into the dark. Whoever wrote this used your office as your name." Her eyes came up. "It thinks of you as part of the mechanism."

"I am part of the mechanism. That was always the job."

"The job was oiling hinges. This—" she turned the letter over, held it to the light, "—this is an invitation to a wedding. And I don't think you're the bride."

She rose and went to the chest beneath her window. I watched her dig past vestments folded with painful neatness—she'd kept them, all these years, the white stole with its nine flames, though she'd never wear them again—and come up with a book. Not a book, really. A gathering of loose quires tied with waxed cord, pages soft as cloth from handling.

"My order's rite book," she said. "Or most of it. They burned the copies they found. Mine survived because I hid it inside a book of tax law, and no warden in history has ever opened a book of tax law."

"Is the unbinding in there?"

"Maybe." She untied the cord with fingers that weren't quite steady. "The rites of binding are all here. We were very good at binding. It's most of what we did—bind the wells, bind the stones, bind the names. Unbinding was..." She paused. "Unbinding was considered a failure state. Like writing down how to undo a dam. Why would you? Who would want to?"

"Someone standing downstream," I said, "when the dam starts leaking."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she began turning pages.

We worked through the afternoon. The hut filled with the smell of tallow and old vellum and the particular dust-smell of a book that has been waiting. Maerin chanted fragments under her breath, matching sounds, discarding matches. Outside, the light went from white to gold to the bruised purple of early winter dusk, and she lit three candles without noticing she'd done it.

It was nearly full dark when she stopped on a page near the end of the last quire and went very still.

"Gideon."

"What."

"Come look at this. And bring the letter."

The page was a fragment—a rite begun and abandoned, its margins crowded with a later hand arguing with itself. At its head, in faded ochre ink, someone had drawn three interlocking circles, each marked with a sigil I knew better than my own heartbeat, because I repainted them on the gate every spring: the Coil, the Hollow Eye, the Root Beneath.

"The Threefold Unbinding," Maerin read aloud, slowly, translating as she went from the old tongue. "'To loose what is bound thrice-bound, the keeper must stand within the seal and give, for each sigil unbound, one full turn of memory. Not a moment. A turn—the whole wheel of it, root to crown.'" She looked up. "Do you understand what a turn means?"

"I think so."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear you say it."

"A year of memory," I said. "Given whole. Everything that happened inside it, given up entire—not blurred, not dimmed. Gone clean, as if the year never happened to me."

"Yes." She exhaled. "And there's more. Read the margin."

The margin note was cramped, urgent, the same hand that had argued with itself down the rest of the page:

*The rite must close before the thirteenth new moon from the first warming. After the thirteenth, the bound god no longer needs the seal's consent to remain—it will have worn its vessel to the shape of the door, and the door will hold itself open. There is no rite for what comes after. Write none. Pray instead.*

I counted before Maerin could. I'd been counting since the first warm sigil; it was the kind of counting keepers do in their sleep.

"First warming was the night the shepherd's fever broke," I said. "Ninth of the Harvest Moon. Four new moons have come and gone since."

"Then we have nine," Maerin said. "Nine new moons. Less than a year." She sat back on her heels, and for the first time since I'd known her, she looked her age—all of it, every defrocked and exiled year of it. "Gideon, do you see what this is? It told you the deadline. The god wrote you a letter telling you exactly how much time you have."

"It's arrogant."

"It's *confident*. There's a difference, and the difference is body count." She stood and paced the length of her hut, three steps, turn, three steps back. "Think. If it wanted you dead, it would send wardens—and maybe it will. But first it wants you to know the clock is running. Why would a thing that hunts give its prey a schedule?" She stopped. Answered herself: "Because it doesn't believe you'll pay the price. Because it thinks nine turns of memory is a coin no one alive will spend. It's showing you the exit door and betting everything that you won't walk through it, because walking through it costs you yourself."

"And if I don't pay," I said, "it keeps Aldric forever."

"And if you do pay, it loses its vessel, starves behind the seal, and you forget—" she gestured at the letter, at me, at the whole valley, "—whatever years it takes. Three sigils. Three turns. Could be anything. Could be the year you learned the keeping. Could be—" she didn't finish it, but we both heard the end of the sentence anyway. Could be the year you loved someone.

The fire ticked. One of the bees, confused by the candlelight, tapped against the window glass, once, twice, and went still.

"There's another problem," Maerin said quietly. "The rite requires voices. Look—the rubric. *Let the keeper stand within; let one voice hold the chant of loosing; let one hand hold iron that has rung in a holy place.* Three roles, minimum. A keeper, a chanter, a bell-bearer. And the chant isn't parish work, Gideon. It's the deep liturgy. Most priests couldn't chant it correctly on a bet. I can, barely, and I haven't spoken those words aloud in eleven years, and my voice isn't what it was."

"So we need a third person. Someone to hold the bell."

"We need a third person who won't run when the god starts offering them things. Because it will offer. That's the first movement of any unbinding—the bound one bargains. It offered your predecessor everything. She refused, and look what refusing bought her: a wall." Maerin rubbed her eyes. "I'm sorry. I'm giving you the shape of the problem and not one inch of solution."

"The shape is enough. Shapes are where solutions start." I took the letter and folded it away. "Who else knows the deep liturgy? Anyone besides you?"

"In this valley? No. In the world?" She laughed, short and bitter. "The ones who did are ash or apostates, and the ash outnumber us. There might be someone in the capital. There might be someone in the mountain orders, if any of them survived the purges. But Gideon—the capital is where the throne is, and the throne is where *it* sits. Asking me to find you a liturgist in the capital is asking me to fish in the shark's mouth."

"Then we fish carefully."

"*We.*" She said the word like she was tasting it. "You're assuming I'm coming."

"I'm assuming nothing. I'm asking."

For a while she didn't answer. She stood at her window looking out at the fallow fields, at the dark line of Thornfell rising beyond them, at the gate she couldn't see from here but clearly felt, the way you feel a bad tooth.

"They took my orders from me," she said finally. "Eleven years ago. Stripped the stole off me in front of the whole cathedral chapter and read out my sins, and half of them weren't even true—they just needed a heretic that season and I asked inconvenient questions. Do you know what I prayed, that night, on the road out of the city? I prayed that I would never again be asked to choose between my faith and my conscience." She turned. "And then you walk in with a letter from a god and a rite that needs a priestess, and here's the joke, Gideon: this is the first time in eleven years anyone's asked me to use what I actually know. Not the pretty parish liturgy. The real thing. The deep words."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes with conditions. I get to correct your pronunciation. You get to stop calling me 'the priestess' like I'm a tool you're borrowing. And when this is done—" her voice caught, just slightly, and steadied, "—when this is done, someone writes down what happened. Properly. Names and dates and what it cost. Because the last time the gods woke, the record was burned, and that's why everyone's forgotten, and forgetting is how it got this far."

"Agreed."

"Then sit down," she said, pulling the rite book between us, "because if we have nine moons, we have no time to waste, and you're going to learn the chant even if I have to beat it into you syllable by syllable. And Gideon—"

"What."

"When you counted the moons just now. You didn't hesitate. You've been counting since the first warming." Her eyes were sharp and kind at once, which was a very Maerin combination. "You already knew, didn't you. Before the letter. Some part of you has been counting down since the shepherd's shadow walked off without him."

I hadn't lied to many people in thirty years of keeping. I wasn't going to start with her.

"Yes," I said. "I think some part of me has been waiting thirty years for the quiet to end. And now that it has, I find I'm not surprised. Only—" I searched for the word and found it, plain and ugly and true. "Only resolved. It woke something up. Fine. Then we wake something up too."

Maerin smiled—the first real smile I'd seen from her, crooked and tired and fierce.

"Well then," she said, and opened the rite book to the first page of the deep liturgy, and began to teach me the words for unmaking.

Outside, the new moon was nowhere in the sky, which meant somewhere above the valley, invisible, the count had already begun.

Eight after this one. Then seven.

We had nine turns of the dark moon to learn a dead language, find a bell-bearer who wouldn't break, reach a king wearing a god like a glove—and pay whatever the seal demanded to take it off him.

I wrote the number on the back of my hand in gate-ink, where I'd see it every morning: **IX**.

Then I picked up the chant.
