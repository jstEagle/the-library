## 6. Prayers Priced in Bone

Maerin had said the liturgy of the Hollow Choir every morning for eleven years, and she had believed every word of it.

That was the thing she could not make the others understand, standing at the lip of the valley as the sun came up behind them and threw their shadows long across the wheat. Belief was not a weakness. It was not a door left carelessly open. It was a cathedral you built inside yourself, stone by stone, and once it was built, anything could live in it. Anything that knew the words.

"Is that it?" Isolde asked. She stood with her hood back despite the cold, her hair silvered with frost, looking down into the valley like a woman reading a letter she already dreaded.

Below them, the village of Candlemere lay in a bowl of fields so green they hurt to look at. Every other farm on the mountain road had been browned by the late frosts; here the barley stood heavy-headed and gold, the orchards bent under fruit out of season, and the streams ran clear as glass. It should have been beautiful. Maerin had seen beautiful things before, in the years when she still served, and she had learned what beauty cost when it arrived too easily.

"It's the valley," she said. "Or it was. The maps call it Sorrel Deep. Three hundred souls, maybe fewer now."

"And the people?"

Ryn Sable crouched at the edge of the overlook, chewing a strip of dried apple, her eyes moving the way they always moved—doors, treelines, the road behind them. "Saw two on the path down," she said. "Didn't speak. Smiled at me." She spat the apple out. "I don't like being smiled at by strangers. Feels like being measured for a coffin."

Gideon said nothing. They had said very little since the wardens' fires at Millbrook, three days gone; Maerin had watched them carry the smoke-smell of that burning in their clothes and their silence, watched it settle into them like ash into wool. Now Gideon pulled the collar of their coat up and started down the switchback trail without a word, and the rest of them followed, because following Gideon had become, without anyone deciding it, simply what they did.

* * *

The first worshipper they met was a woman kneeling in a barley field.

She knelt in the wet earth with her hands folded and her face turned up toward the sky, and she was smiling—the same smile Ryn had described, wide and serene and wrong, the smile of someone listening to music no one else could hear. Her lips moved. Maerin caught fragments of it as they passed: *hollowed be thy name, thy kingdom come, thy will be emptied—*

Maerin stopped walking.

She knew those words. Not the way you know a song you've heard sung badly in a tavern. She knew them the way you know your own hands. The Liturgy of the Emptying had been the morning office of the Order of the Quiet Table, the order that had raised her, clothed her, taught her to read the old scripts—and then cast her out when she'd asked one question too many about where the prayers went.

"Gideon." Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using it. "Stop. Stop, I need you to hear this."

The woman in the field did not pause. Her lips kept moving, silent now or perhaps only beyond hearing, and up close Maerin could see the rest of it: the eyes fixed on nothing, the pupils blown wide and dark as wells, the fine tremor in the folded hands like the tremor of a plucked string. Well-fed. Clean. Healthy as a summer apple. And empty behind the face the way a house is empty when the family has died but the shutters are still open.

"She's praying," Isolde whispered. "In the field. At harvest time, shouldn't she be—"

"She's finished harvesting," Maerin said. "Look at her hands."

Her hands were ruined. Not wounded—ruined in the subtler way, the fingernails split to the quick, the fingertips worn smooth and pale as candle wax, as if she had spent days pressing them into the soil, into bark, into stone. As if the prayer required a price paid in skin, and she had paid it gladly, and would pay again tomorrow, and the day after, until there was nothing left of her but the smile.

Gideon crouched beside the woman, careful, slow, the way one approaches a sleeping animal. "Mother," they said gently. "What is your name?"

The smile widened. The lips parted.

"*Hollowed be thy name,*" the woman said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole, and went on praying.

* * *

The village itself was worse, because the village was orderly.

There was no squalor in Candlemere. No beggars, no sick, no weeping at doorways. The well was clean, the shrine freshly whitewashed, the granary full. Children played in the lane with the loud health of children who have never known hunger. And every adult Maerin saw—in the lanes, at the well, mending nets by the stream—had the same blown-dark eyes, the same serene smile, the same lips moving through the offices of a liturgy she had once chanted in a stone cloister with tears of genuine love running down her face.

They walked the main street like ghosts at their own funeral.

"There's no one home," Ryn murmured. She had stopped chewing. That, Maerin was learning, was how Ryn showed fear. "I've run cargo past plague towns. Plague towns scream. This place is quiet as a purse."

"The miracles took the screaming with everything else," Maerin said. "That's the design of it. Don't you see? This is what answered prayer looks like when it's done properly. You ask for the fever to break, and it breaks—but the fever was yours, part of you, and something has to fill the space it leaves. You ask for the crops to grow, and they grow, but growth is taken from somewhere, from someone, and the god takes its tithe from the asker. Memory first. Then want. Then—" she gestured at the smiling woman in the lane, who was scrubbing her doorstep with the same blissful attention she gave her prayers, "—then everything else. Until all that's left is the gratitude."

Isolde had gone white. "Aldric," she said. "When he healed the whole court of the winter fever, in a night—he stood up from his bed and he didn't know me. I thought—I thought it was grief, or some sickness of the mind, I thought my brother had lost himself to the crown's weight. But it was this. He paid for our healing with himself."

"He didn't know the price," Gideon said quietly. "No one tells you the price. That's the oldest trick there is." They looked at Maerin then, and their eyes were steady and terrible. "You recognized the words. In the field."

"Yes."

"You've prayed them."

"I prayed them every dawn for eleven years." Maerin made herself stand straight under that gaze. She owed these people truth; she had been paying it out in pieces since the watchpost, and here, in the middle of the harvest of her own faith, she supposed the debt came due entire. "The Order of the Quiet Table kept the old rites after the gate was sealed. We told ourselves we were keeping the gods asleep—that the liturgies were lullabies, that as long as the words were spoken gently, by the faithful, in the proper order, nothing behind the seal could use them. We were the lock's oil, Gideon. We oiled the lock every morning and called it devotion."

"And now something's using your lullabies as a war-song."

"Now something is using them exactly as written." She heard her own voice crack and hated it. "Do you understand? It isn't corrupting the rites. It isn't twisting them. The Liturgy of the Emptying was always meant to do this. We just—we never performed it whole. We performed it in pieces, softened, diluted, the dangerous verses reserved for high holy days that never came. Whoever is wearing the king's crown has the complete text. And it is performing my order's work with a thoroughness we never dared."

The silence stretched. Somewhere a child laughed, bright and unhaunted, and the sound was obscene.

"So the god didn't invent this hunger," Isolde said slowly. "It's—it's remembering it. Using tools that were already made."

"The rites predate the seal," Maerin said. "The seal was built to close a door. The rites were the door's hinges. My order forgot that, or chose to forget, because forgetting was easier than grieving." She looked down the street, at the whitewashed shrine at its end, its little bell gleaming. "I need to see the shrine. If they're performing the full office, there will be a table. A quiet table. And on it—"

* * *

The shrine smelled of beeswax and cut grain, and beneath both, faintly, of iron.

Inside, it was beautiful. That was the worst of it. Candles burned in ranks along the walls, dozens of them, each one labeled in a careful hand: *for Marta's lungs, given thanks. For Tam's leg, given thanks. For the child in Sorrel's wife, given thanks.* A wall of gratitude, glowing. Villagers knelt in the pews, smiling, swaying slightly, their murmured liturgy rising and falling in waves.

And at the head of the room stood the table.

It was plain wood, old wood, older than the village—Maerin knew its grain, had laid her forehead against its twin a thousand mornings. On it lay an open book, its pages thick as hide, and beside the book a shallow bowl of salt, and beside the bowl—

"Bones," Ryn breathed. "Why is there a bowl of bones on the altar?"

"Finger bones," Maerin said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Phalanges. Count them."

Ryn counted. Maerin watched her lips move and stop moving.

"That's more than ten hands' worth," Ryn said. "That's—" She looked up, and for the first time since Maerin had known her, the smuggler's face held nothing clever at all. "These are tithes. People are *giving* them."

"Willingly. Joyfully. That's the final verse, the one we never sang. *What is given in gladness is not stolen; what is not stolen cannot be reclaimed.*" Maerin stepped closer to the book. The open page was written in the old hand, the true hand, the script she had bled over as a novice—and below the printed office, in fresher ink, someone had added marginalia. Corrections. Refinements. Notes in a neat, patient script on how to deepen the emptying, how to draw the tithe faster, how to keep the worshipper functional longer so that their gratitude might be harvested again and again.

She knew that handwriting.

She had sat across from that handwriting at the novices' table for eleven years. She had copied that handwriting's sermons. She had loved, in the wordless and obedient way of the young, the woman who wrote it: Mother Superior Vess, who had held Maerin's face in her hands on the day of her casting-out and said, *You ask where the prayers go, child. Some questions are doors, and doors must stay shut.*

"Vess," Maerin said aloud. "Oh, Vess. What did you let in?"

"What is it?" Gideon had come to stand at her shoulder. "Whose writing is that?"

"My Mother Superior's. The woman who expelled me." Maerin heard herself laugh, a small broken sound. "I spent six years telling myself she cast me out because I was dangerous to the faith. The truth is she cast me out because I was asking the questions she wanted to ask and couldn't afford to. And then, somewhere between then and now, she stopped being able to afford not to. She answered the door herself. She's here—or she was. These notes are weeks old at most."

At the front of the shrine, the congregation rose as one, all of them, in perfect unison, and turned to face the altar, and smiled at Maerin with three hundred mouths.

The murmur of the liturgy changed key. She felt it before she understood it—the words shifting, the familiar office bending into a new verse, one she had never been permitted to read, and the candles guttered though there was no draft, and the bowl of salt on the quiet table began, very softly, to sing.

"We need to leave," Gideon said. "Now. Slowly. Don't run."

"They know us," Isolde whispered. "Gideon, they're looking *at* us—they can't see, can they? Their eyes—"

"They see enough." Gideon's hand found the iron bell at their belt—the keeper's bell, recovered from the flooded shrine, and Maerin had wondered why Gideon carried it so constantly, and now she saw why, because the moment its cold shape cleared the leather, the smiles in the shrine faltered, flickered, the congregation swaying like wheat in a wind that wasn't there. "The bell remembers the seal. Walk. Don't look at the table."

They walked. Behind them the liturgy rose to a wail, three hundred throats singing joyfully, ecstatically, a sound like a wedding and a funeral braided together, and Maerin did not look back because she knew—if she looked back, she would see Mother Superior Vess standing among her flock, and she would have to decide, in front of God and everyone, whether she still loved her.

* * *

They made the ridge by dusk, breath steaming, hearts hammering, and only there, with Candlemere's candle-glow small and warm below them like a fallen constellation, did anyone speak.

"It's a farm," Ryn said finally. She was sitting with her arms around her knees, and her voice had none of its usual blade. "That's what I keep coming back to. It's not a rampage, it's not a haunting, it's a *farm*. It planted a village, watered it with miracles, and now it's harvesting them a piece at a time. Finger bones today. What's tomorrow?"

"Memories," Isolde said. "Then names. Then—" She couldn't finish it. She didn't have to. They had all seen the woman in the barley field.

"And the capital is full of people selling years of themselves for cures," Gideon said. "We thought that was corruption of the crown. It's simpler than that. It's agriculture. The whole kingdom is a field, and the king is the farmer's glove." They turned to Maerin, and the last light caught their face, making it old and young at once. "You said the rites were the door's hinges. Then you know how they come apart. You can unwrite what you were trained to write."

"I can try." Maerin wrapped her arms around herself. Below, the shrine's candles burned on, each flame a name, each name a theft. "But Gideon—understand what we're fighting. I spent my life believing I was keeping the world safe with gentle words. Half this kingdom believes the same thing right now, on their knees, smiling. The enemy isn't a monster in the dark. It's grace with the price torn off the tag. It's every kind thing that ever asked too much." She looked at her own hands, whole and unbitten. "And the worst of it—the truly worst of it—is that some part of me knelt in that shrine tonight. Some part of me heard the office sung whole, properly, completely, for the first time, and it was *beautiful*, and I had to drag myself out by the throat."

Gideon put a hand on her shoulder. It was the first time they had touched her.

"Good," they said. "Hold onto that. The part that drags you out. That's the part that unbinds gates."

It was Ryn who broke the moment, because Ryn always broke the moments, standing suddenly with her head cocked toward the valley road.

"We've got company," she said. "One rider, coming up the switchback fast. No lantern." She squinted into the dark, and Maerin saw her whole body change, the smuggler's certainty curdling into something rawer. "And I know the horse. That's a palace horse. That's—"

The rider crested the ridge and hauled the horse to a stop, and the light of their little fire caught a face Maerin had seen only in Isolde's drawings, scratched hurriedly in charcoal at the watchpost during sleepless nights: drawn, hollow-eyed, half-familiar, and unmistakably royal.

King Aldric looked down at them from horseback, alone, unarmed, and wept.

"Sister," he said to Isolde, in a voice frayed down to the thread. "I don't have much time. When it sleeps, I get minutes. Only minutes." He fumbled at his belt with shaking hands and held something out—a ring of keys, iron and black iron, stamped with a sigil Maerin knew from her order's oldest books. "The thirteenth moon is sooner than you think. He's moved the ceremony. And if you're going to reach the gate—you have to go *tonight*."
