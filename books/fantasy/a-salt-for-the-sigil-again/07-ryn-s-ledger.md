## 7. Ryn's Ledger

The tunnel mouth opened behind a dead man's fence, the way all the best ones did. Ryn had learned that lesson at fourteen, running salt for a man who beat her if she asked questions: nobody watches a grave. The fence belonged to a shepherd who'd died three winters back, and his sons still hadn't the heart to pull it down, and so it stood, leaning and lichen-eaten, hiding a door in the hillside that the crown's surveyors had marked on no map worth stealing.

"Charming," Maerin said, looking at it.

"It gets worse," Ryn said, and shouldered through the gap in the rails.

Behind her came the rest of them, the strange little congregation she'd somehow acquired: Gideon Villalobos with that gatekeeper's walk, all patience and iron; Maerin the priestess with her defrocked hands folded like she was still praying; and the girl—no, not the girl, the princess, though Isolde flinched every time Ryn thought the word too loudly. Isolde came last, hood up, a letter in Aldric's handwriting pressed flat against her ribs where she kept it like a second heart.

Ryn checked the sun's angle, marked it against the ridge, and pulled the fence's loose rail back into place behind them. Then it was dark, and the mountain swallowed them, and she did what she always did in the dark: she counted her exits.

There were four between here and the river. She knew because she'd mapped them herself, over six years, in a ledger she carried against her spine in an oilcloth wrap. And that ledger was the reason she hadn't slept in nine days.

* * *

The tunnels were old work, older than the crown, older maybe than the gate Gideon kept. Smugglers' cut, the first stretch—shoulder-width, chiseled by hands that hadn't wasted a stroke—and then wider, where the mountain had been mined for copper before anyone now living drew breath. Ryn walked it with a shuttered lamp and her memory, and behind her the others kept pace, and for the first hour nobody talked, which was the first hour's whole purpose. If you couldn't walk an hour in silence, you couldn't be trusted with the rest.

It was Isolde who broke it, because of course it was. The girl had a talent for asking the question everyone else was swallowing.

"You've been quiet since the valley," she said. Her voice echoed wrong in the copper dark, flattened and close. "Quieter than quiet. What's down here that you don't want us to see?"

"Nothing I don't want you to see," Ryn said. "Everything I don't want to say."

"Those are usually the same thing," Gideon said, from the back.

Ryn stopped walking. The lamp swung; shadows lurched up the ribbed walls like things waking. She stood there a moment with her hand flat against the cold stone, feeling the mountain breathe its slow mineral breath, and she thought: *you can lie to a priestess, you can lie to a gatekeeper, but the princess has been lied to by professionals her whole life, and she'll smell a bad one coming through solid rock.*

"Two hours ahead," she said, "there's a junction room. There's a table in it, and a lamp, and a drop-box in the wall. And there's a ledger with my name on it that says I've been working for the crown since the winter after I pulled you lot out of the river, Gideon, and I'd rather tell you that standing still than running."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far off, water moved through stone with a sound like slow applause.

"Say it plain," Gideon said at last. "All of it."

So Ryn turned around and did the hardest thing she'd done since the winter the crown's men found her: she told the truth with her whole chest, no exits mapped.

* * *

"It started with a debt," she said. They'd made the junction room by then—she'd insisted, because if this went badly she wanted walls she knew and a second exit she could reach in forty seconds. The room was a hollow where three tunnels met, floored with old mine tailings, and the drop-box sat in the wall like a metal eye. She didn't look at it. "Not the one I owe you, Gideon. The other kind. The kind you owe to people who keep accounts."

"You said you were paid in answered prayers," Maerin said. "For the cargo. The whispering crates."

"I said what was true. That was the *first* job. The crown's tithe-runners came to me after the river, after I—" She gestured at Gideon, at all of them, the gesture meaning *after I saved your life and you saved mine and we never finished counting*. "They had my sister's name. Nessa. You know what she is—was—a prayer-keeper, back when prayer-keeping was a poor woman's trade and nobody important cared. The wardens had a list. Her name was on it. And a warden captain sat across a table from me and said: *run our messages, and the list gets shorter.*"

"And you ran them," Isolde said. Not an accusation. Worse—understanding, which Ryn could have gone the rest of her life without hearing in that voice.

"I ran them." Ryn set the lamp on the table. Its light made a small yellow island in all that dark, and she stood at the edge of it like a woman at the edge of a pier. "Messages. Sealed packets, warden's wax, no questions. I told myself it was letters. Requisitions. Warden gossip. I told myself a lot of things, because I'm good at telling myself things, it's most of what smuggling is." She pulled at the oilcloth at her back, and the knot fought her, and she fought it back. "Then the valley happened. Then the shepherd's shadow walked off without him. Then I watched a village that worshipped *nothing* burn because its people had the bad luck to be healed." The wrap came loose. She brought the ledger around and laid it on the table, and it sat there, fat and cracked and swollen with six years of ink. "And I read what I'd been carrying."

"You opened the packets?" Gideon asked. Careful. Neutral. A gatekeeper's voice, holding a door.

"I'm a smuggler, Gideon. I opened everything." She almost smiled. It didn't take. "Prayer tallies. Districts, names, quantities. Which villages prayed loudest. Which prayers got answered and what the answer cost. Routes for moving—" her throat tightened— "for moving the *hollowed*. The ones the miracles ate. They ship them to the capital. There's a market. You've seen it by now, or you will. Memory sells better than salt ever did."

Isolde had gone very still. "My brother's seal was on the packets."

"Some of them." Ryn met her eyes and didn't flinch, because flinching would have been mercy and the girl deserved none from her, not over this. "Your brother's seal, or the thing wearing your brother's hand. I couldn't tell you which. That's the truth of it. I've been carrying orders for a god and telling myself they were requisitions."

The silence went on long enough that Ryn heard her own heart, quick and shallow, a smuggler's heart, always counting exits. Maerin's hands had unfolded. Gideon hadn't moved at all, hadn't blinked, and that was the worst of it—Gideon, who owed Ryn a life and Ryn Gideon, standing there weighing her like a sigil, like a door that might open onto anything.

"So," Ryn said. "Betrayal's on the table. I've laid it out myself, which is more courtesy than most traitors get. There's a second exit behind you, forty seconds at a walk. Take it, and I'll burn this book and vanish so clean the crown will write songs about it. Or—"

"Or," Gideon said.

"Or you tell me what you'd have done." Ryn's voice cracked and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "They had my *sister*. They had her name on a list and a warden's pen over it, and every packet I carried was one more week of her breathing. You of all people know what it is to keep a bad thing sealed because the alternative is worse. I kept my mouth sealed for six years and called it love. So before any of you judges me—*tell me what you'd have done.*"

Nobody answered at once. The lamp hissed. Water applauded somewhere in the dark.

It was Maerin who moved first. The priestess crossed the room, took the ledger off the table, weighed it in her two defrocked hands like a relic of a faith she no longer served, and held it out to Ryn.

"Then we do what the faithless do," Maerin said, "with the things we can't carry."

Ryn stared at her.

"Burn it," Maerin said. "Here, now, in front of us. Not because it absolves you—ink doesn't absolve, I know that better than most. Burn it because the crown keeps its copy, and the crown's copy is a chain. This one's yours. Burn it, and the only account left is the one between you and us, and I've seen how you keep that one."

Ryn looked at Gideon. Gideon looked back, and something in that flat grey gaze shifted—some gate opening a hand's width, some old debt being quietly marked *paid forward*.

"Your sister," Gideon said. "Nessa. Where is she now?"

"The capital. The wardens moved her last month. 'Protective custody.'" Ryn's mouth twisted around the word. "Which in the crown's arithmetic means the same thing it means for everyone they protect."

"Then we're not done," Gideon said. "And neither are you. Burn the book, Ryn. We'll get her back."

Ryn had crossed oceans of trouble in her life and never once had anyone said *we* about her sister's name. She found she had to turn away, just for a moment, to look at the tunnel wall and get her face back in order. The mountain didn't mind. The mountain had watched worse.

She took the ledger. She took the lamp. She tipped the flame into the oilcloth and set the whole fat, guilty weight of six years on the mine tailings, and it caught, and the light of it was enormous in the junction room—pages curling black, names she'd carried and routes she'd run and tallies of prayers and prices going up in a bloom of orange that threw all four of them huge and wavering on the stone. The smell was ink and old leather and something almost like incense, and Maerin murmured something under her breath that might have been a rite or might have been a curse, and Isolde watched the fire with an expression Ryn couldn't read and didn't try to.

When it was ash, Ryn ground it under her heel until even the ash was just dust.

"There," she said, and her voice was steady, and she was proud of it. "No more messages. No more packets. No more list. From here to the capital, I'm yours—wholly, and you can ask the mountain to witness it, because the mountain's the only thing down here that doesn't lie."

"And after the capital?" Isolde asked quietly.

"After the capital," Ryn said, "I get my sister. Whatever's left of the crown afterward can send its wardens. I'll introduce them to my exits."

* * *

They walked the rest of the day's stretch in something easier than silence—still careful, still counting, but no longer circling the wound. Ryn led them down the long copper run where the walls sweated mineral light, and past the flooded gallery where blind pale fish hung in the black water like thoughts nobody had finished, and up through the chimney climb where she'd fixed iron rungs a decade ago and every one of them still held, because Ryn Sable did not do work twice.

At the second camp, in a dry pocket off the main run, Maerin sat sharpening a ritual knife that had no business being as beautiful as it was, and Ryn sat down beside her because the priestess had stopped flinching when Ryn came near, and that was as close to absolution as Ryn expected to get.

"The god," Ryn said, low, so the others wouldn't hear. "The thing wearing your king. You served the old rites. You'd know. What is it, really? What does it *want*?"

Maerin's blade whispered against the stone. "It wants what every god I ever served wanted. To be fed. To be *needed*. The rest—the king, the miracles, the market in memories—that's just the shape its hunger takes this century. A thousand years ago it took other shapes. Worse ones." She looked up, and the lamplight made her eyes hollow as the worshippers in the valley. "The rites it's using now, Ryn. The liturgy in that valley. I wrote parts of it. When I was young and certain and certain things were holy." The knife stopped. "So when you ask what it wants—ask also what it was *given*. That's the part I can't stop asking."

Ryn didn't have an answer for that. She lay back against her pack instead, in the dark under the mountain, and for the first time in nine days she slept, and dreamed of her sister's kitchen, and woke with the taste of ash still in her mouth.

They came up out of the tunnels at dusk on the third day, through a collapsed kiln on the last ridge before the river road, and the capital lay below them in the valley like a coal banked in grey—smoke rising from a thousand chimneys, and over the palace district, where the temples clustered, a light that had no business being there. A glow. Warm and gold and steady, like a hearth seen through a keyhole, burning without smoke.

"That's not a festival," Isolde said. Her voice had gone thin. "The renewal ceremony isn't for weeks."

"It's not a festival," Ryn agreed. She had smuggled past that city for fifteen years and had never once seen it glow from the inside. She pulled her spyglass from her coat, wiped the lens on her sleeve, and put it to her eye.

The temples were lit. All of them, every tower, every dome, and the streets between them were moving—crowds, at night, in the rain that was just starting to fall, thousands of the faithful walking in slow processions with candles, and the candles' light was rising, not falling, streaming *upward* off the city like heat off a forge, pouring toward the palace in a river of gold.

And at the center of it, on the palace's highest tower, something stood against the sky. Small at this distance. Wrong at any distance. It raised what might have been arms, and the river of light bent toward it, and even through the glass, even across the miles, Ryn felt it *notice* the ridge they stood on—felt it the way you feel a door open behind you in an empty house.

She lowered the spyglass. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady.

"Well," she said. "Change of plans. We go in tonight."
