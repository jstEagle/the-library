## 8. The City That Prays

The road to Corvale was paved with prayers.

I mean that literally. Sometime in the last year, the crown had repaved the king's highway with pale stone quarried from somewhere south, and every flagstone bore an inscription cut deep enough to survive a century of cartwheels and boot-heels. *Blessed is the hand that gives. Blessed is the throat that sings. Blessed is the crown that answers.* We walked on them for two days, and by the second day Ryn had stopped reading them aloud in her smuggler's drawl, because Isolde kept flinching at each line like the stones were speaking her brother's voice.

"They're not," I told her, when she finally said so.

"I know they're not."

"But you keep hearing him anyway."

She didn't answer. She pulled her hood lower and walked faster, which was answer enough. Three months ago Aldric hadn't known her face. Whatever wore him now had learned to imitate his handwriting well enough to write her a letter, and letters were only the beginning. A god that could reach through a king's hand could reach through a highway's worth of stone.

Maerin walked beside me and said nothing for most of it, which was unlike her. She was a woman who filled silence with liturgy the way other people filled it with humming—half-forgotten verses, defrocked and unsanctioned, muttered under her breath like a tic. But since the valley of the hollowed worshippers, she'd gone quiet as a closed book. When she finally spoke, it was near dusk on the second day, staring at the inscription under her feet.

"We sang this," she said. "At Vessany's altar. The third canticle of the answering." Her jaw worked. "It was a harvest hymn. It was *harmless*. We sang it to ask for rain."

"And now it's asking for you."

"Now it's not asking at all." She crouched and pressed her palm flat to the stone. She snatched it back like she'd touched a stove. "Warm. Gideon—the whole road is warm. It's been sung over. Thousands of voices, marching up this road to the capital, and every one of them a tithe."

Ryn, ahead of us with her cart-horse and her ruined ribs bound tight under a borrowed coat, glanced back. "So we walk around?"

"You can't walk around a hymn," Maerin said. "You can only stop singing it."

We didn't stop singing it. None of us knew how yet. So we joined the river of pilgrims instead, four more drops in a flood moving toward Corvale, and I kept my head down and my keeper's sigil-ring turned inward against my palm where no one could see it, and I told myself the warmth under my boots was just summer coming early.

I have been a liar for thirty years. I am getting better at it, which is its own kind of damnation.

* * *

Corvale announced itself before we saw it.

The bells started at dawn on the third day—a hundred towers ringing out of sequence, each parish trying to be louder than its neighbor, until the sound stopped being music and became weather. Then came the smell: incense, tons of it, burning in braziers along the walls, sweet and cloying and underneath it something else, something like the air after lightning but wrong, metallic, the smell I had learned to dread at Thornfell gate.

And then the city itself, white-walled and golden-spired, hung with banners of the crowned sun, and every street pouring people toward the great basilica square like blood toward a wound.

"Piety's mandatory now," Ryn said, reining up at the Northgate queue. She'd gone through the capital twice a year for fifteen years; she knew its rhythms better than her own heartbeat. "Used to be you paid your gate-tax and they didn't care what you believed. Now there's a devotional census. You declare your observance, they stamp your tongue-token—" she touched her own mouth grimly, "—and if your token comes up unchanted at inspection, you get a visit from the wardens. The polite kind first."

"The impolite kind being the ones who burned Ashenmere," Isolde said.

"Ashenmere wasn't even a heresy." Ryn's voice went flat. "They worshipped nothing. No gods, old or new. They planted by stars and buried their dead with salt and they wanted the world left alone. The wardens burned them anyway." She looked at me. "You said the crown was feeding them. I didn't want to believe you. I've run cargo for those wardens. I've seen what they ship into the palace. It isn't grain, Gideon."

The queue moved. A warden in white-and-gold walked the line with a censor swinging from a chain, and where the smoke touched, people opened their mouths on cue and recited—the canticle again, the third of the answering, the harvest hymn Maerin had once sung for rain. Every voice in that line recited it perfectly. Not one of them stumbled. I watched a farmwife with a baby on her hip sing it with her eyes half-shut, tender as a lullaby, and I watched the warden nod and move on, satisfied, and I understood that I was looking at the most disciplined choir in the world, ten thousand voices strong, none of whom knew they were singing.

"It's in the smoke," Maerin breathed. "Gideon. The incense. It's carrying the words for them."

"Then don't breathe deep."

"That's not how it works anymore. Look at them. They love it." Her hands were shaking. "That's what I can't—I served a god of small mercies, a god who took decades to answer a single honest prayer, and these people are getting miracles *wholesale*, and they aren't even asking. They're just—grateful. Gratitude without asking. Do you know what that is, Gideon? That's not worship. That's *harvest*."

The warden reached us. The smoke curled toward my face and I let it, because refusing would mark us faster than anything, and I felt the canticle rise in my own throat like a burp, involuntary, obscene—and I bit down on it. Thirty years of keeping a seal teaches you to hold a muscle clenched past all comfort. I held my throat shut until the warden passed, and beside me Isolde did the same, her knuckles white on her walking staff, and Ryn coughed theatrically and claimed a lung fever, and Maerin—

Maerin sang it. One verse, clear and true, in the cadence of her old order, and the warden smiled at her with genuine warmth and waved her through, and afterward she spat into the gutter and wouldn't speak for an hour.

Inside the walls, Corvale was beautiful the way a spider's web is beautiful, and for the same reason.

* * *

We lodged in the Tanners' Reach, a rookery district on the east slope where the whitewash ran thin and the inscriptions on the doors were older and smaller—chalk marks, mostly, petitions rather than praises. Ryn called in three favors and got us a room above a dyer's shop that stank of urine and madder-root, which was perfect, because nobody lingers in a smell like that, including gods.

By evening we'd seen the economy.

You couldn't miss it. It ran openly, in the basilica shadow-markets, in the colonnades off the Square of the Answered. The poor of Corvale lined up—not for bread, though bread was given too, stamped with the crowned sun—but for *healing*. And the price was posted on boards in plain letters, because the crown had learned that honesty about the transaction made it holier, not worse.

*A fevered child: one year of memory.*

*A crooked spine straightened: three years.*

*The dying brought back from the door: five, and a song of praise besides.*

They sold willingly. That was the part that sat in my chest like swallowed ice. An old man ahead of us in the colonnade queue was negotiating with a gray-robed acolyte, haggling, actually *haggling*, trying to get his wife's blindness cured for two years instead of three, and the acolyte was kind about it, gentle, patient, offering him a payment plan—two years now, one at midwinter—and the old man wept with relief and shook his hand.

"What do they take?" Isolde asked quietly. She'd gone pale. "Exactly. Where does it go? What do they—"

"You've seen it," I said. "Ashenmere's survivors. The hollowed in the valley."

"No. Those were taken. This is different. These people are *choosing*—"

"Isolde." I turned her gently toward the alcove where the healed emerged. There was a curtain, and beyond the curtain a chair, and acolytes with a basin of water and a silver bell, and when the old man came out from behind that curtain his wife was holding his arm and sobbing with joy because she could see his face. And his face was fine. His eyes were wet and glad and empty as two coins dropped down a well.

He remembered his wife. He remembered he loved her—he said so, kissed her hands, laughed at his own good fortune. He just didn't remember *why* anymore, or when, or the winter they'd met, or the name of the village, or his mother's voice, or thirty years of small warm things that make a life weigh anything at all. He was lighter. Everyone who came through that curtain was lighter. They laughed easily. They stood straight. They praised the crowned sun with full throats and total sincerity, because gratitude was the one thing the god left in the account, always, every time. Interest paid on stolen principal.

"That's why the city loves it," I said. "Look at them. They're happy. The god takes the weight of a life and leaves the pleasure of one, and calls it grace, and the priests call it mercy, and the wardens call it order, and everyone gets what they want except the person who paid." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Thirty years I guarded a gate against this thing breaking *in*. It never needed to break in. It needed a market."

Maerin had drifted down the colonnade and come back with a handbill, one of hundreds plastered on every pillar. She smoothed it out with trembling fingers. It was printed in red ink, crowned sun at the top.

**THE KING'S MERCY FUND**, it read. **IN THIS SEASON OF PLENTY, HIS MAJESTY ALDRIC THE BELOVED HAS OPENED THE ROYAL RESERVE OF GRACE TO ALL FAITHFUL SUBJECTS. BRING YOUR SICK. BRING YOUR BROKEN. THE CROWN ANSWERS.**

Below it, in smaller type: *Donations of memory accepted at all parish offices. The generous shall be first in the kingdom.*

"He signed it," Isolde whispered. "That's his seal. That's his—that's his actual hand, he used to practice his signature for hours when we were children, he thought a king's name should look like a promise—" Her voice cracked and hardened in the same breath. "It's using him to sign receipts."

"It's using him to sign everything." Ryn jerked her chin across the square. "Look there. The reserve."

Beyond the basilica, built onto the palace hill itself, stood a new structure. I had assumed it was a granary—white, vast, windowless. But now, with the dusk lamps lit, I saw the procession: acolytes carrying basins like the ones behind the curtain, climbing the processional stairs in shifts, hour after hour, pouring something into the building's upper vents. From the vents rose a shimmer, faint as heat-haze, faint as breath on glass in winter.

Years. Decades. Whole childhoods, going into the royal reserve, and the reserve fed the palace, and the palace wore the king.

"That's where they're keeping it," Maerin said. "All of it. Stolen time doesn't vanish, Gideon—it pools. It's the richest substance in the world. That building holds more living years than any army ever did." Her eyes were wide and horrified and, beneath the horror, bright with the terrible clarity of someone recognizing scripture. "Do you know what you could do with that much? You could wake anything. You could hold anything forever. You could make a king who never dies and never doubts and never—" she stopped.

"Never forgets his sister," Isolde finished.

Nobody said anything. The bells rang vespers, a hundred towers out of sequence, and the crowd in the square knelt as one, ten thousand faces lifted toward the palace hill, and I watched them kneel and understood that we were not standing in a city that had been conquered. We were standing in a city that had been *fed to* something, one willing year at a time, and called the eating salvation.

* * *

We planned by candlelight in the dyer's loft, and the plan was thin, and we all knew it.

"The rite needs the bell, the relic, and the witness," I said, laying out what we had: the keeper's iron bell recovered from the flooded shrine, wrapped in wool in my pack; the relic—Vessany's fingerbone, cold as riverstone; and the witness, which was Maerin, willing and scarred. "What we don't have is the king. The thirteenth moon is nineteen nights out. We need Aldric away from the palace, away from the reserve, long enough to pull the crown's grip loose, and there is exactly one person alive who can get close enough to try."

They all looked at Isolde.

She stood at the loft's one window, looking out across the rooftops at the palace hill, at the windowless white reserve drinking its nightly river of years. In the candlelight her face was doing something complicated—grief and fury and a sister's stubborn arithmetic, counting the distance between here and her brother's throne room.

"There's a renewal ceremony," Ryn said slowly. "End of the month. The coronation renewal—Aldric re-crowns himself before the people, swears the oath fresh. Every year. Public, ritual, the whole court on display."

"And the crown," Maerin whispered. She'd gone very still. "In the old liturgy—the liturgy I served—the crown was the vessel. The answering god entered through the crown. If we could reach him during the ceremony, while the crown is *off*—there's a moment, between the lifting and the swearing, ninety heartbeats maybe, when the vessel stands empty—"

"Ninety seconds," Isolde said.

Everyone looked at her again.

"My brother used to count things," she said. "When he was nervous. Heartbeats. Steps. He counted the ninety seconds between the crowning and the oath every year of his reign, and last spring, when I still lived in the palace, he told me—" Her voice caught. She pushed through it. "He told me he'd begun to lose time. Minutes. Hours. He said, *Issy, if I ever look at you like a stranger, count for me.*"

She turned from the window. Candlelight moved on her face, and I saw the runaway princess burn away and something older stand up underneath, something with a knife's edge and a purpose.

"Then that's how we go in," she said. "Not smugglers, not heretics. Me. They'll let a princess through those doors—they'll *insist*. I'll get close to my brother, and I'll find out how much of him is left, and whether there's anything left to save." She looked at each of us in turn, and last of all at me, and her chin came up in a way I'd learn to recognize as the exact mirror of her brother's. "And if there isn't—if what's wearing him has eaten him down to the smile—then I want to know that too. Before we spend everything we have on a ghost."

Outside, the bells rang compline, and the city prayed in its sleep, and high on the palace hill the white reserve drank another night of other people's lives, patient as a granary, full as a wound.
