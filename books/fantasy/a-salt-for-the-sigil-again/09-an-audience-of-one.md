## 9. An Audience of One

The palace had not changed. That was the cruelest part.

Isolde had expected it to feel smaller, the way childhood houses shrink when you return to them grown. It didn't. The marble corridors ran long and white as bone, the same as when she was nine and racing Aldric past the scandalized chamberlains, the same as when she was sixteen and storming down them after their father's funeral, the same as three months ago when she'd fled them with a satchel of clothes and a letter she still couldn't explain. The palace was a thing that did not change, and she had built her whole life on the belief that she could.

Now she walked those corridors in borrowed silk, under her own name, and felt like a ghost haunting a house that remembered everyone but her.

"Princess Isolde." The chamberlain bowed at exactly the correct depth—no deeper than protocol required for a sister who had abandoned court, no shallower than courtesy demanded for royal blood. He had rehearsed this. They all had. "His Majesty will receive you in the Amber Room."

*Receive* her. As if she were an ambassador from a foreign power. As if she hadn't taught him to whistle through a blade of grass behind the stables until a groom threatened to tell their mother.

She smiled the way Maerin had drilled her—warm, penitent, slightly dim. "I'm grateful he'll see me at all."

"He is *gracious*, Highness." A pause, thin as a razor. "He has been much occupied with matters of faith."

Matters of faith. She kept the smile fixed while something cold crawled up her spine. In the weeks since they'd come down from Thornfell, since Ryn's tunnels and Gideon's grim maps and Maerin's whispered liturgies, Isolde had learned what that phrase meant now, in the capital. It meant the tithe wagons rolling in from the provinces heavy with grain that should have fed villages. It meant the prayer-houses glowing all night, full of people trading years of themselves for cures and harvests. It meant her brother's face on banners three stories tall, haloed in gold leaf, eyes that looked painted on.

They had argued for two days about whether she should come at all. Gideon had been against it—Gideon was against everything that involved risk, which was everything—and Ryn had been loudly, profanely against it, which meant roughly the same thing. But Maerin had sat with her hands folded and said, quietly, *the god knows your name already, child. Better it learns you're here by choice.* And Isolde had known, in the place where grief lived, that she would come regardless. She had run once. She was done running from her brother.

Even if her brother was gone.

* * *

The Amber Room smelled wrong.

That was the first thing. Not incense—she'd braced herself for incense, for the thick resinous smoke that clung to every prayer-house—but something underneath it, sweetish and faintly rotten, like fruit going over in a closed room. The amber panels along the walls caught the afternoon light and threw it back too warm, too gold, so that standing in the room felt like standing inside honey. Inside something's mouth.

Aldric sat at the far end beneath the great window, and for one heartbeat—one treacherous, hopeful heartbeat—he was simply her brother. Tall, thin in the way he'd always been thin, dark hair pushed back from his forehead, wearing the crown that had sat on their father's head for thirty years. He was looking out the window at the gardens where they used to play.

Then he turned, and the heartbeat died.

His eyes were fine. That was the horror of it. They weren't black or burning or hollow; they were brown and clear and kind, and they looked at her with polite interest, the way you look at a portrait of someone you've been told you should know.

"Sister." His voice was his voice. Warm. Mild. "You've come home. I'm glad."

"You remember me?" Her throat was dry. Behind her, the door had closed. No guards inside—the god was arrogant, or secure, or both. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, she thought she heard a sound like breathing.

"Of course I remember you." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and none of it was real. "You are Isolde. You left in the autumn. I have missed you."

*I have missed you,* said like a line from a play. Like a courtesy copied from etiquette. She crossed the floor slowly, watching him, cataloguing the differences the way Gideon had taught her to read a seal: *look for what's fresh, look for what's warm.* His hands lay folded on the armrests, perfectly still. Too still. People fidgeted. People shifted. Aldric had never in his life held still unless he was concentrating hard on something else—chess, usually, or pretending to listen to the treasurer.

"Aldric," she said. "Do you remember the winter we were twelve? When the lake froze early?"

Something flickered. Just at the edge of his expression—a twitch, a hesitation, like a candle guttering. "We skated," he said. "You fell."

"I fell because you pushed me."

"I would never push you." Smooth again. The flicker sealed over like ice refreezing. "Come. Sit. Tell me about the valley. I hear you've been keeping... rustic company."

She sat, perched on the edge of a chair opposite him, close enough to see the fine details: the faint shadows under his eyes, deeper than sleeplessness accounted for. The way the skin around his temples seemed slightly translucent, veined with gold, though that might have been the light. The crown sitting on his head like it had grown there, roots down into the skull.

"The company is good," she said carefully. "There's a woman who used to be a priestess. She remembers the old rites better than anyone alive."

"Old rites are dead rites." Pleasantly. "Dead things should stay buried. Don't you think?"

"I think some dead things aren't dead," Isolde said. "I think some things just wait."

For a moment the room went very quiet. Even the breathing in the walls seemed to hold itself. Aldric regarded her with those clear brown eyes, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense of looking into a well and seeing, far down at the bottom, a coin glinting—something small and bright and real, sunk impossibly deep.

And then he moved.

It wasn't a lunge. It was worse—it was slow, deliberate, his hand rising to grip the armrests, knuckles whitening, his whole body straining against itself like a man trying to surface from deep water. His face changed. The pleasantness drained out of it and what rushed in behind it was so nakedly, desperately *him* that Isolde's heart cracked straight down the middle.

"Izzy." Barely a whisper. "Izzy, listen. Ninety seconds. Maybe less. I don't know how long I get."

"Aldric—" She was on her feet without deciding to stand.

"Don't call for help. There's nothing wrong with me that help can fix." His jaw clenched; sweat beaded at his hairline beneath the crown. "It talks to me through my own voice. Do you understand? I pray and *it answers in my mouth*. I haven't spoken aloud in—I don't know how long—in words that were only mine. So listen. Listen fast."

She knelt in front of him, took his hands. They were ice. "I'm listening."

"It came through the coronation renewal. Father's rite. It was supposed to be a formality." His breath shuddered. "It's been building for years, Izzy, I think—I think Father knew, I found letters after, I burned them, God forgive me, I burned them because I was afraid—" His eyes squeezed shut. "Ninety seconds. Focus. The crown isn't metal anymore. It's a root system. Every ceremony waters it. Every prayer in the city feeds it through me. I can feel them, all of them, thousands of voices pouring in and it drinks through me and it's starting to drink *me*—"

"Then take it off." Her hands flew to the crown. "Let me—"

"No!" The word cracked out of him with such force that she flinched back. He was shaking. "No. It has to be done right or it kills me—or worse, it doesn't kill me, it just—loosens the fit. You can't pull a glove off halfway. It needs the unbinding. The gate. Gideon Villalobos—is that who you're with? I see it sometimes, when it sleeps. I see the mountain. I see the gate." His grip crushed her fingers. "Tell them the thirteenth moon. Tell them it knows about the thirteenth moon. Tell them—"

His voice hitched. Something in his face broke open, and suddenly he wasn't a king or a vessel or a hostage, he was just her brother, twenty-three years old and terrified, and tears were cutting clean lines down his cheeks.

"Izzy. Izzy, before I forget again. Listen to me." He leaned forward until his forehead nearly touched hers, and his whisper was ragged, urgent, the most honest sound she had heard in this gilded rotting room. "When it takes me back—and it will, any moment now—you have to promise me you won't waste time grieving. Promise me you'll be angry instead. Anger thinks clearly. Grief just sits down." A broken half-laugh. "That's what you told me at Father's funeral. You said, *we don't sit down, Aldric, we burn.*"

"We burn," she whispered.

"And Izzy—" His hands tightened on hers, trembling. "If it comes to it. If there's no other way. Cut the crown from my head. I mean it. I would rather die a man than live a glove. Swear it. Swear it on the lake, on the grass-whistle, on anything real—swear you'll do it if the unbinding fails."

"I swear." The words came out wrecked. "On everything real. I swear."

He exhaled, and for a moment he simply looked at her, memorizing her, she realized—she was doing the same thing. The shape of his eyebrows. The scar on his chin from falling out of the pear tree. The exact brown of his eyes, so she could carry it out of this room and recognize it later, wherever it surfaced next.

"Ninety seconds," he murmured. "It gave me ninety seconds. I think—I think it wanted me to say goodbye properly. It likes our grief. It eats it." His lips brushed her knuckles, a gesture from childhood, from when he'd knighted her with a wooden sword and kissed her hand with tremendous solemnity. "Go back to the others. Trust the priestess. Trust the smuggler. Trust Villalobos even when they lie to you—they will lie to you, they lie beautifully, it's how keepers survive. And Izzy—"

"Yes?"

"When I don't know you tomorrow—when I look at you like a stranger—don't believe it. It's not me. Find the ninety seconds. They're always there. Small. Hidden. Like coins at the bottom of a well." His eyes were already changing, the desperation receding behind glass, the tide coming back in. "Don't stop looking for the coins—"

The light in his face went out like a snuffed candle.

* * *

"—and how long you intend to stay with us, Sister. The court has missed your... presence."

Aldric sat back in his chair, hands folded, smiling his mild smile. The transition had taken less time than a blink. If she hadn't seen it, she would never have believed a human face could do that—empty and refill like a cup.

"A few days," Isolde said. Her voice held. She was distantly proud of that. "I've missed the capital."

"We are glad." He rose, courteous, and escorted her toward the door himself—an honor, the chamberlain hovering outside would note, a mark of restored family feeling. At the threshold he paused. "Sister. One more thing."

She turned. Hope flared stupidly, treacherously, in her chest.

"You left in autumn," Aldric said pleasantly. "But I find I cannot recall why. Isn't that strange? There's a hole where the reason should be." He tilted his head, and the afternoon light through the amber panels made his eyes gleam gold. "Do you remember why you left, Isolde?"

Every instinct screamed. This was a test, or a trap, or both. She thought of Maerin's instruction: *never lie to it outright. It tastes lies. Give it truths bent sideways.*

"I left because I was angry," Isolde said. "At Father's death. At being a princess. At everything." All true. All sideways. "I needed mountains. Mountains put things in proportion."

"Mm." He considered her for a long moment, and the room's sweet-rotten smell thickened, and somewhere in the walls the breathing resumed, slow and satisfied. Then he smiled, warm and clear-eyed and utterly false. "Mountains. Yes. I hope they helped."

"They're helping."

"Good." He kissed her cheek, and his lips were cold as riverbed stones. "Come to evening prayers tomorrow, Sister. You'll find such peace here now. Everyone does, eventually."

She walked out of the Amber Room with her spine straight and her heart in pieces, past the bowing chamberlain, down the white bone corridors that never changed, and she did not let herself shake until she reached the servants' stair, and then only briefly, one hand pressed flat against cold stone.

*Ninety seconds. Coins at the bottom of a well.*

She climbed toward the roof, where Ryn would be waiting with the escape route and a flask of something illegal, and by the time she reached the top step her grief had cooled, hardened, taken the shape he'd asked for. Anger. Clear-thinking anger.

Because he'd given her one more thing, in those ninety seconds, almost as an afterthought—words she'd turned over and over on the walk out, words that changed the shape of everything:

*Tell them it knows about the thirteenth moon.*

Which meant the god knew its deadline. Which meant the god was not waiting passively to be unbound. Which meant that somewhere between this palace and the Thornfell gate, between tonight and the dark of the thirteenth moon, the thing wearing her brother's smile was going to move first.

Isolde stepped out onto the roof into the cold wind, found Ryn's silhouette against the last light, and said, "We have a problem. Several. Get Gideon."

Below them, across the city, the prayer-houses began to glow for evening devotions—thousands of small golden lights, like a congregation of eyes opening.
