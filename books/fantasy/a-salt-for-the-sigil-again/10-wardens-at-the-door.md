## 10. Wardens at the Door

Ryn Sable had survived eleven years of smuggling by trusting three things: the weight of a coin, the sound of a lie, and the feeling of being watched.

The first two had never failed her. The third was screaming now.

She stood at the window of the safehouse above the dyer's shop in Lowmark, watching the street through a gap in the shutters, and the feeling crawled up her spine like ice water poured slow. Nothing moved. That was the problem. Lowmark never went quiet—not at this hour, not with the fish carts due and the taverns opening their doors to the evening shift. A street that empty wasn't empty. It was held.

"Maerin," she said, without turning from the glass. "How long since you last checked the ward-strings?"

The priestess looked up from the table where she'd been copying the rite's components onto rice paper thin enough to swallow. Candlelight caught the gray in her close-cropped hair. "An hour. Why?"

"Check them again."

Maerin didn't argue. That was one of the things Ryn had come to respect about her—no wasted breath, no wounded pride. She crossed to the doorframe where three threads hung dyed to match the wood: red for the alley, blue for the roofline, green for the sewer grate below. All three hung slack and unremarkable.

Then Maerin touched them, and Ryn saw it in her face before she said a word.

"The green's cold," Maerin whispered. "It shouldn't be cold."

Ryn was already moving. "Gideon. Upstairs. Now."

Gideon came down the ladder from the loft with the unhurried calm that had infuriated Ryn for weeks and reassured her more than she'd ever admit. They carried the first component of the unbinding wrapped in oilcloth against their chest—the keeper's relic, a sliver of gate-iron old enough that it hummed when Gideon slept too near it. Ryn had watched them check the wrapping eleven times a day like a woman counting a heartbeat.

"We're blown," Ryn said. "Sewer approach. Maybe more. We have minutes, if we're lucky, and I stopped believing in luck around the time I started believing in gods that answer prayers."

Gideon didn't panic. They listened—head tilted, eyes half-closed—and Ryn realized they were doing the thing Isolde had described once, half-drunk on cheap wine: *listening for the seal*, the way a keeper listens to the gate, feeling for warmth in the world's skin.

"They're here," Gideon said quietly. "More than six. And something with them that isn't warden-trained. It moves wrong."

"Wardens don't move wrong," Maerin said. "Wardens are just men with warrants."

"That's what frightens me." Gideon turned to Ryn, and there it was—the look she'd been dreading since Lowmark, since the moment she'd understood what family meant when you'd spent a decade making sure you had none. "Back way?"

"Flooded cistern under the dye works. It comes up behind the tannery. It stinks like a month of dead eels, but it runs clean of the main streets." She was already pulling her kit together: blades, smoke flasks, the coil of tarred rope she'd trusted longer than any person. "Isolde?"

"Gone an hour since. Market run." Maerin's voice tightened. "She'll walk back into it."

"Then someone meets her before she does." Ryn jerked her chin at the window. "I'll hold the front. You two take the cistern. Gideon keeps the iron; Maerin keeps the pages. Nobody argues, nobody plays the hero. I've done this a hundred times—delay, break contact, vanish. It's the only prayer I know how to answer."

"You've done it alone," Gideon said.

"Yeah." Ryn grinned, and it felt like the old grin, the one from the mountain pass, the one that had gotten them both nearly killed and then, somehow, saved. "And look how well that turned out for both of us."

* * *

They came through the door ninety seconds later, and they came wrong.

Not the battering ram, not the shouted warrant—wardens liked their paperwork, liked the theater of law. These came silent, through the front and the roof at once, and the ones who entered first moved with a smoothness no drilled soldier had. Their lantern-light didn't flicker. Their boots made no sound on the boards. And when the light found their faces, Ryn saw why Gideon had said *moves wrong*: their expressions were perfect. Too perfect. Calm as painted saints, eyes lit from somewhere behind.

Hollowed. Or near enough. The High Warden was using men the god had already begun to drink.

Ryn gave them the smoke flask and the whole shelf of dye vats, and the room went to color and chaos—crimson fog, lye-stink, screaming that might have been hers or theirs. She fought the way she ran cargo: low, fast, mean, all elbows and exits. A blade glanced off her shoulder guard. She put her knife into a wrist and felt no satisfaction, only arithmetic—*three down, how many left, how long can I make this doorway cost them.*

Long enough. It only had to be long enough.

She heard Gideon's boots hit the cistern ladder. Heard Maerin follow, pages clutched to her chest like a psalter. Heard the trapdoor bang.

And then she heard the voice.

"*Smuggler.*"

It came from the doorway, from a figure the smoke wouldn't touch—a tall man in warden black with silver braid, unhurried, his face half-lit and half-hollowed, one cheek smooth as wax. He looked at her the way merchants looked at ledgers.

"You're the one who carries messages. We know your routes. We know your debts." He extended a hand, palm up, courteous as a dance. "Walk away from them. Your ledger burns clean. Your sister in Saltmere gets her lungs healed—you've prayed for that every night for two years. We've heard."

The world narrowed to that open palm.

Two years of praying into a pillow so no one would hear. Two years of sending coin south to a girl who coughed blood into her apron and smiled about it. And here was a thing that had heard every word, priced every one, and now offered the receipt.

That was how she knew, finally and completely, what they were fighting. Not cruelty. *Commerce.*

"No," Ryn said.

The hollow-eyed warden sighed, almost gently. "Then bleed for them."

He was faster than he should have been. She got her parry up and it wasn't enough—the blade slid past her guard and across her ribs, a line of white heat that turned red before she finished gasping. She went down against the dye table, took a fistful of mordant powder, and threw it into his face, and while he reeled she rolled off the table, through the smoke, out the back and down into the dark.

* * *

The cistern water was waist-deep and freezing, and it hurt worse than the wound, which was saying something. Ryn followed the smell of dead eels through the dark with one hand pressed hard against her side, listening to her own breath come wet and short.

She found them at the tannery grate—Gideon hauling her up by the arm with a strength that had nothing to do with muscle, Maerin tearing her shirt open to look at the cut with a priestess's flat efficiency.

"Deep but clean," Maerin said. "Missed anything vital. You're lucky."

"I'm skilled," Ryn managed. "Luck's for people who pray."

Gideon laughed—one short, cracked sound that was half relief and half grief—and pressed their hands over the wound as if pressure could be a kind of promise. Behind them, Lowmark burned orange against the sky, and somewhere in that orange was a safehouse, a life's worth of gear, and a girl with a market basket who was going to walk home to ash.

"Isolde," Ryn rasped. "Someone has to warn—"

"We will," Gideon said. "But not tonight. Tonight we keep you alive, and we keep the iron moving." They lifted the oilcloth bundle, checked it for the twelfth time that day, and Ryn saw their hands shake—just once, just slightly, the first crack she'd ever seen in thirty years of keeper's stone.

"They offered me my sister," Ryn said quietly, because someone had to say it out loud. "That's how they fight. Not with fire. With receipts."

"I know," Gideon said. "I've been hearing offers for a month." Their jaw set. "Which means it knows our names now. All of them. It's done watching."

Above the tannery, bells began to ring—the wardens' call, spreading through Lowmark district, and beneath the bells, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, came a sound none of them could name and all of them felt in their teeth: something vast and patient turning its attention toward the road out of the city, toward Thornfell, toward the gate.

Toward wherever they would run next.

Maerin tied off the bandage and met Ryn's eyes. "We need the second component," she said. "And we need it before they find us again."

Ryn looked at her bleeding ribs, at the burning city, at the family she hadn't asked for and couldn't imagine losing—and started counting escape routes.
