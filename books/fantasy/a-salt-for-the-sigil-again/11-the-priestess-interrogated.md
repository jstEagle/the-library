## 11. The Priestess Interrogated

They took her boots first.

Maerin did not understand why until the third hour, when the cold of the flagstones had climbed through her feet into her ankles and begun its slow argument with her knees. It was an old technique—older than the crown, older than the liturgy she'd once served. Discomfort that never quite became pain. The body kept waiting for the pain to start, and the waiting wore grooves in a person.

The cell was beneath the Wardenhall, which meant it had been a temple once. She could see it in the stonework: the vaulting cut for acoustics, so that hymns would bloom; the shallow niche in the wall where a votive flame would have stood, now bricked over but still faintly smoke-stained, as if the wall itself remembered burning. They had built their cells out of the bones of the old faith and called it justice.

She sat on the bench—they had allowed her that much—and worked her fingers against the manacles, not to escape, but to keep them honest. Circulation. Feeling. A priestess of the Ninth Choir learned early that the body was an instrument, and instruments were tuned before they were played.

*They will play you,* she told herself. *Let them find you in tune.*

The lock turned at the fourth hour.

The High Warden came in without a lantern, which told her two things: he wanted her to know he could see in the dark, and he did not want anyone else to see his face. He closed the door behind him with the softness of a man entering a sickroom.

"Maerin Vael," he said. "Defrocked. Heretical. Author of three commentaries on the Hollow Liturgy, all burned by your own order." He settled onto the stool across from her, unhurried. "You wrote beautifully, for a heretic."

"You read my work," Maerin said. "I'm almost flattered."

"I memorized it." His voice was level, courteous, and wrong—the way a room is wrong when someone has just left it. "That's why I'm here instead of the questioners. The questioners ask what you did. I want to know what you *understood*."

In the dark, she studied him. High Warden Corvane—she knew the name from Ryn's whispered briefings, from Isolde's careful intelligence gathered in palace corridors. A tall man gone stooped at the shoulders, as if carrying something heavy against his chest. And there, when the light from the door-slit crossed his face: the eyes. Too bright. Too wet. The shine of a man whose pupils had been polished from the inside.

Half-hollow. She had seen it in the valley worshippers, in the ones who gave a little and kept a little. The hollowness started at the eyes. It always started at the eyes, because that was where you looked when you prayed, and prayer was a door, and doors opened both ways.

"You're praying right now," she said quietly. "Aren't you. Even sitting here. Some part of you is always saying the words."

His hands, folded in his lap, went very still.

"Careful," he said.

"It's not an accusation. It's a diagnosis." She leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "I served the Ninth Choir for twenty years, Warden. I know what devotion looks like when it stops being chosen. You have the cadence of a man who can't stop. So let's skip the part where you pretend to be the crown's instrument and tell me what you actually serve. Because it isn't Aldric. And it isn't the law."

For a long moment the only sound was water ticking somewhere in the old temple walls, finding its way down through stone that remembered being holy.

Then Corvane laughed—a small, exhausted sound, like a hinge giving way.

"They said you were dangerous," he murmured. "The reports. *Dangerous rhetorician. Do not permit public speech.*" He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and when his hands dropped, the brightness in them had guttered, just slightly, like a candle deciding whether to go out. "You want to know what I serve. Very well. But knowledge has a price in this kingdom now. You know that better than anyone. You've seen the valley."

"I've seen what it leaves," Maerin said. "Hollowed worshippers. People who traded pieces of themselves for miracles and couldn't remember what the pieces were."

"And do you know *why*?" He rose, paced to the bricked-over votive niche, laid one palm flat against it as if taking a pulse. "No. Of course not. Your order burned the true liturgy centuries ago and kept only the parts that made people obedient. You never learned the name."

"The name of what?"

He turned. In the dark, his face was half shadow and half that terrible inner light, and for the first time she saw that he was afraid—not of her, not of the crown, but of the thing he carried.

"*Vethruel*," he said.

The name went through the cell like cold water through cloth. Maerin felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her defrocking, in the place behind her sternum where her vows used to live. Not because the name was loud—it was barely a whisper—but because it fit. It slid into all the empty shapes the last weeks had carved in her understanding, the way a key slides home, and everything she'd seen—the blooming crops, the walking shadows, the king wearing his smile like a borrowed glove—clicked into alignment around it.

"Vethruel," she repeated, testing it. "That's not in any canon I know."

"No. It predates the canons. It predates the *gates*." Corvane sat again, and now he spoke quickly, the words tumbling like a man confessing after decades of silence. "Your order called the things behind the seals *old gods*. Comfortable word. Gods can be propitiated. Gods can be bargained with. Vethruel isn't a god. It's a hunger that learned to wear the shape of one. It was starving when the first keepers sealed it away, and it has been starving for a thousand years behind Thornfell, and starvation—" his voice caught, steadied— "starvation teaches patience."

"Torture teaches confession," Maerin said dryly. "Is this the torture? The truth?"

"This is the price." He held up his hand, and in the dark she saw it clearly at last: the fingers were translucent at the tips, faintly luminous, like wax held before a flame. "Every time I speak of it truly, it takes something. That's how it feeds. Not blood. Not bone. *Certainty.* Memory. The weight of a person. Every miracle it grants is paid in exactly that—the thing the supplicant can least afford to lose. The shepherd who was healed lost his shadow, because his shadow was where he kept his grief for his dead wife, and grief was the heaviest thing he owned. The farmer whose fields bloomed overnight paid in the memory of every harvest he'd ever earned honestly. It doesn't take randomly, priestess. It takes *precisely*. It takes what hurts most to lose, because that's where the nourishment is."

Maerin sat very still. Somewhere above them, boots crossed stone; somewhere beyond the walls, the city slept in mandatory piety, selling its years for healing, and she had thought she understood the horror of it. She had not. She had seen the ledger's entries without seeing the ledger's logic.

"Why does it want certainty?" she asked. "Why memory?"

"Because it was unmade once." Corvane's voice dropped. "This is the oldest fragment of the liturgy, the one even the Ninth Choir never had. Before the gates, before the seals, there was a war—not of armies, of *names*. The first peoples unmade a devourer by taking its name apart and hiding the pieces in mortal keeping, in oaths, in songs, in the memories of those sworn to guard them. A name scattered cannot be spoken. A name unspoken cannot command. Vethruel has spent a thousand years reassembling itself, syllable by syllable, and every memory it devours is a piece of its own name coming home." He looked at her, and the wet brightness of his eyes was suddenly unbearable. "It doesn't eat us because it hates us, Maerin Vael. It eats us because we are its missing alphabet."

* * *

They came for her at the turn of the watch.

Not Corvane—the questioners this time, four of them, with their instruments laid out on a cloth with the neatness of surgeons. Maerin had expected this. Truth freely given was worth more than truth extracted, and whatever game Corvane was playing, the machine he served did not pause for his confessions.

They began with her hands.

She would not give them the satisfaction of a full account—not here, not in her own mind, where every detail surrendered was a detail she'd have to carry forever. She fixed instead on the tuning: breath slow, spine straight, the pain arriving wave after wave while she named it silently, *this is my hand, this is fire, this is my hand*, refusing to let the body's screaming become the self's. A priestess of the Ninth Choir had been taught that suffering was a liturgy too, if you refused to lie during it.

What she had not been taught—and what she learned in the white hours between questions—was that pain loosened the tongue in ways willpower could not always govern. Fragments surfaced. Corvane's voice. *We are its missing alphabet.* The shape of the name in her mouth. And threaded through it, unwelcome, illuminating: the letter Isolde had carried, Aldric's handwriting that Aldric didn't remember. If the god reassembled itself through stolen memory, then a king who forgot his sister was not merely a symptom. He was a page being rewritten. Every person Vethruel hollowed was a line of its name restored—and Aldric, crowned, anointed, prayed over by a whole city, was the largest manuscript it had ever held.

Which meant the unbinding rite Gideon sought was not destruction at all. It was *unwriting*. And it would require knowing the name entire—all the scattered pieces—or the seal would close on a half-spoken word and hold for another thousand years while the hunger learned patience again.

"Who else knows the name?" the chief questioner asked, for the tenth time. "The keeper. The girl. The smuggler. Names, priestess."

"You're asking me to give you their names," Maerin said, through blood and the taste of copper and something strangely like laughter, "to a god that eats names. Do you hear yourself? You're not wardens anymore. You're *couriers*."

The blow that followed was almost a relief. Pain with a simple grammar. She rode it down into the dark, and in the dark, Corvane was waiting—which was to say, she found him there when she woke, alone with her in the cell again, kneeling beside the bench with a cup of water he did not offer, because offering kindness before questioning was technique, and this, she sensed, was not technique.

"You're dying by inches," she rasped at him. "I can see it. Whatever it promised you—"

"It promised me nothing." His voice was ash. "I prayed to it *before* it ever spoke to me. Twenty-two years ago, when my daughter was fevered, I prayed to anything that would listen. I didn't know the gate was thin. I didn't know prayers traveled." He set the cup down untouched. "It healed her. It took my wife's face from my memory—I woke and loved a woman I could no longer picture. And I thanked it. Do you understand? I *thanked* it. That gratitude is the chain. Every day since, some part of me has knelt without choosing to, and fed it without meaning to, and enforced its law believing it was mine." He finally looked up, and Maerin saw the last thing she'd expected in the High Warden's face: a plea. "You wrote that the hollowed could be called back. Chapter nine of your second commentary. *The emptied vessel remembers the shape of its filling.* Tell me it wasn't heresy. Tell me there's a rite."

Maerin studied him for a long moment—the most powerful hunter in the kingdom, kneeling in a cell among the instruments of his own trade, asking a defrocked prisoner for absolution or at least arithmetic.

"There's a rite," she said. "It's hard. It costs more than it should. Everything true does, now."

"Will it save my daughter?"

"She was healed at a price. What did she lose?"

Corvane's hands closed slowly in his lap. "Her fear," he whispered. "She has never been afraid since. Not of fire, not of deep water, not of me. She's eleven."

Maerin closed her eyes. *It takes precisely.* A child without fear was not healed; she was *hollowed at the root*, and the root was the oldest part of the name. "Then yes," she said quietly. "There's a rite. And when my people come—if they come—you will open this door yourself, Warden, and you will stand between me and your own questioners, and that act will cost you everything you have left. That's the fee. I don't set the prices in this kingdom anymore. I just refuse to pay them lying down."

For the first time, the High Warden smiled, and it was a terrible, human, broken thing.

"They're already coming," he said. "The keeper and the others. They've gone to ground at Thornfell—I've read the patrol reports. And the god knows it. Vethruel has stopped hunting them, Maerin. Do you understand what that means? It has stopped hunting them because it wants them at the gate. It wants the keeper inside the seal." He rose, and the wet light in his eyes flared like something surfacing. "The thirteenth moon is in nineteen days. And it isn't preparing a defense."

He paused at the door, his hand on the iron.

"It's preparing a *feast*."

The lock turned behind him. Maerin sat alone in the dark of the old temple, her ruined hands cradled against her chest, and began—to the rhythm of her own heartbeat, to the cadence of a liturgy only she remembered—to commit the name *Vethruel* to the one place it could not reach.

Not to memory. Memory could be eaten.

To *meaning*. To the reason she would whisper it, nineteen days from now, standing at a sealed gate with a bell in one hand and a family at her back:

so that it could be unwritten forever.
