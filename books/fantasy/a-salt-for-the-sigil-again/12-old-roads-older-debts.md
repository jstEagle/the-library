## 12. Old Roads, Older Debts

They traveled by night and slept in the hollows of the day, the way Gideon had learned to move thirty years ago when the mountain still had teeth. Ryn rode slung across her mule like cargo she didn't trust herself to carry upright, the warden's blade having opened three ribs on her left side and sewn them shut again with the smuggler's own needlework—crooked, ugly, holding. Isolde walked beside her without being asked, one hand hovering near the bandages as if proximity alone could keep the stitches from failing.

Gideon led.

The road up into Thornfell was not a road anymore. It had been one once—flagstones laid by hands that believed mountains could be persuaded, switchbacks cut with ceremony, wayshrines every league where travelers had left salt and small iron bells. Now the flagstones were teeth of bone under moss, and the shrines were cairns, and the only traffic was weather. Gideon knew every stone anyway. Knew which ones shifted in thaw, which ones sang when the wind came off the high pass at the right angle, which one, two miles below the gate, had been split by lightning the night their keeper died.

They did not speak much. Speaking cost breath, and breath was thin up here, and everything any of them might say was already sitting between them like a fourth traveler: *Maerin is in the cells. The High Warden knows your faces. The god knows your names.*

It was on the third night, camped in the shell of a wayshrine with a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, that Isolde finally broke the silence.

"You've been here before."

Not a question. Gideon fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch.

"Once or twice," they said.

"Bullshit." Ryn's voice came out sanded rough by pain. She'd been half-asleep against her pack; now her eyes were open, fixed on Gideon with the flat attention she gave to cargo manifests and lies. "You walk this road like you're walking through your own house in the dark. You haven't looked at a single fork. You put your hand on that lightning-split stone back at the second switchback like you were greeting an old dog." She shifted, winced, kept going. "I've run this mountain for eleven years, Villalobos. I never saw you on it before this winter. But the mountain's seen you plenty."

Isolde pulled her cloak tighter. "We nearly died getting that bell," she said quietly. "Maerin's rotting in a cell because we ran. I think we've earned the truth about what we're carrying it toward."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound almost like breathing.

Gideon looked at the two of them—the runaway princess with frost still in her hair, the smuggler gray-lipped and stubborn as a burr—and felt the old loneliness shift its weight inside their chest. Thirty years they had carried this story alone. They had told it to no one, not even to themselves in words, only in the shape of their days: the rounds, the sigils, the salt, the silence. A life built over a grave, furnished like someone meant to come home.

Maybe that was why they spoke. Or maybe it was simpler: the bell was heavy, the moon was turning, and dead weight shared is lighter than dead weight carried.

"All right," Gideon said. "But let me start at the beginning, because the beginning isn't what the valley thinks it is."

* * *

"The valley remembers the gods waking as a storm," Gideon said. "A bad season. The kind of thing grandmothers blame for blighted crops and dead lambs. That's wrong. It wasn't weather. It was hunger with a direction."

Isolde sat very still. Ryn had stopped pretending to rest.

"There were seven gates in the old days. Not doors—*gates*. Places where the world wore thin, where the old things pressed against the skin of it like moths against a lamp. The first keepers didn't seal them out of hate. Sealing was mercy, both directions. The gods were starving even then; worship had thinned to villages, villages to families, families to one old woman with a bowl of salt. And a starving god doesn't ask. It takes. Prayer answered at the price of the pray-er. Miracles paid in bone." Gideon turned their hands over, palms up, and the firelight caught the old scars there, fine white lines like script. "So the keepers bound them. Sigil by sigil, gate by gate. Six of the seven held quiet within a generation. People forgot. Forgetting was the point."

"And the seventh?" Isolde asked, though Gideon could see she already knew. She'd grown up in a palace full of histories; she'd simply never been taught this history, which meant someone had made sure it stayed unwritten.

"Thornfell." Gideon said the word the way you'd set down something fragile. "The strongest of the old things. The hungriest. The one whose name Maerin served before she understood what she was serving." They drew a slow breath. "Thirty-two years ago, it woke. Not all at once—a crack, a seep. Prayers answered in the high valleys. Shadows walking off without their owners. The crown sent soldiers; the soldiers prayed for victory and got it, and forgot their children's names on the march home. It feeds through faith, but it eats through *love*. That's the part no one writes down. It takes what you hold dearest and calls the taking grace."

Ryn's hand had drifted unconsciously to her bandaged ribs. "And the keeper," she said. "The one before you."

"Sera Vane." The name came out of Gideon worn smooth from thirty years of handling in silence. "My teacher. My—" They stopped. Started again, flatter. "She took me as an apprentice when I was fourteen. An orphan off the valley roads, good at memorizing, too stubborn to die of the winters. She taught me the sigils, the salts, the rites of watching. She taught me that a keeper's job is not to fight the god. Keepers don't fight. We *fast*. We stand at the gate and offer it nothing—not fear, not hope, not love—and a fasted god starves. That's all the seal ever was. Not a wall. A diet."

"And then it woke," Isolde said.

"And then it woke." Gideon stared into the fire. "It went for the valleys first. Then it found the palace—it's always known how to find power; power prays loudest. Sera rode down to turn it back and realized halfway there that she couldn't. The binding needed more than rites. It needed a keeper *inside* the seal. Willing. Permanent. The sigils drink a soul slowly, forever, the way a wick drinks oil. That's the price of a true sealing. There's no rite around it. There's no cleverness. Someone stands in the door and becomes the lock."

The wind gusted through the broken shrine, scattering sparks. Nobody moved to feed the fire.

"She didn't tell me," Gideon said. "That's the part I've hated her for, all these years, and the part I understand completely, and I have never once managed to make those two things sit together. She told me she was riding to the low passes to negotiate. She told me to keep the watch. She kissed my forehead like a mother—which she was, in every way that counted—and she walked up this road past these shrines, and she stood inside the gate, and she spoke the words, and the sigils drank her." Their voice did not break. It did something worse; it went level, the levelness of a floor over a cellar. "I felt it happen. From the watchpost. Every sigil warming one after another, like fingers closing. I ran the whole way and arrived in time to see the last light of her go into the stone. The gate sealed. The god starved back behind it. And I stood there, fourteen years old, keeper of Thornfell by inheritance, with salt on my belt and no one left in the world who knew my name except a woman who couldn't say it anymore."

Silence. The fire burned down a fraction.

"So when I tell you the unbinding will cost memories," Gideon said, "when I tell you each sigil drinks a turn of what I am—I'm not guessing. I know exactly what that stone does. I watched it eat the person I loved most, grain by grain, for the sake of everyone she'd never meet." They finally looked up. "That's the debt I inherited. That's the road. Any questions?"

Ryn was the one who answered, and she answered by throwing a stick at Gideon's head.

It caught them on the shoulder, light as an insult. "You absolute mule," she rasped. "Thirty years. Thirty *years*, and you let us think you were just some grim old hermit with a salt hobby. You could have said something at the shrine. At the flooded temple. When the god offered you—" She stopped, recalculating, and Gideon saw the moment it landed: the offer at the drowned shrine, the thing Gideon had refused and never explained. "It offered you *her*, didn't it. It offered you Sera Vane back."

"It offered me everything I'd lost," Gideon said. "Which was her."

"And you said no." Ryn's voice cracked on it—pain and fury and something underneath both that she'd deny to her grave. "With your whole heart standing right there in the water, and you said no, because you're the lock now too. You've been the lock since you were fourteen." She lay back against her pack, grimacing, done with the effort of feeling so hard. "Fine. Fine. But you don't get to die polite about it this time, Villalobos. Whatever the unbinding costs—you tell us first. All of it. Before the moon turns. Swear it."

"I swear," Gideon said, and found they meant it, and found that meaning it was its own strange relief.

Isolde hadn't spoken. She sat with her knees drawn up, staring at the dark mouth of the pass above them, where the gate waited invisible in the night. When she finally turned, her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady.

"My brother is wearing a glove," she said, "and the hand inside it is the same thing that ate your teacher. And you've spent thirty years alone at the top of this mountain keeping it starved while the whole kingdom forgot you existed." She swallowed. "No more. Whatever happens at the thirteenth moon—whatever the sigils take—you will not stand in that door unaccompanied. That's not a request. That's the family talking."

Gideon opened their mouth to explain—kindly, carefully—that the rite required a keeper alone, that witnesses were for funerals—

And stopped.

Because Maerin's warning, relayed in a smuggler's coded letter before the raid, had been specific: *the unbinding requires a keeper inside the seal, one turn of memory per sigil.* Three sigils. Three turns. But Maerin had also written, in the cramped margin she used for things she wasn't sure she believed: *the old liturgy names a witness. The texts disagree on whether the witness must be outside the door.*

The texts disagreed. And Gideon had spent thirty years assuming the worst reading, because the worst reading was the one that matched their grief.

"We'll need Maerin for that answer," Gideon said at last, and heard their own voice change—heard the door in it open a crack. "Which means we get her back."

"We get her back," Isolde said. Not a question either.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, moving in the blue-black hour when the mountain forgave footprints. Gideon led them up past the lightning-split stone, past the sixth shrine, past the place where the flagstones ended and the true approach began—the long bare ramp of granite where nothing grew, not because the soil was poor but because Sera Vane's sealing had salted it, deliberately, a moat of barrenness a mile wide.

Halfway up the ramp, Ryn stopped.

"Gideon." Her voice was strange. "Your shadow."

Gideon looked down. The pre-dawn light was thin enough that shadows barely existed—but theirs was there, thrown long and faint by the rising moon behind them. And at its edge, at the trailing fringe where a shadow frays into the ground, something was *pulling*. A thread-thin darkness, stretching up the ramp toward the gate, like smoke drawn into a chimney. Like a leash running taut.

Toward the seal. Toward the soul soaked into it.

The god had noticed them at the flooded shrine. Now it was doing arithmetic.

"It can smell her in me," Gideon said quietly. "What the gate took. What's left of the taking." They flexed their hand, and the shadow flexed with it, and the thread trembled like a plucked string. "It's learning what I'll pay."

Isolde stepped closer, putting her body between Gideon's shadow and the gate as if geometry could argue with a god. "Then it should also learn," she said, "what it's up against."

Above them, in the gray light, the Thornfell gate came into view—and every sigil carved across its face was glowing. Faintly. Warmly. Like embers remembering fire.

All seven of them.

Even the ones that had been cold for thirty years.

Even the one shaped, unmistakably, like an open hand—reaching out.
