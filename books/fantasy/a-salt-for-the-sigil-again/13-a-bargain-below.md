## 13. A Bargain Below

The prison beneath the Warden's Hall did not smell of blood. That was the first thing Isolde noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. It smelled of incense.

Frankincense and something sweeter underneath—honey left too long in the sun, going to rot. The sweetness clung to the stone stairs as she descended behind the laundry cart, clinging to her skin, her hair, the inside of her mouth. She had grown up in a palace full of perfumes designed to make lies pleasant. She knew this one. This was what grace smelled like when it was being sold by weight.

She kept her head down between the folded linens and counted doors. Maerin had been taken six days ago. Six days of Gideon pacing the safehouse like a caged animal, of Ryn's bandaged ribs seeping through the wrappings while she swore through her teeth that she could still walk, still fight, still climb. Six days of Isolde sitting alone with the only weapon she had ever owned: her name.

*Princess Isolde Villalobos.* Dead to the palace for three years. Erased from the rolls, struck from the succession, mourned in secret by exactly one person—who no longer remembered mourning her.

Her name was worth nothing to her brother now. But names were currency below, and below was where she'd come to spend it.

The cart stopped. Hands lifted linens away, and Isolde looked up into the face of a warden.

Not the hollow ones. She had learned to see them now—the way their eyes shone a half-second after they should have moved, the way they smiled with all their teeth but none of their intent, the way they prayed without moving their lips, constantly, like men scratching at an itch that lived under the skin. The High Warden's chosen were easy to spot once you'd seen one up close. They were puppets pretending very hard to be men.

This one was not pretending. This one looked *tired*, in the deep way, the way Gideon looked tired—thirty years tired compressed into a younger face. Grey threaded his temples under the warden's helm. A scar split his chin. And when he looked at her, he did not look away, and he did not smile.

"Captain Voss," he said quietly, to the air more than to her. Then, to the laundry-master: "Leave us."

* * *

They met an hour later in a records room that smelled of dust and old ink, because even traitors needed somewhere no one listened. Voss bolted the door himself, then stood with his back against it, arms crossed, studying her the way a man studies a contract he already intends to sign but wants to hate first.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. "If the High Warden learns a princess walked into his cells disguised as linen, he won't ransom you. He'll hollow you. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand," Isolde said. "I've seen the poor sell memories in the prayer-markets. I've seen what comes out the other side." She set both hands flat on the table between them, because her father had taught her that flat hands read as honesty, and she had learned since that honesty performed well enough until the real thing arrived. "Then let me give you a reason to help me anyway."

"I don't help heretics."

"No," Isolde agreed. "You help the law. That's why I came to you instead of anyone else in this building." She let a silence stretch, long enough to itch. "Captain, how many answered prayers were logged in the capital last month?"

His jaw tightened. "Four hundred. Give or take."

"And how many of those who received them are still whole?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She had spent two weeks in the lower city learning the arithmetic: four hundred miracles, and of the recipients, sixty-three missing, eleven dead of nothing any physician could name, and the rest walking around with gaps behind their eyes where pieces of themselves used to live. A mother who no longer recognized her own children but wept at the sight of them anyway. A cooper who signed over his workshop for the healing of a wound he couldn't remember receiving.

"The crown isn't suppressing the old god," Isolde said softly. "It's running his shop. Every miracle logged is a tithe collected. My brother's court has turned faith into an economy, and the coin is people." She leaned forward. "You know it. You've known it for months. So my question isn't whether you hate it, Captain. It's what you're willing to do about a prisoner in your cells who can tell us exactly which rites the god is using—and how to starve him."

Voss was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, gone rough at the edges. "I took this post because I believed in the wardens. Twenty-two years. I burned villages for the crown because the writs said heresy, and I told myself the law was the law." His hand drifted to his sword hilt—not reaching, just resting, the way a man touches an old wound. "Last spring I executed a girl of fifteen in Harrowmere. Her crime was praying for rain during a drought. It rained. She healed a dying field with it." His eyes came up, and there was something in them Isolde recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person deciding what they are willing to become. "The field bloomed overnight. The village called it a blessing. I called it a warrant."

"So you'll do it."

"I'll get your priestess out of the cells," Voss said. "On one condition. Not yours. Mine." He straightened off the door. "When this is done—whatever this becomes—I want to see it finished. No running to some border province. If there's a way to cut this out of the crown, I want to be holding the knife. I've got forty soldiers under my command who've watched the hollowed poor get loaded onto wagons like cordwood. Half of them pray to nothing at all anymore, because they're afraid of what answers." He extended his hand across the table. "Justice outside the law is still treason, Princess. I need to know you understand the price before I shake it."

Isolde looked at his hand. She thought of Aldric's face in the throne room—lucid for ninety seconds, begging her to cut the crown from his head before he forgot her again. She thought of the letter in his handwriting that he didn't remember writing. She thought of Gideon, who had already paid thirty years for other people's sins and was preparing to pay more.

"My brother is a prisoner wearing a crown," she said. "The thing that holds him made a bargain with my family's throne before I was born. Everything lawful ended a long time ago, Captain. We're just the first ones saying it out loud."

Voss shook her hand. His grip was cold and steady as a key turning.

* * *

The rescue went wrong in every way Isolde had planned for it not to, and right in every way that mattered.

Voss's plan was simple, because simple things could be explained to soldiers without paper. At the change of the midnight watch, the cells' eastern gate would report a breach drill—a fire in the records annex, prisoners to be moved as precaution. Moving prisoners meant opening cell doors. Opening cell doors meant, if one happened to be standing ready in the corridor of the deep block with a stolen warden cloak and Voss's own signet ring, that a certain defrocked priestess might walk out among the condemned and simply keep walking.

What the plan did not account for was the priestess herself.

Isolde heard her before she saw her—singing. Down in the deepest tier, past the ordinary cells with their drunks and debtors, down where the walls sweated sweet rot, a voice rose thin and cracked through the dark. It was a liturgy. Isolde didn't know the words, but she knew the shape of it, the rising-falling cadence that Maerin must have sung ten thousand times in another life, in another order, before the order was ash. She was singing it hoarse, defiantly, a hymn as a wall against whatever whispered to her through the stones.

The corridor was dim. Torches burned low, and the few wardens on duty moved with the wrongness Isolde had learned to dread—too smooth, too quiet, heads tilted as though listening to something just behind the world. Voss had warned her: the deeper tiers were staffed by the High Warden's chosen. Avoid their eyes. Do not speak near them. Their master heard through them the way a spider hears through its web.

So Isolde walked the corridor with her hood up and her heart hammering, past cells where prisoners pressed to the bars with hollow eyes, past a cell where a man knelt facing the wall, whispering thanks for a healing he no longer remembered wanting—and reached the last door on the left, where the singing stopped mid-verse.

Maerin stood at the bars. Six days in a hole, and she stood like a pillar. Her hair had gone wild, her wrists were ringed with burns where manacles had been, and there was blood at the corner of her mouth—but her eyes were clear, ferociously clear, the clearest thing in that entire underground.

"You," she breathed. "You're supposed to be safe. Where's Gideon? Where's Ryn?"

"Recovering. Planning. Hating every second that I'm down here instead of them." Isolde fitted Voss's key to the lock. "We're getting you out."

"They asked me questions." Maerin's voice dropped as the lock clicked. She stepped out slowly, testing her legs. "For six days. And I gave them nothing, but Isolde—" Her hand closed around Isolde's wrist, hard, desperate. "I learned things anyway. Things they said in front of me because they thought I was broken. The god's name. Its hunger. Why it wears kings." Her eyes were wet and burning at once. "We have to get to Thornfell. There's less time than Gideon thinks. Less than half."

"Tell me on the road. Right now we walk."

They made it forty feet.

A figure detached itself from the shadows at the corridor's end—one of the smooth-moving ones, helm gleaming, and beside it two more, silent as spilled milk. The lead warden's head tilted. Listening. Then it smiled, all teeth, none of intent, and spoke in a voice like a bell rung underwater.

"The princess," it said. "The king's sister. He asks after you sometimes, in the quiet hours. He misses the shape of you."

Isolde felt Maerin go rigid beside her. Her own hand found the knife at her belt—Ryn's knife, borrowed, its grip worn soft—and she thought, absurdly, of her brother at seven years old, teaching her to skip stones on the palace lake, laughing when hers sank.

*That boy is still in there,* she reminded herself. *Ninety seconds at a time. Fight for the ninety seconds.*

"Captain Voss!" Her voice rang off the stones, loud, deliberate, buying seconds. "Breach in the east annex! Your orders were—"

The false wardens moved. And then the corridor erupted, because Voss had never trusted the deep tier to hold to schedule—he had posted six of his own men at the stairhead, honest soldiers with honest fear on their faces, and they came down at a run with cudgels and lanterns and the sheer ungainly courage of men doing the right thing for the first time in years. It was not a battle. It was barely a fight. But it was loud, and noise meant witnesses, and witnesses meant the clock had started whether they were ready or not.

Voss himself appeared out of the smoke of a dropped lantern, seized Isolde by the shoulder, and shoved them both toward the stair. "Laundry gate. Now. Go!"

Maerin stumbled; Isolde caught her; together they ran, up through tiers of shouting and torchlight, past honest soldiers holding a line against the smooth-moving things in warden's armor, past Voss bellowing orders that were technically legitimate and entirely fiction. At the laundry gate, the night air hit them like cold water, and then they were running through the wet streets, three shadows among hundreds, while bells began to toll behind them—not alarm bells, Isolde realized with a chill. The cathedral bells, ringing the hour at midnight, though midnight was hours past.

The city was waking up to pray. And something in it had been told their names.

* * *

The safehouse received them like a held breath finally let out. Ryn got up off her cot despite the stitches, swore fluently, and hugged Maerin hard enough to hurt. Gideon simply stood in the doorway for a long moment, looking at Maerin as though confirming she was real, and then said, very quietly, "You're thinner."

"They fed me," Maerin said. "Mostly gruel. Occasionally questions." Her attempt at a smile collapsed almost immediately. She sat down heavily at the table, and everyone gathered, and the candle guttered, and she told them.

"The god has a true name. *Vessagarath.* The Hollow Shepherd—it was called that once, kindly, by shepherds who thought it guarded their flocks. It doesn't want worship. Worship is just the shape hunger takes. What it eats is *selfhood*. Memory, love, name, grief—the parts of a person that make them a person. It eats those and gives back miracles, the way a lender gives back coins." Her voice shook. "And it was bound the first time by a keeper who fed it willingly. Gideon—that's what your mentor did. That's what the seal is. A meal that never ends."

Silence. Gideon's face did something terrible and still.

"It gets worse," Maerin said. "The binding weakens every thirteen turns unless renewed. And the renewal rite—the unbinding, the one that frees Aldric—requires a keeper inside the seal. One memory per sigil. Three sigils, Gideon." She reached across the table and gripped their hand. "Three turns of everything you are."

Gideon nodded slowly, as though they had known all along, as though they had been counting the cost in the dark for thirty years. Perhaps they had.

It was Ryn who broke the silence, her voice flat with a smuggler's arithmetic. "Then we've got one moon and a king buried in a sanctum under the palace. How in all the hells do we get to him?"

It was Voss who answered—standing in the doorway where he had followed them, soaked to the knees, having crossed the city through streets that were no longer safe for him either. Behind him, in the rain, waited a dozen soldiers who had chosen treason with their boots still muddy from it.

"There's a ceremony," Voss said. "In twelve days. The Renewal of the Coronation—every year, the king re-anoints his crown before the assembled court. The whole sanctum opens for it. Everyone who matters, in one room, with the king at the center." His jaw worked. "It's the only door that opens from the outside. And Princess—if we take it, there's no coming back from what we are afterward. Every one of us will be a traitor by dawn."

Isolde looked around the candlelit room: the disgraced priestess, the bleeding smuggler, the keeper with thirty years of secrets in their eyes, the captain who had just burned twenty-two years of honor in a single night. Traitors all, now. Outlaws bound together by something the law had no word for.

"Good," Isolde said, and found that she meant it. "Let the crown learn what treachery looks like when it's the honest kind."

Outside, the cathedral bells rang on, and on, and would not stop.
