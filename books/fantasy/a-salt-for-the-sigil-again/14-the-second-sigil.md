## 14. The Second Sigil

The relic came first, and it was almost easy.

Maerin had said the rite needed three things: a relic of the old keeping, a renunciation spoken by a keeper's own mouth, and a willing witness who would not look away. The relic, she told them, still existed—buried in the drowned shrine of Saint Vessal, two days' ride south of Thornfell, where the river had swallowed the valley forty years ago and never given it back.

"The keeper's bell," she said, spreading a water-stained map across Ryn's table in the safehouse above the tannery. "Cast from the same iron as the gate's hinges. When it rang, the gods behind the seal heard silence instead of prayer. It's the only thing they ever feared."

"And it's at the bottom of a lake," Ryn said.

"At the bottom of a shrine," Maerin corrected. "The lake is just what's holding it."

Isolde looked up from the letter she'd been reading for the hundredth time—Aldric's handwriting, Aldric's words, none of which her brother remembered writing. *Find the bell. Ring it where I can hear.* "How deep?"

"Deep enough that we'll want someone who knows flooded places." Maerin's eyes went to Ryn, and Ryn swore with feeling.

"I'm a smuggler, not a pearl diver."

"You're a smuggler who once moved cargo through the sunken aqueducts under Corvane," Gideon said quietly. "You told me that yourself, the night you saved my life. You said there wasn't a wet hole in the mountains you couldn't crawl through."

Ryn glared at each of them in turn, found no allies, and reached for the bottle. "Fine. But if I drown, one of you is going to explain to my creditors why they shouldn't dig me up and make an example."

* * *

They rode south through country that grew stranger the farther they went.

Gideon noticed it first in the orchards—apple trees heavy out of season, fruit so ripe it split on the branch and drew wasps in black rivers. Then in the villages: roofs newly mended, children round-cheeked where famine had been, wells that never ran dry. Prosperity like a fever, too bright to be healthy. And everywhere, small shrines at the crossroads, uncarved stones slick with fresh offerings—milk, honey, blood, and once, arranged with terrible care, a child's milk teeth.

Nobody worked the fields when they passed. Everybody watched.

"They're not afraid," Isolde murmured as they rode through the third such village. "That's what frightens me. They're *grateful*."

"Gratitude is how it farms," Gideon said. The words came out flat, and they were surprised how little they felt saying them. Thirty years of watching the gate had taught them this much: the thing behind the sigils did not take. It gave. It gave until the giving became a debt no mortal could carry, and then it collected.

Ryn rode point, silent for once, her hand never far from her knife.

On the second evening they crested the ridge above the drowned valley, and all four of them stopped talking at once.

Lake Vessal stretched below them, gray-green and perfectly still, without a breath of wind to wrinkle it. The tops of the old shrine's spires broke the surface like fingers reaching up from a grave—three stone fingers, black with weed, gulls circling them in slow, purposeless spirals. The water was so clear near the edges that Gideon could see the drowned road running down into the dark, fence posts standing in rows, a cart still hitched to nothing.

"It doesn't look forty years deep," Isolde said.

"It isn't," Ryn said grimly. She'd gone pale around the mouth. "It looks like twenty feet. My eyes are telling me twenty feet. Everything about that water is telling me twenty feet." She dismounted and started unpacking rope anyway. "Which means it isn't."

* * *

They made camp above the shoreline, and Gideon took first watch because they did not trust themselves to sleep.

The lake did not move. All night, it did not move. No current, no ripple, no fish rising. The gulls left at dusk and did not return. And sometime past midnight, Gideon realized what had been wrong since they arrived: there were no reflections. The water reflected the stars, the spires, the moon—but not the four of them sitting on the shore. Where their faces should have been, the surface showed only depth.

"Maerin," Gideon said softly.

She was already awake. She'd been sitting cross-legged facing the water for hours, Gideon realized, her defrocked priestess's hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral. "I know."

"What is it doing?"

"Listening." Her voice was very quiet. "Or maybe waiting. There's a difference, and I don't like either one." She turned. In the firelight her face looked older than her years, hollowed the way the worshippers in the valley had been hollowed—not fed upon, exactly, but *used*. "When we go down there tomorrow, it will speak to one of us. That's how it works. It finds the crack in a person and pours itself in. I've seen it. I served it before I understood what I was serving."

"It won't find a crack in me," Ryn said from her bedroll, not opening her eyes. "I'm solid flaw."

"It will find one," Maerin said simply. "It always does. The question is whether the person it speaks to has anyone beside them when it does." She looked at Gideon then, and held the look a moment too long, and Gideon looked away first.

* * *

The dive began at dawn, in fog so thick the spires seemed to float free of the world.

Ryn went down first with a length of oiled rope and a smuggler's lantern sealed in glass—the kind that burned underwater, a trick she refused to explain. Isolde held the line at the surface with Maerin. Gideon stood waist-deep in water that should have been freezing and was not, water that was the exact temperature of blood, and hated it.

Ryn was under for a long time. When she surfaced, gasping, her face was the color of curdled milk.

"It's there," she managed. "The bell. Hanging in the bell tower like the flood never happened. Dry, Gideon. The whole tower's dry inside, there's air, there's—" She stopped, coughed lake water, and would not meet anyone's eyes. "There's something else down there. Something wrote on the walls. Fresh writing. It knew we were coming."

They went down together after that—all but Isolde, whom Maerin refused to risk; *the god wants the king's sister most of all*, she'd said, *and we do not give it what it wants*. So Gideon followed Ryn down the drowned road, along the shrine's outer wall, through a doorway whose door still hung on leather hinges that should have rotted decades ago, and into air.

The bell tower was exactly as Ryn had described: dry, intact, lit by the strange green glow of the lantern and something else besides—a faint luminescence in the walls themselves, as if the stones remembered daylight. Stairs spiraled upward. And on every wall, floor to ceiling, written in a hand Gideon recognized with a sickness that started in the gut and climbed:

Aldric's handwriting.

*COME DOWN, KEEPER. THE WATER IS WARM. THE WATER IS KIND. YOU HAVE BEEN COLD FOR THIRTY YEARS.*

"He writes through anything he touches," Maerin had warned. "Paper, stone, skin. Don't read it. Reading is listening, and listening is an invitation."

Gideon fixed their eyes on the stairs and climbed.

The bell chamber opened at the top like a throat. And there it was—the keeper's bell, hanging from a beam of black iron, untouched by rust or weed. It was smaller than Gideon had imagined, no larger than a war-helm, its surface covered in sigils worn nearly smooth by centuries of ringing. Salt crusted its lip in a white rime, though no sea had ever come within a hundred leagues of this valley.

Salt for the sigil. The old keepers' phrase rose unbidden, and with it, unbidden, the memory of Halvenne's voice: *Salt keeps what iron binds, little apprentice. Remember that when I'm gone.*

Gideon reached for the bell.

And the god spoke.

Not through the walls. Not through the water. Through the space between heartbeats, through the marrow, through the place where a person keeps the name they call themselves at three in the morning when sleep won't come. It did not boom. It *sang*—a voice like a mother's, like a lover's, like the voice you remember from before you could remember.

*GIDEON.*

They froze, hand on cold iron.

*I KNOW WHAT YOU LOST. SHALL I TELL YOU?*

"Don't," Gideon whispered, to the god or to themselves, they weren't sure.

*BUT YOU WANT ME TO. THAT IS WHY YOU CAME ALONE UP THE STAIRS. THAT IS WHY YOUR FRIENDS ARE A HUNDRED FEET OF DARK WATER AWAY. YOU WANTED ONE MOMENT WHERE NO ONE COULD SEE WHAT YOU MIGHT AGREE TO.*

The lantern guttered. In its failing light, the bell chamber changed—or Gideon's eyes changed, or the god reached in and rearranged the world like furniture. The stone walls became the watchpost at Thornfell. The doorway became the gate. And standing in the doorway, whole and warm and laughing, was Halvenne.

Not a ghost. Not a memory. *Halvenne*—gray braid, burn-scarred hands, the crooked smile she'd worn the day she'd caught young Gideon stealing salt from the stores and, instead of punishing them, had taught them what the salt was for.

*THIRTY YEARS YOU HAVE KEPT MY PRISON AND CALLED IT DUTY,* the god sang through her mouth, sweetly, sweetly. *THIRTY YEARS OF COLD BREAD AND COLDER NIGHTS AND NO ONE WHO KNOWS YOUR NAME EXCEPT PEOPLE YOU ARE AFRAID TO LOVE BECAUSE EVERYTHING YOU LOVE GETS TAKEN OR LEFT. I CAN GIVE HER BACK. NOT AN ECHO. HER. SHE IS NOT GONE, KEEPER. SHE IS IN HERE WITH ME. THEY ARE ALL IN HERE WITH ME. EVERY KEEPER WHO EVER BOUND THEMSELVES INTO MY SEAL—THEY DID NOT DIE. THEY CAME HOME TO ME.*

"Halvenne bound herself to starve you," Gideon said. Their voice sounded very far away. "That's what the records say. That's what Maerin says."

*AND WHAT DO RECORDS KNOW OF IT? ASK HER.*

Halvenne stepped forward. Her boots made no sound on the stone. Her eyes were kind, and they were wrong, and they were kind.

"Gideon," she said, in her own voice now, rough and warm and thirty years familiar. "Little apprentice. Look how tall you got. Look how tired. Come here. Let me see your face."

Every year of Gideon's solitude rose up at once like floodwater. The empty watchpost. The festivals spent alone on the mountain while the valley sang below. The way they'd flinched from Ryn's loyalty, from Maerin's kindness, from Isolde's grief, because everything loved was a hostage future grief would collect. Thirty years of duty, and here was the woman who'd taught them the word, asking for nothing but their hand.

Gideon's hand moved. They watched it move, from somewhere outside themselves, reaching toward her—

—and stopped, because through the water below, muffled and distant, came the sound of Isolde shouting Ryn's name, and beneath it, thinner, Maerin beginning to chant the first line of a warding liturgy. Not because anything was attacking. Just because she'd promised. *You will not be alone with it. None of us will be alone with it.*

Someone was counting them. Someone was keeping watch over *them*, the way they'd kept watch over the gate—without glory, without thanks, night after night, because that was what family meant when it chose you.

Gideon closed their fist around the bell's cold lip instead of Halvenne's warm hand.

"No," they said.

The chamber shuddered. Halvenne's smile cracked like river ice.

*YOU WILL COME BACK TO ME,* the god said, and the sweetness was gone from it now, and underneath the sweetness Gideon heard the hunger, vast and patient and *old*, older than the crown, older than the valley, older than the salt. *THEY ALL COME BACK. EVEN YOUR PRIESTESS CAME BACK ONCE. EVEN THE KING WILL BEG TO. AND WHEN YOUR NEW FAMILY FAILS YOU—AND THEY WILL, THEY ALWAYS DO—I WILL STILL BE HERE, AND SO WILL SHE, AND YOU WILL KNEEL AT THIS GATE AND ASK ME FOR WHAT I OFFERED FREELY TONIGHT.*

"Then I'll kneel tired," Gideon said, and pulled the bell from its hook.

The scream that went through the water was not sound. It was pressure—it shoved them against the wall, it filled their teeth, it bent the lantern flame sideways. The writing on the walls ran like wax. The false Halvenne unraveled from the feet up, her kind face last, mouthing something Gideon refused to read.

Then the bell was in their hands, real iron, real weight, and the chamber was only a chamber, and the water beyond the doorway waited, dark and ordinary and cold.

Gideon took one breath of the dead shrine's stale air, spat the god's name for the first time in thirty years—"Vessal"—and jumped.

* * *

They surfaced gasping into gray morning light, the bell wrapped in oilcloth against Gideon's chest, Ryn hauling them onto the rocks with curses that would have embarrassed a dockworker. Isolde dragged Maerin back from the waterline. For a moment the five of them—four of them, and the bell—lay on the shore breathing like landed fish.

Then, all around the lake, every drowned thing rose at once.

Not bodies. Lights. Hundreds of them, thousands, rising from the flooded houses and roads and fields—small silver points, like stars surfacing. They hung above the water in a slow spiral, converging over the shrine's broken spires, and formed, briefly, unmistakably, a shape: an eye, open, regarding them.

Maerin made a sound Gideon had never heard from her before, low and continuous, a hymn and a warning at once.

"It sees us," Isolde whispered. "For thirty years it ignored the keepers. Why now?"

"Because now we're not guarding the door," Gideon said. They sat up, dripping, and put their hand flat on the bell's salt-rimed curve. "Now we're coming for it."

The eye held them a moment longer. Then the lights sank, all together, all at once, and the lake went still again, and the fog rolled back in like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

Ryn was the first to stand. She wrung out her coat, looked at the water, and spat into it.

"Two components down," she said. "One to go. Somebody find me a drink and a witness."

But Gideon was looking at the bell, at the sigils worn smooth by centuries of keepers' hands, and thinking of the third requirement Maerin had named. A willing witness. Someone who would stand at the gate and watch the unbinding and not look away—and who, the old texts said in letters none of them had yet read closely enough, must be someone the keeper trusted more than they trusted themselves.

Gideon looked up and found all three of them watching back.

None of them spoke. None of them had to. The thirteenth moon was coming, and it was coming fast, and somewhere north a king wearing a god's glove was waiting for his bell to ring.
