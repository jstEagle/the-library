## 15. What Gideon Wants

The flooded shrine had given up its bell two days ago, and since then the god had not stopped talking.

It didn't use words, exactly. Not at first. It was more like weather—a pressure behind the ears, a warmth in the chest that arrived uninvited and stayed. Gideon had spent thirty years learning to ignore that warmth, the way a sailor learns to sleep through the creak of a hull. But tonight, alone in the watchpost above Thornfell while Maerin slept and Isolde kept her vigil over the bell, the warmth found the crack in them and poured itself in.

*You are tired,* it said, in the voice of someone who had earned the right to say so.

Gideon sat up on their cot. The fire had burned down to coals. Outside, the thirteenth moon's absence pressed against the shuttered window like a face.

"I know what you are," they said quietly. "I've known since I was nineteen years old."

*I know what you are, too.* A pause, delicate as a held breath. *Keeper's apprentice. Gate-watcher. The last of a line that ends with you, because you chose duty over everything else. Over love. Over rest. Over being known.*

The words were true, and that was the obscenity of it. Any lie could be refused. Only truth could be *wanted.*

Gideon rose, pulled on their coat, and went out into the cold rather than sit with it. The mountain path was glazed with frost. Below, the valley lay dark except for one lamp in Maerin's cottage and, far off toward the lowlands, the smudge of orange that never quite left the horizon anymore—the burning villages, the prayer-fires, the crown's idea of mercy. Somewhere down there, Aldric wore his crown like a yoke and smiled at crowds who adored him for reasons none of them could name.

Somewhere down there, Ryn Sable was sleeping off her wound, or pretending to.

Gideon walked to the gate.

* * *

It looked as it always looked: a seam in the mountain's shoulder, older than any kingdom, framed by stone that the first keepers had carved with sigils deep enough to hold weather and time. Three great seals. Two now cold. One still warm—the third, the lowest, the one shaped like an open hand—and beneath it, faint as breath on glass, the shimmer of something pressing outward.

Gideon knelt and laid their palm flat against the stone. The warmth answered immediately, eagerly, the way a dog answers its name.

*There you are,* it said. *There you are. I have been looking for you for thirty years.*

"You've been looking for a way out."

*Those are not different things.* The voice shifted then—settled into a shape Gideon knew better than their own heartbeat. Old, dry, amused. The voice of Sera Vane, keeper before them, teacher, the woman who had walked into this seal when Gideon was twenty-two years old and left them standing here alone with a bell and a lifetime of watching.

"Stop that." Their own voice came out rough. "You don't get to wear her."

*I am not wearing her. I am offering her. There is a difference, and you know it, because you have spent thirty years studying the difference between what a god takes and what a god gives.* The warmth pulsed gently under their palm, patient as tide. *Everything I take, I take because it is given. That is the oldest law, older than your sigils. Prayer is only giving with the name changed. So: give me something, Gideon Villalobos, and I will give you something back.*

"I want nothing from you."

*That is a lie, and we both know it, and lying to yourself is the one prayer I cannot answer for you.*

The frost crackled. Far below, a nightbird called once and thought better of it.

*Here is what I know about you,* the god said, and its voice was Sera Vane's voice, and it was gentle, which was worse than cruelty. *You were nineteen when you first stood this watch. You are fifty-one now. In thirty years you have buried four travelers caught in the passes, tended two hundred sick shepherds, and spoken aloud—to another person—an average of eleven words a day. You have kept every record. You have let no detail rot. You are very good at remembering, Gideon, because remembering is all you have been permitted to do.*

"Permitted," Gideon repeated. "You make it sound like a cage."

*Is it not?*

They wanted to say no. They opened their mouth to say no, and what came out instead was silence, and the silence was answer enough, and the god received it the way it received everything: gratefully, hungrily, without judgment. That was the terrible thing about it. It never judged. Judgment would have given them something to push against.

*She died for this gate,* the god said softly. *Sera Vane. She gave herself to the seal because we were waking, and the rite required a keeper, and there was no one else. She did not hesitate. Do you know why?*

"Because she was better than me."

*Because she was lonely,* the god said. *And she knew that loneliness, given thirty more years, would make her mine. She chose the quick version of the sacrifice because she did not trust herself with the slow one. But you—you have already served the slow one. Thirty years of it. You have paid almost everything, Gideon. You have simply never been handed the receipt.*

The warmth under their palm deepened. And then—

Light. Not the gate's light; something warmer, domestic, impossible. Gideon blinked and the mountainside was gone and they were standing in the kitchen of the watchpost as it had been thirty years ago, woodsmoke and rosemary, and Sera Vane sat at the table mending a cloak with that crooked stitch she'd never let anyone correct, and she looked up, and she said:

"You're late. Soup's gone cold. Sit down, apprentice."

Her hands were whole. Her eyes were sharp and brown and annoyed at them in the particular way that meant affection. The scar on her jaw from the wolverine in the Kess Pass, the one Gideon had helped stitch by lamplight when they were barely more than a child—it was there. Everything was there.

"This isn't real," Gideon said. Their voice shook. They hated that it shook.

*No,* the god agreed pleasantly, from everywhere and nowhere, wearing her mouth. *But it could be. That is my offer. Not a memory. Not a ghost. Me, poured into the shape you miss most, sustained by the worship of a kingdom that already prays to me nightly without knowing my name. She would walk. She would argue with you about the stitching. She would grow old beside you at this gate, and you would not be alone again—not for one day of whatever years remain to you. And all it costs—*

"Nothing," Gideon whispered. "It costs nothing."

*Almost,* the god said, smiling with her smile. *It costs only that you stop fighting me. Lower the third sigil's warding from inside—just a finger's width, just enough that I can reach the lowlands properly, finish what the crown has so kindly begun. And your teacher lives. Your family—those three broken strays you've collected, the priestess, the smuggler, the princess—I will leave them untouched. You have my law on it. Given is given.*

The kitchen flickered. Sera Vane set down her mending and looked at Gideon with an expression they had seen only once before, the night before she walked into the seal: calm, resolved, and unbearably kind.

"Well?" she said. "Are you going to stand there all night, or are you going to sit with your old teacher?"

Gideon's knees nearly went.

That was the truth of it, the thing they would never say aloud to Maerin or Isolde or anyone: thirty years of duty had not made them strong. It had made them *empty*, and emptiness is a vessel, and vessels exist to be filled. Every night of the last three decades they had told themselves the loneliness was a kind of armor. Tonight, with the thirteenth moon one turn away and the end of everything approaching, the armor felt less like protection and more like a room they had locked themselves into, and here at last was a door, and here at last was someone on the other side of it holding a lamp.

Their hand moved. Not to the bell, not to the knife at their belt—to the sigil. Fingers finding the carved groove of the third seal, the open-hand mark, feeling for the warding knot Maerin had taught them to read. One turn. One loosening. A finger's width.

Sera Vane smiled.

And in the smile, for half a heartbeat, something else looked out—something vast and patient and starving, something that had eaten a shepherd's shadow and a village's faith and a king's entire self, something that called its hunger *grace*—and Gideon saw it, really saw it, the way you see a rip current only when you're already in it.

Their fingers stopped.

Because Sera Vane would never have smiled like that. Sera Vane had taught them the first lesson of the gate before she'd taught them anything else: *The gods do not tempt you with evil, child. They tempt you with good. Anything wicked can be refused by a decent heart. Only the good can undo one.*

"You're not her," Gideon said. Quietly. Steadily. Saying it to themselves as much as to it. "She wouldn't come back this way. She'd come back furious. She'd come back asking why I haven't re-cut the second sigil yet, and whether I've been eating properly, and she'd call Ryn 'that smuggler girl' and secretly like her. You got the face right. You even got the scar. But you got the love wrong, and the love is the only part that matters."

The kitchen guttered like a candle in wind.

*Think carefully,* the god said, and the gentleness was fraying now, threads of something colder showing through. *One moon remains. When it turns, I will be permanent, and your king will be a glove with no hand in it, and everything you refuse tonight I will take anyway—later, from people who cannot refuse me. You are not saving them by starving yourself. You are only choosing the manner of your loss.*

"Yes," Gideon said. "That's what a keeper does."

*Then you will die empty, and alone, and forgotten—*

"No," said a voice from the dark behind them. "They won't."

* * *

Ryn Sable came up the path with a lantern in one hand and her wounded side carefully braced against her walking stick, breathing hard from the climb, snow in her dark hair. She stopped a few paces off, squinting against the lantern light at Gideon kneeling at the gate.

"There you are. Maerin woke up and you were gone, and Isolde's convinced you've been taken, and I drew the short straw because apparently I'm the only one who can climb in the dark without falling off the mountain." She limped closer, frowning. "What are you doing?"

"Checking the seal."

"At midnight. In a frost. With your ribs showing through that coat." She crouched beside them, wincing, and held the lantern up to the stone. Her breath fogged. For a long moment she just looked at the warm sigil, and Gideon watched her face do the arithmetic she didn't know she was doing—the warmth, the hour, the way Gideon's hand was positioned.

"You heard it," she said finally. Not accusing. Just knowing. Smugglers knew transactions when they saw one mid-negotiation. "Just now. It offered you something."

Gideon said nothing.

"It offered me things too, back on the roads. After the village burned." Ryn settled onto the cold stone beside them with a grunt, close enough that their shoulders touched, and stared into the dark below. "Told me it could bring back my brother. Drowned off the Salt Coast eleven years ago. All I had to do was keep running cargo and stop asking what was in the boxes." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "I nearly did it. Three nights I sat with my hand on a crate, listening to it whisper in his voice. You know what stopped me?"

"What?"

"He couldn't sing anymore." She turned to look at them, lantern light catching the scar along her jaw. "Whatever that thing built out of my brother's memory, it had his face, his laugh, the way he said my name. But he couldn't sing. And Tam sang constantly—off-key, always off-key, drove our mother mad. If it was really him, even a piece of him, the singing would've come with. It didn't. Because the thing doesn't understand us, Gideon. It understands what we *miss*. Missing has no music in it."

Gideon's throat worked. "It had her stitching," they said. "Every crooked stitch. It had the scar on her jaw."

"And?" Ryn prompted gently.

"And it asked me to sit down and eat soup with her." Their voice cracked on the last word. "Thirty years, Ryn. Thirty years of eleven words a day. And it put her at that table, and my legs nearly went, and my hand was on the sigil before I even decided to move it."

Ryn was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "But you're still here."

"Barely."

"'Barely' is how everyone survives everything worth surviving." She bumped her shoulder against theirs, careful of her ribs. "Look—I don't know what it promised you. Don't tell me if you don't want. But I know what it costs, because I've seen the ledger. Everything that thing sells, somebody pays in bone. Always. No exceptions, no discounts, no matter how sweet the shopkeeper's smile." She nodded at the warm sigil. "So here's my professional assessment, as the only person on this mountain with actual criminal expertise: whatever's on offer, the price is hidden somewhere, and hidden prices are the expensive ones."

A sound escaped Gideon that startled them both—a laugh, ragged and wet and real. The god's warmth recoiled from it slightly, the way cold water recoils from a flame.

"She'd have liked you," Gideon said. "My teacher. She'd have pretended not to."

"Yeah?" Ryn grinned. "What was her name?"

"Sera Vane."

"Sera Vane," Ryn repeated, tasting it. "Well. When we starve this thing back behind its door tomorrow-morrow-whenever, you can introduce us properly. The real version. The one that sings, or stitches, or whatever it is dead mentors do." She pushed herself upright with the stick, then paused, looking down at Gideon still kneeling at the gate. "Come inside. Maerin's making tea she'll deny making if you mention it, and Isolde's reading that letter again, and honestly the watchpost is warmer than the inside of a coffin, which is where you'll end up if you sit here freezing all night talking to doors."

Gideon looked at the sigil one last time. The warmth was still there, patient, waiting—but dimmer now, and they understood suddenly that it had not dimmed because it was weaker. It had dimmed because it had lost. Somewhere in the arithmetic of hunger, a bargain refused was a meal denied, and the thing behind the gate had just gone hungry in a way it had not expected to, from a keeper it had counted as certain.

*Sera Vane chose the quick sacrifice because she didn't trust herself with the slow one,* it had said. But that wasn't quite true either, Gideon realized, rising stiffly, taking Ryn's arm when she offered it. Sera Vane had trusted herself fine. What she hadn't trusted was the god—and she'd been right.

"Coming," Gideon said.

They walked down together, two silhouettes and one lantern, and behind them the gate's warmth faded into ordinary cold stone, sulking, counting the turns remaining.

Inside the watchpost, the fire had been fed and Maerin's tea steamed on the table and Isolde looked up from the letter with red-rimmed eyes and asked, "Anything?"

Gideon hung their coat by the door and found that their hands, for the first time in days, were steady.

"It's afraid of us," they said. "It finally noticed we're not going to take its deal. And things that are afraid start making mistakes." They looked around the table—at the defrocked priestess, the wounded smuggler, the runaway princess, the iron bell wrapped in wool in the corner—and heard themselves say, with a certainty that surprised them, "Tomorrow we go down the mountain. We have a king to cut free."

None of them saw, in the dark beyond the window, the shadow that detached itself from the treeline and turned, without hurry, toward the capital—carrying word of the keeper's refusal to a god who was done bargaining.
