## 16. Moon Twelve

The scars ached when the weather turned, and the weather had been turning for three days.

Maerin sat in the watchpost's back room with her sleeves pushed up, cataloguing what the High Warden's people had left of her. The burns on her forearms had healed crooked, silver-pink ridges that caught firelight like old wax. Her left hand still trembled when she was tired—she'd learned to write with it braced against her knee. And somewhere behind her sternum, where they had pressed the hot iron and asked their questions, there was a coldness that no amount of blanket or broth seemed to reach.

She had given them nothing. That was what she told herself at night, staring at the rafters while Ryn snored in the next room. She had given them *nothing*.

Except the god's name had come out of her mouth anyway, hadn't it? Not under the iron—under the *prayer*. They had knelt her before an altar she recognized from her own novitiate, twenty years gone, and made her recite the liturgy of the Hollowing Sun, and her mouth had known every word, and by the third verse she had been weeping without deciding to, and the name had risen in her throat like water finding a crack.

*Vessarion.* The One Who Answers. The god whose hunger wore her old order's rites like a stolen coat.

Maerin flexed her scarred fingers and returned to the work spread across the table: Gideon's journals, forty years of them, the keeper's cramped hand marching through decades of quiet. Salt inventories. Sigil inspections. Weather notes. And buried in the middle years—the ones after the previous keeper went into the seal—a single page that changed everything.

She had read it eleven times now. It did not get easier.

The door creaked. Isolde came in with two cups of willowbark tea and set one down without asking, which Maerin appreciated; asking would have required an answer, and Maerin's answers lately had all been variations of *I don't know yet.*

"You haven't slept," Isolde said.

"I slept."

"Liar."

"Former priestess," Maerin corrected mildly. "We're trained to lie beautifully. Ask me again and I'll make it convincing."

Isolde didn't smile. She'd stopped smiling much since the coronation renewal, since Ryn had been dragged away covering their retreat, since Aldric had been taken deeper into whatever sanctum Vessarion kept beneath the palace. Eleven days ago. The moon above Thornfell was a thinning coin, twelve days from dark.

Maerin watched the princess look at the journals, at the diagrams Maerin had inked across three sheets of parchment—the gate, the seal, the sigils arranged like a lock seen from inside—and saw the moment understanding arrived. Isolde had been avoiding this room. Everyone had. They came to Maerin for bandages and tea and reassurance, and they walked past the table where the price was written down as if not looking at it would unwrite it.

"Say it," Isolde said quietly. "Whatever you found. Say it out loud so I can stop pretending I haven't guessed."

Maerin set down her pen. Her trembling hand she pressed flat to the table, and the wood took the shaking, and she was grateful for something solid.

"The seal isn't a wall," she said. "It never was. It's a *wound*—a wound the first keepers made in the world when they shut the old gods out, and wounds need tending, and tending needs blood." She turned the journal toward Isolde, to the entry dated thirty-one years past, in a different hand than Gideon's: older, more formal, the letters of someone who knew she was writing her last testament. "Your keeper before Gideon. Sera Vane. When the gods last stirred, she didn't reinforce the seal from outside. She went *in*. She bound herself into it—her memories, her selfhood, fed into the sigils turn by turn until there was nothing left of her but the holding."

"Gideon said she died."

"Gideon believes she died. Gideon has spent thirty years believing the kindest available version." Maerin tapped the page. "But this is the rite, Isolde. This is how it's done. A keeper must stand inside the seal—one soul anchoring three sigils—and each sigil, as it's unbound, takes its payment. Not blood. Not life. *Memory.* One full turning of everything a person is, per sigil. Three sigils. Three turns."

"Turns of what?"

"Of the whole." Maerin heard her own voice go flat and clinical, the voice she'd used delivering hard diagnoses to frightened families, and hated it, and used it anyway because gentleness would break her. "Childhood. First love. Every road ever walked. Every face ever learned. The rite strips them in layers, oldest last, so the keeper stays lucid enough to finish the work. And when the third sigil closes—" She stopped. Started again. "When the third sigil closes, the person who walks out is not the person who walked in. They'll be *hollow*, Isolde. Alive, breathing, healthy as a summer morning. And empty as a cup."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines above the valley, and the sound of it was very old and very indifferent.

Isolde sat down slowly, across the table, and looked at the diagram for a long time. "There's another way."

"No."

"There's always another way. There's a smuggler's tunnel under every wall in this country; Ryn taught us that. There's a bargain, a back door, a—"

"A bargain is what started this." Maerin's voice cracked like ice. She steadied it. "Forgive me. But I have *seen* what Vessarion does with bargains. I prayed to him once, Isolde. Did you know that? Before I was defrocked, before any of it—I was a young priestess with a dying mother, and I prayed, and he answered, and my mother woke the next morning bright as a bell." She turned her forearm over, showing the burn scars. "And she lived nine more years and forgot my name in the sixth. Sold it off a piece at a time to pay for her healing, though she never knew she was paying. He doesn't take. He *trades*. And his prices are always memory, because memory is the only currency souls are minted in."

"So the rite is honest, at least," Isolde said bitterly. "It tells you the price up front."

"It's honest," Maerin agreed. "That's the horror of it. The old keepers built it clean. No trick, no loophole, no clever escape clause. Just arithmetic." She pulled the final sheet toward herself—the one she'd been working on for six days, the one covered in her own annotations, cross-referencing Sera Vane's testament against the liturgy she'd been raised in. "And here is the part I've checked four times hoping I'm wrong. The keeper must enter willingly. Must know the price in full and walk in anyway. If Gideon goes in deceived, or coerced, or half-hoping for rescue mid-rite, the seal rejects them and Vessarion gets exactly what he wants: a hollow keeper *inside* the lock, holding the door open from within."

Silence. Isolde's hands were fists on the table.

"He'll do it," the princess said at last. "Gideon will hear this and start packing salt and iron and won't even argue. Thirty years alone at that gate—they've been waiting for a way to follow Sera Vane since the day she vanished. You're not telling them the cost. You're telling them the *invitation*."

"I know."

"And you're going to let them?"

Maerin looked at the fire. She thought about the cells beneath the warden citadel, the iron, the liturgy rising out of her own mouth. She thought about Ryn Sable in a god's keeping, and whether she was still Ryn Sable, and how many days were left to find out. She thought about Gideon Villalobos standing at a sealed gate for thirty years, keeping a lonely watch over a silence everyone else had agreed to call peace, and how Maerin had watched them these past weeks—watched them refuse the god's sweet offers, watched them carry other people's burdens like they weighed nothing—and understood, with a clarity that felt like grief, that some people are built for exactly one sacrifice, and spend their whole lives waiting for it.

"I'm going to tell them the truth," Maerin said. "All of it. Because the rite requires willingness, and willingness requires knowledge, and because—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—because if I lied to buy them a few more years, I'd be doing Vessarion's work with kinder tools. He wins by hiding prices. We don't get to win that way. We only get to win *clean*."

Isolde wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, quick and furious. "Then we find another keeper."

"With what training? With what thirty years of salt and vigil? Isolde—" Maerin reached across and took the princess's fist, unfolded it, held the shaking hand inside both of hers. "There is no other keeper. There is only the question of whether we let Gideon go in alone, or whether we spend these last twelve days making sure they walk in knowing exactly how loved they are. Because that's the one thing the rite can't strip first, if we're careful. The oldest memories go last. So we make new ones. Every hour. Until the twelfth moon dies."

* * *

They gathered at dusk, all of them, in the main room of the watchpost: Maerin at the table with the journals open, Isolde by the fire, the warden captain Dain Corvess standing awkwardly by the door with his soldier's discomfort at being indoors among civilians, and Gideon coming in from the evening round with frost in their hair and the iron bell wrapped in oilcloth under one arm.

Maerin had rehearsed this speech forty times. She delivered it in under five minutes, flat and complete, and when she finished, the silence had a texture to it, dense as wool.

Gideon set the bell down on the table. Unwrapped it, absently, the way a person fidgets. Then they read Sera Vane's testament page by lamplight, top to bottom, and Maerin watched their face and saw—not shock. Not denial. Something worse. Recognition. The expression of someone finding a letter they'd always known was coming.

"One turn per sigil," Gideon said at last. Their voice was steady. That was the most frightening thing in the room. "Three sigils. And then?"

"Then you walk out empty," Maerin said. "Alive. Whole of body. And you won't know my name, or Isolde's, or the roads, or the gate itself. You'll stand in front of the thing you guarded for thirty years and see only an old door."

Gideon nodded slowly. Ran a thumb along the bell's cold rim. "Sera told me once," they said, "the year before she went in. She said, *a seal is just love with nowhere else to go.* I thought she was being poetic. She was being literal." They looked up, and their eyes were dry and terribly calm. "How long do I have to decide?"

"You don't," Maerin said. "That's the cruelty of it. The rite needs willingness, and willingness can't be performed. Either you're already decided, or you're not the right keeper." She made herself hold their gaze. "So I'm not asking you to decide. I'm telling you what I found, and then I'm going to spend twelve days teaching Isolde the chant, drilling Corvess on the bell-work, and pretending to sleep. And on the thirteenth dark moon, whoever stands at that gate stands there because they chose it, fully informed, and no one in this room carries the weight of having pushed them."

Corvess cleared his throat. "And Ryn?" His voice was rough. He'd taken her capture harder than any of them, in his silent soldier's way. "If we close the seal while she's in the sanctum—"

"Then whatever holds her lets go," Maerin said. "A starved god can't keep puppets. She may come back to us changed. She may come back angry enough to kill us all. But she comes back." She paused. "That's the other thing the arithmetic gives us. It's not mercy. But it's not nothing."

Isolde stood. Crossed to Gideon. For a moment Maerin thought the princess would say something grand—an oath, a plea—and instead Isolde simply put her arms around the old keeper and held on, and Gideon stood stiff as a fence post for three heartbeats, and then, slowly, folded.

"We'll find you afterward," Isolde said into their shoulder. "However long it takes. Whatever it costs. We'll sit you down and tell you your own name until you believe it."

"You can't promise that," Gideon said. "The rite—"

"I'm a princess," Isolde said. "Promising impossible things is the family trade."

* * *

Later—deep later, the fire burned to coals, the house breathing around them—Maerin climbed to the watchpost roof with her blanket and her aching ribs and looked east, where the moon hung low and thinned, one night from its last quarter.

Twelve days. In twelve days the sky would go dark and the gate would open its one window of vulnerability, and Gideon Villalobos would walk into a wound in the world and pay for the world with everything they were.

Maerin had served gods for twenty years. She had learned their liturgies, their hungers, their arithmetic. And she knew, watching that moon, that Vessarion was watching too—that somewhere beneath the capital, in his sanctum of answered prayers, the god had done this same calculation and arrived at the same figure, and was even now arranging his pieces: Ryn in his keeping, Aldric on his throne, wardens on the roads. He thought the thirteenth moon was *his* appointment.

Let him think so. Let him count his coins and savor his victory. Because Maerin had read Sera Vane's testament eleven times, and there was one line she had not yet spoken aloud to anyone, one line she'd saved like a blade held flat against her wrist:

*A willing keeper who is loved cannot be wholly emptied. The seal takes the memories. It cannot take what others carry for them.*

She didn't know if it was true. It might be a dead woman's hope, inked in a desperate hand. But it was the only line in the whole grim ledger that pointed forward instead of down, and so Maerin folded it into her chest beside the cold place the iron had left, and began, silently, to practice the chant.

Below her, faint through the shuttered window, she could hear Isolde reading aloud to Gideon—reading the journals, their own forty years, giving them back to themselves one page at a time while there was still time.

The moon thinned. The clock ran. Twelve nights left, and Maerin meant to spend every one of them building the thing the seal couldn't eat.
