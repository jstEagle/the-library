## 17. The Crown Comes Down

The crown of Veylmark was not gold. That was the first thing I had forgotten in my years away from the capital, and the last thing I needed to remember now, crouched in the cold dark beneath the Cathedral of the Renewed Word with ash on my face and Ryn's smuggler's grease stinging my eyes.

It was iron. Old iron, black as a closed door, set with seven chips of pale stone that the priests called *tears of the faithful*. I had watched my brother crowned at fourteen, and even then—before any of this, before the prayers and the hollowed faces—I had thought it looked less like a coronet than like a manacle sized for the head.

Now I knew it was exactly that.

"Breathing," Ryn murmured beside me. She wasn't looking at me; she was watching the grate above us, where torchlight crawled in amber stripes across the flagstones of the processional hall. "You've stopped breathing, princess."

"I breathe when it's useful."

"That's a Gideon habit. You've been spending too much time with them." But there was no edge in it. There never was, anymore—not since the cells, not since Maerin came back to us with her wrists scarred and her voice steady and a god's true name folded up inside her like a knife wrapped in linen. Ryn reached over without asking and pressed two fingers briefly against my wrist, counting my pulse the way she counted everything: as a debt coming due or paid. "Steady enough. Good. Because once we're up there, there's no ledger left to balance. Whatever happens happens fast."

Above us, faint through stone, the cathedral organ began its long, glacial prelude.

* * *

We had planned it for nine days, and the plan had been beautiful. That was what I kept returning to, later—the beauty of it. Maerin had mapped the liturgy down to the breath, because she had once served rites that the god had stolen from her order, and she knew its tastes the way a cook knows a palate. The ceremony of renewal happened every seventh year: the king would kneel at the high altar while the High Warden anointed the crown with oil burned from prayers written on slips of vellum, and for one interval—the *Vigil of the Open Hand*—Aldric would be alone on the dais steps, unguarded by design, because the god wanted its vessel accessible to worship and the wardens had stopped knowing there was a difference between the two.

One interval. Ninety heartbeats, maybe a hundred. Enough time for three people to reach him if the fourth made sure the fifth thing never arrived.

Gideon's role was the bell. They would stand in the bell tower with our keeper's iron relic and ring it at the moment of anointing—a sound the god remembered from the gate, a sound that would drag every eye in the cathedral upward and every hollowed prayer in the building momentarily silent. Ryn's role was the doors: the western postern, wedged open with an iron shim, our road out. My role was Aldric himself. A name opens doors that crowns cannot, Maerin had said, and she'd been right; I had walked into the cathedral an hour ago in mourning gray, unveiled, and the usher who recognized me had bowed so low his forehead nearly touched the floor, and then had looked quickly away, ashamed, because everyone in this city knew the princess had fled and no one knew why, and shame makes people obedient.

And Gideon's second role—the one they had argued about and lost—was to stay behind me all the way up the nave, close enough that if the god noticed me, if the wearing of my brother turned its attention toward the bloodline it hadn't finished feeding on, they could put their body between us. *I am the only one here it can't buy*, Gideon had said, in that flat way of theirs. *Everything I love is standing in this room already.*

I should have argued harder. I have replayed that argument every night since. I should have told them that being unbuyable is not the same as being unbreakable.

But the plan was beautiful, and we believed in it, and the organ was playing, and the great doors opened onto light.

* * *

From beneath the grate, we watched the procession the way you watch weather approach across a plain.

First the wardens, two hundred strong, their white-and-silver livery gone strange in the last months—more silver than white now, as if the metal were winning some slow argument with the cloth. Then the priests of the Renewed Word, swinging censers whose smoke did not rise so much as *lean*, bending toward the altar like iron filings toward a lodestone. Then the congregation, thousands deep, and this was the part that made my stomach turn over: they were smiling. All of them. Not the smiles of joy. The smiles of people who had been promised something and had decided, in advance, not to examine the price.

And then the king.

My brother came up the nave on foot, as the rite demanded, and the crowd's hush was total, reverent, wrong. He wore the iron crown already—the rite of renewal required it—and he walked the way Aldric had always walked when he was forcing himself through something unbearable: spine straight, chin level, each step placed like a coin paid out of a purse running empty.

He passed directly over our hiding place. I saw the soles of his boots, worn at the heel the way he'd always worn them, because my brother bit his nails and dragged his feet and laughed too loud and forgot to eat when he was reading—and none of that was in his face anymore. His face was serene. Serene the way a pond is serene, with things underneath.

"Aldric," I whispered, so quietly it was barely breath.

His step hitched. Just once. Half a heartbeat's stumble, caught and smoothed so fast the crowd never saw it—but I saw it, and Gideon's hand found my shoulder in the dark, and Ryn exhaled slowly through her teeth.

"He heard you," Ryn breathed. "He's still in there. Ninety seconds at a time, just like the throne room. He's still *in there*, Isolde."

I held onto that half-stumble for the rest of my life. Remember that. Whatever else I tell you about this night, hold that first: my brother was still in there, and he knew me, and he was afraid.

* * *

We moved on Maerin's count.

The anointing began. The High Warden—half-hollow, Maerin had warned us, praying to the thing he served with the devotion of a man who has sold everything and cannot afford regret—lifted the vessel of burning prayer-oil, and the smell of it rolled through the cathedral like honey and grave-dirt.

In the tower above, the keeper's bell rang.

I will not be able to describe the sound honestly. It was not loud, though it filled everything. It was the sound the gate made, Maerin said afterward; it was the sound of thirty years of keeping, of salt and iron and someone standing watch in the snow; and every head in the cathedral snapped upward toward the belfry, and every mouth that had been silently mouthing prayers went slack, and for four full seconds the Cathedral of the Renewed Word belonged to the old gods' oldest enemy.

"Now," Ryn said, and we were moving.

The grate gave to the shim. We came up out of the undercroft into the shadow of a pillar forty feet from the dais, and no one saw us, because no one was looking at anything but the sky through the belfry windows, faces tilted, eyes wide, the whole congregation suspended like puppets with cut strings.

Aldric knelt alone on the dais steps, exactly where the Vigil of the Open Hand put him, and I crossed the marble floor of my childhood in eleven strides with Gideon a pace behind me, and when my brother lifted his head and his eyes focused—

There he was.

Not serene. Terrified. Twenty-two years old and terrified, kneeling in front of his own people, wearing a crown he could not lift.

"Isolde." His voice cracked in the middle of my name. His hands came up off the steps as if he didn't trust them, as if he'd been told they weren't his. "Isolde, you have to go—you can't be here, it knows your name now, it's been saying it to me for *weeks*—" His gaze flicked past me, to Gideon, and something desperate broke open in his face. "Keeper. Keeper, please. I held as long as I could. I held—"

"You held," Gideon said quietly, kneeling to his level, and I had never heard their voice do that, go soft as snowfall. "Thirty years I held this gate, Your Majesty, and I'm telling you as one holder to another: you held. Now we take it the rest of the way."

"We have ninety seconds," Aldric gasped. "Maybe less—it lets me surface near the anointing, it likes to watch me see it happen—" And then his whole body seized, spine arching, and his hands flew to the iron crown and *pulled*, and the crown did not move, and the sound he made—

I will hear that sound until I die.

"Help me take it off," he whispered through his teeth. "Please. Before I forget you again. Please, Issy, before I forget—"

Gideon's hands went to the crown. Mine went to Gideon's shoulders. And the iron was cold, colder than the marble, cold as the gate at Thornfell in midwinter, and it did not move, it did not *move*, it sat on my brother's head like it had grown there—

The bell stopped ringing.

That was the first wrongness. The bell had been timed for sixty counts, and it stopped at forty-one, cut off mid-peal, the silence slamming down over the cathedral like a lid.

The second wrongness was the smoke. Every censer in the building leaned at once—all of them, thousands of threads of incense, swinging from their lean toward the altar to a lean toward *us*, converging, pointing, a thousand grey fingers selecting.

The third wrongness was the High Warden turning around on the dais, unhurried, his face composed into something worse than anger: pity.

"Sister of the king," he said, and his voice came out doubled, a man's voice with something vast speaking through the spaces between the words. "You are late for your own anointing."

* * *

Afterward, reconstructing it, I understood that the god had known everything. Had perhaps always known. That the Vigil of the Open Hand was not an opening but a trapdoor, that the ninety seconds of lucidity were bait, that my brother's terror was real and his helplessness was real and both were permitted, the way a fisherman permits the fish to run.

But in the moment there was only chaos.

The congregation woke all at once—not as people but as a single animal, ten thousand heads turning, ten thousand throats beginning a note that had no words in it. Wardens poured up the side aisles. And Aldric screamed "RUN"—his own voice, his true voice, cracking across the din—and Gideon made the decision that haunts me, the right decision, the terrible one.

They let go of the crown.

"Ryn's door," Gideon snarled, hauling me backward by the arm as the first warden line closed. "We get out, we come back with the rite, we do this at the gate—we can't win it *here*, Isolde, this is his sanctum, his ground—"

"He's right here—" I was fighting them, I'm ashamed to say, fighting Gideon's grip while my brother clawed at his own scalp. "We have him, we have him *now*—"

"We have ninety seconds and two hundred blades!"

The warden line hit us like a wave on rock. Gideon fought the way water fights—flowing around every strike, using weight and patience, thirty years of quiet discipline in every movement—and I fought the way grief fights, which is badly, and between us we broke through the first rank, and then the second, and then we were in the aisle and the postern was thirty feet away and Ryn was holding it, Ryn was *in the doorway* with her knives out, waving us through—

"GO!" she roared, and grinned, because that was Ryn, grinning with blood already on her teeth. "Door's open, princess! Best exit in the city, no refunds—"

What happened next took four seconds and has taken me years to forgive.

The god let go of the crowd and picked up the wardens instead. I saw it happen—saw the doubled light come up behind the eyes of the nearest rank, saw their movements stop being men's movements and become something coordinated and vast, twenty bodies moving as one hand. And that hand reached for the doorway. For Ryn. For the only exit.

She could have run. The moment Gideon and I cleared the threshold she could have pulled the shim and bolted into the alley dark, and no one would ever have blamed her—she owed us a life debt and had repaid it twice over by then, and everyone in that cathedral knew it except her.

Instead she stepped back *inside*. She planted herself in the postern with a knife in each hand and a smuggler's oath in her mouth, and she bought us the length of the alley, one blade-stroke per stride, laughing that terrible laugh of hers, shouting—"Go, go, GO, don't you dare look back, princess, I'll catch up, I always catch up—"

The last thing I saw through the closing door was the warden-line folding around her like a fist closing, and Ryn Sable going down under it still swinging, still laughing, and the door booming shut on the shim snapping like a bone.

* * *

We ran. Of course we ran. That is the arithmetic of it: she spent herself so that we could run, and we ran, because anything else would have made her spending worthless.

Gideon got us through the tanners' quarter with blood on their sleeves and no memory of acquiring it. We came out the far side of the river as the cathedral bells began to ring—not the keeper's bell, the cathedral's own, pealing victory—and somewhere behind us, rolling out over the rooftops in a voice that was my brother's voice and not my brother's voice at all, the proclamation:

"The traitor Isolde is sentenced. The heretic keeper is sentenced. The smuggler Ryn Sable is taken alive, and will be anointed at the next renewal, that her debts may be paid in the manner of the faithful."

*Paid in the manner of the faithful.* Hollowed. Fed to the thing my brother wore, made into one more smiling face in the congregation, one more leaning thread of smoke.

I stopped running in a drainage culvert under the tannery wall and put my fists against the stones and did not cry, because crying was not useful yet. Gideon stood over me, breathing hard, and after a long time they said, very quietly:

"They took the bell tower too. Maerin's up there."

And that was how the night ended—not with the crown coming down, as we had named it, as we had dreamed it, but with everything else coming down instead. Our plan shattered on the marble. Ryn in chains. Maerin hunted. The king carried deeper into the sanctum beneath the cathedral, behind doors that had opened for him like a throat.

Only one moon remained before the thirteenth turn. One moon, and the gate at Thornfell, and a rite that needed a relic we still held, a renunciation no one had spoken, a willing witness we might never free—

Gideon crouched down beside me in the dark of the culvert, and their hand, iron-hard and gentle, settled on the back of my neck the way my mother's used to.

"We bury no one tonight," they said. "Do you hear me? We dig them out. Both of them. Starting now."

Far off across the city, the cathedral bells were still ringing.

They sounded, to me, like chewing.
