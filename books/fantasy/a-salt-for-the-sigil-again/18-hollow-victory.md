## 18. Hollow Victory

The notice came nailed to the door of the watchpost, written in a hand too beautiful for its message.

*By grace of the Crown Eternal, the prisoner Ryn Sable shall walk the Processional Road at midday, three days hence. Let all who love her witness her freedom — purchased by their surrender. The road is open. The god is merciful.*

Below it, in smaller script, as if added as an afterthought: *Gideon Villalobos is known to Us. Come home.*

Maerin read it twice and then set it down on the table with the care of someone handling a snake. "It knows your name."

"It's known my name for thirty years." I turned the paper over, looking for a seal that wasn't there. "It just never bothered to use it before."

Isolde stood at the window, arms crossed against the cold that had come down off Thornfell early this year. "Three days," she said. "One moon left after that. It's not offering us time to think. It's offering us time to *break*."

"It's offering us a trap," Maerin said.

"Both things can be true." Isolde's voice was flat and steady in the way it had been since the coronation went wrong, since Ryn had stepped backward into the warden's net so the rest of us could go over a wall and live. She hadn't slept properly since. None of us had. But grief sat differently on each of us: Maerin wore hers like penance, I wore mine like an old coat, and Isolde wore hers like armor she'd forged herself from the wreckage of her family.

I looked at the notice again. *Come home.*

Thirty years ago, those words would have worked.

* * *

We spent the first day doing nothing but counting what we had, because that was the only honest arithmetic left to us.

What we had: one defrocked priestess who knew the liturgy of the thing we fought better than any living soul should; one princess whose name still opened doors in a city that had forgotten her face; one iron bell pulled from a flooded shrine, wrapped in oilcloth in the corner like a sleeping animal; the rite's components, two of three; and one moon left before the thirteenth turn closed every door forever.

What we did not have: Ryn. A plan. An army.

"We could surrender," Maerin said quietly, on the second night. She said it the way you have to say terrible things out loud sometimes, just to prove they don't own you. "All of us. It might even keep its word about her."

"It won't," Isolde said.

"No," I agreed. "But thank you for saying it anyway. Someone had to."

Maerin looked at me across the candle flame. Her face had changed since the cells — there were lines around her mouth that hadn't been there in the spring, and her left hand still didn't close all the way. "You're going to the city."

"I'm going to the Processional Road," I said. "There's a difference."

"And when it offers you everything you've ever lost in front of a thousand witnesses?"

I fed another stick into the stove and watched it catch. "Then a thousand witnesses will see me refuse."

She didn't believe me. I could tell, because she reached over and touched my wrist, once, briefly — the gesture of a woman who had spent her life administering last rites and knew exactly how they felt from the giving end.

* * *

The capital had grown worse in the weeks since we'd fled it. We came in through Ryn's tunnels — her tunnels, empty now, the chalk marks she'd left on the walls fading but legible to anyone who'd learned to read them, which was exactly one person fewer than before. Isolde walked ahead of me with her hood up, and Maerin behind, and the city above us breathed its sick incense down through every grate.

Piety hung over Vael Corin like weather. You could hear it from the tunnels: bells at odd hours, choirs rehearsing for the Processional, and underneath it all, that low continuous murmur of ten thousand people praying at once, because praying had become the tax you paid to be healed, to be fed, to be permitted to remember your mother's face.

We surfaced in a dyer's cellar near the tanners' quarter, in a room that smelled of urine and mordant, and waited for midday.

The Processional Road ran from the Godsgate to the palace steps, a mile of white stone kept clean by crews who swept away anything the crowds dropped — including, in the last months, people. At midday the bells began, and the crowd gathered, and I found my place among them with my hood low and my heart going like a fist against a door.

They brought her out at the third bell.

Ryn Sable walked between six wardens in white-and-gold, and the crowd roared, because the crown had told them this was mercy and crowds believe what they are told. She was thinner. There was a bandage at her ribs where the blade had gone in during the raid, and her wrists were bare — they'd taken her knives, her rings, the little copper charm she'd worn since I'd known her. But her head was up. Her head was *up*, and she was scanning the crowd the way she used to scan a customs house, quick and sideways, cataloguing exits, counting friends.

Looking for us. Even now. Even like this.

A platform had been raised at the road's midpoint, draped in white, and on it stood the High Warden — hollow-eyed, half-gone, his voice coming out of him like wind through a ruined house — and beside him, wearing the king's body like a borrowed coat, *it*.

I had seen Aldric twice before, from a distance. Up close, even through a crowd, the wrongness of him made my teeth ache. He stood too still. He smiled at moments that had no reason for smiling. And when he raised his hands, the whole mile of road went silent so fast it was like a wound closing.

"People of the city," he said, and his voice carried without effort, warm as banked coals. "You know this woman. She stole from you. She lied to you. She consorted with the heretic Gideon Villalobos, keeper of a dead gate, worshipper of dead gods." A ripple of hatred moved through the crowd, and I felt it pass over me like the shadow of a bird. "And yet. The god is merciful."

He turned to Ryn. Took her chin in his hand — the king's hand, young and unscarred — and tilted her face up toward the crowd.

"This woman may walk free. This very hour. Unhurt, unmarked, forgiven." His smile widened by a degree, precise as a mechanism. "She need only point to the friends who hide among you. The priestess. The princess. The keeper. Point, and be free. Stay silent, and the process continues."

The crowd murmured. Somewhere near me a woman whispered *point, girl, point* with the desperate pragmatism of someone who'd sold three years of her own memory for a cure and saw nothing wrong with selling someone else's freedom for a blessing.

Ryn looked out over the sea of faces.

She found me. I don't know how — hood, distance, a thousand bodies between us — but she found me, and something passed across her face that wasn't fear at all. It was the look she'd worn the day she'd dragged me out of the river below Thornfell with a hook and a rope and no good reason except that leaving me there would have been *wrong*, and Ryn Sable had never once in her life done the thing that was wrong when the thing that was right was available.

"I don't see anyone I know," she said, loud enough to carry. "Except you. Wearing my king."

The silence that followed had weight. The High Warden's hand went to his sword. The thing in Aldric's body tilted its head, and for a moment the smile fell away entirely, and what looked out of the king's face was old beyond reckoning and hungry beyond describing.

"You would die for them," it said, curious, almost gentle. "Why? They cannot save you. They cannot even save themselves. I have watched your keeper for thirty years, little thief. I know what he dreams of. Ask him what I offered him at the shrine. Ask him why he still walks alone."

"Because he keeps saying no," Ryn said. "It's his best quality."

The crowd laughed. It was a small sound, nervous, quickly smothered — but it happened. In the heart of the god's own city, in front of its own borrowed king, someone laughed at it, and the laugh spread like fire in dry grass until the wardens started shoving people to stop it.

That was when Captain Serah Vane stepped forward.

I had seen her once before, at the raid on the safehouse — a tall woman in warden grey, giving orders in a voice like a drawn blade, and I had marked her then as dangerous and thought no more about it. Now she walked onto the platform past the High Warden, past the god in the king's skin, and stopped at the edge where the crowd could see her, and she did the single most impossible thing I had witnessed in thirty years of watching the law devour the innocent.

She removed her warden's badge and held it up.

"My name is Serah Vane," she called. "For eleven years I have served the crown of Vael Corin. I have burned villages on the crown's order. I have arrested healers and hanged smugglers and called it justice, because the law told me it was justice and I did not look closely enough to see otherwise." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "Last month I was ordered to clear the beggars' ward in Saltrow. Two hundred souls. They had prayed for rain during the drought, and their prayers were answered, and the answer ate them. I stood in that ward and I looked into faces that had nothing left inside them — mothers who couldn't remember their children's names, old men smiling at walls. And I understood that I had not been keeping the peace. I had been feeding it."

The crowd had gone utterly still. Even the god had gone still.

"The law bends today," Serah Vane said, "or it breaks. I choose bending." She turned to face the thing wearing Aldric, and her hand did not shake. "Release the prisoner. All prisoners. Or every soldier I command walks off this road right now, and the city sees which side its wardens choose."

Behind her, forty soldiers in warden grey stepped forward in perfect unison and removed their badges.

Forty. I counted them twice. Forty men and women who had seen the hollowed poor and could not unsee them, standing in the god's own light and refusing it.

The thing in the king's body looked at Serah Vane for a long, long moment. When it spoke again, the warmth was gone from its voice, and what remained was the sound I had heard through the gate for thirty years, muffled and patient and vast.

"You would trade your law for one thief."

"I'd trade it for the truth," Vane said. "The law was supposed to be the truth's servant. It's been your kitchen long enough."

Something moved through the crowd then — not a riot, not yet, but a shifting, a great slow turning of bodies, thousands of people facing the platform instead of the spectacle, and I realized with a jolt that the god had made a mistake it could not take back. It had wanted witnesses. It had wanted a city watching mercy performed. And now the city was watching something else: the law itself, kneeling.

The god released Ryn.

I watched it happen — watched the king's hand open, watched the wardens fall back, watched Ryn Sable stand alone on that white platform with her wrists bare and her head high, and I understood that this was not defeat. Not for it. This was bookkeeping. It had one moon left, and it had decided that a public surrender cost less than a public schism, and it filed Vane's defection away the way a lender files away a debt.

But it had not counted on what the crowd did next.

They didn't cheer. That was the terrible, wonderful thing. They went to their knees — not to the god, but *away* from it, turning their backs by degrees, a tide of ordinary people choosing with their bodies what they had never been permitted to choose with their voices. A woman near me took her child's hand and walked out of the square. Then another. Then dozens.

The god stood in the middle of its own miracle and watched it walk away.

* * *

We got Ryn back through the tunnels by dusk.

She was quiet for most of it, leaning on my shoulder where the bandage pulled, and I matched my steps to hers and said nothing, because some debts are paid in silence. It was only when we reached the dyer's cellar and Maerin was wrapping her ribs again and Isolde was holding her hands hard enough to hurt that Ryn finally spoke.

"He talked to me," she said. "In the cells. Every day. Told me stories." She laughed, a thin, ugly sound. "Told me about you, Gideon. About the river. Said you dream about your mentor's voice and can't remember her face anymore, and that it could give you both back, any time you asked."

The room went very still. Isolde's eyes came up to mine. Maerin's hands paused on the bandage.

"That's true," I said.

"Gideon—" Isolde started.

"It's true," I repeated, "and it doesn't matter, because I'm going to say no. I've been saying no for thirty years. I'll say it one more time under the dark moon, and then none of us will have to hear its voice again." I looked at each of them in turn — the priestess with her scarred hands, the princess with her forged armor, the smuggler with her bare wrists — and I found that my voice, when it came, was steadier than I had any right to expect. "But listen to me. All of you. When I go into that seal, it will offer you things too. It will offer you each other. It will offer you *me*. Whatever it shows you — whatever it promises —"

"We know," Ryn said softly.

"You don't. Not yet. You will, and it will feel like the truest thing you've ever—"

"*Gideon.*" Ryn caught my sleeve. "We know. Because we already chose. All of us. In our own ways, at our own worst moments — we already chose each other over what it offered. That's the whole point of us." She smiled, and it was crooked and tired and more precious to me than anything the god could have minted. "You're the only one still deciding. So decide faster."

Outside, far off toward the palace, the bells began to ring — not the god's hours, but alarm bells, ragged and urgent. Shouts rose in the streets above. Boots on stone, many boots, moving fast.

Maerin went to the grate and listened, and when she turned back her face was pale in the lamplight.

"They've taken the palace," she said. "Or something has. Listen — that's not wardens shouting. That's *servants*. The god is moving Aldric somewhere deep, somewhere warded, and it's taking the whole sanctum with it." She met my eyes. "It's not waiting for the thirteenth moon anymore. It's making its own darkness under the mountain."

I picked up the iron bell, and it was cold, and heavier than it had ever been.

"Then we go down after it," I said. "Tonight."
