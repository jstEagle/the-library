## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The dark moon rose as a hole in the sky, and the gate drank it.

Gideon stood before the Thornfell seal for what they knew—whatever else the night would take—might be the last time as themselves. Thirty years of frost and lichen clung to the stone arch. Behind it, the sigils waited: three of them, carved into the living rock by hands older than any kingdom, each one a lock on a door that should never have been built.

They had come at moonset, all of them. That had not been the plan. The plan, such as it was, had required only Gideon, Maerin's voice, and Isolde's steady hands on the bell. But Ryn had refused to stay behind with her ribs still bound, and Aldric—thin, hollow-eyed, three days free of the crown and not yet whole—had insisted on walking every mile of the mountain road himself.

"I need to see it end," he'd said. "Even if I don't remember seeing it."

So they came, six souls and one bell, up through the pines where the snow held last year's shadows, to the clearing where the world had been stitched shut.

Maerin built the circle first. She worked in silence, chalk made from crushed bone-meal and ash, laying down the old geometry with hands that shook only when she thought no one watched. She had served this god once. She had sung its liturgy in a white robe, and she had learned, too late, what the hymns were for. Now she sang them backward, note by note, unwriting her own faith with her mouth.

"Keeper." Her voice cracked on the title. "When you cross the line, you'll feel it start. Don't fight the first one. Fighting makes it worse."

"I know," Gideon said.

"You don't. No one does until—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry it has to be you."

"It was always going to be someone," Gideon said. "Better me than the world."

It wasn't bravery. It was arithmetic. Three sigils, three turns of memory, and the thing behind the door starved back into the long dark where it belonged. Gideon had done the sums a hundred times since Maerin had spoken the price aloud under the twelfth moon, and every sum came out the same: their mentor's face, thirty years of roads, the names of everyone they loved. Against that: everything. Every valley, every child not yet hollowed, every prayer answered honestly by rain instead of appetite.

Isolde stood at the eastern point of the circle with the iron bell cradled against her chest. It was small, ugly, dented along one side—the keeper's bell, dredged up from the flooded shrine, still cold enough to burn bare skin after all these centuries. When she rang it, the sound did not travel outward. It fell. It dropped through the air like a stone through water, and Gideon felt it land somewhere behind their sternum.

"Say it again," Isolde said quietly. "Before we start. I want to hear it."

Gideon looked at her—the runaway princess who had crossed a mountain range alone rather than stop loving a brother who couldn't remember her—and gave her the truth one more time.

"When the seal closes, I won't know you. Any of you. I won't know why I'm standing here. I might be afraid of you. Don't let that stop you."

"It won't."

"And Isolde—" Their throat tightened around the next part. They had rehearsed it and it still wouldn't come easily. "Read me the journal. All of it. Even the parts about him." They nodded toward the treeline, where Ryn leaned against a pine with her arms crossed and her knife loose in its sheath, pretending to be bored. "Especially those."

"I will." Her jaw set the way Aldric's did, when the king was being stubborn about something kind. "Every word."

Ryn pushed off the tree and came close. For a moment she said nothing, just looked at Gideon with the flat assessing stare she used on cargo and cliff faces and other things she intended to move whether they liked it or not.

"You saved my life on the Kessrin pass," she said. "You didn't have to. Nobody would have blamed you for leaving me in the snow. You carried me eleven miles." She tapped her own ribs, above the warden's blade-scar. "So here's how this goes. You forget me, fine. But I don't forget you. And I've got roads, keeper. I've got roads nobody's ever walked twice. I'll walk them until your face comes back."

"That's not how it works," Maerin murmured. "Probably."

"Probably is good odds where I'm from."

Aldric came forward last. He looked better than he had in the capital—color in his face, some of the terrible smoothness gone from his expression—but he still flinched when the wind moved wrong, still glanced at the gate like a man glancing at a cell he'd once shared with something hungry.

"Gideon Villalobos." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "My sister wrote it in her letter to herself forty times. I want to say it while it means something to me." He extended his hand. "Whatever it costs you—I will spend the rest of my reign paying it back. I swear it on the crown I mean to break apart."

Gideon took the hand. The king's grip was warm and human and shaking.

"Don't swear oaths to me," they said. "Go home and feed people. That's oath enough."

* * *

Maerin began the chant as the last light left the sky.

She started low, almost below hearing—a drone that lived in the chest rather than the ear. The words were Old Thallic, the tongue of the first keepers, and Gideon knew them the way they knew their own heartbeat: *Vessel open, vessel willing, salt for the sigil, salt for the wound.* The rite's oldest line, the one the drowned shrine had carved over and over until the stone wore soft. Salt for the wound. A keeper's grief, offered freely, to close what greed had opened.

Gideon stepped across the chalk.

The change was immediate and total, like stepping out of a warm house into a river. Cold climbed their legs. Not mountain-cold—older. The cold of the space between stars, leaking thin through the seam of the world. Ahead of them, the first sigil began to glow, a dull ember-red like coals breathing.

"First turn," Maerin called, her voice steady now, locked into the liturgy. "The teacher. Speak the renunciation."

Gideon closed their eyes.

Serra Vane. Ninety years old and straight-backed as a spear. Hands like tree roots. She had found Gideon half-starved at fourteen, stealing bread from a shrine offering, and instead of striking them she'd sat down beside them and asked if they wanted to learn why the bread mattered. Twenty years she had taught them: the wards, the watchwords, the patience. She had died at this very gate—or she had not died; she had gone into the seal when the gods last woke, binding herself into the stone so her apprentice could live and keep the watch. Gideon had inherited her post and her ghost and thirty years of talking to a wall because the wall was the closest thing left to her voice.

*I renounce the keeping of Serra Vane,* they said. *Her face, her voice, her years. Take them.*

The sigil flared white.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it. It simply lifted things out of them, gently, the way you lift stones off a sleeping body. Gideon felt the memories go—felt the specific weight of them leave—and reached for Serra Vane's face and found—

Nothing. A shape where a person had been. An apprenticeship with no master. Thirty years of standing at a gate, mourning someone whose name dissolved even as they groped for it.

They heard themselves make a sound. Small. Animal. Somewhere beyond the chalk line, Isolde rang the bell, and the falling note caught Gideon like a hand catching a stumble.

"Steady," Maerin said. "Second sigil. The roads. Speak it."

The second sigil woke amber-gold, and this time Gideon spoke faster, before courage could reconsider.

*I renounce the roads. Every mile I ever walked. Every map in my head, every pass and ford and smuggler's stair. Take them.*

And the world folded up like a letter. Thirty years of geography poured out of them—the Kessrin pass where they'd pulled a dying smuggler from the snow (a debt, they knew there was a debt, they could see its shape but not its face); the flooded shrine; the capital's prayer-markets; the valley road where the wardens had burned a village that worshipped nothing. All of it went, and what remained was a body standing on a mountain knowing only *here*, this stone, this cold, this task. They had been nowhere. They had done nothing but stand.

Their knees hit the frost. Someone cried out beyond the circle—Ryn, maybe, or Isolde—and the bell fell again, that sinking note, holding them to the world by a single thread of sound.

"Last one." Maerin's voice broke the liturgy's rhythm for half a breath. Then she steadied. "Third sigil. The names. Speak the renunciation, keeper. Finish it."

The third sigil opened like an eye.

Behind the gate, something moved.

Gideon saw it then—really saw it, for the first time in thirty years, unmediated by stone and sigil. It was vast and it was thin, stretched across the dark like smoke across a doorway, and it was starving. It had fed on answered prayers for a season and it had grown fat and then leaner than fat, because nothing it ate had ever filled it. Its hunger had a shape now. Its hunger was looking directly at Gideon with a thousand stolen eyes—shepherd-eyes, child-eyes, the High Warden's milky half-hollow gaze.

And it spoke. Not in words. In offering.

*Stay,* it said, pouring itself into the shape of everything Gideon had lost. *The old woman is here. I kept her, keeper. I keep everything that prays. Stay inside the seal and have her back—her voice, her hands, twenty more years of teaching. Stay and remember your roads. Stay and know their names forever. I ask so little. Only remain. Only be mine.*

The third sigil burned between them, waiting.

Gideon knelt in the frost with a hole where a mentor had been, a blank where a lifetime of roads had been, and reached for the names of the people standing beyond the chalk line—and found them going, going, slipping like water, and understood with perfect clarity that this was the moment the god had waited thirty years for. Not strength. Strength it could starve. But loneliness—loneliness it could feed on forever.

*I renounce the names,* Gideon said, and their voice came out ragged and human and absolutely certain. *Isolde. Ryn. Maerin. Aldric. I renounce knowing them. I give them up freely, and I give them nothing else—not my fear, not my longing, not my company. Take the memories and choke on them. Starve.*

They reached out and pressed their palm to the third sigil.

The scream started behind the gate.

It was not one voice. It was every voice it had ever worn, every shepherd and sickbed child and crowned boy, all screaming at once as the seal drank back what it had lent out. The god thrashed against the closing dark, and the dark was not a prison—it was an absence, and absence cannot be argued with or bargained with or bribed with borrowed love. It only closes. It closed.

Sigil by sigil, the light died. Red to ember to ash. Gold to gutter to gray. The third burned longest, white-hot, searing the renunciation into the stone in letters that would glow faintly for a thousand years—*willingly given, freely lost, salt for the sigil again*—and then it too went out.

The bell fell one final time, and the silence afterward was the deepest silence in the world.

Gideon Villalobos stood at the gate and knew nothing.

Not the mountain. Not the year. Not the blood on their split lip or the reason six strangers were running toward them through the snow, shouting a name they no longer owned. The cold was just cold. The stone was just stone. And the figure reaching them first—a woman with a dented iron bell and tears freezing on her cheeks—was a stranger, and Gideon shrank back from her hands, heart hammering, and asked the only question left in them:

"Where am I? Who am I?"

Isolde caught their face in both hands, gentle as handling a wounded bird, and smiled through the ruin of her crying, and said:

"You're the keeper. And I'm going to tell you a story. It's going to take a while, and it starts with a smuggler who owes you eleven miles."

Behind them, at the treeline, a man who had been a king for three days stood in the falling snow with his head bowed—and high overhead, unseen by anyone, the thirteenth new moon began, at last, to turn.
