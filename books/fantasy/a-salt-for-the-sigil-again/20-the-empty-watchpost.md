## 20. The Empty Watchpost

The bell stopped ringing at dawn.

Isolde felt it in her chest before she heard the silence—a pressure lifting, like a hand that had been pressed against her sternum for thirteen months finally withdrawing. She was kneeling in the frost-slicked grass below the gate with Maerin's chant still drying on her lips and Gideon's iron bell heavy in her hands, and when the last sigil went dark beneath the stone, the whole mountain exhaled.

So did she. She hadn't realized she'd been holding that breath since the twelfth moon.

"Gideon?" Her voice came out cracked, half-swallowed by the wind off the pass.

Gideon stood inside the seal-line where they'd been standing for three days—inside it, which no living keeper should have been able to do, which Maerin had wept to permit—and they turned around slowly, the way a door turns on a hinge that has not been oiled in years.

They looked at her.

Isolde had prepared herself for grief. She had rehearsed it on the long climb up from the valley, composing sentences like *I'm sorry* and *you knew the price* and *we'll find a way*, arranging her face into something worthy of what Gideon had paid. She had imagined Gideon weeping, or raging, or collapsing into Ryn's arms.

She had not imagined this: Gideon looking at her with the flat, courteous attention of a stranger assessing weather.

"Can I help you?" Gideon said.

The words landed like a blade between ribs. Not cruel—there was nothing left in them to be cruel with. Just empty. Just a person standing in their own body with all the furniture carried out.

"They're gone," Maerin whispered behind her. The priestess was on her knees, her scarred hands pressed flat to the cold stone of the first sigil. "All three turns. Mentor, roads, names. It took everything in order." She looked up at Isolde, and her eyes were red-rimmed and terrible. "It took everything."

* * *

Aldric woke on the second day, in the watchpost, under wool blankets that smelled of woodsmoke and old pine.

Isolde was sitting beside him when his eyes opened—she had barely slept, had spent the night watching his face for any flicker of the thing that had worn him, some residue of the god behind the seal—but what she saw instead was worse than corruption and better than anything she'd dared hope. She saw her brother. Just her brother, blinking at the rafters, confused, human, thirty years old and looking every hour of it.

"Izzy," he said.

And she broke. All the composure she'd kept through the infiltration, through the ceremony gone wrong, through Ryn's capture and the warden captain's defection and the terrible march back to Thornfell—it came apart in about four seconds, and she was crying onto her brother's chest while he patted her shoulder with the bewildered gentleness of a man who has been told he was ill and is only now beginning to believe how ill.

"How long," he said.

"Thirteen moons. Maybe longer. Maybe it started before anyone noticed."

"The prayers. The crops. I remember—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I remember watching myself sign things. I remember being very far away inside my own head, and something else driving, and it liked the singing. Gods, Izzy, it liked the singing so much." He shuddered, full-bodied. "There were people who sold their memories. In the capital. For healing."

"Years of them. The poor mostly. They called it tithing grace."

His face changed—the king coming up through the man, or the man coming up through the king, Isolde could never quite tell the difference and had stopped thinking it mattered. "Then it ends today," Aldric said. "Every tithe-house closed. Every ledger of bought memory burned. Every person who sold years of themselves gets them written down somewhere safe, and if the memories can't come back, then the crown owes them a life worth as much." He pushed himself upright, swaying, and gripped her wrist hard enough to hurt. "Where's the keeper? The one at the gate. Maerin told me—I remember fragments, someone chanting, someone walking into the stone—"

Isolde looked away.

"Izzy. Where are they?"

"At the gate," she said. "They're always going to be at the gate. That's the problem."

* * *

Ryn Sable arrived on the third day, riding a stolen warden horse at a pace that would have gotten her hanged in peacetime, with her ribs wrapped in linen and her face gray with pain, and a freed Maerin clinging to the saddle behind her because the priestess's legs had not yet remembered how to be trusted.

They found Isolde outside the watchpost, sitting on the step with a leather-bound journal in her lap, and Ryn read the situation in one sweep of her sharp smuggler's eyes.

"He doesn't know us," Isolde said.

"Yeah." Ryn swung down from the horse badly, caught herself on the stirrup, swore. "I figured. Saw the look on their face when we rode past. Polite. Like we were travelers asking directions." She grimaced and pressed a hand to her bandaged side. "Thirty years keeping that gate, and it ate them like a meal."

"It didn't eat them. It ate what they loved. There's a difference." Isolde heard her own voice go strange. "Maerin says the seal takes one turn of memory per sigil, and it takes what binds deepest first. So it started with Vessa. Their mentor. Then the roads—all the years of walking Thornfell. Then names." She looked down at the journal. "It saved the names for last because they were the hardest to chew."

"And that?"

"This is Gideon's journal. From the watchpost. Thirty years of entries." She turned it over in her hands. "Maerin found it in the strongbox under the floor. She says—" Isolde stopped, steadied herself. "She says love leaves traces. That the seal eats memory but it can't eat what memory built. The shape of it. The habit of it. She says if we tell them who they were, often enough, patiently enough, some of it might grow back. Like a bone knitting. No promises. But possible."

Ryn was quiet a moment. Wind moved through the pines above the gate, and somewhere down the valley a shepherd's dog barked at nothing, at everything, at the world continuing.

"My debt's repaid," Ryn said finally. "Has been for a while. I could walk away. Take my ledger money—which, incidentally, I burned—and disappear somewhere warm." She lowered herself carefully onto the step beside Isolde, hissing at her ribs. "Instead I'm going to sit here and help you talk at a stranger until the stranger loves us again. Because that's what family does, apparently. Nobody warned me. I'd have negotiated harder."

Despite everything, Isolde laughed—a wet, broken sound, but a laugh.

"Start tonight?" Ryn asked.

"Start now," said Isolde. "We've lost enough time."

* * *

Gideon was at the gate, of course. Standing before the sealed stone with their hands loose at their sides, watching it the way a man watches a fire he doesn't remember lighting.

Isolde approached slowly, the journal held against her chest like a shield. Up close, Gideon looked diminished—not physically; they were as lean and weathered as ever—but somehow smaller, the way an empty house looks smaller than a full one. Their eyes tracked her without recognition and without fear. Just attention. A keeper's attention, even hollowed. Some things ran deeper than memory.

"You stand here a lot," Isolde said. "I've watched you. You come out every morning and every evening, and you check the sigils, and you touch the third one from the left twice, like you're greeting it."

"It seems important," Gideon said. Their voice was level, pleasant, terribly empty. "I don't know why. My hands know things. The rest of me doesn't."

"That's why I'm here." Isolde sat down on the flat rock at the gate's base—the same rock, she now knew, where Gideon had sat for thirty years eating supper alone. She opened the journal. "This is yours. Maerin says reading it might... help. If you'll let me."

Gideon considered her for a long moment. Then they sat down on the other end of the rock, leaving a careful stranger's distance between them, and folded their hands. "Go ahead."

Isolde cleared her throat. Began.

*"First entry. Year one of my keeping. Vessa walked me the whole circuit today and made me name every wardstone aloud, and when I got the fourth one wrong she hit me across the knuckles with her stick and said, 'The mountain will forgive a lazy keeper, Gideon, but it will not forgive a sloppy one.' I hate her. I think I am going to love her. I can already feel it happening, like weather coming."*

She glanced up. Nothing moved in Gideon's face. She kept reading.

*"Vessa died eleven years ago. I still set out two cups at supper. Tonight I drank both. I am not ashamed of this. Or I am, a little. She'd laugh at me either way."*

Still nothing. But Gideon's hands, folded in their lap, had shifted—one thumb moving slowly across the back of the other hand, a small unconscious motion, like someone smoothing a page.

Isolde read on. She read about the winters, the wolves, the smuggler caught on the high road nine years ago who'd turned out to be carrying a wounded child instead of contraband, and how Gideon had let her go and been let go in turn, later, on a night when it mattered. She read about a runaway princess who'd stumbled half-frozen into the watchpost fourteen months ago, and how Gideon had fed her stew and asked no questions for three days, and then finally asked one, and how the answer had changed everything.

At that entry, Gideon frowned slightly. "That's you," they said. "In the story."

"Yes."

"I don't remember you." The words were apologetic, almost gentle. "But I appear to have liked you."

"You did," Isolde said, and her voice nearly failed. "You do. Somewhere in there. Keep listening."

She read until the light failed. She read about the letter in Aldric's handwriting that Aldric didn't remember writing. About the thirteenth moon. About the rite, and the price, and the night—she found the entry, dated four days ago, the handwriting suddenly hurried, suddenly human—where Gideon had written: *If you are reading this aloud to me, then I did it, and I don't remember doing it, and I need you to know that I would do it again. Whoever you are. Whatever I've forgotten. I chose this with my whole self, and my whole self thought you were worth it.*

Isolde stopped. The tears came then, finally, the ones she'd been rationing for days, and she let them fall without wiping them away because it seemed important that Gideon see them—that they understand the weight of what their forgotten self had decided.

Gideon was staring at her. Something had moved behind their eyes—not memory, not yet, but the shadow where memory used to live. The place a picture leaves on a wall after it's taken down.

"I wrote that," they said quietly. "About you. Whoever you are."

"Isolde. My name is Isolde."

"Isolde." They tried it out. Their brow furrowed. "Why does that taste like snow?"

"Because you pulled me out of a snowdrift," Isolde said, "and put me in front of your fire, and when I woke up screaming for a brother who'd forgotten me, you sat with me all night and told me about your gate. You said, 'Everything worth keeping costs something. The trick is deciding what's worth it.'" She wiped her face with her sleeve. "I've been deciding ever since."

The wind came down off the pass and moved through the pines, and the first sigil—the one Gideon touched twice each morning—caught the last red light of sunset and held it, faintly warm, faintly alive.

Gideon reached out and touched it. Twice. Then they looked at their own hand as though it belonged to someone braver.

"Read me another one," they said. "The one about the smuggler. I want to know why my chest hurts when you say her name."

* * *

They read for six nights.

On the seventh, Ryn brought stew up to the gate—bad stew, scorched, made by a woman whose talents lay entirely elsewhere—and set it down between the three of them without comment, and Gideon ate it and said, "You cook like someone who learned on a ship," and Ryn went very still and then laughed until her stitches nearly tore, and Isolde understood that Maerin had been right. Love leaves traces. The seal couldn't reach them.

On the ninth night, Aldric came. No crown, no guard, just a king in a plain coat climbing a shepherd's path in the dark because his sister had sent word that it was time. He stood before Gideon at the gate, and Gideon studied him with that courteous blankness, and Aldric bowed—actually bowed, low, the way kings bow to no one.

"You don't know me," Aldric said. "But I'm told you gave up everyone you loved to give me back to myself. So I've come to be known again. However long it takes. I'm told I'm a slow student, but I'm told you're a patient teacher."

Gideon looked at him a long moment. Then, slowly, they touched the third sigil from the left—twice, the greeting, the habit outliving its meaning—and said:

"There's room at the watchpost. If you're staying."

It wasn't memory. It wasn't forgiveness, even. But it was a door, opening, and Isolde stood in the dark with the journal pressed to her heart and understood that this was how it would go—not one great restoration but a thousand small returns, a name offered here, a story there, a bowl of bad stew, a bow from a king. Bone by bone. Name by name.

And then, as they turned toward the watchpost together, Maerin came running up the path from the valley, torchlight jumping ahead of her, her voice raw:

"The eastern sigils. Three of them, on the far range—they're warm. Not awake. Not yet. But warm."
