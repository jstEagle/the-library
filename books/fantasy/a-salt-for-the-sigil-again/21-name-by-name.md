## 21. Name by Name

Ryn Sable had smuggled a hundred things over her years on the roads: salt, silk, fugitives, a live wyvern chick once, in a crate lined with wool and lies. She had never smuggled a memory back into a person, and it turned out the cargo was heavier than anything she'd ever carried.

Gideon sat at the watchpost table where they'd sat a thousand times before—Ryn knew that number now, because Isolde had counted the notches in the bench and Maerin had counted the entries in the journal—and looked at her with the flat, patient courtesy of a stranger. Their hands were folded on the wood. Their eyes were clear. That was the worst part. The seal hadn't left them mad or empty-eyed; it had left them *tidy*, a person with all their edges sanded off, every name filed away behind a door that no longer opened.

"Tell me again," Gideon said. "The part with the river."

"You've heard it four times."

"I know." A pause. Something moved behind their eyes, like a fish under ice. "It feels different each time. Like I'm hearing it through a wall, and the wall is getting thinner."

Ryn pulled her chair around to the same side of the table, close enough that her knee bumped theirs, because Maerin had said proximity mattered, that the body remembered what the mind had surrendered. "Fine. But you're hearing it from me this time, not from Isolde's reading voice. Isolde reads like she's reciting scripture. I tell it like it happened."

"Which is?"

"Ugly. Cold. And the best night of my life until recently, so don't interrupt."

* * *

She started, as she always did, with the debt.

"The year I was nineteen I ran cargo for a man named Vetch Corrin, who paid well and lied beautifully, which should have been my first warning. He had me moving crates up the Thornfell switchbacks in dead of winter—said they were dye. They were not dye. They were prayer-anchors, little iron bells packed in salt, though I didn't know that then, and neither did you, probably, or you'd have burned them on sight instead of doing what you did.

"What you did was catch me. Third switchback, whiteout coming down the mountain, and you stepped out of the snow like you'd grown there. Grey coat, grey eyes, this expression like I was a stain you were deciding whether to scrub. You had a crossbow. I had a knife and a very fast mouth, and I used both—the mouth first. I talked for ten minutes straight about dye lots and tariffs and my poor sick mother, and you listened to all of it with your head tilted, and then you said—" Ryn dropped into her imitation of Gideon's flat keeper's cadence, which Isolde swore got better every week—"'Your mother has been dead six years. I attended the burial.'

"And I stopped talking. Because nobody knew that. Nobody alive knew that. And you said, 'Vetch's bells are waking something on the mountain. Every crate he moves takes a day off someone's life down-valley. So here are your choices.' And you laid them out so reasonably. Give up the cargo. Or give up the road. Or—" she tapped the long pale scar that ran from her left ear down under her collar— "this one. Except you never fired. You just stood there while the whiteout came down, and I realized you'd been standing between me and the drop-off the whole time, because you'd seen the ice crack under my feet before I had.

"Then the ice went anyway. All of it, the whole shelf, and we went down together into the ravine, and you caught a root one-handed and caught *me* with the other, and held us both against the cliff face in a blizzard for—I don't know how long. Long enough that I stopped being able to feel my legs. And you talked to me the whole time. Not comfort. Instructions. Where to put my weight, how to breathe, how to keep my grip when my fingers quit answering. You kept me alive by being absolutely relentless about the next five seconds, over and over, until dawn.

"When they pulled us out—you wouldn't let go of me until the rope reached you, did you know that? The rescuers said later you'd frozen your hand around my coat so bad they nearly took the fingers. You gave me your coat on the mountain and your blood on the rope and you never once asked me for anything. So I burned Vetch's bells in his own warehouse and took the brand for arson and spent eleven years running *away* from Thornfell because every time I came near this mountain I could feel you watching, and I couldn't stand owing you."

She stopped. In the corner of the room, Isolde had gone still over her mending; she'd heard the story before too, but she never tired of it, and Ryn understood why. It was the only version of Gideon Villalobos that still existed outside other people's mouths.

Gideon's brow had furrowed. Small. But real.

"The hand," they said slowly. "My right hand. It aches when it snows."

"It does," Ryn said, and her throat went tight. "It does ache when it snows. First thing you've remembered on your own."

* * *

Maerin called it residue. She'd found the word in a rite-book older than the gate, in a margin written by some dead priestess with cramped handwriting: *What is given in love is not taken clean. The seal eats the memory but not the shape it left.* Love leaves traces, she said, the way a ring leaves a mark on skin after years of wearing. The unbinding had taken Gideon's memories whole—but it couldn't take the calluses, the scars, the flinch, the ache in the right hand when it snowed. The body kept its own ledger.

So the family had made a plan, sitting up three nights running while Gideon slept in the loft, polite and lost among strangers who clearly loved them and whom they clearly did not know. They would tell everything. Not the journal—Isolde read from the journal, but the journal was Gideon's voice, and Gideon's voice meant nothing to Gideon yet. No. Each of them would give back what they alone carried. Name by name. Memory by memory. And if Maerin was right, if love left traces the seal couldn't eat, then somewhere in the scar tissue and the habits and the aching hand, the person would find their way home along roads already cut.

Ryn took mornings. She told herself it was because she was an early riser and Isolde wasn't and Maerin needed her prayers at dawn. She told herself a lot of things. The truth was that mornings were when the fear was worst—when she woke in the watchpost that wasn't hers and remembered that Gideon had looked at her two days after the sealing and asked Isolde, quietly, *who is the woman who keeps staring at me*, and Ryn had walked out into the snow and been sick behind the woodshed like a green recruit.

Debt repaid forward, she thought. That was the phrase she'd built her whole life around without knowing it. Someone holds you over a cliff; you spend the rest of your life holding other people. Well. Here was the last person who'd ever held her, and they didn't remember holding her, and the only payment left was to hold them instead.

* * *

On the ninth morning she changed tactics.

"Forget the stories," she said, hauling Gideon's coat off its peg and throwing it at them. "Stories aren't working. Your face does the thing. The polite thing. Put your coat on. We're going out."

"Where?"

"Roads. Yours. Mine. Ours." She was already pulling on her boots. "You spent thirty years walking these mountains. Your feet remember. Maerin says the body keeps its ledger. Fine. Let's audit it."

They walked the lower switchbacks in fresh snow, and Ryn narrated nothing. She just watched. At the third bend Gideon slowed—not stopped, nothing so obvious, but slowed, the way a horse slows passing a grave. At the fifth they reached out without looking and touched the trunk of a lightning-split pine, palm flat, the gesture of someone checking on an old wound. At the seventh they crouched, frowning, and brushed snow from a flat stone set into the bank, and beneath it was a carved mark: a circle with a line through it, worn shallow by weather and by thumbs.

"What's this?" Gideon asked.

"That's yours. Keeper's marks. You cut them every mile of the mountain road—thirty years of them. They're how you knew the seal was holding. Warm sigil, trouble. Cold stone, sleep easy." Ryn crouched beside them. "You used to touch that one every time you passed. Isolde says you did it absent-minded, like scratching an itch. Go on. Touch it."

Gideon touched it. And Ryn watched their shoulders come down half an inch, watched something unknot between the shoulder blades that had been knotted since the night the god starved and the seal closed and Gideon Villalobos forgot everyone they loved including themselves.

"I've done this," Gideon said. Not a question. "Many times."

"Ten thousand times, give or take."

"Why can't I see it?"

"Because somebody stole it out of your head to save the world." Ryn stood, offered her hand, hauled them up. "And because everybody who loved you is standing around waiting to steal it back. Come on. Next bend's the good one."

At the next bend she showed them the smuggler's path—a crack in the rockface no wider than a shoulder, invisible unless you knew, and Gideon knew, because their feet turned toward it before their head agreed. Inside, dry and dark, old grease-marks on the stone from Ryn's lantern, and scratched into the wall at shoulder height, small and unmistakable:

*R.S. + G.V. — even.*

"What's that?" Gideon asked.

"That's us settling accounts. Year after the ravine. You saved my life, I saved yours—we got pinned in here by a warden patrol during the Bellwinter riots, and you took a blade meant for me, and I dragged you up this crack bleeding, and afterward you said, 'Now we're even,' and I said, 'We will never be even, you impossible stone-faced saint, because you'll never let me repay you properly,' and you said—" Ryn's voice cracked, and she let it, because Maerin said that mattered too, that grief honestly shown was itself a kind of memory— "you said, 'Then stop counting. Just stay.'"

Gideon's hand rose to the scratch in the stone. Traced it. *Even.*

"I don't remember saying that," they whispered. "But I remember—" They stopped. Pressed their palm flat against the wall. "There's a weight. On my chest. When I look at those letters. Like something heavy was carried and set down."

"A knife wound," Ryn said. "Right side. Six inches. You have the scar. Look, if you want."

Gideon pulled up their shirt with the unselfconsciousness of a person who had forgotten modesty along with everything else, and there it was, the pale seam of old damage. They stared at it for a long moment.

"Someone bled for me," they said.

"Someone bled for *you*," Ryn agreed. "Took me three days to get you down the mountain and a month of stolen medicine to keep you alive, and when you woke up the first thing you said was 'the ledgers, check the ledgers,' because you thought I'd gone through your records. I hadn't. Mostly."

A sound came out of Gideon then—short, startled, unmistakable. A laugh. Ryn felt it land in her chest like a struck bell.

"There it is," she said softly. "That's the laugh. Isolde says you laugh maybe twice a year and it sounds like a door opening. Don't lose it. I'm keeping count now. That's one."

* * *

They fell into it, after that. Mornings on the roads, evenings at the table, and Ryn learned the strange arithmetic of restoration: a retold memory returned a fragment, but never the whole fragment, and never predictably. The story of the ravine brought back the ache in Gideon's hand. The story of the ledgers brought back nothing at all, but the next morning Gideon woke and automatically reached for the shelf where the journals lived, and stood there holding a book they couldn't read, weeping without knowing why. The story of the Bellwinter riots brought back the smell of woodsmoke, and Gideon stood in the yard breathing it in off the cold air like a drowning man.

"Some of it's coming back wrong," Gideon admitted one evening, turning a cup of tea they didn't drink. "I remember a river. I remember falling. I don't remember you. I remember the feeling of being caught, but there's no face on it. Is that—is that how it works? Pieces without people?"

"For now." Ryn sat across from them. "Maerin says the names come last. Says the seal ate in order—memories first, then faces, then names, deepest last. So they come back deepest first. You're rebuilding from the foundation up. Right now you're all foundation. The walls come next. Then the roof. Then—" she shrugged, aiming for lightness and missing by a mile— "then the name over the door."

"And whose name goes over the door?"

"Mine, eventually. Ryn Sable. Smuggler, liar, reformed arsonist, the woman who owes you a life and is paying it forward whether you like it or not." She grinned, because grinning was armor and she'd worn it thirty years. "But before me comes Isolde, because she's known you longest since the sealing and she reads to you every night and she's too proud to admit she's terrified you'll never say her name. And before Isolde comes Maerin, who blames herself for teaching you the rite that cost you everything, and needs to hear you say *it wasn't your fault* more than she needs bread. And after me—" Ryn hesitated. This was the hard one. This was the one she'd argued about with Isolde for two nights. "After me comes Aldric."

Gideon went still. "The king."

"The king. Who visits every eighth day, plain-cloaked, with an escort he keeps at the treeline because he knows crowds frighten you now. Who stands in this doorway like a boy caught stealing. Who owes you his mind and his crown and his sister, and doesn't know how to look at you because every time he does he sees the price you paid for him." She leaned forward. "He's coming tomorrow. And I'm telling you now so you can decide how to be. Because he'll introduce himself, and he'll apologize, and he'll offer you anything, and the kindest thing you can do—the thing the old Gideon would have done—is tell him to stop groveling and sit down and have tea. Can you do that?"

Gideon looked into their cup for a long time. When they spoke, their voice was quiet and strange, like someone speaking from the bottom of a well.

"I remember tea," they said. "I don't remember him. But I remember—" the fish under the ice again, circling closer to the surface— "there's a feeling. When you say his name. Guilt. My guilt. As if I... left something unfinished with him. As if I promised him something and then—" their hand closed slowly around the cup— "*forgot*."

"You promised him you'd come back," Ryn said. "That's what Isolde says. Ninety seconds lucid in the throne room, and you promised him you'd come back, and then you paid the price and couldn't remember promising. So he's been coming every eighth day to a promise you don't know you made." She stood, collected the cups, paused at the doorway with her back to them, because some things were easier said to a wall. "Tomorrow, when he walks in—say yes to the tea. It's a start. Names come last, but doors open first."

Behind her, quiet as snowfall, Gideon said: "Ryn."

She froze. Turned.

Gideon was looking at her—really looking, the grey eyes narrowed, searching, the way they must have searched the whiteout twenty years ago for a girl standing on cracking ice.

"That's your name. Ryn. You've said it a dozen times and I only just heard it." Their brow furrowed, deeper than before, the fish breaking water. "Why does it taste like smoke? Why does my chest hurt when I—"

And then the bell rang.

Not the keeper's iron bell on its shelf—Maerin would have heard that, Isolde would have come running. This was the gate-bell, down the mountain, the alarm-line Gideon had strung in another life, shivering once through the floorboards like a struck tooth. One peal. Then silence, which was worse. The alarm-line rang twice for travelers. Three times for wardens.

Once meant something the old Gideon had never coded for, because it had never happened in thirty years of watching.

Once meant the gate itself.

Gideon was already on their feet, coat in hand, moving toward the door with the certainty of a body following its own ledger—and Ryn, scrambling after them into the dark, understood that whatever waited down at the sealed gate had chosen the exact moment the first name found its way home.
