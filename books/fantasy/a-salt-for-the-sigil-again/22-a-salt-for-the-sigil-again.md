## 22. A Salt for the Sigil Again

The frost came early that year, silvering the bracken on the slopes above Thornfell, and Gideon woke before dawn out of habit rather than need.

That was the difference, they had learned. Habit was a rope with one end frayed; memory was a rope with both ends held. They still woke in the dark and still put the kettle on the iron stove and still took the salt down from its shelf above the door — but now, when they stood at the gate as the sky went grey to rose, they knew why. The knowing sat in them like a stone warmed by a fire: not hot, never quite hot again, but warm enough to hold.

They remembered almost everything.

Almost was a country Gideon had learned to live in. There were gaps, soft-edged, like places where snow had fallen and been swept. Their mother's face was there but her voice arrived a half-beat late, as if crossing a long room. The name of the village where they'd broken their arm at nine years old came back only when Ryn said it aloud over cards, laughing at how Gideon cheated, and then it settled into place like a bone set by a good bonesetter — slightly crooked, fully healed. The seal had eaten whole meals once. What the family had fed back, spoonful by spoonful, name by name, was thinner fare. But it was theirs.

Gideon measured the salt into their palm — coarse grey salt, cut with ash from Maerin's brazier — and walked the last hundred paces to the gate.

It did not look like much. It never had. Two pillars of mountain granite, leaning toward each other like tired old friends, the space between them filled with nothing at all. No door, no light, no shimmer. Just air, and the sense — which you felt in your teeth, in the roots of your hair — that the air was *held*. That something on the far side of it was pressing, patiently, the way water presses on a dam, without malice and without end.

Thirty years Gideon had kept it alone. Six months now they had kept it not alone.

They scattered the salt along the threshold in the old pattern — three lines, then the sigil-strokes drawn with a forefinger through the fall of it — and spoke the words Maerin had rewritten. The original liturgy had been a lock built by frightened people, all iron and prohibition. Maerin's version was different. She had spent the winter of her convalescence reworking it, cross-legged by the watchpost fire, muttering that a lock made only of fear rusts from the inside. Her rite was a lock made of *welcome refused*: you greeted what waited, you named it honestly, and you told it no. Every morning. Kindly. Firmly.

"Good morning," Gideon said to the empty air between the pillars. "You are hungry. You will stay hungry."

The air said nothing. The air always said nothing now. But Gideon felt the pressure shift, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight against a fence, and knew the words had landed where words were the only food allowed.

They turned back down the path, and the smell of frying onions met them halfway.

* * *

Ryn Sable cooked the way she smuggled: fast, loud, technically in violation of several laws of nature. She had commandeered the watchpost kitchen within a week of arriving and never given it back, and the little stone room that had smelled of lamp oil and dust for three decades now smelled of grease and pepper and, inexplicably, oranges.

"You're up early," she said without turning. "Or late. With you it's hard to tell."

"Salt's laid. Sigils are quiet." Gideon sat on the bench and accepted a plate. "All three cold as river stones."

"Course they are." Ryn flipped something in the pan with a flourish she absolutely did not need. "I've been thinking about the north road."

"There is no north road anymore. You burned your ledger."

"I burned *a* ledger." She pointed the spatula at them. "A woman keeps copies. In her head. Which, I'll remind you, is the one thing about me nobody's ever managed to eat." She grinned, and the scar across her ribs pulled white under her rolled-up sleeves — the warden's blade, the cost made flesh, the night everything nearly ended. It had healed crooked too. Everything worth keeping did. "There are passes over Thornfell the crown doesn't know. Always were. If the prayer-economy comes apart the way Aldric wants it to—"

"*When*," Gideon said.

"When," Ryn conceded, "there'll be folk running. Folk who sold their memories for a fever-break and got nothing back. Somebody's going to have to move them quietly, off the books, past the wardens who haven't gotten the news yet that the news has changed."

"You're offering to be a road."

"I'm offering to *be* a lot of things. It's my best quality." She set down the pan and finally looked at them, and beneath the grin was the thing she almost never let show: the ledger she kept of debts, the one written in no ink. "You pulled me off a mountainside with a rope and a bad attitude when I owed you my life. Then I watched you walk into a seal and come out with your eyes swept clean, and I spent four months handing you back to yourself one story at a time like some kind of — I don't know. Storyteller at a deathbed run backwards." She shrugged, rough, embarrassed. "Debt's paid. This isn't debt anymore. This is just Tuesday."

Gideon understood. It was the same distinction they'd made themselves, in the journal Isolde now read aloud to them every seventh evening: *duty is what you owe; this is what you choose.* The seal ate duty. It could not touch choice.

"Tuesday," Gideon agreed, and stole an onion off the plate just to hear her squawk.

* * *

Isolde arrived at midmorning, riding up the switchback trail with her hood back and frost melting in her dark hair, and behind her — as always now — rode the king.

Aldric had stopped wearing the crown in public. He wore it perhaps twice a year, by law, and each time he came up the mountain afterward looking like a man who had handled something dead. Today he dismounted stiffly and stood a moment looking up at the gate pillars with an expression Gideon had learned to read very carefully: the look of a man checking whether his hands were still his own.

"They're quiet?" he asked.

"Cold as stones. Salted this morning. Maerin's rite, full form."

"And you—" He hesitated. He always hesitated here, and Gideon loved him a little for it. "You remember me? From before?"

Gideon considered. The seal had taken names first, then faces, then the shape of love itself, leaving only the fact of it, like a footprint in ash. What the family had returned came in fragments: Isolde reading letters by firelight. Ryn's voice doing an unflattering impression of him. Maerin chanting the restoration liturgy with her ruined hands. And threaded through all of it, a tall young man with tired eyes who visited as a friend because he could not bear to visit as anything else.

"I remember a boy who used to sneak extra bread to the gatekeeper's apprentice," Gideon said. "And I remember a man who knelt in the snow outside a sanctum and begged his sister to cut a crown off his head. Between those two, I think I have most of you."

Aldric laughed — short, startled, real. "Maerin says the fragments knit stronger if you laugh while they set. She may be inventing doctrine. She does that."

"She's a priestess. It's the family trade."

Isolde had already gone inside to bully Ryn about the state of the common room, and her voice and Ryn's rose together in comfortable discord. Aldric lingered at the gate a moment longer. When he spoke again, he didn't look at Gideon; he looked at the empty air between the pillars, and his hand drifted up toward his head, toward the absent weight.

"Ninety seconds," he said quietly. "That's all I got, that day. Ninety seconds of being myself inside my own skull, long enough to beg." His jaw worked. "Some nights I dream the glove goes back on. Some nights I wake up reaching for prayers I don't remember making."

"The sigils are cold."

"I know. I know they're cold." He exhaled. "But you feel it too. Don't lie to me, keeper. You feel it *waiting*."

Gideon did. They would not insult him by denying it. The god behind the gate was starved — starved the way only a thing that had tasted worship could be starved — and hunger that patient did not die. It composted. It waited for the thirteenth moon after the thirteenth moon after the thirteenth, for some future keeper grown careless or lonely, for some crack in the welcome-refused.

"Yes," Gideon said. "I feel it waiting. That's the work. Not killing it — you can't kill it, we tried, the word for trying is *thirty years alone* — but standing here while it waits, so it waits alone." They touched the pillar, granite cold under their palm. "It wanted worship. It gets salt and a polite refusal instead. Forever, if forever's needed."

"And if forever needs more than you've got?"

"Then I'm not alone," Gideon said simply. "That was its whole strategy, you understand. Loneliness. A keeper by herself is one life to spend. A keeper with a family is—" they gestured vaguely at the watchpost, from which a clatter and a shriek of laughter suggested Ryn had lost an argument involving a broom, "—is considerably harder to eat."

Aldric smiled, and for a moment the tiredness fell away and Gideon saw the boy with the bread, and then the moment passed and he was a king getting back on a horse, and both of those were true at once, and that was all right.

* * *

Evening brought Maerin, walking up from the valley with her staff and her satchel and the slow deliberate gait of a woman whose hands had been broken in a warden's cell and had healed into instruments of a different kind of precision. She inspected the salt line without comment, ran her thumb along the third sigil-stroke, and grunted approval.

"Your strokes are getting confident," she said. "Confidence is good. Fear is also good. Keep both."

"Any word from the valley?"

"The hollowed ones?" Maerin sat heavily on the watchpost step. Six months on, and she still described them without flinching, though her voice went quieter. "Four woke fully when the god starved. Twelve woke partly — they know their names, they remember their mothers, they don't remember the years they gave. The rest..." She turned her palms up, the scars silver in the dusk. "The rest are gardens after a flood. Something can grow there. Not what grew before."

She was silent a moment. Guilt still lived in her — the liturgy she'd served, the rites she'd taught, repurposed by the thing behind the gate into engines of harvest. Gideon had watched her carry it the way she carried everything: visibly, honestly, refusing to pretend it weighed less than it did.

"And you?" Gideon asked. "Today. How is today?"

Maerin considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions. "Today I taught six children in the valley to sing a song that has no power in it whatsoever. Pure nonsense syllables. They sang it badly." Her mouth curved. "It was the holiest thing I've done in twenty years. Power is not the point, Gideon. It never was. That was the lie we all believed — that the divine must *do* something, must answer, must prove. The point was always just—" she gestured at the darkening sky, the first stars, the smoke rising from Ryn's chimney, "—the singing itself."

Inside, Isolde had lit the lamps and spread paper on the table. Gideon could see her through the window, writing — the letters she wrote every week now, to wardens and magistrates and village councils across the kingdom, dismantling the prayer-economy clause by clause with a runaway princess's knowledge of exactly where the bodies were buried. She wrote the way she did everything: precisely, furiously, and with her whole heart barely leashed.

"Seventh evening tomorrow," Maerin said. "Journal night."

"I know."

"Do you dread it? Hearing your own hand read back to you?"

Gideon thought about it. The journal was strange scripture: thirty years of entries, and then the blank months, and then the entries in other hands — Isolde's careful script filling the gap, Ryn's scrawled marginalia, even one page of Aldric's royal handwriting recording, with visible discomfort, *the keeper laughed today at something I said and I found I needed to sit down afterward.* Reading it aloud was the ritual that fed the fragments back. Some nights the words landed and knit. Some nights they slid off, and Gideon heard their own history like a story about a stranger, and grieved, and slept anyway.

"No," they said. "I don't dread it. Dread was the old work. This is the new one."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to bed and the fire had burned down to a red heart, Gideon walked out to the gate one last time.

This was not required. The rite was a morning rite. But Gideon had discovered a private practice they told no one: coming to the gate at night, unarmed, unsalted, and simply standing there. Looking at the held air. Letting the pressure press.

It was the closest thing to honesty the arrangement allowed. By day, the salt and the sigils and the rewritten liturgy were armor. At night, Gideon let themselves feel the truth underneath: that the thing behind the gate was vast and patient and terribly beautiful in the way a glacier is beautiful, and that it knew Gideon's name, and that someday — not soon, but someday — it would try again. And that Gideon would say no again. And that the saying-no would be easier than it had been this year, because next year there would be five voices saying it, and the year after, perhaps more.

Power held lightly. That was the phrase Maerin had coined, sitting on this same step, and Gideon had stolen it shamelessly. The old keepers had gripped. They had bound themselves into stone and died of it, alone, their names worn off their own memorials. Gideon's predecessor — mentor, mother-of-sorts, the memory that had come back last and hardest and sweetest — had chosen solitude and called it sacrifice, and the seal had eaten the sacrifice and left the solitude. Gideon had nearly made the same bargain once, alone in the dark with a god offering back everything they'd lost. Ryn had interrupted without knowing why, and the refusal had been the truest act of Gideon's life.

Not because refusal was noble. Because refusal, done in company, was *sustainable.*

"You're awake," Gideon said softly to the held air. "I know you're awake. Good. Listen, then."

The pressure leaned in, faint as a change in weather.

"I kept your secret for thirty years and it nearly killed everyone I love. So here's mine, free of charge." Gideon rested both hands on the cold granite, one on each pillar, bridging the gate without entering it. "We're not afraid of you anymore. We're not ashamed. We're not alone. Come back in a hundred years, in a thousand, whenever you like — you'll find the same thing. Salt, and songs with no power in them, and a keeper who laughs at bad jokes, and people who came back." They smiled, and meant it, and the smile was itself a kind of weapon older than any sigil. "We choose the work. Every morning. Try to starve on that."

The air held. The stars turned. Somewhere down the slope, a fox screamed and went quiet, and the ordinary world resumed its ordinary business, which was, Gideon had come to believe, the entire point.

Behind them, the watchpost door opened, spilling lamplight onto the frost.

"You're talking to it again," Ryn called, not unkindly. "Isolde says if you're going to hold philosophical conversations with an eldritch god at midnight, the least you can do is bring blankets."

"Tell her the god and I have reached an understanding."

"What understanding?"

"That it's very cold," Gideon said, "and I'm coming in."

They walked back down the path together, away from the gate, leaving it behind them — sealed, salted, patient, and guarded, for the first time in its long hungry history, by people rather than a person. Inside, the fire was warm, and Isolde had left the journal open to tomorrow's page, and Maerin had set out the salt for the morning, and the sigils slept cold as river stones, and would sleep cold tomorrow, and the day after that.

Gideon banked the fire, and chose the work again, and went to bed.
