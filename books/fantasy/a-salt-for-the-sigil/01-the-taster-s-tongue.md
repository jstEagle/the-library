## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The wine was poisoned with arsenic, and it was, Hazel thought, a rather clumsy attempt.

They held the goblet to the light of the great hall's chandeliers and watched the liquid catch it—deep garnet, almost black at the center, the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Arsenic in wine had a signature if you knew how to read it. A faint sweetness that sat wrong on the tongue, like honey poured over iron. A whisper of garlic that wasn't garlic. And beneath it all, so subtle that only a tongue trained since childhood could find it, a dryness at the very back of the throat, the taste of dust in an empty room.

Hazel swirled, sniffed, and sipped.

Around them, the banquet roared on. Two hundred candles guttered in their sconces. The Duke of Marrowvale's household guard stood along the walls like furniture that had learned to breathe. At the high table, Lord Aldous Vane—Master of Coin, keeper of the royal purse, a man whose smile had more hinges than his ledgers—leaned toward his neighbor and laughed at something that was not funny.

Hazel let the sip sit on their tongue. Counted. One. Two. Three.

The sweetness bloomed first, then the false garlic, then the dust. Yes. Arsenic, and enough of it to kill a grown man inside an hour, delivered in a vintage that had probably cost more than the taster's annual wage. Someone in the duke's kitchens—or someone who had visited them—had been careless with both the poison and the choice of vessel. Because everyone knew Lord Vane drank only red, and everyone knew the king's taster would drink first, and therefore everyone should have known better than to try.

Hazel swallowed.

The poison slid down into a body that had long ago stopped being merely a body. It was a ledger, Hazel's flesh. Every poison they had ever survived was written there in ink no apothecary could see: the hemlock at nine years old, administered by a tutor who wanted to test the limits of the crown's investment; the aconite at twelve, slipped into a pottage by a cook bribed by a rival house; the mercury vapors of the gilding room at fifteen; the nightshade, the strychnine, the slow drip of lead in the palace water pipes that had taken two years to build a tolerance against. Each one had nearly killed them. Each one had failed. And each failure had left its entry, its small annotation in the blood, until Hazel Eriksen had become the most expensive piece of parchment in the kingdom—a page written on so many times that nothing new could mark it.

The arsenic found no purchase. It never did. It settled somewhere in Hazel's chest like a guest arriving at a full inn, looked around, and left without complaint.

Hazel set down the goblet, turned it a quarter-turn clockwise—the signal—and caught the eye of the under-steward, a nervous man named Pell who had served three masters and buried none of them yet.

"The Vane red is off," Hazel said, pitched for the table but loud enough for the nearest guards. "Turned. Vinegar at the bottom of the cask, I'd guess. I'd send for the Rhenish instead."

A lie, smoothly told. The wine hadn't turned. The wine had been *made* to turn, in the sense that all things made to kill are, in the end, simply ruined. But you did not say *poison* at a banquet. You said *off*. You gave the host a face-saving exit and the assassin a face-losing one, and you let the machinery of the court grind forward without anyone having to name what had almost happened.

Lord Vane's smile acquired another hinge. "Off! Well. The Rhenish, then. And someone look into that cellar."

Someone would look into the cellar. Someone always did, and found nothing, because whoever had done the poisoning was either clever or gone or both, and the court preferred unsolved mysteries to solved embarrassments. Hazel would file the flavor in memory—*arsenic, heavy dose, kitchen access, likely between the second and third decanting*—and later tonight, when the hall emptied, they would write it down where it actually belonged.

Not in the palace records. In the other book.

* * *

Between the fish course and the meat, Hazel made their rounds.

This was the part of the job no one envied and no one thanked them for: the walking. A taster did not simply sit and drink. A taster moved through the banquet like a slow tide, pausing at each dish as it came up from the kitchens, taking the ceremonial spoonful, holding it, tasting, nodding. Roasted capon with preserved cherries—clean, though the cherries were old and their stones bitter with amygdalin, harmless at this quantity, noted. Buttered leeks—clean, over-salted, someone in the kitchens was angry about something. The venison pie—clean, magnificent, and Hazel took a second spoonful purely out of respect for the cook, whoever they were, wherever they'd learned to layer suet like that.

At each dish, servants watched them with that particular expression Hazel had spent twenty years learning to ignore. It was not gratitude. Gratitude would have required seeing Hazel as a person, and the whole architecture of the office depended on not doing that. No—it was something closer to relief mixed with faint disgust, the way one looks at a lightning rod or a latrine. *There but for the gods go I, thank the gods, poor wretch.* Hazel was the court's designated dying, performed nightly so that no one else had to. They were valued precisely to the degree that they could be killed safely. It was, when you thought about it—which Hazel tried not to—a kind of holiness. The temple sacrifice also ate well.

"Master Eriksen." Lady Corvane, at the third table, beckoned with two fingers. She was seventy, sharp as a fishhook, and had survived four husbands and one regency. "Tell me honestly. Is there anything wrong with the sweetmeats?"

Hazel tasted the sweetmeats. Candied violets, sugared almonds, a honey paste flavored with rosewater. Clean, all of it, except—

"There's foxglove in the honey," Hazel said quietly. "A trace. Medicinal dose, not a killing one. Enough to steady a heart, or slow one, depending."

Lady Corvane's eyes glittered. "Is there," she said, and ate three sweetmeats with visible relish. "How interesting. My physician prescribed me something for my palpitations this morning. He must have sweetened his own supply."

She said it loudly enough for half the table to hear, and somewhere across the hall a young physician went the color of the wine Hazel hadn't drunk. Hazel filed that too—noting the dose, the delivery method, the target, the probable motive (an inheritance accelerated, or a lover removed, or simple malpractice; the court produced all three in equal measure)—and moved on.

By the time the final course arrived—a towering spun-sugar confection shaped like the mountain that loomed north of the city, the one everyone pretended not to think about—Hazel had catalogued eleven dishes, two near-poisonings, one actual poisoning, and a slow-building headache from the candle smoke. They had eaten perhaps a thousand calories of other people's food and enjoyed perhaps a hundred calories' worth of it. Their own supper waited in their rooms: bread, hard cheese, an apple. Food nobody wanted them dead over. It was the only meal Hazel ever truly tasted.

The sugar mountain gleamed. Someone had crafted it lovingly, ridge by ridge, and someone else would smash it with a small silver hammer after the toasts, and everyone would laugh, and no one would say aloud what everyone knew: that the real mountain, the one under which the old courts had once held their revels before the old gods went silent, was not the sort of thing you could smash with a hammer. That it kept accounts of its own.

Hazel looked away from it and began, slowly, working their way toward the pantry corridor.

* * *

The guild's ledger lived in a place no one would ever look, because no one would ever want to.

The pantry corridor behind the great hall ended in a door marked *Rendering & Tallow*, which smelled exactly as advertised and was therefore avoided by every living soul in the palace except the renderers themselves, who worked dawn shifts and slept like the dead by evening. Inside, among the hanging cloths of fat and the great cooling vats, there was a shelf of account books—tallow yields, wax weights, sums owed to chandlers. Boring beyond endurance. Perfect.

The fourth book from the left was bound in grey calf-hide, unmarked, its spine cracked with honest use. To any inspector it was one more tedious inventory. But its pages had been hollowed—not cut, *grown*, by a binder in the guild's employ who claimed the paper remembered being a tree and could be persuaded to remember being a box—and inside the hollow lay the true ledger of the Gild of Impossible Things: forty leaves of thin rice-paper, sewn with silk thread, written in an ink made of oak gall and river water and one drop of something the guild had never disclosed even to its own members.

Hazel checked the corridor. Checked again. Then unlocked the rendering room with the key that hung, in plain sight, on the steward's board—because the best hiding place in any palace is a chore nobody wants—and slipped inside.

The smell hit them like a wall, as it always did, and as it always did, Hazel found it strangely comforting. Everything in here was honest. Fat was fat. Tallow was tallow. Nothing in this room pretended to be anything other than what it was, which was more than Hazel could say for the two hundred people feasting fifty feet away.

They lit no lamp. Twenty years of tasting had given them eyes that worked well in the dark, and besides, light through the keyhole was the kind of mistake you only got to make once. They drew out the true ledger, uncapped the little inkhorn they carried sewn into their sleeve—disguised as a pomander, because a taster carrying ink would raise questions, while a taster carrying a scent-ball raised none—and turned to tonight's page.

The guild kept its books the way merchants kept theirs, because Magpie insisted that miracles without accounting were just chaos with better manners. Every favor granted, every debt incurred, every impossible thing bought, sold, borrowed, or traded—all of it went in the book, in Hazel's neat, narrow hand.

Tonight's entries:

*To the Widow Anselm, Candlemaker's Row: one season's luck, drawn from the common pool, repayment deferred per standing terms. Her boy sits the scholarship exam in spring. Note: she wept in the shop. Note: I told her luck is a loan, not a gift. Note: she said she knows, and that knowing is why she trusts us.*

*From Sergeant Bray, discharged: returned courage, one measure, as contracted. He asked me to tell whoever holds his old fear that he doesn't want it back. Entered as a donation to the pool. Someone will need it.*

*Observed, uncommissioned: arsenic attempt upon Lord Aldous Vane, this date, the Vane red. Dose sufficient for one death. Source unknown, kitchen access certain. Logged for pattern-matching against the winter's other attempts. This is the third poisoning this month aimed at the Master of Coin. Someone is doing arithmetic on him.*

Hazel's pen paused over that last line.

Three attempts in a month was not ambition. Ambition was one attempt, well-made. Three attempts meant either incompetence—which the court had in plenty, but rarely concentrated in one project—or a campaign, patient and funded, grinding forward one ruined cask at a time. And a campaign against the Master of Coin meant money moving somewhere it shouldn't, which meant debts, which meant—

Which meant the guild might end up involved, because the guild was always involved in debts. Debts were the guild's native element. Favors, memories, luck, courage, years of life, last words, first loves—the guild traded in all of it, but underneath every trade ran the same current: something owed, something owing, the great double-entry book of human wanting.

Hazel wrote the date, blotted the page with a corner of tallow-cloth, and sat back on an overturned vat in the dark, listening to the distant roar of the banquet and feeling the familiar shape of the hour settle over them.

This was the part no one knew about. Not the king, not the court, not the guards who nodded to Hazel in the corridors with that mixture of pity and superstitious respect. As far as the world knew, Hazel Eriksen was the royal taster: a function, a vessel, a body kept alive so it could die on schedule if necessary. Valued. Fed. Housed in a good room with a good lock. Utterly alone.

But the guild knew. The guild saw the hand that kept its books. And twice a week, in this room that smelled of rendered fat, Hazel sat with the only work they had ever chosen rather than been assigned, writing down the secret commerce of the city—the widow's borrowed luck, the soldier's returned courage, the thousand small impossibilities that kept the poor afloat in the gaps between the rich—and felt, for an hour, like a person.

Then the hour ended, and the book went back in the tree-paper dark, and Hazel became the vessel again.

They were just rewrapping the ledger's oilcloth when the pomander-inkhorn shifted in their sleeve, and beneath it they felt the folded slip of paper that had been there since morning. Magpie's note, passed through the usual channel—a baker's boy, a hollow loaf, a system older than the current dynasty. Hazel had read it at dawn and read it again now, though they knew it by heart:

*Books tonight. Late. Something in the winter entries doesn't balance, and you know what Magpie says about unbalanced books. — M.*

*P.S. Eat something that isn't other people's dinner. You're getting thin. This is a professional observation, not a maternal one.*

Hazel smiled in the dark, briefly, the way one does when no one can see, and tucked the note away.

Something didn't balance. In six years of keeping the guild's accounts, Hazel had never once heard Magpie say those words. Magpie—who had fished Hazel out of a gutter at fourteen, taught them letters and poisons and the difference between a debt and a sin—Magpie treated the ledger's balance as a theological matter. If the books balanced, the world, whatever else it did, was at least *honest*.

Unbalanced books meant a trade that hadn't been recorded. Or a price that hadn't been paid. Or—

Or a debt that had come due somewhere far away, in a place the guild's ink didn't reach.

Hazel locked the rendering room, hung the key back on the steward's board, and walked back through the service corridors toward the noise and light, past the kitchens where the Rhenish was being decanted and the arsenic cask was already being quietly buried in the yard. Above, the banquet sang its toasts. North of the city, past the rooftops, the mountain stood against the stars, patient as a creditor.

Hazel did not look at it. They never did.

But that night, balancing the guild's books by candlelight, they would find the discrepancy Magpie had sensed—a single entry, decades old, in a hand too much like their own younger handwriting for comfort. A sale. A soul. A name Hazel knew better than their own.

And beside it, in the margin, in faded ink: *paid in full by another.*
