## 3. A Prayer Answered

The lamps of the Lamplighter's Reach went dark one by one behind Wren as they walked their route, and Wren counted them the way other people counted money.

Forty-one. Forty-two. The wick in the forty-third had gone soft and mushroomy with age; it took three strikes of the flint before it caught, and when it did, it burned sullen and orange, like a grudge. Wren made a note of it—*replace 43, ask Magpie if the Gild still had a line on good wicks*—and moved on.

That was the whole of the job, as most people understood it. Light them at dusk, snuff them at dawn, walk the same streets every evening until your knees gave out or the city ate you. Nobody thanked a lamplighter. Lamps were like breath: you only noticed them when they stopped.

Wren noticed everything about them. That was the secret vanity of the trade. Each lamp had a temperament. The ones on Tanner's Row burned hot and fast because the air there was greasy with rendered fat. The lamp outside the Chandlery chapel flickered whenever anyone lied beneath it—or so Wren liked to believe, though it flickered plenty when no one was saying anything at all. The great iron lamp at the mouth of the Reach, the one called Old Mother, had a crack in her glass that threw a long pale scar across the cobbles, and Wren had learned to walk that scar like a tightrope on bad nights, just to feel something.

It was a small life. Wren knew it was a small life. They also knew—with a certainty that lived somewhere below the ribs, below thought—that it was a *borrowed* life, a life with a corner folded down, the way you fold the corner of a page to find your place again. There was a blankness in them shaped exactly like something missing. They'd stopped trying to name it years ago. Naming it only made the edges ache.

So: lamps. Forty-four, forty-five. The evening fog coming up off the river in grey ropes. Somewhere a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing.

At forty-six—the crooked little lamp on Weir Street, leaning against the wall of the ferryman's tollhouse like a drunk against a friend—Wren ran out of oil.

They tipped the reservoir. Tilted it. Held it upside down over the flame and watched the last silver thread crawl along the glass and refuse, absolutely refuse, to reach the wick.

"No," Wren told it. "Don't you do this."

The lamp guttered. Died.

Wren stood in the sudden dark and swore with the fluency of someone who had learned profanity from dockworkers and refined it in the company of accountants. The tollhouse was locked; the chandler's depot was half a mile back the way they'd come; and Serane's patrols—no. Not Serane. Not yet. But there were patrols now, new men in new grey livery, walking the districts after dark with lanterns of their own and lists in their pockets, asking questions about who prayed to what. Unlicensed divinity, the criers called it. Wren didn't know what divinity cost to license, but they knew what it cost to be caught without papers for anything in this city, and it was never paid in coin.

An unlit lamp was a fine. A fine Wren could not pay. An unlit lamp on Weir Street, where the ferryman's tollhouse stood and the river came closest to the streets—where the fog was thickest and the patrol would want to look hardest—

Wren sat down on the tollhouse step, put the dead lamp between their knees, and laughed once, without any humor in it at all.

"Fine," they said. "Fine. You win."

There was an old custom among lamplighters, older than the Gild, older probably than the city: when a lamp wouldn't light, you asked its forgiveness. You apologized to the fire for disturbing it. Wren had always thought it was superstition dressed up as politeness, the way sailors had, but tonight, alone in the fog with a fine waiting for them at sunrise, they were willing to be polite to anything.

"I'm sorry," they told the lamp. "Truly. I should have checked the oil at forty. That's on me."

The lamp did not care.

And then—because the night was already ruined, because the fog had swallowed the last of the daylight and the patrol boots were somewhere out there in it, because Wren was tired in a way that sleep had never once touched—they heard themselves say the other thing. The thing lamplighters weren't supposed to say anymore. The thing that had been scrubbed off the guild rolls two hundred years ago when the old gods went quiet and the new courts decided silence meant death.

"Look," Wren said, to the fog, to the river they couldn't see, to whatever might be listening in the wet dark under Weir Street. "I know you're not there. I know nobody's there. But my gran used to say that before the kings bought the gods' silence, there was one who kept this stretch of the water. The one they called the Under-Weir. The one who lit his own hall with drowned lanterns." They turned the empty reservoir over in their hands. "I'm not asking for a miracle. I'm asking for oil. Or the loan of a flame. Or five minutes of somebody's attention. I'll pay it back. I always pay things back. It's—" and here Wren almost laughed again, because the word arrived from some folded-down page inside them, "—it's a matter of accounts."

The fog held still.

Not stilled—*held*, the way a hand holds a bird. The whole grey weight of it stopped moving at once, and the river sound changed, deepened, became less like water and more like a room full of people breathing carefully so as not to wake someone.

Wren's skin prickled from the nape down the spine, vertebra by vertebra, like fingers counting.

"Oh," Wren said. "You *are* there."

The lamp between their knees lit itself.

No flint, no spark. The wick simply bloomed, white-gold, steady as a held note—and then the lamp on the tollhouse roof caught, and the lamp at the weirs caught, and the lamp on the ferry barge moored out in the black water caught, and then the light went down Weir Street like a shout going down a stairwell, lamp after lamp after lamp, forty-six and forty-five and forty-four lighting backward along Wren's own route, every wick they had trimmed that evening flaring up together until the whole district burned bright as noon, brighter than noon, bright enough that Wren could see the fog from inside for the first time in their life—a cathedral of it, gold-walled, moving—and every single flame burned the same color. Not orange. White. River-colored, if rivers were made of light instead of water.

Wren did not move. Wren was fairly sure they had stopped breathing somewhere around *you are there*.

In the heart of the brightness, just at the edge of what eyes could hold, something stood in the street. It had the shape of a tall man the way a flood has the shape of a riverbed. Water moved through it—Wren could hear it, the small industrious sound of a stream over stones—and where its face should have been there was a slow turning, like light on the underside of ice.

*Drowned lanterns,* Wren thought, absurdly. *He really does.*

"You asked," the god said. Its voice was the voice water makes in a pipe in the walls of a house at night, when everyone else is asleep and only you are awake to hear it. "Nobody asks. Two hundred years, and the priests sold my name for candlewax, and the courts wrote me out of the rolls, and still—I keep the weirs. Habit. Or spite. And then a lamplighter sits down on a cold step and asks me for *oil*." Something that was almost laughter moved through the fog, a ripple. "Not glory. Not vengeance. Oil. Do you know how long it has been since anyone asked me for something small?"

"I didn't mean—" Wren's voice came out cracked. They tried again. "I didn't think anyone was listening. That's the only reason I said it. If I'd thought—"

"Yes," the god said. "That is why I answered."

The light held. Wren knelt there in it, on the cold step, with the warm lamp glowing between their hands, and felt the strangest sensation of their entire strange life: *seen*. Not watched—patrols watched, landlords watched, the whole city was built out of watching—but seen, the way you see a person rather than a function. The blank place below their ribs, the folded-down corner, the shape of the missing thing—it hadn't filled in. But something had looked directly at it, kindly, and noted it down.

"Who am I?" Wren whispered. "To you. Why me?"

"You keep lights," the god said simply. "I keep the water that reflects them. We were always in the same business." The brightness leaned closer, and the temperature dropped, and Wren understood suddenly and completely that they were kneeling in front of something old enough to remember when this whole valley was river and nothing else. "But hear me, lamplighter, because I can only be generous once and I am choosing to spend it on you. What I have done tonight cannot be hidden. Every lamp in the Reach is burning my color, and my color is *remembered* by things older than your courts. By morning, the ones who hunt answered prayers will have smelled it. They are very good. They are better than the priests ever were."

"The patrols," Wren said. Their mouth had gone dry. "The grey coats."

"Worse than grey coats. There is a woman coming. She carries a ledger too." The god's voice roughened, water over broken glass. "She counts prayers like debts. And she will find the place where mine was answered, and she will find the one who asked, and she will call it a crime, because to her it is one. So: hide. Be ordinary. Be *boring*. Burn no more prayers, speak to no more gods, and if a woman with kind eyes and a ledger asks you what you saw tonight—"

"What do I tell her?"

"Nothing," said the Under-Weir. "Tell her nothing, and live, and keep your lights. That is the whole of my wisdom. It took two hundred years of silence to learn it, and I give it to you free, which should tell you what it cost me."

The light began to go out.

Not all at once—inward, lamp by lamp, each flame folding down into itself like a hand closing, until only the little lamp on Weir Street still burned, ordinary orange, ordinary small, and the fog came back in grey and dumb and moving, and the river sounded like river again, and the god was gone the way a dream of falling is gone: completely, leaving only the landing.

Wren knelt on the step for a long time.

Then they got up, because the body is a stupid loyal animal and it knows its route, and they walked the rest of the round on legs that belonged to someone else. Forty-seven. Forty-eight. Each lamp they touched now felt like touching a sleeping animal that had spoken to them once. At fifty they realized they were crying, quietly, without drama, the way you bleed from a cut you haven't found yet, and they wiped their face on their sleeve and kept working, because that was the job, that had always been the job: keep the lights on, feel nothing, be furniture.

*Be boring.*

By the time they reached the last lamp—Old Mother, at the mouth of the Reach, with her long pale scar of cracked glass—the fog had thickened into soup, and through it Wren could hear boots. Several pairs. Unhurried. The particular unhurried rhythm of men who are being paid whether they find anything or not.

Grey coats. Lanterns. Lists.

Wren did the only thing there was to do. They climbed Old Mother's ladder, opened her little glass door, and pretended, with their whole heart, to trim a wick that needed no trimming, while below them the patrol stopped at the corner and one of them said, low:

"Weir Street. All of it lit at once. Half past dusk. You believe that?"

"Believe nothing," said another voice, drier, and younger, and wrong somehow, like a knife wrapped in velvet. "Write it down. Everything gets written down. That's how we catch them—not with fire, with *arithmetic*."

Wren's hands went still on the wick-trimmer.

Because Wren knew arithmetic. Knew it better than the back of their own hands, knew it in the blank place below their ribs where something was missing—knew ledgers, and entries, and debts, and the particular terror of a column that doesn't balance. Hazel had taught them, laughing, over cheap wine: *everything is a transaction, love, the trick is knowing whose book you're written in.*

Whose book was Wren written in? Whose book had just been written in *about* them?

Below, the boots moved on toward the tollhouse. Toward the step where Wren had knelt. Toward the lamp that had lit itself.

Wren trimmed the wick that needed nothing, closed Old Mother's little door, and began, very calmly, to plan how to get to Hazel before the arithmetic did.

* * *

Hazel would be at the banquet tonight—tasting, sipping poison for the crown with that terrible graceful calm, cataloguing flavors like scripture. The palace kitchens let Wren in through the fish door if they carried a crate, and crates were easy to come by, and Wren had walked that route a hundred times without once being seen as anything but furniture.

Furniture. Boring. Invisible.

Tonight, for the first time, invisibility felt less like safety and more like a debt coming due.

Wren shouldered the crate and set off through the fog, and behind them, far down Weir Street, the little crooked lamp on the tollhouse wall burned on and on and on, long past its oil, refusing—with what Wren could only describe as devotion—to go out.
