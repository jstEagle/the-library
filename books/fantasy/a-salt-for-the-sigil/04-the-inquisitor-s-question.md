## 4. The Inquisitor's Question

The herald's voice cracked on the name, which was the first interesting thing about her.

"—Serane Vail, Inquisitor of the Crown, sworn to the Office of Unlicensed Divinity—"

Hazel stood at their station along the east wall of the receiving hall, hands folded, face arranged into the pleasant blankness that had kept them alive through three reigns and one regency. Around them, the court did what courts do when power arrives wearing a new face: it leaned. Silks rustled. Fans opened like peacocks deciding whether to be afraid.

Serane Vail crossed the marble without hurrying. That was the second interesting thing. Everyone at court performed speed or its absence; she simply moved as though the room had been built around her route and she was being polite enough to use it. She was tall in the way of things designed rather than grown—a grey coat cut close as a blade's edge, silver thread at the cuffs worked into what Hazel's eye, trained by years of reading hallmarks and assay marks, recognized as script. Actual writing. Someone had embroidered a sentence down her sleeves, and she wore it where every hand she shook would feel it.

Her hair was white. Not old-white—new-white, the white of paper, of salt, of a page nothing has been written on yet. She was perhaps thirty-five. The color made her age unreadable, which Hazel suspected was the point.

King Aldous received her from the throne with the warm, hollow courtesy he gave to anything he couldn't eat, marry, or tax. "Inquisitor Vail. The crown is honored. Though I confess I hoped your office might find our kingdom quiet."

"Every kingdom hopes that, Your Majesty." Her voice was low and even, a voice for reading terms aloud. "That is why I visit them."

A ripple of laughter, quickly smothered. She let it die without smiling.

"I come regarding answered prayers," she said, and the word *prayers* landed in the hall like a dropped goblet. "The old gods have resumed correspondence. My office is charged with cataloguing such correspondents. Not punishing them," she added, in the tone of someone adding a clause no one will read before signing. "Cataloguing. The crown cannot regulate what it cannot count."

*And you can't tax what you can't count either,* Hazel thought, and then thought, more carefully: *and you can't burn what you can't find.*

They had known she was coming, of course. Magpie's birds had carried the news a week ago—*inquisitor inbound, grey coat, asks after miracles*—and Hazel had spent the intervening days doing what they always did with danger: tasting it in advance. They had reviewed the guild's exposure the way they reviewed a suspect dish. The lamplighter district had gone strange two months back—every wick in nine streets lit at once on a windless night, a story the papers called a gasworks accident because papers are kind that way. Wren had come home that night smelling of ozone and terror and had told Hazel everything by telling them nothing, which is a language lovers develop.

Hazel had entered it in the guild ledger under *pending*, which was the most they could do without lying to themselves.

Now the inquisitor was here, and the first thing she did upon finishing her formalities was look directly at Hazel.

Not past them. Not through them, the way courtiers looked at furniture that could die. *At* them. Her gaze had weight, like a thumb pressed to the inside of the wrist.

"You," she said. "You're the taster."

"Lord Hazel Eriksen," the chamberlain supplied, fluttering. "In service since—"

"Since the old king's reign. Yes. I read the rolls on the barge." She descended the dais steps and came toward Hazel across the floor, and the court parted for her the way water parts for something that does not displace so much as *insist*. "I'd like to borrow them, Your Majesty. For an hour. Professional curiosity."

"They're not a curiosity, Inquisitor, they're an institution—"

"All the better. Institutions leave records." She stopped an arm's length from Hazel and inclined her head, courteous as a headsman. "Do you consent?"

There was only one answer available in front of the throne. "I am the crown's to command, Inquisitor."

"Mm." Something flickered behind her eyes—disappointment? No. Recalculation. "We'll see how much of that is true."

* * *

She took them to the map room, dismissed the attendants herself, and then did the last thing Hazel expected: she sat down, poured two cups from a plain ewer of water, and pushed one across the table.

"Drink first," she said.

Hazel looked at the cup. Water, by smell—clean, drawn that morning, nothing riding on it but a faint mineral signature of the palace cisterns. But rules were rules, and Serane Vail watched them drink with the attention of a scholar watching a proof unfold.

"No hesitation," she noted. "No ritual. Most people in this building check their cups. You don't."

"The checking is my job. It seems redundant to do it twice."

"It isn't redundancy. It's faith." She turned her own cup slowly, not drinking. "You believe the world will hand you nothing you can't survive. That's not caution, Master Eriksen. That's theology."

"I assure you I'm a very bad believer. I've never managed a whole prayer."

"No?" She produced, from inside that grey coat, a slim case of black leather, and from the case, vials. Twelve of them, ranked in a velvet rack like organ pipes. Each was labeled in a small, precise hand. "Then let's conduct an experiment instead. Science requires no faith, only witnesses. You'll be my witness, and I'll be yours. Agreed?"

"What are we witnessing?"

"What hasn't killed you." She set the rack between them. "Your medical records list forty-one poisonings survived. Forty-one. The physicians call it constitution. The apothecaries call it luck. I've read the autopsies of everyone else who took those doses, and none of them were available for comment." She uncapped the first vial—Hazel smelled it before she offered it, nutty-sweet, bitter almond underneath. Cyanide in apricot kernel oil, old recipe, monastic origin. "So we proceed by elimination. A drop at a time. Tell me what you taste, and tell me true, because I'll know if you don't."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I write down that the royal food-taster refused a test administered by the crown's own inquisitor, in a sealed room, the week after miracles began appearing in his city." She smiled for the first time, and it was the worst thing about her—it reached her eyes and warmed them and meant nothing at all. "I wouldn't write that about anyone I liked. Do you like me, Master Eriksen?"

"Not yet."

"Honesty. Good. Begin."

Hazel took the drop on the tip of the tongue, as instructed, and let it sit. Bitterness bloomed, then the ghost of almonds, then the familiar cold bloom at the back of the throat that forty-one survivals had taught their body to greet like a rude cousin. They swallowed.

"Bitter almond. Apricot carrier oil, pressed hot—the oil's scorched, so whoever distilled it was working over an open flame, probably a monastery kitchen or somewhere equally poor. Monastic, I'd say; there's a chalkiness from stone mortars. The cyanide itself is maybe four years old. It's lost its top note."

Serane Vail wrote all of it down. Word for word, Hazel judged from the movement of her pen. When she finished, she looked up, and the friendliness was gone, replaced by something far more dangerous: interest.

"Four years old," she repeated. "You tasted the *age*."

"Everything ages. Poison just ages honestly."

"Second vial."

This one was metallic, green-glass sharp—arsenic sublimated with antimony, a forgery's blend, cheap work. Hazel said so. Third: aconite, gathered late in the season, the alkaloids concentrated, root cut by someone left-handed—the scrape-marks ran the wrong way. Fourth, fifth, sixth. Hazel catalogued each one like scripture, because that was what they were: a litany of everything the world had tried and failed to do to them, recited in order of bitterness. Somewhere around the eighth vial Hazel realized their hands were steady and their mouth was dry, and that the dryness wasn't fear of the poisons.

It was the way she was looking at them.

She had stopped taking notes. She was leaning forward with both elbows on the table, chin resting on laced fingers, watching Hazel's face the way believers watch statues—waiting for it to do something no object should do.

"Ninth," she said softly.

Foxglove. Digitalis, syruped to hide the bitterness, honeyed with something floral—lavender, a woman's hand, a domestic poisoning, the kind that happened in bedrooms rather than dungeons. Hazel held it on the tongue, named the lavender, named the season of the foxglove, named the fact that the syrup had been reheated twice, and Serane Vail closed her eyes briefly as though in prayer.

"Do you know what you are?" she asked.

"A taster."

"You're a *ledger*." She opened her eyes. "Every poison in this rack has written itself into your body and been refused entry. Forty-one entries, forty-one refusals, and now twelve more today, and you haven't so much as flushed. Do you understand what the Church used to call that? Before my office existed, when the old gods still picked up their mail?" She tapped the table once, precisely. "They called it grace. Proof of favor. A body the divine had marked as its own, that no corruption could touch. Kings used to pay pilgrims' wages for a glance at such a person."

"I'm not favored," Hazel said. "I'm employed."

"Are you?" She drew out the word until it frayed. Then, briskly, as though changing the subject would make it true: "Tell me about the lamps."

The question fell into the room like a coin down a well. Hazel kept their face pleasant and empty and felt, beneath the pleasantness, the specific cold of a hook going in.

"Lamps, Inquisitor?"

"Two months ago. Nine streets. Every flame lit within the same minute, on a night with no wind, while the gasworks—your city's official explanation—stood unattended and intact." She rose and walked to the map room's great table, where the capital lay rendered in painted wood, and set one finger on the lamplighter district. "I've interviewed forty-three witnesses. A baker saw a young man standing in the middle of Cordwainer Street with his arms raised. A widow says the light came *downward*, which is theologically interesting, since fire rises unless something tells it otherwise. And the lamplighters' guild roster shows one member unaccounted for during the event—" her finger slid a half-inch on the map, and Hazel's heart went with it, "—a journeyman named Wren, who was found an hour later, soaked in river water despite the drought, claiming to have fallen in."

"Wren falls in things," Hazel said. "Rivers, fountains, other people's business. It's practically a profession."

"Is it." She turned. The lamp above the map table caught the white of her hair and made it briefly incandescent, and for a moment she looked less like a woman than like a document held up to a flame. "You love him."

It was not a question, which was how Hazel knew it was a trap. Any answer fed it. They chose the truth, worn smooth: "He lights my street. I notice when he's late."

"Mm." She came back to the table, gathered her vials one by one, seating each in its velvet slot with a small click, a rosary being counted in reverse. At the twelfth she paused. "Last one. This isn't poison."

The vial she held up was empty—or seemed empty. Clear as water. But when she uncapped it, the air above it bent slightly, the way air bends over a candle, and a smell arrived that Hazel had no shelf for: rain on hot stone, myrrh, the iron tang of blood that has been spilled *on purpose*. Old-smelling. Older than any vintage in their experience.

"What is it?" Hazel asked, and hated how carefully they said it.

"That," said Serane Vail, "is the question of the age. It was collected from a churchyard in Vessany where the dead got up and walked home. One drop on the tongue. If you taste nothing—if your famous constitution refuses even *this*—then I will know that whatever protects you isn't chemistry." She held it out. "If you taste something, I'll know what you are. Either way, Master Eriksen, I get my answer. That's the thing about questions. They always collect."

Hazel looked at the drop trembling at the vial's lip. Their body—that patient, poisoned, well-read body—was silent. No alarm. No recognition. Just the ordinary hum of a vessel waiting to be filled, which was, they understood suddenly, exactly what frightened them: for the first time in twenty years, they could not pre-read what they were being served.

They thought of Wren, soaked in impossible river water, lying badly and lovingly about it. They thought of the guild ledger hidden beneath their floorboards, and Magpie's oldest rule, spoken like a prayer said backward: *the guild survives by being unrecorded.* They thought: *she is not hunting miracles. She is hunting the margin notes. The people the miracles happen around.*

They took the drop.

Cold. That was the first sensation—not bitter, not sweet, but cold, a clean arctic line from tongue to sternum, as though they had swallowed a winter morning. And beneath the cold, faint as a watermark, something that tasted the way an unread letter looks. Possibility. Address. A name they almost—

Hazel blinked. The map room returned. Serane Vail was watching them with an expression of profound, scholarly satisfaction, and Hazel realized their lips had moved.

"What did I say?" they asked.

"*Here,*" she said quietly. "You said *here*. As though answering a summons." She capped the vial with a click that sounded, in the silent room, like a lock turning. "Forty-one poisons refuse you, and holy water calls you present. Do you see my difficulty, Master Eriksen? I came here hunting the touched. And I find the crown's own taster, a man whose body reads like a saint's relic and who answers to divinity like a dog to its name."

"I'm not—"

"Don't finish that sentence." She was packing the black case now, unhurried, certain. "Denials are data too, and yours are already the most interesting thing I've collected this year. I'll want to speak with you again. Regularly. Consider yourself—what's the phrase your guilds use?" She smiled that warm, meaningless smile. "*Retained.*"

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth, I hope you're merely lucky. Luck is legal." The white head tilted, considering. "But I tasted that water myself, Master Eriksen, before I ever offered it to you. To me, it tastes of nothing at all. Isn't that curious? The same drop, two tongues, two entirely different answers." She stepped into the corridor, her voice trailing back like ink spreading through water. "Whatever is written on you, someone wrote it. And I intend to read the whole page."

The door closed. Hazel stood alone in the map room with the taste of unread letters fading on their tongue, and did the arithmetic they were built for, fast and cold:

An inquisitor who cross-referenced miracles like debts. A journeyman lamplighter who had answered a god and lied about it. And a taster whose immunity had just stopped looking like survival and started looking like a signature.

Somewhere below the city, beneath the floorboards, the guild's ledger waited in the dark—and for the first time in Hazel's life, they were afraid not of what was written in it, but of what Serane Vail would write next to their name.

They needed to see Magpie tonight.

They needed, far more urgently, to warn Wren.
