## 7. The Guild Objects

Magpie had been stealing things since before they could write, and the first thing they had ever stolen was a name.

Not their own. Their own name had gone into the ledger the night the guild took them in—a child of nine, fevered and orphaned and worth nothing to anyone except as a line item: *one name, surrendered, in exchange for bread, a bed, and instruction.* The guild did not take charity cases. It took deposits. Magpie's name went into the book under *Assets Held in Trust*, and in its place they were given a new one, a bird's name, a thief's name, a name that meant nothing anywhere except inside these walls.

Twenty-six years later, they still caught themselves reaching for the old one in dreams, like a tongue probing the socket of a pulled tooth.

So when Hazel Eriksen stood before the council table on the third night of the waning moon and asked the Gild of Impossible Things for permission to steal a soul back from the court beneath the mountain—openly, with witnesses, with fire if it came to fire—Magpie understood, better than anyone else in that room, exactly what was being asked.

They were being asked to give back the only thing the guild had ever truly protected.

* * *

The council chamber lived in the cellar beneath the Chandlery on Vesper Lane, where the smell of tallow masked the smell of ink and where no one who mattered ever looked twice at a building that sold candles by the pound. Nine chairs sat around a table made from a ship's keel, because the guild's founders had believed that all important decisions should be made over something that had already survived drowning. Seven of the chairs were filled tonight. Two stood empty: one for the Ferryman, who never came above ground, and one for the Founder, which had been empty for two hundred years and would remain so until someone committed an act of theft so perfect it retroactively justified the entire institution.

Nobody had managed it yet. The guild kept trying.

Magpie sat in the seventh chair, the handler's chair, closest to the door, because handlers were supposed to be the ones who left first when things went wrong. They had argued for that arrangement themselves, years ago, and had privately congratulated their own foresight ever since. Tonight the door felt very far away.

Hazel stood at the head of the table with the guild's working ledger open in front of them, and even now, even asking for the impossible, they held the book the way they held everything—carefully, like something that might be taken away. Magpie had trained that carefulness into them, or thought they had. It occurred to Magpie, watching Hazel's hands, that perhaps the carefulness had come first, and the guild had merely found a use for it.

"Read it again," said Corvus, from the second chair. Corvus ran the memory trade and had run it for forty years, and her face had the smooth, sanded look of a woman who had forgotten more things than most people ever learned, on purpose, for money. "The entry. Word for word."

Hazel read it again. Their voice did not shake. Magpie noted this with a kind of grim professional pride and a much less professional grief.

"*Entry four hundred eleven,*" Hazel recited. "*Received into escrow: one soul, whole and unencumbered, belonging to Wren Ashgrave, lamplighter, age nineteen. Consideration rendered: settlement of outstanding debt incurred under entry three hundred ninety-eight, debtor Hazel Eriksen, apprentice, age seventeen. Term: perpetual, or until redeemed at par plus accrued interest. Custodian: the Court Beneath.*"

"And the signature," said Corvus.

"The signature is mine." Hazel turned the ledger around so the council could see it. The ink had gone brown with age, the way old ink does, the way old promises do. "I signed it. I was seventeen years old and I signed a debt instrument I did not read, and the court beneath the mountain has been holding my beloved's soul as collateral for eight years, and I am asking this council for leave to go and take it back."

Silence, of the particular quality that cellars produce—dense, mineral, ancient.

It was Sable who spoke first, from the fifth chair. Sable handled the miracle trade, what little of it remained in the years before the gods woke up, and she had the voice of someone who had spent decades explaining to grieving people why miracles were priced the way they were. "You said *take*. Not buy."

"The mountain doesn't sell," Hazel said. "I've been through the precedents. Entry ninety-one, the cooper's daughter, 1809. Entry two hundred six, the midwife's husband. Every attempt at redemption was refused. The court doesn't want our coin. It wants the collateral. A soul in escrow is the only asset down there that appreciates."

"Then you're proposing burglary," said Corvus.

"I'm proposing *recovery*," Hazel said. "Of guild property. That soul entered escrow through a guild transaction, brokered by a guild handler—" here Hazel's eyes flicked to Magpie, and Magpie made themselves endure it, "—executed under guild seal. By the guild's own articles, any asset held improperly may be reclaimed by force of the original contract. I'm not asking you to break your rules. I'm asking you to enforce them."

"That," said Sable, "is a very lawyerly way to propose suicide."

"It's a very lawyerly institution."

Another silence. Magpie let it stretch, because letting silences stretch was part of a handler's work, and because every second of silence was another second in which no one had voted to send two fools down a root-stair into the dark.

But there was a third thing in the silence too, and Magpie knew the others could feel it, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The gods were awake. Everyone at this table had felt it—the prayers landing, the miracles sprouting up through the city's cracks like mushrooms after rain. And Inquisitor Serane Vail had arrived at court three weeks ago with her grey eyes and her grey ledgers, asking after unlicensed divinity, and she had already burned a priest in Lowmarsh, and she was not stopping.

An open act. A theft with witnesses. Fire, if it came to fire.

The guild had survived two hundred years by being nothing. No records in any archive but its own. No names in any registry. No footprint on any snow. Magpie had explained this rule to Hazel a hundred times, in a hundred lessons, usually over supper: *We are not hidden because we are afraid, little bird. We are hidden because we are load-bearing. The moment the world can see us, the world will start needing us, and the moment the world needs us, the world starts making lists, and the moment the world makes lists, someone starts crossing names off them.*

They had taught Hazel that rule the same year they'd helped Hazel sign away Wren Ashgrave's soul without reading the contract.

Magpie had countersigned that entry. Handler's mark, third column, small neat bird-foot. They had checked the arithmetic and found it sound. One debt settled, one soul received, one apprentice's future secured. They had not asked what the debt was for. Handlers did not ask; handlers balanced. It was the guild's oldest discipline: the books must balance, and feeling is not a currency.

Eight years of nights, Magpie had told themselves that.

* * *

"The vote," said Corvus finally, "is straightforward. This council exists to protect the guild. Not its clients, not its history, not its heart. The guild. An open theft from the court beneath the mountain—an act of force against the oldest creditor in the world—cannot be concealed. The dead talk. The ferryman talks. Gods, apparently, talk now. Within a season we would have the mountain's collectors in our cellars and the crown's inquisitor at our doors, and every name in every book we keep would become a target. I vote no."

"No," said Sable.

"No," said Heron, who handled favors and rarely spoke above a murmur.

"No," said Kestrel, youngest of them, and looked sorry about it, which somehow made it worse.

Six chairs had spoken. Six noes. Magpie watched Hazel absorb each one the way they absorbed poison—without flinching, cataloguing, filing it somewhere behind the eyes for later study—and thought: *they learned that from us too.*

Seven chairs. One abstention available. One vote that could, by the guild's own articles, be recorded either way or not at all.

Corvus turned. So did Sable. So did Hazel.

Magpie looked at their hands. They were an old thief's hands, scarred across the knuckles from locks that had bitten, stained permanently at the fingertips with the special iron-gall ink the guild used, the ink that could not be forged—which was funny, Magpie thought, given what Hazel was presumably planning to do the moment this room voted no. Magpie had raised that fool. Had fed them, taught them letters, taught them flavors, taught them how to slip a note into a ledger between courses while smiling at a duke. Had watched them fall in love with a lamplighter who laughed too easily and owned nothing, and had said nothing, because love was not a currency and the books must balance.

And the books had balanced. That was the obscene thing. Entry three hundred ninety-eight had balanced perfectly. Some debt of Hazel's apprenticeship—some mistake, some price, some stupid generous childish thing Hazel had done at seventeen that Magpie had never bothered to learn the details of—had been paid in full, with interest, and the ledger showed a clean line, and Magpie had initialed it, and everyone had walked away whole except the person who wasn't in the guild at all.

*We survive by being unrecorded,* Magpie had told Hazel once. *Visibility means death.*

But that wasn't quite true, was it. That was the rule they gave the children. The deeper truth, the one Magpie had learned at nine years old in a fever bed, was this: the guild survives by recording *other people* and remaining itself unrecorded. It takes names, luck, courage, memories, souls—and gives back services. It is a ledger with one side open. And every so often, the ledger produces a Hazel Eriksen, someone who stands on the open side of the book, and the guild spends them like coin and calls it stewardship.

Magpie had been that coin once. They knew the weight of it.

"The handler's vote," said Corvus quietly. "Magpie."

The candle flames stood perfectly still. Even the cellar draft seemed to understand that this mattered.

Magpie thought about the institution. They were good at thinking about the institution; they had practiced for twenty-six years. The institution had taken in a fevered child and given it bread and a trade and a name that meant nothing anywhere, and that name had become the only name that fit, and the institution was the only place in the world where it fit, and if the institution burned, Magpie burned with it—not their body, necessarily, but the self that lived in these cellars, the self that was real. Loyalty was not a currency either. But it compounded.

And then Magpie thought about Hazel at fourteen, vomiting into a bucket after their first controlled dose, gripping Magpie's sleeve and saying, *will it always feel like this*, and Magpie saying, *no, little bird, one day it won't feel like anything at all*, and hating themselves for the mercy of the lie, because the truth was that the numbness was the point, the numbness was the product, and they had known it and said it anyway.

They thought about Wren Ashgrave lighting lamps. About a district that glowed like a hearth because one man loved his street enough to pray to a drowned god. About a soul sitting in a drawer beneath a mountain, filed like grain, appreciating.

About entry three hundred ninety-eight, and the small neat bird-foot beside it, and the question Magpie had never asked.

"I abstain," Magpie said.

Hazel closed their eyes. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

"Then the motion fails," said Corvus. "The council's answer is no. The guild will not sanction this theft, will not fund it, will not record it, and will deny all knowledge of it if asked under any circumstances whatsoever—including torture, including divine compulsion, including death." She gathered her papers with crisp finality. "Which means, Handler, that whatever those two do next, they do as private persons. Outside the guild. Unrecorded."

"Yes," said Magpie.

"You understand what that means." Corvus's voice softened by a degree, which from Corvus was a landslide. "If they're caught, we cannot claim them. If they die, we cannot mourn them in the book. If they succeed—" she paused, and her sanded face did something almost human, "—we cannot thank them."

"I understand," said Magpie, "exactly what it means."

* * *

The council filed out through the candle-smell and up the stairs, one by one, noes trailing behind them like smoke. Magpie stayed, as handlers stayed, to bank the lamps and lock the working ledger in its iron box.

Hazel stayed too. They stood by the keel-table with their coat already on, already half-gone, already outside the guild in every way that counted, and Magpie found they could not look directly at them, the way you cannot look directly at the thing you have broken.

"Hazel."

"Don't." Hazel's voice was gentle, which was worse than anger. "You voted the only way you could. I know what this place is to you."

"Do you." Magpie locked the iron box. The lock was ceremonial; the real security was that no one alive could find the box. "I stole my first thing at six. An apple. I stole my name at nine, or sold it, depending how you read the entry. I have spent my whole life learning the difference between taking and keeping, and I will tell you what I know for free, little bird, since the guild charges everyone else." They turned. "The mountain will not fight you with locks. It will fight you with paperwork. It will offer you forms. It will offer you trades that look fair and are not. And somewhere down there, in some file, in some drawer, there is a page with your name on it that you have never seen, and when you find it—and you will find it, because you are your mother's daughter and your father's son and the guild's own stubborn handiwork—that page is going to tell you something you do not want to know about what your life has been *for*."

Hazel went very still. "What do you mean?"

"I mean I countersigned your apprenticeship," Magpie said, "and I never asked what the debt was, and I have regretted it for eight years in a language I don't have words for. Ask me nothing else tonight. I'm out of votes and nearly out of nerve." They pressed the iron key into Hazel's palm and folded the fool's fingers over it. "But the Chandlery's back gate sticks. Lift, then push. And the ferryman on the Root Stair drinks at the Drowned Bell and owes me a favor from before your mother was born—he'll take you down for the price of a true confession, and he'll pretend it's cheap."

Hazel stared at the key. Then at Magpie. "You're helping us."

"I'm *abstaining*," Magpie said. "There's a difference. The difference is deniability, and deniability is the only inheritance I have to give you." They picked up the lamp. "Go home. Eat something. Sleep beside him. Tomorrow, steal my life's work if you have to—but do it like a thief, not like a martyr. Martyrs make such *messes* of the books."

Hazel hesitated in the doorway, backlit by the stair-lamp, and for one moment they looked seventeen again, the day they'd signed, pen in hand, trusting everyone in the room.

"Thank you," Hazel said.

"Don't thank me," said Magpie. "Write it down. Somewhere I'll never find it. That's the only thanks this institution can't confiscate."

The door closed. The cellar settled. Magpie banked the last lamp and sat alone at the keel-table in the dark, in the chair closest to the door, doing what handlers did.

Balancing.

On the table before them lay the council's tally-slip, six noes and one abstention, official and final and clean. Magpie looked at it for a long time. Then they took out their pen, and the iron-gall ink that could not be forged, and beneath the abstention they drew a single small bird-foot—the handler's mark, third column, the same mark that had countersigned a soul away eight years ago.

It meant nothing. It committed the guild to nothing. Any auditor would read it as a stray flourish, a habit of the hand.

But Magpie had spent twenty-six years learning what marks meant, and they knew, pressing the nib down, that this one meant: *I was here. I chose this. Whatever comes of it, put it on my account.*

Outside, far above the cellar, in a lamplighter's narrow bed, two fools were presumably awake and planning, and somewhere beneath the mountain a drawer waited with a soul in it, and somewhere across the city a grey-eyed inquisitor was cross-referencing answered prayers by lamplight, closing in, methodical as winter.

Magpie blew out the lamp and sat in the warm dark, an old thief in an empty vault, waiting to find out what the cost of one small bird-foot would turn out to be.
