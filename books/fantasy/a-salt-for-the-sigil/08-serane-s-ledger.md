## 8. Serane's Ledger

The inquisitor's office in the Citadel of Vell had been a counting-house once, and Serane Vail approved of that. The floors were pale oak worn into shallow rivers by two centuries of clerks' feet. The windows were tall and narrow, built to admit light for reading figures, not views. Someone had hung tapestries in a vain attempt at comfort; she had them taken down the first morning and replaced with shelving. By the end of her second week, the room held eleven ledgers, each bound in gray calf, each labeled in her own hand in a script so regular it might have been set by a press.

The twelfth ledger lay open before her now, and it was the one that mattered.

She had titled it *Answered*, and in it she recorded every prayer in the city of Vell that had, within the past season, demonstrably been answered.

Not claimed. Not rumored. *Answered.* The distinction was the whole of her method, and she guarded it the way a moneylender guards his scales. The city was thick with claims—every scullery maid with a headache cured by prayer, every gambler who credited a saint for a lucky throw. Claims were noise. Answers were debts. A prayer that was truly answered left evidence, the way a paid bill left a receipt, and evidence could be cross-referenced.

She dipped her pen, blotted it against the well's rim, and wrote in her columnar hand:

*Fourth of the Waning Moon. Marla Ostrewe, laundress, Copperlane. Prayer of petition, offered at the shrine of Saint Ferren, ninth of the month. Petition: recovery of a wedding ring lost in the Osterwash. Answer: ring recovered intact from the belly of a pike, caught by her son the following morning. Verification: the pike was displayed at the Copperlane fish-stalls for two days; forty witnesses. Assessment: probability of coincidence, negligible. Probability of divine agency, high. Agency class: unknown. Cross-reference: pending.*

She blotted the entry and sat back.

Forty-one entries in the ledger now, each one verified to the standard she would have demanded of any auditor, because that was what she was. The court called her an inquisitor, and she wore the title as she wore the gray-and-silver of her office, but the title was imprecise. An inquisitor hunted heresy. Serane hunted *arithmetic*. Somewhere in this city, the old gods had resumed answering prayers after three hundred years of silence, and every answer was a transaction, and every transaction had two parties, and one of those parties was mortal, and mortals could be found.

She did not think of it as hunting. She thought of it as reconciliation. The accounts of heaven had fallen into arrears, and someone—some priest, some cult, some desperate soul who had knocked on the wrong door—had reopened them without license. Unlicensed divinity was not a sin against the church. It was a breach of contract. The Concord of Ash, signed in blood and tallow three centuries ago, had bound the old gods out of the world in exchange for the world's stability, and stability was not an abstraction to Serane. She had been a child in the border provinces when the last of the old shrines had been sealed, and she remembered what the years before had been like, from her grandmother's stories: villages given over to river-gods who demanded drownings, hearth-gods who burned the houses of the insufficiently grateful, harvest gods who took a tithe in teeth. The Concord had ended that. The Concord had made the world legible.

And now someone had scribbled in the margins of it.

She turned back three pages, to an entry she had been circling for days the way a tongue circles a cracked tooth.

*Twenty-first of the Waning Moon. District: Lamplighters' Row and environs, the Weft, the Saltstairs. Phenomenon: every lamp in the lamplighter district lit simultaneously at the hour of dusk, including forty-one lamps not yet trimmed, three lamps with broken wicks, and the memorial lamp at the Drowned Chapel which has held no oil in sixty years. Duration of anomaly: one hour, eleven minutes. Witness count: in excess of two hundred. Verification: corroborated by the district watch log, the guild tally-book, and the testimony of the under-warden. Assessment: not coincidence. Not trickery—the memorial lamp contained no fuel to trick with. Probability of divine agency: certain. Agency class: riverine. Cross-reference: pending.*

Riverine. That word had cost her four days of archival work and one bribe to a seminary librarian, and it was the most valuable word in the ledger. The god of the Vell had been called the Ferryman once, before the Concord, before the bridges and the customs houses had domesticated the river into something with a name on a map. The Ferryman had answered prayers. The Ferryman had *carried* things. And the Ferryman's old jurisdiction, according to a boundary-stone she had personally gone to look at, half-sunk in mud at the edge of the Weft, had included the lamplighter district—because in the old days, the lamps along the water had been lit to guide the dead home, and the god of the river had been the god of that guiding light.

Someone in the lamplighter district had prayed, and something riverine had answered, and the answer had been *generous*—every lamp, not one. Serane had read enough of the old accounts to know that generosity was the tell. Coincidence was stingy. Trickery was economical. Only divinity wasted, because divinity did not keep books.

*But I do,* she thought, and dipped her pen again.

The problem was the lamplighter. An answered prayer required a petitioner, and a phenomenon that size required a petitioner who had been *seen*—by the god, if not by men. The district held eleven hundred registered lamplighters, lamplighters' widows, lamplighters' apprentices, and lamplighters' dogs. She had already narrowed it. The anomaly had begun at the Drowned Chapel end of the Row and propagated outward, which meant the petitioner had been at the chapel, or near it, at dusk. The watch log placed nine souls in that stretch of street at the relevant hour: a fishwife, two drunk soldiers, a beggar who was always everywhere, a woman selling roasted chestnuts, and four lamplighters, one of whom had been found, according to the watchman's marginal note, "sitting on the chapel steps like he'd seen a ghost, and wouldn't say why."

The note did not name him. Notes that did not name were notes that had been paid not to name, or notes written by men too lazy to ask. Serane had interviewed the watchman. He had been lazy. He had also, when she mentioned the sum she was prepared to pay for a name, become suddenly and unhelpfully certain that he had never written any such note, and that someone must have forged his hand, and that really the whole evening was a blur to him, officers, a blur.

Which was, in its way, the most interesting thing she had learned all week.

She closed the ledger, squared it on the desk, and rang the small brass bell that summoned her clerk.

* * *

Father Anselm Oduya had been a priest of Saint Ferren for thirty-one years, and he was dying of it. The illness had begun in his lungs and was working its way, with the patience of a lit candle, toward the rest of him. When Serane was shown into his cell at the Ferrene house in Copperlane, he was propped up on pillows the color of old snow, copying out a psalm in a hand that trembled like a plumb line in wind.

"You are the inquisitor," he said, without looking up. "They told me you were coming. I told them to send you up anyway. I haven't the strength to be frightened of you, so we may as well be comfortable."

"I'm told comfort is your specialty," Serane said. She did not sit. There was one chair, and it was his, and she had learned long ago that standing in a sick man's room did something to the conversation that sitting could not. "You run the alms kitchen. You hear a great many prayers."

"I hear a great many *people*," Anselm said. "The prayers are between them and God. I try not to eavesdrop."

"Saint Ferren is a licensed saint. His intercessions are registered with the Concord office. When a prayer to him is answered, it is accounted." She let that sit a moment. "I am looking for prayers that were not addressed to him. Prayers that slipped past the license. Prayers that were answered anyway."

The old priest set down his pen. Up close, Serane could see that his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of a sky before snow, and that they were not, despite the tremor in his hands, confused eyes. "You are asking me to inform on the dying," he said.

"I am asking you to help me keep the peace."

"Are you." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; she watched the effort of it move through his face like a stone moving through water. "I have heard about you, Inquisitor Vail. They say you are fair. They say you are exact. They say you have never once punished an innocent person, and that this is somehow worse than if you had."

"They say correctly."

"And the guilty? What do you do with them, when your ledger is balanced?"

"That depends on the entry," Serane said. "Mostly, I correct them. A soul that has transacted with an unlicensed power is not damned, Father. It is *in arrears*. My office exists to bring such accounts back into order. Punishment is what happens when people refuse to be reconciled. I would very much prefer not to punish anyone. I have found that most people, given the choice between honesty and suffering, choose honesty, and I consider this the finest thing about our species."

Anselm laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on long enough that Serane's hand moved toward the water pitcher and stopped, because he raised one finger first, declining assistance with the small tyranny of a man who had been declining it for months.

"You will get your honesty," he said, when he could speak. "That is the joke of it. I have nothing to give you that you will not take anyway. Ask your questions."

"Three weeks ago, the lamps of the lamplighter district lit themselves. Before that, a pike gave up a wedding ring. Before that, a fever broke in the Weft in a single night across forty households, all of whom had prayed at the same hour. Someone is praying to the old gods, Father. Someone is being answered. The pattern is spreading. You run the largest alms kitchen in Copperlane. The poor talk to you. Who is it?"

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Serane had interrogated archivists and smugglers, a duchess and a child of nine, and she knew every species of silence there was: the silence of the guilty, the silence of the terrified, the silence of the stupid, the silence of the loyal. This was none of them. This was the silence of a man deciding how much of the truth a person could carry.

"You genuinely don't know what you're asking," he said at last. "That's the remarkable thing. You've built your little book, your beautiful columns, your probabilities. You think the old gods are a smuggling operation. You think someone opened a door and you can close it and everything goes back to the way it was in your ledgers." He shook his head, slow as a swaying bell. "Child, the Concord didn't bind the gods out of the world. It bound the world out of the gods' attention. Do you understand the difference? We built a wall not to keep them away but so they wouldn't *hear* us. And now the wall is leaking, and you are hunting the leak with a pen."

"Then help me plug it."

"I could give you a name," Anselm said. "Perhaps. If I thought a name would save anyone. But I have been a priest for thirty-one years, Inquisitor, and I will tell you what I have learned about names: they are how the powerful avoid looking at the poor. You take a name, and you feel you have done something. You put it in your book. You balance your column. And the thing itself—the hunger, the fear, the desperate kneeling in the dark—goes on as it always has, and finds another mouth to pray through." His pale eyes were steady on hers. "I give up nothing. Not because I am brave. Because it would do no good, and I am too old and too tired to do things that do no good."

Serane regarded him for a while.

"Father," she said, "you are protecting someone who is, whether they know it or not, in contact with powers that ate villages before the Concord. I have read the old accounts. I have read what the Ferryman's congregations looked like at the bottom of the river. The person you are protecting is not safe. They are *chosen*, which is the opposite of safe. Every hour you withhold, they move deeper into debt to something that has not kept a promise in three hundred years. You are not protecting them from me. I am the mercy in this room."

"I know," Anselm said gently. "That's the terrible thing about you. I believe you."

He gave her nothing else. She asked her questions in eleven different ways and he answered all of them honestly and none of them usefully, and she catalogued his evasions as she went—not as failures of her method but as data, because a man who would not name a name but would not lie about it either was a man protecting someone he loved, and love had a shape, and the shape could be traced.

She left the Ferrene house at dusk.

* * *

The message reached her at the ninth hour of the night, and it reached her the way all her worst messages reached her, which was to say through a boy with cold feet and no curiosity, who handed her a folded paper and was gone before she had opened it.

The paper was from her watcher at the Ferrene house. Four lines, in the cipher she had taught him:

*The priest is worse. The house physician called the brothers to his bedside at the eighth hour. He asked for ink. He wrote something and burned it in the chapel lamp. He has asked that no one be admitted until morning.*

Serane stood in her counting-house office with the paper in her hand and felt the particular cold that she had learned, over eleven years, to trust more than any other instrument she owned. It was the cold of a column that will not balance.

A dying priest. A request for ink. A writing, and a burning, and a lamp.

She had interviewed Father Anselm Oduya that afternoon. She had told him, in words she had chosen with care, that the petitioner she sought was in danger, and that she was the mercy in the room. She had believed it when she said it. She believed it still. And a dying man who believed her, who had *nothing to hide*, had asked for ink in the last hours of his life and burned what he wrote rather than let it be found—because he had understood something in the hour since she left that he had not understood before, or had understood and only then resolved to act upon.

He had not been protecting a name from her.

He had been protecting a name *for* someone. A warning, sent by fire and lamplight, to whoever it was the old gods had chosen—and the medium of the message, she noted, writing it into the ledger with a hand that did not tremble because she had trained it not to, was a lamp. Of course it was. Everything came back to the lamps.

At midnight, word came from the Ferrene house: Father Anselm Oduya had died in his sleep, peacefully, the brothers said, with the smell of lamp-oil and burnt paper still in his cell.

Serane sat for a long time in the lamplit office, the eleventh ledger open before her, and then she took out a fresh page and wrote, in her columnar hand, the entry she had been avoiding for a week, the one that required no verification at all because it verified itself:

*Assessment, revised: the petitioner is warned, mobile, and connected. The priest died holding the door shut behind him, which means the door is now unheld. The pattern of answered prayers in the lamplighter district is not random generosity. It is a courtship. Something old is being wooed, or something old is doing the wooing, and the petitioner is either the suitor or the bride.*

She blotted the page. Then, after a moment, she added one more line, not because her method required it but because eleven years of honest accounting required it, and she was, whatever else they said of her, an honest accountant:

*Note: the priest chose the fire over the confession. Cost of this operation, to date: one good man. Enter it. Do not round it down.*

Then she closed the ledgers, called for her cloak, her clerk, and four of the Citadel's quiet men, and gave the order that she had been circling for days as a tongue circles a cracked tooth:

"Wake the under-warden of the lamplighter district. I want the name of every soul who was on the Drowned Chapel steps at dusk three weeks ago—and I want the lamplighter who sat there like he'd seen a ghost."

She pulled on her gloves. Outside, over the rooftops of Vell, the city's ten thousand lamps were burning in their orderly rows, every one of them licensed, every one of them accounted for.

"And someone," Serane Vail added, as she went out into the cold, "find me the guild that keeps the books nobody admits to keeping. If the gods have resumed their accounts, then someone in this city is doing their bookkeeping—and I want to know who taught them the columns."
