## 9. Down the Stair Under the Root

**HAZEL**

The ferryman's name was Sallow, and he charged us three things: a copper coin minted before the mountain had a name, a true confession spoken aloud, and the promise that we would not look back at the water.

Hazel paid the coin from a purse they had sewn into their coat lining years ago, against exactly this kind of night. The confession they were still working up to. The promise they gave freely, because looking back at water was a habit of the living that the dead found tedious.

"Root-stair tonight," Sallow said. He was a long man folded into a short boat, his skin the gray-green of things that grow in cellars, and his eyes were the color of coins at the bottom of a well—round, dull, and worth more than they looked. "Flood season down below. The prayers are high." He said it the way a miller says *the river's up*, with a professional shrug. "Mind your boots."

The boat slid under the city through a culvert that smelled of tallow and rain, and then the culvert opened its throat and swallowed them whole.

Hazel had catalogued ten thousand flavors in their life—every poison the crown could buy or brew, every antidote, every harmless wine dressed up to kill—and none of them had prepared them for the taste of the air beneath the river. It was sweet. Not honey-sweet; sweeter than that, sweeter than anything with a price. It was the sweetness of something wanted very badly for a very long time.

"What is that?" Wren whispered beside them. In the dark, Wren's voice was the only fixed point, the way lamplight was above.

"Prayers," Sallow said, poling them forward. "They seep. Everything anybody ever asked the old gods for goes somewhere, and mostly it goes down. It pools. Like groundwater." He tapped the hull twice. "This whole tunnel sits on an aquifer of asking. Don't drink it. People who drink it start answering their own prayers, and that never ends well."

The boat grounded on a shore of black sand, and the stair began.

It wasn't built so much as grown—a spiral of roots thick as temple pillars, braided into steps that descended into a dark so complete it seemed less like an absence of light than a presence of it. The roots wept. That was the first thing Hazel understood, standing at the top with Wren's hand cold in theirs: the stair was crying, and what it cried was sweet.

They went down.

* * *

**WREN**

Wren counted lamps. It was what they did instead of praying, had been since childhood—counting the lights they lit, one hundred and forty on a good night in the lamplighter district, each flame a small argument against the dark. Down here there were no lamps. There was only the glow that came off the pooled prayers themselves, a pale gold shimmer rising from channels cut into the root-walls, and Wren counted those instead, because counting kept the fear in a box with holes punched in it.

Forty-one channels. Forty-two. Each one ran with slow golden water, and each one, if you leaned close, was whispering.

Not words, exactly. More like the shape of words. *Let her live. Let him come home. Let me be enough.* A thousand voices pressed flat and thinned out until they were just current, just flow, running downhill toward whatever sea collected everything people had ever begged for.

"Do you hear yours?" Hazel asked quietly.

Wren hadn't meant to answer honestly. But the stair had a way of pulling truth up out of you like moisture through clay. "Every night," Wren said. "For years I prayed to the river god without knowing his name. *Keep the lamps lit. Keep me seen.* And when he finally answered—" They stopped on a step that groaned under their weight like an old man. "When he answered, it was the first time I felt real. Isn't that a terrible thing? That it took a god to make me feel like I existed?"

"No," Hazel said. "I think it might be the most honest thing anyone's said on this stair in a century."

Sallow, somewhere below them with his lantern turned low, made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been laughter.

* * *

**HAZEL**

The first confession was owed at the third landing. Sallow stopped, turned his coin-colored eyes on them, and held out one gray hand as though collecting a fare.

"You said a true confession," Hazel said. "You didn't say to whom."

"The stair," Sallow said. "It keeps accounts too. Everyone does, down here. Speak."

Hazel looked at Wren. Wren looked back, patient, lamplighter-steady, and that patience was worse than any interrogation Serane Vail could have devised.

"I've known about your soul since before you knew," Hazel said. The words came out of them like a tooth coming loose—all at once, with blood behind it. "Not all of it. But I knew something of yours was missing. I could taste it. You have a flavor, Wren, everyone does, and yours has had a gap in it for six years, like a word with a letter missing, and I told myself it was grief, or distance, or my imagination, because the alternative was opening the guild's books and finding out what I'd done." Their voice cracked on the last word and they let it. "I signed the debt. My apprenticeship. I didn't read the terms because reading them would have meant admitting I'd cost the guild something, and I was nineteen and desperate to be worth keeping. So I signed, and the guild settled, and what they settled with was you."

The stair drank it in. Hazel felt it go—the confession sliding down between the roots like rain into soil—and where it landed, somewhere far below, something glowed briefly brighter.

Wren did not look shocked. That was the terrible mercy of it. Wren looked like someone hearing a diagnosis they'd already suspected, delivered by a physician gentle enough not to say *I told you so.*

"I know," Wren said. "I've always known something was pawned. You don't spend six years feeling like a coat left at a friend's house without figuring out you're waiting to be claimed." They took Hazel's hand. "Come on. We're not done descending, and I owe mine at the next landing anyway."

* * *

**WREN**

The fifth landing was narrower, the roots pressing close, and the prayer-channels here ran thicker, almost syrupy, gold gone amber with age. Old prayers. Wren touched one channel's edge and the whispering sharpened for a moment—*let the war end, let the war end, let the war end*—a hundred years of mothers in a single stream. Wren snatched their fingers back.

Sallow waited, hand out.

Wren breathed. Above ground, they lit lamps for a living; they knew how to make a small fire hold against a large dark. They tried to be that now.

"When I was fourteen," Wren said, "my mother sold half of me already. Not my soul—she didn't have the paperwork for that—but my name. To a moneylender, for a winter's coal. I answered to 'boy' for two years because saying my own name cost a fee." The words came easier than expected, which frightened them; it meant they'd been rehearsed in the dark for a long time. "So when the mountain bought the rest—the soul, the self, whatever the ledger calls it—I didn't fight. I couldn't even feel where it had been taken from. That's the joke, isn't it? They took the part of me that would have cared."

"It's not a joke," Hazel said fiercely. "It's a theft."

"It's both. Most thefts are." Wren managed a smile. "But here's the part I've never said aloud, not even to you. When I prayed to the river god, and he answered, and every lamp in the district lit at once—" They paused. The stair waited. Even the prayers seemed to hush. "For one moment I thought: *this is what the missing piece feels like.* Wholeness. Like a lamp with its wick trimmed right. And then I thought: *the inquisitor will see this,* and I put the light out myself, every lamp, one by one, and I hated myself for it. Because I chose invisibility over wonder. Again. The way I've chosen it my whole life."

"You chose survival," Hazel said. "There's no shame in—"

"There's a little shame," Wren said. "Just a little. Enough to keep me honest." They turned to Sallow. "Paid?"

The ferryman inclined his gray head. "Paid. Two more landings. Then the customs gate, and after that, no bribes will serve you. What waits below doesn't take coin." He smiled, and his teeth were the color of drowned things. "It takes signatures."

* * *

**HAZEL**

Between the fifth and sixth landings, the stair changed character. The prayers in the channels grew younger. Hazel could taste the difference—older prayers were deep and slow, like aged wine; these new ones were sharp, raw, barely fermented. *Let me pass the examination. Let her write back. Let the inquisitor look elsewhere.*

That last one stopped Hazel mid-step.

They crouched over a channel, watching the golden current carry its cargo of fear downhill, and saw—distinctly, unmistakably—the shimmer of their own name moving in the flow. Not once. Dozens of times. The whole city was praying to be overlooked, and a great many of those prayers were prayers to be overlooked *instead of Hazel Eriksen*, because Hazel had spent years quietly arranging it: the guild's small mercies, the misdirected suspicions, the clerk bribed to misfile a report. Every favor they'd ever pulled had become someone else's prayer, and every prayer had come down here, pooling, accruing interest.

"Hazel?" Wren's hand on their shoulder.

"My whole career is down here," Hazel said. "In solution. All the times I made myself unnoticeable—it didn't vanish. It drained. It's been draining for years, straight down to the mountain." They stood, wiping their hands on their coat as if the gold might stain. "Wren, I don't think we're sneaking anywhere. I think we're walking down our own account."

"Then let's hope we're solvent," Wren said, and squeezed their shoulder, and they kept descending.

* * *

**WREN**

At the seventh landing, Sallow called a rest, and the three of them sat on a root-step wide as a tavern bench while the amber light moved below them like slow weather. The ferryman produced a flask of something and drank; whatever it was, he offered none, which Wren respected.

"Can I ask you something?" Wren said. "You're dead. Or you're not-dead, or you're something. You work for the mountain court. Why take our bribe? Why help the living steal from the dead?"

Sallow considered this with the unhurried air of a man who had eternity to consider things and had learned to spread them out.

"Because the court beneath has forgotten what it's for," he said at last. "Souls used to arrive here wanting. Wanting to be judged, wanting to rest, wanting to be *finished*. Now they arrive filed. Grain in a silo. And the judges speak only contract, and the contract speaks only itself." He swirled the flask. "A soul that was sold—not given, *sold*, with a signature and a settlement—that soul has been sitting in a drawer for six years, and every year the drawer gets heavier. Things that are wrongly filed have weight. You'd know," he added, with a glance at Hazel that was almost kind. "You keep books."

"I keep them honestly," Hazel said.

"That's why I'm helping you." Sallow stood, joints cracking like green wood. "An honest ledger is the most dangerous thing in either court. Come. One more descent, and then the gate."

* * *

**HAZEL**

The final confession was owed at the eighth landing, and Hazel had saved the worst for it, deliberately, the way you save the strongest poison for the last course because you'll need the tolerance built up.

"Wren," they said, "when I found the entry—your entry—in the guild's old books, do you know what my first feeling was? Before the guilt. Before the horror." They made themselves say it. "Relief. Because it explained everything. Why you sometimes looked through me. Why loving you felt like holding water. It wasn't me failing—it was arithmetic. Someone had subtracted, and I could *fix* it. I could balance the account and get you back whole." Hazel's hands were shaking, and they watched them shake with a taster's detachment, noting symptoms. "And underneath the relief, underneath everything, there was this small vile voice saying: *finally, something I'm qualified for.* Not brave enough to love you plainly. But qualified to audit your soul back into your body. That's who's standing here. That's what you're getting."

The stair took the confession down. Somewhere in the deep, a channel flared.

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The prayer-light moved across their face, gold and gold and gold.

"You know what I heard in all that?" Wren said finally. "I heard someone who thinks love is supposed to be a competence." They stepped close, close enough that Hazel could count the faint scars on their knuckles from decades of lamp-tending burns. "It's not. It's the one thing nobody's qualified for. You signed papers you didn't read because you were young and scared. I put out my own miracle because I was young and scared. We are *spectacularly* unqualified for each other, Hazel Eriksen. Good. That means neither of us can claim credit."

Then Wren kissed them, there on the root-step, in the amber light of a hundred years of other people's begging, and it tasted—Hazel noted, helplessly, professionally—like the sweetness of the air, but honest. Like the same want, unwatered.

"Ahem," said Sallow.

* * *

**WREN**

Below the eighth landing there were no more landings. The stair ended at a door.

It was a door the way a cliff face is a wall: vast, seamless, grown from root-wood older than the city above, and set into it was a single brass plate, green with age, bearing words worn nearly smooth. Wren leaned close and read them aloud, because lamplighters read signs; it was the trade's whole soul.

*"ALL WHO ENTER DECLARE THEIR BUSINESS. ALL WHO ENTER ARE ENTERED."*

"Charming," Hazel murmured. "Bureaucracy with teeth."

Beyond the door—if door it was; there was no handle, no seam, only the plate—they could both feel it now, a pressure like standing before a waterfall of silence. The prayers stopped flowing downhill here. They flowed *in*. Every channel they had passed converged on this point, pouring centuries of human wanting through some invisible slot, and on the other side, something enormous and patient sorted it all.

Sallow set down his pole. This was the end of his water, apparently; the dead ferried the living only so far.

"Last advice," he said. "Down there, they'll ask you what you're carrying. Everyone lies. Say you're carrying nothing and they'll search you. Say you're carrying everything and they'll tax you." His coin-eyes flicked between them. "Say you're carrying a correction. Auditors who carry corrections get waved through to the filing floors. It's the one thing the court respects more than payment: a mistake admitted."

"And after the filing floors?" Hazel asked.

"After that," Sallow said, "you're past anything I know. Which, for a dead man, is saying something." He turned his boat, then paused. "Lamplighter. Taster. When you find the drawer with the soul in it—it'll be lighter than you expect. They always are. The heavy part isn't the soul. It's the waiting."

He poled away upstream, into the gold, and did not look back, and they remembered, just in time, not to watch him go.

Wren put their hand flat against the great door. The wood was warm, like a sleeping animal, like a banked lamp.

"Together?" Wren said.

"Together," said Hazel, and knocked.

The sound did not echo. Instead, from everywhere at once, dry as paper and vast as weather, a voice like a stamp coming down on vellum said:

***"State your business for entry. State it precisely. Precision will be checked."***

Hazel drew a breath that tasted of every prayer the city had ever wasted, and began to lie with the truth.
