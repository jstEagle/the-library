## 10. The Court Beneath

The ferryman let them off at a stair of black stone that went down farther than stairs should be permitted to go.

"Mind the queue," he said, poling his skiff back toward the dark water. "They open the doors at what you'd call dawn. Bring your own pen. They charge for ink."

Hazel had imagined many things about the court beneath the mountain—bone thrones, rivers of wailing, some vast and howling majesty. They had not imagined a waiting room.

But there it was: a cavern vaulted high enough that its ceiling vanished into darkness like the underside of a held breath, lit by lanterns of greenish flame that gave off no heat and, when Hazel listened closely, a faint sound like pages turning. Benches ran in rows. The benches were full. The dead sat in them with the particular patience of people who had been told their number would be called and believed it, eventually, on faith alone.

Some were fresh—wearing the clothes they drowned or burned or bled out in, still holding the shape of their last surprise. Some were old, worn smooth as river stones, dressed in fashions three centuries dead, sitting with the composure of those who had made peace with paperwork.

A wooden sign hung at the head of the queue. It read:

**ALL SOULS PROCESSED IN ORDER OF ARRIVAL. NO EXCEPTIONS. COMPLAINTS MAY BE SUBMITTED IN WRITING TO THE OFFICE OF COMPLAINTS, WHICH IS CLOSED.**

Wren read it twice. "I hate this place," they said quietly, "and I've been here ten seconds."

"You've been here before," Hazel said. It wasn't a question. Wren's soul had passed through this line years ago, filed away somewhere below like a deed to a house whose owner kept living in it.

"I don't remember it," Wren said. "That's the worst part. I'm standing in my own receipt."

Hazel took their hand. Wren's fingers were cold from the ferry, and Hazel held on as if warmth could be transferred by stubbornness alone. Around them the dead stirred, noticing the two warm bodies in the queue the way a room notices a candle.

An elderly woman in mourning dress leaned toward Hazel. Her face was kind and translucent, like paper held against a window. "Living," she observed. "You'll want the Auditor's Door. Don't stand here—the processors get twitchy around breath." She paused. "No offense meant. I was a customs officer myself, forty years, then sixty years dead. Old habits."

"None taken," Hazel said. "Which way?"

"Left past the weighing hall. Say you're from the Gild." She winked, which for a ghost was an unsettling act, one eyelid folding inward like a closing book. "Everyone says they're from the Gild. But you lot have the look. Ink under the fingernails."

Hazel did not look at their fingernails.

* * *

The Auditor's Door turned out to be exactly what its name promised: a door, tall and plain, with a brass plate reading **AUDITORS — BY APPOINTMENT OR AUDACITY**. A clerk sat behind a lectern beside it, a small gray person who might once have been human and now seemed mostly stationery, sheets of vellum layered into the skin of its forearms like feathers.

"Purpose," said the clerk.

"Audit," said Hazel.

The clerk looked up. Its eyes were two punched holes, and through them Hazel glimpsed shelves receding into darkness. "Of what?"

"The holdings," Hazel said, and reached for every word Magpie had ever taught them about sounding bored and expensive at the same time. "A routine reconciliation. There are discrepancies between the upper registers and the deep files. My superiors prefer these things settled quietly, before anyone upstairs notices the arithmetic."

It was a gamble built on a single observation: bureaucracies feared auditors the way wolves feared nothing, but mice feared cats, which was to say constantly, professionally, and with good reason.

The clerk's holes narrowed. "Name?"

"Hazel Eriksen," Hazel said, and heard Wren go very still beside them. Giving their true name in a court of the dead was either brilliant or catastrophic; Hazel had decided, somewhere over the last mile of root-tunnel, that if the gods already had their name written down—as Hazel suspected, in the marrow-deep way one suspects a debt—then hiding it bought nothing. Better to spend the coin openly and watch who reached for it.

The clerk consulted a ledger. Hazel watched its finger slide down a column and stop, and watched something happen in the gray face that was not quite fear and not quite respect but the exact alloy of both.

"Eriksen," the clerk repeated. "You're... expected."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Your appointment is noted." The clerk stamped a slip of vellum without being asked, slid it across the lectern, and opened the door. "Third corridor. Ask for Undersecretary Vetch. Do try not to touch anything. Everything down here is someone."

* * *

The corridors beyond were lined with filing cabinets.

This was the thing Hazel would carry out of the mountain, if they carried anything out: not horror, exactly, but scale. The cabinets rose from floor to unseen ceiling, row upon row, aisle after aisle, each drawer labeled in a script that rearranged itself politely when Hazel looked directly at it. Souls filed like grain. Somewhere in these miles of oak and iron was a drawer with Wren's name on it, and inside the drawer, folded small, everything Wren had ever been.

They passed a trolley stacked with drawers awaiting processing. One label read **MILLER, ALDER — COURAGE (RETURNED), CONDITION: USED**. Another: **ANONYMOUS WIDOW — LUCK (BORROWED), OVERDUE 11 YEARS, INTEREST ACCRUING**.

Wren stopped walking. "That's—" They swallowed. "Magpie told me once she'd seen a widow's luck come back through the books. I thought she was being poetic."

"The guild borrows from here," Hazel murmured. "Or lends. Or launders. I balance accounts I never asked where they came from." They had known this, in the abstract. Knowing it in the concrete—seeing eleven years of interest accruing on borrowed fortune, seeing courage marked *used*—was different. It was the difference between knowing you ate animals and watching the abattoir.

"Don't," Wren said softly.

"Don't what?"

"Do the face. The ledger face. Where you turn everything into numbers so you don't have to feel it." Wren touched Hazel's elbow. "I know the face. I love the face. Leave it up here."

Hazel left it. What remained underneath was worse, but Wren was right that it was truer.

* * *

Undersecretary Vetch occupied an office the size of a modest chapel and furnished entirely in paperwork. Stacks of contracts rose around a single desk like stalagmites. Vetch himself—he introduced himself with a bow that creaked, like a binding cracking—was tall, desiccated, and dressed in robes of black vellum covered in fine print. As he moved, clauses caught the lamplight.

"An audit," Vetch said, steepling fingers like dried reeds. "How delightful. We so rarely receive visitors who want nothing."

"I want several things," Hazel said. "I merely intend to appear as though I want nothing. That is the profession."

Vetch laughed—a sound like a drawer sticking. "Oh, I like you. Sit. Tea?" He gestured, and a teapot poured itself two cups of something steaming and amber. Hazel tasted it first, out of lifelong habit: wormwood, ash, a mineral tang of deep stone, and beneath it all, unmistakably, a trace of honey. Someone in this bureaucracy still remembered kindness.

"Delicious," Hazel lied smoothly, and drank. Poison was a country they held citizenship in; whatever this was, it wasn't poison. Just very old tea. Possibly older than the city above.

"Now." Vetch drew a contract toward himself and smoothed it flat. "State the scope of your inquiry, and I shall state the limits of my cooperation, and we shall discover together where those lines intersect. It is my favorite part."

"Souls held as collateral," Hazel said. "Specifically, souls sold to settle debts incurred by third parties. Living third parties."

Something shifted in the office. Not the light. The *print*. Every contract in the room rustled, a thousand documents turning slightly, like a field of grass aware of weather.

"Ah," said Vetch. "The old business." His voice lost its dry playfulness and gained something older and drier beneath it, like bone under bark. "You understand that such arrangements predate me. Predate the mountain, arguably. When the courts above were young and the gods still answered in person, debts between mortals were often secured against souls, because a soul is the only collateral that cannot be stolen, forged, or devalued. Only surrendered."

"And can it be released?"

"That," said Vetch, "depends on the terms."

He produced, from a stack that had definitely not contained it a moment ago, a document. He laid it facing Hazel with the ceremony of a dealer laying an ace.

It was a bill of sale. The handwriting on it was elegant, antique, and horribly familiar—not because Hazel had seen it before, but because it was *their own*. Younger. Shakier. An apprentice's hand, fourteen years old, signing away a soul not their own to settle a debt they hadn't known they'd signed.

Hazel had found this entry in the guild archives a week ago and it had detonated quietly, as planned. But seeing the original—seeing the actual ink, actual parchment, the actual moment of their childhood treachery preserved in a dead man's filing system—was another order of wound.

"There it is," Hazel said. Their voice came out level. Years of tasting arsenic with a smile will do that.

"There it is," Vetch agreed gently. "One soul, one lamplighter, surrendered freely by his—" he checked—"by his guarantor. Held in perpetuity or until terms are met. The terms being..." He turned the page. And turned it again. And frowned.

"Being?" Wren demanded. They had been silent, white-knuckled, letting Hazel work. Now they leaned forward. "What releases it?"

"That's the difficulty," Vetch said slowly. "The release clause is blank. It has always been blank. In four hundred years of service I have never seen a blank release clause. Contracts here are drafted by the Contract, you understand—the great engine of terms, older than the judges. It does not leave blanks." He looked up, and his reed-dry eyes were, for the first time, uncertain. "Someone wrote this contract with the clause removed. Deliberately. Which means the debt was never meant to be paid."

The greenish lanterns hummed. Somewhere far off, a bell rang the hour, though hours meant nothing here.

"Not paid," Hazel repeated. "Then what was it meant to be?"

"Collected," said Vetch. "Eventually. By whoever holds the note." He hesitated—a hesitation so un-bureaucratic that Hazel's skin prickled. "Auditor Eriksen. I am going to do something irregular, and I will deny it under any subsequent examination, including divine. Come with me."

* * *

He led them down, past the weighing hall—where a line of the newly dead stood on scales that weighed nothing visible, while clerks called out figures like *"Four drams of regret, two pounds of unfinished business, standard"*—and deeper, to a door that did not match its corridor.

Every door in the court beneath was oak and iron and brass-labeled. This one was plain pine, unpainted, the sort of door a gardener might keep tools behind. No plate. No lock. Just a small chalkboard hanging from a nail, on which someone had written, in a quick and living hand:

**OUT TO LUNCH. BACK NEVER. — MGMT**

"This is not a department," Vetch said. "This is a mistake. Or rather, it is the room where we keep the mistakes that are too large to file." He pushed the door open.

Inside was a single desk. On the desk lay a ledger.

Hazel had balanced the guild's books for nine years. They had handled ledgers that recorded favors, memories, miracles, luck, courage, grief, and one memorable transaction involving a storm and a promise. They thought they understood what a ledger was.

This was not that. This ledger was *awake*. Its cover breathed. Its pages turned themselves, slowly, restlessly, like a sleeper shifting under blankets, and the sound it made was the sound Hazel had heard in the lanterns upstairs—pages turning—but concentrated, close, alive. The air around it tasted of rain on hot stone. Of lightning about to decide.

"What is it?" Wren whispered.

"The master ledger," Vetch said. "The book the Contract drafts from. The record the judges read from. The original, from which all other entries are copies." He did not enter the room. Neither did his shadow. "And it is open, auditor, to a page I have never seen it open to before. It has been open to this page for eleven days. Since the lamps in your city lit themselves, if my correspondence is accurate. Since the old gods began answering prayers again."

Hazel stepped closer. Against every instinct trained into them by Magpie—never touch, never look too long, never let the work see you looking—they bent over the page.

The handwriting was the same antique elegance as the bill of sale. And there, in the margin of a column of names and sums, in fresh ink that shimmered like the surface of the ferry's dark water, a new entry was writing itself even as Hazel watched:

*A taster who cannot be poisoned. A lamp-lighter half-sold. Let them come down. Let them read.*

Below it, in smaller letters, almost shy:

*We apologize for the terms. We did not draft them well. We were very hungry.*

Hazel straightened. Their heart, that organ they had spent a lifetime treating as mere plumbing, was slamming.

"Undersecretary," they said. "I withdraw my audit."

Vetch blinked. "You only just arrived."

"I am revising the scope," Hazel said. "Extensively. I need access to the full holdings on one account. All of it. Every entry, every amendment, every payment collected against it since the day it was drafted."

"Whose account?"

Hazel looked at the page. At their own name, written there in a god's patient hand, listed not as debtor but—as far as Hazel could parse the ancient columns—as *asset*. Collateral, held since childhood. Every poison survived, a payment collected. Every year lived past the cup, interest earned for someone else.

"Mine," said Hazel. "The account is mine. And I want to see the whole bloody amortization schedule."

Behind them, in the corridor, the green lanterns guttered—all of them, at once—and from somewhere above, muffled through a mountain of stone and statute, came a sound like a very large stamp coming down on a very important document.

Vetch went gray even for a dead man. "Ah," he said. "I believe that will be the judges. They've noticed the ledger is awake."

He looked at Hazel with something that, in a living face, might have been hope.

"I would decide quickly, auditor. The judges speak only in contracts—and their current contract, I suspect, concerns *you*."
