## 11. An Entry in the Gods' Book

The archive of the court beneath the mountain did not smell like death. That was the first thing that unsettled Hazel about it.

It smelled like a countinghouse. Ink and cold stone and the faint animal sweetness of tallow candles burning down to their sockets, the way candles burn when no one is watching them. Somewhere below, water moved through channels cut before any kingdom above had learned to write, and the sound it made was not dripping but *ticking*—a vast, patient clockwork of seepage, keeping time for accounts that never closed.

Hazel moved between the shelves like a thief who had studied to be exactly this kind of thief. Their borrowed robes—clerk's grey, hemmed short for walking—brushed against ledgers taller than they were. Each spine was labeled in a script that hurt slightly to read, the way certain frequencies of light hurt: names that predated naming. Titles that were less titles than *weather*. They kept their eyes down and let the guild training do its work. Do not look at what you cannot afford to be seen looking at. Count everything else.

Wren was three shelves over, playing the part of an under-clerk with a soul-debt claim to review, which was even true, in the way that a knife is true. The mountain court had admitted them both without ceremony. Bureaucracies, Hazel had realized somewhere on the ninth stair, are the most gullible institutions in existence—not because they are stupid, but because they are confident. No one forged credentials here. One simply arrived, stated a purpose, and waited to be processed. The dead had all the patience in the world. It was the living who made mistakes, and so the living were waved through with stamps.

A judge-thing drifted past at the far end of the aisle—a shape like a robed column of ledger paper, pages turning where a face should be—and Hazel bent immediately over the nearest volume as if absorbed. The volume was titled *Debts of Breath, Collected*. Hazel did not open it.

*Find the master ledger,* Magpie had said, pressing the last of the guild's instructions into Hazel's hand back above ground, written in the ink that only showed itself to people who had signed something. *Every court has one book that all other books defer to. Find it, find Wren's entry, and we will know what we're stealing. You can't forge a signature you haven't read.*

And then Magpie had said something else, softer, which Hazel was still carrying around like a stone in the boot: *And Hazel—if your name is in it anywhere, don't read past it. Some doors are easier closed than opened.*

They had been finding their way for six hours now by a method the guild called *reading a room's debts*. Every institution leaks. Every archive has a spine, a load-bearing truth, and if you watch what the clerks flinch away from, you find it. Here, the clerks—the dead ones, gray and courteous and endlessly patient—flinched from nothing except one corridor on the lowest level, behind a door of black salt, which no one entered and no one mentioned and past which everyone's eyes slid as though the door were merely weather.

That was where the master ledger lived. Things deferred to are never advertised.

Hazel waited until the judge-thing turned its page-face away, touched Wren's elbow once—two taps, the guild signal for *hold my position*—and slipped down the stair into the salt-dark.

* * *

The black door had no lock.

Of course it didn't. Locks invite picking, and locks imply that someone outside is permitted to want in. What the door had instead was a question, inscribed across its surface in letters of frost that re-formed themselves as Hazel read:

**WHAT DO YOU BRING?**

Hazel considered lying. Then considered that lying to a door older than language was probably how one became a stain on the floor, and answered honestly, under their breath, the way one speaks in a temple.

"Nothing. I'm an auditor."

The frost rippled. For a long moment nothing happened, and Hazel understood that somewhere beneath them, something enormous was consulting a rule. Then, with a sound like a sigh drawn through grit, the door swung inward.

Auditors, apparently, were welcome everywhere. Hazel filed that away with the small collection of terrible facts the descent had given them—that the dead honored paperwork above courage, that grief here was measured in installments, that the ferryman of the root-tunnels had charged them not coin but two memories each, one sweet and one bitter, and had seemed embarrassed by how little it came to.

Inside was a room the size of a cathedral and lit like a study. A single desk. A single lamp. And upon the desk, open, waiting, a book.

Not large. That was the obscenity of it. All the ledgers upstairs—souls filed like grain, centuries of the dead sorted and stamped and stored—all of it deferred to a volume scarcely bigger than a psalter, bound in leather gone soft as skin, its pages thin as onion-skin and crowded with handwriting that changed style every few lines, because different hands had written it across millennia, or perhaps the same hand wearing different gloves.

The old gods' book. The original. The account against which every other account reconciled.

Hazel approached it the way one approaches a sleeping animal of unknown temperament, and began, hands steady by long professional discipline, to read.

* * *

The book was organized not by name but by *obligation*, which made sense once Hazel stopped expecting it to make sense. There were entries for harvests promised and not delivered. Entries for prayers accepted as loans. Entries where whole cities appeared as collateral, their populations itemized in columns so fine the ink looked like stitching.

Hazel was hunting for Wren. The sale had been registered, Magpie said, in the year of the Flood-Tax, under the seal of a broker whose guild-title translated roughly as *One Who Weighs What Cannot Be Carried*. If the master book recorded the transaction—and it must; the mountain court could not enforce a contract the gods' own book did not ratify—then somewhere in these pages was the line where Wren's soul became an asset.

They worked backward through the years, fingers hovering, never touching. The lamp flame leaned toward them as they read, attentive, like a cat pretending not to care.

And then, on a page dated thirty-one years ago, in ink still dark as fresh blood, Hazel found their own name.

*Eriksen, H. Collateral.*

They read it again. Their vision performed a strange trick, the way it did when they'd once drunk something that paralyzed the optic nerve for four minutes while leaving everything else intact: the words remained perfectly sharp and entirely impossible.

There was more. Of course there was more. Below the name, in the cramped marginal hand of some celestial actuary, ran a schedule. An amortization table. Hazel had spent their life among ledgers; they knew the format the way a musician knows notation, and their eye translated it before their mind consent to:

*Installments due annually, commencing age seven. Payment collected in the following instruments: arsenic (age 7), henbane (9), aconite (11), mercury vapor (12), ergot (14)...*

Down the column. Poison after poison after poison, each one dated, each one *checked off* in that same neat hand, each one marked **PAID**.

Hazel stood very still in the lamplight and let the arithmetic finish itself, because arithmetic is crueler when you do it yourself.

Seven years old. The year they had been apprenticed. The year they had signed, without reading, the document their mistress had slid across the table with the comment that all tasters must sign *something*, dear, it's only formality. The year of their first poisoning—the arsenic in the wedding wine, the convulsions, the three days of fever, the waking up and being told they had a gift.

They had thought it a gift. They had built a life on it. Immunity, they'd called it—hard-won, earned, *theirs*, the one possession that made them valuable enough to keep and unremarkable enough to survive the court's appetites. Every cup they had drained without dying had been proof of their cunning, their discipline, their body's stubborn alchemy.

It was none of those things.

It was a payment schedule.

Every poison they had ever survived was a line item. Every survival, a collection. Someone—something—had invested in a seven-year-old child's body the way a merchant invests in a ship, and had been drawing down returns for twenty-four years, and the returns were *this*: a taster who could drink anything. A vessel so saturated with paid-up death that no further poison could collect. The gods had been amortizing Hazel Eriksen since childhood, and Hazel had mistaken the bleeding for a blessing.

Their knees informed them, quietly, that the floor would be acceptable. Hazel sat down on the cold stone beside the desk and put their head briefly in their hands, and did not cry, because crying was a noise and noise was fatal, but something in their chest tore along a seam anyway, silently, the way wet paper tears.

Magpie's warning rang back: *if your name is in it anywhere, don't read past it.* Too late. They read past it. They read all of it.

Because there was a final line. Below the schedule. Below the last unchecked installment, dated—Hazel counted forward with numb lips—to next winter. A closing entry, drafted but unsigned, awaiting only completion:

*Upon final payment: asset matures. Collateral converts to ownership. Holder may take delivery of the vessel entire.*

*Vessel entire.* Not their labor. Not their tongue. Not their service.

Them. Body and whatever else was left over once the payments stopped. In less than two years, the schedule ended, and whatever god held this note would come to collect the thing itself—the whole amortized, poison-proofed, perfectly seasoned Hazel Eriksen, delivered like grain to the granary.

All those years the court had prized them. All those years they had been safe precisely because they were valuable. They had thought invisibility was their craft. It had been their cage's paint job.

The lamp flickered. Above, faintly, through fathoms of stone, Hazel heard—or imagined they heard—the great tick of the water-channels stumble, just once, like a heart skipping.

* * *

"Wren," they said, when they found them again among the soul-shelves. Just the name. Nothing else. Their voice came out level, which frightened them more than shaking would have.

Wren looked up from the claim-slip they were studying—Wren's own death-in-installments rendered in bureaucratic gray—and saw it instantly. Twenty years of loving a food-taster teaches you to read faces the way Hazel read wines. "What did you find?"

"I found mine." Hazel kept their voice below the hearing of the dead clerks. "I wasn't looking for it. It was—" A breath. "Wren, I'm collateral. I've been collateral since I was seven. The poisons—they weren't immunity. They were *payments*. Somebody's been collecting on me my whole life, and the account closes in two years, and when it closes they take delivery."

Wren went very pale. Pale, and then—Hazel watched it happen with something close to horror—furious. Not at Hazel. At the book, at the stairs, at the whole patient architecture of the place. "They sold me," Wren said slowly, "to cover a debt. Your debt. And the debt was—you. This whole time, the debt was *you*."

"Yes."

"So my soul is sitting in some drawer downstairs as the down payment on a god's purchase of the person I—" Wren stopped. Recalibrated. When they spoke again their voice had gone quiet and terribly clear. "Then we're not stealing one thing tonight. We're stealing two. And Hazel—we're not buying either of them back. Because whoever holds your paper isn't selling either. Are they."

"No," Hazel said. "No one down here sells. They only foreclose."

Somewhere in the dark above the shelves, a page turned. Neither of them had moved.

Hazel looked back the way they had come—down toward the salt door, the small obscene book, the neat column of checkmarks that was their life—and felt the old guild fear rise up in them, the warning Magpie had drilled into their bones: *the guild survives by being unrecorded; visibility means death.* To fight this, they would have to write. To write, they would have to be seen. By courts. By gods. By the whole reading world.

Two years until the final payment. One night to steal a soul.

And somewhere in Hazel's chest, beneath the tearing seam, a new resolve set like poured wax: *then I will learn to forge divinity.*
