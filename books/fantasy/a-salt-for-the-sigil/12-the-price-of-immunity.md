## 12. The Price of Immunity

The dark below the root-stairs was not empty. Wren had learned that much in the last day. It was *occupied*—the way a courtroom is occupied, the way a customs house is occupied—by things that watched with the patience of furniture.

They had been walking for hours. Or what felt like hours; time down here moved like syrup off a cold spoon. The tunnels were threaded with roots thick as oxen, and where the roots wept, prayers pooled in the low places—Wren could hear them if he let himself listen, a hundred small voices seeping through the stone like groundwater through a cellar wall. *Let her live. Let me win. Let him come home.* Some of the pools glimmered faintly, lit from within by wishes that had been waiting so long they'd begun to ferment.

Hazel walked ahead of him, one hand trailing the wall, and every few steps Wren counted their breathing the way he counted lamps on his rounds. In. Out. Still alive. Still his.

He was getting tired of counting.

The ferryman had left them at a landing where the river ran black and slow beneath a ceiling of woven roots, and had taken his coin without looking at either of them, which Wren understood to be the professional courtesy of the dead's employees. From there, a stair. From the stair, a corridor lined with doors that had no rooms behind them—Hazel had checked, opening three, finding only more stone—and then the corridor opened out into a chamber that stopped them both mid-step.

It was an office.

That was the only word for it. A vast cavern dressed as a clerk's workroom: shelves carved directly into living rock, stacked with ledgers whose spines were labeled in no alphabet Wren knew but which he could somehow *read*, the way you can read a face. Desks of black wood. Inkwells full of something darker than ink. And behind the largest desk, on a chair too grand for it, sat a small god.

He looked like a tax assessor who had once been handsome and had never quite gotten over it. Grey at the temples. Rings on every finger. His eyes were the color of coins left too long in water, and when he smiled—which he did immediately, warmly, the way a fisherman smiles at a rising bobber—it showed teeth filed flat.

"Ah," said the god. "The lamplighter. And the taster." He spread his hands over the desk as though presenting a feast. "I've been expecting you both since before either of you was born, but it's lovely when punctuality finally arrives."

"We're not here to trade," Hazel said.

"Everyone is here to trade," the god said pleasantly. "That's all this place is. Sit."

There were two chairs. They were ordinary chairs, wooden, worn smooth, and that frightened Wren more than anything else in the room—the small domesticity of them, as though people sat here often, as though this happened all the time.

Hazel didn't sit. Wren did, because his legs had opinions of their own, and because sitting made him look less like a man about to run and running, he suspected, was not survivable here.

"I know you," Wren said, before he could stop himself. "You answered my prayer."

The god's smile widened by one tooth. "Did I? How flattering. I do answer the occasional prayer. Keeps the hand in. The old ones—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the world, toward the sky where bigger gods presumably lived, "—they've been asleep so long they've forgotten the taste of being believed in. I never forgot. I'm modest in my appetites. A lamp district here. A widow's luck there. Small miracles, fairly priced." He laced his fingers. "And now you've come down my stairs, into my court's jurisdiction, looking for a soul that was lawfully purchased. So. Let us discuss price."

"It wasn't lawful," Hazel said quietly. "The signature was forged."

"The signature was *valid*," the god corrected, with the gentle relish of a man who has waited years to say this. "Validated, witnessed, entered, and sealed. Whether the signer read what they signed is, I'm afraid, a matter of moral interest rather than legal one. We are a bureaucracy, taster, not a nursery."

Wren watched Hazel's jaw tighten. He knew that jaw. He'd kissed it a thousand times, traced it in the dark of their narrow room above the chandler's shop, learned its weather the way sailors learn coastlines. Right now it was doing the thing it did when Hazel was holding something back—not lying, never lying, but rationing truth like bread in a siege.

"What's your name?" Wren asked the god. It seemed important. Down here, everything came down to names and entries.

"You may call me Vessel," the god said. "Everyone does. It's apt. I hold things." He opened a drawer and drew out a ledger—small, personal, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling—and laid it open between them. "Now. Before we descend into tedious litigation, let me show you something. Lamplighter. You struck me, when your prayer reached me, as a man who understands light. So I'll be direct with you, and let the taster do the arithmetic afterward, as tasters do."

He turned the ledger around.

Wren couldn't read the script. But beside one entry, in the margin, someone had drawn a small picture: a figure with a flame cupped in its hands, and next to the figure, a number. The number was very large.

"That's you," Vessel said. "Your soul, appraised. It's a fine soul. Bright, unbroken, *beloved*—do you know how much beloved adds to an appraisal? Sentiment is the rarest commodity in any market. Most souls down here belong to no one. Yours belongs to someone so thoroughly that collectors have inquired twice." He tapped the number. "Which brings me to why I invited you to sit."

"You didn't invite us," Hazel said. "We broke in."

"I built a door with a weak lock and waited thirty years," Vessel said mildly. "Everything is invitation, if you're patient enough. Now." He folded his hands. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, and I'll make it honestly, because honest dealing is the whole of my religion."

The chamber seemed to lean in. The roots overhead creaked like ship rigging.

"The mountain does not sell souls. That is true, and your taster knows it, and it has been a source of some frustration to them, I can see it in their face. But the mountain *trades*. And there is one commodity in the possession of Hazel Eriksen that this court values above any soul in inventory—including yours, lamplighter, forgive me, though yours is lovely."

Wren felt it before he understood it: a cold starting under his breastbone, spreading outward like frost across a window.

"Every poison survived," Vessel said, "is an immunity banked. Every death walked away from is a debt the body records. Your beloved has spent twenty years drinking the cups meant for kings. Do you know what that makes them? Not lucky. Not blessed. *Paid.* They are owed, by the chemistry of their own flesh, a sum of survival so vast that no mortal ledger holds it. Their blood is a treasury. Their body is a vault. And this court"—he spread his ringed hands—"would very much like to make a withdrawal."

"No," Hazel said.

"Hazel," Wren said.

"*No.*"

"They haven't heard the terms," Vessel said, with the serenity of a man watching a fire catch exactly where he'd laid the kindling. "The terms are these. Hazel Eriksen surrenders every immunity ever accumulated—all of it, poured out, complete, irrevocable. Every resistance, every hard-won tolerance, every survival, transferred to this court's account. In exchange, the soul of Wren Ash is released, restored, re-entered into its original body, debt discharged, file closed." He smiled again, gently. "Poured out like wine, taster. All those years of drinking poison so that others might eat safely. Wouldn't it be fitting, if it bought back the one person you love?"

The silence after was enormous. Somewhere far up the root-tunnels, a prayer pool overflowed with a sound like a sigh.

Wren looked at Hazel and saw a person doing arithmetic they'd been doing their whole life. He saw the flinch they were suppressing—the specific flinch of a food-taster offered a cup. Because that's what this was. It was a cup. The biggest cup ever poured, and everyone in the room was waiting to see if Hazel would drink.

And the horror of it, the thing that made Wren's hands go cold on his knees, was that it was a *good* offer. It was fair. It was almost beautiful. Twenty years of arsenic and nightshade and whatever else the court had slipped into Hazel's wine, all of it transmuted at last into something worth having: Wren, whole. Wren, returned. Wren walking out of the dark with edges he'd never known were missing.

All it cost was everything Hazel had bled to become.

"There's a catch," Wren said.

"There's always a catch," Vessel agreed cheerfully. "Would you like to say it, or shall I?"

"Say it."

"The immunities are not merely protection," the god said. "They are *payment collected*. Your beloved's name has stood as collateral in a greater ledger than mine since childhood—I'll not pretend otherwise; you'll find that entry yourself soon enough, and I envy neither of you the reading. Every poison survived was a payment made *on their behalf*, against a debt they never contracted knowingly. If Hazel pours out the accumulated balance—" he turned his coin-colored eyes on Hazel, and for just a moment the assessor's warmth fell away and something older and hungrier looked through, "—then the account stands empty. Unprotected. Collectible. The next cup that touches their lips, whatever it is, will find nothing standing between it and them. No tolerance. No grace. Nothing."

"So it kills them," Wren said. "Not today. But eventually. One bad oyster. One jealous cook. One drop of anything."

"One drop of anything," Vessel agreed. "Though I'd note, in fairness, that most people live their entire lives in precisely that condition. It's called being mortal. Billions manage it daily. Some even enjoy it." He slid a pen across the desk—a plain pen, a human pen, and that detail snagged in Wren's mind like a burr. "The contract requires only one signature. Either party may sign for the other, provided the intent is genuine. Love, after all, is the oldest form of proxy."

Wren stared at the pen.

He thought about the night he'd prayed to the river god—half-joking, tired, standing in the rain with his ladder and his oil can, saying *if anyone's listening, I'd like to matter to somebody*. And the lamps had lit, all of them, all at once, the whole district blooming like a field of paper flowers, and he had stood in the middle of it feeling seen for the first time in his life and terrified in the same breath, because being seen, he'd learned young, was the first step toward being taken.

He had been right to be afraid. Here he was. Taken.

But he hadn't been wrong to pray, either. That was the part nobody warned you about—that the wanting itself was the trap, and you'd spring it anyway, because the alternative was never wanting anything, and that was just a slower way of dying.

"Wren." Hazel's voice, low. They hadn't moved, but they had gone very still, the stillness of a person standing on ice and listening for the crack. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't even think it. I can see you thinking it. You have a thinking face and it's terrible and I can see it from here."

"You'd do it for me," Wren said. "In a heartbeat. You've been doing it for twenty years—for strangers, for a king you don't even like. You'd drink this cup and call it Tuesday."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because—" and there Hazel stopped, and Wren watched them run headlong into the wall of their own argument, and hated how much he loved them in that exact moment, and hated the god for building the argument in the first place.

"Because I'm already spent," Hazel said finally, quietly. "Because I'm a ledger with every line filled in, and you're—you're the part of my life that isn't accounting. You're the margin. I don't get to spend the margin."

Vessel made a small sound that might have been a sigh or might have been appetite.

"Touching," he said. "Genuinely. I collect touching things; it's a hobby. But the hour grows late, and courts below the earth keep older hours than you're used to. So let me simplify." He turned the pen to face Wren. "One signature. Either of you. The other walks free regardless—this I swear on my name, which is the only oath I keep. Lamplighter: sign, and Hazel leaves here emptied but breathing, and you leave here *whole*, and the two of you climb back into the daylight with perhaps ten good years together before some kitchen accident or vengeful cousin does what nature does to everyone. Taster: sign, and the same, mirrored." He shrugged, elegant, awful. "Ten years, bought with everything. Or walk away now, and keep what you have: half a soul apiece, and a love that lasts exactly as long as the inquisitor's patience. I'm told she's very patient."

The pen lay on the desk between them, ordinary as a kitchen knife.

Wren found that his hand had moved. Not far. Just resting on the desk's edge, near the pen, the way a man's hand rests near a railing over a long drop. He hadn't decided. He wanted to be clear with himself, later, in whatever life he got—he had *not decided*. But his hand was near the pen, and the pen was near the ink, and somewhere above them the whole city was going about its evening lighting lamps that had no idea what they cost.

He thought: *ten years.* Ten years of mornings. Ten years of Hazel complaining about his cooking and eating it anyway. Ten years of the narrow room above the chandler's, of rain on the skylight, of counting breaths instead of lamps because he'd finally run out of reasons to count anything else.

Ten years was more than his father got. Ten years was more than most souls in this room had ever been promised by anybody.

His fingers closed around the pen.

And Hazel's hand came down over his—not grabbing, not wrenching, just covering, warm and steady and shaking slightly, the way a hand shakes when it has held too many cups and never once been allowed to refuse one.

"No," Hazel said. Not loud. Just absolute, the way a wall is absolute. "You don't get to buy me back with myself. That's not love, Wren. That's just another ledger, and I am *done* with ledgers."

"Then what's your plan?" Wren's voice cracked on it. "Walk away? Both of us? Leave my soul down here in a filing cabinet and go home and be *grateful*?"

"No," Hazel said again. And then, softer, with something in it that Wren had never heard before—something that sounded almost like the moment before a lamp catches: "I'm going to write a better contract."

Vessel's smile flickered. Just at the corner. Just for an instant—the first honest expression he'd shown since they walked in.

"Now *that*," the little god said softly, "is the most dangerous thing anyone has said in this chamber in four hundred years."

Behind him, deep in the rock, something began—very faintly—to drip. Like ink. Like a quill touching paper somewhere far below, keeping count.

And up the root-stairs, thin as a thread of smoke, came a smell that did not belong underground: lamp oil, and rain, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the cold clean scent of a woman who had been following them for three days.

Serane Vail had found the door with the weak lock.

She was coming down.
