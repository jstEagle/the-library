## 13. Caught Between Courts

Serane Vail had always believed that every crime left a signature, and that most criminals were too vain to vary it.

The guild's ink was the vanity of an institution that had hidden for four hundred years. She had found it in a pawnshop ledger in the Saltmarket—a receipt for borrowed luck, written in a hand so ordinary it was almost insolent—and she had held the page to a candle and watched what the flame did to it. Ordinary ink browned. This ink *withdrew*, curling away from the heat like a living thing asked an unwelcome question, and in the space of a heartbeat the words on the page rearranged themselves into something innocuous: a price for turnips.

Ink that lied under interrogation. Ink that had been taught to.

She had taken three such pages since—two from a chandler's shop, one pried from the floorboards beneath a dead priest's altar—and laid them side by side in her rooms above the Chapterhouse, and she had done what she did best, which was nothing dramatic at all. She read. She compared. She waited for the pattern to confess.

The ink was not made. It was *grown*. Gall from oaks that grew only over old god-ground, mixed with rainwater caught in a churchyard, fixed with salt—salt, always salt, the oldest preservative, the oldest covenant—and something else, something that moved. The scribes of the Gild of Impossible Things had not merely wanted their records hidden. They had wanted them *alive*, capable of forgetting on command, and that meant the records could be made to remember.

Serane ground a little of the dried ink with a mortar stolen from the palace apothecary, wet it with holy water blessed by a priest who no longer believed and therefore had nothing for his god to take from him, and set a needle of silver in the mixture overnight.

By morning, the needle pointed down.

Not north. Not toward any guildhall or safehouse in the city's sprawl. Down, steady as a plumb line, toward the roots of the mountain at the city's back—the mountain that had not had a name in living memory because names were prayers and prayers had stopped being answered, until recently, until lamps lit themselves in the lamplighter districts and widows won impossible lawsuits and a soldier walked out of a battle he should have died in three times over.

The needle pointed down, and Serane Vail, who had spent eleven years hunting unlicensed divinity through the upper world, took her coat, her warrant, her father's knife, and went where the evidence went.

* * *

The way down began behind a ferryman's boathouse on the black river, where the water ran slow and cold and did not reflect the stars so much as swallow them. The ferryman was waiting for her, which told her everything about how porous the underworld's borders had become.

"You're not one of mine," he said. He was old in a way that suggested he had been old before the concept of age was invented, and his boat was long enough for passengers who did not breathe.

"I'm the Inquisitor." She showed him the seal. "I have a warrant."

He looked at the wax and the ribbon and the embossed sunburst of the Crown, and he laughed—not cruelly, but with the tired amusement of a customs official watching someone present a passport to the wrong country. "Paper," he said. "Down here we honor other instruments." He held out a gray, calloused palm. "But you've brought payment anyway. I can smell it. You've been carrying a debt your whole life, Inquisitor. Everyone who hunts gods owes them something. Come aboard and we'll discuss the fare."

She boarded. She was not a woman who flinched at metaphors, but she noted, with professional displeasure, that the fare turned out to be exactly that: a confession, spoken aloud into the dark water, of the thing she owed. She gave it in four sentences. The river took it. The boat moved.

She would not write those four sentences in any report. But she carried them the rest of the way down like stones in her shoes.

* * *

The root-tunnels were older than the city, older than the kingdom, older perhaps than the idea of kingdoms. Great pale roots of some tree that must have died before the first brick was laid threaded the earth overhead and underfoot, and where they crossed they formed arches, doorways, the ribs of a cathedral nobody had built. And everywhere, pooling in the low places like groundwater, seeping down the walls in slow glistening runnels: prayer.

She felt it before she understood it. A pressure behind the eyes. A taste at the back of the throat like coins and honey. Prayers that had been spoken upward for centuries and never answered had not vanished—they had sunk, the way all heavy things sink, and here at the bottom of the world they lay in standing pools, thick with longing, and the air itself was drunk with them.

Serane knelt once beside such a pool and looked at her own reflection and saw, faintly, superimposed over her face, the faces of everyone whose prayers had pooled there. A child asking for a mother's fever to break. A man asking for forgiveness he hadn't earned. A girl asking, over and over, in the small flat voice of the desperate, *please, please, please.*

She stood up. She did not pray. That was the discipline that had made her: at nine years old, kneeling at her mother's deathbed, she had prayed with everything in her and been answered with silence, and she had concluded—not bitterly, she insisted even now, not bitterly—that silence was also an answer, and that anyone who claimed otherwise was either deceived or selling something. The old gods returning changed nothing. It only meant the sellers were back in business.

Her lamp—ordinary oil, ordinary wick; she trusted nothing that lit itself—showed her marks on the roots ahead. Fresh ones. A knife had scored bark, and someone had tied strips of cloth, and the cloth was *wet*, though nothing up here was wet except the prayers themselves.

Two travelers. One light-footed, one limping slightly on the left. One carrying something wrapped in oilcloth that they checked at every turning, the way a miser checks a purse.

And one more scent, fainter, woven through: tallow smoke and lamp-oil. Lamplighter's hands.

Serane smiled in the dark, alone, where no one could see it and be frightened by it. The lamplighter district. The answered lamps. Wren, the beloved of the food-taster. And the food-taster herself—or himself; the court records were maddeningly inconsistent on Hazel Eriksen, as though the clerks could never quite hold the name in mind—whose bloodwork she had commissioned in secret and whose results she kept locked in a drawer, because the results said things about human physiology that results should not say.

Arsenic, thrice weekly, for years. Antimony. Belladonna in measured doses. Mercury, lead, compounds she'd had to send away to the university to identify. Any one of them, at those accumulations, should have killed a person slowly across a decade. Together they should have killed them twice.

Either Hazel Eriksen was a miracle, or Hazel Eriksen was a *payment plan*, and Serane had learned to distrust the second word almost as much as the first.

She followed the wet cloths down.

* * *

She caught them at the threshold of the court beneath, and she chose her moment the way she chose all her moments: when flight was impossible and violence would be suicide and only words remained. Words were her home ground.

They stood in a cavern where the roots opened onto a stair of black stone descending into a glow that was not firelight and not daylight but something bureaucratic—a light without warmth, the light of ledgers and seals. The mountain court hummed beyond it. She could hear, distantly, the sound of quills. Thousands of quills. It sounded like insects. It sounded like judgment.

Hazel Eriksen turned first. Of course they did; tasters survived by noticing. Wren—the lamplighter, narrow-shouldered, burn-scarred hands, eyes that had recently seen a god and hadn't recovered—turned a half-second later, and Serane watched the fear move through them both like weather.

"Inquisitor." Hazel's voice was level. Good. Trained. Everything about this one was trained, including the parts they thought were natural. "You're a long way from your chapterhouse."

"Further than you know." She stepped into the glow-light so they could see her face, see the warrant case at her belt, see the knife she did not reach for. "I traced your guild's ink, Master Eriksen. Or Mistress. The records disagree, which is itself a kind of record." She let that sit. "Grown gall-ink, churchyard water, consecrated salt. It points. Did you know it points? Someone in your Gild built a compass into their own secrecy, and I simply asked it where home was."

"We're not going back up," Wren said. Their voice cracked on *up*, and Serane catalogued it: grief, not guilt. Guilt hides; grief leans forward. "Whatever you think we stole—"

"I think you're going to steal a soul," Serane said, "from the court beneath the mountain. I think you signed something once, Master Eriksen, without reading it, and the interest has come due in the worst currency there is. And I think—" she took one step closer, boots silent on stone worn smooth by centuries of the dead walking it, "—that you are the single most valuable witness my office has ever had the fortune to corner."

Hazel's eyes narrowed. "Cornered people don't make good witnesses."

"Cornered people make excellent *converts*." Serane opened the warrant case—not the warrant itself, but the folded paper beneath it, which she had written by candlelight two nights ago in language she had composed with more care than she had ever given a sermon. "So hear me. Hear me fully, before you go down those stairs, because after them I may not be able to protect you, and I am offering protection. That is not a sentence I say often."

She read it to them. She had memorized it, of course, but she read it anyway, because documents read aloud acquire weight.

Full immunity. Not just for Hazel—for the record, for the past, for every poison swallowed and every entry written in the Gild's hidden books. Immunity from prosecution, from inquest, from the ordeal-by-poison that her own office reserved for the divinely tainted. In exchange: the books. All of them. Every ledger of the Gild of Impossible Things, surrendered to the Crown, its trades licensed going forward, its miracles taxed and registered and dragged into the light where law could hold them.

"You'd burn them," Hazel said quietly.

"I'd *read* them. There is a difference, though I understand why you might not believe that." She folded the paper closed. "Master Eriksen. Hazel. Look at what you are. You have drunk enough poison to kill a garrison. You walk through banquets that are minefields and you taste arsenic the way other people taste wine. Do you imagine that continues? My colleagues are already asking questions. Serane Vail asks questions and then stops asking, but the others—" she shook her head. "There is a trial coming. I have seen its shape for weeks. They will put you in a public square and hand you a cup compounded of everything you have ever survived, and they will call it justice, and whether you live or die, the Gild dies with you, because a hunted taster cannot keep secrets and a revealed secret cannot keep a guild."

Wren made a sound—small, involuntary—and Hazel's hand found theirs without looking. Serane noted that too. Filed it. Love was leverage; she hated that it was, but hating arithmetic has never once changed a sum.

"And if I refuse?" Hazel said.

"Then you go down those stairs, and you attempt whatever impossible theft you've planned against a court that has been filing souls since before my ancestors learned to farm, and you will fail, or worse, you will succeed, and the mountain will notice, and when the collectors come up into the daylight looking for whoever rewrote the divine accounts—" she met Hazel's eyes and did not look away, because mercy delivered while flinching is not mercy, it is theater, "—they will find a city full of frightened people and one guild whose every member can be named from your own handwriting. Your widow with her borrowed luck. Your soldier with his returned courage. Every soul you ever helped, delivered to the inquisition in alphabetical order, because you wanted to save one lamplighter."

The cavern was very quiet. Somewhere below, the quills went on scratching, patient as erosion.

"That's not mercy," Hazel said at last. "That's a bill of sale."

"It is both," Serane said. "Everything worth signing is. I learned that from the church, and the church learned it from the gods, and the gods—" she gestured downward, at the whole humming bureaucracy of the dead, "—apparently learned it from us. I am offering you the only safety that exists in this world, Hazel Eriksen, which is to belong to an institution strong enough to defend you. I am offering you what you have spent your whole life avoiding. A master. A place in the accounts. Protection."

"And everyone I protect goes in the book with me."

"Yes."

Hazel looked at her for a long moment, and Serane saw the calculation moving behind those poison-proof eyes, fast and honest, weighing lives the way a taster weighs courses. She respected it. She had expected pleading, or rage, or the wet-eyed nobility that made witnesses so tiresome. Instead she got accounting. For one strange, unwelcome instant, she thought: *this one would have made a fine inquisitor.* Then she put the thought away where she kept all thoughts that endangered her certainty.

"No," Hazel said.

Just that. No speech, no justification. Wren's grip tightened on their hand, and Hazel stepped forward—toward the stairs, toward the light of quills—drawing Wren with them, and paused at the edge of the glow.

"For what it's worth, Inquisitor," Hazel said, "you're the first person from up there who's offered me anything true. It's a rotten bargain, but it's honest rot. When this is over—if it's over—you'll find me easier to catch than you expect."

Then they descended, the two of them, into the light without warmth, and the quill-sound rose to meet them like surf.

Serane stood alone at the top of the stairs for a long time.

She should follow. Every protocol said follow; the warrant said follow; the needle in her pocket, she was sure, still pointed down. But something else had happened in that cavern, something she had not planned and did not enjoy examining: when she had read her offer aloud, she had heard her own voice the way the dead must hear it, and she had recognized the shape of the document for what it was. Not immunity. An amortization schedule. Herself, offering to become the mountain's agent in the upper world, collecting payments on a guild of miracles until nothing impossible remained anywhere at all.

She had built her life on the belief that silence was the only honest answer a god ever gave. And now she had watched a poisoned little clerk refuse the safest bargain in the kingdom for the sake of a lamplighter, and somewhere in her chest, in the sealed room where she kept her mother's deathbed, a door had rattled on its hinges.

"They're going to do something stupid," she said aloud, to the roots, to the pools of unanswered prayer. "Something magnificent and stupid and *written down*."

Because she had missed it—she realized it now, with the cold bloom of the investigator's worst discovery, the one that arrives too late—she had missed it in the oilcloth bundle Hazel kept checking like a miser's purse. She had assumed papers. Records. Leverage.

But the bundle had been the wrong shape for paper. It had been the shape of a *book*. A large one. Old. The kind of book that a guild keeps for four hundred years and teaches to forget.

Or—the thought arrived like ice water—the kind of book a guild keeps that remembers *everything*, including, if a sufficiently desperate keeper held a pen over the right page—

"Oh," Serane Vail said softly, to the dark, to the quills below, to whatever gods were listening and whatever ones weren't. "Oh, you absolute fool. You're not going down there to steal a soul."

She was already running, coat streaming, down the black stair, warrant forgotten, knife drawn, toward the light of ten thousand patient quills.

"You're going to *write* in it."
