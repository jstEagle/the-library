## 14. Forgery

The stair spat us out into the root-cellar of the Lamplighters' Hall at an hour when even the rats had gone to bed ashamed of themselves. I came up the last steps on hands and knees, tasting stone and cold iron and, beneath it all, the faint green sweetness of prayer-water that had soaked into the wood of the ferryman's boat and would never fully leave it. My knees were bleeding through my trousers. My mouth was full of the mountain's dust, which tastes the way a closed door sounds.

Wren came up behind me, carrying the lamp.

Not one of theirs—those were all snuffed and locked away since Serane's patrols began walking the district in pairs, counting flames like a moneylender counting coins. This was the river-god's gift: a stub of tallow no bigger than my thumb that burned without consuming anything at all, which is either a miracle or a very elegant fraud, and after what we had seen below, I no longer believed there was a difference.

"Put it out," I said, and then, because Wren was looking at me with that expression they got when I gave orders like a person who expected to be obeyed: "Please."

They set it on the cellar floor between us. The flame stood perfectly still, like a held breath.

Above us, muffled by three floors of timber and neglect, the Hall slept. Below us, the tunnels went down and down to a court where souls were filed like grain and judges spoke only in clauses. And somewhere between those two places—between the sleeping world of lamplighters and the waking world of creditors—there was a line I was about to cross with ink still wet on my fingers from the ferryman's contract.

"You could still stop," Wren said.

"I know."

"I'm not asking you to stop. I'm telling you that you *could*." They crouched beside the lamp, and its light climbed their face the way water climbs paper. "That's different. Nobody's made you do this. Not Magpie, not the guild, not me. If it goes wrong—if the mountain comes for someone—it should come for the person who chose this. That's you. You have to actually choose it, Hazel. Out loud. Or none of the rest of it counts."

I sat back against a root as thick as a wine cask and let myself feel, for one unguarded moment, everything I had been carrying since the court beneath: the weight of my own name written in the gods' master ledger as collateral since childhood; the arithmetic of every poison I had ever survived, each one a payment collected against a debt I had never known I owed; and underneath all of it, the small, unbearable fact that Wren's soul had been sold to settle *my* account, signed in my hand, unread, when I was nineteen years old and hungry enough to sign anything.

I had spent my whole life being a vessel that could die safely. It turned out I had also spent my whole life being a debtor who could not die at all.

"I choose it," I said.

The lamp flame leaned toward me, just slightly, the way a listener leans in.

* * *

We worked through the hours before dawn, because forgery, like cookery, is mostly preparation and only briefly courage.

I laid out my tools on Wren's worktable the way I laid out a tasting service: everything in order of use, nothing within reach that wasn't meant to be reached. The guild's traveling ledger, wrapped in oilcloth—the real one, smuggled up from the archive before Magpie's council could vote it into a lockbox, though I suspected Magpie had looked the other way with the particular precision of a woman who wanted deniability she could point to later. A pen knife honed on riverstone. A candle stub of ordinary, mortal wax, because some things needed to stay honest. And the ink.

The ink was the problem.

Guild ink is made from things that should not be combined: lampblack from a widow's vigil, gall from an oak struck by lightning, the dried blood of a man who died forgiven. It writes debts. It writes favors. It writes every impossible thing the Gild has ever brokered, and it does so legibly to gods, which is precisely why the guild survives by being unrecorded anywhere else. But guild ink writes *guild* entries. What I needed to write tonight was an entry in a book I had no right to touch, in a hand older than the guild, and the ink for that had to be something else entirely.

Prayer, I had learned below, pools like groundwater. It seeps. It collects in low places—in root-cellars, in the foundations of old temples, in the silt of rivers where people once knelt on banks and asked forgotten gods for rain. The ferryman had shown me where it gathered in the tunnels, black water in black stone, and he had sold me a stoppered vial of it for a memory I still can't quite locate, which I try not to think about.

I uncorked the vial now. The smell hit first: ozone and honey and something like the moment before crying. Then the taste reached me—not through my tongue but through whatever it is in me that has spent twenty years cataloguing poisons—and I understood that this was the most dangerous substance I had ever handled, more dangerous than arsenic, than aconite, than the compounded cup Serane would someday put in front of me if she caught me. Poisons kill the body. This rewrote the terms.

"Hazel." Wren's voice, quiet. "Your nose is bleeding."

I touched my upper lip and looked at the red on my fingertips, ordinary blood, mortal blood, and thought: good. Let it be mixed. Let the entry carry both of us.

"All right," I said. "Here's how it works. In the gods' ledger, nothing is deleted. That's the whole architecture of the place—every debt, every payment, every sale stays written forever, and the mountain's clerks spend eternity reconciling columns that never balance. So I can't erase the record of your soul being sold. I can't cross it out. All I can do is add a new line." I dipped the pen. "A transfer. Soul of Wren Ashlar, formerly held as collateral by the Court Beneath the Root, returned to the body of Wren Ashlar, living, per the guarantor named below."

"And the guarantor," Wren said, "is you."

"The guarantor is me." I kept my eyes on the page. Saying it while looking at them would have made it a promise between two people, and this couldn't be that. It had to be a promise between me and every god still listening, and gods, I had learned, are terrible witnesses—they take everything literally. "If the entry is challenged, the guarantor stands for it. Whatever the mountain claims, it claims from me instead. My immunity. My anonymity. My—" I stopped.

"Your life," Wren said, not gently, because gentleness would have let me pretend otherwise.

"My account," I corrected, because accounting was the only language I trusted myself to speak without shaking. "Everything I've built up over twenty years of surviving what should have killed me. Every immunity is a credit. The forged line draws against all of it. If the gods honor my signature, the poisons will never find purchase in me again—but neither will anything else. No protection. No invisibility. No guild shadow to hide in. I'll be as mortal as a cook and as visible as a bonfire."

"That's the trade you're describing."

"That's the trade."

Wren was silent for a long moment. The god-lamp burned between us, patient as tide. Then they said, "You know what Magpie will say. She told you the rule herself. The guild survives by being unrecorded. One visible keeper and the whole book burns."

"Yes."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway." I finally looked up. "Magpie taught me the books, Wren. Every column, every rule. But she also taught me what the books are *for*. An entry isn't a cage. It's a testimony. Somewhere, somebody wrote down that a thing happened, so that it couldn't be unwritten. That's all forgery is, when the original crime was legal. It's testimony, made by hand, outside all law, because the law was the thing that sold you in the first place."

Wren crossed the room and stood behind my chair, and I felt their chin rest on top of my head, light as a bird landing, the way they'd done since we were both too young to know better. "Then write it," they said. "And write it clean. You always said a bad entry is worse than no entry."

"A bad entry is worse than no entry," I agreed, and bent to the work.

* * *

The writing took less time than the preparation, as courage always does.

I wrote the date in the old reckoning, the one the guild used before it went underground, because dates are anchors and I wanted this one moored deep. I wrote the parties: the Court Beneath the Root, holder of record; Wren Ashlar, lamplighter, subject of the holding. I wrote the consideration—which was the hard part, because consideration must be true or the whole entry collapses, and the truth was ugly. The consideration was my own collateral status, disclosed at last, offered at last: *all immunities accumulated by Hazel Eriksen, food-taster, being payments collected against the undersigned's standing debt, hereby surrendered in full satisfaction thereof.*

My hand did not shake. Twenty years of lifting poisoned cups in front of kings had trained it steadier than any surgeon's.

Then the signature line.

Below it, in smaller script, the words every guild child learns to fear: *Guarantor accepts all liability, present and contingent, known and unknown.* Known and unknown. That was the clause that had ruined me at nineteen—I had signed it unread, and it had cost Wren their soul. Now I read it twice, aloud, syllable by syllable, and signed it again with my eyes open.

The pen touched the prayer-ink to the page, and the room changed.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible part. No thunder, no wind, no lamps guttering—just a sensation like a vast attention turning toward us, slow as a lighthouse beam, the way the whole night sky might lean if it noticed a single candle. The ink drank into the paper and the paper drank the ink and somewhere far below, in a hall of filing cabinets and patient clerks, a line moved from one column to another, and the reconciliation that had stood for eleven years tipped, and tipped, and held.

The god-lamp flared once, white, and settled.

"It's done?" Wren whispered. They had both hands pressed flat over their sternum, over the hollow place they'd carried since before we met, the missing-weight they'd described to me once, drunk, as *the feeling of a coat I lent out and never got back.*

"It's done," I said. "Breathe. Tell me what you feel."

Wren breathed. Their face did something complicated—grief and relief fighting for the same ground—and then they made a sound I had never heard from them, small and cracked, and said, "Edges. Hazel, I have *edges*. I didn't know I was missing them until—" They laughed, wet and disbelieving. "It's like a tooth that ached so long you forgot it was yours."

I blew out the ordinary candle. I left the god-lamp burning, because putting it out felt like ingratitude, and I am many things but not that.

What I did next, I did because the books demanded it. I opened the guild's traveling ledger to a fresh page and recorded the transaction in our own hand, our own ink, our own tongue: *Entry 4,417. One soul, returned. Consideration: everything. Witnessed by the guarantor alone, who accepts the consequences.* And then, because the beat of the pen paused there anyway, I added a line no auditor would approve:

*Done gladly.*

I blotted it. I closed the book. And I sat in the dark of the lamplighters' cellar with my beloved breathing whole beside me, and I waited to feel damned, and what I felt instead was cleaner than absolution: the specific, terrible freedom of a person who has broken the one rule that mattered and found, on the other side of it, that the rule was load-bearing for everyone *except* the people it was written to protect.

"Hazel," Wren said slowly. They were looking at the little lamp. "Hazel, look at the flame."

I looked. The flame was leaning east. There was no wind in the cellar.

Then it leaned north. Then it lay nearly flat, pointing down toward the floor, toward the roots, toward the mountain, like a compass needle that had found not north but *attention*—and around us, faint through three floors of sleeping timber, every lamp in the Lamplighters' Hall guttered at once, though every wick was cold.

Far off, deeper than sound, something vast shifted its weight, the way a creditor shifts in a chair when a number stops adding up.

"They've noticed," I said.

The flame pointed at the door.
