## 15. The Mountain Notices

The first lamp to die was the one outside the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, and it did not go out so much as *flinch*—a stutter of gold, a gasp of shadow, then dark. The chandler's apprentice saw it happen and thought at once of his mother's funeral, though he could not have said why.

Then the second lamp guttered. Then the third.

By midnight, a third of the city's lights were failing in waves, district by district, like a tide going out of flame. Lamplighters stood in the streets with their poles raised and useless, watching fire behave like something frightened. In the Warrens, an entire block went black for eleven minutes, and during those eleven minutes every clock in the block stopped—including a watch that had not run since its owner drowned in the canal nine years ago. It ticked twice, loudly, and stopped again.

The dead noticed before the living did.

In the crypt beneath the Cathedral of Saint Verity, where the honored dead lay in rows like spoons in a case, Sister Anthea heard knocking. Not from above—from *below*, from under the flagstones, patient and rhythmic, as though someone down there were politely requesting entry after a long journey. She fetched the prior. The prior fetched salt. By dawn they had chalked three circles on the floor and none of them held, because whatever was moving below the city had already moved past them, unhurried, with the confidence of a creditor who has never once been refused.

And far beneath all of it, in the court beneath the mountain, a bell rang that had not rung in four hundred years.

It was a small bell. It hung over a clerk's desk in a corridor of desks that stretched farther than the corridor had any right to be long, and it rang once, with a sound like a fingernail tapped against bone, and every soul-filed grain in every drawer shifted in its slot.

A clerk looked up. The clerk had no name anymore—names were removed upon hiring—but it remembered being called Peth once, when it had hands warm enough to feel things. It rose, crossed to the master ledger, and read the new entry.

Its pen trembled.

There was a signature in the gods' book that should not have been there. A guarantor's line, filled in human ink, in a human hand, naming itself liable for a debt that belonged to heaven. Below it, in letters that seemed to have been written by the page itself rather than by any pen: *SOUL RETURNED TO BODY OF ORIGIN. ACCOUNT REBALANCED BY PRIVATE PARTY.*

Peth had worked the desk for six hundred years. It had seen petitions, bribes, appeals, and once, memorably, an invasion. It had never seen a forgery.

It picked up the small bell and rang it again, and this time the sound went all the way up through the roots of the mountain, through the stone, through the sleeping city, and out into the world, where it was heard by exactly three people—one of whom was asleep in a guild safehouse with a woman who had been dead of soul for seven years and alive of body for one night, and who was, at that moment, dreaming of standing in a queue that stretched into darkness.

* * *

Serane Vail woke because her candle went out.

She did not light another. She lay in the dark of her rented rooms above the inquisitor's annex and listened, the way she listened to everything—with her whole attention, like a hand pressed flat against the world's chest.

The lamps outside her window had gone out in sequence, west to east, each one a heartbeat apart. That was wrong. Wind failed lamps randomly. Wind did not count.

She got up, dressed in the dark, and pulled out the folder she had built over six weeks of patient, unglamorous work: answered prayers, mapped by district and date. She had charted them like outbreaks of plague, because that was what they were—a contagion of the impossible, spreading along the old channels of belief. And she had noticed, weeks ago, what no one else had bothered to notice: the outbreaks clustered around certain addresses. A widow's house where luck had turned inexplicably kind. A soldier's lodging where courage had come back after desertion. And always, always, within two streets of these miracles, some small commercial enterprise that appeared on no tax roll, employed no one, sold nothing, and paid its rent anyway in coin that smelled faintly of river water.

Someone was laundering grace. Someone was keeping books on the divine.

Tonight the pattern had broken open. Whatever her quarry had done tonight, they had done it *loudly*—loud enough to gutter a city's worth of lamps and wake things under the cathedral floor. Amateurs made noise. But this was not amateur noise. This was the noise of someone breaking a rule that had been kept for centuries, which meant they had found the rulebook, which meant—

Serane opened the folder to the page she had been saving. A single sheet, recovered from a burned brazier behind a chandler's shop, half-consumed, bearing three lines of ledger notation in iron-gall ink with a distinctive violet undertone. She had sent a sample to the apothecaries' guild a week ago. The reply had arrived that evening, unopened until now.

She broke the seal.

*Ink contains gallnut, gum arabic, copperas, and an additive consistent with riverbed clay from the Graywash delta. Proportion unusual. Recommend inquiry with scriveners specializing in archival or ecclesiastical record-keeping.*

Archival. Ecclesiastical. Or both.

Serane set the letter down and, for the first time in her career, felt something adjacent to respect. Whoever had written that entry had used ink designed to last a thousand years. They had known their work would be examined by eternity and had prepared accordingly.

They had also, she thought, looking out the window at the dead lamps, just signed their own arrest warrant in the most legible hand imaginable.

She began packing her satchel. Salt, chalk, silver wire, the good manacles with the prayer-etched bands. And after a moment's hesitation—the hesitation itself noted and filed away for later self-examination—the vial of antidote she had compounded after tasting Hazel Eriksen's blood-poisons in that long afternoon interview. Not because she expected to need it. Because she had liked the taster, in the way one likes a well-made lock, and well-made locks deserved to survive being picked.

Outside her window, the last lamp relit. All of them did, in the same counted rhythm, west to east, like a held breath released.

"Interesting," Serane said to the empty room, and went to find her boots.

* * *

Magpie smelled the smoke before anyone told her about the smoke, which was fortunate, because the smoke was hers.

She had come to the archive at the hour when honest people sleep and guildkeepers work, carrying a lantern, a keyring, and a decision she had spent three days not making. The archive lived under the Tannery Row safehouse, forty feet of shelving carved into old cellar stone, and it held four hundred years of entries: favors granted, memories brokered, luck lent at interest, griefs bought and resold, one miracle from 1311 that Magpie had personally verified and still did not fully believe. Every entry was in the guild ink. Every entry was evidence.

And every entry was now a map leading straight to Hazel.

Magpie stood at the top of the stairs for a long time, listening to the silence of the shelves. Somewhere overhead, muffled by floors, the safehouse's other residents slept: two ferry-brokers, a retired memory-cutter, and a boy of nineteen whose job was to forget things professionally and who therefore slept more peacefully than anyone she knew.

"The rule," she said aloud, to the shelves, to the guild, to the four hundred years of careful invisible people stacked around her in their neat acid-free boxes. "The rule is we are not recorded. The rule is we do not exist. You knew that. Both of you knew that."

Hazel had known. Hazel had recited it back to her in the counting room a dozen times, dutiful as scripture. And Hazel had gone below the mountain anyway and forged a signature in the oldest book there was, using guild ink, in guild hand, with guild training—and had not even tried to hide it well, because Hazel had known, even then, that hiding would cost Wren the chance to keep what they'd won.

Fools. Both of them. Her fools. Raised in the counting room between the ledgers, fed on broth and bookkeeping, taught to value a balanced column over a full heart—and then they'd gone and valued a heart instead, and now the whole architecture of four centuries of invisibility was coming down around them like a shelf in an earthquake.

Magpie walked down the stairs.

She started with the modern entries. Ten years back, twenty, thirty—anything in her own hand or Hazel's, anything that could be cross-referenced, anything with the violet-clay ink that Serane Vail's apothecaries had already identified. She carried the boxes to the brazier in the corner, the one the memory-cutters used for disposing of expired contracts, and she fed the archive into it page by page.

It should have hurt more than it did. That was the thing she noticed, kneeling in the firelight with soot climbing her forearms: it didn't hurt at all, and that absence frightened her worse than grief would have. Because if burning four hundred years of her life's work felt like nothing, then perhaps the work had become nothing, or perhaps she had, or perhaps—in the thought she flinched away from and then made herself look at directly—institutions were only stories people agreed to die for, and she had simply finished dying for this one.

The widow's borrowed luck. Burned. The soldier's returned courage. Burned. The miracle of 1311, which she had verified herself at thirty years old with wet eyes and shaking hands. She held it a moment longer than the others.

"For the record," she told it, "I'm sorry. You deserved better than me."

She put it in the fire.

She was three hours into it, hair singed, throat raw, when she came to the box at the very back—the one containing Hazel's apprenticeship records. The trade documents. Including, filed without fanfare among routine entries, a contract of sale: one soul, lamplighter-class, consigned to the court beneath the mountain against debts incurred, countersigned by a fourteen-year-old apprentice who had not read the terms.

Magpie had signed it too. As witness. She had watched a child sign away a stranger's soul to settle a debt the child didn't know existed, and she had said nothing, because the guild's rule was that the guild survived and everything else was inventory.

That page did not burn easily. She had to hold it in the flames herself, watching the ink lift off the paper like something escaping, and when it was ash she stayed kneeling at the brazier a while longer, and if anyone had seen her they would have said the guildkeeper of the Gild of Impossible Things was praying, which would have been the cruelest joke of a very long night.

Near dawn, she climbed the stairs, washed her hands and face, and wrote a note in plain, traceable, deliberately careless ink:

*Archive lost to fire. Cause unknown. Keeper uninjured. — M.*

Then she pinned it to the safehouse door where any inquisitor in the city could find it, and went upstairs to wake the boy who forgot things professionally.

"You're leaving," she told him, when he sat up blinking. "Tonight. There's a boat at Graywash and a name you'll use and a life you'll have, and I need you to do your job on the way there. Forget this house. Forget the guild. Forget me."

He stared at her. "You're burning us all out," he said—not accusing. Understanding. "Because they'll come here next."

"They're already coming." She packed his bag while she talked, briskly, because if she slowed down she would stop. "Collectors from under the mountain, and worse, inquisitors from above it. We're caught between two sets of bookkeepers and neither one forgives."

"And you?"

"I have one more piece of accounting to do." She closed the bag and handed it over. "Go. Now. Don't look back; it's sentimental and it slows you down."

He hugged her, quickly, hard, the way the young do—as though affection were a door slammed shut—and then he was gone down the back stair, and Magpie stood alone in a safehouse that was no longer safe, in a guild that was no longer hidden, and began, methodically, to erase herself.

* * *

The collectors came up out of the ground at the place where the river met the mountain's roots, and the ferryman who saw them arrive rowed away so fast he left his oar behind.

There were three of them. They wore the gray of undyed wool and the faces of people you almost recognized—every collector wore a face assembled from the memories of whoever looked at them, so that meeting one felt like passing a stranger who might be your dead brother, might be your childhood neighbor, might be nobody. They carried no weapons. They carried clipboards. This was somehow worse.

The first collector consulted its clipboard at the riverbank. "Entry 4471-H," it said, to no one and everyone. "Soul of Wren Ash, lamplighter, returned to body by unauthorized amendment. Guarantor: Hazel Eriksen, food-taster, court of the Marquisate." It looked up. Its face settled into something like a schoolteacher's, patient and disappointed. "Guarantor is liable. Collect the guarantor."

They walked into the city at dawn, three figures in gray, unhurried, and everywhere they passed, dogs began to howl and clocks ran backward and flowers bloomed out of season in window boxes, because the old world leaked a little wherever they stepped. People watched them pass and forgot them immediately, and then spent the rest of the day uneasy without knowing why, checking locks, missing appointments, weeping at small music.

The second collector paused at the foot of Candlewick Row and touched the nearest lamp post. The flame above bent toward its fingers like a plant toward sun.

"She wrote him back in," it said. Its voice was the voice of a colleague remarking on weather. "With a guarantor's clause. Do you know how long it's been since anyone accepted liability for a soul?"

"Four hundred years," said the third collector.

"Do you know what happens to guarantors who default?"

"No one does," said the second collector. "That's the interesting part."

They continued up the hill toward the palace, toward the court, toward a royal food-taster who was, at that moment, asleep beside a lamplighter with a whole soul, neither of them yet aware that the morning was assembling itself into a trap—or that the inquisitor's men were already surrounding the street, quietly, competently, from the other direction.

Two courts. One doorway. Hazel Eriksen between them, still dreaming, one hand curled loosely around Wren's wrist, holding on the way people hold on in dreams, when they know, somewhere below the dreaming, that the world is coming to collect.

The knock, when it finally came, was polite.

It was also, unmistakably, from below.
