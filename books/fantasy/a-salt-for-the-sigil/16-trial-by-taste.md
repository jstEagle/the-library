## 16. Trial by Taste

They came for me at the oyster course.

I remember that detail because it was the last ordinary thing. The hall smelled of brine and lemon and beeswax candles, two hundred courtiers murmuring over the king's midwinter feast, and I had just lifted the taster's silver spoon to my lips—oysters in saffron cream, no poison but the natural cruelty of shellfish past their prime—when the doors opened and Inquisitor Serane Vail walked through them with six of her gray-cloaked wardens and a face like a closed book.

The hall did not go silent all at once. Silence spread the way cold does, from the doors inward, conversation dying table by table as people understood what was entering among them. Somewhere a fork rang against porcelain and was not picked up again.

I set down the spoon. There was no sense pretending to finish the oysters.

"Hazel Eriksen," Serane said, and her voice carried without effort, the way a blade carries light. "Keeper of the Gild of Impossible Things. Forger of entries in the Book of Heaven. Come forward."

*So,* I thought, with the strange calm that had lived in my body since the mountain, since the ledger, since I signed my name as guarantor with ink that was not entirely mine. *Magpie's fire didn't hide everything. Or it hid everything except the one thing that mattered.*

I stood. Two hundred faces turned to me—not the faces they usually turned, the polite incurious glances given to a servant, a vessel, a thing that could die safely on the king's behalf. These were different. These were the faces people make at a fire, or an execution, or a miracle. I supposed I might be all three before midnight.

Wren was not in the hall. I had made sure of it—sent them north on a lamplighting errand that didn't exist, kissed them at the door of our rooms with a lie in my mouth that tasted worse than anything I had ever swallowed professionally. If this went badly, Wren would be whole and alive and far away when the collectors came for the rest. That was the only entry in any ledger I still cared about balancing.

I walked the length of the hall. My shoes sounded very loud. The king watched from his high table with the expression of a man watching weather he cannot control, which was, I had long suspected, his expression for nearly everything.

Serane Vail waited for me at the center of the floor. Up close she was exactly what she always was: beautiful the way a contract is beautiful, every line precise, every clause load-bearing. She carried no chains. She carried a cup.

It was plain pewter, dented at the rim, the kind of cup a scullery maid would use. That was the first wrongness. The second was what sat inside it—a liquid so dark it seemed less like wine than like a hole cut in the shape of a cup, and so many things moved beneath its surface that my tongue, that treacherous educated instrument, began cataloguing them against my will. Hemlock. Arsenic. Belladonna. Aqueous lead. Something mineral and old that had no name in any apothecary's Latin, something I knew by flavor alone, because I had died of it once, briefly, in an apprenticeship I never finished.

"Every poison you have ever survived," Serane said. "Compounded. I have spent four months compiling the list." She held the cup out to me as if offering refreshment at a garden party. "Your guild kept excellent records. You should be proud. It made my work remarkably simple."

"You burned nothing," I said. "You *read*."

"I read everything." Her eyes were gray and patient and absolutely certain. "That is what makes me good at my work, Master Eriksen. You forged an entry in the gods' own ledger. You wrote a soul back into a living body with your own hand and named yourself guarantor. Under the old law—and the old law is the only law the gods still honor—such an act must be tested. If heaven ratifies your signature, you live." She smiled, small and terrible. "If it does not, you die. Here. Now. Before witnesses."

"And if I refuse the cup?"

"Then I arrest you for forgery, heresy, unlicensed divinity, and contempt of ordeal, and you hang in the market square by week's end, and the gods' silence on the matter will tell us everything we need to know about whose side heaven takes." She tilted her head. "I confess I hope you refuse. It would simplify my reports considerably."

The hall breathed around us. Two hundred people holding their breath sounds like the sea.

I looked at the cup. I thought about what was actually in it—not the poisons, those I could name blindfolded, but the other thing, the thing underneath. Every poison I had ever survived had been survived for a reason. I had learned that under the mountain, reading my own name in the master ledger of the old gods, written there as collateral since childhood: every agony endured was a payment collected, every death cheated was interest accrued. My immunity was not mine. It had never been mine. It was an amortization schedule wearing my skin.

And three nights ago, in a locked room above a shuttered print shop, I had poured all of it out into a bargain with a minor god of thresholds—every accumulated protection surrendered, wine poured from a cracked cup—to buy back the soul of a lamplighter who loved me.

Which meant the cup should kill me. By every law of chemistry, theology, and arithmetic, it should kill me.

Unless the gods honored the forged signature. Unless heaven itself had decided to count my handwriting among its scriptures.

There was only one way to find out, and Serane Vail was holding it under my nose.

* * *

They gave me the hall's center, a circle of candlelight, and the courtesy of removing my coat, which I took as either respect or theater—I have never learned where one ends and the other begins at court. The wardens formed a ring. The courtiers pressed close behind them, silk and fur and hungry eyes, everyone who had ever eaten a meal I tasted come to watch me taste my own death.

A herald read the charges. I listened to my life recited as a list of crimes: *unrecorded commerce in favors; trafficking in memories; the restoration of a soul without license; the forging of divine instruments.* It occurred to me, hearing it aloud, that every crime on the list was a kindness someone had needed. The widow's borrowed luck. The soldier's returned courage. Wren, waking whole. If these were crimes, then I had committed them gladly and would commit them again, and no ordeal would change the arithmetic of that.

Serane stood at the circle's edge with the cup in both hands, and for a moment—just a moment—something crossed her face that was not triumph. I had seen it before, in her office, over her ledgers of answered prayers: the look of a woman doing arithmetic she did not enjoy. Serane believed she was justice. I understood that now better than I wished to. She had spent her career in a world where mercy required paperwork, and here was a man who had simply written it himself, freehand, in a god's book.

"Master Eriksen," she said formally, "do you consent to the ordeal?"

"I consent." My voice was steadier than I had any right to expect. "Though I'd note for the record that the record is incomplete. You've listed what I've survived. You haven't asked what I've *paid*."

"I don't deal in payments," Serane said. "Only in accounts." And she handed me the cup.

It was heavier than it looked. Everything true is.

* * *

Before I drank—if I was going to drink, and the cup in my hands told me I was, because some part of me had already decided in the dark hours before dawn, standing over Wren's sleeping body, watching their chest rise with edges they'd never known were missing—I looked up. Past the candle ring. Past the vaulted ceiling with its painted constellations going brown with age.

To whatever was listening.

Because here is the secret I had carried out of the mountain, the one I had told no one, not even Magpie, not even Wren: the old gods are starving. They answer prayers now not out of benevolence but out of hunger—the desperate hunger of powers who went centuries unfed and discovered, when the prayers resumed, that belief is the only meal that keeps. They had let Wren's soul be sold. They had let me be poisoned across twenty years of dinners. They had arranged, patiently, amortized, the creation of exactly one person reckless enough and literate enough to forge an entry in their book—because a book that is never amended is a book that dies, and dead gods starve faster than living ones.

They wanted me to write. Very well. But writing goes both ways, and I had learned accounting at the knee of Magpie of the Gild of Impossible Things, and the first lesson of accounting is: *read the fine print before you sign, and after you sign, hold them to it.*

"If you're watching," I said quietly, under the noise of the hall, under the herald's droning, under Serane's gray patient gaze, "know that I know what this costs. I paid it already, under the mountain. This cup holds nothing I haven't survived and nothing I haven't lost. So if you honor my name—" I raised the cup, and the dark liquid shivered, and beneath its surface something shivered back, "—honor it *fully*. Not as a favor. As a debt. I am owed."

Somewhere far off, so far it might have been memory, water moved in a riverbed that had been dry for a hundred years.

I drank.

* * *

The first poison was hemlock, and it found me the way an old friend finds you at a funeral: instantly, intimately, unwelcome. Cold climbed my legs. The second was arsenic, and it arrived in my blood like a tax collector, itemizing me. Third belladonna, and the candles bloomed into flowers, and the flowers into eyes, and the eyes—

The eyes were the worst, because they were *looking*, and I understood suddenly that this was not chemistry anymore. Every poison I had ever survived had left a residue in the gods' ledger, and the ordeal was not asking whether my body could endure them. It was asking each poison, individually, in order, oldest first: *was this debt paid? Is this signature valid? Do you recognize the guarantor?*

And one by one, in the dark beneath my skin, they answered.

No.

Yes.

Paid in full.

Recognized.

The hall saw none of this. The hall saw a food-taster swaying in a ring of candlelight, lips graying, pupils blown wide, sweat cutting channels through the powder-white of court formality. The hall saw a man dying slowly in front of two hundred witnesses, which was, I suppose, the most honest performance of my entire career. Serane Vail watched with her hands folded and her jaw tight, and I saw her lips move once—counting, always counting—and I understood that she was tracking the sequence, checking my symptoms against her list, and that somewhere around the twelfth poison the numbers stopped making sense to her.

Because I should have been dead at the ninth. I knew it; she knew it. The compounded dose of the first nine alone exceeded anything flesh tolerates. Yet I stood. Gray-lipped, flower-eyed, heart stuttering like a lamp in wind—but I stood.

The last poison was the oldest one, the nameless mineral thing from my apprenticeship, and it did not ask permission. It rose through me like floodwater through a cellar and it spoke, in the voice of the mountain court, in the voice of contracts and grain-counts and judges who dealt only in obligations:

*This account is disputed.*

And I, hollowed out, emptied of every immunity I ever built, mortal as any cook, answered it the only way I have ever answered anything that frightened me.

*I know. Audit me.*

Silence. Real silence, the kind that presses on the ears. The candles guttered—all of them, two hundred flames bending toward me at once like grass toward sun—and above the painted constellations something vast turned over in its sleep, or woke, or remembered it had woken before and liked the taste.

Then the candles stood upright again, and I was still alive, and the cup slipped from my fingers and rang on the flagstones, empty.

I did not fall gracefully. I want that recorded somewhere, in some honest ledger: the hero of the hour folded up like a bad letter and went down onto the stones on his knees, then his hands, then his cheek, tasting stone and vomit and, faintly, impossibly, river water. Clean water. Water that had traveled a long way to be in that room.

The hall erupted. I heard it as though from underwater—shouting, screaming, the scrape of two hundred chairs, the king's voice rising in alarm or awe. Hands took my shoulders. Serane's face appeared above mine, upside down, gray eyes wide with something I had never expected to see in them.

Doubt. Beautiful, catastrophic doubt.

"That's not possible," she was saying, to me or to herself or to the ceiling. "The dose was lethal nine times over. The dose was—" She stopped. Recounted. Failed to balance. And I watched, through dimming vision, the moment when Inquisitor Serane Vail, who had built her life on the principle that everything answers to a thorough enough audit, encountered a line item that refused to sum.

"The gods," I managed, through a throat full of ash and river, "honored the signature."

"They don't *honor* signatures." Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "They haven't honored anything in five hundred years. They're *dead*, Eriksen. They're stories. Someone forged this. Someone rigged the ordeal, someone—"

"Look at the cup."

She looked. We all looked. The dented pewter cup lay on its side in the candlelight, empty, and in its bottom, where no liquid remained, something had been written. Scratched into the metal from the inside, in letters of tarnish and shadow, in a script older than the kingdom, older than the alphabet, older than the mountain:

**PAID IN FULL — WITNESSED**

The hall went quiet again, that spreading cold silence, and every eye that had come to watch a poisoner die now turned instead to heaven's handwriting on a scullery cup, and I lay on the stones of my king's feast-hall and laughed until it hurt, which did not take long, because everything hurt.

Serane stared at the cup for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet, and it was not addressed to me.

"Then they're not dead," she said. "Then they've been *reading*."

She knelt beside me—an inquisitor kneeling in front of the entire court, a sight no one in that room would forget, least of all her—and put two fingers to my throat, finding the pulse there, thin and stubborn and entirely mortal.

"You're empty," she said. "Whatever you were, whatever protected you—it's gone. I can hear it. Your heart is just a heart now." And then, softer, with the terrible gentleness of a woman realizing her ledger has a page she never wrote: "What did you trade?"

But I couldn't answer. Because the river-water taste had risen in my mouth again, and beneath the shouting and the candlelight and the ringing of that dented cup, I heard it—distant, vast, patient as sediment—the sound of the old gods clearing their throats.

They had let the sale happen. They had let me be poisoned. They had starved themselves on schedule to produce one reckless accountant willing to amend their book.

And now, having witnessed the payment, they wanted to discuss the terms.

The candles bent toward me again, all two hundred at once, and this time they did not straighten.
