## 17. What the Old Gods Want

The river had been called many things before it was called anything at all. It remembered the taste of glaciers, of mammoth blood, of the first clay jar set into its current by hands that shook with hope. For nine hundred years it had gone unanswered. Prayers still fell into it—thin as silt, most of them—but no one listened from the far bank, and so the river had learned to listen to itself.

Then a lamplighter had prayed half-laughing on a bridge, and the river god had felt it like a hook behind the ribs.

*You're real?* the boy had asked. *Anyone home?*

The river had answered with light. It had not thought about consequences. That was the trouble with gods who had been starving: they ate whatever was offered, and they did not count the cost until the bill came due.

Now the river pooled in the gutters beneath the great hall where a taster sat before a cup of compounded death, and it watched through the eyes of every drop of water in that room—the wine-dark sheen on the cup's rim, the sweat at Hazel Eriksen's temple, the tears the crowd would deny shedding. The river had brokered nothing in this affair. But it had whispered to its siblings, the old ones under root and stone and salt, and together they had agreed on one thing:

The ledger had to be rewritten by a mortal hand. Gods could not amend their own book; that was the first law, older than names. And mortals, left to themselves, never volunteered for suffering. So the old gods had arranged the terms to be unbearable enough that only someone already ruined by them would dare.

They had let Wren's soul be sold. They had let a child sign a contract she could not read. They had let poison pool in a girl's blood for twenty years, drop by patient drop, building an immunity like a cathedral built of small deaths—all of it collateral, all of it interest accruing toward this single hour.

It was not cruelty. The river told itself that. It was arithmetic. Someone had to be desperate enough, and precise enough, and loved enough to try.

The river watched the taster lift her chin, and it felt—for the first time in nine hundred years—something like shame.

It drowned the feeling. Rivers are good at that.

* * *

Hazel had expected the courtroom to go quiet when Serane Vail produced the cup. Instead it went loud—the way markets go loud before a riot, the way rivers go loud before they take a bridge. Two hundred courtiers murmuring behind fans and gloved hands, the king's heralds hammering their staves for order, somewhere near the back a woman weeping without any attempt to hide it. Wren. Of course Wren wasn't being kept out. Serane wanted an audience for this. An audience was the point.

The cup sat on a cushion of black velvet at the center of the dais, and it was beautiful, which was the first obscenity. Silver-wrought, chased with scenes of harvest, wide as a chalice and deep as a well. Inside it swirled a liquid the color of old bruises—not one color but many, shifting, because Serane had done her work properly. Every poison Hazel had ever survived, compounded. Arsenic from the banquet three years ago, the one that had killed the ambassador of Vell and put Hazel abed for a week while their body learned the shape of it. Hemlock from the assassination attempt during the midwinter feast. Belladonna, aconite, mercury ground fine as flour, the bitter green of foxglove, the almond ghost of cyanide, and things older and stranger that the guild's own apothecaries had brewed for testing and that Hazel had swallowed on Magpie's orders with a steady hand and a racing heart. Twenty years of the ledger written in Hazel's blood, decanted into a single cup.

"Let the record show," Serane said, and her voice carried the way a blade carries light, "that the accused is offered ordeal, not torture. If the gods favor the signature forged in their book, the accused will live." She turned, and her eyes found Hazel's with the patience of a cat that has already closed the door of the room. "If the gods do not favor it—well. Then the gods have spoken, and none of us need feel anything about it at all."

*You think this is your trap,* Hazel thought, looking at her. *You think you've built this.*

But Hazel had spent the night in a cell with water seeping through the stones, and the water had talked. Not in words, exactly. In pressure. In the way damp will find the crack in a wall and widen it. The river god had pressed against the mortar of Hazel's cell all night long, and what it pressed through was this: *the cup is not hers. The cup is ours. We have been waiting for you since you were six years old.*

Hazel had not slept. Hazel had done sums instead, because sums were the only prayer Hazel knew how to make.

"Accused." The herald's staff struck stone. "Approach the cup."

Hazel rose. Their legs worked, which surprised them faintly. The chains had been struck off for the ordeal—Serane wanted no mechanical excuse for what happened next—and the weight of their own unbound wrists felt obscene, like walking outdoors without skin.

The hall stretched ahead, forty feet of marble worn smooth by centuries of processions, and Hazel walked it the way they had walked to the tasting table a thousand mornings: spine straight, breath even, face arranged into the pleasant blankness that had kept them alive. Only now the blankness was a choice rather than a cage, and that made all the difference, and none.

Wren stood at the barrier with two guards' hands on his shoulders, and he was not crying anymore. He was doing something worse. He was mouthing words, over and over, and it took Hazel four steps to read them: *you don't have to. You don't have to. You don't have to.*

*I know,* Hazel mouthed back, and kept walking.

Because here was the thing Hazel had understood in the cell, in the dark, with the river whispering through the stones: the cup was a lie in both directions. Serane believed it was a trap—if the gods disowned the forgery, Hazel died and the law was satisfied. The old gods believed it was a coronation—they had starved for believers, they had engineered a soul's sale and a lifetime of poisoning, all to produce one mortal reckless enough to pick up their pen. Both of them were wrong about what the cup was.

The cup was a question. And questions belonged to the person answering them.

Hazel reached the dais. Up close, the compounded poison smelled like everything: like orchards and hospitals, like the inside of locks, like rain on hot slate, like the almond-sweet breath of a dying man Hazel had once held while he went. It smelled like Hazel's whole life, distilled.

"The accused will state their name," the herald intoned, "for the gods to hear."

And there it was. The hinge. The whole mechanism turning on one small sentence.

Hazel Eriksen, food-taster, vessel, instrument. Property of the crown's kitchen, insured against loss like a silver spoon. Unrecorded keeper of the Gild of Impossible Things, whose entire survival rested on never being named aloud. A person who had spent thirty years being nobody on purpose, because nobody was the only safe thing to be.

Serane Vail stood ten feet away with her hands folded, watching, and Hazel saw it cross her face—the flicker, quickly mastered. She knew too. If Hazel spoke their name, the invisibility ended. Even if the gods spared them, even if the forgery held, the guild's keeper would stand revealed before two hundred witnesses and every ear Serane had planted among them. Win or lose, Hazel paid.

That was the elegance of it. That was why Hazel almost admired her.

"Hazel," Hazel said.

Their voice came out steady. Small miracle.

"Hazel *what*?" the herald prompted, and Serane leaned forward by a degree, and somewhere in the rafters the old gods leaned forward too—Hazel could feel them now, the way you feel weather coming, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron and river-mud at the back of the throat.

"Hazel Eriksen," Hazel said. "Taster to the crown. Keeper of the accounts of the Gild of Impossible Things. Guarantor of the entry dated the third of Thawmonth, in the master ledger of the old gods, by which the soul of Wren Ashlane was returned to Wren Ashlane's body." A breath. "I wrote my name in your book. I am here to answer for it."

The hall erupted. Hammers hammered. Serane's composure cracked—not into anger, Hazel noted, but into something hungrier, the look of a scholar watching a rare text burn and trying to memorize it. *There,* Hazel thought. *Now you have me. Now I'm worth more than the books ever were.*

But the gods—

The gods were silent.

That was the wrong silence. Hazel had braced for thunder, for the lamps to gutter, for some sign that the ancient parties to this contract acknowledged their guarantor. Instead: nothing. The pressure behind the eyes didn't lift, but it didn't *answer*. It waited, the way a creditor waits, the way the mountain court waits, the way every ledger in creation waits for the payment to actually be tendered.

*Oh,* Hazel thought, cold spreading under the sternum, colder than any poison. *You want me to drink first. You want the payment rendered before you confirm receipt.*

Of course. They were gods of the old school. Contracts first, mercy never. They had let a child be sold to force this moment; they were not going to spend a scrap of grace confirming it in advance. Faith, it turned out, was just a word for signing without reading—and Hazel, of all people, knew exactly what that cost.

"The accused will drink," said the herald.

Hazel looked at the cup. Twenty years of survival swirled in it, every death they'd already died, waiting to be died all at once. Their immunity had been poured out of them in the mountain court—Hazel had felt it leave, felt the armor drain like wine from a breached cask—and what remained was a body like anyone's body, a cook's body, a mortal's body, holding nothing between itself and the cup but a forged signature and a promise.

Wren had stopped mouthing *you don't have to.* He had gone very still, the way he went still when a lamp caught on the first strike, that held-breath instant before the flame took or didn't. His lips moved once more, and this time the words were different, and Hazel read them clear across the marble:

*Come back and cook for me.*

Hazel picked up the cup.

It was heavier than it looked. Everything true was heavier than it looked; that was the first lesson of ledgers.

"For the record," Hazel said, and their voice cracked at last, finally, humanly, "I did it for love. Write that down."

And Hazel drank.

* * *

The first swallow tasted like the ambassador of Vell dying. The second like the midwinter hemlock, sweet-then-bitter-then-sweet, the flavor of a knife going in politely. The third—

The third was different.

The third tasted like rain on a bridge at dusk, like lamp-oil catching, like a boy laughing at his own joke to a god who hadn't been prayed to in nine hundred years. The third swallow tasted like the river.

And the river rose.

Not in the room—in *Hazel*, up through the soles of the feet, through the marble, through the roots of the palace and the bones of the mountain, every drop of water in the building suddenly remembering it belonged to something older than the crown. The torches burned blue. The compounded poison in Hazel's veins stopped being poison and became, for one impossible moment, *communion*: every toxin a psalm, every survived death a verse, the whole liturgy of Hazel's ruined, armored, magnificent body recited at last to an audience that had been listening all along.

*We let it happen,* the river said, or the thing speaking through the river said, vast and tired and terribly reasonable. *The sale. The debt. Your childhood, signed away in interest. We needed a hand small enough to hold the pen and stubborn enough to use it. Forgive us. Or don't. You are the first honest entry in our book in nine hundred years either way.*

*You used us,* Hazel thought, drowning upright, the cup slipping from their fingers, the hall tilting, Wren screaming somewhere beyond the blue fire. *You starved yourselves on purpose and called it strategy—*

*Yes,* said the old gods, with the terrible mildness of arithmetic. *What else were we supposed to do?*

And Hazel, falling, emptying, mortal as bread, thought: *find out.*
