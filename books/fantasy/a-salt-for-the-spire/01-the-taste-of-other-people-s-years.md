## 1. The Taste of Other People's Years

There are three rules to stealing memories in Vell's Landing, and I've broken two of them exactly once each.

Rule one: never take what you weren't paid for. Rule two: never keep what you take. Rule three — the one I've kept clean my whole career — is don't ask the client why they want it. Curiosity is the only theft that costs the thief.

The merchant's name was Odell Vasse, and he was dying the way rich men die: slowly, expensively, and with a great deal of paperwork. His house sat on the Stilt Row above the harbor, where the smell of brine and tar came through the shutters like a tax nobody could dodge. I'd been sent for by his factor, a damp little man named Quill who counted coins the way other men count rosaries, and the terms were simple. One memory. First love. Payment: forty crowns, half up front, half when the extraction took.

Forty crowns for a memory is a good price. Forty crowns for a *first love* is an insult dressed as a compliment, because first loves are the deepest-rooted things a person carries. But Odell Vasse was dying, and dying men set their own markets, so I said yes and drank his tea and waited for the sun to go down, because extraction goes easier in the dark. That's not magic. That's just people. It's easier to let go of something when you can pretend it's already gone.

"You'll feel a pulling," I told him, arranging the salt dish on the table between us. "Like a hook behind the eye. Most men describe it as grief. A few describe it as relief."

"And which do you recommend?" His voice was a dry leaf. He'd propped himself up against a mountain of pillows, and even now, with death already sitting in the room like an unpaid guest, he wore a nightshirt worth more than my year.

"I don't recommend anything," I said. "I'm not a physician. I'm a thief with better manners."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into that wet rattling sound I've heard enough times to know the schedule it keeps. I gave him water. He waved it off.

"My daughter thinks I should leave everything intact," he said. "For the estate. As if memories can be willed." Another laugh, weaker. "She doesn't know what I did to earn this house. She doesn't know what I'd have done. There's a woman in Corvess who knows, but she's been dead thirty years, and what she knew of me lives in one afternoon in a garden in Corvess, and I would rather she take it with her than leave it here for lawyers to pick over."

I didn't ask why. Rule three. But he told me anyway, the way dying men do, as if the telling were the payment and the crowns were just a formality.

"Her name was Sefa. I was nineteen. She sold figs at the market and had dirt under her fingernails from her father's orchard, and I thought that was the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen — dirt, Sebastian. Dirt under a girl's nails." He looked at his own hands, clean and soft and spotted with age. "One afternoon. We didn't even — it wasn't that kind of story. We talked. About nothing. About whether figs dream. She made me laugh until my ribs hurt, and then her father called her home, and I went back to my apprenticeship, and three years later I married money and built all this." The pillows. The shutters. The smell of tar. "And every year on the first warm day of spring, I think about figs dreaming, and I cannot tell you why, because I let that afternoon go soft on purpose. I rounded it off. I filed it down. And now it's the only thing I want to carry out of this world, and it's worn through like an old coin."

I'd heard versions of this speech maybe two hundred times. They always end the same way: *take it, take it whole, take it before I lose it to the rot.* People think memory-theft is about taking. It isn't. It's about giving someone the chance to hand off the thing they can't hold anymore. Every thief in the valley knows we're not robbers. We're ferrymen. We just charge like robbers because the work ruins you and nobody thanks a ferryman.

"Salt first," I said, and poured the line across the table between us — a thin white seam, unbroken. Salt is the whole trick. Not the incantations, not the oils, not the sigils the guild sells to idiots at triple price. Salt. The valley runs on it. Past the salt lines, magic can't follow, which means past the salt lines, magic can't *interfere*, which means inside a properly drawn ring, a man's mind is a locked room and I am very good at picking locks.

Odell put his hands flat on the table, palms down, the way I asked. Old hands. Steady, though. Dying men are often steadier than the living; they've stopped pretending there's anywhere to run.

"Now look at me," I said, "and think about the garden. Don't reach for it. Just let it be true. The harder you grip, the more it tears."

His eyes went somewhere else. Somewhere warm, I hoped. Somewhere with fig trees and a girl with dirty fingernails.

I put two fingers to his temple and reached in.

People ask what it feels like, from my side. The honest answer is that it feels like walking into a house where someone used to live. You know the shape of them from the furniture. In Odell's case the house was vast and mostly shuttered — ninety years of rooms, most of them dark, some of them nailed closed, one or two that I touched by accident and wished I hadn't. Guilt has a taste. So does greed. So does the particular flavor of a man who cheated his own brother out of a shipping share in the Year of the Low Tides and told himself a story about it every night for sixty years until the story was load-bearing.

I walked past all of it. Down the long corridor, past the doors marked with years, to the small bright room near the beginning where a nineteen-year-old boy kept an afternoon in a garden.

It was still beautiful. That's the thing nobody tells you — even a memory worn soft, even one the owner has handled so much the edges have gone smooth, keeps its light. I stood in it for a moment. Sun through leaves. The smell of cut grass and ripe fruit. A girl laughing at something, her voice coming from everywhere and nowhere, because I wasn't in the memory, I was beside it, the way you stand outside a lit window on a cold street.

*Figs dream,* she was saying. *Everything that sleeps sweetly dreams.*

Then I took it.

The taking is quick if you're good, and I'm good. One clean pull, like drawing a splinter. Behind me, in the shuttered house of Odell Vasse, a door went dark. On the table, the old man exhaled — a long breath, the kind you breathe at the end of a story — and when he opened his eyes, they were wet and calm.

"It's gone?" he asked.

"Gone."

"Do you—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you see it? When you carry it? Do you know what happens to figs?"

"No," I said, which was rule two talking, and also a lie, because of course I saw it. I always see it. That's the job. You can't lift a memory without carrying it up the stairs, and you can't carry it without it leaving its fingerprints on you.

But I don't keep them. That's rule two, and I meant what I said about breaking it only once. What I keep is the *shape* of them — the weight, the temperature, the way they sit in a man's chest. First loves are warm and slightly green, like early apples. Griefs are heavy and cold at the center. Regrets have teeth. I have carried hundreds of memories to market and I could not tell you a single name from any of them, but I know what a life weighs, and I know that almost everyone's is heavier than they think and lighter than they fear.

Quill counted out the second twenty crowns with the reverence of a priest, and I pocketed them, and I stood to go, and Odell Vasse caught my sleeve with fingers that had gone papery-thin.

"What will you do with it?" he whispered. "Her afternoon. Where does it go?"

"That depends on the buyer," I said. "Sometimes they're collected. Sometimes they're studied. Once, a woman bought her dead husband's happiest day just to sit inside it for an hour." I hesitated, because rule three, but he was dying and dying men get answers. "Mostly they sit in vaults. Memories keep. Yours will keep. Some spring, some stranger will stand in a garden in Corvess and laugh at a joke they never heard, and they won't know why, and that will be her, still going."

He smiled. It was a real smile, tired around the edges. "Good," he said. "Good. Let it keep going."

I let myself out through the garden door, down the stairs, onto the Stilt Row walkways where the harbor lamps were coming on one by one, little orange eyes opening over black water.

Here is the part of the evening I don't tell clients, the part I barely admit to myself, and I'm telling you now only because a man's account book ought to balance somewhere.

I stopped at the rail and I held the memory a moment longer than the job required. Just a moment. Sun through leaves. Figs dreaming. A girl's laughter with dirt under its fingernails. Nineteen years old and the world enormous and one afternoon enough to last a lifetime — no, more than enough, because look what it had done: it had lasted a lifetime, it had outlasted the house and the money and the cheating and the brother, and a dying man's last words about it had been *good, let it keep going.*

I have stolen courage and cowardice, baptisms and betrayals, the face of a mother seen from a crib, the exact color of a sky over a battlefield. I have never once stolen anything for myself. Not because I'm noble — I'm a thief, nobility is bad for margins — but because of what happened the one time I broke rule two, and because of a simpler arithmetic I learned young: a man who owns nothing can't be robbed, and a man who remembers nothing of others belongs entirely to himself.

That's the lie I tell myself, anyway. Belongs entirely to himself. Sounds very clean. Very professional. Like a room with the furniture covered in sheets.

The truth is I stood at that rail in the harbor wind holding a stranger's first love like a coal I couldn't put down, and I thought: *somebody once looked at me the way that boy looked at a girl selling figs. Somebody, somewhere, sometime. And I wouldn't know it if I walked past them tomorrow. I wouldn't know it if they died in front of me.*

Then I put the coal away, sealed it in wax and lead the way the guild taught us, and went to sell it, because rent in Vell's Landing is due whether your soul is furnished or not.

* * *

The buyer met me in the back room of the Drowned Bell, which smells permanently of lamp oil and bad decisions. He was a collector's agent — grey coat, grey gloves, grey eyes that catalogued you while shaking your hand. I never learn their names. Names make people real, and real people are hard to cheat and harder to forget.

"One first love," I said, setting the lead seal on the table. "Merchant class, elderly, extracted clean. High clarity. Emotional residue intact."

The grey man weighed the seal in his palm like a plum. "Provenance?"

"Dying donor, voluntary sale. Estate-sanctioned." Which was true, more or less, if you squinted at the factor's paperwork the way the law permits squinting.

He cracked the seal, pressed the memory-wax to a blank slate, and read it the way collectors do — lips moving, eyes distant, borrowing the afternoon for a moment the way you try on a coat. I watched his face do something complicated. Collectors always look surprised the first time. They buy happiness secondhand and are astonished to find it fits.

"Adequate," he said, which in collector language means *lovely*, and counted out my fee.

I was three steps from the door when he spoke again.

"The factor mentioned," he said, carefully casual, the way people are casual when they've rehearsed, "that the donor experienced something unusual during extraction. A resonance, he called it. The memory *answered* something. Or something answered the memory."

I turned around slowly. "Memories don't answer. They're not questions."

"No," the grey man agreed. "They're not." He folded the slate into a case lined with velvet, and for just a moment his grey eyes came up and looked at me — really looked, the way you look at a lock you're deciding whether to pick. "But prayers are. And lately, in this valley, Master Almeida, some few of them have been getting replies."

The lamp oil smell seemed suddenly very loud. Outside, the harbor bells rang the ninth hour, and somewhere below us the black water moved against the pilings, patient as everything else in Vell's Landing.

"I wouldn't know anything about prayers," I said. "I'm a thief with better manners."

"Of course," said the grey man, and smiled, and let me go.

I walked home the long way, along the salt line road, where the white seam of it runs pale under the moon like a scar the world decided to keep. I told myself the crawl up my spine was the sea wind. I told myself a lot of things that night.

Because here's what I hadn't put in my report, hadn't put anywhere, hadn't even put into words: when I lifted that afternoon out of Odell Vasse — in the last instant, in the space between the garden and the dark — something on the far side of the memory had stirred. Something had turned toward me the way a sleeper turns toward a candle.

And it had said, in a voice like ash settling:

*At last. At last, a carrier.*
