## 2. Salt Lines

There are three laws in the Salt Valley, and every thief worth the name can recite them drunk, bleeding, and upside down.

One: nothing that lives past the salt lines can be compelled by anything that doesn't. Two: no god, demon, spirit, saint, or well-meaning grandmother's curse may cross a salt line. Three: what happens inside the valley stays inside the valley — not because of any enchantment, but because nobody outside cares enough to come looking.

I learned them from a woman named Corvo who ran a memory-house on the Tallow Steps when I was fourteen. She'd rap my knuckles with her pipe between recitations. "Say it again, boy. What keeps you safe?"

"Salt," I'd say, sucking blood off my thumb.

"Salt. Not your wits. Not your luck. Salt. The day you start believing it's your wits is the day some old thing eats you through the eye." Then she'd pour herself another cup of something that smelled like turpentine and regret, and look at me with an expression I'd spend twenty years learning to name. Pity, maybe. Or the particular tenderness people feel for lambs they're fattening.

Corvo's gone now. Her house too. But the salt stayed, and so did I, and if you're wondering why a man who can reach into your skull and lift out your wedding day like picking a coin from a fountain chooses to live in a wind-scoured valley where the wine is bad and the gods are deaf — well. That's the whole answer. The valley is the one place in the world where magic cannot follow. Not won't. *Cannot.* The salt in the earth does something to the air, or the air does something to the salt, and whatever the mechanism is, the result is this: inside the lines, there is no power but the kind men carry in their hands.

Which makes it paradise for thieves. A lockpick works the same here as anywhere. A knife too. And a memory-thief — a *taster*, in the trade — works better, because outside the valley, half my clients would be sorcerers who'd notice the withdrawal, and the other half would be things wearing client-shaped skins.

Inside the lines, everyone's just meat. Meat with secrets. Meat willing to pay.

* * *

The client came to me on a Tuesday, which should have been my first warning. Nothing good has ever happened to me on a Tuesday.

He was waiting at the Drowned Bell when I came down for breakfast, sitting in the corner booth with his back to the wall and a cup of tea he hadn't touched. That alone marked him as an outsider — locals drink the Bell's tea the way you'd drink medicine, quickly and while holding your nose. He wore traveling clothes that were trying very hard to look poor and not quite managing it. Good wool, dyed down. Boots resoled but expensive underneath. Hands soft except for one callus, high on the right middle finger, the kind you get from years of holding a stylus.

A clerk, then. Or a priest. In my experience the two professions differ mainly in how they handle their ledgers.

"Sebastian Almeida?" His voice was careful, pitched low. Valley accent worn like a coat over something else.

"Depends who's asking and what they're asking for."

"Oren Vasek." He didn't offer a hand. "I'm told you take memories out."

"I'm told lots of things. Half of them are even true."

His jaw tightened. He was younger than I'd first thought — thirty, maybe, with the kind of face that aged early from indoor living. There were shadows under his eyes deep enough to store valuables in. "I need something taken. Tonight, if possible."

"Everything's possible for coin. What's the memory?"

He flinched. They always flinch. People think they want the thing gone until someone actually offers to take it, and then they discover the horrible arithmetic of it: that the worst night of your life is still *yours*, and selling it means some stranger will hold it, wear it, know it better than anyone alive.

"It's not — " He stopped. Started again. "It's not what you think. It's not shame."

"They never are. Until they are."

"It's a prayer," he said.

I looked at him properly then. Up close, in the grey light coming through the Bell's salt-fogged windows, he had the look of a man who hadn't slept in a week and had spent most of it arguing with himself and losing.

"I don't steal prayers," I said. "Prayers are cheap. Anyone can have one. You want it gone, stop saying it."

"You misunderstand." He leaned forward, and now I could smell him — ink and candle smoke and under it something faintly wrong, like a room where a fire had been put out badly. "I don't want to stop saying it. I want to stop *remembering* it. There's a difference." His hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the wood might buckle. "Three months ago I prayed. Properly. With intent. I'm a lay brother of the Order of the Closed Door — was, rather; I've since been relieved of my duties — and I know better than anyone breathing that the old gods are dead, burned, ash, gone. We made certain of it ourselves, generations back. So when I prayed, I prayed to nothing. To the dark. As an experiment. As blasphemy, if you like. Call it what you want."

"And?"

"And it was quiet for three months." His voice dropped further. The Bell was mostly empty — dockhands sleeping off night shifts, the barkeep pretending not to listen, which is the barkeep's whole art — but he spoke anyway like a man afraid of walls. "Then, nine days ago, it wasn't."

"Wasn't quiet."

"Wasn't *empty*." He swallowed. "Every night since, when I say the prayer, something says it back. Word for word. Before I finish. It knows the ending. Brother Cade says — said — that this is madness, that I invented it, that guilt does strange things to the faithful. Maybe he's right. Probably he's right. But I can't unhear it, Almeida, and I can't keep hearing it, and the Order doesn't treat mad brothers kindly. They burn them. Quietly, in the cellar, with paperwork afterward." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all year. "So. Take it. Take the prayer out of my head. Whatever it is — madness, memory, sin — I want it *out*."

Now, I want you to understand something about me. I am not brave. Bravery is for people with something to protect, and I've spent forty years making sure I'm not one of them. I am also not stupid, which is rarer and more useful. And everything in me that wasn't stupid was standing up, putting on its coat, and walking toward the door.

But he put a purse on the table. Heavy. The clink of it had that specific weight that means silver, not copper, and enough of either to buy a year of not asking questions.

And I told myself the oldest lie in my trade: *it's just work.*

* * *

We did it that night, in my rooms above the chandlery, because I don't work in places with witnesses. My method isn't elegant. No incense, no chanting, none of the theater the valley's other tasters use to justify triple rates. You sit across from the client, skin to skin — palm to palm, usually, though I once did a job through a handshake and wouldn't recommend it — and you find the thread.

Everyone's mind has threads. Bright ones, dull ones, knotted ones. Most tasters grope around like drunks in a cellar. I learned from Corvo to go in gently, to read the weave before pulling anything, because a memory torn out wrong takes pieces of the person with it. You can leave a man who can't remember his mother's face. You can leave a woman who flinches at her own name. The trade calls these *frays*. I call them reasons I charge what I charge and sleep with the window shut.

Vasek's mind was a disaster. Lay brother or not, his head was a cathedral mid-demolition — scaffolding everywhere, rubble, half-burned doctrine stacked against the walls. I picked through it carefully, following the wrongness, the cold spot, the place where the weave went strange.

I found it near the center. It didn't feel like the others.

Here's what memories usually feel like, from the inside: warm, heavy, human. A first kiss tastes like honey and embarrassment. A funeral tastes like wet wool. Even the terrible ones are *textured*, rooted in a body somewhere, in weather, in hunger, in the small animal business of being alive.

This was not textured. This was smooth all the way through, like river stone, like the inside of a bell. And it was *loud* — not in volume but in the way a struck bell is loud, humming at a pitch I felt in my teeth. A prayer, yes. I could see it laid out in his mind, words in an old liturgical cadence, addressed to nothing, to the dark, to the space where a god used to be.

Except it wasn't addressed to nothing anymore. That was the thing I couldn't unsee, crouched there in the wreckage of Oren Vasek's faith. The prayer had a shape now. An address. Somewhere far away — farther than the valley, farther than anything — something had turned toward those words the way a man turns toward a knock at a door he'd forgotten he owned.

I should have left it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when thieves die. I touched it, I understood what it was, and every instinct Corvo had beaten into my knuckles said *leave it, boy, leave it, some doors are doors for a reason.*

But the purse was on my table. And worse — worse — the thief in me looked at that smooth, ringing, impossible thing and thought: *nobody has ever stolen one of these before.*

You can't understand unless you've wanted something your whole life without knowing its name. Every taster knows the feeling. We handle the best of humanity all day — loves, triumphs, moments of grace — and we give them all away, because keeping them is how you fray. The rule is absolute: taste everything, keep nothing. And every taster, in the dark, in the honest hour, wonders what it would be like to keep just one. Just one beautiful thing, forever, that was never ours.

This was the first memory I'd ever found that wasn't human. And God forgive me — whichever God is still taking deliveries — I wanted it.

I pulled.

It came out clean. Too clean. Memories resist; they cling; they come away with little tearing sounds you feel more than hear. This one slid free like a key from oiled lock, like it had been *waiting* to be held by someone else, and the moment it settled into me — because that's the dirty secret of the trade, the memory lodges in the thief until delivery, a passenger riding behind your eyes — the moment it settled, the hum changed pitch.

And the room got colder.

Not draft-cold. Directional cold, if such a thing can be, pouring off the memory itself like breath off a lake in winter. Vasek shivered and wrapped his arms around himself and said, "Is it done? Is it out?" and I said yes, because it was, and because my voice seemed to be working only on technicalities.

I walked him to the door. He paid me — counted the silver into my palm with shaking hands, and I noticed he wouldn't touch my skin again, wouldn't shake on it, and I thought: smart man, though not smart enough by about four hours. At the threshold he stopped.

"It'll be quiet now," he said. Not a question. A man begging confirmation of his own cure.

"Quiet as the grave," I said, which was meant to be reassuring.

He nodded, thanked me in the flat voice of the recently hollowed, and went down the stairs into the fog. I watched him from the window until the dark took him, and I remember thinking he walked like a man who'd set down something heavy and couldn't yet believe his shoulders.

Then I went back inside, sat on my bed, and made the second-worst decision of my life, which was to examine the merchandise.

* * *

A delivered memory fades fast. By morning it's a smudge, a rumor, a weight you can't locate. That's the design of it — Corvo called it the mercy of the trade. So I sat there in my cold room with this thing humming behind my eyes, fully intending to let it dissolve, and I told myself I was just checking the edges, confirming clean extraction, professional diligence.

What I was doing was listening.

Because it was still ringing. Delivered memories don't ring. And as I paid attention to it — I'll say attention, though it felt more like the memory paying attention to *me* — the words of the prayer unfolded themselves, slow, like a hand opening. Old liturgy. Words for a god whose name had been struck from every book, addressed to a door that had been sealed for generations, spoken by a man who believed in nothing, answered by —

By something that knew the ending.

That was the detail I kept circling. Vasek had said it plainly and I hadn't understood it until now, holding the prayer myself: *something says it back, word for word, before I finish.* A prayer that gets answered before it's finished isn't a coincidence. Coincidence doesn't know your liturgy. Madness doesn't know your liturgy. Only one thing knows a prayer's ending, and that's the thing the prayer was written for.

I sat very still on my bed in my cold room above the chandlery, in the Salt Valley, past the salt lines, where magic cannot follow, cannot enter, cannot exist — and behind my eyes, in a memory I had stolen from a frightened man, something that was not a memory turned over.

Like a sleeper hearing footsteps.

The cold spread. Down my spine, out along my arms, pooling in my palms — the same palms, I noted distantly, that had touched Vasek's, that had *pulled*, that had carried this thing across the valley's sacred, inviolable geography like a courier carrying plague through a quarantine. Because that was the thought, wasn't it. The thought that arrived all at once, fully formed, wearing my own voice:

*Memories pass the salt lines. Thieves carry them in every day. If a prayer could hitch a ride on a memory — if an answer could lodge itself in a stolen thing and be smuggled past the one border in the world built to keep it out —*

Then the law was still true. Magic cannot follow.

It doesn't have to follow. It only has to be *invited*. Carried. Paid for, in silver, by an idiot named Sebastian Almeida who should have thrown the purse in the harbor and moved two valleys over.

Behind my eyes, the prayer finished itself. All of it, beginning to end, the ending arriving a half-breath before I could have recited it — and in the half-breath after, in the silence where Vasek had heard nothing for three months and then something for nine days, I heard it too.

Not words. Not yet. Just an attention, vast and patient and impossibly tired, settling on me the way winter settles on a valley. The way a debt settles.

Somewhere out beyond the salt, beyond the maps, something that had been waiting a very long time had just been handed, by me, personally, the first foothold it had gotten in centuries.

And it knew my name.

I don't mean that figuratively. I mean that in the last moment before I blew out my candle and sat in the dark telling myself I was drunk, tired, and rich, the attention behind my eyes gathered itself and pronounced — clearly, fondly, in the tone of a creditor finding a debtor who'd changed cities —

*Sebastian.*

The rules have changed. I want that written down too, wherever the record goes. Three laws in the Salt Valley, and I broke the only one that mattered without ever crossing a line: I let the magic in myself, and it paid me for the privilege.

I didn't sleep. Around dawn, the fog off the harbor thickened until it pressed white against my windows, and beneath the foghorn and the gulls and the Drowned Bell's early drunks singing three streets over, I heard — or didn't hear, I genuinely could not swear to it — a second voice underneath the city's noise. Low. Waiting. Asking, in the wordless way of large patient things, for the rest of its name.
