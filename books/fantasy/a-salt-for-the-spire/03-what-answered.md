## 3. What Answered

I didn't sleep that night, which was nothing new. What was new was the reason.

The prayer-memory sat in my head like a swallowed coal. I'd carried stolen memories for eleven years and I knew all their textures — grief is wet wool, joy is warm bread, terror is a fishhook you can't stop tonguing. This was none of those. This was *warm*. Not comfortable-warm. Hearth-that-shouldn't-be-lit warm, in a house everyone swore was empty.

I lay on my cot above the counting-house I rented a room from — Marda kept the ledgers downstairs and her opinions everywhere else — and I stared at the ceiling beams and listened to Vell's Landing do its night business: gulls arguing over fish guts, a drunk singing three verses of one song, the harbor bell counting the tide. Normal sounds. Salt Valley sounds. The whole point of this valley was that nothing here could reach into your skull and rearrange it.

So why did my skull feel rearranged?

"Talk to me," I said to the ceiling, because I'm the kind of fool who talks to ceilings. "Come on. Whatever you are."

Nothing. Of course nothing. Things that answer prayers don't take requests from thieves.

I got up, dressed, and went down the back stairs into the alley where the air tasted of brine and old fish, which is to say home. I told myself I was going for a walk. I told myself a lot of things that night, and every one of them was a lie with good posture.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about stealing memories: you never steal just the memory. You steal the shape the memory left behind. Take a man's first love and you get not just the girl's face but the way his chest used to feel at the sight of her, the specific ache of being twenty and certain. The merchant's prayer had come with its shape too, and the shape was *waiting*. Decades of it. A man kneeling at a shrine no bigger than a cupped pair of hands, whispering the same words until they wore smooth as sea glass, and getting nothing, nothing, nothing — and praying anyway, because what else does faith have left when the answers stop?

That kind of patience isn't devotion. It's hunger wearing devotion's coat.

I walked the long way along the seawall, past the salt lines' white boundary stones glowing faintly under the moon like teeth in a dark mouth. Every thief in the valley knows those stones. We kiss them, some of us, quietly, the way sailors kiss the deck after a storm. Past them, magic works. Inside them, it doesn't. That's the covenant, older than the Church, older than the maps: the gods' power stops at the salt, and so everything that wants to be free of the gods comes here. Thieves. Debtors. Heretics. Me.

I'd crossed those stones twice in my life, both times for jobs, both times hating every minute outside. Magic has a smell, did you know that? Like copper and cut grass and something almost sweet underneath, and it gets into your lungs and stays there for days. I hated it because it made me feel *watched*.

Tonight, inside the salt, inside the safest ground in the world, I felt watched again.

"You're not supposed to exist," I said to the night. "Whatever answered him. The whole point of this place—"

*Thief.*

One word. It arrived the way cold water arrives through a boot seam — sudden, total, already everywhere before you notice the hole. I stopped walking. My hands did something clever without my permission; I found myself holding the little knife I keep in my sleeve, though there was nothing in the alley to stab except moonlight.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. You're real. Wonderful. Great night for it."

*You carry me,* the voice said. It wasn't loud. Loud would have been easier, somehow. It was close, the way your own thoughts are close, except my thoughts don't taste of ash and honey. *Not all of me. A piece. The piece he gave.*

"The merchant." I started walking again, fast, because standing still felt like agreeing to something. "He paid me to take it. That's theft, friend, not invitation. Whatever contract you think exists between us, it doesn't."

*Names make contracts.* A pause, and then something that might have been amusement, if amusement could be ancient and sad. *You know this better than most. You sell names every day.*

"I sell memories. Different trade."

*Is it?*

Gods, apparently, are insufferable conversationalists.

I cut inland, away from the water, toward the maze of streets behind the chandlers' row where I could lose anyone — or anything — in the crowd of shuttered windows and sleeping cats. The voice came with me, patient as tide. And here's the thing I'll admit now, in whatever privacy these pages afford: under the fear, under the animal part of me screaming to find a priest or a boat or a very deep hole, there was another part. The part that had lifted its first purse at nine years old and felt not shame but *recognition*. The part that sees any lock as a question addressed personally to me.

A god was talking to me. To *me*, Sebastian Almeida, who once got thrown out of a temple in Corvane for asking too precisely how the offering box locked. Some men would feel chosen. I felt like a mark. But even a mark wants to know the size of the con.

"What do you want?" I said finally.

*I want the rest of my name.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin. "You're joking. You're an actual god and you've lost your name?"

*A god is a name,* it said, and for the first time the voice bent, strained, like a rope taking weight. *The name is the power and the power is the name. They took the country and they took the name and they left me — this. A fragment in a dying man's mouth, waiting fifty years for someone to carry it across the salt.*

"They?"

*The ones with the closed door.* Ash and honey and now, underneath, iron. *You wear their symbol without knowing it. Look at your coin.*

I looked. Everyone looks at their coin; it's a habit of the poor. The silver bit of Vell's Landing, minted by the Trade Council, stamped with the old seal of the Church of the Closed Door: a door, shut, with a salt line drawn across the threshold. I'd bitten that coin a thousand times. I'd never once wondered why a currency needed a church's sigil on it, or why the door was drawn with such care, such *finality*, the lintel heavy as a lid on a coffin.

"Everyone carries it," I said slowly. "It's just money."

*It is a leash,* said Ashen-Who-Waits, *and every soul in this valley wears it, and most of them were born into the collar and call it a necklace.* The voice softened, or did the thing gods do instead of softening, which is worse. *But you. You were not born to the collar, thief. You were brought to it.*

My feet stopped working. That's the only way I can describe it — the street kept going and my legs declined to participate. Brought to it. What kind of thing was that to say? I was born in Corvane, in the tanners' quarter, or — I was born — I opened my mouth to say where I was born and found, to my genuine astonishment, that I had to reach for it. Corvane. Tanners' quarter. Mother a—

A blank. A smooth, pale blank, like a wall where a window should be.

Everybody has holes in their childhood. That's normal. That's what people say. But I have never had holes. Memory-work sharpens your own memory like a blade on leather; I can tell you the smell of the alley where I slept at six years old, the exact weight of the first purse I stole, the color of the sky the day I walked across the salt line the first time. I am a man made entirely of remembered things. And there was a wall where my mother's face should be, and I had never noticed the wall, because who notices a wall that's always been there?

"You did something," I whispered. "Just now. You touched something in me."

*I touched nothing,* the god said, and I believed it, which frightened me more than anything else that night. *I only spoke near it. The wall is yours. Or rather — it was put there for you, long ago, by hands I cannot see from inside this crumb of myself. But I know sealed things, thief. I am a sealed thing. And I know the masonry when I hear it.*

I stood in the empty street with my knife still out like an idiot and felt the ground tilt under assumptions I'd been standing on for thirty years. Then I did what I always do when the world breaks: I made it about money.

"Say I believe you," I said. "Say you're a god, a real one, cracked into pieces and smuggled around in other people's heads. Say I'm carrying your fragment. What's in it for me? Because I've met powerful beings before — magistrates, guild-masters, one archmage with a gambling problem — and every single one of them wanted something, and every single one of them lied about what."

Silence. Long enough that I thought maybe I'd offended it into leaving, which had been perhaps forty percent of my goal.

*Honesty, then,* it said at last. *I like you better for the question. Here is honesty: I want to be whole. I want my name back, all of it, syllable by syllable, and I cannot gather it alone. Fragments of me sleep in memories scattered through this valley — prayers, mostly, offered by my faithful in the last years before the erasure, carried out by refugees who did not know what they carried. You steal memories, Sebastian Almeida. You are the only man alive whose trade is exactly what I need.*

"There it is," I said. "There's the ask."

*And what do you want?* The voice curled around the question like smoke. *Do not say coin. A man who steals first loves for strangers' coin and keeps none of them — I have lived in your head for hours now, thief. Coin is not what empties you.*

"Don't—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Don't psychoanalyze me. You're a lodger, not a confessor."

*You keep nothing,* it went on, gentle as a blade going in. *Every year you give away what you take, and every year you grow lighter and lighter, and you call it professionalism. But a man does not hollow himself out for discipline. He does it because somewhere, someone emptied him first, and he cannot bear to be the only full thing in a world of thieves.*

The alley swam. I put a hand against a wall — chandler's, beeswax smell — and breathed until the street agreed to hold still.

"I want my mother's face," I heard myself say. Quietly. To the wall, mostly. "If you know anything about walls. If you can crack one. That's my price. Not coin. Not glory. If we do this — if I go digging up prayer-fragments for a broken god — you help me find what was taken out of me. Deal?"

Another silence. When the voice came back, it had changed register — lower, careful, the way a man speaks when he's just realized the map he's holding leads somewhere he's afraid of.

*You bargain with a god for your own self. Do you understand what you may find behind that wall?*

"I'm a thief. I open things knowing they might bite. It's the whole job."

*Then hear the rest, and understand the size of the thing:* the voice gathered itself, and for a moment the whole alley smelled of ash and honey, and the moonlight seemed to lean in. *My name is not one word but many, and the last syllables were hidden where the Church would never burn — inside the door itself, in the country they erased. To make me whole, the fragments must be brought together, and the gathering ends at the door. Thirteen turns of the moon, thief. The door opens on the thirteenth turn, as it has opened since the sealing, and this time the Order means to be there with fire enough to end it forever. And this time, they will follow a trail of stolen prayers — straight to the man who carries me.*

"So I'm either your courier or your bait."

*You are my vessel,* said Ashen-Who-Waits, *and I am sorry for it, and I will spend what I have of myself to protect you. That is also honesty. It is all I have left to offer.*

I stood there a long time. Gulls. Drunk. Harbor bell. The world insisting on being ordinary while my entire life quietly caught fire.

Then I sheathed the knife, because a man can't negotiate with steel in his hand, and said the stupidest sentence of my career:

"Tell me where the next fragment is."

The voice that answered was smiling. I could hear it smile, and I want that noted: I *knew* it was a trap, knew it the way you know rain coming, and I walked into it anyway, because the wall in my head had a mother behind it, and no lock ever built could keep me out of my own life.

*South of here,* said the god. *A widow in Brackish Row prays nightly to a saint she thinks is me. She carries my third syllable in a memory of her wedding day.*

Of course. Of course it was a wedding. I closed my eyes and saw, unbidden, the merchant's prayer-shrine, small as cupped hands, and understood that I had just signed something, and that names make contracts, and that mine was now written somewhere I couldn't read.

"One condition," I said. "Nobody dies for this. Not for a syllable. Swear it."

The pause before the answer lasted exactly one heartbeat too long.

*I swear what I can,* said Ashen-Who-Waits.

Which, from a god, is the most frightening sentence I have ever heard.

I went home. I didn't sleep. And two streets over, in a room I couldn't see, a woman knelt by her bed and began, without knowing why, to pray — and this time, when something answered, it answered in my voice.
