## 5. A Thief, a Nun, a Debt

I'd been followed for three days before I decided to let her catch me.

That's not bravado. That's arithmetic. Inquisitor Ravelle of the Church of the Closed Door walked like a woman counting steps, and she counted mine the way I counted a mark's purse — patiently, professionally, with the quiet certainty that sooner or later everything comes out in the wash. She'd appeared at the Gilded Eel two nights after the prayer answered itself inside my skull. She'd asked the barkeep about me without asking about me, which is a skill I usually charge people to teach. And this morning, when I took my usual route through the fish market and doubled back through Tanner's Row, she didn't take the bait.

She just kept walking beside me, three paces back, letting me know she knew that I knew.

So on the fourth day I stopped dodging. I bought two cups of bitterleaf tea from the old woman at the corner of Saltcutter Street, and I sat down on the low wall outside the ropewalk with both cups steaming, and I waited.

She came around the corner four minutes later, saw me sitting there, and had the grace to look mildly annoyed at herself for being predictable. Up close she was younger than her reputation — mid-thirties, maybe, with the kind of face that had been pretty once and had since been sanded down by doctrine into something more useful. Her grey habit was travel-worn at the hem. Her boots were good. Inquisitors always have good boots; it's the first thing the Order spends money on, because you can't kick down doors in bad ones.

"Inquisitor," I said, and held up the second cup. "Tea? It's terrible. The water here tastes like the salt it's named for."

"I know what you are, Sebastian Almeida."

"Everyone in Vell's Landing knows what I am. It's half my advertising." I set the cup on the wall between us anyway, an offering she could ignore with dignity. "The other half is discretion."

"You stole a memory from Doss Meric, salt-factor, eleven days ago. Payment rendered: forty crowns and forgiveness of a debt." She recited it flatly, like scripture. "What you actually took was not what he believed he was selling."

I kept my face pleasant and my heart very still. "Memory's a strange commodity, Sister. Men rarely know what they're carrying until someone weighs it."

"What he carried was a prayer." She sat down on the wall after all, but she didn't touch the tea. She looked at it the way you look at a snake that might be somebody's pet. "An old one. Older than the Church. Older than the erasure. He inherited it from his grandmother, who inherited it from hers, all the way back to a woman who stood at the border of a country that no longer exists and begged the sky for rain." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were the color of wet slate. "And something answered him through you."

The tea steam curled between us. Somewhere behind the ropewalk, men were singing one of those work songs that's mostly swearing set to rhythm.

"Here's my problem, Almeida." She said it conversationally, which somehow made it worse. "By the canon of the Order, you are now a vessel. Contaminated. The protocol is arrest, interrogation, and burning — in that order, though the Order is flexible about sequence when the vessel is uncooperative." She picked up the tea at last, examined it, and drank. Her nose wrinkled. "You were right. It's terrible."

"And yet you're drinking it."

"I'm establishing rapport. It's in the manual." She set the cup down. "Here's my other problem. The zealot wing arrived in the Landing yesterday. Brother Cade and nine of his faithful. They don't follow protocol. Protocol involves questions. Cade involves fire."

I let a slow breath out through my nose. "And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I watched Cade work in Harrow Fen. There was a vessel there — a girl of nineteen who'd swallowed a hymn fragment whole, didn't even know what it was, sang it at her mother's funeral because her mother used to sing it. Cade burned the house with the mother's body still in it, then burned the girl, then burned the neighbor who objected." Ravelle's voice never changed register. That was the frightening part. Every horror came out at the same temperature as the tea. "He called it mercy. He believes it. That's what makes him dangerous. Doubt makes you hesitate; faith doesn't."

"So walk away. You're an Inquisitor. You outrank him or you don't, but either way you could be home by winter drinking decent tea."

"I could." She turned on the wall to face me fully, and for the first time I saw the thing under the slate — not doubt exactly, but its foundation. A crack where the light got in. "Eleven days ago something answered a prayer in the Salt Valley. Do you understand what that means, thief? Nothing answers in the valley. Nothing *can*. We built the lines ourselves. We spent three generations building them. If a god can reach into the one place we made godless—" she shook her head, "—then either our walls are failing, or they were never as high as we told ourselves. Either way, the door those prayers point toward is going to open. On the thirteenth turn of the moon. And the only man alive who knows where fragments of the way there are hidden is sitting on a wall drinking terrible tea."

"I don't know where any door is."

"You do. You just don't know that you know." She leaned forward. "Every memory you've ever stolen is still in your head, Almeida. I've read the accounts of your kind. Thieves tell themselves they pour the memories out, sell them clean, keep nothing. But that's a lie thieves tell clients. Nothing pours out clean. Residue remains. Echoes. And somewhere in twenty years of echoes is a map no one alive has drawn — the road to a country the Church erased so thoroughly that even the maps forgot it." She smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman holding a knife she hoped she wouldn't use. "Help me find it before Cade does. Or die as a vessel. Those are the options. I'm sorry they're not better; I didn't write them."

* * *

We talked for another hour, and I want to be honest about how it went, because this is the part of the story where I'm supposed to say I weighed noble things against base ones and chose with a heavy heart. What I actually did was haggle.

"If I help you," I said, "and we find this door, and your Church decides it wants it sealed — I walk free. Full pardon. My name scrubbed from every ledger the Order keeps, and the Order keeps ledgers on everyone, including you, Sister, don't make that face."

"Granted."

"And if Cade burns anyone else while we're working — anyone — I get to walk away and you cover my exit."

"That's not—"

"That's the price. I've seen what his mercy looks like from your description. I sell memories, Inquisitor. I know exactly what people will pay to forget. Nobody pays to forget a thing they were glad happened."

She studied me for a long moment. "You're more frightened than you're letting on."

"I'm a professional. Fear is overhead."

"And the voice?" she asked, quietly. "Ashen-Who-Waits. Does it talk to you still?"

I hadn't told her about the voice. I was certain I hadn't told her about the voice. I looked at her, and she looked back with the patience of a woman who interrogates liars for a living, and I understood that she hadn't needed me to tell her. The Order had records. The Order had known what Doss Meric carried before Meric's grandfather was born.

"It talks," I said finally. "It asks for its name. Like a man locked out of his own house asking a stranger to read the address off the door."

"And what do you say?"

"I say I'm a thief, not a locksmith."

Ravelle nodded slowly. "When I was novitiate," she said, "they taught us the erasure as a mercy. The old gods' power rotted everything — kings, crops, children. Sealing their country away saved the world. I believed it the way you believe stone is hard." She stood, brushing salt dust off her habit. "But I've started reading the pre-erasure archives. The ones with the burn marks on them. And I've begun to wonder whether we sealed a poison, or buried a patient." She looked down at me. "Either way, thief, the answer's behind that door, and I would rather it be me standing there than Cade. Come to the chapel of Saint Verren at dusk. Bring nothing you'd mourn losing."

"Why?"

"Because Cade knows about you too. And unlike me, he isn't interested in haggling."

* * *

I went to the chapel, because the alternative was staying home and waiting to see which came through my door first: a nun with a bargain or a zealot with fire. I've survived twenty years in Vell's Landing by never waiting to find out what's on the other side of a door. You pick the door yourself, or the door picks you, and the second option is how thieves die.

Saint Verren's was the smallest chapel in the Landing, squeezed between a chandler's and a pawnshop, smelling of tallow and old incense. Ravelle was there alone, kneeling before an altar with a closed door carved into it — the Church's one symbol, the shut gate, the silence of heaven. She rose when I entered, and I noticed she'd laid out a map on the altar rail. An old one. Hand-drawn, faded, with coastlines I didn't recognize and blank space where a country should have been.

Not blank, though. Not quite. Someone had written over the emptiness in tiny, furious script, again and again, layer upon layer, until the paper there had gone dark with ink.

"What is that?"

"A map of the world," she said, "as the Church insists the world is. And underneath the insistence—" she peeled back a corner of the top sheet, glued down with wax, "—the truth someone tried to hide."

Beneath was another map. Older. And on it, where the blankness should have been, there were mountains. Rivers. A spire, drawn with obsessive care, at the center of everything.

"The Cartographer's Guild has a daughter," Ravelle said. "Ilsa Venn. Her father drew charts of the vanished country before the erasure. The Order burned most of them. Not all." She rolled the map closed. "Tomorrow we collect her. Tonight—" she handed me a small tin, "—you sleep with this under your tongue. It dulls the voice. Buys you a night without it asking for its name."

I took the tin. I shouldn't have. Taking things from Inquisitors is how thieves end up in ledgers.

"One condition," I said.

"You're in no position for conditions."

"Humor me. When this is done — whatever's done — I want to remember choosing this. Whatever happens to the rest of me, whatever the Order takes or burns or files away, the choice stays mine." I shrugged, trying to make it light. "A thief's retirement plan."

Ravelle looked at me for a long time, and something moved behind the slate of her eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. One professional acknowledging another's terms.

"Noted," she said. "In the only ledger that matters."

Outside, the moon was rising over the salt flats, one turn brighter than it had any right to be, and in the back of my skull, muffled but patient, something old began — softly, hopefully — to hum.
