## 6. The Cartographer's Daughter

My father used to say that a map is a promise you make to a stranger. *Follow this line*, the map says, *and you will not die*. He made promises for a living, and he kept every one of them, which is more than can be said for the people who came to buy them.

He also said, more often and with more feeling, that a mapmaker's daughter inherits ink stains and debts in equal measure, and she should learn early which of the two washes out.

I was three days into deciding whether to sell the shop when they came for the charts.

* * *

The shop sat — sits; I haven't sold it yet, though God and the landlord both know why not — at the bottom of Rope Street in Vell's Landing, where the tide brings in everything the world wants to forget and the tide goes out having forgotten it. My father, Aldric Vane, had drawn maps there for forty years. Coastlines mostly, trade routes occasionally, and once — once, in a locked drawer beneath the floorboards, in a folio wrapped in oilcloth and bound with a strap he touched only when he thought I wasn't watching — a country that does not exist.

I grew up knowing it was there the way children grow up knowing about the loose board where the coin is hidden. Not what it was. Just that it mattered, and that it was mine eventually, and that my father's hands shook when he retied the strap.

He died in the winter, coughing his way out of the world one wet February night, and left me the shop, the debts, the ink stains, and the drawer. For four months I did not open the drawer. There is a kind of grief that makes a person superstitious, and mine had decided that if I never looked at the folio, some part of him would still be waiting to explain it.

Then the knock came at an hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest ones are working, and I opened the door on a woman in grey church wool with eyes like a ledger — everything recorded, nothing forgiven.

"You're Ilsa Vane," she said. Not a question. Inquisitors don't ask questions they've already paid to have answered.

"I'm closed," I said, which was true, and "I'm not interested," which was a guess, but a good one.

"Madam Vane." She produced a folded paper from her sleeve with the smooth economy of someone who has done this many times and been refused most of them. "I am Ravelle, of the Order of the Closed Door. I'm here about your father's work."

And there it is. That sentence. That exact sentence, which I have heard in one form or another since I was old enough to grind pigment: *your father's work*. Never my father. Never Aldric, who sang off-key and burned the fish and cried at the naming of ships. Always the work, as though the man were merely the apparatus by which the work had been manufactured, and now that the apparatus was broken, the Order would like to speak directly to the product.

I should have shut the door. Any sensible woman would have shut the door.

Instead I said, "Which work?" because apparently I wanted to know how much trouble I was in before I declined it.

* * *

Ravelle sat in my father's chair without asking, which told me something about her, and accepted tea without drinking it, which told me something else. She set the paper on the table between us and unfolded it, and I confess my breath went strange, because it was a chart. A copy of a chart — the ink too fresh, the vellum too clean — but a copy of *my* chart, or rather of my father's, down to the little anchor symbol he always hid in the cartouche like a signature he was afraid to sign.

It showed a coastline I had known since childhood as a bedtime story. Bays named in a language that isn't spoken anymore. Rivers with names like *Sorrow-Takes-All* and *Where-the-King-Laughs*. And inland, drawn in my father's careful hand, a spire.

"Where did you get this," I said.

"The Order has held copies of your family's charts since before you were born, Madam Vane. We hold copies of most things." She said it without pride, the way you'd report the weather. "What we do not hold is the original folio. Your father removed certain sheets from our archive twenty-two years ago. We would like them back."

Twenty-two years. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. Twenty-two years ago I was nine years old, and my father came home from a trip to the capital with salt-cracked lips and a look I'd never seen on him before or since, and he put something under the floorboards, and after that he never drew anything that wasn't a coastline or a trade route again. Forty years of mapmaking, and the last eighteen of them spent drawing shallows.

"He took back his own work," I said. "You mean he stole it."

"I mean," Ravelle said, with the patience of a woman explaining tides to a barnacle, "that the contents of that folio are the property of the Order of the Closed Door, commissioned by the Order, drawn under Order seal, and their removal was theft. That your father was permitted to live out his life in peace was a mercy extended in recognition of his service. The mercy does not extend past his death."

"And so you've come to shake down the orphan."

Something moved behind her eyes at that. Not guilt — I'd have bet the shop against guilt — but a flicker, like a candle in a draft. "I've come to offer you a choice," she said. "The Order has two ways of acquiring things it needs. I represent the slower one."

"And the faster one?"

"Burns things."

I looked at her for a long moment. She let me. That was the worst part, later, looking back — how she just sat there and let me look, as if she'd decided in advance that being seen clearly was a cost she could afford.

"What's in the folio," I said, "that the Order burns people over?"

She folded her hands. "A country."

"There is no country."

"No," she agreed. "There isn't. That was rather the point."

* * *

Here is what I knew, before that night, about the vanished land: nothing, officially. Unofficially — the way cartographers know things, through margins and errata and the drunken confessions of old sailors — I knew that every chart older than sixty years had a hole in it. Not blank space; blanks are honest. A *hole*. Space the eye slides off, space where the ink refuses to sit, space that shows up different depending on who's holding the page. My father called it the Slip. He'd say it the way other men say a prayer or a curse, depending on the day. Every serious mapmaker in the valley has heard of the Slip. None of them will tell you what's under it, partly because they don't know, and partly because the ones who found out stopped answering letters.

Here is what I learned that night, sitting across from a woman whose church had erased a nation and then filed the paperwork: my grandfather had drawn that country. His father before him. Four generations of Vanes had mapped its coasts, its rivers, its roads, its spires — and the last of them, my father, had helped the Order erase it, and had spent the rest of his life trying to un-help.

"They needed a survey," Ravelle said. "An accurate one. You cannot erase what you cannot define. Your father was young, brilliant, and ambitious, and the Order offered him the commission of ten lifetimes." Her voice didn't change at all. "He was very good at it. When he understood what the survey was *for*, he was already the best map of that country in existence. So he stole himself."

"And you let him."

"We watched him," she corrected. "There is a difference. It seemed sufficient. He was one man with a folio, and the door was sealed, and the seals were holding." She paused, and for the first time she looked less like a ledger and more like a woman reading one. "The seals have stopped holding."

I want to be able to say I asked the right question next. I want to say I demanded to know what was behind the door, what the Order was, what right any institution had to delete a country and everyone in it. What I actually said was:

"How much are you paying?"

Because the shop had debts. Because my father's funeral was still unpaid. Because resentment keeps a body warm but doesn't keep the roof on, and I was thirty-one years old and tired of being the custodian of a dead man's secrets instead of the author of my own living ones.

Ravelle named a figure. It was insulting. Then she named a second figure, and it was not.

"The folio," she said, "and your assistance in reading it. The originals contain marginalia we were never given. Surveyor's notes. Corrections. If your father spent eighteen years hiding corrections inside his own erasure—" and here she stopped, and chose her next words with visible care, "—then those corrections may be the only reliable guide in the world to a place that no longer exists."

"And if I say no?"

"Then Brother Cade comes tomorrow, and he does not name figures."

* * *

I opened the drawer that night after she left. Pried up the board my father had oiled every season for eighteen years, so that it would lift quietly, so that it would lift easily, so that — I understand this now, I did not let myself understand it then — so that his daughter would find it without difficulty when the time came.

The folio was exactly as I'd imagined and nothing like it. Oilcloth, strap, the smell of linseed and salt. Inside, eleven sheets. Six were the master surveys, beautiful enough to hang in a temple, precise enough to hang a man. Five were something else. They were drawn in a different hand — older, rougher, the lines confident where the surveys were careful — and they were annotated in my father's writing, dense as scripture, in the margins, between the lines, upside down along the borders. Dates. Distances. And over and over, in a shorthand I had to work an hour to crack, the same phrase:

*Door holds. Door holds. Door holds.*

Until the last sheet, where the phrase changed.

*Door thins. Thirteenth turn. If anyone finds this — the girl will know the key. Teach her the key.*

*The girl.* Me. Nine years old, asleep upstairs, while my father knelt on the floorboards below writing instructions for a rescue he couldn't attempt and a daughter he hoped would never need to read them.

I sat on the floor of my father's shop until the candles guttered, holding five sheets of a country that had been unwritten, and I was angry. I want to be clear about the anger, because it matters. I was not angry at the Order — well, I was angry at the Order, furiously, permanently, but that anger came second. First, hottest, closest: I was angry at *him*. Eighteen years. Eighteen years of coastlines and small talk and *never mind the old work, Ilsa, it's done, it's finished, help me with the shallows instead* — eighteen years of being handed the shallows when he was sitting on a continent. He'd decided what I was allowed to carry, and he'd decided wrong, and he'd died before I could tell him so.

That was the resentment. Clean, hot, useless.

And underneath it, the way bedrock sits under topsoil, something else. Because he *had* taught me the key. All of it, all those years, disguised as craft: how to read a coastline's memory, how to triangulate from nothing, how to find the true shape of a thing that lies about itself. He hadn't withheld the inheritance. He'd buried it in plain sight and trusted me to dig.

*The girl will know the key.*

I spread the five sheets across the drafting table and weighted their corners with ink pots, and I began, line by line, to translate my father's secret hand into a living map — not of a country that exists, because none does, but of a country that *insisted*.

* * *

They came back at dawn. Both of them — Ravelle, and a thief.

I'll say what I saw, because what I saw is what I got: a lean man with river-grey eyes and the particular stillness of someone who has practiced not being noticed until it became his whole personality. Sebastian Almeida, Ravelle called him, and said the word *vessel* the way you'd say *infected*. He looked at my charts the way starving men look through bakery windows, and he looked at me like I was a lock he hadn't yet found the shape of, and I decided in the first ten seconds that I did not trust him further than I could throw the drafting table.

He repaid the sentiment, I'm fairly sure, with interest.

But when I laid out the fifth sheet — the last one, the one with *door thins* scrawled across the border in my father's tightening hand — the mockery went out of his face all at once, like wind dropping, and he said, very quietly, "I've seen that spire," and Ravelle went white, and I understood that whatever I had inherited from my father was no longer a folio in a drawer.

It was a fuse.

I had spent four months resenting a dead man for leaving me ink stains and debts. It took one dawn and two strangers to teach me the third thing he'd actually left me: the only map in the world to a door that was opening, and a reason, at long last, to draw it.

"Pack what you need," Ravelle said. "We leave within the day."

I looked at the shop — at forty years of my family's promises to strangers, drying in racks, stacked in drawers, worth nothing to anyone but me — and I blew out the lamps, and I locked the door behind me, and I did not look back.

Mostly.
