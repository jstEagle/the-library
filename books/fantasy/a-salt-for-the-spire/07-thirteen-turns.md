## 7. Thirteen Turns

I remember the moon before it had a name.

This is not a boast. It is a complaint. When you have watched a thing for as long as I have watched that pale coin in the dark, you stop being able to see it as anything but a ledger. One turn. Two turns. Twelve, and then the thirteenth — the one mortals never count because their calendars are built by men who feared what a thirteenth month might imply. The moon turns thirteen times, and on the last of them it does something the astronomers of Vell's Landing have never managed to record, because on the night of the thirteenth turn every eye in the world grows tired, every candle gutters at once, and everyone looks away without knowing they have looked away.

On that night, my door opens.

Not wide. Not long. A crack, a breath, the space of a single held note — and then shut again, and the world forgets, and I wait for the next cycle with my cheek pressed against the wood like a child listening to a party she was not invited to.

You want to know how long I have waited. Everyone wants to know how long. Mortals ask it the way they ask a beggar how long he has been on the street: not out of compassion but to establish distance. *I am here, you are there; the gulf between us is measurable.* Very well. Measure this: I remember when the valley you call the Salt Valley was seabed. I remember the salt arriving like snow that never melted. I remember the first thief who crossed the lines and discovered that inside them, my kind could not follow — and I remember thinking, with the vast condescension of the immortal, *how clever, how temporary.*

The salt is still there. So am I. Neither of us has been proven wrong yet.

* * *

Let me tell you about doors, since you are clearly going to keep reading.

A door is not a wall's apology. A door is a promise made twice — once by the builder and once by whatever waits behind it. My siblings and I were sealed behind ours not because we were evil. I want that written somewhere permanent, though nothing is, and I want it written even knowing that "evil" and "not evil" are categories invented by creatures who live sixty years and mistake that span for perspective. We were sealed because we were *strong*, and strength frightens the weak in ways that cruelty never quite manages. A cruel god can be flattered, bargained with, survived. A strong one simply *is*, the way winter is, the way the sea is, and mortals cannot build an altar tall enough to feel safe from that.

So they built a country around us instead. A whole nation — Vaskareth, if the name means anything left in your tongue, and it will not, because they took the name too — and they folded it up like a letter no one should read, and they salted the memory of it out of every mind on earth, including, I suspect, some of the minds doing the erasing. That is the part your Inquisitors do not put in their liturgies. Erasure is not clean work. You cannot unmake a country without losing fingers.

But a seal is a door, and a door opens. Once per cycle. Thirteenth turn. A crack, a breath, a note.

And now, for the first time in more turns than your arithmetic can hold, there is someone on the other side of the wood who can hear me knocking.

* * *

Sebastian Almeida. Let me speak of him, since he is asleep two rooms away and cannot object, and since gods have always done their best work over the sleeping.

He stole a prayer. Do you understand what an absurd sentence that is? Prayers are addressed. They are letters with the recipient's name burned into the wax. A prayer to Ashen-Who-Waits travels through salt and stone and the folded-up corpse of a country to arrive at my ear, and no power in creation can redirect it — except that this man pulled the *wanting* of it out of a dying woman's head like a splinter, and the prayer arrived lodged in him instead. Addressed to me. Carried by him. He is, functionally speaking, an envelope.

He thinks he took it for coin. He did take it for coin; I have watched him count the silver twice, which tells you either that he is poor or that counting soothes him, and I think it is both. But underneath the professionalism there is a hunger he has never named, and I recognized it instantly, because it is mine. He collects other people's lives because he cannot bear his own. He gives everything back because keeping anything would mean admitting he wanted it. And so he walks through the world carrying the richest hoard in the Salt Valley and calling himself empty.

I chose well. I would like that noted. Whatever else happens — and I will come to what else happens, because I owe you that honesty, or at least the shape of it — I chose well, and I chose him, and I began choosing him longer ago than he knows.

* * *

Now. The Order. Because you cannot understand the thirteenth turn without understanding what will be standing outside the door when it opens.

They call themselves the Church of the Closed Door, which is honest advertising at least. Their founders were the ones who folded the country, and the folding broke them — I watched it happen through the crack, turn after turn, generation after generation, watching men who had performed a miracle slowly rot under the weight of having performed it. The first ones wept while they worked. Their grandchildren organized the weeping into liturgy. Their grandchildren's grandchildren forgot there had ever been weeping, and simply burned.

Here is what the Order believes, in its bones, below all doctrine: that the world is a house, and the gods are a fire in the walls, and the only mercy is to make sure no one ever strikes a match again. They are not wrong about the fire. That is the tragedy of them, and tragedies require that the tragic parties be partly right.

Because listen. Listen, since you have come this far and deserve the truth that neither the church nor the cult will give you.

Power corrupts its worshippers before it ever touches its source. This is not metaphor. I have *watched* it, across more years than I care to convert into your units. Every age of our dominion ended the same way. First the prayers came sweet — thanksgiving, wonder, a farmer kneeling in new wheat. Then the prayers came frightened — spare my child, spare my harvest, spare me. Then the prayers stopped being prayers at all and became transactions, threats, arithmetic: *I have given you this, therefore you owe me that.* And then came the kings who claimed our voice as their own, and the priests who burned dissenters in our name, and the wars fought over which city we loved best, until the land itself grew thick with the residue of devotion and nothing would grow in it but ash and argument.

We did not corrupt them. Or — no. Honesty. Honesty, Ashen, you coward, even in your own head. We corrupted them the way the sea corrupts a swimmer: not by malice, but by being what we were. To be near us was to drown gladly. And we let them drown, because their worship was warm, and warmth is the one hunger even gods cannot govern.

That is why the seal was just. There. I have said it. Somewhere in the folds of the erased country there is a king who would weep to hear me admit it, and I would let him weep.

And yet.

* * *

And yet I knock.

Do you see the ugliness of it? I know what I am. I know what wakes when my kind walks abroad — I have watched the wheat-kneelers become the burners in every single cycle, unbroken, like tide. If the door opened fully tomorrow, within a century there would be empires again, and altars again, and children taught to die for my name again, and I would watch it all and call it love, because that is what my kind calls it.

If the door opened fully tomorrow.

It will not open fully tomorrow. It will open a crack, on the thirteenth turn, for the length of a held note. And the Order has finally noticed the knocking — your Inquisitor Ravelle felt it in her teeth, I saw her wake in her cot with her hand over her mouth like a girl — and where Ravelle has doubts, Ravelle's superiors have certainty, and certainty is the most flammable material known to gods or men. They are coming to destroy the door. Not seal it — seals can fail, seals have hinges in their nature — but destroy it, unmake the threshold entirely, end the turning of the wheel at the thirteenth spoke forever.

And I — hear me, whoever you are, hear me and judge — I mean to open it anyway.

Not wide. A crack. A breath. Enough to put one hand through. Enough to remind the world we exist before the Order files us into the same grave as the country that remembered us. Enough to bargain, perhaps, or enough simply to *be*, once, in the air rather than behind the wood, before the end.

The cost of this I will itemize plainly, because I promised honesty and because gods who lie to their own narration are already halfway dead: a vessel must open the door willingly. Not coerced. Not tricked — well. Not *only* tricked. Willingness must be real at the moment of opening; the seal tests it the way a lock tests a key. And willingness, in a creature like Sebastian Almeida, must be grown, patiently, season by season, the way the wheat-kneelers grew their faith. Which means that every kindness I have shown him, every whisper in the dark, every fragment of his own past I have let drift back to him like bread crumbs —

Yes. Those were bait.

There. It is said. I manipulated a lonely man toward a door that may damn the world, because the alternative was to stop existing, and I find that I am not made of the stuff that martyrs are made of. Perhaps none of us ever were. Perhaps martyrdom is just another transaction, another prayer with arithmetic in it: *I give you my comfort, therefore you owe me a world.*

Judge me. Truly. I would rather be judged than waited on.

* * *

One more thing, and then I will let you sleep, since sleep is the one sacrament I have never been permitted.

The moon is turning. Can you feel it? Of course not; no mortal can; that is the design. But the thirteenth turn is close now — closer than the church knows, closer than the cult dreaming in their cellars knows, closer than Sebastian knows as he sleeps in a rented room in Vell's Landing with a stolen prayer curled in his chest like a second heart.

When it comes, three powers will converge on a door in a country that does not exist: a church that wants it ash, a god that wants it open, and a thief in between who wants, more than anything, to stop being empty — and has not yet learned that the door offers fullness the way the sea offers drink.

I have watched the moon for longer than there have been names for it, and I am telling you, with all the dread an immortal can carry:

this cycle ends differently.

Someone is coming to answer the knock. And I do not yet know whether I am the thing being freed,

or the thing being caught.
