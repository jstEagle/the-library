## 8. Leaving the Valley

I had crossed the salt line twice before in my life, and both times I'd been running from something with teeth.

This time I was walking toward something with teeth, which felt different in the way that drowning feels different from being pushed into the sea. Same water. Very different intentions.

We gathered at the western marker an hour before dawn, when the salt fog lay over the flats like a shroud someone had measured carefully and cut to fit. The line itself wasn't much to look at — a row of white posts driven into the earth, each one crusted with mineral bloom, spaced a cart's width apart, marching off north and south until they dissolved into haze. No wall. No gate. Just posts, and a smell like a hot spring gone sour, and the particular quality of silence that lives in a place where nothing supernatural has been able to draw breath for four hundred years.

"It looks smaller than I imagined," Ilsa said.

"Everything does, from the safe side." I was checking my kit for the fourth time: flasks of oil, spare knives, the little tin of salt I'd packed against all reason. "You've never crossed?"

"I'm a mapmaker. Maps are how people who don't cross pretend they know things." She adjusted the strap of her chart case, which she wore across her chest like a bandolier. Her father's charts were in there, wrapped in oilcloth. The ones that showed a country that didn't exist. "Ravelle's crossed."

"Twice," Ravelle said. She stood apart from us, as she did everything, hands folded at her back, watching the fog. "Once on Order business. Once on my own." She didn't elaborate. Ravelle treated information the way misers treat coin — not because she lacked it, but because giving it away made her itch.

"And you?" I said to Ashen.

The god was wearing its current skin, which it had chosen, apparently, by pointing at a beggar's corpse in Vell's Landing and asking if the coat came in anything less conspicuous. A lean man, gray-eyed, unremarkable in every particular except that animals wouldn't come near him and neither, on bad days, would my own reflection. It went by Ash now, which it claimed was a compromise and I suspected was a joke only it got.

"I have never needed to cross," Ash said. "I have always been everywhere the door is near. But I have not worn legs in a very long time, and I find them unsatisfying. Knees especially."

"You're a god. Can't you fix your own knees?"

"Can you?" Ash flexed one leg experimentally, winced, and looked at me with those gray eyes that held too much weather behind them. "You'll want to hear this before we go, thief, because once we're past the posts, the conversation changes character."

Ilsa stopped fussing with her straps. Ravelle turned around fully. Even the mule — we had a mule; her name was Duchess and she hated all three of us equally — lifted her head.

"When you cross the line," Ash said, "the world remembers what it is. And so will you. Every memory you carry is going to wake up. In the valley they sleep because the valley starves them. Past the line, they'll eat."

"How badly?" I asked, though I already knew the answer was going to be unsatisfying.

"They scream first. Then they speak. Whether they ever stop speaking—" Ash shrugged with the borrowed shoulders. "I have watched men cross that line carrying stolen years. Some of them learned to live in the chorus. Some of them walked into the sea. I recommend the chorus."

"Wonderful," I said. "Anything else? Locusts? Plague of frogs?"

"The frogs can wait their turn."

* * *

The crossing itself took about nine steps.

That's what nobody tells you about borders — the ones that matter, anyway. Not walls, not rivers, not armies. Nine steps of ordinary ground between one law of the world and another, and your body doesn't feel the difference until it's too late to turn back.

I counted them anyway. Old habit. Thieves count.

One. Two. Three. The fog thickened around the posts, beading on my sleeves.

Four. Five. Something behind my eyes shifted its weight, like a man turning over in bed who hasn't decided yet whether to get up.

Six. Seven. Ilsa's hand found my elbow — she'd moved without me noticing, which should have frightened me more than it did — and she said something, but her voice came through as though from underwater.

Eight.

Nine.

And the world came back.

I don't have a clean way to describe it. The valley is quiet in a way that gets into your bones after a while — you stop noticing it, the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot or a debt on your ledger. Magic isn't gone from the world beyond the salt lines; it's just been locked out of the one place where people like me could afford to live. And I hadn't understood, in forty-one years, how loud the lockout was. How much of my life had been spent braced against a silence I mistook for peace.

Past the ninth step, every stolen memory I carried woke up at once.

The merchant's first love hit me first, because it was the loudest and the oldest and because it had been dying to get out of me since the day I took it. Her name was Sefa. Her hair smelled of cardamom because she worked in her mother's spice stall. He'd kissed her under the fish-market awning during a rainstorm, both of them laughing, and the taste of the rain on her mouth was—

—was not mine, I reminded myself, gagging on the sweetness of it, gripping Ilsa's arm hard enough to leave bruises. Not mine. Not mine. I am Sebastian Almeida and I am standing in wet grass and the sun is coming up over hills that are greener than any hill in the Salt Valley has a right to be.

Then the prayers started.

There were seven of them lodged in me — no, eight; I'd forgotten the midwife's prayer, tucked small and desperate in a corner of my skull where I'd hoped it would starve quietly. Eight prayers, and past the salt line every one of them opened its mouth and *prayed*, out loud, in voices that were not mine, in languages two of which I didn't even recognize, and the words came up through my throat like bile and I dropped to one knee in the wet grass and was violently, thoroughly sick.

"Sebastian." Ilsa, crouched beside me, her hand flat between my shoulder blades. Steady. Warm. Real. "Breathe. Look at me. What do you see?"

"Green," I managed. "Hills. Too many hills. Who approved this many hills?"

"Good. Keep talking."

"There's a woman in my head named Sefa and she smells like cardamom and she's been dead for eleven years and right now she's kissing somebody under a fish awning and it is *not* me." Another heave. "And there's a farmer praying for rain and a midwife praying for a mother's life and some bastard in a dead language praying for *revenge*, and they're all talking at once, and I want everyone in my skull to shut up and file a complaint in writing."

"That's the screaming part," Ash said, crouching down to my level. The god's face, for once, held something that looked almost like sympathy, or possibly indigestion. Hard to tell with gods. "It passes. Or it doesn't. You're doing better than most."

"What's the metric for 'better'?"

"You're still insulting the geography. Most men past this stage can only weep."

Ravelle stood watch at the line, facing back toward the posts, one hand resting on the pommel of her sword. She didn't look at me while I was sick. That was a courtesy, or discipline, or both. When she finally spoke, her voice carried that flat institutional calm I'd learned to hate and, lately, to lean on. "Inquisitor's field manual, third chapter. Vessels crossing the line are given quarter hour to stabilize. If they haven't stabilized, the manual recommends immediate—"

"I know what it recommends."

"Then you know I'm choosing not to follow my own book. Note the date and hour. It may not happen again."

* * *

An hour later we walked, because stopping felt dangerous and standing still felt worse. The land beyond the salt line rolled green and soft, stitched with hedgerows and stone walls older than the valley itself, and every mile of it hummed.

That's the word I keep reaching for. *Hummed.* The valley is a struck bell with the sound choked out of it. Out here, the bell was ringing. I could feel magic the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — in my teeth, in the scar tissue on my knuckles, in the space behind my eyes where eight strangers' memories paced their cages and screamed and, gradually, began to talk instead.

By midmorning the screaming had faded to something I could work with. The memories settled into layers, the way sediment settles in disturbed water. The merchant's love sat nearest the surface, warm and aching and mostly harmless. The prayers churned deeper. And underneath everything, so far down I might have invented it, something else stirred — old, cold, patient, shaped like a city I had never seen.

"Tell me about the fragments again," Ilsa said. We'd stopped to water Duchess at a stream, and I was sitting with my feet in it, because the water was cold and real and being cold and real seemed important just then.

"Bits and pieces. A tower with a door at the bottom. Streets laid out like the spokes of a wheel. A harbor full of ships that sailed without wind." I turned my hands over, studying them as if the fragments might be visible, like ink stains. "I stole them off a pilgrim six years ago. He was dying and delirious and paying in the only coin he had left. I thought they were fever dreams."

"And now?"

"Now I think they were directions."

Ilsa unrolled one edge of her father's chart, careful as a surgeon, weighting the corners with stones. The vanished country spread across the vellum in her father's precise hand — coastlines, rivers, the wheel-streeted capital with its spire at the hub. She'd shown me before, in the valley, where the parchment had been just paper. Out here, past the line, the chart seemed to breathe. The ink looked darker. Fresher. As though the country on it were insisting on itself.

"My father drew this from older maps," she said quietly. "Grandfather's. And his before him. Four generations copying a place none of them had ever seen, because the Order burned the originals and killed anyone who remembered it firsthand." Her finger traced the spire. "He used to say the country wasn't destroyed. Countries can't be destroyed by burning paper. It was *unremembered*. There's a difference. Fire kills a thing. Forgetting just makes everyone agree not to look at it."

"Which is worse?"

"Fire leaves ash," Ilsa said. "Forgetting leaves a hole shaped exactly like whatever you lost. And holes, in my experience, fill up with whatever's nearby." She glanced at me, quick and sideways. "Including stolen memories."

I didn't answer that, because the fragment beneath everything else chose that moment to shift, and for three heartbeats I was somewhere else entirely.

A street paved in white stone, radiating from a central square like spokes. Banners the color of deep water hanging from balconies. People everywhere, thousands, and every single one of them looking up — not at me, past me, through me, at something above the rooftops. And the feeling coming off all of them, rising like heat off a summer road: joy. Enormous, communal, unguarded joy, aimed at the sky.

Then it snapped shut and I was sitting in a cold stream with a wet map in front of me and Ilsa saying my name for the third time.

"—bastian. Sebastian. You went somewhere."

"Yeah." I wiped my face. My hands were shaking. "I went somewhere. There was a street. Wheel-shaped, like your chart. Everyone was looking up at something and they were happy. Really happy. It didn't feel like a trap, is the thing that bothers me. It felt like the best day of somebody's life."

Ash had gone very still. Gods shouldn't be able to go still the way Ash did — the stillness of a held breath four centuries long. "What color were the banners?"

"Blue. Deep blue. Like the bottom of the sea."

"*Cael.*" The god said the name like a wound reopening. "That is Cael. The capital. That is—" It stopped. Started again. "Thief, listen to me. Those streets are gone. Unmade. If you saw them, then they are not gone. They were only hidden, and hidden things can be found, and if you can see Cael then the door is closer than I dared hope, and also we are all going to die, probably, but the second part is negotiable."

"Comforting," Ravelle said dryly, from her post upstream. She'd heard everything; she missed nothing. "The god says we die. The manual says I should stab him. I continue to disappoint both documents." She paused. "Almeida. How many memories are you carrying? Exactly."

"Eight prayers. One love. A dozen assorted trades — a sailor's navigation sense, a gambler's nerve, a cook's palate, things I've sold or kept for utility. And the pilgrim's fragments." I hesitated. "And one I don't talk about."

"Which is?"

"Mine." The word came out rougher than I intended. "From when I was a kid. Before age six, it's just... static. Fog. I've always told myself it was a bad childhood. Bad childhoods make fog." I looked down at the stream, at my warped reflection breaking and reforming. "Out here, past the line, the fog feels less like damage and more like a lid. Like there's something under it, holding its breath."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The stream talked instead, indifferent and clear.

"Every vessel says that," Ash said finally, gently, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Every one. 'There's a room in me that's locked.' It's why the Church hunts you and the cults court you. Locked rooms, thief. The world is full of them, and half of them are doors."

"Well," I said, standing, brushing off my knees, putting the wry back on where it belonged, because the alternative was letting them see the fear underneath, and I'd spent forty-one years getting good at exactly one thing. "Let's go open mine last, then. I've earned at least that much scheduling control."

Ilsa laughed — short, surprised, real. Ravelle almost smiled, in the way that a glacier almost moves. Ash just watched me with those gray, weather-filled eyes, and I had the distinct impression the god was thinking about locked rooms, and lids, and breath held under them, and finding all of it very interesting indeed.

We walked west until the hills swallowed the salt line behind us. And all afternoon, underneath the prayers and the love and the fragments of a hidden city, something in the deepest cellar of my skull pressed its palm flat against the underside of the lid, and waited, and did not scream.

That was the worst part. Not the noise.

The patience.
