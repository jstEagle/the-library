## 9. Roadside Prayers

I was raised to believe that prayer was a letter with no address. You wrote it, you sealed it, you sent it up, and nothing came back — and that was the mercy of it. The Order taught that the old gods were dead, and the new liturgy was a kind of beautiful silence: you spoke into the dark, and the dark had the good manners not to speak back.

Three days out of Vell's Landing, the dark started answering its mail.

The first sign was the well.

We'd stopped at a crossroads hamlet called Brindle's Rest — twelve houses, a shrine with its god's face chiseled off, and a well that the villagers had ringed with salt in a pattern I recognized from my own catechism. Old ward-work. The kind the Order burned people for drawing, two generations back, before we decided forgetting was cleaner than burning.

The salt circle was fresh. And inside it, the water stood perfectly still, though there was no wind anywhere on the road.

"It's been like that since the rains," said the woman who owned the nearest house. She had flour on her forearms and a way of looking at my cassock that made me feel like an unpaid debt. "Since the night old Marta prayed."

"Prayed for what?" Ilsa asked. She was crouched by the well's edge, sketching the salt pattern into her chart-book with the quick, hungry strokes she used for coastlines.

"For rain." The woman laughed, and it wasn't a laugh at all. "Marta's garden was dying. She went out at moonrise and knelt at the broken shrine and asked for rain. Just that. Rain for her beans."

"And?"

"And it rained," the woman said. "It's still raining. On her garden. Only on her garden. It hasn't stopped in nine days."

I walked around to the back of the house, because I am the sort of fool who has to see things for himself, and I saw it. A plot of beans perhaps twenty paces square, drowned black, the stalks rotted to lace. Above it hung a column of falling water so dense it looked like grey silk, running from a cloud no bigger than a tablecloth, a little private storm that had been wringing itself out over one dead garden while the rest of the valley baked. Marta sat at the edge of it under an oilcloth, watching her beans drown, and she was smiling.

"Answered," she said when I approached. Her voice had gone soft and strange, the way voices go after too much crying or too much joy. "First time in my whole life anything answered. I knew they'd listen eventually. I knew it."

"Marta. Your garden is dead. You have to ask it to stop."

She looked at me with genuine pity, this drowning woman in her drowning yard. "Why would I ask it to stop? It *answered* me."

I could have explained. I could have told her about the thing wearing Ashen's voice that rode along with us, about vessels and doors and thirteenth turns, about how the gods coming back through a cracked seal were less like dawn and more like water finding every crack in a dam. But she wouldn't have heard any of it. There is a hunger in people, and I know it intimately because the Order fed on it for centuries — the hunger to be *heard* by something vast. It doesn't matter much what the vast thing says back. That it says anything at all is enough to make a sane woman sit smiling in the rain until the rain takes the ground out from under her.

I blessed her garden anyway. Habit. She thanked me. The rain fell on us both, warm as bathwater, and smelled — I will write this down even though it shames me — it smelled like my mother's kitchen. Like bread. Something down in that little storm knew exactly what would keep me standing there listening.

Sebastian found me at the gate. He'd gone pale somewhere between the well and the beans, which for a man who makes his living rummaging through other people's skulls was saying something.

"You felt it," he said.

"I felt a rainstorm."

"You felt it *looking* at you." He rubbed his arms like a man shaking off insects. "It's not just Ashen. The seal's cracked all along its length, Ravelle. Things are leaking through everywhere. Small things. Hungry things. Things that hear a prayer like Marta's and think — " he waved a hand at the drowned garden — "*that* is a good and faithful answer."

"Your god told you this?"

"My god," Sebastian said, with a bitterness I was starting to collect examples of, "told me it's sorry. It says the small ones don't understand scale. They're like dogs hearing a whistle through a wall. They come to the sound and they bring everything they are, and everything they are is *too much*, always, for people like Marta." He looked back at the column of rain. "Power without wisdom. That's what the king's going to tell us, isn't it? When we get there. I can already hear it."

I didn't confirm or deny. Inquisitors learn early that prophecy is just confession with better timing.

* * *

The second village was worse, and I want to be precise about that word, because I spent fifteen years administering the Order's definition of worse and I thought I had calibrated it.

Corven's Ford sat where the road dipped toward a brown river. We heard it before we saw it — singing. Dozens of voices, ragged and joyful, carrying over the fields. My first thought was festival. My second thought, when I saw the faces of my companions, was that none of us believed in festivals anymore.

They were gathered in the churchyard around a man laid out on a bier. He'd been a farmer; his hands said so, cracked and broad. He'd been dying of a fever, the village rector told us later — a wasting sickness, weeks of it, the kind that hollows a man out like a gourd. His wife had prayed. Not at the broken shrine, because Corven's Ford's shrine had been knocked flat by the Order before I was born. She'd prayed in her kitchen, over the soup, the way women pray when the priests have nothing left to offer.

And health had come.

It stood in the churchyard now, and I use the word *stood* deliberately, because it had shape. The villagers couldn't see it. I could, and so could Sebastian, and later Ilsa admitted she'd seen something and assumed it was heat-shimmer. It looked like a man built out of harvest-light, tall past reason, kneeling beside the bier with its hands pressed to the farmer's chest. And the farmer was *healing*. I watched flesh knit. I watched color climb back into a face that had been grey as ash. The singing villagers wept and reached toward him, and their fingers passed through the light-thing without resistance, and they sang louder.

"He'll live," Sebastian whispered. He sounded like a man watching a beautiful crime. "Ravelle, he's actually — "

"Yes."

"But look at his hands."

I had looked. The healing was real; the cost was being paid elsewhere, quietly, the way these things pay. The farmer's healed body was drinking him. Every heartbeat the light-thing mended, it took something — fat, then muscle, then, I suspected, years. He was growing younger and older at once, skin smoothing over a frame going brittle beneath it, a candle burning brilliantly from both ends. By spring he would be either a corpse or a husk, and the village would call it a miracle either way, because they'd watched it happen with their own eyes.

"Should we stop it?" Ilsa asked. She had her hand on the child's shoulder beside her — some rector's daughter who'd attached herself to our company outside Brindle's Rest and whom Ilsa had, without discussion or permission, begun feeding and shielding like a hen with one chick. "Can we?"

"No," Sebastian and I said together, and then looked at each other with mutual distaste at the agreement.

"If I break that connection," Sebastian said, "the fever comes back and finishes him tonight. If it runs its course, maybe he sees planting season. Maybe." He was quiet a moment. "This is what it's like, isn't it. This is what the Order found, all those years ago. Not monsters. Just — this. Answers that don't know how big they are."

I should have defended the Order. It was my office, my oath, my whole architecture of self. Instead I heard myself say: "We didn't find it. We *made* it worse. We burned the priests who understood scale, and we erased the country where the wisdom lived, and then we told everyone the gods were dead so that when the prayers started landing again, nobody remembered how to ask safely." I looked at the light-thing kneeling in its terrible tenderness. "There were rites, once. Ways of asking small. We burned the books that held them."

Sebastian stared at me. "Say that again slower, Inquisitor. I want to enjoy it."

"Don't be cruel. It costs me enough as it is."

"It doesn't cost you anything," he said, but without edge, and I understood he meant it kindly, which from Sebastian Almeida was nearly a sacrament.

We stayed the night in Corven's Ford because Ilsa refused to leave while the singing was happening, and because the child needed sleep, and because — though I'd have denied it under torture — I wanted to watch. Faith is my profession, and I had never once seen faith *work*. I stood in that churchyard until the candles guttered, watching a village love something that was slowly eating one of their own, and I understood with perfect clarity why Brother Cade did what Cade does. Fear looks exactly like this from the inside. It looks like devotion with the volume turned up.

Near midnight the light-thing lifted its head, and across the churchyard, its gaze found mine.

It had no face. It had a place where a face goes, and attention lived there, and the attention settled on me like a hand on the back of the neck. And it *knew*. Not what I was — it was too small and too old for doctrine — but what I carried. The doubt. The crack Ravelle had been hiding under the cassock since the day the Order showed me the burning records and called the ash *mercy*.

It smiled, in the way that a place where a face goes can smile.

*You doubt,* it said, directly into the marrow of me, no voice involved. *Good. Doubt is a door.*

I put my hand on my medallion of the Closed Door and recited the First Repudiation, and the thing laughed — a sound like a full granary, like plenty, like too much of everything — and went back to its work.

I did not sleep that night. I wrote my report instead, the one I'd been not-writing since Vell's Landing, the one addressed to the Order's provincial chapter detailing everything we'd found. I got as far as *the villages along the eastern road are receiving answers to petitionary prayer* and then I stopped, because I realized I was writing it in two minds. One mind meant to warn the Order. The other mind knew exactly what the Order would do with the warning, and had seen the villages Cade had left behind us on the southern roads, and could not make the second mind shut up.

I burned the page in the rectory stove. First report of my career I ever destroyed. It felt like blasphemy. It felt like honesty. I'm learning those feel identical.

* * *

The third one, we didn't find in time.

We smelled the smoke at dawn, two days past Corven's Ford, and Ilsa stood in the cart and read the wind like a chart and said, "That's not a hearth fire," and Sebastian whipped the mules into a run, and we came over the ridge into a valley where a village called Hallowmere had prayed for protection.

Protection. That was the prayer. A raiding band had been working the river roads — I saw their tracks, thirty riders, maybe forty — and Hallowmere, which had no wall and no militia and a broken shrine in its square like every village on this road, had gotten down on its knees together, all of them, the night before, and asked the old gods to keep them safe.

Something kept them safe.

The raiders' camp was at the mouth of the valley, and I will set down what we found there and then try never to describe it aloud again. Thirty men and horses, unmarked by blade or burn, lying as if they'd lain down to sleep mid-stride. Every one of them empty. Not dead — I checked, because the Inquisitor in me demanded procedure — but *empty*, bodies breathing in slow tides with nothing behind the eyes, faces slack as unbaked clay. Whatever had answered Hallowmere's prayer had reached into forty skulls and taken the part that raids villages, and had not been careful about the borders of the taking. Some of them might come back. Most wouldn't. Their souls hadn't been destroyed; they'd been *removed*, set aside like furniture cleared from a room, and I doubted whatever did the clearing knew how to put furniture back.

And Hallowmere itself —

Hallowmere was untouched. Not one door broken, not one chicken missing. The village stood pristine in the morning light, and its people stood in their doorways looking at the road below with expressions I recognized from my Order's oldest engravings: the faces of people who have witnessed the divine and discovered it fits them like a crown fits a martyr.

"They're safe," Ilsa said. She had the child's face pressed into her coat so she wouldn't see the field of empty men. Her voice shook. "Gods forgive me, Ravelle, they *are* safe. It worked."

"It worked," I agreed. "Now walk down there and look at their eyes."

We did. The people of Hallowmere were luminous with gratitude and utterly serene, and underneath the serenity, when you got close, when you took a housewife's hand or met a shepherd's eye, there was a wrongness I can only call *altitude*. They had touched something enormous and it had left residue. Their prayers had been answered so thoroughly, so *literally*, that a kind of certainty had taken root in them — the certainty of people who now know, with proof, that the universe bends to supplication. I talked with their headman for an hour. He was gentle, hospitable, generous. He also mentioned, twice, casually, that now that the gods were listening again, the village had been discussing what else to ask for. Better harvests. The raiders' horses. The rector's death — the rector had counseled patience, and patience, the headman observed mildly, was easier to pray away than fever.

"Do you hear yourselves?" I finally asked. "You've become the thing the Order always said you'd become."

He smiled at me with enormous kindness. "No, Inquisitor. We've become what the Order always feared we'd become. Those aren't the same thing." Then he offered me breakfast, and meant it, and I ate it, and hated myself a little for how good the bread was.

Sebastian found me at the cart afterward. He looked wrung out; the stolen memories had been loud all morning, he said, all those prayers he'd lifted over the years suddenly ringing like struck bells now that someone was home to receive them.

"You understand the problem now," he said. "Not the theology. The arithmetic. Every village on this road is a Marta. Every one of them is going to kneel down and ask for something small and human, and something enormous and blind is going to give it to them with both hands, and in a year this whole country is going to be a landscape of answered prayers with nobody left standing in the wreckage who remembers how to want carefully." He glanced back at Hallowmere's shining serenity. "Ashen says the seal was never meant to punish the gods. It was meant to protect *us* from getting exactly what we ask for."

"Your god has become very wise very quickly."

"Ashen's terrified," Sebastian said simply. "It keeps apologizing. It says the small ones are its siblings and it can't reach them, can't teach them, can't do anything but watch them love people to death. It says that's why the door matters. Not power. *Parenting.*" He almost smiled. "A god asking to be let out so it can do damage control. Write that into a hymn and watch the Order burn you twice."

I looked east, where the road ran on toward the plain that wasn't there, toward the border only Ilsa's charts could show us, toward a door and a king and a choice I was increasingly certain would end with me choosing wrong no matter which way I chose. Behind us lay three villages and a field of empty men. Ahead lay whatever the thirteenth turn would drag up out of the ground.

"We move faster," I said. "No more stops unless the mules drop."

"Ravelle." Sebastian's voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was about to hand me something heavy. "Look at the road ahead. At the milestone."

The milestone at the valley's mouth was old, older than the Order's roads, a squat finger of granite worn featureless by weather. Except it wasn't featureless anymore. Sometime in the last day, lines had risen in the stone like scars surfacing on old skin — a script I didn't know, cut deep and precise, and beneath the script, freshly carved, a single symbol I knew intimately from a thousand burned churches:

A door, closed. With a keyhole shaped like a praying mouth.

Someone — something — was marking the road ahead of us. Inviting us. Counting us down.

Ilsa came and stood beside me, and the child peered around her coat, and none of us said anything for a long moment, because carved invitations from unknown hands on the only road to a forbidden country are not the sort of thing one discusses cheerfully at breakfast.

"Well," Sebastian said at last, swinging up onto the cart. "At least somebody wants us to come."

"That's what worries me," I said, and flicked the reins, and we rolled east toward the writing on the stones.
