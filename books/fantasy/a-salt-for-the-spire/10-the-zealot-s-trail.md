## 10. The Zealot's Trail

My father used to say that a map was a promise. Draw a river true, and you promised every traveler who followed your ink that the water would be where you said it would be. He believed in his promises the way other men believe in saints — completely, and to his own ruin, because the Order came for his charts of the vanished country and he handed them over rather than lie about a single ford.

I inherited the charts and none of the faith.

Which is why, three days after we crossed the salt line, I sat in the loft of a barn outside a village called Harrow's Rest, correcting a map that had been wrong for two hundred years, while below me Sebastian Almeida argued with a goat.

"You can't eat the strap," he told it. "That's the only thing holding my pack together, and unlike you, I don't regenerate."

The goat chewed thoughtfully. Sebastian let it. That was Sebastian all over — he'd give away anything he owned except what kept him alive, and even then he'd negotiate. The widow whose barn this was, a round-faced woman named Maren with hands like tree roots, had offered us her loft for as long as we liked, because Ravelle had healed her grandson's fever on the road and word of that had spread faster than any map could carry it. Word travels crooked in the countryside. It arrives before you do, arrives changed, arrives wearing clothes you never put on it. By the time we reached Harrow's Rest, the story said an Inquisitor of the Closed Door had performed a miracle, and half the village wanted to touch her sleeve and the other half wanted her gone before the Order noticed she'd stopped behaving like one of theirs.

Ravelle herself sat by the hearth downstairs, cleaning a pistol she no longer had ammunition for, looking like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

And Ilsa Vane — cartographer, orphan of reputation, keeper of dead men's promises — drew lines on vellum by lamplight and pretended not to listen to the goat.

I'd been at it since dusk. My father's chart of the eastern marches showed a river called the Oss running south; the land out the window disagreed. Rivers move, I told myself, rivers are liars with long memories, but the hills hadn't moved, and those were wrong too. Which meant the chart wasn't outdated. It meant it had been *edited*. Someone had redrawn the country around the erased one, nudging rivers and folding valleys, so that anyone navigating by honest memory would find themselves turned gently, persistently, away. You don't build a wall around a country. You just make every road lead somewhere else.

"Found something?" Sebastian asked from the ladder. The goat had apparently won, or been won over; he had hay in his hair either way.

"My father's maps have been lying," I said. "Or someone made them lie after he died. Look." I held up the old chart beside my working copy. "The Oss should bend east here, through the fens. Every landmark says so. But the chart walks you west, toward the coast, in a great polite curve, like a host steering you away from the locked room."

Sebastian studied it the way he studied everything — sideways, like a pickpocket reading pockets. "The Order did that?"

"The Order, or whoever taught the Order to be afraid." I smoothed the vellum. "It's good work. It took me two days to catch it, and I grew up inside these lines."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Your father didn't know?"

"My father knew exactly what he was drawing. He just refused to draw anything else." I heard the bitterness come out of me sideways, sharp as a compass point, and regretted it, because Sebastian of all people understood inheritance. "Sorry. It's late."

"Don't apologize to me. I once stole a man's grief and sold it back to him at a discount." He grinned, and I threw a scrap of charcoal at him, and he caught it without looking, which was insufferable, and which I was already, against every principle I had, getting fond of.

That was the trouble with our company. We'd started as a contract — thief, Inquisitor, mapmaker, bound together by necessity and the mutual contempt that passes for trust among professionals. Somewhere between the salt line and here, the contract had grown clauses nobody signed. Sebastian had begun leaving the last of the bread on my bedroll without comment. Ravelle had begun saying grace quietly, and I'd noticed she now included all four of us in it, though she probably thought I was asleep. And I had begun drawing *us* into the margins of my maps — a small thief's mark, a nun's cross, my own compass rose — the way children draw their family next to the house.

I want it noted that I noticed myself doing it and disapproved thoroughly. Noticing has never once stopped me from anything.

* * *

The dogs started barking an hour before dawn, and then they stopped, all at once, which was worse.

I came down the ladder with the charts under my arm because I'm a coward about the charts specifically — everything else I could lose and relearn, but those pages were the only true map left of the erased country, and I gripped them the way other people grip children. The loft door opened onto cold air and a smell I didn't understand yet. Smoke, yes, but underneath it something sweeter and more wrong, like sugar burnt black.

Maren stood in her yard in her nightclothes, facing north, very still.

"What is it?" Ravelle was already awake, already armed, already dressed — I never saw that woman sleep so much as lean against something.

"Torchlight on the ridge," Maren said. Her voice was flat the way voices go flat when they've done their screaming in advance. "That's the mill road. There's nothing on the mill road worth burning."

There was one thing on the mill road worth finding.

"They followed us," Ravelle said, and her face did something complicated and terrible — guilt arriving ahead of the fire, the way lightning comes before thunder if you're standing close enough to the strike. "I led them here. My reports, my route — I wrote it all down for the Order's eyes, standard procedure, and Cade reads my reports."

"Cade?" I said.

"Brother Cade." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd survived. "The zealot wing. When I requested this investigation, I assumed I'd be given scholars. I was given him." She looked north, at the orange bloom beginning above the trees. "He doesn't arrest people, Mistress Vane. Arrest implies the person might still matter."

Below the ridge, the first house caught. I watched it happen the way you watch anything enormous and inevitable — with a horrible clarity, noting details, because detail is all a mapmaker has. I noted how the fire spread along the thatch like ink soaking paper. I noted how fast. I noted that the wind was coming off the ridge, pushing the flames downhill toward the well and the granary, toward the low houses where the families with young children lived, because villages always put the children nearest the water and fire always finds the water first.

"Get them out the fen side," I said. "The ground's wet, the fire will slow at the ditch, there's a path — I mapped it yesterday." I was already moving, already unrolling my working copy across Maren's table, jabbing my finger at the lines I'd drawn by lamplight. Here is the thing about being valued only for your dead father's work: it teaches you to make sure your own work is worth dying for. "Here, and here. Maren — knock doors starting at the far end, don't argue, don't gather anything. Sebastian—"

"I know," he said. "The ones who won't leave." His face had gone strange and focused, the thief's face, the face that had lifted a lifetime of love from a dying man without flinching. He went.

Ravelle stood one second longer, looking at the burning ridge like a woman watching her own doctrine walk around wearing someone else's face. Then she pulled her coat straight and said, with terrible calm, "I'll meet Brother Cade."

"Ravelle—" I started.

"He'll stop for an Inquisitor. He won't stop for anything else on this earth." She was already walking toward the flames. "Get them out, Vane. All of them. That's an order."

"You're not my commanding officer anymore," I shouted at her back.

"No," she agreed, without turning. "Thank the gods."

She said it absently, carelessly, the way you'd say *thank goodness* — and I remember thinking, later, how strange it was, that the first time any of us spoke the old gods' names aloud without fear or hunger was as a joke, in a burning village, by a woman who'd spent her life hunting their worshippers. Words get loose like that. They travel crooked, arrive changed.

* * *

I will not write down everything I saw that morning. Maps are honest work and I try to keep the habit, but some things belong on no chart.

I will write down this much. I will write down the boy.

His name was Tam. He was six years old and he was hiding in the root cellar beneath the collapsed bakery, because his mother had told him — God help her, she'd told him the truth — *if the soldiers come, hide, and don't come out until someone says the word "wren."* I found him because of the map. The cellar wasn't on Maren's directions; it was on mine. I'd drawn it the day before, idly, the way I draw everything, a little square marked *cold storage, low lintel,* because a map that omits cellars is a map that omits where people go when the world ends.

So when the roof beams came down behind me and the smoke rolled thick and blue along the lane, I didn't guess where the crying was coming from. I knew. I walked into that cellar past a dead woman's outstretched hand, and I knelt down in the dark, and I said, "Wren."

A small face appeared between flour sacks and turnip bins, grey with dust, eyes enormous.

"Are you the soldier?" he asked.

"No," I said. "I'm the mapmaker. I draw where things are. And right now I need you to tell me where things are in here — is there another way out?"

"There's a hole." He pointed at the back wall, where the earth had slumped away from an old foundation stone. "But it's small."

"It's small for a soldier," I said. "You're not a soldier. Push the sacks down and crawl, and I'll follow you, and I promise—" and there it was, my father's word, rising in my throat unasked-for, *a map is a promise* — "and I promise you the hole goes where I say it goes. Out. Toward the fen. Toward your grandmother's cousin's house, the one with the red door. Do you believe me?"

He looked at me the way children look at adults making promises in burning buildings — with total, devastating seriousness.

"You're crying," he said.

"Mapmakers cry when the territory changes," I said. "Crawl."

The hole was barely wide enough. I lost skin off both shoulders and one boot heel entirely, and I emerged into a world of orange light and falling ash with a six-year-old strapped to my back, his arms around my neck, his breath hiccuping against my ear, and my charts — my father's charts, the only true map of the erased country in existence — crushed between us, and I never once considered letting go of either. I want that recorded too. Whatever else the fire took, it didn't take the order in which I chose.

The fen path held. Wet ground, slow fire, exactly as drawn. I got him to the red door. I put him in the arms of a grandmother-cousin who sobbed his name, and he looked back at me over her shoulder and mouthed *wren* like it was a password he'd keep forever, and I turned around and walked back toward the smoke because the job wasn't finished, because half the village was still standing and Sebastian was still in it.

I found him at the well, sitting on the edge, soaked to the bone, holding a cat.

"Everyone?" I asked.

"Everyone," he said. "Forty-one souls. Two goats declined evacuation. One cat accepted under protest." He held it out to me. "This seemed like your department."

I took the cat because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Behind us, the eastern quarter of Harrow's Rest was ash, and the western quarter stood untouched in the grey light, and forty-one people who owed us nothing stood in a field watching their homes burn and looking at us — at the thief, the mapmaker, the runaway Inquisitor — with an expression I couldn't read until Maren arrived, soot-faced, leading the two recalcitrant goats, and explained it.

"You came back in," she said. "For the rest of us. After you were out."

"We were contracted to pass through," I began. "Strictly speaking—"

"Hush," said Maren, who had hands like tree roots and clearly had never once been hushed by a superior argument. She looked at the three of us — Sebastian dripping, me clutching a cat, Ravelle walking up from the mill road with ash in her white hair and a look on her face I'll come to — and she said, "You'll stay until you've eaten. All of you. That's not gratitude, that's arithmetic. People who run into fires for strangers aren't strangers, whatever the paperwork says."

* * *

Ravelle told us about Cade that evening, in Maren's kitchen, over food we had no right to and ate anyway.

"He burned the eastern quarter deliberately," she said. Her hands were steady around the cup; her voice was the steady part. "Not indiscriminate — deliberate. He fired the houses closest to the road first, so that anyone fleeing would flee *toward* the fen, and he posted men at the fen path. To catch vessels coming out." She stared into her cup. "I killed two of them. My brothers. They recognized me. One of them asked me — he asked me, with his last breath, whether I'd found the gods, whether it was true the gods were waking, and I couldn't answer him, because the answer is yes, and because if I said yes he'd have died certain he was right, and he wasn't right, he was *used*, we were all used—"

"Ravelle." Sebastian's voice was quiet. "Breathe."

She breathed. The kitchen was silent except for the fire, which we'd built ourselves, in a hearth, in a house that still stood, and there was something in that — in choosing to light a fire after watching one devour a village — that felt like a small stubborn prayer.

"Cade isn't following my reports anymore," Ravelle said at last. "I burned my copies. He has his own scouts now, and something else — he moves like a man taking instruction. Not from the Order. From *ahead* of us. He knows where we're going before we decide it." She looked up, and her eyes went to Sebastian, and I saw the thought cross her face before she could stop it, the ugly necessary thought: *the god speaks to him.*

"Ashen," Sebastian said. "You think Ashen's showing him the road too."

"I think a god that wants its door opened wouldn't object to zealots driving us toward it," Ravelle said. "Fear herds as well as hope does."

"That's cheerful," I said. "The god is using both sides."

"The god is *starving*," Sebastian said, and there was something raw in it, something that sounded less like theology than like a man defending a friend. "You'd herd too, if you'd waited two hundred years in the dark."

Nobody answered that. Outside, the village that remained was quiet, and somewhere in it a six-year-old was asleep behind a red door, alive because of a word his mother gave him and a hole in a wall I happened to have drawn.

Later, when the others slept, I sat by the hearth and did the only thing I know how to do with grief, which is to draw it. I drew Harrow's Rest as it had been — every house, the mill, the bakery, the root cellar with its low lintel — and then, beneath it, on the same page, I drew it as it stood tonight, with the eastern quarter rendered as blank vellum. Erasure, I thought. This is what erasure looks like up close. Not a country vanishing from maps. A row of houses. A woman's hand in a doorway. A boy learning the word *wren* will be the last thing his mother ever taught him.

I understood, sitting there, why the Order feared the erased country enough to delete it. Not because of the gods. Because of exactly this — because loss that size breaks something in the people who witness it, and broken people ask questions the Order cannot afford.

I also understood, with the same certainty, that I was no longer drawing maps for a contract.

I turned to the margin of my working copy — the margin where, without ever deciding to, I'd been keeping a tally of us. The thief's mark. The nun's cross. My compass rose. I added a fourth symbol now, small, at the bottom: a wren, wings folded, perched on the border line of the erased country itself. Because that's where we were going, all of us, and because Tam would carry that word his whole life, and words travel crooked, and arrive changed, and sometimes they arrive home.

Behind me, Sebastian stirred in his sleep and murmured something. I caught only the end of it — *"not the name, please"* — and filed it away with all the other fragments of him I'd been collecting without permission: the way he counted coins he never spent, the way he flinched from mirrors, the way he'd said, once, drunk on Maren's plum brandy, *I don't remember being a child, isn't that funny?*

Isn't that funny.

I looked at the sleeping thief, and at the blank space where his childhood should have been, and for the first time it struck me as neither funny nor sad but *mapped* — a gap in the chart exactly the size and shape of the erased country, sitting in the middle of a man I was starting to love.

Tomorrow we'd ride east, into the fold in the world my father's edited charts had tried to steer everyone away from.

Tonight I sharpened my pencil, and I drew the gap carefully, precisely, and labeled it the way my father would have:

*Here lies something taken.*
