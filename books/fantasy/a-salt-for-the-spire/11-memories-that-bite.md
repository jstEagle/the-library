## 11. Memories That Bite

The trouble with carrying other people's lives is that they don't stay where you put them.

I'd learned that early, back when I was still learning the trade. A memory isn't a coin you can stack in a drawer. It's a living thing, or close enough — it wants to grow, to attach itself to something, to *mean*. Leave a stolen memory in your head long enough and it starts putting down roots. Starts calling itself yours. I'd seen old lifters go mad from it, wandering Vell's Landing insisting they'd been married to women who never existed, mourning children they'd only ever borrowed. The professionals carved their heads clean every season. Take nothing, keep nothing. That was the rule.

I had broken the rule six times now, and every one of those memories was awake and angry about it.

It started on the third night out of the valley, camped in the green country past the salt line where magic came back into the world like blood into a numb limb — all pins and needles and ache. I woke with someone else's grief in my mouth.

Not my grief. I knew my own grief the way you know your own teeth: by feel, by absence, by the places it had been pulled. This was older. Saltier. It tasted of a harbor at dusk and a woman's name spoken into water, and it belonged to a fisherman who had drowned his sorrows for forty years after the sea took his sons, and whose prayer-memory I had lifted off a chapel wall in chapter and verse because a client paid by the prayer.

The memory was *praying*.

That was the horror of it, once I understood what I was feeling. Prayers aren't words. They're shaped longing, and longing has a direction, and mine — his — was aimed at gods who were waking up, and somewhere out in the dark an old thing named Ashen-Who-Waits could feel every one of them like fingers tapping on glass.

"Sebastian." Ilsa's voice, low, from across the dead fire. "You're talking in your sleep."

"What did I say?"

"You said 'the towers are singing.' Then you said a word I don't know. Then you cried a little."

"I don't cry."

"You did. Quietly. It was very dignified."

Ravelle sat up at the edge of the firelight, already dressed, because Inquisitors apparently slept in their collars. Her eyes went to me the way they always did — measuring, cataloguing, deciding whether tonight was the night I became a problem instead of an asset.

"The memories are fighting him," she said. Not a question. She'd seen vessels before, or read about them in books the Order probably shouldn't have let her read. "Past the salt line, they have strength again. They have *context*. A prayer stored in a dry mind is just an echo. A prayer stored in a wet one, in a world where prayers work—" She made a small gesture, priestly and precise. "They try to finish themselves."

"Finish themselves," I repeated.

"A prayer is a request," Ravelle said. "Requests want answers. Your thief's discipline kept them quiet in the valley. Out here, they're asking."

Wonderful. I was carrying six open letters addressed to the divine, and the postal service had just reopened.

* * *

By the fifth night I'd stopped sleeping properly, which meant I'd started sleeping *deeply*, which was worse, because that's when they took me.

The first life was the fisherman's. Not the prayer this time — the whole man, or enough of him. I stood on a deck I'd never owned hauling nets I'd never rigged, and the sea was the wrong color, green-gold instead of the salt-flat white I'd grown up with, and I loved it with a love so specific and worn-in that for three full minutes I was certain I had lived it. I knew the scar on my left hand came from a hook. I knew my wife's bread burned on the left side of the loaf because she always turned it too soon. I knew my sons' laughter the way you know weather coming.

Then the net came up empty, the way it always came up empty in the years the memory remembered, and the grief hit me like a wave hitting a seawall, and I woke up on my back in the dirt with Ilsa shaking me and my own hands clawing at my face.

"Whose bread burns?" she demanded. "Sebastian. Whose bread?"

"Nobody's," I said. "No one's bread. I don't even like bread."

She didn't laugh. That scared me more than the memory had.

The second life came two nights later and it wasn't a fisherman. It was a soldier, one of the prayer-memories Ravelle herself had asked me to lift — a dying man's last prayer from a battlefield the Order had won. And battlefields, it turns out, are not places. They're *weights*. I spent a night being a man named Corvus who believed absolutely in the Closed Door, who had watched the Order burn a village of praying people and called it mercy, and who prayed anyway, at the end, not to be saved but to be *right*.

I woke up understanding him. That was the part I couldn't say out loud. I woke up knowing exactly how a devout man burns children and sleeps well afterward, and some corner of me had *agreed* with him while I was in there, and I lay in the dark doing arithmetic on my own soul, trying to figure out which parts were mine.

How do you audit yourself when the ledger keeps getting rewritten?

* * *

The third night gave me the capital.

I need to tell it carefully, because fragments lie. Memory-theft is a violent act; you rarely get a whole room, more often a doorway, a smell, a single sentence overheard. What I got was a hill.

A city on a hill, white-walled, built in rings like a stone dropped in water, each ring a different color of roof — copper gone green, slate gone blue, clay gone red — and at the center a spire so tall it made the sky look like a ceiling. And the spire was *singing*. Not bells. Not wind. A sound like a held note that had been holding for a thousand years, and everyone in the streets below had stopped to listen, faces upturned, and their faces were —

Happy. That's what I can't make peace with. They weren't enslaved or terrified or drugged. They were happy, in the way people are happy at a festival, and the god-light pouring down on them was warm as summer, and I stood among them wearing someone's body and someone's joy and I thought, with a stranger's certainty: *this is good, this has always been good, why would anyone seal this away—*

And then the fragment turned, the way fragments do, and I saw the edge of the square. One figure not looking up. One figure standing in the shadow of an awning with her hands pressed over her ears, weeping, mouthing the same word over and over:

*"Stop. Stop. Stop."*

And the light touched her anyway, gently, the way you'd touch a child, and her weeping changed key — became bliss, became worship, became the same upturned happiness as everyone else — and the thing wearing her consent like a coat walked her home.

I came out of it gasping.

Ashen was there. Of course Ashen was there; the god had taken to perching at the edge of our camp at night like a patient crow, a presence more than a shape, a pressure behind my left ear where the first prayer had lodged. When it spoke, it spoke inside the ache.

*You saw.*

"I saw a city that eats its people smiling."

*I saw a home,* Ashen said, and there was something in its voice I hadn't heard before. Something small. *You saw it through the eyes of one who loved it. Both things are true, thief. That is what power is. It does not take your joy from you. It takes everything else until joy is all that's left.*

"That's supposed to make me sympathetic to your cause?"

*It is supposed to make you honest.* A pause, and then, softer, almost sly: *You recognized the spire.*

I hadn't. Or I had — the way you recognize a face in a crowd, that double-take that arrives before thought. "I've never been there."

*No,* Ashen agreed. *But you've been looked at by it.*

I didn't ask what that meant. Asking gods questions is how they get in.

* * *

It was Ilsa who found the real wound, because Ilsa finds everything; it's her profession and her affliction.

We'd made camp in the ruins of a waystation, and she was going through her father's charts by lamplight, cross-referencing, muttering, and I was pretending to sleep, and she said, without looking up:

"Your memories have gaps."

"They're not my memories."

"The ones you stole, no. But you. You have gaps." She turned a page. "I map coastlines for a living, Sebastian. I notice missing pieces. You talk about your childhood in Vell's Landing like a man reading from notes. The orphanage, the lifter who trained you, the first purse you cut — it's all *correct*, but it's all surface. There's no depth anywhere before you were, say, ten years old. No smells. No weather. Just facts."

My mouth was dry. "Lots of people have bad childhoods. We bury them."

"We bury them under other things. Under shame, under drink, under better stories. We don't bury them under *nothing*." She finally looked at me, lamplight catching the ink stains on her fingers. "And here's the thing that keeps me up. You're the best memory-thief I've ever heard of. Do you know what that means? It means you have the finest-grained sense of your own memory of anyone alive. You would *notice* a gap that size. Unless—"

"Unless the gap notices itself back," I finished. "Unless it's wearing a patch. Unless someone sealed it the way the Order seals countries, smooth and story-shaped, so the eye slides off."

Neither of us said the next thing. The next thing sat in the tent between us like a third person:

*Who steals a memory from a child?*

Thieves steal from the rich. Thieves steal from the careless. Nobody steals from a child except someone who wants the child to forget something specific — and nobody pays for that job except someone powerful, and the only people powerful enough to reach into a boy's head and take his first decade and leave a tidy summary in its place were the people who had once erased an entire country from every map and every mind.

From every map. From every mind.

Ilsa's charts proved the country existed. My head, maybe, proved I'd come from it.

"I was born there," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, and given the week I'd had, I couldn't swear it wasn't. "Before the erasure. Before the salt. Someone carried me out and paid to have me emptied and planted in Vell's Landing like a—like a seed in a salt field, where nothing magical could ever grow, where no god could ever find me, where no *memory* could ever wake up and tell me what I was."

"And then you grew up to become a man who steals memories," Ilsa said quietly. "And started collecting prayers to the old gods. And walked straight back toward the door somebody buried you to keep you away from."

The silence had weight. Outside, Ashen waited in the dark, and I felt it listening, felt it *pleased*, and hated that I felt that too.

"Every step I've ever taken," I said, "was I walking, or was I being *carried*?"

Ilsa didn't answer. She reached across the lamp and took my hand — ink-stained fingers over a thief's calluses — and held on, and it was the first honest thing anyone had done for me in longer than I could prove.

Because that was the problem, wasn't it. I couldn't prove anything. Not the orphanage, not the training, not the name. Somewhere behind a door in my own skull was a boy from a singing city, and someone had locked it, and the lock was good, and every year of Sebastian Almeida might be a forgery hanging on the wall of an empty house.

* * *

Near dawn, Ashen spoke one last time, right at the border of sleep, when my walls were thin.

*There is a thirteenth turn coming, thief. And at that turn, the door opens, and you will stand in front of it — I have seen it, we have all seen it, it is the one thing the future refuses to change. When you do, ask yourself the question you are afraid of.*

"Which question?"

*Not whether your life was stolen.* The god's voice was gentle, which was somehow worse. *Whether you want it back.*

And far away, at the edge of hearing, beneath the ground, beneath the world — a spire began, very softly, to sing.
