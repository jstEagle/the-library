## 12. The Border That Isn't There

I have burned heretics. I want that on the record, in my own hand, before anyone tells this story kindly. I have stood in the square at Corvane with the pitch torch in my fist and watched a woman who prayed to something older than the Order come apart like wet paper, and I sang the hymn afterward with everyone else, and my voice did not shake.

So understand that when I say what we found at the edge of the world frightened me more than any pyre ever frightened anyone, I am not speaking as a novice. I am speaking as an Inquisitor of the Church of the Closed Door, and I know precisely how much of my faith was still standing when we arrived. It was less than I pretended. It had been less since Vell's Landing.

The plain announced itself by refusing to be anything.

We had ridden east for nine days out of the drowned villages — nine days of Ravelle-does-not-sleep-well, of Sebastian humming hymns he'd stolen from dead men's heads just to watch me flinch, of Ilsa correcting our course every morning with the flat certainty of a woman reading her father's handwriting. The land grew poor and then poorer and then simply gave up: grass going grey, trees standing sparse and apologetic, no birds. Ashen rode folded up inside Sebastian's shadow like a debt he'd stopped trying to explain to us, and even the god said nothing, which from a god is its own kind of sermon.

Then, shortly past noon on the ninth day, Ilsa reined in and said, "Here."

And I looked at the most unremarkable stretch of grassland I have ever seen in my life, and every instinct I possessed told me to look somewhere else.

Not fear. Not boredom. Something worse than either — a gentle, reasonable insistence that *this* was not worth looking at, that there was nothing here, that there had never been anything here, that the interesting thing was actually that rock over there, or the horizon, or the inside of my own eyelids. My eye slid off the plain the way a boot slides off ice. I caught myself three times turning my horse away without having decided to.

"Stop doing that," Ilsa said, without looking at me.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing it right now. Everyone does it. Papa called it the courtesy of the map." She swung down and unrolled one of her charts across her saddle, weighting the corners with stones. "The erasure isn't a wall. A wall you could find. It's more like — you know how you can be in a room your whole life and never see the ceiling? It's not hiding. You just never look up."

"I look up," said Sebastian.

"You steal," Ilsa said. "It's different."

She'd drawn the border as a line of small careful strokes, like rain hatching, and along it she'd written, in her father's hand copied out in her own: *the country consents to be forgotten; do not mistake consent for absence.* Below that, smaller, almost embarrassed: *the eye is a liar here. Trust the lead.*

She had a plumb line of it, her father's surveying lead, worn smooth as a knucklebone. She held it out over the grass and let it hang, and I will admit I laughed — a short, ugly Inquisitor's laugh — because it hung perfectly straight over perfectly empty ground and told us nothing.

Except it didn't hang straight.

It leaned. Barely. Two degrees, maybe three, toward the east, as if the whole plain were a very slight slope and everything loose in the world wanted quietly downhill into it. As if the erased country were a weight, and forgetting has mass.

"There," Ilsa said softly. "There you are."

Sebastian dismounted and stood at the edge of nothing in particular with his hands in his pockets, wearing the expression I had learned to distrust most: the professional one. The one he wore when he was about to pick someone's head open.

"It's different from up close," he said. "In the valley, the fragments are quiet. Salt keeps them asleep. Out here they're—" He grimaced, hunting for the word. "Loud isn't right. *Crowded.* Like standing outside a house where everyone's awake and arguing and the windows are lit, and none of them know you're listening."

"And what are they saying?" I asked.

He looked at me then, and for once there was no wryness in him at all, and that frightened me more than the leaning lead.

"They're saying *home,*" he said. "All of them. Every fragment. They're all facing the same direction, Ravelle. Like iron filings. Like they remember something I don't."

Ashen stirred in his shadow. When the god spoke through him — and I still have not decided whether that is possession or partnership or some third thing I lack a doctrine for — Sebastian's voice came out layered, doubled, a man speaking under a much older man. "*Closer,*" it said. "*Let me closer. I have not smelled my own country in nine hundred years.*"

"No," I said.

"You don't command me, little candle."

"No," Sebastian said, in his own voice, quieter. "But she's right. Not yet. Because if I let you up to the front, you'll walk straight in, and Ilsa says walking straight in is how people stop existing."

That got my attention back onto the chart. "Explain the danger plainly," I said. "I've had enough of everyone being delicate with the Inquisitor."

Ilsa didn't look up from her father's lines. "The erasure works both ways. The country was taken out of memory — but the door swings both directions. Anything that crosses without anchoring itself gets taken out too. Not killed. *Unwritten.* People have walked in. Traders, soldiers, the Order's own survey teams, back before the Order decided curiosity was heresy. Their horses came back. Sometimes their boots came back. Never them. And nobody remembers them well enough to miss them." She finally raised her eyes to mine. "Your Order didn't lose anyone crossing, Inquisitor. Your Order lost everyone who *remembered* there was a crossing. That's why the charts were burned. That's why my father drew them in code and hid them inside maps of places that exist."

For a moment nobody spoke, and the wind moved over the grass, and the grass did not care about any of us.

"So we anchor ourselves," I said. "How?"

"Three ways," Ilsa said, counting on her fingers. "One: the charts. Papa's ink has a binding in it — ground bone and salt and something he never wrote down. Anyone touching the chart while crossing stays written. Two: the salt. Sebastian's been carrying valley salt since the Landing; it's why the fragments ride in him so quietly. A line of it, held in the hand. Three—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide to tell the truth, and I noted, not for the first time, that she was braver than me, because telling the truth to an Inquisitor is a kind of courage I never mastered— "three: being remembered. Out loud. By name. While you cross. Someone on the outside holding your name like a rope."

"That last one is folklore," I said.

"The first two are engineering," Ilsa said. "The third is the part Papa believed and couldn't prove. But the king's people made it nine hundred years, Ravelle. Somebody must have held *their* names once."

* * *

We crossed at dusk, because Ilsa's charts showed the border thinning at day's turn — the old surveyors' term, *slack water*, as though erasure were a tide, and perhaps it is; I have stopped pretending I know what erasure is.

The preparations were absurd and holy and I could not tell the difference, which I suspect is the honest state of most religion. Ilsa cut a strip of chart for each of us and sewed it inside our collars with sail thread. Sebastian poured valley salt into five twistings of cloth — his, mine, Ilsa's, and two for the mules, because he insisted the mules were persons and I was too tired to argue theology with him. And then he stood at the edge of the sliding grass with his eyes half-closed, listening to the crowded dark inside his own skull, and began to speak names.

Not ours. Not at first. He spoke the names of the dead merchant whose first love he carried. The name of the fishwife whose prayer had started all of this. Names pulled out of stolen years, offered up like coins to a toll-keeper. "They're anchors too," he said. "Everything in me that's real. I'm the most heavily anchored man alive, Ravelle. First time that's ever been a compliment."

"And ours?" I asked. "Who holds ours?"

"You do," he said. "You're the only one of us the erasure doesn't want. You're Order. You're *written* everywhere — reports, ledgers, the burning lists. You're the opposite of forgotten." He smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "Congratulations. Bureaucracy is your soul's anchor."

I chose not to strike him. History should note that as well.

We went in at slack water, in a line, each with a fist closed around salt and a chart against the throat, and Ilsa walked last, calling our names aloud into the greying air — *Gideon Marchetti, Ilsa Vane, Sebastian Almeida* — wait. No. She called *my* name, and I will write what happened next exactly as it happened, because if I soften it now I will soften it forever after.

My name left her mouth and the world *moved.*

Not the plain. Me. It was as though I had been a painting hanging slightly crooked for my entire life and someone had finally set me level — except level felt like falling, because I had organized my whole soul around the crookedness. The grass sharpened. The dusk sharpened. I could suddenly count individual blades for fifty yards in every direction, and every one of them was leaning east, gently, patiently, like mourners at a graveside they cannot find.

And beyond the grass — where there had been nothing, where my eye had slid and slid and slid — there was *country.*

Rolling land. Terraced hills gone wild. The skeleton of a road running toward mountains that had no business being invisible from the outside, mountains tall enough to make their own weather. Far off, catching the last light, something white and impossibly slender: a spire, thin as a needle, standing over the plain like a candle over a sleeping body.

"Oh," I said. Which is not what an Inquisitor says. An Inquisitor says a psalm. I said *oh*, small as a child, and I have decided to leave it in the record.

"It gets worse," Sebastian said beside me. His face was wet. I do not think he knew it. "The fragments — they're singing, Ravelle. All of them. They're *home.*"

Behind us, the plain we had crossed was already wrong again. I turned and looked back the way we had come and felt my eye begin to slide, felt the polite insistence that there was nothing behind me, no trail, no horses, no Vell's Landing, no Order, no pyres, no Corvane — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, what the Order had actually done all those centuries ago. Not destroyed a country. *Deleted the witnesses.* We had not burned the gods' kingdom. We had burned everyone who could grieve it, and then burned the grief, and then burned the word for grief, until the smoke itself was forgotten, and called the silence peace.

I have sung the hymn at Corvane. I want that on the record. I sang it with everyone else.

"Ravelle." Ilsa's hand on my arm. Her face was pale but steady, a woman standing in the wreckage of her father's life's work and finding it was true. "Say it back. Say my name. Keep the rope."

"Ilsa Vane," I said, and her shoulders settled, and I kept saying it, walking, like a psalm, like a penance, and perhaps it was both.

Ahead, the spire caught the last of the light and threw it back white as salt.

And far off, at the edge of hearing — beneath the wind, beneath the fragments crowding in Sebastian's skull, beneath nine hundred years of enforced silence — something in that country heard us arrive, and turned over in its sleep, and began, very slowly, to wake.
