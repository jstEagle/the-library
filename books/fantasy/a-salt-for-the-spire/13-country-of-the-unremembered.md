## 13. Country of the Unremembered

The border didn't announce itself. That was the first thing I noticed, and it bothered me more than I let on.

You'd expect something. A shudder, a sound, a line drawn in the dirt like the salt lines back home — those at least had the decency to burn your skin if you crossed them drunk. But this was nothing. One step we were on a plain that made my eyes slide off it like water off oiled leather, and the next step there was grass under my boots, real grass, knee-high and going to seed, and a road under the grass so old that cart wheels had worn it into two parallel trenches that filled with wildflowers.

"Stop looking for the edge," Ilsa said without turning around. She had her father's charts spread across a board strapped to her chest like armor, checking our bearing against landmarks that shouldn't exist anymore. "There isn't one. Not from inside. From in here, everything is exactly where it always was."

"That's worse," Ravelle said behind me. She'd been quiet since the border, quiet in the way a dam is quiet. "A prison should have walls."

"It has walls," I said. "We just walked through them sideways."

Nobody laughed. Fair enough. My jokes had been getting worse the further east we went, which Ilsa said was because I was scared, and which I said was because she was bad company, and which we both knew was because I was scared.

The road climbed through hills that had no business being so green. Everything here was overgrown but not abandoned — that was the strange part. Abandoned places have a smell. Rot and dust and animals moving in. This smelled like woodsmoke. Somewhere ahead of us, someone was baking bread.

"People live here," Ashen said, from wherever gods keep their voices when they're not using their mouths. In my head, mostly. In my head, always. Three weeks of it and I still flinched. "Of course they live here. We were never unmade, thief. Only unforgotten—"

"Unremembered," I corrected. I'd stopped being polite about it around day nine.

"—unremembered," the god conceded, with the enormous patience of something that had waited centuries in the dark and could wait three more minutes while a mortal got its terminology straight. "Erasure takes the memory of a thing from the world outside. It does not take the thing. The Order never understood that. They thought forgetting was killing. It was only locking the door with the country on the wrong side of it."

"And everyone in it."

"And everyone in it," Ashen agreed. "My worshippers. My children. Sealed in with us." A pause, and when the voice came again it had that roughness in it that I'd learned meant the god was feeling something too large to say plainly. "I have heard them praying at my door for four hundred years. I could do nothing. Nothing. Do you understand what that is, thief? To be a god and to be less than a beggar?"

"I'm a thief," I said. "I understand taking. The other thing's new to me."

It didn't laugh. Gods don't, mostly. But I felt something shift in the back of my skull that might have been the closest it could come.

* * *

We crested the last hill at dusk, and there it was.

A city. Or the bones of one wearing a city's clothes. Walls of pale stone, cracked and mended and cracked again, patched with timber and thatch where the old work had failed. Towers that leaned like tired men. And smoke — dozens of threads of it rising from chimneys, from cookfires, from forge-smelters working late. Terraced fields climbed the lower slopes. Goats grazed on what had clearly once been a temple plaza. Laundry hung between broken columns like bunting after a festival nobody remembered the reason for.

They hadn't rebuilt it. They'd *kept* it. There's a difference, and you can see it if you look — the way a family keeps a dead father's coat, wearing it, patching it, letting the sleeves go short rather than replace it. Four hundred years of keeping.

"They think the world ended," Ravelle said softly. She'd gone pale under her travel-stained collar. "Don't they? Look at the temples. They're maintained. All of them. They think the gods are sleeping and the world outside is..."

"Gone," Ilsa finished. Her voice did something complicated. I'd seen her handle grief over her father's charts, over burned villages, over Cade's trail of ash. This was different. This was personal. Her family had drawn these streets. "Or maybe they know. Maybe this is just what hope looks like after four hundred years of it not working."

"We should go down," I said. "Carefully. As travelers."

"As what?" Ravelle's hand went to the medallion at her throat — the Closed Door, silver, worn smooth by generations of Inquisitor thumbs. Old habit. She caught herself doing it and dropped her hand like the thing had bitten her. "Sebastian. If they learn what the Order did—"

"Then they learn," I said. "Not from us. Not tonight. Tonight we're pilgrims come to see the sleeping gods. Which, conveniently, is even true."

Ashen stirred, pleased, like a cat settling. *Good,* it murmured. *Go among them. They will know me in you.*

*That's what worries me,* I didn't say out loud, because saying things out loud to a god is how you end up agreeing to things.

* * *

The gate guards were two teenagers and a dog, and the dog was the professional of the outfit.

They wore boiled leather over homespun, spears that had been sharpened so many times the heads were narrow as fingers, and expressions of profound suspicion that lasted right up until Ilsa unrolled a corner of one of her father's charts and showed them a drawing of their own gate, done four hundred years ago, accurate down to the crack in the left-hand tower.

The older guard — sixteen, maybe, with a burn scar down one arm — touched the parchment like it might dissolve. "Where did you get this?"

"My grandfather's grandfather drew it," Ilsa said. "In the time before."

"There was no time before," the guard said automatically, the way you'd recite a lesson. Then he looked at the chart again, and at his own tower, and I watched the two facts grind against each other behind his eyes and lose. "There's no time before."

"No," Ilsa agreed gently. "But somebody drew your gate anyway."

That's the thing about erasure that nobody talks about. It doesn't leave a hole in people. People aren't built that way. You take away their history and they grow *around* the absence, the way a tree grows around a fence wire, until the wire's invisible and the tree is just shaped wrong and nobody knows why. These kids had songs about a war with no enemy named. Holidays whose purpose was "old custom." Prayers to gods described as "the Sleepers below the Spire" with rituals intact and theology missing entirely.

They gave us bread and barley stew in the gatehouse and asked where we'd come from, and we told them "over the western hills," which was true in the way that matters, and they nodded, because over the western hills was where the world ended, everyone knew that, the same way everyone knows the sky is up.

An old woman came by with the evening ration of lamp oil and paused at the sight of me. Just me. She looked at my face for a long moment, and then she pressed her oil-flask into my hands and said, "For the Spire, traveler. When you go up. Everyone gives something."

"I'm not—" I started.

"You'll go up," she said, certain as weather. "Everyone comes here to go up, eventually. Even those who say they won't." And she shuffled off before I could ask what she meant, leaving me holding a flask of oil worth maybe a copper, feeling like I'd been handed something considerably heavier.

*She smelled me,* Ashen said, smug as sin. *The vessel-scent. They've prayed to me through four hundred years of silence, little thief. Their prayers soaked into the stone. Some of them would know my coming the way dogs know storms.*

*I'm not a storm.*

*No,* the god said, and there was something almost tender in it, which was somehow much worse. *You are the door I knock on.*

* * *

We found lodging above a tavern called the Sleeping King, which Ilsa found hilarious and Ravelle found blasphemous and I found cheap, which settled the argument. The common room was full of the kind of people you find in any border town anywhere: farmers drinking their earnings, a smith with burned forearms, two women arguing cheerfully about grain prices, children underfoot stealing bread crusts with the practiced speed of professionals I couldn't help but respect.

Normal. That was the horror of it, I decided somewhere around the second cup of thin beer. It was all so completely normal. These people loved and cheated and grieved and got drunk on festival nights and buried their parents and taught their children prayers, and every single thing they were had been built inside a locked room they didn't know was locked. The Order hadn't killed them. Killing would have been kinder and cheaper. The Order had just... closed the book on them, mid-sentence, and left them living in it.

"You're staring," Ravelle said.

"I'm inventorying," I said. "Old habit. You look at a room and count what's worth taking. Except in here everything's already been taken. The whole country. Somebody got here first and lifted an entire nation and left the people standing in the empty space where it used to be."

Ravelle turned her cup slowly on the table. She'd been doing that a lot lately — handling small objects carefully, like she was relearning how much weight things had. "When I took my vows," she said, "they taught us the Erasure as mercy. The old gods' power was rotting the world. The country that housed them was beyond saving. So the Order closed the door and spared the world the cost of burning it." She looked up. "Standing in this room, Sebastian, I keep thinking: mercy for whom?"

"For the ones who got to forget," I said.

"Yes," she said. "That's what I keep thinking."

Across the room, the tavern keeper's daughter — eight years old, gap-toothed, fearless — had climbed onto a stool to sing the evening song. Every head bowed. Even the dog lay down. It was a lullaby, I realized. A lullaby for gods. *Sleep well, sleep deep, beneath the stone, / the Spire stands watch, you're not alone.* Four hundred years of mothers singing their children to sleep with a prayer that the sleepers stay asleep, and meaning it kindly. Meaning it as love.

Ilsa's hand found mine under the table. I don't know when she'd decided we were hand-holding people. Somewhere between the burned village and the border, apparently. Her palm was calloused from map tubes and pen knives and it was the most honest thing I'd touched in years.

"They're happy," she whispered. "Look at them. They're actually happy."

"Yeah," I said. "That's the part the Order will never forgive them for."

* * *

I went up to the Spire the next morning. Alone, or as alone as a man gets with a god in his skull.

The city parted around me as I walked. Not dramatically — no crowds falling to their knees, no bells. Just small turnings of heads, just old women pressing charms into my hands, just a baker setting a loaf on a shrine niche as I passed and saying, "for the climb, blessed one," with the easy certainty of a man feeding a stray cat that had wandered into his shop. By the time I reached the base of the Spire I was carrying bread, oil, a woven bracelet, three copper coins, and a dried flower, and I had given away nothing, and I understood suddenly that I was carrying other people's hopes again, and that I had been doing it my whole life, and that this time I hadn't even had to steal them.

The Spire rose white and seam-less out of the hill at the city's heart, taller than it had any right to be, its stone uncracked in a country of cracks. No door. Of course no door. Doors are for things that open.

At the base, set into the living rock, was a basin of black stone, worn concave by centuries of offerings. Coins, flowers, hair, teeth, letters written in a script Ilsa's charts had taught me to half-read. Prayers, all of it. Four hundred years of prayers, pooled and layered like sediment.

And the moment my shadow fell across the basin, every prayer in it woke up.

I don't know how else to say it. I've carried stolen memories for fifteen years; I know what other people's lives feel like when they sit in your head. This was that, multiplied past counting — thousands of voices, all speaking at once, all of them *to* the same address, and the address was me. A farmer praying for rain in a year of drought. A mother praying over a fevered child. A girl praying that the boy at the mill would look at her. A dying man praying simply *let it not hurt, let it not hurt, let it not hurt.* Layer on layer on layer, patient and hopeless and faithful, four hundred years deep.

They weren't answered. That was the unbearable part. They'd never been answered. Every prayer ever offered at this stone had gone into the dark and stayed there, and the people knew it, and they kept praying anyway, generation after generation, because that's what love does when there's nothing else left to do with itself.

*Now you understand,* Ashen said, very quietly.

"I understand," I said, and my voice came out wrecked. "You want me to open the door so you can answer them."

*I want to answer them,* the god said. *What I want and what opening the door brings are not, I have learned, the same thing. But yes. Thief. Vessel. Door-that-knocks. I want it more than I have ever wanted anything, and I have wanted things since before your valley had a name.*

Behind me, gravel shifted. I turned, and there was a man standing at the edge of the plaza — old, straight-backed, dressed in robes that had been magnificent four centuries ago and were now kept the way the city kept everything, patched and honored and going threadbare. He wore a crown. An actual crown, iron and tarnished silver, sitting on his head like it had grown there.

He looked at me the way the old woman with the oil had looked at me. Recognition. But deeper. Older.

"You," he said, and his voice cracked like dry wood. "You're carrying him. I can hear him in you. After all this time — you're carrying *him*."

"Ashen-Who-Waits," I said. "Yes."

The old king — and I knew, I knew from the crown and the way the whole plaza seemed to orient around him like iron filings, that's what he was — the old king came closer, slowly, leaning on a staff of black stone that matched the basin. He stopped an arm's length away and studied my face with eyes that had watched four hundred years of unanswered prayers.

"Boy," he said gently, terribly. "Whatever he has promised you. Whatever he has shown you. Come inside, and let me tell you what my gods' love did to me. Then decide."

And behind my eyes, for the first time in three weeks, Ashen-Who-Waits said nothing at all.
