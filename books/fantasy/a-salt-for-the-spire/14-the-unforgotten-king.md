## 14. The Unforgotten King

I have spent my whole life around maps, so let me tell you the first thing every cartographer learns: a map is a lie you agree to believe. Every line is a negotiation between what is and what fits on the page. My father used to say that the honest ones are the blank spaces — at least they admit what they don't know.

He never drew a blank space. He drew Vhalen. All of it, coastlines and rivers and the capital with its spire like a needle through the world's heart, in ink that the Order later burned out of every atlas in existence. They missed one copy. It was sewn into the lining of my mother's winter coat, which is how I came to inherit a country that officially has never existed.

I mention this because when we finally stood in front of the Unforgotten King — the actual man, breathing, ancient, wearing a crown of black iron that looked like it had been grown rather than forged — the first thing I felt was not awe.

It was professional offense.

Because he was standing in a throne room my father had drawn from rumor and secondhand accounts, and he was standing in the wrong place. The throne was supposed to be against the eastern wall. This one sat in the center of the hall, on a low dais of cracked marble, and behind it the great windows my father had rendered as rose glass were simply... gone. Bricked over. As if someone had decided that looking outside was a habit worth breaking.

"You're staring," Sebastian murmured beside me.

"I'm comparing," I said. "He's standing where the floor mosaic should be."

"Maybe he likes the view."

"There is no view. There's a wall."

"That's what I mean."

Ravelle cleared her throat the way she did when she wanted us to remember she was an Inquisitor and not just a woman who complained about her boots like the rest of us. She stepped forward, hand nowhere near her sword, which for Ravelle was practically a bow.

"Your Majesty," she said. "We've come about the door."

The king looked at her for a long time. He was not what I expected, though I couldn't have told you what I expected — dust, probably, or a ghost, or nothing at all. Instead there was a man of perhaps sixty, tall, broad through the shoulders, with a face like a coastline that had been argued with by weather for centuries. His eyes were the problem. They were too clear. Everyone else in this forgotten country moved through their days wrapped in a kind of woolly patience, but this man saw everything, all at once, and it had clearly been happening for so long that he'd stopped flinching from it.

"I know why you've come," he said. "You've come because something whispered to you across the salt, and the whisper had a name, and now you think the name is a door." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was kind. "Sit down. All of you. What I have to say takes longer than standing legs want to hear."

There were benches along the walls, stone ones, worn smooth by generations of courtiers who no longer existed. We sat. Sebastian sat closest, because Sebastian sits closest to everything dangerous; it's his one consistent trait, this idea that if he can pick its pocket it can't hurt him. Ashen's voice wasn't speaking just then — the god went quiet whenever we entered places where it had once been worshipped, like a man returning to a house he'd burned — but I could see it in Sebastian's face anyway, that listening look, head tilted toward a sound the rest of us couldn't hear.

"The last time strangers walked into this hall," the king began, "they came to seal the door. There were nine of them. Mages, soldiers, one very stubborn priestess whose name the Order kept and whose face they erased, because even then they understood that martyrs are more useful without features." He folded his hands. "They succeeded. You are living in the success. I want you to understand that before anything else. Everything wrong with your world — the rot, the corruption, the gods' power souring everything it touched — ended on the day they closed it. Nine people bought your whole world with their lives, and you have come here to discuss whether or not to spend it again."

"We've come to *see*," Ravelle said carefully. "To learn the truth before—"

"No one comes here to see." The king's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "Pilgrims came here to see, once, when the gods still answered. Conquerors came to see. The Order came to see, before they decided seeing was the problem. Everyone who arrives at that door believes they will be the one to open it *correctly*. To take only a little. To use it wisely." He turned those too-clear eyes on each of us in turn. "I believed it longest of anyone. Let me tell you what believing cost."

* * *

He told it plainly, which somehow made it worse. Kings, I have decided, should not be allowed to be plain. It strips away all the ceremony you can hide behind.

His name — his true name, the one erased along with his country — was Aldric Vane. He had been crowned young, in a golden age, when the gods of Vhalen walked close enough to touch. And they were generous. That was the thing no one outside ever understood, the thing the Order's histories got exactly backwards: the gods were not cruel. Cruelty would have been simple. You can fight cruelty. The gods were *generous*, endlessly, indiscriminately, the way a flood is generous to everything at the same elevation.

"They gave," Aldric said. "That was their nature. Ask for rain, receive rain. Ask for strength, receive strength. Ask for your dead mother's voice one more time, and hear it, warm as life, in your own kitchen." His hands tightened on the arms of the throne. "And every gift took root. That is what worship does, when the worshipped answer. The rain doesn't stop. The strength grows past the bone. The voice in the kitchen stays, and stays, until the children won't enter the house. Power is not a coin you spend. It is a seed, and it grows according to its own nature, and its nature is to grow until there is nothing left that isn't it."

He showed us. I wish he hadn't. He rolled back his sleeves, slowly, the way a man shows old scars, except these weren't scars. His forearms, below the elbow, were veined in gold — actual gold, bright metal threading through skin like ore through rock, glinting when he moved. Where it pooled thickest, at his wrists, the skin had gone smooth and hard and faintly luminous, like gilded wood.

"Three hundred years of prayer," he said. "My people had nothing left to give the gods but themselves, so they gave me. A king is a vessel a nation already believes in. Every prayer for protection, for wisdom, for endurance — it all came here, because I was the shape their faith fit best." He let the sleeve fall. "I have not slept since before your Order existed. I do not hunger. I do not age. I am the most beloved man who ever lived, and I am made entirely of other people's wanting, and there is no part of me left that wanted anything first."

Sebastian shifted beside me. I knew that shift. It was the shift of a man recognizing his own trade done at scale — a thief hearing about a bigger vault. But there was no envy in his face. Only a kind of horrified recognition, the look of a man realizing the gold he'd been stealing was someone's teeth.

"The nine who sealed the door," Ravelle said quietly, "were they—"

"Rotting? Some of them. The priestess had stopped bleeding years before. Her wounds had filled with light instead." Aldric almost laughed. "Yes, Inquisitor. Your Order's founders were not clean saints descending on a wicked land. They were dying people, corrupted by the gifts they'd accepted, doing the one thing left that made sense. They sealed the door because the door was killing them, and everyone they loved, and eventually it would have killed everything. The sealing wasn't righteousness. It was triage."

"And the country?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Why erase us? Erase *it*?"

"Because a sealed door can be unsealed, but a forgotten door cannot even be found." He looked at me then, really looked, and I felt it like a draft under a door. "Your father drew this country, girl. So did hundreds of others. The Order understood that maps are prayers too — that as long as Vhalen was remembered, it remained a place, and a place with a door in it is an invitation. So they burned the maps and the memories and the names, and they left the people inside to keep each other company." Something moved behind the too-clear eyes, some vast slow grief that had had three hundred years to get comfortable. "We were not punished. We were *amputated*. And I have never decided which mercy is worse."

* * *

Afterward — after the history, after the gold-veined proof of it — he begged. That's the part I keep turning over. A king, begging, and not for himself.

We stood in the ruined courtyard while he walked among us, and I watched him decide how to say it, and I realized he had said it before. To others. Pilgrims, maybe, or dreamers who'd found the seam in the forgetting. How many had he turned away? How many had listened?

"You carry a god's whisper," he said to Sebastian. Not a question. "I can smell it on you. Old divinity leaves a residue, like smoke in cloth."

"It talks," Sebastian admitted. "Mostly it asks for things."

"They always ask for things. Even sealed, even sleeping, even reduced to a voice in a thief's skull — they ask." Aldric stopped in front of him. "And what does yours ask for?"

Sebastian's jaw worked. "Its name. The rest of its name. It says the door holds the pieces."

"And it has promised you something. They always promise. Purpose, or power, or absolution — whatever the vessel is starving for." Aldric's gaze flicked to me, to Ravelle, back. "Look at me. Look at what three hundred years of answered prayer made. Now imagine it awake. Imagine it *pouring*. Because that is what opening the door means — not letting one god out, but letting the whole rotten generosity of them back into a world that has forgotten how to refuse a gift." He spread his gilded hands. "I am the argument, sirs. Madam. Thief. Mapmaker. I am the entire argument, walking and talking and unable to die. Your church burned my country to keep this from happening, and I am telling you they were right, and I know what it costs a devout woman to hear that."

Ravelle's face did something complicated. I'd seen her doubt before — small cracks, hairline fractures — but this was different. This was a founder's daughter being told the foundation held. She looked like a woman whose whole life had been aimed at a target and had just been told the target was real.

"Then help us destroy it," she said. "Permanently. If the door is poison, we don't seal it again — we end it."

Aldric was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved through the courtyard, carrying the smell of the sleeping city beyond — bread, somewhere, and stone dust, and underneath it all that faint sweetish scent I'd noticed since we crossed the border, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. Worship has a smell. Nobody warns you.

"You cannot destroy it," he said finally. "The nine tried. The door is not a thing; it is a wound in the world, and wounds close or they scar, and this one can only scar. Sealing is the best that has ever been done, and sealing requires—" He stopped. His eyes went to Sebastian, and something in his expression made my stomach drop, because it wasn't pity exactly. It was arithmetic.

"Requires what?" I asked.

"A vessel," Aldric said. "Willing. Full. Someone who carries enough taken-things to pour into the threshold." He paused, and the kindness in his face was the cruelest thing about him. "I begin to suspect, thief, that you were not sent here. I begin to suspect you were *made* for this, long ago, by people who knew what the sealing costs — and that the god riding your shoulder knows it too, and wants the door opened before you work that out."

Sebastian went very still. Stillness, on Sebastian, is louder than shouting.

"And you?" he said. "What do you want, Majesty? Truly?"

Aldric Vane, the Unforgotten King, the most beloved man who ever lived, looked up at the bricked-over windows where the sky should have been.

"To rest," he said. "I want to rest, and I cannot, because my people's prayers hold me upright like wires hold a puppet, and they will pray until the world ends or the gods wake, whichever comes first." He lowered his gaze. "So no, I will not help you open the door. But understand what I am telling you: I will not help you save it either. Whatever you choose at that threshold, thief — I will watch, and I will grieve, and I will do nothing. That is the full extent of what three hundred years of love has left me able to give."

* * *

We made camp that night in the shadow of the spire itself — the needle my father had drawn, closer now, close enough that I could see it was less a tower than a wound given architecture, the stone around it warped like heat-shimmer frozen mid-bend. The moon hung above it, fat and pale, one turn short of full.

Thirteen turns. One remaining.

I sat sharpening my surveyor's knives — habit, mostly; you don't fight gods with a blade meant for trimming vellum — and found Sebastian at the edge of the firelight, staring at the spire with that listening tilt to his head.

"She's quiet," he said, before I could ask. "Ashen. Hasn't said a word since the throne room."

"Maybe the king scared it."

"Maybe." He turned his hands over, studying his palms like a man checking for gold veins. "Or maybe it's said everything it needs to, and now it's waiting to see if I'm stupid enough to volunteer."

"You're not stupid," I said. "You're a thief. Thieves case the job first."

He laughed — short, hollow, real. "Yeah. Well." He looked at me then, and there was something naked in it, something that made me want to look away and made me not look away. "Ilsa. If it turns out the king's right — if it turns out somebody built me for this, back before I had a name anyone remembers —"

"Then we'll steal you back," I said. "It's what we do."

He smiled at that, small and crooked, and for a moment the firelight made him look like any tired man on any road, and I let myself believe it.

Above the spire, the moon slid a fraction further along its impossible thirteenth arc, and somewhere in the dark of the camp, Ravelle began, very softly, to pray — and stopped herself halfway through the first word, hand pressed over her mouth, like a woman catching a disease in the act of leaving her lips.
