## 15. Cade at the Gate

I had written the report myself. That was the thing I could not stop circling back to, like a tongue probing a broken tooth. Somewhere on the road out of Vell's Landing, in an inn with bad wine and honest beds, I had sat down with the Order's cipher paper and recorded, in my own careful hand, every detail of how we had crossed the border that wasn't there: Ilsa's charts, the angle of the moonlight, Sebastian's stolen fragments whispered aloud while he sweated and shook. I had done it because it was procedure. Because the Order required documentation of all field operations. Because some part of me — the devout part, the part that had knelt on cold stone floors for thirty years — believed that what we were doing was holy work, and holy work deserved to be witnessed.

Brother Cade read my report the way a butcher reads a ledger of livestock.

I watched him do it. That was the worst of it, or near enough. We had been three days inside the erased country, walking among people who remembered nothing of us and everything of their gods, when the horizon to the east grew a smudge, and the smudge grew banners, and the banners grew teeth.

"They used your route," Ilsa said beside me, her voice flat as one of her unmarked maps. "Your crossing point. To the degree."

"I know."

"Your cipher."

"Cade holds the second key. It's standard." My voice sounded reasonable. Reasonable was all I had left, so I rationed it carefully.

Sebastian stood a little apart from us, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who has just discovered the lock he picked was rigged. "So let me make sure I have this straight," he said. "You reported to your Order. Your Order sent its worst. And its worst is now marching on a country full of people who never did anything except be forgotten by the right paperwork."

"That's a simplification."

"It usually is," he said. "That's how you people get away with things."

I wanted to argue. I want you to understand that — I reached for the argument the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood, the old familiar shapes of it: *the greater good*, *the containment doctrine*, *a door this dangerous cannot be left standing*. But the words came up dry, because down the slope, past the terraced fields where the unremembered were harvesting grain under a sun that shouldn't have existed, Cade's column had halted, and Cade himself had ridden forward alone, white cloak immaculate against the brown earth, and there was something in the way he moved that I had never seen in thirty years of shared devotion.

He wasn't riding like a man coming to destroy something.

He was riding like a man coming to collect.

* * *

We met him at the boundary stones — the old markers Ilsa's father had drawn, worn smooth, half-swallowed by grass that had no business growing there. Cade dismounted with the unhurried grace of a man who has already won. Behind him, his zealots fanned out in perfect formation, forty of them, each carrying not the fire-irons of the Order's burning crews but something else: long silver rods, capped with hooks of black glass. I knew those rods. Every Inquisitor did. They were called *takers* officially, though the older name, the one from before the Reformation, still surfaced in the archive margins sometimes.

Harvest-staves. Instruments for drawing power out of a vessel without killing it. The Order had declared them heretical two centuries ago.

The Order had apparently undeclared them.

"Sister Ravelle." Cade smiled. He had a good smile; it had converted more heretics than his sermons. "You look well. The air agrees with you."

"You're here to destroy the door," I said. "Say it plainly, Brother, so I can hear whether you believe it."

His smile didn't falter, which was itself an answer. "Destroy it? No." He turned, gesturing with one gloved hand at the spire rising beyond the fields — the pale tower at the country's heart, visible even here, the way a wound is visible through cloth. "Do you know why the Order sealed this place, Ravelle? Not the story they give novices. The real reason."

"The power corrupted everything it touched. The kings went mad. The land—"

"The land *bloomed*," Cade said softly. "Yes. It rotted, too. Both are true. But here is what the archives don't print, Sister, because I've read them — all of them, including the sealed folios you were never cleared for." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to the register he used for confessions. "Power doesn't corrupt indiscriminately. It pools. It goes where it's invited. A king who worships becomes a god's puppet. But a man who *takes*—" his eyes glittered, "-a man who takes with the proper instruments, the proper rites, the proper discipline — he isn't corrupted. He's *armed*."

The world went very quiet around me. Even the wind seemed to lean in and listen.

"You mean to take it yourself," I said. "All of it."

"I mean to take it for the Order." He spread his hands, magnanimous, and I understood suddenly how he had risen so fast, how he had gathered forty zealots who would follow him into erasure itself. Men don't follow ascetics. They follow men who promise them the sun. "Think of it, Ravelle. An Order that no longer needs to burn witches because it can *out-magic* them. An Order that never again loses a village to plague or raiders because it holds the source. We have spent nine generations amputating the divine from the world because we feared infection. I am proposing we take the limb back and use the hand."

"And the people here?"

"What people?" He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness broke something in me that the burning crews never had. "Sister. Look at them. They're echoes. A country that exists because a god's dream hasn't quite finished dissolving. When we take the power, the dream ends. That isn't murder. You can't murder a memory."

Behind me, I heard Ilsa breathe in sharp and slow, the way she did before she said something unforgivable. Sebastian's hand found her elbow. Small mercy.

"You wrote your reports faithfully," Cade said to me, and now the smile warmed, became almost paternal, and I hated him for it, hated him with a purity I had previously reserved only for heretics. "Every crossing detail. Every fragment the thief recited. Do you know why I requested you for this operation, Ravelle? Why I asked for *you* by name?" He leaned in. "Because you doubt. Doubters write precise reports. Believers embellish. I needed precision. And I needed a sister whose faith would keep her walking forward right up until the moment it no longer mattered."

* * *

They took the spire by nightfall.

There was no battle worthy of the word. The unremembered fought — I will grant them that, and I will carry the memory of their fighting to whatever grave accepts me. Farmers with scythes and old spears, led by their king, that terrible beautiful ruin of a man who had begged us to leave the door shut and been ignored by everyone, ourselves included. They broke against Cade's line like water on stone, and the harvest-staves drank something out of the air as they fell — out of *them* — and I saw the staves' black glass hooks begin to glow faintly, greedily, and I understood that Cade's men weren't even killing these people so much as *harvesting* them, drinking down the divinity soaked into every forgotten soul.

I stood on the ridge above the fields and I did nothing.

I want that recorded honestly, since honesty seems to be the discipline of my final days. I stood on the ridge with a sword at my hip and a rank at my collar and I did nothing, because my body had finally caught up with what my mind had known since the boundary stones: that everything I had served for thirty years was not a church but an appetite that had learned to wear vestments.

It was Sebastian who moved first. Of course it was. Thieves always understand institutions better than the faithful do; they recognize the architecture.

"Well," he said, appearing at my shoulder with Ilsa behind him, both of them streaked with ash and other people's blood, "your Order's about to become the thing it spent nine centuries hunting. Any chance that bothers you enough to pick a side, or should I start planning around you?"

I looked down at the spire. At its base, Cade's men were dragging the king's loyalists into rows, and the black glass hooks were glowing brighter, and somewhere up in that pale tower, behind a door that had waited thirteen turns of the moon for this exact corruption of the calendar, an old god named Ashen-Who-Waits was waiting still — and I found myself wondering, with a thief's cynicism I seemed to be catching, whether the god had planned for this too. Whether everyone had planned for this except me.

"Sister," Ilsa said. Not unkindly. That was somehow worse than contempt. "Ravelle. Look at me."

I looked at her. A mapmaker's daughter with ink stains she'd stopped trying to scrub off, holding a sword she barely knew how to use, asking nothing of me except that I exist in the same direction she does.

"My faith kept me walking forward," I said. "Cade told me that. He thought it was a compliment."

"And now?"

"Now I'd like to find out what's underneath it."

Sebastian grinned — that quick, crooked grin that made him look twelve years old and dangerous — and jerked his chin toward the spire, where torches were being lit along the walls, where the rite had already begun, where the sky above the tower had started doing something the sky should not do: turning the wrong color, a bruised gold, the color of the pages in Ilsa's oldest charts.

"There's a back way," he said. "There's always a back way. I'm a thief; trust me on this."

And so the three of us — an apostate, a mapmaker, and a memory-thief, the entire congregation I had left — went down toward the burning-gold tower together, while behind us the last free bells of a forgotten country rang out across the fields, calling a god to wake and a zealot to feast, and I understood at last that the door had never really been locked against the world outside.

It had been locked against what the world outside would do if it ever got in.

Cade had gotten in.

God forgive us all — and if none are left listening, then let this chapter of my life stand as the confession instead.
