## 16. What Sebastian Stole

There is a rule in my trade that every thief learns before he learns anything else: never steal from yourself. It sounds like philosophy, but it's really just arithmetic. A memory you take from your own head leaves a hole exactly its size, and holes in your own past don't sit quiet like other people's do. They itch. They fester. They fill up with things you invent and call them true.

I'd broken that rule once, back when I was green enough to think rules were suggestions. I'd gone digging into my own childhood looking for — I don't even remember what. That was the point. I didn't find it. What I found instead was a wall.

Every thief knows walls. They're what people build around the memories they can't bear to look at. Grief gets walled. Shame gets walled. The night your father beat you and the night your mother left get bricked over together into one gray slab of nothing, and most of us live our whole lives walking past that slab without ever pressing our palms against it.

But mine wasn't grief-shaped. Mine wasn't shame-shaped either. Mine had seams.

Walls made by pain are rough. They're made by the person doing the suffering, and hands shake when they're hurting. Walls made by another thief are smooth. Professional. And mine had been mortared with something I recognized the way you recognize your own signature on a confession you don't remember signing.

Salt-work. Valley technique. Somebody had been inside my head with the same tools I used on dying merchants, and they hadn't been clumsy about it.

I told no one this for eleven years.

* * *

Now I sat in the ruins of a chapel in the country that did not exist, across from a king who could not die, and I told everyone at once.

"Show me," I said.

The Unforgotten King — Aldous, he insisted we call him, because titles were for men with countries and his had been eaten by forgetting — folded his hands on his knees. He had the patience of someone who has outlived every argument he ever cared to win. "Are you certain? What's behind a wall like that rarely comes through gently."

"I've been carrying it since I was nine years old," I said. "Whatever it is, I've been paying rent on it my whole life. I want to see what I own."

Ilsa put her hand on my shoulder. She'd been doing that more lately, and I'd stopped pretending I minded. Ravelle stood by the broken window with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore instead of prayer. Ashen — who lived in my skull these days like an unpaid lodger — said nothing at all, which should have worried me more than it did.

The king reached out and touched two fingers to my temple, the old way, the polite way, the way thieves in Vell's Landing never bothered with because politeness costs time and time is coin.

And the wall came down like it had been waiting for permission.

* * *

Here is what I remembered.

A kitchen. Not a rich one, not a poor one — a working one, copper pots and flour dust hanging in the light from a high window, the smell of bread and underneath the bread something else, something floral and strange, salt-flowers, the flowers that grow only where the land remembers being sea. A woman singing. Her voice was low and off-key and absolutely certain, the way voices are when they sing for themselves and not for an audience.

My mother. I knew her the way you know a face seen once across a crowded market — instantly, totally, and with the knowledge that the seeing would end.

She was humming a song about a door.

That's the detail that broke me, later, when I had time to break. Not the fear. Not the running. There was some of both in there — I'll get to it — but the thing my mind had chosen to keep, the pearl it had wrapped itself around when everything else was taken, was a half-remembered lullaby about a door that must stay shut, sung by a woman with flour on her hands.

Then the wall's second layer gave way, and I got everything.

* * *

The city had a name. That was the first shock — that it had ever had a name at all. Corivenne. The capital. I'd walked through its ghost three days ago and felt nothing but the cold of dead stone, and here it was alive: bells, market-cries, the smell of the river, banners in colors I couldn't name because their names had been scrubbed out of every tongue outside this border and apparently out of me too.

I was four years old in the memory. Maybe five. Small enough that the world arrived at knee height, all hems and boots and the undersides of tables. My mother ran a bakery two streets from the cathedral of the sleeping gods, and I knew — with the strange total certainty children have about the geography of love — that she sang to the gods while she worked, quietly, so the priests wouldn't hear. Not worship. Just conversation. The way you talk to an old friend who sleeps a lot.

"They dream," she said once, kneeling to wipe flour off my cheek. "Did you know that, little bird? Even asleep, they dream. And sometimes, if you're very quiet, you can hear what they're dreaming about." She tapped my chest, right over my heart. "It sounds like this."

I heard it, in the memory, as I heard it then: a sound under the floor of the world. Slow. Patient. Like breathing held for a thousand years.

Then the dreams changed.

I can't describe it better than the child experienced it, which is to say badly and all at once. The under-sound stuttered. One morning the bread wouldn't rise. Another morning it rose too much, climbing out of the pans like something escaping, and my mother threw all of it into the street and burned the starter and didn't sing for six days. People in the market were crying without knowing why. A man drowned in a rain barrel — in two feet of water, in a city where it hadn't rained in a month. The gods were dreaming louder, and their dreams were leaking, and everything they touched went wrong in ways that were worse than wrong, that were *wronger*, that bent.

My mother started packing at night. I remember the bags under the bed. I remember asking where we were going and her saying "somewhere the gods can't follow" and laughing in a way that wasn't laughter, and I remember — gods help me, I remember this with my adult mind laid over my child eyes now that the wall was down — I remember thinking she was lying.

Because there was nowhere the gods couldn't follow. Everyone knew that. Except.

Except the valley. Except the salt.

* * *

They crossed at night. My mother, me, and maybe thirty others, guided by a man whose face the memory refused to hold — not blurred, *refused*, the way a witness refuses to name an accomplice. Salt lines burned blue-white as we stepped over them, and I felt the under-sound vanish, cut off like a door closing, and my mother wept with relief and grief in equal measure, holding me so tight my ribs ached.

We were going to make a life in the smuggler cities. She had a plan. She had a cousin in Vell's Landing. She had my hand in hers.

And then, on the third night, in a wayhouse outside a town whose name meant nothing to me, the man with the unfaced companion came to our room.

What happened next, I watched from inside a five-year-old's body with an adult's understanding strapped on like borrowed armor, and I would give a great deal to have been able to look away. You cannot look away from your own memories. That's the cruelty of the trade. Other people's pasts, you can set down. Your own, you wear.

He took my mother's memories first. All of them. Every year, every hour, the bakery, the songs, the bags under the bed, me — he took her entire self out through her temple in long silver threads, and she sat very still while he did it because he had promised her that if she stayed still, I would be left whole.

She bargained. That was the part that undid me. Not screaming, not fighting — bargaining, in a whisper, while her self was pulled out of her strand by strand: *Take mine. Take all of mine. Leave him his. He's small. His memories weigh almost nothing.*

And the man — the unfaced man, the professional, and I knew his work the moment I understood what I was seeing, I had *revered* his work my whole career without knowing whose hands I admired — the man considered, and agreed, and lied.

He left me one memory. The kitchen. The song. The door. Enough that I'd always feel like something was missing without ever knowing what. A wound dressed as an heirloom.

Why? I've turned it over a hundred times since the king's fingers left my temple, and the answer keeps arriving the same way, cold and simple: because a vessel needs bait. Because somewhere in the design — someone's design — a boy was needed who carried a fragment of Corivenne in his chest like a splinter, growing up in the one place magic couldn't reach him, until the day came when the fragments mattered and someone came to collect.

Me. I was the splinter. I was the bait. My whole life — the orphanage, the streets, the apprenticeship to a memory-thief whose techniques matched the ones in my own skull down to the smallest habit — none of it was a life. It was an incubation period.

* * *

When I opened my eyes, the chapel was quiet. Ilsa still had her hand on my shoulder; I don't know how long. Ravelle had uncrossed her arms at some point. Even the king looked away first, which for a man who has watched centuries pass is saying something.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out steady, which I hated, because I wanted it to come out shattered, because steady meant the wall had taught me too well. "Someone spent a fortune making me."

"It wasn't the Order," Ravelle said. "Our rites don't work like that. We burn vessels, we don't *build* them." A pause, and then, quieter, with that crack in her voice I'd been hearing more often: "Though I'm beginning to understand we may not have known half of what was done in our name."

"The cult?" Ilsa asked. "The ones hunting Ashen?"

"No," said the voice inside my skull.

Everyone looked at me, because that's what it looks like when a god talks through your mouth's neighbor.

"You knew," I said. Not a question. My hands had gone cold. "You've been sitting in my head for weeks. Reading my edges. You knew what I was."

"I knew you carried Corivenne," Ashen said, and for the first time since the salt line, the god sounded tired. Old. Afraid, even. "I did not know the rest. Sebastian — listen to me—"

"Who?" I said. "*Who designed this?*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and intent, the kind a god makes when deciding between truth and mercy and finding, as gods always do, that they can't afford either.

"Ashen," I whispered. "*Who.*"

"The one who took your mother's memories," the god said finally, "and left yours seeded, and arranged your road into the valley, and taught your first teacher, and has waited thirty years for you to walk far enough along it to open a door—" the god's voice frayed, "—is the same one who sent you the prayer you stole. Who called to you across the salt. Who has been speaking to you every day since."

The chapel got very small.

"That's you," Ilsa said.

"Yes," said Ashen-Who-Waits. "I am sorry, little bird. But a vessel must open the door *willingly*. And I have run out of time to ask nicely."

Outside, over the ruins of the city that had never existed, the moon began to rise — and I saw, with the clarity of a man watching the trap close, that it was nearly full, and that there were only two turns left.
