## 17. The Thirteenth Turn Rises

I have been called many things across the long centuries of my sleep. Ashen-Who-Waits, which was a kindness. The Ember Under the Hill, which was flattery. The Thief's God, which was an insult delivered by a priest who could not count, since I never stole anything in my life except — well. Except one thing. But I am getting ahead of myself, and gods are not supposed to get ahead of themselves; that is the whole trouble with us.

The moon hung above the plain of the unremembered like a coin held up to the light by a merchant deciding whether it was worth cheating you over. Thirteen turns. Twelve behind us, and this last one swelling toward its fullness the way a wound swells toward its breaking.

I sat — if sitting can be said of what I am, which is mostly a warmth in someone else's skull, a tenant who never signed a lease — I sat at the edge of Sebastian Almeida's sleep and watched him dream, and I confess to you now, whoever you are, whatever is left of you reading this: I watched him the way a fisherman watches water he has already baited.

He dreamed of salt. They all dream of salt, in the valley. It gets into everything there — the bread, the lungs, the years. In his dream the salt had turned to snow, and a woman made of snow was saying his name wrong on purpose, the way his mother used to say it right.

His mother did not say it right, as it happens. She said a different name entirely. But we will come to that, and when we do, I would appreciate it if you remembered that I am old, and tired, and made mostly of waiting, and that even gods lie for reasons they can barely stand themselves.

* * *

Let me tell you how prayers work, because no one prays correctly anymore and it has become a professional embarrassment to those of us on the receiving end.

A prayer is not a request. A prayer is a door knocked on from the inside of the heart, and the god on the other side must decide whether to open it. For a thousand years — for however long the Order's seal held, which felt to me like a thousand years compressed into a single held breath — every knock came through muffled. I heard them the way you hear rain on the far side of a wall. A farmer praying his cow would live. A girl praying her father would stop drinking. A man in Vell's Landing praying that the memory of his dead son might be taken from him gently, because grief was heavier than any cargo he'd ever hauled.

That last one. That was the knock I answered.

You should understand: I had been listening to Sebastian Almeida for longer than he knew. Not because I chose him first. Because he was *there*, the way a key is *there*, lying in a lock's shadow, and eventually even a god with no hands stoops to pick one up.

He had been carrying stolen memories for eleven years. Do you understand what a vessel that makes? Every thief who works the salt lines carries other people's lives in the vaults of their head, and every one of those lives leaves a groove, the way water leaves a groove in stone. He was not a man anymore so much as a corridor of doorways, and I needed — I *needed* — a corridor of doorways, because a sealed god cannot open a door. Only something standing on the threshold, willing, can do that.

Willingly. That word matters. Write it down somewhere. The Order wrote it down once, in a book they burned, and it is the single reason their seal worked for so long. A vessel must open the door willingly. No compulsion, no trick of light, no possession. The will has to be the vessel's own. It is the one law older than the gods, and I hated it the way a drowning man hates the surface of the ice above him.

So I could not force Sebastian. Remember that, when you judge me. Whatever else is true, remember that I *could not*. I could only make him want.

And wanting, friends, is the easiest thing in the world to manufacture, if you are patient and the material is lonely enough.

* * *

Here is what I actually did, laid out plainly, because Ashen-Who-Waits has decided to spend its final honesty in this one place:

The dying merchant in chapter one of his life — the one whose first love Sebastian lifted for coin? I put the ache in him. Years before, I had brushed that merchant's sleeping mind with the smallest feather-touch of myself, just enough to plant the thought: *what was her name? What was her name?* Grief for a lost love is a prayer-shaped hole, whether or not the grieving man knows he is praying. And a memory-thief cannot resist a memory that glows. Sebastian took the bait because the bait was good, because I had spent three seasons curing it.

And then — and here is the part where I ask you to pour one out for my shame — then the client in the second act. The one who paid him to steal a memory that turned out to be a prayer. That client was me. Wearing a body I borrowed from a drunk who owed a temple money, walking on legs I had not walked on in nine hundred years, counting out coins with fingers that shook from the sheer novelty of having them.

The prayer I fed him was real. That is the detail that still stings. I did not fabricate devotion; I gave him a true fragment of worship, pressed flat and folded small like contraband scripture, and I let him carry it home in his head where such things take root. Prayers root. That is their whole nature. You can seal a god behind a door but you cannot seal a prayer inside a thief, because a thief's head is already a country with no borders.

When the prayer rooted and bloomed, I spoke through it. First voice only, faint, deniable — *who is there? who carries me?* — the way a child cries in the dark hoping someone kind rather than cruel comes to the window. He came to the window. Of course he came. Curiosity is the load-bearing sin of every thief I have ever loved, and yes, I have loved thieves, and yes, that is relevant, and no, I will not be elaborating tonight.

I asked him for the rest of my name. Fragment by fragment. Each stolen prayer-memory he carried held a syllable of me, a splinter of worship from before the erasure, and each time he gave a piece back I grew a little more solid in the corridor of him. He thought he was solving a mystery. He was performing a resurrection, one coin-sized piece at a time, and paying for the privilege with his own peace of mind.

Was it manipulation? Of course it was manipulation. I am a god. Manipulation is to us what breathing is to you — except that unlike breathing, we can stop, and feel terrible about it, and then start again anyway, because the alternative is the dark forever.

But here is what the Order's histories got wrong, and what Brother Cade with his hungry eyes will never understand, and what I told the Unforgotten King nine hundred years ago while he wept into his crown: power does not corrupt the worshippers first. It corrupts the god first. We drink your devotion and it ferments in us and comes back down as flood and fever and rain that drowns the very fields it was prayed onto. You were never ruined by loving us. You were ruined because we could not help drinking, and everything we drank came back up poisoned.

I wanted the door open not for dominion. Understand that. I wanted it open because I was *thirsty*, and because the alternative to thirst is nothing, and because after nine hundred years of nothing a god will rationalize anything, including the corruption of one gray-eyed thief with too many other people's memories and not enough of his own.

One thief. I keep coming back to him. Let me tell you why, and let me tell it honestly, because this is the night for honesty and after tomorrow there may be no nights at all.

* * *

The moon stood at its zenith over the plain when I finally stopped hiding in the warm rooms of his skull and spoke to him directly, awake, under the actual sky. He was sitting on the broken lip of a fountain in the forgotten capital, turning a salt-white pebble over in his fingers — the same gesture, I realized with a lurch I did not know I could still perform, the same exact gesture his father used to make when deciding whether to say a hard thing.

"Sebastian," I said, in the voice I had built for him out of his own stolen memories — low, a little rough, the voice of every kind stranger he had ever half-trusted. "Before the turn completes, you deserve to hear it from me and not from Cade, or Ravelle, or the king, or the archives of people who burned my country."

"You're going to tell me you've been lying to me," he said. He didn't look up from the pebble. "Everyone's been telling me things lately. Might as well collect the whole set."

"Yes."

"Then go on." He flipped the pebble, caught it. "I've stolen worse confessions than yours."

So I told him. All of it. The merchant. The drunk with the borrowed body. The prayer folded like contraband. The slow cultivation of his loneliness the way a gardener cultivates a hedge — patiently, along lines already growing, merely shaping what was there. I told him that the fragments leading to the vanished land had been arranged in his head like breadcrumbs, and that he had followed them believing it was his own cleverness, and that his cleverness was real but the trail was mine.

The pebble stopped moving in his hand.

"Ilsa," he said quietly. "The king. The loyalists who fought beside us at the spire road. Were any of them—"

"No. Them I found fair. Them you earned." I let him have that. I needed him to have something. "Only the beginning was mine. The rest — the family you made in the middle of it, the nun who didn't kill you, the mapmaker who draws you like she's angry at the paper — that was all yours. I swear it on what remains of my name, which is, incidentally, mostly in your head."

He laughed. It was not a nice laugh. It was the laugh of a man finding the trapdoor in his own house and realizing he'd been sleeping above it for months.

"A vessel must open the door willingly," he said. "The king told us that much. So you couldn't push me. You could only—" He gestured at himself, at the plain, at the swollen moon. "Garden."

"Yes."

"And if I'd said no? At any point? If I'd handed you to Ravelle that first night in Vell's Landing?"

I was silent for a moment, which for a god is an eternity and also a cowardice. "Then I would have found another vessel. Eventually. In another decade. In another century. And I would have done all of this again, to someone else, because the thirst does not negotiate." I gathered what dignity remains to a thing made of embers and waiting. "I am not sorry, Sebastian. That is the worst part. A truly sorry god would stop being a god. But I will tell you this, and you may weigh it however you like: I chose you not only because you were convenient. I chose you because the first time I touched the edge of your mind, eleven years ago, you were returning a memory you didn't have to return. A child's. Some slum-mother's memory of a christening, worthless on the market. You carried it back to her house and left it on the sill and never told anyone."

"I needed the space," he said. "Vaults fill up."

"You needed the space," I agreed. "And you filled it with mercy instead. That is why the door will listen to you. Doors listen to what people are, not what they intend. I have been a door's neighbor for nine hundred years; believe me, I know its taste."

He looked up at me then — at the shape of me, the ember-shape I wore against the moonlight, more suggestion than body — and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly, humanely tired.

"So what happens now?" he said. "Tomorrow. The thirteenth turn. Cade's already at the gate with his zealots and his stolen rites, and you're asking me to open a door that rotted a king, to wake gods who drowned fields and ate their worshippers' wisdom, because you're *thirsty*."

"I'm asking," I said, "because you're the only one who can choose. Cade wants to take. The Order wants to burn. The king wants to bury. None of them can open it — taking, burning, and burying are all just doors slammed harder. Only a giving can open it. Only a willing hand." I knelt — I made myself kneel, and it cost me more than the confession had. "I will not pretend I'm not desperate. I have been desperate so long it has grown bones. But I would rather stay sealed forever than be opened by Cade. If you close it instead — if you pour yourself into that threshold and end me — I will call it justice, and I will mean it, and I will thank you with my last syllable."

"That's a hell of a sales pitch," he said. "'Open the door and maybe I ruin the world, or shut it and definitely I die.'"

"I never promised you a good choice. I promised you the truth, late. Most gods don't manage even that."

* * *

He walked away along the fountain's broken rim without answering, and I let him go, because a choice chased is a choice murdered. I settled back into the corridors of his sleeping mind that night — yes, even after the confession; a god needs shelter too — and I listened to him dream of salt becoming snow becoming salt, and I did something I had not done since before the erasure.

I prayed.

Not to anything. There is nothing above us; that is theology's dirtiest secret. I prayed the way you talk to a locked door when you've forgotten which side of it you're standing on. I prayed that he would choose the world over me, and I prayed that he would choose me over the world, and both prayers were sincere, and that contradiction is the entire biography of every god who ever lived.

Below us, at the border of the plain, torches were kindling in a line like a lit fuse. Brother Cade had arrived with his zealots and his stolen rite and his appetite dressed up as faith. The Unforgotten King was arming men who had been forgotten so thoroughly that death barely recognized them. Ravelle was sharpening something that was either a blade or her remaining faith. Ilsa was drawing maps of a building that was trying to be destroyed.

And the moon came up over the spire, fat and final, the thirteenth turn rising like a held breath about to be spent.

Sebastian woke before dawn. I felt him reach for the salt-white pebble and find his pocket empty, and I heard him think — clearly, in the plain grammar of a thief — *right, then.*

Whatever he had decided, he was not going to tell me first.

Good, I thought, from inside the dark of him. Good. That's exactly what a vessel should do.

It's what I would have done.
