## 18. Storming the Spire

My father used to say that a map is a promise. Draw the line true, and the line will hold you. Draw it false, and it will lie to everyone who trusts it, and the lie will outlive you by generations.

I had drawn the line true. I could feel it holding us now, the way a rope holds a climber — not comfortably, not kindly, but holding. The spire rose out of the ruined plaza ahead of us, black stone veined with something that glowed like embers under skin, and every chart I'd ever inherited, every marginal note in my father's cramped hand, said: *here*. Here is the door. Here is the wound in the world. Here is where the gods were shut away, and here is where a man named Cade intended to pry it open and drink.

"He's already started," Ravelle said beside me. Her voice was flat in the way I'd learned meant she was terrified. "I can feel it. Can't you?"

I couldn't. Ilsa Voss, cartographer's daughter, mapmaker, forger of borders — I had no vessel in me, no stolen prayers, no divine residue. What I had was ink and memory and a stubborn refusal to die on a day I'd finally found people worth dying beside. But Ravelle could feel it, and Sebastian had gone white as bone-dust when we'd crested the ridge, and the king — the Unforgotten King, Aldric, who looked like a man wearing his own statue — had simply nodded, as if he'd been waiting three hundred years for this particular bad news and was almost relieved it had finally come.

"The rite takes time," Aldric said. "Power must be invited in, and invitation is a slow courtesy. We have until the moon finishes its turn."

"And how long is that?" I asked.

"Hours," he said. "Fewer than we'd like."

Sebastian stood a little apart, the way he always did when the stolen memories got loud. I'd watched him fight them for weeks — the flinches, the borrowed griefs crossing his face like weather. But this morning he looked different. Settled. Like a man who had finally stopped arguing with himself and arrived somewhere.

"You don't have to come," I told him. It was a stupid thing to say. He was the vessel. The whole reason any of this was happening. But I said it anyway, because I wanted him to know that someone in his life valued him for something other than what he carried.

He gave me the crooked half-smile that had gotten him out of a hundred alleys in Vell's Landing and would get him out of exactly none of what was coming. "Ilsa. I've spent my whole life taking things that weren't mine. Let me at least be present for the returns."

* * *

The king's loyalists numbered forty-one. Aldric counted them the way a man counts coins he knows are counterfeit — with love and contempt in equal measure. They were the forgotten ones, the people of the erased country who had never been unmade, only unremembered, and they had kept faith with sleeping gods for centuries in ruins that their own grandchildren couldn't name.

They were not soldiers. Most of them were farmers, masons, a fisherman whose lake had dried up before his grandfather was born. But they had something the Order's zealots didn't, and I saw it as we formed up in the shadow of the outer wall: they were fighting for a home, and the zealots were fighting for a theory.

"The spire has one gate," Aldric said, pointing with a sword so old the blade had gone the color of dusk. "The zealots hold it. Cade will be at the top, in the door-chamber. There are stairs — two hundred and eleven steps, I climbed them once, when I was young and the gods still answered." His voice caught on *answered*, and I understood that for him this was not a battle. It was a funeral he'd been postponing for three lifetimes.

"And the walls?" Ravelle asked. She had her mace out, and her Order tabard torn off and stuffed in her pack — I'd watched her do it that morning, and watched her hand hesitate over it for a long moment before she let it fall.

"The walls are erasure," Aldric said. "They forget what touches them. Stone, flesh, it makes no difference. That is why the door was built here — nothing can approach it that the gods have not permitted, and the gods permit very little these days."

"Then how do we get in?" the fisherman asked.

Everyone looked at me.

Of course they did. I was the map. I was the daughter of the man who had drawn this country before it was erased, and somewhere in my father's marginalia — in notes I'd spent my whole life thinking were the senile scribblings of a broken exile — there was a sentence I had read a hundred times and only understood last night, sitting by the fire with Sebastian's head in my lap while he slept off another seizure of borrowed memory.

*The walls forget what touches them,* my father had written. *But they cannot forget what was drawn true.*

"Maps," I said. "The walls forget. But the country remembers itself, and my father's charts are the country's memory. If we walk the old roads — the exact lines, the true bearings — the erasure can't take us, because we're not trespassing. We're *returning*."

"And you can find those lines?" Ravelle asked.

"I *am* those lines," I said, and hated how it sounded, and meant every word.

* * *

We went in at dusk, because my father's notes said the old roads were truest at dusk, when the shadows of the original survey still lay across the ground like ghost-ink. I walked first with the chart open against my chest like a breastplate, reading bearings aloud — *three degrees off the fallen pillar, walk until the well-shadow touches your heel* — and behind me came forty-one forgotten loyalists, one apostate inquisitor, and one thief who kept up a running commentary under his breath because, he said, if he stopped talking he'd start screaming.

The erasure tested us. I felt it like a hand on my shoulder, patient and enormous, saying *you are not here, you were never here, go home, there is no home.* The fisherman at the rear began to blur at the edges — literally, his outline going soft, like wet ink — and Aldric shouted the man's name, his true name, three generations of it, and the fisherman snapped back into focus gasping.

"Names," Aldric bellowed down the column. "Say your names! Say each other's names! The country forgot you once — do not let it finish the job!"

So we marched into the teeth of a god-sized forgetting, forty-one people shouting names into the dark — *Maren, daughter of Maren; Osten of the dried lake; Ravelle, called Ash-of-the-Order, called traitor, called sister* — and I will carry that sound until I die. It was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard and it was pure terror, and both of those things were true, and I have learned from this company that most true things are.

The zealots found us at the second gate.

* * *

There were more of them than I'd guessed — sixty, maybe seventy, in white-and-grey, Cade's chosen, and they'd fortified the gatehouse with the cold competence of people who had burned villages to learn how. Brother Cade himself stood on the wall above the gate, and even at distance I could see the change in him. He had already begun the rite. Power moved under his skin like light under ice, and his voice, when it came down to us, was wrong — too resonant, harmonizing with itself.

"Ilsa Voss." He knew my name. Of course he did; Ravelle's reports had named all of us, and the thought of that — of her careful, dutiful reports being the knife at our throats — I saw it hit her too, standing beside me. "Daughter of the traitor cartographer. Your father drew the wound. You've come to salt it. I am here to *heal* it."

"Heal it," Sebastian called back, in that drawl he used on people he was about to rob. "That's a funny word for drinking a god."

"I don't *drink* it, thief. I *carry* it. The Order has burned and burned and burned for three hundred years and the world is no holier. Power was never the sin. *Waste* was the sin. I will take what the gods were and give it a purpose — a *discipline* — and the Church will never again be helpless against the dark—"

"You sound like a man explaining why he needs just one more drink," Ravelle said, very quietly, to no one but me.

Then Aldric raised the dusk-colored sword and shouted a word in a language that has no maps anymore, and the forgotten loyalists charged, and the world came apart.

* * *

I have fought before. I'm not proud of most of it. But I had never fought inside a place that was actively forgetting, and I want that recorded: the battle at the spire gate was two fights at once, steel against steel and memory against void, and the second one was killing us faster.

A loyalist woman went down beside me and I watched her start to fade before the blade even finished its work — her face smoothing over like sand in wind. I grabbed her, screaming her name — *Maren, Maren, daughter of Maren* — and Ravelle was suddenly there, mace bloody, shouting the name too, though she'd never met the woman in her life, and between the two of us Maren held her shape long enough to die as herself. It was all we could give her. It was not nothing.

Sebastian fought like a man with forty lifetimes of muscle memory, because he was. I saw him use a sword-form I knew for a fact he'd never learned, a king's guard form, three centuries dead, and I understood that some poor stolen soldier was fighting through him, lending him his last skill the way you'd lend a stranger your coat.

"Stairs!" Aldric roared. "The gate is won — the stairs — Ilsa, the *stairs*—"

The gate was won. It had cost us eleven of the forty-one, and I didn't know their names yet, and that was going to be a debt I'd carry. The zealots fell back into the spire's lower chambers, fighting a retreat, buying their master time, and we went up.

* * *

Two hundred and eleven steps. Aldric had said it like a boast. It was a sentence.

The zealots contested every landing, and the stairwell was narrow, and the erasure was stronger here, pressing in like deep water. Ravelle fought beside me on the hundredth step and I saw the moment her faith finished dying. A zealot — a boy, sixteen at most, someone's son — looked at her Order insignia's ghost, the sunburst she'd torn off but that seemed burned into her skin anyway, and begged her to stand aside, called her *Sister*, said Cade would make her whole again, and she broke his jaw with the mace almost gently, and then stood over him breathing hard and said, to me, not to him:

"I reported everything. Every mile. Every village. He used *my maps of the road* to burn them, and *my reports* to find this border, and I told myself it was duty. I told myself the Order knew things I didn't." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "The Order knew exactly what it was doing. That was the whole horror of it. It was never confused, Ilsa. It was never *lost*. It just didn't care."

"Feel that later," I said. "Climb now. Grieve at the top."

She climbed. She was, I think, the bravest of us, because she climbed knowing exactly what she'd find at the top: proof that her whole life had been in service of the lie.

* * *

The door-chamber at the top of the spire was round and vast and wrong — the ceiling opened onto a sky that held the moon at every one of its thirteen turns at once, all its phases hanging together like coins on a string, and beneath them stood the door.

I had imagined it my whole life without knowing. My father's final chart, the one he'd died with ink under his nails finishing, had a blank space at its center with a single word: *HERE*. The door was that blank space, made real — a slab of night standing upright in a ring of white salt, and it hummed, and the hum was the oldest sound I'd ever heard, older than the country, older than the gods, maybe.

Cade stood before it with his arms spread, and he was *transfiguring* — his body going translucent, filling with light like a lamp of skin, and around him the air was full of voices, thousands of them, the prayers of three centuries being answered all at once and all at once *badly*.

"You're late," Cade said, and his smile was serene, and it was the most frightening thing I had ever seen. "But that's all right. There's room in the vessel for all of you. There's room in the vessel for the *world*."

Sebastian stepped forward, and the voices in the air went quiet, every one, like a crowd recognizing royalty.

"You're not the vessel," Cade said, and for one flickering instant the serenity cracked and I saw the man underneath — not a monster, a believer, which is worse. "I am the faithful one. I am the one who *never stopped believing*. The power should come to—"

"It doesn't come to the faithful," Sebastian said. "It comes to the willing. That's the whole rule. That's why it's been sitting in a thief for a month, Cade. The gods don't want your devotion." He spread his hands, and I saw the stolen light of a dead god moving under his skin, answering him. "They want someone who'll *take them as they are*. Corrupt. Hungry. True. You want to fix them first. They can tell."

And Cade — I will say this for him — Cade stopped arguing and attacked.

* * *

The last fight was short and terrible and I was in it and I remember almost none of it clearly, the way you don't remember the worst storm of your life, only fragments: Ravelle's mace shattering against Cade's raised arm and the arm not breaking; Aldric's dusk-colored sword finally, finally breaking, three hundred years of patience snapping like a twig; the fisherman Osten throwing himself into the gap between Cade's light and Sebastian and coming out the other side of it *changed*, his dried lake suddenly alive in his eyes, water where there had been dust for generations — and then empty again, spent, the gift burning through him like a torch through paper.

He died smiling. I held his head. I said his name. Osten of the dried lake. Osten. Osten.

And then Cade had me.

His hand closed on my throat and the light in him poured toward me and I felt it *reading* me, flipping through me like pages — my father, my charts, my resentment, my hand in Sebastian's hair by the fire — and I understood with perfect clarity that I was going to die as a footnote in a god-thief's ascension, and my last thought was absurdly, furiously *my father drew this room, he stood where I'm standing—*

Then Ravelle hit Cade with the broken haft of her own mace, both hands, all her doubt and all her faith behind it, screaming not a prayer but a *name* — Sebastian's name, the stolen one, the one he'd carried like a coat that never fit — and Cade turned, and Sebastian was already moving.

"Get her clear," Sebastian said. His voice was calm. Too calm. The voice of a man standing at the edge of something he's decided to jump off.

"Seb—" I started, and he looked at me, and I stopped, because I knew that look. I'd seen it on my father's face the day he finished the last chart and burned all his notes and said, *now it's done, and now it's not mine anymore.*

"Whatever happens next," Sebastian said, "don't let go of my name. That's all. Whatever I say, whatever I forget — you hold it. Both of you. That's the job now."

The moon above us — all thirteen turns of it at once — began to move.

And Sebastian Almeida, thief of memories, vessel of a waiting god, walked toward the door through the ring of salt, and the salt parted for him like it had been waiting three hundred years for exactly this, and the last thing I heard him say, over his shoulder, light as a man leaving a tavern:

"Keep the charts, Ilsa. Someone should remember the country, even if I can't."
