## 1. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The moon came up over Thornfell like a coin rubbed thin, and Elodie Ravensworth counted its edges the way other men counted their debts.

Ten years he had walked these passes, and in ten years he had never once spoken to the stone. That was the discipline of it—the whole architecture of his survival built on silence where silence was hardest. The mountains remembered him. He felt it sometimes on the high trails, a pressure at his back like a hand between his shoulder blades, old and patient and full of words he refused to answer. *Swear it,* the ridgelines seemed to say. *Say the truth of this place aloud and we will hold it for you forever.*

He kept his palm closed and his mouth shut, and the mountains went on waiting.

"Ravensworth." Sera Voss's voice came low from behind, warm even when she whispered. "You've stopped walking. Men who stop walking on the Widow's Stair tend to stop being men shortly after."

"I'm admiring the view."

"The view is a sheer drop into a gorge full of last year's snowmelt."

"A very pretty drop."

She came up beside him, lantern shuttered to a slit of amber light, and followed his gaze down the switchbacks. Below them the caravan moved in single file—eleven mules, nine people, cargo wrapped in oiled canvas that smelled of tar and lies. Brandy from the coast, mostly. Salt. A crate of books no customs man would ever be paid enough to look for. Sera ran her loads quiet and clean, and she had one rule above all others: nobody talks their way past trouble. You pay it, you dodge it, you starve before you charm it.

Elodie loved her for that rule more than she would ever know.

"You're pale," she said.

"It's night, Sera. Everyone's pale."

"You're paler than the rest of us." She studied his face with the flat frankness of a woman who haggled for a living and had never once lost. "You heard something."

He hadn't. That was the thing he couldn't explain—not without explaining everything else. Ten years ago, in another life under another name, he had been able to hear oaths the way a tuning fork hears a struck bell: any promise made within a mile of him rang faintly in his bones, and he could tell true from false as easily as warm from cold. He had burned that hearing out of himself by degrees, or told himself he had, wrapping the scar across his palm in wool and habit until even he half believed the gift was gone. But some nights the wind off the high snow carried voices anyway, fragments of vows made around distant fires—I'll pay you Tuesday, I swear I didn't touch her, I'll never leave—and each fragment cost him something he couldn't name.

Tonight the wind carried nothing. And that nothing had a shape.

"Goose crossed my path," he said. "Bad luck."

"You don't believe in luck. You told Wren superstition is what stupid people use instead of maps."

"I contain multitudes."

Sera snorted and moved back down the line, and Elodie stood a moment longer with the moon climbing over the eastern fangs of the range. Thirteen nights to full, by his reckoning. He always knew; it was the one calendar his body kept without being asked, though he'd have been hard pressed to say why this moon above all others. He flexed his scarred hand inside its glove. The skin beneath the wool was smooth and white and wrong, like wax poured over an old wound—a sword-oath made in blood and desperation a decade ago, the last binding he had ever spoken, the one that had unmade a war and made a ghost of him in the same breath.

*Never again freely given, never again taken.* That had been the oath's own teeth, turned inward. It had held. Mostly.

He started walking again.

* * *

They reached the pass at Grey Watch an hour before dawn, where the trail narrowed between two leaning slabs of granite like the shoulders of a kneeling giant. This was the choke point, the place where Sera's route either worked or didn't, and Elodie went ahead alone as he always did, checking the ground, reading the frost, listening to the dark for the particular silence that meant soldiers.

What he found instead was paper.

A proclamation had been nailed to the western slab, driven deep into stone—which was the first wrongness, because no nail bites granite, and the second wrongness followed close behind: it had been posted at eye height, squarely, deliberately, facing the smuggler's path. Not left behind by accident. Set like bait.

Elodie stood very still and let the cold come into him.

By shuttered lantern light he read it twice. The script was courtly, the seal genuine—he recognized the sunburst of Queen Marenne's chancery from a lifetime ago, when he'd sworn service under a different seal entirely. The words were plain, which made them worse. Plain words meant they wanted to be understood.

*By order of Her Majesty Marenne, First of Her Name: let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of oath-binding, called swordspeaking, is hereby declared treason most foul. Let it be known that the gift survives. Let it be known that a hunt is proclaimed, in every shire and every fell, for any who carry it. Any who shelter such a one, any who feed them, any who fail to name them, partake of their crime and shall share their sentence.*

The sentence went unnamed. That was deliberate too. Things left unsaid hang heavier than things written out.

And then, near the bottom, in a hand slightly different from the rest—an addition, a postscript, the kind of thing a clerk adds because someone above him insisted:

*It is known that one remains in the Thornfell country. It is known that he passes unseen. He will not pass unseen much longer.*

*He.*

Elodie read the word until it stopped being a word. Around him the pass breathed—snow squeaking under its own weight, a mule stamping somewhere below, the enormous indifferent patience of stone—and he felt, for the first time in ten years, truly seen. Not found; not yet. But looked for. Hunted in principle, the way a wolf is hunted before the dogs are even loosed, simply because the crown has decided wolves are an insult.

Someone had written *he* because someone knew. Court records, maybe. A survivor of the old war with a memory and a grudge. Or—his stomach turned slowly over—an inquisitor with a talent for reading gaps, the kind of mind that finds a man by mapping everything he isn't. Whatever the source, the arithmetic was simple: the life he had built on invisibility had just been weighed, measured, and found to have an expiry date.

He should tear it down. Burn it. Warn the caravan and cut east over the Shattered Col, three extra days through avalanche country.

Instead he did something worse. He put his gloved hand flat against the granite beside the proclamation, and he listened.

Nothing. No ringing of vows, no resonance of freshly spoken authority. Whoever had posted this had done it without oath or ceremony—had nailed a lie-adjacent truth to the mountain with bare competence and gone home. That frightened him more than magic would have. Magic he understood. This was merely the world, grinding on toward him with the patience of water.

Below, a pebble clattered. Wren's voice, pitched to carry only as far as it needed to: "El? Bram says the mule doesn't like the smell up there. Bram says mules know."

Bram was deaf, not mute, but he signed most days and spoke rarely, and when he did speak through Wren it was usually worth hearing. Mules knew. Mules had smelled iron and oil and too many boots, or else they'd smelled something older than all of it, something the mountain itself had begun to leak now that the thirteenth moon was rising.

"Tell Bram the mule is smarter than both of us," Elodie called back softly. "We go through. Quiet feet. Now."

He took the proclamation down with two pulls—it came free of the stone with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw—and folded it into his coat, against his chest, where it lay cold as a blade. Evidence, he told himself. Something to show Sera, something to plan around.

But his hand, when he drew it out again gloveless in the dark to signal the caravan forward, was shaking. And in the thin pre-dawn light the scar across his palm had gone faintly red, the way an old burn wakes when the weather turns—as if somewhere beyond the pass, beyond the fells, beyond the reach of everything he had renounced, someone had just spoken his old name aloud.

He closed his fist. The caravan came up through the shoulders of the giant, mule after mule, liar after liar, his whole fragile family filing past him into the mountains' keeping.

Thirteen nights to full.

Behind them, at the crest of the eastern ridge, small and patient against the paling sky, stood a single rider who had not been there when the moon rose.
