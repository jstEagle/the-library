## 2. A Family of Liars

The descent into Greyhollow took the better part of a day, and Elodie spent every hour of it listening to the mountain lie to him.

That was how he thought of it, though he'd never say it aloud. The Thornfell passes had a hundred voices—wind through the scree, meltwater ticking under stone, the groan of old pines bending—and a man who didn't know better would swear each one meant something. But Elodie had spent ten years learning which voices were true and which were the mountain's way of talking to itself, and he guided the caravan down the switchbacks with his hood up and his scarred palm curled shut inside his glove, reading the honest sounds and ignoring the rest.

Behind him, six mules picked their way with the patience of animals that had made this trip too many times to fear it. Behind the mules came the cargo: salted fish from the coast, iron nails, a bolt of blue dye worth more than everything else combined. Ordinary things. Smuggled things. The kind of goods that kept three dozen people fed in a haven the crown's maps insisted did not exist.

"You're quiet," said Doss, walking beside him, breath steaming.

"I'm always quiet."

"Quieter." The young man squinted at him. "You've been quieter since the pass. Since the notice."

Elodie said nothing. The proclamation was still nailed to the post at the top of the pass, its wax seal glinting like a wound: *By order of Queen Marenne, all persons possessing the gift of oath-speech are declared enemies of the crown. Any who shelter them share their sentence.* Below it, in smaller letters, the reward. A number large enough to buy a farm. Large enough to buy a family's silence.

He had read it twice, memorized it once, and burned it off the post with a thoughtless flicker of something he refused to call power—a small binding that made the parchment curl and blacken as if age itself had come for it. Then he'd walked on and told no one.

The trail dropped through a curtain of birch, and there it was: Greyhollow, tucked into a fold of the mountains like a coin in a palm. Smoke rising from a dozen chimneys. Laundry strung between cabins. The long low barn where the mules wintered, and beyond it the great hall with its turf roof, its door always open, its smell of bread and tallow and wet wool.

Home. The word still caught in his chest after ten years, like a fishhook he'd never worked loose.

A figure detached itself from the woodpile and came toward them at a run—not running *at* anything, just running, because she was nine years old and the world was worth running through. Wren. Small and quick, hair the color of river mud, sleeves too long for her arms. She reached the lead mule before Doss could greet anyone, planted herself in front of it, and held up one imperious hand.

"What did you bring me?"

"Salt," Elodie said.

"Salt is boring."

"Dye."

Her eyes went wide and covetous. "Blue?"

"The bluest blue in the coastal markets. If you help unload."

She helped unload. She helped unload with the ferocity of a girl who intended to have first claim on that bolt of cloth, hauling bundles half her size across the yard while Sera Voss stood in the hall doorway with her arms crossed and her mouth doing the thing it did when she was trying not to smile.

* * *

Sera Voss counted her people the way other captains counted coin: nightly, by name, out loud if any were missing.

Tonight they were all present. She stood at the head of the long table in the great hall and looked down its length—Doss arguing with the cook about pepper; Bram at the far end signing something obscene about the dye to make the younger ones laugh; Wren asleep sitting up, her cheek on the table, one hand still curled around a scrap of blue cloth she'd stolen fair and square—and felt the particular fullness that was both her greatest wealth and her greatest vulnerability.

Thirty-one souls. That was the ledger. Everything else was negotiable.

She found Montrose at the end near the fire, as she always did—the one who sat apart even while belonging, the guide who took the dangerous passes and asked for nothing but a corner and silence. Ten years he'd walked with them. Ten years, and she knew his knife-hand, his mule-hand, his card-face, and almost nothing else. No family named. No history told. A man shaped like an absence.

And yet.

"Sera," he said, without looking up from the blade he was sharpening. "You're staring."

"I'm auditing."

"Find anything?"

"A man who reads proclamations twice." She sat down across from him, close enough that the firelight caught the gray threading his dark beard. "Doss says you stopped at the pass for a long time today."

"Doss should spend less time watching me and more time watching the trail."

"Doss watches the trail fine. It's you he watches." She leaned forward. "Something's changed. I've run this haven eleven years, Montrose. I know what my people look like when they're carrying something heavy. You've been carrying it since the pass, and tonight you set your knife down twice to look at your own hand."

His hand closed, slow, deliberate. He met her eyes then, and gave her the flat, patient look he gave to customs men and rockslides alike. "It's an old burn. It aches in cold weather."

"It hasn't been cold."

"Sera." His voice stayed gentle. That was the thing about him; even his refusals were gentle. "If there were something you needed to know, I'd tell you. You know that."

And that was the lie, wasn't it? She let it go, because the alternative was pressing until something broke, and she'd learned long ago that some loads a mule will carry only if you don't look too hard at the straps. "Fine," she said. "But when it gets heavy enough that you can't carry it alone, you put it down in front of me. Captain's orders."

He almost smiled. "Captain's orders."

Across the hall, Bram caught her eye and signed, lazily, one-handed: *Trouble?*

*Not yet,* she signed back.

Bram considered this, then pointed at Montrose, then at the fire, then made a small motion like something settling into ash.

*Yes,* Sera signed. *I know.*

* * *

Later, when the hall had gone quiet and the embers ticked, Elodie climbed to the loft where the apprentices slept and found Wren awake, as he'd known he would. She sat cross-legged by the little window, the stolen blue cloth spread over her knees like a king's mantle, watching the moon.

It was nearly round. One sliver shy.

"Can't sleep?" he asked.

"Counting." She didn't turn. "Grandmother used to say you count the moons when something's coming. Thirteen turns, she said, and then—" She stopped, frowning at her own memory, the way children frown when a story slips its moorings. "I forget the end. She told it wrong anyway. Grandmothers do."

"Thirteen turns and then what?"

"That's the part I forgot." Now she turned, and in the moonlight her face was older than nine, sharper than nine, and Elodie felt the familiar prickle along his spine—the sense of being *read*. "You know what I noticed, Montrose? You never swear."

"I swear plenty. Ask Doss."

"No. Not the way we mean." She hugged her knees. "When Bram signs an oath on a deal, you nod. When Sera swears on her mother's grave the cargo's clean, you look away. Everybody here swears on things—graves, gods, mothers. You never do. Not once in ten years." Her voice dropped, not accusing but wondering, which was worse. "Like you're saving them up."

The loft was very quiet. Down below, someone banked the fire; a log settled with a sigh.

"Wren," he said carefully, "what did your grandmother's stories say about oaths?"

"That words can be doors." She shrugged, childlike again, the sharpness folding away. "Or cages. Depends who's speaking." Then, yawning: "Your hand's bleeding through the glove, by the way. It does that when you think too hard. I don't tell Sera things like that. I'm not a snitch."

She lay down, pulled the blue cloth to her chin, and was asleep within minutes, the way only children and the truly exhausted can be.

Elodie sat by the window a long time, unwrapping his palm in the moonlight. The old scar had split along its length, as it always did when the gift stirred, seeping a bead of red as if the wound remembered being made. He had earned it at nineteen, the night he swore his first true oath and learned that a swordspeaker's word cuts the speaker deepest of all. Ten years since. Ten years of silence, of guiding smugglers, of lying by omission to the only people who had ever made him feel like a man instead of a weapon.

Below him slept thirty-one people who shared their bread with a stranger. Above the ridge, in the world of writs and queens, a hunt had begun with his name in its teeth—though they didn't know his name yet. They knew only that somewhere in Thornfell, a voice that could bind steel to truth had gone quiet for ten years.

And they were patient. Crowds always were.

He wrapped the hand, lay back, and watched the moon through the frost-feathered glass. One sliver shy of full. Twelve turns behind it, and a thirteenth coming, whether he counted or not.

Somewhere down in the valley, a dog began to bark—not at wolves, not at wind, but at the road.
