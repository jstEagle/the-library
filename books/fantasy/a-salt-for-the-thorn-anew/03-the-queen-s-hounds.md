## 3. The Queen's Hounds

The road into Thornfell had been unmade by decree—bridges pulled down, waystones chiseled blank—and so Inquisitor Maren Hale read the kingdom's erasure the way other people read letters: in what was missing. She rode at the head of forty soldiers through a pass that no longer appeared on any map sanctioned by the crown, and she noted, without sentiment, that the mountains did not care. Stone remained stone. Weather remained weather. Only the names had died, and names were the cheapest things in the world to kill.

She had helped kill some of them herself.

At the foot of the pass, where the king's road surrendered to smugglers' trails, she dismounted and knelt in the mud beside a boundary marker. Someone had hammered the old inscription flat, but chisels leave ghosts. She laid her bare palm against the granite—the same palm that had once borne an oath-scar, long since burned away and grown back as ordinary skin—and felt the grooves beneath like braille: *Thornfell keeps. Thornfell remembers.* A swordspeaker's formula, half worn away. Her own oath had begun with words very like them.

"Inquisitor?" Lieutenant Corvane sat his horse behind her, breath steaming. He was young, pious, frightened of her in the useful way.

"The locals still come here," she said, rising, brushing mud from her grey coat. "You can tell by the candle stubs. They've been leaving offerings to a kingdom that officially never existed." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which unsettled him more than any scowl would have. "Good. Fear needs somewhere to live. We'll find it a house."

They rode on into the village of Lowmarsh, where the proclamation was already nailed to the well-house, and where the first of her interrogations began before the sun cleared the eastern ridge.

* * *

Hale conducted her interviews in the millhouse, because it smelled honestly of grain and labor, and because the millwheel's slow turning gave nervous people something to look at that wasn't her face. She sent the soldiers away. She poured the tea herself. These were not kindnesses—or rather, they were kindnesses, and she had learned long ago that kindness was simply the sharpest instrument on the rack when it was used deliberately.

The miller's wife sat across from her with hands folded tight enough to blanch.

"You're not in trouble," Hale told her, and meant it. "No one in this valley is in trouble unless they choose to be. I'm looking for one person. One. And I suspect he has been kind to you, or paid you, or both, because that is how people like him survive."

"I don't know what you mean by *people like him*."

"Yes, you do." Hale let the wheel turn twice around. Outside, her soldiers were buying eggs at fair prices, mending a fence for the widow Hedley, being patient and polite in ways the villagers had not been taught to expect from crown men. She had ordered all of it. "Madam, thirty years ago there were perhaps two hundred swordspeakers in the world. Oath-bound. Their word could bind steel to truth, command loyalty out of stone and iron, settle disputes that courts couldn't touch. You'll have heard stories. Most of them are flattering. Mine are better, because mine are true." She sipped her tea. "I know what the gift looks like in a community. It looks like debts that never quite get called in. Like quarrels that resolve themselves too neatly. Like a valley where the smuggling runs smooth because someone's luck is doing arithmetic for it."

The miller's wife looked at the wheel.

"There was a man," she said finally, barely above the sound of the water. "Came through maybe ten years back. Bought rope. Overpaid and wouldn't take it back. My boy had a fever that winter and someone left willow-bark and boneset on our sill, wrapped in oilcloth. Never knew who." Her jaw tightened. "That's not a crime."

"No," Hale agreed gently. "It isn't. What was his name?"

"He called himself Master Elowen. Rope merchant." A bitter almost-laugh. "Rope merchant who walked like a soldier and never once looked at the price of anything."

"And his hands?"

"Gloves. Always gloves. Even in summer."

Hale wrote nothing down. Writing made people clamp shut; memory made them keep talking. But she had already written it that night in her ledger, in her neat, unhurried hand: *Elowen. Gloves. Ten years, more or less. Generosity as camouflage.* Beneath it, older entries, copied from court archives in the capital: *RAVENSWORTH, ELODIE. Last confirmed swordspeaker of the Thornfell line. Age at record: nineteen. Status: presumed dead, Ashfall purge. Presumption unsatisfactory.*

Presumption unsatisfactory. That phrase had carried her across half a kingdom. Dead men did not leave willow-bark on windowsills. Dead men did not keep smuggler roads running so smoothly that the crown's own tariffs wept.

"Thank you," she said to the miller's wife, and rose, and shook her hand, and meant that too. "Your boy's fever—I hope he thrived."

"He's a soldier now," the woman said, with something defiant in it. "In the queen's own guard."

"Then we're all on the same side after all." Hale smiled again. The woman did not smile back until she was safely out the door, and then only once, on the street, glancing back at the millhouse with an expression Hale catalogued without resentment: *the face of someone deciding whether they have just betrayed an angel or fed a wolf.* Both, probably. Both was usually the honest answer.

* * *

Evening found her in the mill loft with her ledgers spread around her like a card game played against the dead. Corvane brought her supper and stood awkwardly among the sacks of flour, watching her lay documents side by side: a court record four decades old, a tax roll, a sketch of a family tree drawn in her own hand.

"You were one of them," he said. He hadn't meant to say it. She watched the color climb his neck.

"I was," she said pleasantly. "Ask your question, Lieutenant. It's kinder than wondering."

"Why would you hunt your own?"

She set down her pen and considered him for a moment—a lean woman past fifty, grey as her coat, with a burn-scar where her oath-scar used to be, and eyes that people always described as warm and then revised.

"When I was twenty-two," she said, "I swore an oath to Lord Aldric Vayle. You won't have heard of him either; another name we buried. My oath bound my sword to his truth. Do you understand what that means? Not his *word*—his truth. Whatever he believed, I was compelled to defend. Whatever he believed himself justified in doing, my blade was obligated to do with him." She turned her palm up, showed him the pale shine of old burning. "He believed, sincerely, that a famine district was hoarding grain. His truth starved nine hundred people, and my gift held my arm steady while it happened, and my conscience screamed behind a wall of my own making, built out of my own mouth."

Corvane had gone very still.

"A swordspeaker's power is real," Hale said. "That's the whole horror of it. It isn't trickery. When Elodie Ravensworth—or whoever he calls himself these days—speaks an oath, steel *does* hold true, stone *does* remember, lies *do* choke in the speaker's throat. Real power. Now ask yourself: whose truth does such a power serve? Whoever it swears to. And who chooses whom it swears to? The speaker. So all the justice in the world becomes one person's private treasury, dispensed by personal favor." She gathered her ledgers into their case, squaring the edges with small precise taps. "I loved the gift, Lieutenant. The way you love a limb. And then I cut mine off at the root, and I have never once regretted it, because justice cannot be a charisma. It must be law—written, fallible, accountable, boring. Law that fails publicly can be corrected. An oath fails silently, forever."

"And this man in the valley? If he's using his gift for—willow-bark on windowsills?"

"Then he is a good man," Hale said, "sitting on a weapon that the next man to find him will not be. There is no safe place to hide a fire, Corvane. There is only the interval between now and the burning." She closed the case. "Besides."

"Inquisitor?"

"The records say Ravensworth took the full oath at thirteen and renewed it at his majority—which means his scar is a decade old at minimum, and a swordspeaker who hasn't spoken in ten years is not a man at peace." She banked the lamp low and lay back on the flour sacks with her hands folded, entirely comfortable, entirely awake. "A silenced gift doesn't die. It goes rancid. He's been swallowing his own voice for a decade, and men who swallow their voices eventually need a reason big enough to spit. The queen wants him alive if possible. I want him found first. Whichever comes second is a matter of policy."

Corvane hesitated at the ladder. "And if he refuses to come quietly? If he speaks?"

Hale looked up at the rafters, where swallows had nested every summer for a hundred years, coming home to a building that kept its promises.

"Then we'll learn something interesting about what ten years of silence buys," she said. "Goodnight, Lieutenant. At dawn we ride for Greyhollow. Every rope merchant needs a buyer, and every buyer in these mountains drinks at the same poisoned well."

The lamp went dark, and the millwheel turned on below, patient as water, counting out something Hale could almost hear: *Thornfell keeps. Thornfell remembers.*

"So do I," she murmured to the dark, and slept without dreaming, as she always did, like an honest blade in a oiled sheath.
