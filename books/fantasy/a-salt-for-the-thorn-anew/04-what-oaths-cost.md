## 4. What Oaths Cost

The oath-scar itched before rain. It had done so for ten years, a thin white seam across Elodie's left palm like a hairline crack in porcelain, and he had learned to read it the way sailors read the sky. Tonight it burned.

He stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Cut with his hand curled shut and watched the storm come down the Thornfell passes in grey sheets, and he told himself the itch meant nothing. Weather. Old wounds remembering themselves. Nothing more.

Behind him, the caravan waited—six wagons draped in oilcloth, mules stamping, Sera Voss at the lead wagon counting crates by lantern-light with her lips moving around numbers she never said aloud. Wren crouched on the tailboard of the third wagon, thirteen years old and all elbows, watching him with that look she thought he didn't notice. The look of someone assembling a person out of clues.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at a mountain like it owes you money."

Elodie almost smiled. "Go inside, sprout. Rain's coming sideways."

"Rain always comes sideways here." But she went, folding herself into the wagon's dark with the ease of a creature born to hiding places, and Elodie turned back to the pass and let himself remember what he had sworn never to remember.

* * *

He had been twenty-two the last time an oath left his mouth, and it had not been a weapon. That was the lie he told himself for years afterward—that the first ones were innocent. They never were.

His mother had taught him the words in the kitchen of their house in Lowmark, back when Thornfell still appeared on maps, back when swordspeakers were honored rather than hunted. She'd held his small hand open on the table and traced the lines of it with a flour-dusted finger. "A sword is just steel until someone swears over it," she said. "Then it's a promise. And promises, little bird, are the sharpest things in the world."

She'd shown him how to bind a blade to truth: *Steel, I name you honest. Let no edge of you cut but what I mean.* A child's oath, a practice oath, spoken over a wooden training sword—and the wood had hummed against his palm like a struck bell, warm as a living thing, and he had laughed because it felt like being loved by something enormous.

That was the part no one warned you about. Not the power. The love of it.

* * *

The rain arrived all at once, as if the mountains had tipped a bucket over the world. Elodie pulled his hood up and walked the line of wagons, checking lashings, and tried very hard to be only what he appeared: a quiet man who knew the passes, hired for his silence and his maps, worth every coin because he asked no questions and told no stories.

Sera fell into step beside him. She didn't look at him; she looked at the dark mouth of the Cut ahead, where lightning was stitching itself into the stone.

"Bram doesn't like this stretch," she said. Bram's silences were louder than most men's shouting, and they had all learned to trust them. "Says the birds went quiet an hour ago. Says there's too many birds going quiet."

"Bram's usually right."

"Bram's insufferable about it, too." She paused. "Elodie. If something's wrong—"

"Nothing's wrong."

It came out too fast, too smooth, the way lies do when they've been polished by use, and he saw her glance flick to him—brief, weighing, the look of a woman who had spent thirty years reading faces by torchlight—and then move on. He hated himself a little. He hated more that he would do it again.

They made camp in the lee of a shattered watchtower, one of old Thornfell's bones, its stones furred with moss and older than any kingdom now on the maps. Fires under canvas. Watches set. Elodie took first watch because sleep had become a country he could rarely enter, and he sat with his back against the tower wall and his scarred palm pressed flat against the wet stone, and the stone seemed to press back.

That was when the bandits came.

* * *

There had been a night, once, when oaths had stopped being bells and started being knives.

He was nineteen, a year into the wars that would end Thornfell, attached to a company holding a river ford against men who wanted it. His captain—a good man, a tired man, a man named Aldric who used to share out his rations to the youngest soldiers—was dying in the mud with three enemies standing over him, and Elodie, half-armed, half-trained, wholly terrified, did the only thing left in him.

He spoke.

*I swear you cannot lift your hands against him. I swear your arms are stone until he breathes his last or walks away free.*

He hadn't known he could do that. Hadn't known the gift stretched so far, reached so deep into other bodies, other wills. The three soldiers froze mid-blow, arms locked, faces slack with a confusion that curdled quickly into horror—they could feel it, the oath sitting on them like a yoke, their own muscles answering a voice that wasn't theirs. One of them wept. Elodie remembered that. He remembered thinking, distantly, *good*, and then remembering nothing else for a long time, because the power had poured through him like a flood through a broken dam and drowned everything else.

Aldric lived. The company held the ford. And Elodie lay awake that night in the reeds with his palm throbbing, understanding two things with terrible clarity: that he could do it again whenever he chose, and that some part of him had *enjoyed* it—the certainty of it, the rightness, the way the world had bent to his word like iron to a magnet.

His mother had seen it before he did. "The gift doesn't corrupt you all at once," she told him near the end, when the hunts had begun and they were burning names off registers. "It corrupts you the way water cuts stone. Every oath makes the next one easier to speak and harder to mean honestly. One day you'll swear something true and won't be able to tell it from something convenient. That's how they get you, little bird. Not with temptation. With relief."

* * *

The bandits hit the caravan from both sides of the Cut at once, which meant they knew the ground, which meant these weren't desperate men but professionals, and Elodie was moving before the first scream finished.

Twelve of them, maybe fourteen, coming down through the rain with crossbows wrapped in oilcloth to keep the strings dry—professionals indeed. The first bolt took a mule. The second missed Sera by a hand's width and buried itself in the wagon beside her head. Shouting everywhere, torches guttering, and through it all Elodie heard one sound that turned his blood to ice water:

Wren. Screaming from the third wagon.

Later, he would try to reconstruct the choice. He would sit in the dark and turn it over like a coin worn smooth, looking for the moment when caution lost. There wasn't one. That was the frightening part. There was no decision, no weighing, no voice saying *you swore you'd never*. There was only Wren's voice in the rain and his own body already turning toward it, and his scarred palm opening like a flower toward a sun that had been waiting ten years for exactly this.

He spoke, and the world changed shape.

* * *

*Steel, I name you mine. Iron, I name you slow. You men of the Cut—I swear you cannot take another step toward these wagons. I swear your hands forget how to hold. I swear your feet are roots and your bows are dead branches, and you will stand in this rain and know whose word holds you, and you will know it is mine.*

Fourteen men stopped as if the mountain had swallowed them. Crossbows sagged. Two dropped entirely, fingers simply opening, weapons falling into mud. They stood in the downpour, fourteen grown men, trembling—not with cold—with the specific animal terror of bodies that no longer answered their owners. And Elodie stood among them feeling—

Feeling magnificent.

That was the word, and he could not unsay it even in the privacy of his own skull. Magnificent. The gift roared through him like a river in spring, and every thread of it was visible to him now: the weight of fourteen wills hanging from his word like coats on hooks, the rain slowing in his perception until each drop hung distinct, the astonishing clarity of a mind that had been fogged for a decade and hadn't known it. This was what he had amputated. This was what he had been living without. Ten years of dimness, and he had called it peace.

*More,* said something in him that wore his own voice. *You could end them. You could make them kneel. You could make them carry the wagons to Greyhollow on their backs and thank you for the honor. No one would ever touch your family again. No one would ever touch anything of yours again.*

And there it was. The water cutting stone. Because the voice wasn't wrong—that was the horror of it. He *could*. The power knelt before him offering everything, and it sounded exactly like love, exactly like protection, exactly like the fierce animal vow he'd made the day he'd found Wren half-starved in a culvert and carried her home: *no one hurts this child while I breathe.*

How much of this was mercy? How much of it was the gift dressing itself in his love and calling itself by her name?

He couldn't tell. Standing in the rain with fourteen men bound and a child crying behind him, he could not tell his own motives apart, and that frightened him more than Hale, more than the hunt, more than anything the crown could send up these mountains.

So he did the hardest thing he had done in ten years. He stopped.

He closed the floodgate. He spoke the release—*I unbind you; go, and remember only rain*—and the world snapped back to its proper speed and proper dullness, and fourteen bandits fled into the dark without knowing why, and Elodie stood alone in the mud shaking like a man coming out of fever.

* * *

Wren had seen.

Not the bandits' terror—she'd been inside the wagon, face pressed to the gap in the canvas. But she'd seen him after. She came out into the rain barefoot, past Sera shouting orders, past Bram materializing from the dark with his knife drawn and his eyes asking questions, straight to Elodie, who was still standing where the gift had left him.

"Your hand," she said.

"It's nothing."

"Your *hand*, Elodie."

He looked down. The scar was glowing. Faintly, guttering, like a coal breathing its last—but glowing, a thin seam of light across his palm, and in that light he saw his own skin clearly for the first time in ten years: not healed, merely closed. A wound pretending to be a scar. An oath pretending to be finished.

Wren took his wrist—small fingers, iron grip—and turned his palm up to the rain, and she did not flinch, and she did not run, and she did not shout for Sera. She looked at the light in his hand the way she looked at boundary stones and coded ledgers and all the other puzzles the world offered her, and then she looked up at him with an expression he had no map for.

"I knew," she said quietly, under the rain, under Sera's voice, under the whole collapsing architecture of his decade of lies. "I didn't know *what*. But I knew you were more than a guide." Her grip tightened. "My grandmother told me stories about people like you. About doors that open for them."

The rain fell. The scar glowed between them like a secret finally given a body.

"Doors," Elodie said.

"For people who can swear true," Wren said. "Before the thirteenth moon fills."
