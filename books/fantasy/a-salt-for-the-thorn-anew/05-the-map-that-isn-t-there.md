## 5. The Map That Isn't There

Wren had stolen things before—candles, apples, a silver buckle once, from a merchant so fat he couldn't have chased her if she'd robbed him in his own kitchen. But she had never stolen a truth, and she found it sat differently in her pocket. Heavier. The scarred palm of Elodie Ravensworth, glimpsed by firelight when the bandits fell and the world went strange and ringing with words that were not quite words—that was hers now, whether she wanted it or not.

She wanted it. That was the shameful part.

For three days she watched him. Not obviously; Wren had grown up learning to watch without watching, an orphan's first skill, older than reading. She watched him rub his left palm against his thigh when he thought no one saw, the way a man worries an old burn. She watched him flinch when Sera joked about the queen's new writs, laughing too easily, agreeing too fast. She watched him stand at Greyhollow's eastern rim at dusk, looking down at the valley where the king's road ran, and she watched his mouth move—just slightly, just once—as though tasting a word and refusing to swallow it.

He had saved them all. He had spoken *something* on that road, and four armed men had dropped their blades as if the steel itself had turned traitor in their hands, and Wren had seen his face while it happened. It hadn't looked like power. It had looked like drowning.

So on the fourth night, when the haven's fires burned low and Bram had gone to check the snares and Sera was deep in her cups arguing tariffs with old Fenwick, Wren followed Elodie to the woodshed where he kept the ledgers.

She didn't knock. She stepped inside and said, "I know what you are."

* * *

The lamplight caught him badly. He was seated at the plank table with a ledger open before him, and for one long moment he simply looked at her, and Wren understood that she had made a mistake—not in coming, but in thinking she would be the one in control of this conversation. His stillness was not the stillness of guilt. It was the stillness of a man measuring the distance to every exit.

"You know what I am," he repeated. "Say it, then."

"A swordspeaker." Her voice held. She was proud of that. "The last one. The ones the queen is hunting. The ones they burned at Carrowmere when I was six." She had been six. She remembered the smoke on the wind for two days, and her grandmother closing the shutters and saying nothing at all, which from Grandmother had been louder than screaming.

Elodie closed the ledger. Slowly, deliberately, like a man putting down a weapon. "And what do you imagine happens now? You run to Sera? To Hale's soldiers?" He shook his head. "Wren. If you're going to threaten someone, don't do it alone in a woodshed where no one would ever find your body."

"That's not—" Heat climbed her neck. "I'm not threatening you. I'm offering."

That stopped him. She saw it land, saw the word go into him like a splinter working toward the heart. "Offering what?"

"Help." She pulled the bundle from under her cloak and set it on the table between them: a square of oilcloth, and inside it, wrapped in wool gone grey with age, a strip of leather. Tooled leather, cracked and dark, worked over with marks that might have been decoration or might have been writing. "My grandmother's. She died two winters back. Before that, she talked. All the time, when she'd been drinking, about Thornfell. Not the mountains—the kingdom. The one that isn't on any map anymore."

Elodie didn't touch the leather. "There is no kingdom of Thornfell. There hasn't been for three hundred years. The maps were burned, the records sealed, the name struck from—"

"From law and language both, yes, I know the story." Wren sat down across from him, uninvited, because sitting felt braver than standing. "Everyone knows the story. Nobody knows why. My grandmother knew why. She said her grandmother told her, who was told by hers, all the way back to the erasure. They were boundary-keepers. Little folk, low folk, the people who walked the lines between the kingdoms and set the stones. And she said—" Wren took a breath, because she had carried this sentence alone for years and it had gotten heavy— "she said the last true thing of old Thornfell wasn't destroyed. It was hidden. A door. And it only opens for an oath freely spoken, and it will close forever when the moon finishes its thirteenth turn."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Elodie's face did something complicated—hope and horror fighting for the same ground—and Wren realized with a small cold shock that he already knew. Some part of him had always known. That was why he hid in these mountains, of all the mountains in the world. That was why he guided smugglers along passes no honest map showed.

"The thirteenth turn," he said quietly. "That's this moon. The hunter's moon. It fills in eleven days."

"I know."

"And you've known since when?"

"Since I saw your hand." She lifted her chin. "Since I heard you speak on that road and the air went strange. I started counting that night."

For a long time neither of them spoke. Somewhere outside, Sera's laughter rolled through the haven wall, and the sound of it made Wren's chest ache. Everything out there—the banter, the bad wine, Bram's silent grins, the whole crooked warm machinery of Greyhollow—all of it stood on ground Elodie had never told them was hollow.

"Why didn't you tell me?" he asked finally. "Three days. You could have sold me to the crown for more coin than you'd see in ten lifetimes of smuggling."

"Because you'd have run," Wren said. "And then we'd all be dead anyway, because the queen's hounds are coming regardless, and the only person in this haven who knows anything worth knowing is you." She pushed the leather strip an inch closer to him. "Besides. You lied to us. I lied to you. We're even. Now we can start being honest, if you can stomach it."

Something moved through his expression then—something that might have been the first real smile she'd seen on him. It was tired and crooked and it didn't stay long. "You're thirteen."

"Fourteen."

"Gods help us all."

* * *

They worked by lamplight until the candle guttered, and then by the grey pre-dawn, and what they found in the leather strip was not a map. That was the first lesson, and Elodie taught it to her, tracing the tooled lines with one careful finger.

"Boundary stones," he murmured. "These aren't roads. Look—the marks repeat in groups. Three, seven, three again. These are counts. Distances between stones." His finger stopped. "But there are no stones like this in any survey I've ever read."

"Because the surveys were of the new kingdom," Wren said, and felt the pleasure of knowing something he didn't, a rare and delicious sensation. "Grandmother said the old stones weren't destroyed either. Just demoted. Farmers used some for gateposts. Some got buried. Some are still standing out there, and everyone thinks they're just... rocks." She tapped the strip. "This is a list of them. Twelve stones. And each stone has a mark cut into it that matches one of these." She pointed to the small glyphs clustered along the strip's lower edge. "Grandmother called them the twelve truths. She said each stone keeps one, and together they say the way to the door."

"So we find the stones, we read the marks, and the marks tell us where the door stands." Elodie leaned back, rubbing his eyes. "Eleven days, twelve stones, half of them possibly buried under three centuries of farms and roads."

"Ten days now. It's past midnight." Wren tried to keep her voice light and failed. "And the ninth stone is under the mill at Lowbridge, if Grandmother was right, and the miller won't let anyone near his foundation stones without a fight."

Elodie laughed—a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. Then he grew serious, and turned the leather over, and was quiet for a while.

"Wren." He didn't look up. "What I did on that road. What you saw. I need you to understand something before you put any hope in me. The gift doesn't feel like telling the truth. It feels like *winning*. Every oath I speak, I can feel how much more I could take. How much I could bend. When those bandits dropped their swords, some part of me wanted to make them kneel. Wanted to make them forget their own names. And the frightening thing isn't that the gift wants that." He looked at her then, and his eyes were the eyes of a man reporting his own treason. "It's that I couldn't swear honestly which part of me wanted it."

Wren considered this. She was fourteen and an orphan and a thief, and she had spent her whole life around liars, and she knew the species well. Liars lied for gain, mostly, or fear. But there was another kind—the kind who lied to themselves last and best, and those were the dangerous ones. Elodie was confessing to being exactly that kind.

"Then don't use it," she said simply. "Not for the door. Grandmother was very clear. The door opens for an oath *freely spoken*. Not forced. Not bent. Not powered up like you did to those bandits. A true oath, plain as bread." She shrugged. "Maybe that's the whole point of it. Maybe the old Thornfellers built a door that liars can't open."

He stared at her. "How old are you?"

"Fourteen. Stop asking."

* * *

They went out at first light, before the haven woke, climbing the sheep track above Greyhollow to where the land flattened into old pasture. And there it was—or the ghost of it. A squat grey pillar leaning in the grass like a drunkard asleep, its carvings worn to shadows, lichen mapping continents across its face.

The first stone.

Wren crouched and swept the growth away with her sleeve, and there beneath the crust of moss was the glyph—small, deliberate, unmistakably the twin of the first mark on her grandmother's leather. She touched it and the stone was cold even in the morning sun, colder than stone had any right to be.

"It's real," she whispered. "Grandmother, you old drunk, it's *real*."

Beside her, Elodie had gone very still. He had his scarred palm pressed flat against the stone—not reading it, Wren thought, but listening—and when he spoke, his voice came out strange and soft.

"There's something underneath the words. Underneath everything. Like a room behind a wall." He pulled his hand back as if the stone had bitten him. "It's waiting, Wren. It's been waiting three hundred years, and it knows what I am."

"Then it knows you're the last one," Wren said. She stood, brushing dirt from her knees, and looked east toward Lowbridge, where the second stone waited under the mill, and beyond it the other ten scattered across a country that had forgotten its own name. Ten days. Twelve stones. One door.

"We should tell Sera," she said.

"No." Elodie's answer came fast and hard. "No one. Not yet. If Hale finds out what we're looking for—"

"Hale?"

Elodie's jaw tightened. "The queen's inquisitor. She arrived in Thornfell two weeks ago. I've heard the names of the villages she's visited." He met Wren's eyes, and whatever he saw in them made him add, quieter: "She was a swordspeaker once herself. Before she gave them all to the fire."

The wind came down off the high passes and moved through the grass, and far below them Greyhollow's chimneys began to smoke, one by one, the little signals of the family that didn't know yet what its youngest member knew.

"Ten days," Wren said. "Then we'd better start walking."

And somewhere south, down where the valleys narrowed and the king's road ran straight as a blade, the hounds were already walking too.
