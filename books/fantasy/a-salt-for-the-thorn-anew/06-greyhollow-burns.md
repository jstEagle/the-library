## 6. Greyhollow Burns

The ravens came first. Elodie saw them from the ridge above Greyhollow, a black spill of wings rising off the pines in ragged spirals, and his stomach turned to stone before his mind caught up with the reason. Ravens did not lift from a haven at dusk unless something had disturbed the trees. And nothing disturbed the trees above Greyhollow except soldiers.

He counted them as he descended through the scree, the way Bram had taught him years ago—count what you can carry, leave what you can't. Thirty riders, maybe more, fanned along the valley floor with torches burning blue-white in the failing light. Not pitch. Alchemist's fire, the kind that burned cold and bright and made shadows sharp enough to cut. The kind used for interrogations.

At the center of the camp stood a pavilion he did not recognize, and before it, a scaffold that had not existed yesterday. Soldiers had raised it from Greyhollow's own timber—the long beams of the old drying shed, the planks Sera had oiled every autumn against the wet. They were building it into something with a crossbar and a rope, and they were doing it slowly, deliberately, within sight of the haven's single door.

Elodie pressed himself flat behind a granite shoulder and felt the oath-scar on his palm begin to ache, deep and rhythmic, like a second heartbeat. It always ached when truth was near. It was aching now because somewhere below him, tied to chairs in their own common room, were the people who had taught him to count what you can carry.

* * *

Sera Voss had been captured once before, at nineteen, by river pirates off the Weirstone flats, and she remembered it as an inconvenience punctuated by violence. This was different. This had the shape of ceremony.

They had bound her wrists with silk cord—not rope, silk—and seated her at her own table, in her own chair, facing the door. Across from her sat a woman in grey with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a ledger: patient, balanced, waiting to be read. Behind the woman stood six soldiers and a clerk with a portable writing desk, and beyond them, visible through the window slit, the scaffold taking shape against the sky.

"You're Captain Voss," the woman said. "Inquisitor Hale. I've read your seizure records. Eleven cargoes impounded over nine years, no convictions. You're very good."

"Thank you," Sera said, because her mouth had always moved faster than her judgment.

Something flickered at the corner of Hale's mouth. Not quite a smile. "I'm not here for you. I want you to understand that. Smuggling is a wound; I'm hunting the blade." She laid a document on the table between them, squared its edges with two fingers. "A swordspeaker has been operating out of these mountains. Someone guided your caravans through passes that don't appear on any crown survey. Someone whose oaths could open locks, still blades, bend men's wills. I believe you know him."

"I know every guide from here to the Salt Coast," Sera said. "Most of them are liars. None of them are sorcerers."

Hale nodded slowly, as if Sera had confirmed something rather than denied it. The clerk's pen scratched. "Do you know what a swordspeaker's oath does, Captain? When one binds steel to truth, the steel remembers. A man commanded by an oath will swear afterward that he chose freely. He'll defend the choice with his life. I have seen villages do exactly that." Her voice never rose. It didn't need to. "That is why there can be no trial for the gift itself. Only removal. But harboring—" she tapped the document, "—harboring is a crime with a procedure. Trial. Sentence. Execution, if the writ holds. Publicly. Here, where your people can watch."

"You're going to hang smugglers on a scaffold built from our own shed," Sera said, "to bait a man you haven't named."

"I'm going to post a promise," Hale corrected gently. "Promises travel faster than soldiers. Especially promises made by someone who keeps them." She rose, gathered her papers, and paused at the door. For a moment the ledger-eyes softened into something almost human. "Captain. If he comes for you—and he will—I want you to tell him I said this: whatever he offers, the gift will take more than he thinks he's paying. I know the exchange rate personally."

Then she was gone, and Sera sat alone with the scratching of the clerk's pen, writing down everything she'd said, and understood with cold clarity that even her denials were being filed as evidence. Through the window, the scaffold stood finished against the last of the light, and Bram, deaf as bedrock, was watching her from across the room and reading her lips around one silent word: *run*.

* * *

Elodie reached the back wall of the haven an hour past full dark, moving through the drainage cut Wren had shown him only three weeks ago, laughing about how no soldier would ever be stupid enough to follow it. His hands shook so badly he could barely grip the iron rungs.

Inside, the common room stank of alchemist's fire and fear. Fourteen of his family knelt in rows under guard. Sera at the table, wrists silk-bound, chin up, furious and magnificent. Old Marta with her bad hip kneeling anyway. The twins, Joss and Pell, sixteen years old, trying to look brave and managing only to look young. And Bram, huge and calm, positioned deliberately between the guards and Wren's empty place.

Wren's empty place.

Panic hit him like cold water. Then a hand closed on his ankle in the dark beneath the stair, small and strong, and he nearly cried out before he saw her face—smudged, tear-tracked, alive. She pressed a finger to her lips and pointed upward, toward the loft, toward the loose board where Elodie had hidden the decoded boundary-stone rubbings, and he understood: she had been in the loft when they came. She had watched the whole raid through the floor gaps. She had seen which way the guards looked and which way they didn't.

*We go now,* she mouthed. *You can't save fourteen. You can save the map.*

It was the cruelest true thing anyone had ever said to him, and it was Wren, so of course it was.

He went to Sera first. There was no helping it; his feet simply went. He knelt beside her chair as if adjusting his bootlace and whispered against her ear, fast and low: "Back gate, three nights, moonless hour. If I'm not there, take the children east to Cousin Aldous at the salt pans. Don't wait. Don't ever wait for me again."

Sera's jaw tightened. She stared straight ahead at Inquisitor Hale's pavilion through the window and answered without moving her lips, a smuggler's trick, breath shaped into words: "If you trade yourself for us, Edmund Ravensworth, I will come back from the grave to strangle you." She had never used his true name aloud before. It fell between them like a dropped knife.

"I won't," he lied.

"I know," she breathed. "That's what frightens me."

A guard turned. Elodie slid back into shadow, took Wren's hand, and went down through the drainage cut while behind them the alchemist's fire burned cold and the clerk's pen kept scratching, recording, preserving, promising.

* * *

From the ridge on the far side of the valley, they stopped once—because Wren stopped, because she had earned the right to stop—and looked back.

Greyhollow glowed like a coal in the dark, ringed with cold fire. At the edge of the light, a soldier was nailing a proclamation to the great pine at the valley mouth, and even at this distance Elodie could read the shape of the letters, because he had helped draft proclamations once, in another life, in a court that no longer admitted he'd existed. He knew the format. Promise of trial. Promise of sentence. Promise of execution for any soul found harboring a swordspeaker. And beneath all of it, the line that was meant for him alone, the hook dressed as mercy:

*Let the gifted come forward. Let him weigh his gift against fourteen lives. The crown is generous. The crown waits.*

"They're not going to wait," Wren said quietly. "She's not going to wait. That woman doesn't build scaffolds she doesn't use."

"No," Elodie said. His voice came out scraped hollow. "She builds them early so we can watch them being built. So we learn to count the days."

Below, the fires burned. Above, through torn cloud, the moon rode thin and white—not yet full, not yet the thirteenth turn, but filling, filling, a coin being saved toward some terrible purchase. Elodie looked at it and then at his scarred palm, and the scar pulsed in answer, hungry, awake, offering.

Fourteen lives. One door. One oath, freely spoken, that would cost him everything he was.

"We go to the stones," he said. "All of them. Tonight. Before the moon fills."

Wren squeezed his hand, hard, a child's grip with a smuggler's strength. "Then we run," she said, "and this time, we don't hide."

Behind them Greyhollow burned cold and bright, and ahead the mountains opened their old erased roads, and Elodie ran toward the door that wanted his name, carrying fourteen reasons in his chest like coals he refused to set down.
