## 7. Bargains with Traitors

The writ was nailed to the chapel door in Lowmere, and beneath it someone had chalked a child's drawing: a stick figure with a sword, and a crown above it, and the words *she will find you*.

Elodie stood before it in the grey hour before dawn, hood up, breath steaming, and read the thing for the third time. The language was careful. That was what frightened him most. No ranting, no fire-and-brimstone. Just a list of names — Sera Voss, Bram Ostrey, Wren of Greyhollow, eleven others — and their crimes, which were not smuggling at all but *harboring*, and the sentence, which was trial by tribunal at Thornhold on the turn of the thirteenth moon, with execution to follow conviction. And at the bottom, in ink still sharp after two weeks of mountain weather:

*Let the swordspeaker come forward. His family's fate is his own.*

His family. She had used the word deliberately. Inquisitor Hale knew exactly what she was doing, and she had done it well.

He had spent six days telling himself there was another way. Six days of circling Thornhold's outer ridges like a moth around a lamp, mapping guard rotations he had no army to exploit, counting soldiers he could not kill without becoming the monster they said he was. Six days of Wren watching him with her grandmother's stubbornness in her face, saying nothing, because she understood that some doors a man has to walk through alone or not at all.

On the seventh night he left her sleeping under a shelf of granite with a fire banked small, took the pass road down into the valley, and walked toward the enemy camp with his hands empty and his heart hammering like a boy's.

* * *

They let him through.

That was the first sign of how thoroughly he had been expected. A sentry saw him coming across the frost-burned meadow, raised a horn, and then — visibly, deliberately — lowered it again. Word passed down the picket line like water finding its level. By the time he reached the palisade gate, two soldiers were holding it open, and neither would meet his eyes. They were young, both of them. Farm boys conscripted into a hunt they did not understand, standing in a country whose name had been scraped off every map for thirty years.

Inside, Thornhold was less a fortress than a wound. The old keep of the erased kingdom had been garrisoned by Marenne's forces a generation ago and never loved, and it showed — scaffolding rotting against walls no one repaired, banners of the new queen hung over masonry carved with thorn-roses by hands long dead. Torches guttered in iron brackets. Somewhere a prisoner coughed, deep in the stone, and the sound went through Elodie like a blade.

An officer met him in the yard. A captain, gray at the temples, sword worn honest at the hip.

"You're the one," the captain said. Not a question.

"I'm the one."

"She said you'd come tonight." He almost smiled, and it made him look briefly human. "She said you'd come the moment the moon cleared the ridge. I lost a week's wages betting against it." He gestured toward the keep. "Inquisitor Hale is waiting. Alone, as you asked. If you try anything in there—" He stopped. Reconsidered. "No. You won't. That's the whole trouble with your kind, isn't it? You can't lie where it matters."

Elodie said nothing. But the words followed him up the stairs like a second set of footsteps.

* * *

Hale had chosen the old map room, and Elodie understood why the moment he entered.

It was a long chamber under a barrel-vaulted ceiling, lit by a single brazier, and its walls were hung with charts — not the new kingdom's maps, with Thornfell rendered as blank parchment and a printed legend reading *UNSETTLED TERRITORY*, but older ones, salvaged from who knew where, showing the mountains in their true shape with their true names. Rivers that had been renamed. Roads that had been struck through. A whole country murdered by cartography, and here it all was, pinned to the walls like butterflies.

Hale stood among them with her back half-turned, feeding papers to the brazier one at a time. She was smaller than he remembered from the proclamation woodcuts, and plainer, with a broad peasant face and ink-stained fingers, and she moved with the unhurried economy of someone who had stopped performing for anyone years ago. On the table between them lay a single candle, unlit, and two chairs.

"Elodie Ravensworth," she said, without turning. "Son of Aldous Ravensworth, sworn swordspeaker of the last true court of Thornfell. Recorded oath-taker, forty-one bindings, all lawful, all witnessed. You were fourteen when the purges began." She dropped another paper into the flames. "I've read everything. There is very little about you that isn't in an archive somewhere. Did you know that? You hid for ten years, and the whole time you were a file in a drawer in the capital."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know what you want." Now she turned, and her eyes were steady and tired and terribly kind, which was worse than cruelty. "You want your people released. You're offering yourself in exchange. It's written all over you — you have the look of a man who rehearsed a speech the whole way down the mountain and is dreading having to give it." She pulled out a chair and sat, folding her hands. "So don't give it. Sit down. Let's skip to the part where we talk honestly, since honesty is supposedly your religion."

He sat. The chair creaked. Up close he could see the scar at her collarbone, thin and white and old — the mark of a binding broken, or refused, or survived. Every swordspeaker carried one. His own lived in his left palm, a seam of silvered tissue he had hidden under gloves and lies for a decade.

"Bargain accepted," Hale said. "You surrender. They go free. I'll put it in writing, sealed, witnessed by whatever god you like. I have that authority; the writ gives me discretion in sentencing harboring charges, and harboring is all they're guilty of." She said it flatly, a fact laid on a table. "But you should understand what you're buying them with, and what you're buying them *from*. So ask me. You came all this way. Ask me the question you've been carrying."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. The brazier popped. Somewhere below, the coughing prisoner fell silent.

"Why?" he said finally. "You were one of us. The records say so — I checked, before I came. Serah Hale, oath-bound to Lord Corvane of the Marches, the youngest speaker ever recorded. Then Corvane fell, and you didn't run. You turned. You built the hunt that's hunting me." His voice roughened despite himself. "Why?"

For the first time something moved behind her composure — not pain exactly, but the scar tissue where pain had healed badly.

"Because I know precisely how much good a swordspeaker can do," she said, "and precisely how little it costs him to do it. You think the gift is a burden. I thought so too, once. We tell ourselves that story, don't we? That our oaths bind *us*, that we pay and pay for the power we hold. Here is what actually happened." She pulled back her collar, fully, letting the torchlight find the scar. "Corvane held my oath. Not my loyalty — my *oath*. I swore myself to his justice when I was sixteen, believing he meant the word the way I meant it. And for nine years he used my voice the way a man uses a tool. I bound witnesses to testify falsely and called it truth. I bound debtors into servitude and called it law. I bound a woman to silence about what her husband did to her children, because the husband paid Corvane's taxes, and my mouth made the silence iron." Her voice did not shake. That was the horror of it; it was worn smooth as river stone. "And every time, the gift felt *good*, Ravensworth. That's the part no archive records. When you speak a binding, the world aligns itself around your word, and for one shining moment you are the most certain creature alive. Certainty is the sweetest poison ever brewed. I drank it for nine years and called it service."

"And then?"

"Then Corvane ordered me to bind a village to forget a massacre he'd ordered himself. Four hundred people. And I stood in the square with the words rising in my throat, and I discovered that I couldn't refuse — not because he compelled me, but because I had sworn, and the oath was in me deeper than my own will. I opened my mouth to speak the forgetting." Her thumb moved once across the scar, unconscious, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "And I bit through my own tongue instead. That's how the binding broke. Pain. The only key that fits a lock you built yourself."

The brazier settled. Elodie realized his hands were fists in his lap, and that his scarred palm ached in sympathy, the way old wounds ache before weather.

"So you decided the gift must die," he said quietly.

"I decided that no one should hold it. Not the corrupt, who will sell it. Not the righteous, who will *believe* in themselves, which is worse." She leaned forward. "Listen to me, because I am going to say this once and I am not your enemy, whatever the writ says. The gift always takes more than it gives. Always. It takes your certainty first, then your judgment, then your ability to hear any voice but your own. You've felt the beginning of it — don't pretend you haven't. Whatever you did to survive these ten years, whatever oaths you whispered in the dark when no one watched, you've felt the pull. The sweetness. Tell me I'm wrong."

He could not. The ambush on the ridge road was three weeks gone and it lived in him still: Wren screaming, the bandits' blades, and the words pouring out of him like light out of a cracked lantern — *be still, be bound, be done* — and the terrible, singing rightness of it, the way the world had bent around his voice like iron to a lodestone. He had saved everyone. He had frightened himself more than any enemy ever had.

"No," he said. "You're not wrong."

"Then understand the bargain you're making. You're trading your freedom for theirs, and it's a clean trade, and I'll honor it. But you're also handing the crown a swordspeaker, and the crown does not want you dead, Ravensworth. Dead martyrs are inconvenient. They want you *bound* — sworn to Marenne's justice, your voice nailed to her law like a bell to a church tower. Think what that means. Think what my nine years meant. That is the future you're buying for your family with your body." She held his gaze. "I'm telling you this because no one told me. Because I was sixteen and certain, and certainty is how they get you."

Elodie sat very still. Outside, through the arrow-slit window, he could see the moon climbing — swollen now, days from full, days from the thirteenth turn. Behind it, far up in the black shoulder of the mountains, a seam of stone waited that answered only to an oath freely spoken. Wren's grandmother's door. The way *out*.

Hale watched him watch the moon, and something flickered across her face — recognition, maybe, or regret.

"There's something else," she said slowly. "Something in the old records I haven't burned yet. About the boundary stones. About what the erased kingdom kept behind its borders, and why the cartographers were ordered to leave it blank." She rose, crossed to the wall of maps, and rested her fingertips on one of the oldest — Thornfell whole and named, its heart marked with a symbol like a door seen edge-on. "You didn't come down that mountain only to bargain. You came because you already have another road. I can see it in you. So here is my last honesty, freely given, no oath required: whatever you intend to do before that moon fills — do it fast. Because the tribunal convenes in four days, and if you are standing in my custody when it does, I will not be able to protect you from what comes next. Not even with the bargain. Especially not with the bargain."

She held out her hand — palm up, scar exposed, the gesture of one survivor to another.

"Do we have terms?"

Elodie looked at the offered hand, and at the moon beyond the window, and heard Wren's voice in memory: *the door opens for a true oath, and closes when the thirteenth moon fills.*

Four days.

"Yes," he said, and took her hand. "We have terms."
