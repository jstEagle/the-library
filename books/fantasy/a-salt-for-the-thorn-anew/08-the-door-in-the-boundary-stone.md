## 8. The Door in the Boundary Stone

The stones began to speak on the third day.

Not aloud—Elodie had learned long ago that the mountains rarely wasted breath on words—but in the way old things speak: through placement, through wear, through the patient grammar of a kingdom that had been erased so thoroughly that only its bones remembered its name.

Wren walked ahead of him along the ridge, her grandmother's journal open against her forearm despite the wind, her lips moving as she matched the sketches to the country before them. She had not spoken much since Greyhollow burned. Grief sat on her the way ash sits on a hearthstone—thin, grey, everywhere—and she carried it without complaint, which frightened him more than weeping would have.

"There." She stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her. "The forked stone. Grandmother drew it with a lightning scar, see—" she tapped the page, "—and there it is."

The boundary stone rose from the heather like a broken tooth, taller than a man, split down its face by some ancient violence. Moss had claimed its lower half, but where the lichen thinned, Elodie could see the carving beneath: not the crown-and-cross of Marenne's writs, nor any sigil he knew from the smugglers' false papers. A door. Simple, rectangular, standing open, with a moon above it rendered as a circle left deliberately unfinished—a crescent that seemed, as the light shifted, to be closing.

"Thirteenth turn," Wren murmured. "The stones all carry it. The door and the unfinished moon."

Elodie laid his palm flat against the stone, careful to keep the oath-scar from touching it—an old habit, though the scar was only dead tissue now, a brand of what he'd sworn away and what he'd sworn never again to draw. The rock was cold in a way stone shouldn't be in late autumn. Not winter-cold. *Waiting*-cold.

"It's a marker," he said. "A signpost. The old kingdom marked its edges so its people could find their way home."

"Home from where?" Wren asked, and neither of them answered, because the answer was the thing they were walking toward.

* * *

Wren had grown up on stories the way other children grew up on bread—daily, unquestioned, sustaining. Her grandmother had told them by firelight in the haven's lower room while the smugglers argued over routes above: stories of Thornfell Before, when the mountains belonged to no crown because they belonged to their oaths. Stories of swordspeakers who did not hide. Who stood in market squares and spoke binding words over contracts and borders and marriages, and whose truth was trusted more than any king's seal, because a swordspeaker's lie would crack his own teeth and blacken his own tongue, and everyone knew it, and so no one needed to fear it.

"And then?" young Wren had always asked.

"And then a king decided trust was a throne he couldn't sit on," her grandmother said, "and burned the maps."

The maps were burned. But maps are drawn by the living, and the living die, and what they leave behind—their stones, their wells, their stubborn carved marks in high lonely places—that endures past the burning. Wren understood now why her grandmother had made her memorize the journal instead of simply reading it to her. *If they come for me, girl, the words have to live somewhere they can't be confiscated.*

She had thought, at eight years old, that this meant soldiers. It had taken her until Greyhollow's smoke was visible from the eastern passes to understand it meant queens.

Now she climbed behind Elodie through a defile so narrow the sky was a ribbon overhead, following stones that appeared at intervals like breadcrumbs left by a civilization: each one bearing the open door, the closing moon, and beneath them, in script older than any alphabet taught in Marenne's schools, a single repeated word that the journal transliterated as *freely*.

Freely. Freely. Freely.

"What does it mean, do you think?" she asked, when the defile widened into a hanging valley floored with grass gone gold and silver with frost.

Elodie didn't turn around. "It means the door can't be forced. Not by armies, not by lies, not even—" his voice caught on something, "—not even by an oath spoken under compulsion. It has to be given. Like everything true."

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen menhir, sharing dried plums and the last of Bram's smoked trout, and Wren watched Elodie stare at the moonrise and understood that he was counting. Two nights shy of full. The thirteenth turn. Whatever waited at the heart of old Thornfell was waiting too, and it was keeping time.

* * *

On the fifth day they came to the heart of the erased kingdom, and it was nothing like Elodie had imagined.

He had imagined ruins. He had braced himself for shattered towers, for the melancholy arithmetic of a dead nation's bones. Instead there was a valley that should not exist—sheltered, green going to rust-gold, with a stream running clean over white stones and terraces cut into the slopes where orchards had once grown and might grow again. No ruin. No rot. Only absence, kept tidy, as if the whole valley had been swept and shuttered by hands that intended one day to return.

And at the valley's far end, where the cliffs gathered themselves into a wall a hundred feet high, there was a seam.

It ran floor to crest, straighter than any fracture, thin as a knife-cut, and the stone on either side of it was polished smooth as river glass. As they approached, Elodie felt it before he saw anything change: a pressure behind his sternum, familiar and terrible, like the moment before an oath leaves the mouth. The gift waking in him after ten years of deliberate starvation, turning toward the seam the way a compass needle turns north.

"That's it," Wren whispered. "That's the door."

"How do you know?"

"Because," she said, and her voice shook, "it's the first thing I've ever seen that looks exactly like my grandmother's drawings. Everything else I've had to take on faith."

There was no handle, no hinge, no keyhole. There was only, at the height of a man's heart, a shallow recess in the polished stone—the size and shape of a hand.

Elodie knew, then. The way you know the last step of a staircase in the dark. He knew what the recess was for, and he knew what the word *freely* meant, and he knew—with a dread that came up through his boots like cold water flooding a hold—what the price would be. Doors out of the world do not open for free. Nothing in Thornfell had ever been free except the oaths themselves, and the oaths had always taken their toll in the coin of the speaker.

"Wren," he said. "Wait here."

"No," she said immediately.

"You don't even know what I'm going to—"

"I know you're going to do something noble and stupid and leave me standing in a field holding a journal." Her chin came up, and for a moment she looked so much like Sera—like the captain in the doorway of the haven, arms crossed, daring him to argue—that his chest seized. "Sera took us in because family doesn't wait outside doors. So either tell me what the price is, or I start reading every page of this book out loud until I find it myself."

* * *

The journal told him before he could decide whether to tell her.

They read it together by the stream, sitting shoulder to shoulder on a flat stone worn smooth by centuries of other sitters, and the entry was in her grandmother's careful hand, copied from somewhere older:

*The door asks the bond. Not blood, not life—the bond. What holds the speaker to the world he leaves: every tie, every debt, every name that knows his name. He steps through whole and hale, but he steps through alone, and those he leaves behind will forget the shape of him as a dream forgets itself by noon. This is mercy, say the old accounts. This is so the exiled do not grieve, and the grieving do not follow. I say it is the cruelest clause ever written into kindness.*

Elodie read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times his eyes slid off the middle lines as if the words were greased.

"Forget," Wren said quietly. "Forget *you*?"

"If I go through."

"But you wouldn't—you're not—" She stopped. Started again, angrier, which was how she handled fear; he'd learned that much about her in three years of teaching her card tricks and map-reading. "You're not actually considering it. After everything. After Hale, after the tribunal, after—we get them back, Elodie. We have two days. You walk in there, you bargain, you *oath* something—"

"And become exactly what Hale says I am." He heard his own voice come out flat and tired. "You've seen what the gift does when I feed it, Wren. In the pass, with the bandits. You saw my face after. Don't pretend you didn't."

She looked away, toward the seam in the cliff, and said nothing, which was its own admission.

"The full power," he went on, because once the dam cracked he could not stop it, "would let me stand in front of the queen's tribunal and bind every soul in that hall to whatever I spoke. Truth or lie—it stops mattering, that's the poison of it. At the deep end of the gift, my word becomes the world's word. I could free Sera and Bram. I could dissolve the hunt. I could make Marenne herself kneel in the mud and *believe* whatever I told her to believe." His hand closed over the scarred palm, an old reflex, hiding the wound from himself. "And I would never again know whether anything I wanted was mine, or just the gift wearing my face and calling its hunger mine. That's what it costs. That's what it *took* from Hale—I saw it in her eyes. She wasn't lying. The gift ate her life and left her convinced she'd chosen the meal."

"So you'll leave instead." Wren's voice cracked. "You'll walk through a hole in the world and let us forget you, and call that better? Elodie, we'd rather have you *corrupted*. We'd rather have you flawed and frightening and *here* than perfect and gone. That's what family *means*, you absolute—"

"Don't." It came out sharper than he intended, and he saw her flinch, and hated himself for it. He gentled. "Don't make me choose between two ways of losing you. Let me at least look at the door first. Let me understand it. That's all I'm promising."

Above the western rim of the valley, the sun was going down, and the moon was already up—pale, impatient, one night short of full, a circle with a sliver missing, like the unfinished moon carved on every boundary stone between here and the sea.

* * *

He went to it at dusk, alone, because some thresholds must be crossed unaccompanied even by the ones who refuse to wait.

The seam was taller close up than it had seemed from across the valley, and colder; frost feathered the polished stone around the hand-recess though the evening was mild. Elodie stood before it with his bare right hand hovering over the hollow, and the pressure behind his sternum swelled until his ribs ached with it, the gift straining toward the door like a dog toward an open gate.

*Speak freely,* the stones had said, five valleys' worth of *freely*, and he understood at last that the door was not asking for power. It was asking for honesty. An oath freely spoken—not coerced by desperation, not sweetened by escape, not bent into a bargain. Just true. Just given.

*I could say it now,* he thought. *I could put my hand in the hollow and swear myself out of the world, and by tomorrow none of them would ache for me. Sera would remember hiring a guide whose face she couldn't picture. Wren would have a journal and a gap where a teacher used to be. The hunt would find no swordspeaker because there would be no swordspeaker to find. Clean. Painless. For everyone but me, and I'd be beyond pain's reach too, wouldn't I. Beyond everything.*

His fingers trembled an inch above the stone.

Beyond everything. Beyond Hale's writ and Marenne's tribunal. Beyond the gift and its hungers. And beyond Sera's rough laugh, and Bram's silent, fluent hands, and Wren's furious, faithful heart. Beyond the smell of woodsmoke in Greyhollow and the taste of stolen apples and every small unglorious morning he had spent ten years building a life out of.

*Out* did not mean safe. Out meant *unwritten*.

Behind him, boots on frost-brittle grass. He didn't turn. He knew the stride—quick, stubborn, too loud for a tracker's daughter.

"They've moved the tribunal," Wren said, breathless, and the words fell into the valley like stones into a well. "I climbed the ridge to check the passes like you taught me. There are signal fires on the far peaks—three of them, crown-pattern. And I found this nailed to the last boundary stone below the valley." She pressed a paper into his hand, official vellum, the queen's seal fat and red in the dying light. "They're not waiting for the moon, Elodie. They've moved it up. The trial is *tomorrow*. Sera, Bram—all of them. Condemned already, unless someone speaks for them."

Elodie looked down at the paper. Then at the seam in the cliff, gleaming faintly now as the moon climbed, as if the door itself had begun to count the hours.

One night. One night to choose between the door that would erase him gently and the tribunal that would destroy him loudly—and somewhere between those two annihilations, a third road he could not yet see, narrow as the seam in the stone, that might cost more than both.

He turned from the door.

"Wren," he said. "How fast can you run?"
