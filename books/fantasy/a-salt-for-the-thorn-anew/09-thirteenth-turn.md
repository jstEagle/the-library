## 9. Thirteenth Turn

The moon rose over the tribunal square like a coin held up to a dying man's eye—full, and one turn past every moon that had ever filled before it.

Elodie stood at the edge of Verrance's great square, hood drawn low, and watched them build his family's scaffold by torchlight. The carpenters worked with the unhurried diligence of men who had done this many times: three posts, three crossbeams, a platform of pale new wood that still smelled of sap. Sera would hate that smell, he thought absurdly. She'd say it was the smell of being handled by amateurs.

Below the scaffold, in a ring of iron, knelt the people he loved. Sera Voss, chin high even on her knees, her braid torn loose. Bram with his hands bound before him, reading the crowd the way he read trails—by vibration, by pressure, by the tremor in the ground when soldiers shifted their weight. And behind them, eleven others from Greyhollow, grey-faced in the torchlight, waiting for a trial that everyone present knew was not a trial at all.

The tribunal dais stood opposite the scaffold, draped in royal blue. Queen Marenne herself had come, which told him everything. A queen did not travel to watch smugglers hang. A queen came to watch *him* hang—or to draw him out trying not to.

Inquisitor Hale stood at her right hand, robed in ash-grey, her hands folded. From this distance she might have been carved from the same stone as the courthouse behind her. But he had sat across a fire from her two nights ago, close enough to see the oath-scar on her own palm, old and white as a riverbed in drought. He knew what lived under that stillness. He knew what it cost to stand there.

*You will not win this way,* she had told him. *Whatever you're planning, the gift will take more than you have.*

*I know,* he had said. *That's rather the point.*

* * *

He had walked through the city gates without hiding, because hiding was finished. The thirteenth moon did not rise twice. Wren had wanted to come—he'd seen it in her face, that fierce, stubborn set of her jaw—and he had lied to her one last time, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

*Stay with the horses,* he'd said. *If I'm not back by dawn—*

*Then I'm coming in after you,* she'd said, *and we'll both be idiots together.* But she hadn't moved from the treeline, and he'd seen her understand, finally, what he was asking. Not obedience. Trust. The thing they'd been teaching each other since the boundary stones.

Now he crossed the square through a crowd thick as wheat. They had come from every village in Thornfell, drawn by proclamation and spectacle, and their faces in the torchlight were hungry and uneasy both. He heard his own name whispered—not Edmund Montrose, not the smugglers' quiet guide, but the older name, the erased one. *Ravensworth. They say a Ravensworth lives. They say the crown has writs for every soul who ever spoke steel true.*

The soldiers at the rope line saw him coming and did not stop him. Of course they didn't. Hale had left orders; he'd bet his life on it, and he supposed he was.

"Inquisitor," he said, standing at the foot of the dais. "I've come to surrender."

Hale looked down at him for a long moment. Torchlight moved across her face and found nothing to hold.

"You came to do more than that," she said quietly. Only she could hear it over the crowd's murmur. "I can see it in how you're standing. Swordspeakers always stand like that before the end—like men setting down something heavy."

"Then you know what I'm going to ask."

"I know what you're going to *do*." Her voice did not change, but something behind it did—a hairline fracture running through all that competence. "Elodie. There is another door. There is always another door. That is the first lie the gift ever tells."

"And the second?"

"That you'll survive using it." She turned to the queen. "Your Majesty. The swordspeaker Edmund Ravensworth, surrendered under the terms discussed."

Queen Marenne rose. She was younger than her coins made her look, and harder than her portraits. She surveyed Elodie the way a surveyor surveys land she intends to strip.

"The smugglers are condemned," she said, pitched to carry across the square. "Harboring a gifted traitor is treason against the crown and against the law that keeps honest people safe. Their sentence will be pronounced tonight, before this witness, so that all Thornfell understands what sheltering sorcery costs." Her gaze flicked to him, brief and cold. "Kneel with them, if you like. You'll hang beside them regardless. Your surrender changes nothing."

"It changes everything," Elodie said. "But not the way you think."

He stepped forward onto the open ground between dais and scaffold, into the space where every eye in the square could find him, and he drew back his hood.

The crowd's sound changed—the way wind changes before it decides to become a storm. Somewhere a child asked a question and was shushed too hard. On the scaffold, Sera lifted her head, and across forty feet of torchlit air her eyes found his, and he watched her understand, and he watched her mouth shape a curse at him that he would treasure for whatever time he had left.

He opened his scarred palm to the moon.

* * *

There is a grammar to oaths, and most speakers never learn it, because most speakers learn only the imperative: *steel, obey; truth, bend; enemy, break*. It is the grammar of command, and command is sweet, and sweetness is how the rot gets in. Elodie had spent ten years learning the other grammar—the declarative, the witness-grammar, the one that binds no one and compels nothing except the speaking of what is.

An oath of power says: *you will.*

An oath of witness says: *this happened.*

And an oath of witness, freely spoken, spoken whole, cannot be refused by anyone who hears it. Not because it enslaves them. Because it is simply, terribly true, and truth spoken whole leaves nowhere to stand except inside it or outside the human circle altogether.

He began quietly.

"I am Edmund Ravensworth," he said, "last swordspeaker of the line of Thornfell, and I speak under the thirteenth moon, and what I speak now is witness."

The words went out of him like heat out of an open hand. He felt the gift wake—not the intoxicating surge he'd tasted against the bandits, not the corrupting flood that had frightened him half to death, but something older and colder and cleaner, as if the gift itself recognized what he intended and braced itself like a soldier for a blow it could not return.

"Hear this," he said. "On the ninth day of the hunt, in the village of Lowmarsh, soldiers under writ of this crown burned a granary to drive out a suspected speaker, and the fire took four houses that had no gift in them, and a boy of six years died of smoke while his mother was held back by pikes."

The words left his mouth and entered the square like iron entering water. Every person heard them—not as accusation, but as fact, undeniable, unrefusable, laid down in their minds the way stones are laid in a road. Elodie felt them land. He felt the crowd's hunger curdle into something else.

"On the twelfth day," he said, "in Verrance itself, a woman named Tessa Corr was taken for harboring a speaker who proved to be no speaker at all, only a stammering man who had once been overheard swearing by his sword. She was questioned for nine days. She confessed to nothing, because there was nothing to confess, and she was sentenced anyway, because the writ does not require proof, only suspicion."

Queen Marenne had gone very still. "Guards," she said. "Take his tongue."

The guards did not move. You could see them trying—see the effort in their shoulders, the order straining against the oath's simple gravity—and failing, because the oath compelled nothing. It only made the truth impossible to unhear, and a man cannot cut out a tongue while his own mind is busy hearing.

Hale had closed her eyes. Her lips were moving faintly, and he realized she was not resisting. She was listening. Perhaps she had spent ten years waiting for someone to say these things aloud where power could not smother them.

"On the fifteenth day," Elodie said, and his voice was beginning to fray now, the gift drawing its price from his throat, his memory, the warm animal continuity of himself, "the haven called Greyhollow was burned. Its people were taken not for trial but for bait, by written instruction, which I have seen, which reads: *let the smugglers hang slowly and publicly, and the swordspeaker will come to us.* This happened. I bear witness that it happened."

He turned to the dais. His knees were shaking. Something was leaving him with every sentence—some load-bearing wall of himself coming down beam by beam—and he could feel the shape of the emptiness it would leave, vast and clean, like a house after the furniture is carried out.

"Queen Marenne," he said. "I do not bind you. No oath can bind a free crown—that is the oldest law of Thornfell, older than your maps. But I make you hear. And everyone in this square makes you hear, now, whether they wish to or not. Every farmer. Every soldier. Every mother who came for a hanging and stayed for the truth. You cannot execute my family tomorrow in front of a crowd that carries these words home in their chests. You cannot hunt the gifted in front of witnesses who have just learned what the hunting looks like from the inside. The writ is not broken. It is *dissolved*—not by force, but by light. An oath of witness, freely spoken, under the thirteenth moon. Let any lawyer in Thornfell find the flaw in it."

Marenne stared at him, and for one unguarded moment he saw not a queen but a woman doing arithmetic and hating the sum: the crowd, the words, the impossibility of a massacre that would live forever in ten thousand memories sharpened by oath. Her jaw tightened.

"Take them down," she said at last, to her captain, in a voice like frost cracking. "The smugglers are remanded. The tribunal will—" and here she faltered, because the tribunal itself was now a thing that had been witnessed, "—will reconvene. Under review."

* * *

It was Hale who caught him when he fell.

The gift did not go out like a candle. It went out like a river dammed at last after centuries—first a shudder through the whole channel, then silence, then a dry bed where something vast had run. He felt it leave and did not try to hold it. Holding it was the corruption. Letting go was the only clean act of his life.

He was aware of stone under his cheek, of Hale kneeling beside him with her hand pressed hard to his palm, of her saying his name in the flat careful voice of someone refusing to grieve in public.

"It's gone," he managed. "All of it. I can't—I don't feel it anymore. I don't feel anything *under* anything anymore. Just me."

"Yes," Hale said. "That's what freedom feels like. It's smaller than you expect. It stays smaller." A pause. "It's also yours."

Across the square, soldiers were cutting the prisoners' bonds. Sera stood, rubbing her wrists, staring at him with an expression he couldn't read at this distance—rage and relief braided tight. Bram was already scanning the crowd, reading exits out of habit. And at the square's far edge, small and furious and crying without shame, Wren was pushing through the rope line because of course she was, because she had never once done what she was told.

The moon stood at its height, full past full, the thirteenth turn complete. Somewhere north, in the heart of old Thornfell, a seam in a cliff was closing forever—and Elodie, giftless, empty-handed, lighter than he had been since he was nineteen years old, found that he did not want it anymore. He had thought the door was exile. He understood now that exile had been the price of keeping the gift, and he had paid differently instead.

Wren reached him first, dropping to her knees in the trampled snow, grabbing his collar with both hands.

"You absolute *liar*," she said.

"I know," Elodie said, and smiled, and meant it for the first time in ten years. "Ask me anything. I'll tell you true."

Behind her, Sera was coming, and Bram, and the whole ragged family of Greyhollow walking free across the square—and beyond them, at the dais, Inquisitor Hale was removing the ash-grey robe and folding it over her arm like a woman setting down a burden she had carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't hers.
