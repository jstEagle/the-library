## 2. Wards Like Old Scars

The border stones of Vaelmoor were older than the road that led to them, older than the village of Lowmark where Zephyrine bought their ink, older — Tam Voss insisted — than any record the ward-keepers' guild had ever bothered to keep. There were nine of them, one for each compass bearing and one for the center of things, though no one had ever explained to Zephyrine how a stone could mark a center it did not stand upon. They were grey as old teeth, taller than a man, and each was carved with a single line of script that ran from base to crown like a vein of silver someone had tried to scratch out and failed.

Zephyrine had mapped them eleven times.

They knew this the way they knew their own heartbeat, because they kept a ledger, and the ledger said so: *Border stones, survey the eleventh, autumn, light failing early.* Eleven surveys of nine stones in a valley whose borders never moved, whose roads never changed, whose rivers ran the same ten courses they had run since the first map Zephyrine ever drew with a child's unsteady hand. A cartographer could spend a lifetime on such a valley and produce nothing new. Zephyrine had spent what felt like a lifetime, and had produced everything, and had never once asked why the work felt less like mapping a place and more like keeping watch over it.

Until now.

Now there was Tam Voss walking three paces ahead with his guild coat turned inside out so the sigil faced his own heart instead of the world's eyes, and his boots crunching through frost that sang when it broke, and his voice pitched low in the way of a man who has learned that certain silences can be wounded by loud sounds.

"Touch nothing," he said, without turning around. "Not yet. First you look. Then you touch. Then, if the stone lets you, you write."

"I've touched them before," Zephyrine said. "Eleven times."

"And how did they feel?"

Zephyrine considered. The morning was thin and bright, the kind of light that made even familiar country look newly invented. Frost smoked off the fields in long white breaths. Somewhere behind them, a crow was worrying at something in a hedgerow with the patient malice of its kind. "Cold," Zephyrine said. "Like touching the far side of winter."

"Wrong answer," Tam said. "They should have felt warm. Body-warm. They're fed, you see. The wards eat slowly, all year, and give back warmth. Every keeper knows the stones by their heat before they know them by their script." He stopped at the crest of the low rise where the ninth stone stood — the northmost, the one Zephyrine's maps always rendered with a small careful annotation: *road ends here.* "If yours have been cold for eleven surveys, then either your hands are dead or these stones have been starving for longer than you've been alive."

Zephyrine looked at him. He was perhaps thirty, hard-used, with the raw-knuckled hands of someone who had done labor after his disgrace rather than starve respectably. His eyes were the pale grey of slate before rain, and they did not soften when he spoke, which Zephyrine had come to understand was not cruelty but economy. He spent nothing he didn't have to.

"You said cracked," Zephyrine said. "In your letter. You said come quickly, and you said *cracked from the inside*, and I want you to explain that sentence properly, because stone does not crack from the inside unless something inside it is trying to get out."

Tam's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "You'd have made a fair keeper, cartographer. Wrong trade. Come on."

* * *

The ninth stone stood alone in a field gone to stubble, and up close it was wrong in ways Zephyrine's eye catalogued faster than their mind could refuse them.

First, the frost. Every fence post, every stalk of stubble, every cowpat and clod within fifty paces wore its rime of white. The stone wore none. It stood in a circle of bare earth as if the cold itself had decided not to look at it.

Second, the birds. Zephyrine realized only now that the crow's racket had faded as they climbed the rise, and that there were no tracks anywhere near the stone — none of fox or hare or man since the last snow — though the field beyond was scribbled with them.

Third, the crack.

It began at the base, a hairline no wider than a knife's edge, and ran upward in a slow spiral, the way ivy climbs, and it was not dark. That was the thing Zephyrine would remember later, lying awake: the crack was not dark. It glowed faintly, a color with no honest name — the white of a star seen through smoke, the white of milk held too near a lamp — and the glow pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

"Eleven surveys," Tam said quietly. "Tell me this was here before."

"It wasn't." Zephyrine's voice came out smaller than intended. "I would swear it wasn't. I drew this stone in spring. Every face of it. There was no flaw. I remember thinking—" They stopped.

"Thinking what?"

"That it was the healthiest of the nine." Zephyrine crouched, slowly, the way one approaches a sleeping animal, and held their palm above the crack without quite touching. Warmth rose off it. Not body-warmth. Fever-warmth. The heat of something burning its own substance to keep going. "It's hot. You said they should be warm. This one's *burning*."

"Yes." Tam crouched beside them, and for a moment his professional stillness slipped and Zephyrine saw underneath it: a young man's fear, quick and animal, mastered but present. "That's what *cracked from the inside* means. A ward-stone isn't a wall, whatever the villagers think. It's a heart. It holds a charge and spends it against the border, all day, every day, holding the shape of the valley the way your hand holds the shape of a cup. When a stone fails, it cools. It goes grey. It crumbles. That's natural death, and we can mend it, feed it, ease it along." He nodded at the spiral seam. "That's not death. That's something eating its way out of the heart and using the heart's own fire to do it. I've read about it in the guild archives. One entry. Four hundred years old. The entry doesn't say what happened next, because the man who wrote it was the last keeper of wherever-it-was, and there was no one left to write the second entry."

The wind moved through the stubble with a sound like paper being turned. Zephyrine found they had risen to their feet without deciding to.

"The other eight?" they asked.

"Worse, most of them. The third stone's split clean down the script-line. The seventh hums — actually hums, you can feel it in your teeth. The center stone—" Tam paused, and Zephyrine watched him choose honesty over comfort. "The center stone is in the middle of the valley, under the shrine at Hollowmere. I haven't been to it. I'm afraid to be standing on it when it goes."

"When it goes," Zephyrine repeated. "You're speaking as if it's certain."

"Nothing about this is certain. That's the whole horror of it." Tam stood too, brushing frost from his knees with sharp, unnecessary motions. "Ward-work is the most certain craft in the world, cartographer. That's why I loved it, before. Ink fades, wood rots, kings die, but a true-set ward outlives its maker's language. And now every stone in this valley is dying in a way my order has no word for, and my order—" his jaw worked, "—my order declined to send anyone. So here I am. Exiled, disgraced, inspecting the border of a valley nobody important has ever heard of, because I was the only fool who answered your letter."

"Why did you answer it?"

He looked at Zephyrine then, a long look, and something passed behind the slate-grey eyes like a fish under ice.

"Because your letter described the cracks," he said finally, "in terms I'd only ever read in that one four-hundred-year-old entry. Word for word. Phrases I assumed were archaic flourish. *A seam like a vein of milk. A pulse like breath. The birds will not land.*" He tapped the ledger under Zephyrine's arm. "You wrote those words, cartographer. Before you'd ever seen a failing stone. Explain that."

"I can't."

"No," Tam agreed. "Neither can I. But I've decided to find it more interesting than frightening, which is a lie I tell myself daily, and it's working poorly. Walk the circuit with me. All nine. I want your eye on every stone, and I want your hand nowhere near any of them."

* * *

They walked the border the way pilgrims walk, sunwise, and the day aged around them, and with every stone Zephyrine's unease grew a new room.

The third stone, split down its script-line, wept a fine dust that fell upward — Tam swore it did, and Zephyrine watched it, grain after grain rising off the broken seam into the windless air like ash returning to a fire. The fifth stone had begun, impossibly, to sink; the earth around it was scoured down to clay in a perfect ring, as if the ground itself were flinching away. The seventh hummed, and the note it hummed was one Zephyrine knew. They could not say from where. It sat in their chest like a word remembered in a dream, meaning attached to nothing.

And through all of it, stranger than the stones, stranger than the upward-falling dust, ran the other thing — the thing Zephyrine could not put in the ledger because there was no column for it.

Homesickness.

It came on them at the fourth stone, sudden as a change of weather: an ache high in the breastbone, sweet and terrible, the feeling of standing in a doorway of a house you grew up in and cannot return to. Zephyrine stopped mid-stride and gripped the strap of their satchel and breathed through it, bewildered. Homesick. Here. In the valley where they had lived their whole life, in sight of hills they had drawn so often they could render them blindfolded. Homesick for what? For when?

For *where?*

"You've gone white," Tam observed, several paces on, without turning.

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of good paper. Sit down."

"I said I'm—" But the ache crested again, and this time it brought an image with it, brief and blinding: a harbor. Grey water, grey sky, gulls hanging motionless on a wind that smelled of salt and tar and some spice Zephyrine had no name for, and bells — many bells, bronze and deep, ringing from towers down a curved waterfront, and a door. A great door set into the cliff at the harbor's end, banded in bronze, carved with a dragon curled around a star.

Then it was gone, and Zephyrine was kneeling in frostbitten grass at the border of Vaelmoor with their heart slamming and their eyes wet, and Tam Voss was crouched in front of them saying their name in a voice stripped of all its economy.

"Zephyrine Eriksen. Look at me. What did you see?"

"A harbor." Their own voice sounded foreign. "There's no harbor in Vaelmoor. There's no sea within a hundred leagues. I saw a harbor, and bells, and a door, and I *grieved* for it, Tam, I grieved like it was my mother's kitchen, and I have never—"

They stopped. Because Tam had gone very still, and his face had changed, and the change was worse than fear. It was recognition.

"Say the door again," he said softly. "Describe it."

"Bronze-banded. Set in the cliff. A dragon curled around a star, cut deep into the—"

"*Don't*," Tam said, and his hand shot out and closed on Zephyrine's wrist, hard, and his eyes were fixed past them, up the slope, at the eighth stone — and Zephyrine turned and followed his gaze and felt the world tilt beneath them like a deck in weather.

The eighth stone's spiral crack had widened. And in the widening, in the milk-white glow of the seam, shapes were moving. Or — no. Not moving. *Showing.* Like images seen through smoke, there and gone: a curve of waterfront. Gulls. Bells.

A door, banded in bronze, carved with a dragon curled around a star.

"That's not possible," Zephyrine heard themselves say. "Stones don't remember. Stones don't *show*—"

"They don't," Tam said. He had not let go of Zephyrine's wrist. His grip trembled. "Unless the stone isn't showing you something. Unless it's *returning* something. Cartographer — how long has Vaelmoor been sealed? Truly sealed, wards on every border, no gate, no road out that leads anywhere but back in? Think. Don't guess. *Think.*"

Zephyrine thought, and the thinking was like pushing against a locked door in their own mind, and the door did not open, and behind it something vast shifted its weight.

"I don't know," they said at last. "I've never asked. No one asks. The maps show roads that end at the stones, and I've surveyed them eleven times, and I never once wondered why I never wanted to follow one past the end. Tam—" their voice dropped, "—why have I never wanted to leave?"

The wind died. The whole border went silent — no crow, no frost-song, no breath of air — and in that silence the eighth stone pulsed once, twice, three times, like a heart gathering itself.

And from somewhere far to the north, beyond the ninth stone, beyond the edge of every map Zephyrine had ever made, came a sound none of them had words for: a long, low exhalation, vast and weary, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Or waking.

Tam released Zephyrine's wrist and stood, and when he spoke, his voice was the flat, careful voice of a man reading his own death warrant aloud to check for errors.

"We finish the circuit today," he said. "All nine stones. And then, cartographer, you and I are going to sit down with every map you own — every map, do you hear me, including the ones you don't remember drawing — and we are going to find out what this valley is actually sitting on top of. Because I no longer believe these wards were built to keep something *out*."

Above them, the sky had begun, very faintly, to darken — though the sun stood at noon, and there was no cloud anywhere from horizon to horizon.

Only stars, coming out one by one, in a sky that should have been blue.

Stars that Zephyrine, with the certainty of a lifetime's habit, reached for their ledger to record — and found, when they opened it, that the page for today was already filled.

In their own handwriting.

With a date two weeks hence.
