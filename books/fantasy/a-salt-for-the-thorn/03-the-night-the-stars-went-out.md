## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The lamps of Vaelmoor were lit against nothing, as they always were, and Zephyrine Eriksen sat at their drafting table with a cup of cold tea and a map that refused to be finished.

It was the border again. It was always the border. They had drawn it four hundred times in eleven years — the long unbroken line where the valley met the world, rendered in careful sepia, annotated with elevations and stream crossings and the precise placement of every ward-stone. And every time, they arrived at the same problem: the line simply *stopped*. Not at a mountain pass or a river ford. It stopped because Zephyrine's pen stopped, because somewhere past the last ward-stone their imagination failed like a lamp running dry.

They had told themselves for years this was a quirk of temperament. A cartographer who loved one place too much to imagine another. There were worse afflictions. The valley rewarded devotion; it asked only that you stay, and stay, and stay, and never once ask what lay beyond the stones.

Tonight the tea had gone cold because Zephyrine could not stop looking at the northern stretch of the border, where Tam Voss had found the cracked stone three days ago. Cracked from the inside, he'd said, which meant nothing and everything. Ward-stones did not crack from the inside. Ward-stones were not *supposed* to have insides.

Zephyrine dipped the pen. Held it over the paper. Did not write.

Outside, the evening had gone strangely quiet. Not the soft quiet of a valley settling into dusk — the lamplighters' bells still rang on schedule, the millrace still muttered under the bridge — but a quiet underneath those sounds, like the silence beneath music. Like something holding its breath.

They set down the pen and went to the window.

Vaelmoor at nightfall: slate roofs going blue, woodsmoke standing straight up in windless air, the terraced fields above town stitched with the small moving lights of farmers walking home. The mountains shouldered the sky on every side, black against a sky that was —

Zephyrine frowned.

The sky was wrong.

Not cloudy. Not overcast. There were no clouds; there was nothing to be overcast *by*. But the stars — and Vaelmoor's stars were famous, were painted onto crockery and sung into lullabies, the Shepherd's Fork and the Salt Crown and the long bright river of the Queen's Spine — the stars were dimming. Not fading the way stars fade behind cloud, gradually, apologetically. They were going out the way candles go out: here, then not, with no interval between.

One by one. Then ten by ten. Then all at once.

Zephyrine's hand found the windowsill. The wood was real under their fingers — pine, sun-warmed earlier, now cooling — and they gripped it because the alternative was gripping nothing at all.

The sky was black. Not night-black. Night has texture; night is a country with weather and depth and a thousand familiar lights. This was the black of the inside of a closed eye. Of ink before the pen touches it. Of a page that has never been written on.

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, to the room, to the valley, to whoever kept accounts of such things. "No, that isn't —"

Below, the first bell began to ring. Not the lamplighters' bell. The alarm bell from the ward-house, ragged and urgent, and then a second bell answering from the far side of the valley, and a third, until the whole bowl of Vaelmoor rang like a struck glass.

* * *

They ran. Everyone ran. That was the thing Zephyrine would remember afterward, if there was an afterward — not the darkness itself but the sound of a whole town running, doors banging open along the lane, boots on cobbles, someone weeping without knowing why, dogs howling at a sky that gave them back nothing.

The ward-house square was full when Zephyrine arrived, breath burning, map-roll forgotten under their arm out of pure habit. Torches had been lit, and the torchlight made a small trembling island in a darkness that pressed close as water. Faces Zephyrine had mapped a hundred times — the miller, the ferryman's widow, old Sef with his bad hip — all of them tilted upward, all of them wearing the same expression: not fear exactly. Something worse. *Disbelief.* The look of people waiting for a trick to end.

Tam Voss stood on the steps of the ward-house with the guild's old surveying instruments scattered around him and none of them working. Zephyrine pushed through the crowd to him.

"Tell me it's cloud," they said. "Tell me some weather I haven't charted."

"It's not cloud." Tam's voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut from the inside. He held up a compass. The needle spun, lazy and continuous, a drunkard's turn with nowhere to point. "North's gone. All of it. Every bearing. The stones are dead, Zeph — all nine, not just the northern one. Dead like snuffed wicks."

"Dead how? Wards don't die. Wards are maintained, they're —"

"I know what wards are." For a moment something moved under Tam's flatness — fury, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "I was guild, remember. Before they threw me out. And I'm telling you these wards didn't fail. Failing looks like fraying, like thin spots, like rot at the edges. This looks like—" he searched for it, hating the word even as he said it— "*withdrawal.* Like something pulled out of them. From the inside."

Cracked from the inside. Zephyrine heard him say it again in memory, three days old, and felt the first true cold slide down through their chest.

Above the square, above the dead sky, the darkness *moved*.

It was subtle at first — a shimmer, like heat-haze inverted, like the air over a winter road. Then Zephyrine saw it clearly and wished they hadn't: at the valley's rim, where the mountains met the starless black, the outline of the world had gone soft. The ridge-line that Zephyrine had drawn four hundred times, that they could have traced blindfolded, was *blurring*. Edges that had been granite for as long as granite had meaning now rippled gently, the way paper ripples near a flame.

And along that rippling edge, here and there, tiny points of light were peeling away and drifting upward and going out. Embers. The valley was shedding embers into a sky that ate them.

"What is that," someone whispered. "What *is* that?"

"The boundary," Tam said quietly. He alone did not look away. "The wards held the valley's shape. Without them, the shape is coming loose." He turned, and in the torchlight Zephyrine saw that his hands were shaking and his voice was not, and understood that this cost him everything. "Listen to me. All of you. Get indoors. Bar your doors. Do not touch the edges of anything tonight — not the walls, not the fences, not the borders of your own beds. Things that lose their edges stop being things. Stay in the middle of your lives until morning."

"And if there's no morning?" Sef called out, and no one laughed.

"There'll be morning," Tam said. "I don't know whose."

* * *

Zephyrine did not go home. Or rather: they went home, bolted the door as instructed, lit every lamp they owned, and then stood in the middle of their own house feeling the instructions dissolve around them, because home was the one place the terror could reach them properly.

The maps helped. Maps always helped. They unrolled them across the floor — the valley surveys, eleven years of them, the obsessive loving record of a place that had never once tried to leave — and knelt among them like a person kneeling among friends. Here was the millrace with its October flood-marks. Here was the orchard road, the ferry crossing, the little fold of hills behind the temple where fog pooled on autumn mornings. Here was the border, drawn and redrawn, ending always in that failure of imagination.

Except.

Zephyrine lifted the oldest sheet, the first survey, done in their first year. The border there ended the same way — pen faltering, line dissolving into nothing. But tonight, in the lamplight, the nothing beyond the line was not empty.

There were marks in it. Faint. Bleeding up through the paper like damp through plaster. Not Zephyrine's hand — older, stranger, a script that hurt slightly to read — and beneath the script, ghostly, half-formed, the suggestion of *geography*. A coastline. Harbors rendered in dotted lines, as if the cartographer had been drawing from memory rather than sight. A word surfacing letter by letter across the strait of blank paper:

*Cael*

Zephyrine sat back on their heels. Their heart was doing something arrhythmic and strange.

"Cael," they said, testing it. "Cael... Adair?"

The word left their mouth and the room tilted.

It wasn't dizziness. It was worse than dizziness — it was the vertigo of a floorboard proving false, of a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. Because they knew that name. They knew it the way you know a word in a language you've never studied: completely, and with no idea how. Cael Adair. Harbor-bells. White roads. A door. The knowledge rose in them like water into a well shaft, sourceless, and with it came a feeling so violent and specific that Zephyrine pressed both hands flat to the floorboards to keep from being carried off:

Homesickness.

For a place they had never been. For harbors they had never seen, salt wind they had never breathed, a country that surfaced in their mind whole and shining and *gone*, gone the way a dream goes, and the grief of it doubled them over the maps of a valley that suddenly felt like a coat borrowed from someone larger.

"That's not mine," they whispered. "That's not — none of that is mine."

The lamps guttered. All of them, together, though there was no draft.

And then someone knocked at the door.

Three small knocks. Polite. Unhurried. The knock of someone who has walked a long way and knows the door will open.

Zephyrine crossed the room on legs that felt borrowed and opened it.

A child stood on the step. A girl, perhaps eight years old, in a nightdress far too thin for the season, barefoot on stone that should have been freezing and did not seem to trouble her. Her hair was pale as unbleached linen. Her eyes were the color of deep water, and they were calm — perfectly, terribly calm, in a night when every adult in the valley had come apart.

"You're the mapmaker," she said. Not a question.

"I'm — yes. Zephyrine Eriksen. Are you lost? Sweetheart, where are your parents?"

"They're asleep." She said it simply, the way you'd report weather. "Everyone's asleep except us. The ones who remember." She looked past Zephyrine into the lamplit room, at the maps spread across the floor, and her face changed — softened, brightened, the way faces change at the sight of home. "You have it on yours too," she said. "The bleeding-through. I can see it from here."

Zephyrine's mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"Yes you do." The girl stepped forward, and Zephyrine stepped aside without deciding to, and she walked into the house like someone returning to it. She knelt at the oldest map, the one with the surfacing word, and laid her small hand flat upon the ghost-coastline with the tenderness of touching a sleeping animal.

"Cael Adair," she said. "That's what's coming through your ink. That's what's on the other side of your border that doesn't go anywhere." She looked up at Zephyrine, and in the lamplight her eyes held the deep-water patience of someone far older than eight. "My name is Isolde. I remember it. I remember all of it — the harbors, and the dragon-priests, and the white roads, and the Door. Nobody else does. The grown-ups forgot, all of them, all at once, years before I was born. But I remember, and now the remembering is leaking, and that's why the stars went out."

She said it the way children say impossible things: plainly, without apology, leaving the impossibility to be someone else's problem.

"The stars," Zephyrine managed, "went out because you remember a country?"

"The stars went out because a country is *dying*," Isolde said. "Yours is next, if nobody holds the edges. You're the mapmaker. You hold edges for a living." She rose, small and certain in the failing light. "So I've come to ask you to map it. Before it finishes going."

"Map what?" Zephyrine heard their own voice crack. "Map what, Isolde? There's nothing there. There's never been anything there. My maps end at the border because there's *nothing* —"

But even as they said it, the words curdled in their mouth, because they could feel the lie of them now, feel it the way you feel a tooth that's gone bad. Nothing there. Then why did the name Cael Adair taste like childhood? Why did the ghost-harbors ache like a face seen in a crowd, known and unplaceable?

Isolde watched them struggle, and something in her calm flickered — pity, maybe, or recognition.

"You feel it too," she said softly. "Don't you. The missing place. Like a tooth-socket. Like a name on the tip of the tongue." She took Zephyrine's hand. Her fingers were warm — impossibly, unnaturally warm, warm as banked coals. "Come to the window," she said. "I want to show you something. And then I want to tell you about the dragon."

Beyond the glass, the valley's edges rippled in the dark, shedding embers into a sky with no stars, and somewhere out on the fraying border, faint and far and getting closer, something began to sing.
