## 4. What Isolde Remembers

Isolde had learned early that grown-ups only believed things they could trip over.

They believed in the table because their shins found it in the dark. They believed in rain because it wet their collars. They believed in Vaelmoor because they could walk to its edge and touch the ward-stones and feel the hum of them in their teeth, like a spoon held against a struck bell. But a country that lived only behind her eyes — a country with harbors full of tide-bells and streets that smelled of tar and honey — that, the adults would not believe, no matter how carefully she described it, no matter how much she ached.

So she had stopped describing it to them. Mostly.

But the night the stars went out, and the sky over Vaelmoor became a smooth black lid like the inside of a pot, and the edges of the world began to curl and brown like paper held too near a flame — that night, Isolde knocked on the cartographer's door, because if anyone alive was going to believe an impossible place, it would be someone who spent their life drawing places that did not exist yet.

The door opened before her second knock landed. Zephyrine Eriksen stood in the gap with a lamp raised, and behind them the whole room was lit and strewn with maps, every one of them pinned or weighted or draped, as though the house itself were made of folded paper. Zephyrine's face was the color of the ash drifting down from the fraying valley-edge. But their eyes — their eyes were awake. Frightened-awake. The way a person looks when they have finally seen the thing that has been standing behind them for years.

"You," Zephyrine said. "You're the miller's granddaughter."

"I'm Isolde." She hugged her coat around herself. The wind coming off the border-fry smelled wrong — like a book burning somewhere far away, like the world forgetting how to smell like anything. "And I remember something you need to hear."

* * *

Zephyrine did not send her away. That was the first miracle. They sat her by the stove and gave her tea too strong for a child and then stood among their maps with their back to her, hands flat on the table, shoulders up around their ears, waiting. Waiting was a kind of believing. Isolde took it.

"It's called Cael Adair," she said.

She watched the name land. She watched it land like a stone dropped into very still water, ripples going out through the room — the lamp guttering, a map unpinning itself from the wall and sliding loose, the kettle beginning without warning to sing.

"There's nobody called anything," Zephyrine said quietly. "There is no Cael Adair. There's never been—" They stopped. Their jaw worked. "Say it again."

"Cael Adair." She said it slowly, giving each syllable its proper weight, the way her — the way someone had taught her, long before Vaelmoor, long before the mill and the flour dust and the gray little years. "It was a nation. It isn't anymore. It was taken out. Not destroyed like a house burns down — you can still find where a burned house was. Taken out like a word scratched off a page so hard the page tears."

"By whom?"

"That I don't remember." This was the truth, and it shamed her. Her memories were not like ordinary memories. They came in pieces, and some of the pieces were more like feelings wearing shapes, and some of them were so vivid she could taste them, and between the two kinds there were holes, wide holes, and she had learned not to look straight down into them because looking made her dizzy in a way that felt like being unwound. "I remember what it was like. I don't remember how it ended. Only that it's ending still. Right now. Tonight. That's why the stars went out. The stars were ours, do you understand? Cael Adair had its own stars. Different ones. When they erased the country, the stars went into the bargain, and now the bargain is dying and it's taking everything back with it."

Zephyrine turned around slowly. "Everything meaning—"

"The people. Everyone who came from there. Even the ones who don't know they came from there." Isolde wrapped both hands around her cup. Her fingers were cold all the way through; the tea didn't seem to reach them. "Everyone born of the erased land. We're like ink that got blotted. While the blotting-paper stays pressed down, you can't see us. But it's lifting."

For a long moment the only sound was the kettle singing itself hoarse and, beneath it, faint as a heartbeat in another room, the sound of the valley's edges unraveling — a dry whisper, patient, enormous.

Then Zephyrine said, in a voice stripped down to boards: "Tell me about it. All of it. Everything you remember. Leave nothing out."

And oh, Isolde could have kissed them. Nobody had ever asked before. She put down her cup, folded her legs under her on the chair like a storyteller, and began.

* * *

"It had harbors," she said. "Not like here — here the water is just water, it sits in the lake and minds its own business. In Cael Adair the sea remembered every ship that ever crossed it, and at night it used to hum the ships' names back to the shore. There were bells on the harbor chains, and each bell rang a different family's name, and when your family's ship came home you always knew before you saw the sails, because the bell started ringing your name by itself."

She could see it. That was the terrible gift of it — she could see it better than she could see the mill kitchen where she'd eaten supper an hour before the stars died. The harbor at Serelai, white stone gone gold in evening light, gulls hanging motionless on the warm updraft, the smell of salt and pitch and frying fish and, underneath it all, faintly, sweetly, dragon-smoke. The long quay worn smooth by four hundred years of children running along it. Her mother's hand around hers, her mother's rings clicking, cold and real.

"There were dragon-priests," she went on, and her voice went strange on the words, went reverent without her permission. "Not priests of dragons. Priests who were partway becoming dragons, or dragons partway becoming priests — nobody could ever agree which direction it went. They tended the last fires. Every hearth in Cael Adair was lit from a mother-flame carried from the great fire, and the priests walked the whole country once a year relighting any hearth that had gone cold, because a cold hearth meant the family had forgotten something important, and they would sit with you, sometimes for days, until you remembered it."

"What sort of important things?" Zephyrine asked. They had come nearer without seeming to move. Their hands were trembling. So were their maps — no. No, their maps were trembling on their own.

"Small things," Isolde said. "That's what everyone gets wrong about remembering. They think the important things are big. But the priests said a country doesn't live in its kings, it lives in its kitchens. Whether your grandmother salted the bread before or after. What song your father hummed when he thought no one listened. Which stair creaked. Those are the load-bearing memories. Lose those and the whole country comes down even if the walls are still standing." She looked up. "That's what's happening now. The walls went first, years ago. Now it's the stairs creaking. Now it's the songs."

"And the door?" Zephyrine said. "You told the constable there was a door. He said you were making it up."

"He would." Isolde felt her chin lift, stubborn, the way it did. "Yes. There was a door. The Door Out. It stands — it stood — at the top of the Salt Stair, past the white road, in a place where the geography gets thin. Like the skin over your knuckles. You can see the light through it if you press." She spread her own small hand against her knee. "It's the way the country kept for itself. A way out that couldn't be mapped. The royal cartographers tried for three hundred years and every one of them failed, and that failure was the proudest tradition we had. Because a door that can't be drawn is a door that can't be erased."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Isolde saw the moment they landed in Zephyrine — truly landed — because the cartographer went white, whiter than the lamplight, and turned, very slowly, to look at the wall of their own work. Twenty years of maps. Vaelmoor rendered in loving, obsessive detail: every stream re-walked, every field re-measured, every road traced again and again in inks mixed to match the exact muds of the season.

Every road ending at the border. Every road leading nowhere.

"Zephyrine?" Isolde said.

"I need you to keep talking," Zephyrine said, in a voice like someone walking on ice. "About the geography. The shape of it. Where the mountains ran, where the rivers went. Everything."

"Why?"

"Because—" Zephyrine stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Because I think my ink already knows."

* * *

Isolde kept talking, and the impossible happened, and afterward she could never decide whether it was the most wonderful or most terrible thing she had ever witnessed.

As she spoke, Zephyrine's maps began to change.

It started with the coast. One of the older surveys hung nearest the stove, Vaelmoor's northern lake rendered in blue-gray wash, and while Isolde described the harbor of Serelai the lake's shoreline shivered — actually shivered, like a reflection disturbed — and stretched. The blue-gray bled outward, raggedly, eagerly, the way frost climbs a window. Within a minute the tidy lake had become a bay, and the bay had become a harbor, and piers were unfurling across the parchment in fine sure lines no human hand had drawn, and tiny bell-shapes bloomed at intervals along the quay like flowers made of notation.

Isolde stopped speaking. The map kept going anyway.

"It's listening to me," she whispered.

"No," Zephyrine said hoarsely. They had both backed to the middle of the room. Map after map was stirring on its pins, rustling like roosting birds. Ink moved beneath varnish. Rivers that had been Vaelmoor rivers bent themselves into new deltas; hills migrated; the careful stippled shading of the Thornwood crawled westward and reorganized itself into something taller, older, with mountains rising through it in gray peaks that hadn't existed ten minutes ago. "No, it's — it's remembering *through* me. Through my inks. Through—" Their voice cracked clean in half. "Through whatever I am, apparently."

The room filled with the smell of a country. Isolde recognized it before she could name it: tar and honey and dragon-smoke and the green cold of high salt marshes. It rose out of the drying ink the way perfume rises out of a crushed leaf. Somewhere in the walls, the house groaned, resettling, as though it too were being redrawn.

A third map slid entirely off its nail and drifted to the floor between them, landing face-up. Isolde looked down at it and felt the floor tilt.

It showed a coastline she knew. It showed the Salt Road, white as a scar, running from the lowlands up toward a mountain whose peak was drawn in a shape she knew better than her own hands — a spiral, like a horn, like a shell, like the inside of an ear listening. And at the summit, drawn in ink darker than any of the rest, darker than seemed possible, a small rectangle with a hairline crack down its center.

The Door Out. Drawn. Mapped. Impossible — and yet there, in Zephyrine's unmistakable hand, in Zephyrine's unmistakable hatching, in Zephyrine's own signature curled into the corner like a worm:

*Eriksen.*

"That's not mine," Zephyrine breathed. "I never drew that. I've never — I don't draw doors. I draw borders. That's all I've ever—"

But their hand was already reaching down. Isolde watched them pick it up. Watched them hold their own impossible signature an inch from their nose. Watched the color drain out of their face in stages, like water finding its level.

"Zephyrine," she said. "Your hand is shaking."

"I know," they said.

"It's shaking like you're trying to hold something down."

"I know."

Outside, the whisper of the unraveling edges grew a fraction louder. Ash ticked against the window glass, soft as moth wings. On the floor between them, the map of Cael Adair continued to bleed upward through the survey of Vaelmoor beneath it, harbor over lake, salt road over farm track, the erased country rising through the false one like a body surfacing through still water — and Isolde understood, with a child's terrible clarity, that the map was not changing at all.

It was being corrected.

"Zephyrine," she said softly. "Who taught you to draw?"

The cartographer did not answer. In the corner of the map, the signature *Eriksen* seemed, in the shifting lamplight, almost to darken — as if fresh ink were still flowing into it from somewhere, from twenty years away, from a hand that had signed it once and been made to forget.
