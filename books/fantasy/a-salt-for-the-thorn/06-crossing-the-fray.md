## 6. Crossing the Fray

The border of Vaelmoor had always been a line on Zephyrine's maps — a neat inked stroke running the valley's circumference, annotated in their own careful hand: *Here the world is kind. Here the world holds still.* They had drawn that line four hundred times and never once asked what lay on the other side of it.

Now they stood close enough to touch it, and it was screaming.

Not the wards — those had gone quiet at midnight, gone out like snuffed candles, and their silence was worse than any alarm. It was the *edge* that screamed: a sound like wind forced through a keyhole the size of the sky, a long high note with no beginning and no end, rising from the place where Vaelmoor's hills simply stopped being hills and became something else. Something unfinished.

Zephyrine stood at the crest of the last ridge with Isolde's small hand gripping two of their fingers and Tam Voss a pace behind, arms crossed against the cold, wearing the expression of a man who had been right about something terrible and found no comfort in it.

"There," Tam said. "Look at it properly. Don't flinch."

Zephyrine did not flinch. They looked.

The valley's edge was fraying. There was no truer word for it. The hillside beneath their boots continued northward for another thirty paces, forty, and then — not ended, not cliffed, not walled — *unraveled*. Grass became threads of grass became suggestions of green became a pale nothing that hurt to look at directly, the way a blank page hurts when you know words were written there once. Beyond the fray there should have been more world. There was instead a color Zephyrine had no pigment for and no name for either: not white, not grey, but the absence that remains when someone takes the map away from underneath the territory.

And through that absence, wind. Wind where there was nothing for wind to cross. It came off the wound in long, keeling gusts that smelled of salt and old paper and something faintly burnt, like the memory of a fire rather than the fire itself.

"It's bigger than I thought," Zephyrine heard themself say. Their voice sounded far away, borrowed. "The erasure. It isn't just Cael Adair that's gone. It's the *shape* of it. The space it used to hold."

"Geography doesn't forgive," Tam said. "You can tear a page out of an atlas, but the binding remembers. That's what we're looking at. The binding." He nodded at the pale nothing. "A country that size leaves a hole the size of a country."

Isolde tugged Zephyrine's fingers. She had been uncharacteristically silent since the ridge — since the stars went out, if Zephyrine was honest, though she had been the only one of them who hadn't seemed surprised by the dark. Now her face was tipped up toward the wound, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling.

"That's my country's ghost," she said. "I can feel it. Like standing in a house after everyone's left and the kettle's still warm."

Zephyrine's chest did something complicated and painful. "Isolde—"

"I'm not scared," she said, in the tone of a child who has decided fear is a thing that happens to other people. "I'm *homesick*. It's different."

*It's different.* Zephyrine knew that feeling intimately, had known it all their life without a name for it — the ache they'd carried through every contented morning in Vaelmoor, every well-mapped lane, every evening of lamplight and ink. Homesickness for a place they'd never been. For a place, perhaps, that had never properly existed at all.

They took out their satchel. Inside were the maps — the true ones now, the ones that had begun bleeding Cael Adair's geography up through the ink like bruises surfacing on skin. Harbors that weren't in Vaelmoor. A road called the Salt Road running straight off the edge of the page into nothing. And at the center of the bleeding, a mark Zephyrine had not drawn and could not explain: a small circle with a door drawn inside it, annotated in handwriting that was almost theirs.

*The Door Out.*

"Once we cross," Tam said, "there's no coming back this way. You understand that. The wards are down, but the fray moves. By tomorrow this ridge will be nothing, and the day after, the nothing will be eating the valley itself. Everything behind us is on a clock."

"I understand," Zephyrine said.

"And you're certain you can find a way across? Because I'll tell you what I see when I look at that—" he gestured at the wound, and his voice, for the first time since Zephyrine had known him, wavered. "I see nothing. I've spent my whole life learning to read wards, and that out there is a language with no letters. If you're wrong about your instincts, Eriksen, we walk off the edge of the world and keep walking."

Zephyrine looked at the fray. And something happened in them that they could not have described to save their life — something that felt less like thought than like a compass needle swinging home.

Because the nothing wasn't nothing. Not to them.

It was *blank*, yes. Unwritten. But Zephyrine had spent twenty years reading blankness — the white margins of charts, the unexplored quarters of old maps, the spaces where other cartographers had given up and left the paper empty. Blankness was never empty. Blankness was a room with the furniture removed, and the floor still bore the dents of everything that had stood there. And Zephyrine, looking out at the wound where Cael Adair had been, could *see the dents*.

There. Where the light bent strangely — a river had run there; rivers left a particular hush in the air even after they were gone. There, where the nothing rippled in a long low swell — hills. And there, threading through it all, faint as breath on glass: a road. Running straight as a struck bell from the fraying ridge out into the deep of the wound, toward the circle-and-door at the heart of their maps.

"I can see it," Zephyrine said slowly, wonderingly, hating how much they meant it. "Tam, I can see where it *was*. The whole country. It's like — like reading a palimpsest. Someone scraped the vellum clean, but the ink sank too deep. It's still down there. All of it."

Tam stared at them. "That's not possible. Reading an erasure requires—"

He stopped. He didn't finish the sentence, and the shape of the sentence he didn't finish hung between them like weather. *Requires the hand that made it.* Zephyrine felt the unsaid words settle somewhere behind their ribs, cold and patient, and chose — deliberately, the way one chooses to step onto ice — not to pick them up.

"Well," they said instead. "Lucky for us I'm very good at reading."

Isolde laughed — a bright, startling sound against the wind's long scream — and squeezed their fingers hard enough to hurt.

* * *

They crossed at dawn, or what the sky insisted was dawn: a bruised grey light with no sun in it, because the sun, apparently, had also been part of what the bargain took. The first step off the ridge was the hardest, the way the first step onto frozen water is the hardest — the body's whole animal wisdom rising up in revolt. Zephyrine's boot came down on grass, then on thread-grass, then on a green suggestion, and then on nothing at all, and held.

It held because Zephyrine decided it would hold. They understood that immediately, and the understanding frightened them more than the void below. This was not ground. It was *attention*. The erased land remembered being walked on, and if you walked on it believing, it believed back. Every step was a small act of stubborn faith, and Zephyrine had twenty years of cartographer's faith stored up like ballast.

Behind them, Tam's boots rang oddly — hollow, as if he walked on the skin of a drum. He kept his eyes fixed on Zephyrine's back and his jaw set, a man crossing a river on stones he didn't trust, trusting the person who'd placed them instead.

Isolde didn't walk so much as *arrive*, over and over, each step landing her exactly where she meant to be, as though the erased country recognized her feet and hurried to be under them. Once, glancing back, Zephyrine saw that where the girl had stepped, the nothing briefly *bloomed* — a patch of wildflowers surfacing like fish rising to feed, there and gone.

"You see that?" Tam murmured.

"I see it."

"What is she?"

"A child who remembers," Zephyrine said. "Which may be the rarest thing left in the world."

They walked. Hours passed, or something that wore hours' clothing. The wind never stopped its screaming, but Zephyrine noticed — with a thrill that shamed them even as it lifted them — that they had begun to *navigate* it. The scream changed pitch over the river-hollows, dropped to a moan along the buried road, rose to a whine where the hills' ghosts crowded close. It was a language. It was, gods help them, a *map* — the whole wounded country crying out its own lost geography, and Zephyrine understood it the way they understood ink, instantly, totally, without ever having learned.

"This way," they said, turning east along a swell of nothing that their hands insisted was a ridgeline. "There's shelter ahead. A hollow. There was a village here once — Harrowmere, the maps say — and its cellars go down into stone, and stone is the last thing to forget."

"How do you know that?" Tam asked. "You've never seen these maps before three days ago."

"I don't know how I know," Zephyrine said. And it was true, and it was a lie, and both truths walked together like companions.

Because here was the exhilaration, and they could not deny it: they had never in their life felt so *capable*. In Vaelmoor they had been good — the best mapper the valley had ever produced, people said, though the praise had always landed strangely, like coins into a well with no bottom. But this — walking a dead country by the sound of its grief, reading absences like scripture, steering three lives through unmapped space on instinct alone — this was what their hands had been *for*. Some deeper self was waking in them, stretching muscles they hadn't known they owned, and it felt like flight.

And here was the wrongness, riding the exhilaration like a rider on a horse: the instincts fit too well. The knowledge ran too deep. Nobody learned a country's bones in three days from stolen maps. This fluency had been built over years. Over decades. Over a life they could not remember living, in a service they could not remember rendering, for a purpose that made their stomach turn whenever it surfaced near the light.

*You drew this,* whispered the wind, in no words at all. *You know the way because you made the wound.*

"Zeph?" Isolde's voice, small. "Your face went strange."

"Just the wind," Zephyrine said.

* * *

They found Harrowmere's cellar at what might have been midday, descending a stair that surfaced out of the nothing like a whale rising — twelve steps of honest granite going down into honest dark, the only solid thing in a hundred miles of unwritten world. The cellar below was intact in the way dreams are intact: jars on shelves, a stool, a candle-end, all of it *almost* real, all of it slightly translucent, as if seen through frosted glass. When Zephyrine lit the candle with shaking hands, its flame burned steady, and the little room grew a shade more solid around the light, as though grateful for the company.

They ate what they'd carried. Tam paced the length of the cellar twice, touching the walls, frowning. "Stone remembers longest," he said. "Ward-keepers' proverb. We can rest here. An hour. No more."

Isolde fell asleep almost at once, curled against Zephyrine's side, her breathing quick and shallow, the way children breathe when they are dreaming hard. Zephyrine sat with the candle and their maps spread across their knees and let themself, for one dangerous moment, simply look at what they had drawn.

Cael Adair bled up through the ink of Vaelmoor everywhere now. Harbors with names like *Sallowdeep* and *the Nine Quays*. A capital whose name appeared and vanished depending on the angle of the light — *Adairhold*, maybe, or something older. Mountain ranges rendered in a hand identical to their own. And threaded through it all, the Salt Road, running white as a scar from the Door Out to the sea.

"Ask me," Tam said quietly, from across the candle. He had stopped pacing. "Go on. You've been circling it for two days. Ask me what you're afraid to ask."

Zephyrine kept their eyes on the map. "Why did the guild exile you, Tam? Truly. Not the story you told Isolde."

A long silence. The candle fluttered though there was no draft.

"I questioned the Grand Warding," he said at last. "The great working our order maintains — the one we're taught holds all the borders of the world safe. I found records that didn't reconcile. Expenditures of ward-power that matched no known border. One enormous sealed expenditure, centuries old, renewed every generation, and no record of what it *wards against* or *for*. I asked. Politely, even." His mouth twisted. "They gave me a choice. Recant, or carry the shame out the door. I carried the shame." He looked up, and his eyes in the candlelight were bleak and clear. "Now I'm standing in the middle of what the sealed expenditure bought, Eriksen. And the man who signed the order that exiled me — Master Corvane — his seal is on half the documents I stole. Whatever your instincts are telling you about the way ahead, mine are telling me the guild didn't just fail to stop this erasure. The guild *keeps* it. Maintains it. Like a lighthouse keeper maintaining a light that wrecks ships."

"The Door Out," Zephyrine said slowly. "If the guild keeps the erasure—"

"Then the Door Out is either our salvation or the trap at the end of the maze," Tam said. "And I no longer trust myself to know which. Which is why I'm following a cartographer who reads wounds by ear, into a hole in the world, on the strength of instincts nobody can account for." He smiled without humor. "Sleep if you can. One of us should."

Zephyrine did not sleep. They sat with the sleeping child against their ribs and the impossible maps across their knees, and watched the candle burn, and listened to the wind above the cellar mouth scream its long, grieving, legible scream.

Exhilaration and wrongness, walking together. They had crossed the border of Vaelmoor and discovered that the country they'd entered was themselves: vast, unmapped, and bearing everywhere the marks of a hand they did not recognize and could not stop recognizing.

Near dawn-that-was-not-dawn, Isolde woke suddenly, sat bolt upright, and said, with total clarity:

"She knows we're coming. Ashwing. She smelled the map on you from the other side of the erasure." The girl's eyes were wide and ancient in the candlelight. "She says to tell you she remembers your face, Zephyrine. She says she's been waiting a very long time to see it again."

Outside, far off across the unwritten dark, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the wind changed its note — and for the first time, Zephyrine heard the scream of the fray answer it.
