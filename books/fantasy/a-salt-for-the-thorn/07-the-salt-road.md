## 7. The Salt Road

The road began where the world stopped pretending.

One moment we were walking through grass that bent in a wind I could not feel — the hollow country had weather like a rumor, all suggestion and no substance — and the next, the ground beneath my boots turned white. Not snow-white, not chalk-white, but the white of old bone left out in sun, of salt crusts on a harbor's edge at low tide. It stretched ahead of us in a straight line through the gray nothing, narrow as a footpath, wide as a cart road, and it hummed faintly underfoot, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended.

I crouched and touched it. It was warm.

"Salt," Tam said behind me. He said it the way he said most things now — flatly, carefully, as though words might crack something if he leaned on them too hard. Since the confession of the hidden map, since the moment I'd held my own handwriting in my hands and felt it accuse me, he had been measuring every sentence before he spent it. "Sea salt, by the look. Coarse-cut. This is harbor road."

"There's no sea," I said. "There's no anything."

"There was," said Isolde.

She came forward past me, small feet in cracked leather shoes, and stepped onto the salt without hesitation, the way you step onto a stair you've climbed ten thousand times in the dark. The road brightened where she stood. I mean that literally: the white deepened to silver, then to a pale gold, as if her weight pressed light up out of it like juice from fruit. And beneath our feet — faint, half-heard, more feeling than sound — came the cry of gulls.

Tam went very still. "That's not possible."

"It's the only thing that is," Isolde said. "It's a memory road. Grandmother told me about them when I was — " She stopped. Her brow furrowed, a small crease appearing between her eyes like a hairline crack in porcelain. "When I was young," she finished, but slower, as if she'd had to go looking for the word *young* in a drawer she'd already emptied once.

I looked at Tam. He was looking at Isolde's shoes, at the road, at the horizon of nothing, and his jaw did the thing it did when he was deciding whether to say a hard thing or swallow it whole. He swallowed it. I saw him do it. And because I had spent three days learning to read this bitter, exiled, careful man, I understood that what he'd swallowed was a warning — that memory roads cost something, that nothing in the hollow country gave without taking — and that he had chosen, for reasons of his own, to let us walk on ignorant a little longer.

I let him. That is the part I keep returning to, in the long dark hours since. I saw him choose silence, and I chose silence too, because the road was beautiful and going somewhere, and I was afraid that asking questions would make it stop.

We walked.

* * *

The road only existed where we had already been.

This took me an hour to understand and another hour to accept. Behind us, the salt faded — not crumbled, not washed away, but *unremembered*, thinning from gold to silver to white to a translucent shimmer and then to nothing at all, so that we walked always at the leading edge of a road that was being invented one stride ahead of us and erased one stride behind. Ahead, there was only the gray. But when Isolde walked, the gray resolved. Her footsteps fell and the salt bloomed under them, spreading outward from each print like frost forming on glass, and where the bloom spread thickest, details surfaced: a groove worn by cart wheels; a milestone stubbed off at knee height, its carved letters smoothed away; a hitching post with no rope and no horse and a shadow that didn't match the angle of any light.

And always, always, the gulls. Faint as breath on a window.

"Tell me about it," I said to Isolde, somewhere past the second hour. We had fallen into a rhythm — Tam ranging ahead, watchful, hand never far from the ward-knife at his belt; me mapping as I went, sketching the road's behavior in the battered notebook I'd carried out of Vaelmoor, recording distances between milestones that shouldn't exist; Isolde walking in the middle, humming a tune none of us knew and all of us, by the third verse, could have sung.

"About what?"

"The harbor. Cael Adair. Any of it." I kept my voice light, the way you speak to a bird you don't want to startle. "It helps the road, doesn't it? When you talk about it."

She glanced up at me with those grave dark eyes, and for a moment I thought she would say *how did you know*, and I would have to lie to a child, which is a particular kind of wound. Instead she shrugged, the way children shrug when asked to explain breathing.

"The road likes stories. Roads always like stories. That's what they're *for*." She kicked a pebble of salt; it skittered ahead and vanished into the gray, and where it vanished, I swear the gray flinched. "There were nine harbors. Ours was Sallowmere, which isn't a pretty name but it was a pretty place. The water came in green, like bottle glass, and at dawn the fishing boats went out in lines, all their lamps lit even in summer because the mist was thick, and the dragon-priests would stand on the breakwater and bless the first catch." She smiled at the middle distance. "They wore robes the color of storm-light. Gray-gold. Like this road."

The road *was* like that. I hadn't noticed until she said it. The salt had taken on the color of her description, storm-gray shot with gold, and the gull-cries grew louder, and suddenly there was a smell — brine and tar and fish-scale and woodsmoke — rising out of the ground itself, out of the memory packed beneath it like coal seams.

Tam had stopped walking. He was staring back the way we'd come, at the road's fading tail, and his face had gone the color of old ash.

"What?" I said.

He shook his head. Said nothing. Walked on.

* * *

By midday — if the hollow country's unchanging gray could be said to have a midday, which it couldn't, but our bodies insisted anyway — I noticed that Isolde had stopped humming.

She was still talking. That was the terrible part. She talked steadily, generously, laying out Cael Adair mile by mile like a woman laying out cards: the terraced vineyards above Sallowmere, the bell-tower that rang nine times at dusk for the nine harbors, the market square where they sold candied kelp and star-anise wine, the Door — she spoke of the Door often, matter-of-factly, a great ringed gate of black stone in the cliffside below the capital, older than the dragons, older than the guild, the way out and the way in and the reason the ward-keepers existed at all. She talked and talked, and the road drank her words and grew richer, wider, more real, until we were walking down the actual cobbles of an actual street with lampposts and drain-grates and a bakery whose windows breathed the ghost of bread.

But she wasn't humming anymore. And when she paused between sentences — and the pauses were getting longer — she would stand very still with her lips slightly parted, her eyes fixed on nothing, wearing the expression of someone standing in a room they've just realized they don't recognize.

I caught up to Tam, dropped my voice below the gull-cry. "How does the road work? Truly. You know these things. Ward-keepers are taught the theory of — "

"I was expelled before theory," he said. Too fast. Too smooth. A rehearsed lie, and both of us knew it, and neither of us acknowledged it.

"Tam."

He walked six more paces. Then, quietly, not looking at me: "Memory roads aren't built. They're *paid* for. Every road in the old countries was paid for once, in the founding years — that's what the guild's first oaths were about, the tolls, the keeping of accounts. A road remembers itself as long as someone walks it who remembers why it's there." He finally turned his head. His eyes were red-rimmed with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion. "The girl isn't telling stories because she likes to. She's telling stories because the road eats them. Every mile, it takes its toll, and the toll is whatever she feeds it, and it takes the *root* along with the branch. She won't just forget the vineyards, cartographer. She'll forget that she ever loved anyone who worked them."

The world tilted. I stopped walking. Behind us, Isolde's voice went on, bright and tireless, describing a festival of lanterns on the water, and the road around us obligingly filled with the smell of lamp-oil and the distant sound of a crowd that had never existed and never would again except as a child's dying recollection.

"And you knew," I said. "At the beginning. On the first morning. You looked at her shoes and you swallowed it."

"I did."

"Why?"

He was quiet for a long time. The gray pressed in on either side of the road, patient as tide.

"Because she'd have done it anyway," he said at last. "Because she loves that country more than she loves her own mind, and if I'd told her the price she'd have paid it faster, gladly, to get us there sooner. Because I'm a coward. Because I'm a coward and I wanted the road, and I let a child buy it for us." His voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a fault-line showing through the flat stone of him. "There. Are you satisfied? You wanted the ugly thing said out loud."

I hadn't wanted it. I had needed it, which is different, and worse. Because the truth underneath his truth was mine as well: I had seen him choose silence and I had chosen it too, and I had let myself be bought with gull-cries and the smell of bread. My guilt had a map drawn in my own hand — I was becoming intimately familiar with that sensation — and every mile of this road was inked in a child's memory while I sketched comfortably in my notebook, *recording*, *documenting*, doing the one thing I knew how to do while the payment was taken from someone else.

"We tell her," I said. "Tonight. At the next rest. She deserves to choose."

"She's choosing. She's been choosing since the border." Tam rubbed his face with both hands. "But yes. Tonight. I'll — I'll tell her. It should be me."

"No," I heard myself say. "Together. I don't get to hide behind you anymore. I've done enough hiding. Apparently I'm very good at it. There's a whole map that proves it."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might live, in better country. "You keep making jokes about it."

"If I stop making jokes about it I'll have to feel it."

"Aye," he said softly. "That's how it works."

* * *

We rested when the road reached a well.

A well should not have surprised me — roads need wells, wells follow roads the way shadows follow bodies — but this one rose out of the salt fully formed as Isolde described it: a round stone lip, a wooden crossbeam, a bucket on a rope, and water at the bottom that reflected stars even though the sky above us held none. Stars in the well-water. I knelt and looked into it and my own face looked back, and behind my reflection, tiny and impossibly far away, constellations drifted that I did not know and somehow grieved for.

Isolde drank first. Then Tam. Then me, last, and the water tasted of cold stone and, faintly, of salt — of course of salt — and of something else underneath, something like the taste of a word you've forgotten and are trying desperately to recall.

Isolde sat on the well-lip, legs swinging, and looked out at the road behind us, at the miles of fading silver stretching back into the gray.

"Do you remember your grandmother?" I asked. Gently. Testing the water of her, the way you test a floor that might be burning through.

Her feet stilled. "I — yes. Of course. She had — " The pause stretched. Five seconds. Ten. I watched her reach for it and I watched the reaching fail, watched her hand close on empty air inside herself, and I will carry the sound of what she said next until I die: "She had a name," Isolde said, wonderingly, like a discovery. "Everyone has a name. Isn't that strange? I can't find hers."

Tam closed his eyes.

"Isolde." I knelt in front of her so she'd have to look at me, so I couldn't hide behind my own height. "Listen to me. The road. The stories you tell it. They're not free. Each mile — "

"I know."

The two words landed like stones in still water.

"You *know*?"

"Of course I know." She said it with the infinite patience of the young for the slow. "I can feel them going. It's like — you know when you fall asleep in the daytime, and you dream, and when you wake the dream is still warm but you can't get back into it? It's like that, but the dream is my whole country, and I'm falling asleep one piece at a time." She swung her feet again, resuming her rhythm, and smiled at me, and the smile was the worst thing I had ever seen, because it was brave, and she had learned to be brave somewhere, and I could no longer remember — and soon neither would she — *where*. "But here's the thing, Zephyrine. Here's the thing I figured out on the first night, when the stars went out and everyone in Vaelmoor forgot everything except me."

I waited.

"Somebody has to remember," she said. "That's all remembering is. Somebody has to do it. And I'm the only one who can pay, because I'm the only one who has anything left to pay *with*. You don't remember Cael Adair. Tam doesn't. The echoes in the hollow places barely remember themselves. If I stop paying, the road stops, and then nobody ever gets home, and the Door Out seals forever, and Ashwing burns down to nothing alone in the dark, and that's — " Her chin trembled, once, and she mastered it with a ferocity that made my chest ache. "That's not allowed. So I pay. And when I run out, I'll have paid for you to get there, and you'll draw the new map, and the country will come back, and everyone will remember everything again except me, and that's fine. That's a fair trade. That's what salt is *for*."

"Salt," I repeated. My voice came out strange.

"For preserving," she said, as if explaining rain to a fool. "Grandmother — " and there it was again, the little stumble, the reaching and failing — "*someone* told me. Salt keeps things from spoiling. Salt keeps things. That's why the road is made of it. It's keeping me while it eats me. It's very polite that way."

I could not speak. Beside me, Tam had turned away entirely, shoulders rigid, facing the gray, and I understood that he was weeping and would deny it to his grave, and I loved him for the denial and hated the necessity of it.

Isolde hopped down off the well-lip, brushed salt dust from her skirt, and set off up the road at her usual tireless pace, calling back over her shoulder: "Come on, then. Next is the bridge at Corrow Fen, and it's a good one, all hung with bells. You'll want to write that down, cartographer. You'll want to get it right."

* * *

We walked into the evening — the gray's false evening, the light draining to a deeper gray — and Isolde told us about the bridge at Corrow Fen, and the road built it for us plank by plank, bell by bell, and each bell rang with a note she'd forgotten she knew.

I mapped it all. Every span, every bell, every milestone. My hand did not shake, because I would not permit it to shake; shaking was a luxury belonging to people who had not helped erase a nation. But somewhere past the fiftieth bell, as Isolde's voice described the ferryman who used to work the fen — a broad man, laughing, with a scar — and then stopped, mid-sentence, and frowned, and said, *there was a ferryman, wasn't there, there must have been, why can't I see his face* — somewhere in that moment, I made myself a promise in the shape of a vow:

Whatever waited at the end of this road, whatever the Door Out demanded, whatever price it named — it would not be paid by her. Not the last coin. Whatever I had been, whoever had held the pen before me, I would find a way to spend myself instead. I had, after all, practice at erasure. Perhaps that was the one skill the old Zephyrine had left me worth keeping: I knew exactly how a thing was unwritten.

Which meant I might be the only one alive who knew how to write it back.

The road climbed. The salt turned from silver to a deep storm-gold, and the air grew warmer, and far ahead — impossibly far, at the edge of the gray where the horizon should have been — I saw a glow. Not starlight. Not dawn.

Firelight. Vast, slow, guttering, red as a dying coal the size of a mountain.

Tam saw it too. He stopped dead, and his hand found his ward-knife, and his voice came out hoarse with a reverence I had never heard in him and never expected to.

"That's no beacon," he said. "Cartographer. Girl. That's a hearth-fire."

Isolde shaded her eyes, and smiled, and said, simply, joyfully, unbearably:

"Ashwing. We're almost there. He's waiting for us."

And the road beneath our feet shivered — the whole length of it, root to tip, salt ringing like a struck bowl — as if something ancient, at the far end of a child's memory, had lifted its head and smelled us coming.
