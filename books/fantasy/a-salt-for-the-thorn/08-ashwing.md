## 8. Ashwing

The salt road ended where the world forgot to be anything.

They had walked for three days out of the hollow country, through fields of grey grass that whispered with half-remembered names, past orchards whose fruit hung pale and unfinished, like thoughts someone had started and abandoned. Isolde walked between them now, quieter than before. Each mile of the road had taken something from her, and though she never said what, Zephyrine could read the subtraction in her face — a smallness gathering around her eyes, the way a candle gathers shadow as it burns down. Tam noticed too. Tam noticed everything and said nothing, which was its own kind of wound.

On the third evening the land began to slope downward, and the grey grass gave way to a plain of black glass, smooth as a frozen lake, stretching to every horizon. And rising out of that glass, alone in the middle of all that emptiness, was a mountain that was not a mountain.

It was breathing.

Zephyrine saw it first as geography — the cartographer's curse, seeing the world as contour and line even when the world was doing something no contour should do. The shape before them rose and fell in a slow, tectonic rhythm, and the black glass plain flexed with it, ripples spreading outward from the base like the rings around a struck bell. Then the shape shifted, and scales caught the last of the light — scales the color of old iron, of rusted hinges, of a hearth gone cold — and Zephyrine understood that they were looking at the largest living thing they had ever seen, and possibly the last.

"Oh," Isolde said softly. It was not a word of fear. It was the sound a person makes coming home.

The dragon lay coiled at the center of the glass plain like a letter no one had opened, and around it — Zephyrine had to squint, had to make themselves look properly, because the eye kept sliding off — around it were chains. They rose from the black glass at a dozen points, great links thick as tree trunks, running up and over and around the dragon's body in a geometry that made no structural sense. They did not hold the dragon down. Zephyrine's eye, trained on load and tension and the honest physics of bridges, could see that they held nothing at all. They simply *were*, anchored in nothing, fastened to nothing, and the dragon lay inside them the way a word lies inside a sentence it can no longer leave.

"It's chained to the erasure," Tam said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when the wards spoke to him and he didn't like what they said. "I've read about this. In the guild archives, the sealed sections, the ones they burn you for reading." He swallowed. "A dragon's fire can unmake a country. But a dying dragon's fire, given freely, can *unwrite* one — and keep it unwritten, for as long as the fire lasts. The bargain binds the fire to the erasure. The dragon can't die until the country does. It can't live while the country doesn't. It just — burns down. Slowly. Like a lamp in a locked room."

"Like a lamp in a locked room," Zephyrine repeated, and did not know why the words made their chest ache, or why they felt, suddenly and horribly, like a confession.

* * *

They crossed the glass plain as the light died. There was no other way. The hollow country had narrowed behind them into a corridor of grey, and ahead the black glass was the only ground that still agreed to be ground, and so they walked out onto it with their footsteps ringing strangely, each one echoing back a half-second late, as though the plain had to think about whether to remember them.

The cold came up through the glass and into their bones. Not winter-cold. *Absence*-cold — the cold of a room where a fire has just gone out, the cold of a bed where someone used to lie. Zephyrine pulled their coat tighter and found themselves counting their footsteps, an old habit, and then found themselves counting in a rhythm they did not recognize, a surveyor's cadence, and stopped, and their heart was pounding, and they could not have said why.

Isolde had begun to run.

"Isolde—" Tam reached for her and missed.

The girl went across the glass like something loosed, her braid flying, her small boots ringing, and she did not stop until she stood at the base of the great iron head, which was resting on the glass with its eyes closed, and she put one small hand flat against the dragon's nostril, which was larger than she was, and said, in a voice that broke in the middle:

"I remember you. I remember you. You came to the harbor festival and the priests put salt on your tongue and you said my grandmother's name was a good name for a storm."

The dragon's eye opened.

It was the color of a coal an hour after the fire — not dead, but past glowing, a deep and banked red, and in it Zephyrine saw reflected a tiny image of themselves: a thin figure in a cartographer's coat, standing on a black mirror at the end of the world, looking like exactly what they were beginning to suspect they were.

The eye moved. Past Isolde. Past Tam. It found Zephyrine, and it stopped, and the vast iron head lifted — slowly, with a sound like a ship's anchor chain paying out, like a cathedral door that has not been opened in a century — until the dragon was looking down at them from a height that made the sky seem close.

And the dragon said, in a voice like the grinding of continents, like the settling of mountains, like something that had not spoken in a very long time and had forgotten how to be gentle:

"*Vaelric.*"

* * *

The name went through Zephyrine like a nail through a map.

They did not know it. That was the terrible thing — they stood there on the cold glass with the dragon's coal-red eye fixed on them and the name *Vaelric* ringing in the hollow air, and they knew it the way you know a word in a language you've forgotten: by its shape, by its weight on the tongue, by the way some sealed room behind their ribs strained against its door at the sound of it.

"I'm not—" Zephyrine's voice came out thin. They tried again. "My name is Zephyrine Eriksen. I'm a cartographer. From Vaelmoor."

The dragon made a sound. It might have been a laugh. It was the saddest sound Zephyrine had ever heard — a vast exhalation, hot and smelling of ash and salt and something older than either, something like the inside of a star.

"Cartographer," the dragon said, tasting the word. "Yes. That is what they made you. That is the shape they poured you into, the way a smith pours iron into a mold. A cartographer. From Vaelmoor." The great head lowered, and the coal-red eye came level with Zephyrine's face, near enough that they could see their own reflection swimming in it, near enough that the heat of the dragon's breath pressed against them like a hand. "I watched you draw, Vaelric. Do you understand that? I watched you for eleven years. You came to my roost on the Saltspire with your ink and your beautiful hands, and you asked me questions about the coastline of Cael Adair, and I answered, because I thought — I was old and I was foolish and I thought no one could do harm with a map. A map is a love letter, I told myself. A map is how a country says *I am here, I am this, remember me.*"

"I don't—" Zephyrine's hands had come up, without their permission, in front of their face — and they froze, staring at them, at those hands, because the dragon was looking at them too, with an expression like a wound.

"Those hands," the dragon said. "I know those hands. They drew my harbor. They drew my mountains, my rivers, the nine bridges of Cael Adair and the dragon-priests' salt stairs. They drew my country so beautifully that when the time came, when the bargain was struck and the erasure was asked of someone — there was only one hand in all the world that knew Cael Adair well enough to unwrite it." The eye flared, a dying coal remembering fire for one furious instant. "You drew the Erasure Map, Vaelric. With the same pens. With the same beautiful, careful, loving hands. You drew my country out of the world, and they took your memory of doing it, and they built you a little valley full of quiet roads and kind neighbors, and they left you there to be happy."

"Stop." Zephyrine's voice cracked. "Stop it. I don't — I have never been anywhere but Vaelmoor. I was born there. I have maps in my own hand of every road—"

"Of every road that leads nowhere," the dragon said, softly now, which was worse. "Yes. You drew those too. Or your hands did. The border you never questioned. The roads that end in blank paper. You are a cartographer, Zephyrine Eriksen. Tell me — in all your years of loving maps, of needing to know where every path goes — why did you never once try to leave?"

* * *

The silence after that was very long, and the black glass held it, and the wind came across the plain and found nothing to say and moved on.

Zephyrine stood with the question lodged in them like a splinter of ice. *Why did you never once try to leave.* They had asked themselves a version of it, in the first chapter of their own unease — the ache of a life that felt borrowed — but they had never let themselves finish the sentence, and now a creature older than kingdoms had finished it for them, in a voice like the end of the world.

Tam had moved. Without seeming to decide to, the ward-keeper had placed himself slightly in front of Isolde, and his hand was inside his coat, on the broken ward-sigil he carried, and he was looking at Zephyrine the way he had looked at the cracked border stones — with the particular, professional grief of a man watching something fail from the inside.

"Tam," Zephyrine said.

"Don't," Tam said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "Don't ask me to say it's not true. Because I read the sealed archives, Zephyrine. I read about the Erasure, before they exiled me for it. And the archives said the map was drawn by the royal cartographer of Cael Adair, the one who knew the country best, the one whose hand the ink would trust." He swallowed. "The archives never said what happened to that cartographer afterward. The guild records just — stop. Like a road on one of your maps."

"Tam." Isolde's voice, small and furious. She had not moved from the dragon's side. Her hand was still flat against the iron nostril, and the dragon's breath moved her hair like a wind off a summer harbor. "Stop it, both of you. Look at them. *Look.*" She turned, and her face — thinner than it had been, older, the face of a girl who had paid miles of herself for this road — was wet and blazing. "If they drew the Erasure Map, then they've been punished for it their whole life without even knowing why. That's not a villain. That's a *hostage*. And I don't care what name the dragon knew them by — they carried me out of a dissolving valley on their back. They walked the salt road. They're here. Whatever they were, they *came back*."

The dragon's eye moved to the child. And something in the vast iron face changed — a softening, tectonic and slow, like a glacier deciding to become a river.

"Little storm," the dragon said, and its voice had gentled into something that made Zephyrine's throat close. "Daughter of Adair. Last leaf of the salt tree." The great head dipped, until the dragon's brow nearly touched the glass beside the girl. "You remember my name. Say it. No one has said it in two hundred years, and a name is the last thing a fire keeps."

Isolde wiped her face with her sleeve. She stood up straight, the way Zephyrine imagined a princess of a drowned country might stand, and she said:

"Ashwing. Your name is Ashwing, and you were the fire of the west harbor, and you are not done yet."

The dragon closed its eyes. When it opened them again, the coal-red had brightened — barely, a degree, the way a hearth brightens when someone finally comes home and feeds it — and it looked past Isolde, past Tam, and settled again on Zephyrine with a weight like a verdict.

"You came back," Ashwing said. "Or you were sent. I cannot tell, and it may not matter. But hear me, Zephyrine-who-was-Vaelric, because my fire is guttering and I do not have the strength to say this twice." The chains around the dragon shifted, groaning, though nothing moved them. "The erasure runs on my fire, and my fire is nearly spent. When it dies, everything born of Cael Adair dies with it — every echo in the hollow country, every half-remembered name. And the child. And you, cartographer, whoever you are now — you are inked into that map, root and branch, and when it goes, *you* go. There is a Door. The ward-keepers' guild keeps it, or kept it, in the years when keeping meant anything. Through the Door, a thing can be un-erased. A country can be drawn back. But the ink that draws it back must come from the same hand that erased it." The eye flared, and held. "So. You have a choice to make, and not much time to make it in, and I am the last creature in the world old enough to tell you the truth about both roads."

Zephyrine's mouth was dry. The glass plain rang faintly beneath their feet, like a bell deciding whether to toll.

"And if I say I'm not that person?" they asked. "If I say the dragon is wrong, and the archives are wrong, and the map in my own handwriting is wrong?"

Ashwing regarded them for a long moment. Then the dragon exhaled, a long slow plume of smoke and salt, and said:

"Then tell me, *cartographer* — why did your hands know the way here? Why did they count in surveyor's cadence on my plain? Why, when I said that name, did you not look confused?" The coal-red eye burned steady, terrible, and sad. "You looked *caught*."

Far off across the black glass, in the direction of the hollow country, something screamed — a thin, high, dissolving sound, the sound of an echo running out of remembering. And Ashwing's chains groaned, and the dragon's fire flickered low in its throat, and the last light of the guttering world dimmed one more degree.

"We need to move," Tam said quietly. "Tonight. Whatever you are, Zephyrine — we need to move."

And Zephyrine, who had no answer for the dragon and none for themselves, could only nod, and turn toward the dark where the Door waited, carrying a name like a stone in their chest.

*Vaelric.*

They did not look back. But they heard, as they walked away across the ringing glass, the dragon begin — very softly, in a voice like the far end of the world — to sing.
