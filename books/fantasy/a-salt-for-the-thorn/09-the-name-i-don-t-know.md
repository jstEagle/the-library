## 9. The Name I Don't Know

The dragon's name for me hung in the ash-warm air of the hollow country like a bell that had been struck and refused to finish ringing.

*Vessarin.*

I did not know the name. I want to be precise about that, because precision was the one discipline I had never lost, even when everything else had been taken from me: I did not know the name the way a person does not know a word in a language they have never studied. It was not forgotten. It was not buried. It was simply *not there*, a clean absence, smooth as an unmapped coastline.

And yet when Ashwing said it — when the last dragon of Cael Adair dragged those two syllables out of a throat full of dying embers — something behind my sternum answered. Not memory. Nothing so kind as memory. More like the way a tuning fork hums when its twin is struck across a room.

"No," I said.

The word came out too fast. I heard it come out too fast. Tam heard it too; I felt him go still beside me in that particular way he had, the stillness of a man who has spent his life reading wards and has just begun to read me instead.

Ashwing's eye was the size of a cartwheel and the color of a banked fire, all coals and patience. The dragon lay along the floor of what had once been the great amphitheater of Cael Adair's capital — I knew it was an amphitheater because Isolde had said so an hour before we found it, her small voice steady in the way of someone reciting a lesson rather than telling a story, *there will be seats cut into the hillside and moss on every seat and the dragons used to land in the middle* — and now the moss-grown terraces rose around us in rings, empty, each stone remembering an audience that no longer existed to fill it.

The dragon's chains were not iron. That had been the first horror. They were lines of *absence*, drawn through the air and the stone alike, the same burnt-paper fraying I had seen at Vaelmoor's border but older, deeper, deliberate — strokes of unwriting laid over the dragon's wings and neck like harness-leather. Whoever had bound Ashwing had done it with the same tool that had erased a nation.

With a map, said a voice in me that was not mine. With ink.

"You are mistaken," I said again, slower this time, and made myself meet the coal-dark eye. "My name is Zephyrine Eriksen. I am a cartographer from Vaelmoor. I have lived there my whole life. I mapped its roads, its rivers, the border stones. I have never—" My voice snagged on the shape of the valley, which was suddenly, treacherously dear to me, dearer than it had been two weeks ago, dearer than it had any right to be. "I have never been here before."

"Liar," Ashwing said, gently, which was somehow worse than if the dragon had roared it. Smoke curled from nostrils wide enough to put my fist inside. "Or worse than a liar. A map that believes itself to be the country."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means," the dragon said, and the amphitheater carried the voice up through the empty seats and scattered it among the stars-that-were-not, "that you were drawn over. The way a forger draws a new coast over an old chart, pressing hard enough that the vellum takes the lie. Someone wrote a life on top of yours, little cartographer, and the ink was good ink, and you have lived inside it so long you think the forgery is your skin."

Behind me, Isolde made a sound — small, furious, the sound she made whenever an adult said something untrue about a thing she remembered truly. She stepped forward off the terrace edge onto the amphitheater floor, salt-white road dust still on her boots, and planted herself between me and a creature whose exhalations could have stripped a barn roof.

"Don't call them that." Her chin was up. She was nine years old and half a head shorter than the dragon's eyelashes. "They walked me across the fray. They held my hand in the screaming part. Whatever they were, they're—"

"Isolde." I crouched to her level. My knees cracked. Somewhere in the false life, I had gotten old enough for my knees to crack, and I hated how much comfort I took in that small mundane proof of self. "It's all right."

"It isn't."

"It is." And then, because lying to her felt like a crime against whatever she was paying in memories to keep us walking: "I don't know what it is. But I know I'm standing here, and I know your hand was cold in mine on the salt road, and neither of those things stops being true because a dragon says otherwise."

Ashwing rumbled. It took me a moment to recognize the sound as laughter, or the ruin of laughter, the way a dead hearth can still hold the shape of warmth.

"Spoken like a person defending a house they built," the dragon said. "Or defending a house they were *told* they built. Come closer, Vessarin. Let me smell the truth on you."

"I'd rather not."

"Cartographers," Tam said quietly.

I turned. He had not moved from the third terrace step. He stood with his arms crossed and his ruined guild-sigil hanging crooked at his throat, and his face wore the expression I had learned, over our weeks together, to dread more than any monster: the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Tam."

He looked at the dragon. He looked at me. When he spoke, his voice had the flat carefulness of someone walking on ice they have already heard crack. "You navigated the fray. Nobody navigates the fray. The ward-keepers' first law says the fray cannot be crossed without a Door, and we didn't use a Door, and you walked us through it like a man walking home drunk — wrong turns, sure, but always correcting toward something." His jaw worked. "You knew where north was in a place where north had been deleted. You told me yourself you didn't know how you knew."

"Instinct isn't identity."

"No," Tam agreed. "But it's evidence."

* * *

The dragon let us rest. I think — though I would not trust this thought further than I could throw the amphitheater — I think Ashwing wanted witnesses. A creature chained by erasure for longer than my counterfeit lifetime must hunger for someone to *see* the chain.

We made camp at the top of the seating, under a fragment of colonnade, where the hollow country's strange grey light came down thin as skimmed milk. There was no wood to burn; nothing here burned except memories and guilt. Tam chewed dried plums and pretended they were dinner. Isolde slept curled against my side with her fingers hooked into my sleeve, and I sat very still so as not to wake her, and watched the dragon watch me.

"Why do you keep calling me that?" I asked at last, low, so the name wouldn't reach Isolde's sleep. "Vessarin. If I'm not that person, the name costs you nothing to drop. If I am—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "If I am, then saying it should cost you everything. I'm told that name drew the map that unmade your country."

The coals of the dragon's eye shifted. For a long moment there was only wind moving through empty seats, making of the amphitheater a vast dry whisper, ten thousand ghosts shushing one another.

"When the Erasure came," Ashwing said, "I was young, for my kind. Young enough to be foolish, old enough to be trusted with the vaults beneath this city. The royal cartographer came down to me on the last night. Do you know what a royal cartographer *is*, Vessarin? Not a maker of charts. A keeper of the country's truest memory. Every river's honest bend, every mountain's true height, every door and grave and name. In Cael Adair the maps were not pictures. They were vows. As long as the country was written, the country stood. We were a people who had made a covenant with geography itself."

Wind. Whispering stone. Isolde's breath against my arm.

"The bargain was struck high above us — the crown and the guild and the dying powers of the north, I never learned all its teeth. But its price fell to the map-keepers. One of them had to draw the Erasure. To take the vow in their own hand and turn it inside out, stroke by stroke, until the country was unwritten. Unmaking by cartography. The most sacred skill we had, turned to the one act it existed to prevent."

"And the cartographer refused," I said. It was not a question. I don't know why I said it with such certainty. Perhaps because I needed someone, anyone in this story, to have refused.

"The cartographer bargained." The dragon's voice dropped into something almost tender, which was the cruelest register it had used yet. "That was the poison of it, Vessarin. They did not refuse. They asked what would be spared. And when they were shown a small green valley at the world's cold edge — a valley of no strategic account, full of quiet people who kept themselves apart — they asked that *it* be spared. Their own valley. Their neighbors. Their mother's kitchen garden. And the bargainers laughed, in the way of powerful things laughing, and said: done. Draw the Erasure, and your valley keeps its borders. We will even build it walls. Wards, rather. So that the saved will never wander out and ask inconvenient questions about the shape of the world."

The wind stopped. Or I stopped hearing it. The amphitheater went so silent I could hear Isolde dreaming, small syllables without words.

"They drew it," Ashwing said. "Every line of it, in their own hand, signed in their own name — for a signature is salt, in our craft; it closes the wound. And when the last line closed, the guild took the cartographer's memory of the drawing and gave them a new life inside the spared valley. A life with no past but the valley. A life that loved the valley. Because the cruellest prison is the one whose walls the prisoner waters like flowers."

"That's not—" I started, and could not find the end of the sentence. "That's a story. That's a story you're telling yourself because you need the erasure to have a face. You've been alone in the dark for however many years, chained by the thing that ate your whole country, and you need someone to *blame*, and I'm the first warm body to walk into your dark in a generation, so you've dressed me in the villain's clothes because it hurts less than—"

"Than what?" Ashwing asked softly.

Than believing the country deserved it. Than believing it was nobody's choice at all, just weather, just entropy, just the universe grinding indifferent teeth. Than carrying a grief with no address.

I said none of it. The dragon watched me not say all of it, and the coals of its eye banked lower, and something in its stillness said: *yes. That. You understand exactly what I chose to believe, because you are choosing it too.*

* * *

Tam found me at the colonnade's broken edge after midnight — or after what passed for midnight in a country with no sky worth the name. The grey light dimmed on a schedule, like a lamp someone remembered to trim. Below, Isolde had migrated in her sleep to the warmth of the dragon's foreleg, an act of trust so enormous and so casual it made my chest hurt to look at. Ashwing's wing, the unchained one, curved over her like a cliff learning tenderness.

"She snores," Tam said, sitting beside me. His boots dangled over the drop. "Never met a child who snores like that. Sounds like a sawmill arguing with a goose."

"She's spent most of her memories getting us here." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "What's left snores."

"Mm." He was quiet for a while. In the dimness his face was all angles and withheld verdicts. Then: "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing since the dragon said your other name. Ask me if I believe it. You've been waiting for me to say it unprompted, and I haven't, and you've decided that means something." He shrugged. "It doesn't. I'm a coward about certain conversations. Guild training. We were taught to let the wards fail slowly rather than admit we'd misread the foundation stones."

"Then let the conversation fail slowly," I said. "Tell me about the foundation stones instead. Tell me anything that isn't about me."

"All right." He pulled a plum from his pocket, considered it, put it back. "When I was apprenticed, my mentor — Magister Corvel, you'll meet him if we live, God help us — taught me the first law of warding. A ward is a promise made of geometry. Every seal, every border-stone, every Door is just a promise that somebody, somewhere, considered a boundary worth the cost of keeping. And the second law, the one they only teach you when you're being expelled: *find out who benefits from the boundary, and you'll find who built it.*" He turned to look at me straight on. "The wards around Vaelmoor weren't built to keep the world out, Zeph. Any fool could read that in the stone. They were drawn inward-facing. All the pressure points face the valley. I told you the border stones were cracked from the inside? I lied, a little. They weren't cracked. They were *straining*. Straining to hold something in."

The grey light seemed to pull further back from us, like a tide going out.

"You believe the dragon," I said.

"I believe the geometry," Tam said. "Dragons can lie. Angles can't." He paused. "And I believe a man who walks through the fray like he's homesick for a place he's never seen. I watched you do it. You cried the whole time and called it wind."

I had not known he'd seen that. Something in me tilted, a shelf of books deciding whether to stay upright.

"If it's true," I said, and my voice came out sanded down to the grain, "then Vaelmoor wasn't my home. It was my cell. Everyone I ever — Mrs. Aldery who taught me letters, the ferryman who fished me out of the Miren when I was twelve, every friend, every—" My hands had begun to shake; I pressed them flatter against my knees. "Then I never lived a life. I lived a sentence. Served out daily, with good behavior."

"Yes," Tam said. No cushion in it, because he knew me well enough by now to know I'd rather fall on stone than feathers. "And also no." He tapped my shoulder with one blunt finger, once, twice, like testing a wall for studs. "The man who held a terrified kid's hand through the screaming part of the fray — the dragon didn't write that. The guild didn't write that. Whatever they forged you out of, they couldn't forge *that*. So whichever name turns out to be yours, I'll judge the hands, not the signature."

I laughed, wetly, disgracefully. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in a counterfeit life."

"Don't get sentimental. It makes me itch." But he stayed sitting next to me, shoulder almost touching mine, both of us watching the child sleep against the flank of the last dragon in the world, and for a few minutes the hollow country felt almost inhabited.

* * *

Isolde woke at dawn-that-wasn't, sat up out of the curve of the dragon's wing, and looked at me across the amphitheater floor with an expression I had never seen on her face before, and hope died in me before she opened her mouth, because I knew — the way you know weather coming — that whatever she said next, the three of us would stop being what we had been.

"You dreamed," Ashwing said to her. Not a question. Dragons, apparently, could smell dreams the way Tam smelled lies in stonework.

She nodded. She did not come to me. She stayed standing by the dragon's foreleg with her fists at her sides, a herald receiving a message she wished she could refuse to deliver.

"I dreamed about the palace," she said. "The real one, the one before. There was a room full of maps, and everyone in white robes was shouting, and there was a woman at the big table with ink on her fingers up to the wrist, and—" Her voice wobbled. She steadied it the way she had learned to steady it, by gripping something true. "Everyone was calling her Vessarin. And she was crying. But she kept drawing anyway. She drew the whole night, and every line she finished, a star went out, and she apologized to the stars while she worked, I remember that, she kept saying *forgive me, forgive me, forgive me*—"

"Stop," I said.

"—and at the end she signed it, and the salt came off her finger onto the page, and she looked up right at me, right at where I was standing in the dream, and she said—"

"*Isolde.*" Louder than I meant. It rang around the amphitheater and came back at me from ten thousand empty seats, my own voice accusing me from every direction.

She flinched. Then she set her jaw, nine years old and armored in everything she had left, and finished it anyway, because she was the daughter of a line that had kept a country alive by remembering it, and she had never once in all our miles let an adult's discomfort interrupt the truth.

"She said: *tell the girl with the harbor eyes I'm sorry.*"

Silence. Even the wind in the colonnade quit, as if ashamed.

I stood at the edge of the terrace with the grey light crawling over me and the dragon's name for me lodged under my ribs like a splinter working toward the heart, and I understood, finally, the shape of the trap we had walked into. It was not the Door Out. It was not the guttering fire or the collapsing country or the guild waiting ahead with their kept doors and their complicit geometry.

It was this: I could not prove I was innocent. And I could not bear to prove I was guilty. And either answer unmade one of the two people standing in front of me — the stranger who had crossed the fray for a child, or the coward who had sold a country to save a valley — and I did not know which of them I would be killing, or which of them I could live without.

Tam rose slowly to his feet beside me. Isolde stood below, waiting, her harbor eyes fixed on my face.

"So," she said, in the smallest voice I'd ever heard her use. "Was it you?"
