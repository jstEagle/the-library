## 10. The Guild of Kept Doors

Tam Voss had spent eleven years telling himself the guildhall would be smaller than he remembered.

It was a petty comfort, the kind of lie a man feeds himself the way you feed a fire one damp stick at a time — not because it will catch, but because your hands need something to do. He had imagined returning to the Guild of Kept Doors as a beggar at a gate, a disgraced apprentice come crawling back to men who had already forgotten his name. In that imagining, the hall was always diminished: its towers squat, its slate roofs cracked, its famous door — the Door Out itself, or the guild's facsimile of it — no taller than a barn door and twice as dull.

The hall was not smaller. That was the first cruelty of the day.

It rose out of the grey hills of the Marchlands like a fist of black stone, seven towers crowned with copper that had gone green as old bottle-glass, and every tower bore a bell, and every bell was silent. The road up to it was paved in fitted granite worn smooth by centuries of ward-keepers' boots, and Tam knew every stone of it. He had walked it at six years old, holding his mother's hand, both of them turned away from the gate. He had walked it again at fourteen, alone this time, admitted. He had walked down it at twenty-six, stripped of his sash, his oath-braid cut off and burned in front of him while the whole order watched from the high gallery with the particular stillness of people witnessing an execution they have all signed for.

Now he walked it a fourth time, with a cartographer who did not know what they were and a child who was spending herself mile by mile to pave the way here, and the stones felt colder than he remembered. Colder than stone should feel.

"They're watching us," Isolde said.

She was walking between them, the way she always did now, as if she'd appointed herself the mortar between two walls that might otherwise drift apart. Her eyes were on the towers. "In the windows. They're not hiding it."

She was right. Faces at the glass — pale ovals in the dark of the bell-chambers, motionless, arranged along the height of the towers like ornaments hung out to dry. Not hostile, exactly. Worse than hostile. *Expectant.* As if the guild had been waiting for them the way a trap waits for a foot.

"They know we're coming," Zephyrine said quietly. Their voice had changed since the hollow country; since Ashwing called them another name. It had gone careful, the voice of someone handling their own words like cracked porcelain. "They've known since the wards fell. Maybe since before."

"Since before," Tam said, and hated how steady he made it sound.

Because here was the thing he had not said aloud, the thing he had been carrying since the border stones cracked inward instead of outward, since he'd knelt in the frost and read the fracture-lines the way he'd been trained to read them and understood, with a sick lurch, that the damage came from the *heart* of the warding, not its edges. Wards did not crack from within unless something inside them wanted out. Or unless the hands that built them had built them wrong on purpose.

He had told himself it was sabotage by some rival faction, some heretic cell, some foreign power. He had offered Zephyrine those explanations in the early days like a man dealing cards he knew were marked. And underneath the cards, quiet as a debt, another suspicion had sat and waited: that the guild he still loved — God help him, after everything, he still loved it — had known exactly what it was doing.

The great doors of the guildhall stood open.

That was wrong. The doors of the Guild of Kept Doors were never open. There were forty-one doors under the guild's keeping across the world, and the guild's own threshold was the forty-first and least of them, and it was sealed with three locks and a spoken word that changed each dawn. Tam stood before the open dark of the entrance hall and felt his skin crawl the way it crawled near failing wards — that animal certainty that a boundary had been crossed without permission.

"They want us to walk in," Isolde said.

"Aye." Tam rolled his shoulders. His missing oath-braid ached sometimes, a phantom itch at the nape where the hair had been shorn, and it ached now. "And I'd like to know what it costs us to be expected."

Zephyrine looked at him. Their face in the grey light was drawn, sleepless, ink-stained at the cuticles even now — the cartographer's mark, permanent as sin. "You could wait outside," they said. "Both of you. This is my—" They stopped. Swallowed whatever word had been coming. *My fault*, probably. Or *my crime*. They were carrying Ashwing's accusation like a stone in the chest these days, and Tam could see the weight of it in the set of their shoulders.

"No," Isolde said, flat and immediate, in the tone she used when adults suggested foolish things like leaving her behind.

Tam almost smiled. Almost. "We're a map-party," he said. "You said so yourself, back on the salt road. Three names, one purpose. You don't get to unname us just because the road's gone ugly."

Something moved through Zephyrine's face then — quick, painful, grateful — and Tam looked away from it, because there were things a man couldn't look at straight on without his own composure cracking.

They went in together.

* * *

The entrance hall smelled the same. That was the second cruelty.

Ward-smoke and cold iron and the particular tallow the guild rendered from mountain-mutton fat, burned only in the hall lamps — a smell Tam had breathed through eight years of apprenticeship until it was more home to him than any house. It hit him at the threshold like a hand flat against the sternum, and for one treacherous heartbeat he was fourteen again, standing in this exact spot, hearing the Provost's voice roll down the stairwell: *Another mouth. Feed him, teach him, keep him.*

The hall was empty. Long tables, bare. Lamps burning low and blue — ward-lamps, fed on salt and silver, the flame that showed true shapes. On the far wall hung the Roll of Doors: forty-one iron plaques, each stamped with the sigil of a door under the guild's protection, each polished bright by generations of dutiful thumbs.

Forty plaques gleamed.

The forty-first — lowest, leftmost, the plain square stamp that meant *the Door Out itself* — was black. Not tarnished. Painted over, deliberately, in a lacquer so deep it looked like a hole in the wall.

"They've decommissioned it," Tam said. His voice came out strange. "You don't paint over a plaque. You strike it, if a door's lost. You hold a rite. You carve the keeper's name on the mourning-stone." He stepped closer, close enough to see his own reflection warped in the black lacquer, and his stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water. "Paint is what you use when you want a door forgotten but not *gone*. When you want the record to say nothing happened."

"When you're hiding it," Isolde said.

"Aye. When you're hiding it."

Footsteps above. Measured, unhurried, descending the spiral stair with the deliberate cadence of a man who has decided exactly how long the wait should be. Tam knew the cadence before he saw the boots. Eleven years, and his body still counted the steps the way it had counted them every morning of his apprenticeship.

Provost Aldous Crane came down into the lamplight unchanged, which was its own obscenity. Time had passed over Tam like a file — sharpened him, thinned him, taken pieces — but Crane was exactly as Tam had left him: tall and dry as a winter stalk, silver at the temples in two precise wings, robes of office immaculate, hands folded. His eyes found Tam first. Of course they did. There was a flicker in them — not surprise, not guilt. Recognition, and beneath it, faintly, something that might have been relief.

"Tamsin Voss," Crane said. "You kept your posture. Good. Someone taught you well."

"You taught me," Tam said. "Then you burned what you taught me for."

"I struck a braid. Braids grow back." Crane's gaze slid past him, unhurried, and settled on Zephyrine, and Tam watched the Provost's face do something very small and very controlled — a tightening around the eyes, the look of a man confirming a ledger entry. "Cartographer Eriksen. And the child." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "Isolde. We wondered which of you would come first. We had wagers on it."

"We?" Zephyrine said.

"The Conclave." Crane gestured, and doors at the hall's upper gallery opened, and figures came down — nine of them, ten, senior ward-keepers in their grey-and-copper, faces Tam had eaten beside, slept beside, sworn beside. Magister Hale, who had mended Tam's first broken focus-stone with his own hands. Sister Ondrey, who had wept openly at his disgrace and then voted for it anyway. They arranged themselves along the wall with the ease of long practice, and Tam understood, with a cold settling in his gut, that this was not an ambush. It was a *reception*. They had rehearsed it.

"What is this," Tam said. Not a question. He made it not a question so it wouldn't shake.

"This is the truth being returned to you," Crane said, "eleven years late, at the cost of considerable inconvenience, because the wards have failed and the inconvenience has become unavoidable." He folded his hands the way he did before lectures, and Tam's whole body braced against the familiar gesture. "You were exiled for insubordination, officially. For tampering with the Vaelmoor ward-signs, unofficially. Both charges were true, and both were cover. The real reason, Tamsin, is that you asked the wrong question at the wrong age, and you were too good a student to stop asking it."

"What question?"

Crane looked at him with something that in a kinder man would have been pity. "*Who are the wards for?*"

The hall was silent except for the blue hiss of the lamps. Tam heard his own pulse. Behind him, Zephyrine had gone very still, and Isolde had taken half a step closer to them both, small and fierce as a coal.

"Every apprentice learns the doctrine," Crane went on. "Wards protect. Wards enclose. Wards are the guild's gift to a frightened world. And every apprentice who is worth anything eventually notices the arithmetic doesn't balance. A valley the size of Vaelmoor needs a boundary-ward, Tamsin. What it was given was a *containment*. Forty-one anchor-stones. Triple redundancy. Power routed from the Door Out itself — the one door in the world whose entire purpose is to let things leave." His voice dropped, and the drop was worse than any shout. "We do not spend the Door Out's strength on sheep-fences. Unless the thing inside the fence matters more than the door."

"The valley," Zephyrine said. Their voice was thin. "Vaelmoor. You warded it to keep—"

"Not the valley." Crane turned to them, and for the first time the Provost's composure showed a hairline crack, something moving underneath it like a fish under ice. "Valleys don't need keeping in. People barely need keeping in. But one person — one person needed a prison so complete that the prisoner couldn't even remember it was a prison. So we gave them a life. A whole stitched-together life. A trade they loved. Neighbors. Seasons. Contentment." His eyes flicked to Tam, and there it was at last, the guilt, arriving eleven years late and wearing the mask of duty. "And we posted a young keeper on the border stones to maintain the fiction, and when that young keeper started reading the ward-arithmetic correctly, we cut his braid and sent him away rather than answer him."

The floor did not move, but Tam felt it move. Eleven years of exile reorganized themselves around him in a single breath — the jeering, the burned braid, Ondrey's tears, the long bitter years of freelance ward-work in towns that paid badly and trusted worse — all of it, not punishment. *Housekeeping.* He had been swept out of the way of a secret, the way you sweep a floor before company comes.

"Say the name," Tam said. His voice came out low and level and it surprised him, how level, how much it cost to keep it so. "Say the name of the person the wards were built to keep."

Crane held his gaze for a long moment. Around the gallery, the other keepers looked at their hands, at the floor, at the black-painted plaque — anywhere but at the center of the room.

"You already know," Crane said quietly. "You've known since the dragon spoke. You simply haven't let yourself finish the thought, because finishing it means admitting what we did to you, and what we asked you to unknowingly do to them." He drew a breath. "The wards of Vaelmoor were raised to keep Zephyrine Eriksen inside. Every stone of them. For nineteen years."

Tam didn't turn around. He didn't need to. He heard it land in Zephyrine behind him — a small sound, half breath, half wound — and he heard Isolde move toward them, quick and certain, and he stood facing his Provost with his hands shaking at his sides and his heart breaking in two directions at once: backward, toward the boy he'd been, who had loved this hall and been thrown away by it; and forward, toward the person behind him, who had been given a counterfeit life as casually as a coin tossed to a beggar.

"Why," Tam said. "What did they *do*?"

"That," said Crane, "is not mine to tell. I only kept the door." And then, softer, with the first honest weariness Tam had ever heard in the man's voice: "Tamsin. You asked once why the guild is called the Guild of Kept Doors. I told you it was because we keep doors safe. That was the lesson. Here is the correction, and may it rot me: we are not keepers of doors. We are keepers *by* doors. We decide what goes through and what stays shut, and we have decided, for nineteen years, that one person stays shut in, and a nation stays shut out, and the last dragon burns down to nothing to pay for it." He unfolded his hands at last, and Tam saw they were trembling, very slightly, the tremor of a man who has held a pose too long. "The Door Out is below. Third sublevel, past the salt-vaults. It is still sealed, and it will stay sealed until the Conclave votes — and the Conclave does not agree on anything anymore, which is the only reason your errand has gotten this far."

"And if we go down there anyway?" Isolde said.

Every head in the gallery turned to her. She stood with her chin up and her fists at her sides, and Tam saw, with a lurch, how thin she'd gotten since the salt road — how much of her had been spent paving the way here — and how utterly unafraid she was anyway.

"Then you will find," Crane said, "that the door is guarded by the only member of this Conclave who never doubted. And she has been waiting nineteen years for the cartographer to arrive." He paused. "She will tell you the price. I would not wish to spoil it."

Behind Tam, Zephyrine spoke at last, and their voice was the flattest, quietest thing in the hall. "Take us down."

Crane bowed — actually bowed, the full formal depth of it, the bow owed to a superior, and Tam watched his former master bend his spine to the person he had helped imprison and thought: *so it was never contempt. It was fear. All these years, it was fear.*

The Provost straightened. "This way," he said. "And Tamsin—" a hesitation, human and terrible, "—I am glad you kept your posture. I am sorry about everything else."

Tam walked past him without a word, past the black-plaqued wall, past the nine grey faces that would not meet his eyes, and followed the lamps down toward the third sublevel, toward the Door Out, toward the price — with a cartographer who had been caged by kindness, and a child who was running out of self, and eleven years of his own life lying behind him in the lamplight like a coat he'd finally been allowed to take off.

The stairs went down farther than he remembered.

That, at least, was no comfort at all.
