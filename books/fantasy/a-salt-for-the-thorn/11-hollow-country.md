## 11. Hollow Country

The salt road ended where the sea should have begun.

Zephyrine felt it before they saw it — a wrongness in the soles of their boots, the way the ground beneath them had stopped being ground at all and become merely the idea of it, thin as paper over a well. They stood at the lip of Cael Adair and looked down into a country that was not there.

"Isolde," Tam said quietly, "hold my hand."

The girl did not ask why. She took his hand with the grave obedience of someone who had already learned that some instructions were not explanations but lifelines. Her other hand found Zephyrine's sleeve and gripped it, small fingers bunching the wool, and Zephyrine understood that she was not holding on for her own sake. She was anchoring them.

Below — or ahead; direction had grown unreliable since the stars went out — the land lay hollowed. Not ruined. Ruin implied something had happened to it, fire or flood or war, some violence that left bones to name. This was different. This was absence wearing the shape of presence, like a coat still hanging on its hook after the body has gone. Hills rose in the distance with the softness of hills, but their slopes were the color of unwritten things, grey-white as the inside of an eggshell, and when the wind moved through them Zephyrine could see straight through to the sky behind.

"The erasure didn't kill them," Isolde said. Her voice had changed over the miles of salt road, grown older, thinner, the voice of a girl spending herself coin by coin. "It just stopped remembering them hard enough."

* * *

They descended into the hollow country by a road that existed only because Isolde remembered walking it once, with her mother's hand around hers, toward a harbor market where they sold candied kelp and little tin boats that sailed on nothing. The road appeared under their feet as she spoke, cobblestones surfacing out of the grey like fish rising to feed, and behind them, as her attention moved forward, the cobbles sank away again into nothing.

Zephyrine walked at the rear and watched this happen, and each time the road dissolved behind them they felt a small cold click in their chest, like a lock turning in a door they hadn't known they carried.

Because they knew what it cost. They had watched Isolde pay it mile after mile on the salt road: the color of her grandmother's kitchen going first, then the name of her schoolteacher, then the tune she used to hum while skipping stones. Each memory spent paved a few yards of way. And now here, in the hollow heart of the erased nation, the road needed more than cobblestones. It needed people.

They came upon the first echo in a field of grey wheat that bent without wind.

It was a woman standing ankle-deep in the not-wheat, facing away from them, and she was almost entirely there — a blue dress, dark hair pinned up with what might have been knitting needles, hands busy at some task Zephyrine couldn't resolve no matter how they looked. The task kept sliding. Their eyes would find the woman's hands moving, folding, working, and then lose the thread of what they were doing, because whatever she was doing had been erased along with everything else, and only the doing remained, the shape of industry without its content.

"She can't see us," Isolde said softly. "Not really. We're not real enough to her anymore." A pause. "Or she's not real enough to us."

Tam made a sound low in his throat — the ward-keeper's sound, Zephyrine had learned, the one he made when magic was behaving badly and he was cataloguing exactly how badly for later report to no one. "She's caught in a loop. Whatever the erasure left of her, it runs on rails now. Same hour of the same day, forever." He said it flatly, professionally, but Zephyrine heard what was underneath it, the same thing underneath everything Tam said about wards: *this is a wound and I know who made it.*

As they passed, the woman turned her head — not all the way, just enough — and her mouth moved. No sound came out. But Zephyrine's lips shaped the words anyway, unbidden, reading them off her face the way one reads a familiar phrase across a crowded room:

*Have you seen my son?*

Zephyrine's step faltered. Isolde's grip tightened on their sleeve.

"Don't answer her," the girl whispered. "If you answer, you have to remember the answer. And we don't have it. Nobody has it. That's the whole—" Her voice cracked, and she pressed on harder, dragging them both forward. "That's the whole point of this place."

* * *

The city came next, because of course it did. Every country has a heart, and even hollowed ones keep the shape of theirs.

Cael Adair's capital — Isolde called it Serevan, and the name surfaced in Zephyrine's mouth a half-second before she said it, which frightened them more than anything else that day — rose out of the grey in terraces, white stone and copper roofs gone green, a harbor opening below onto water that was not water but the memory of water, flat and silver and utterly still, holding no reflections. Streets climbed the hillside in switchbacks. Lanterns hung from iron brackets, unlit, though it was neither day nor night here; the light came from everywhere and nowhere, the diffuse glow of a place photographed rather than lived in.

And the streets were full.

Echoes everywhere. A fishmonger calling prices in a language Zephyrine's ears refused to parse — the sounds arrived, the meaning did not, erased along with everything else. Children playing a game whose rules had been unwritten; they ran and laughed and tagged one another, and the laughter reached Zephyrine's ears as pure sound, beautiful and meaningless, music without words. Two old men sat outside a tavern playing a game with pieces on a board, and the board was there, and the pieces were there, and the game itself was a hole shaped like a game.

"They're not suffering," Tam said, and Zephyrine realized he'd been watching them too, watching Zephyrine watch them. "That's the mercy of it. Or the horror. I haven't decided. A loop doesn't hurt. It just is."

"I decided a long time ago," Isolde said, "that mercy isn't the right word for being turned into furniture."

They walked the terraces of Serevan, and the echoes parted around them without seeing them, and Zephyrine began, horribly, to recognize things.

Not the city — they had never been here, or if they had, the knowing was buried deeper than memory, down in the sediment where the Erasure Map had buried it. What they recognized was the *style*. The way the gutters curved outward at corners to spill rainwater into stone basins instead of onto the street. The way every third doorway bore a small carved thorn above the lintel. The way the harbor bells hung silent in their frames, seven of them, sized in descending order, tuned — Zephyrine knew this the way they knew their own heartbeat, which was to say without any evidence at all — tuned to the seven winds off the southern sea.

*I mapped this,* they thought, and then corrected themselves with the reflex of a drowning man grabbing driftwood: *I would have mapped this. Anyone with my eye would have.*

But the thought rang false, and they knew it rang false, and the harbor bells hung silent overhead like seven open mouths.

* * *

It started small, the way catastrophes do.

They stopped in a square where a fountain stood dry — the memory of water again, silver-flat, refusing to reflect — and ate the last of the bread Tam had carried from Vaelmoor. Isolde sat between them, quieter now than she had ever been on the road, her spending nearly done; Zephyrine could see it in her, the way one sees a candle near its end, the flame the same height but the wax gone translucent, light coming through where substance used to be.

Zephyrine closed their eyes for a moment. Just a moment. To rest them against the unremitting grey.

And reached for the image that had been their compass their whole life: the view from their window in Vaelmoor. The crooked chimney of the tannery across the lane. The rowan tree in the yard, heavy with red berries come autumn. Mrs. Aldery's cat, orange and half-feral, who slept on their windowsill and whom they had never named because naming it would mean admitting it was theirs.

The image came. But it came *thin.*

The berries were red, yes, but when Zephyrine looked for the exact red — the specific slightly-orange red of those particular berries, the red they had mixed ink to match a dozen times for their maps' borders — the color slid away from them like water off oiled cloth. The chimney was crooked, but crooked which way? The cat was orange, but had its ears been nicked, or had they only always believed they were?

Their eyes snapped open. Heart slamming. The square swam.

"Tam." Their voice came out wrong, flattened. "What color are the rowan berries in Vaelmoor?"

He looked at them for a long moment. He was not a kind man by habit — exile had sanded the kindness off him and left the grain — but he was honest, and honesty was its own terrible kindness. "Red," he said. "Ordinary red. Why?"

Ordinary red. As if there were such a thing. As if any red anywhere were ordinary.

"And the tannery chimney—"

"Zeph." He set down the bread. "Breathe."

"It's happening to me." The words tumbled out now, unstoppable, a cart losing its wheel downhill. "The memories. They're going the same way Isolde's went on the salt road, except I'm not spending them, I'm not *paying* for anything, they're just—" They pressed the heel of their hand against their sternum, against the cold clicking lock inside. "They're dissolving. Like sugar in water. And I know what that means, Tam. I've known since the border. Memories don't dissolve unless they were never *set*. Ink that hasn't dried smears. And mine—"

"Yours were never dried," Tam finished quietly. "Because they were never written properly in the first place."

Isolde had gone very still beside them. When Zephyrine turned to her, the girl was looking at them with an expression Zephyrine had no map for — grief and recognition braided together, the look of someone watching another person arrive at a place she had lived alone for years.

"You're starting to remember what I remember," Isolde said. "That the fake life is thinning out. That's why it hurts now. It didn't hurt before because you weren't looking at it closely." She reached out and took Zephyrine's hand, and her fingers were cool, cooler than a child's should be. "I'm sorry. I hoped you'd have longer."

"Longer before what?"

"Before you had to grieve it." She said it simply, the way children say unbearable things, because they have not yet learned to wrap them. "You have to grieve it, Zeph. Even though it wasn't real. *Especially* because it wasn't real. Because you loved it, and love doesn't check its receipts."

* * *

They walked on, because stopping meant drowning, and the hollow country offered no harbor for the drowning.

Through streets where echoes bought and sold nothing. Past a temple — dragon-priests, Isolde had said, and indeed the temple's doors were carved with coiling serpentine shapes, wings folded, mouths open in song — where a congregation of echoes knelt in rows before an altar that was blank, the god having been erased out of the middle of his own house. Past a schoolhouse where lessons continued in silence, chalk moving over slate, writing characters that dissolved even as Zephyrine tried to read them.

And all the while, Vaelmoor drained out of them.

It went gently, which was the cruelest part. Not torn away but *released*, the way a fist opens, finger by finger, until the hand forgets it was holding anything. Zephyrine walked the terraces of a dead city and felt their mother's laugh go — not the fact of it, they had lost the fact of it long ago, but the *sound*, the specific warm broken note at the end of it when she laughed too hard. Then the smell of their first workshop, beeswax and lamp oil and the iron tang of fresh ink. Then the name of the street they had lived on, then the number of the house, then — they checked, frantically, the way one checks a pocket for keys — then the face.

They still had the face. For now. But it had gone general. It was *a* mother's face, kind and lined, a face from the stock of faces, not *their* mother's, not anyone's in particular, and Zephyrine understood with a clarity that felt like falling that this was how erasure worked from the inside. It didn't take things away. It replaced the particular with the plausible. It traded the true for the typical, and the typical was so much easier to carry, and you thanked it, you *thanked* it, for making your burden lighter.

"Here." Tam's voice, close. He had put something in their hand — the flask he carried, the one he pretended held only water. It did not hold only water. Zephyrine drank, and the burn of it grounded them briefly to the cobbles, to the moment, to the body that was apparently the only true thing they owned.

"How much is left?" they asked.

"Of Vaelmoor?" Tam looked at the horizon, where the grey hills met the greyer sky. "Enough to miss. That's the trick of it. The erasure leaves you enough to miss. Grief needs material." His jaw worked. "I've watched wards fail before. Never watched a person fail. It's slower. Worse."

"I'm not failing."

"No," he agreed, and there was something in his voice Zephyrine couldn't read — belief, maybe, or the decision to believe, which he had taught them on the salt road was sometimes the stronger thing. "You're not. You're the only thing in this whole hollowed-out country that's getting *more* real the further in we walk. Did you notice that? The echoes can't see us. The road won't hold for us. But you—" He tapped their chest, once, hard, over the sternum. "You cast a shadow here. Nothing else does. Whatever you were, whoever drew you into that counterfeit valley — they built you strong, Zeph. Strong enough to walk back out."

*Strong enough to walk back.* Zephyrine turned the words over and found the trap inside them, smooth as river stone. Built. Drawn. Made. A tool doesn't grieve its counterfeit life. A tool is *glad* of it, glad of any life at all.

But grief had arrived anyway, and it did not consult its receipts.

* * *

They camped that night — if night it was; the light simply dimmed, courteous as a servant — in the shell of a harbor inn called, according to a sign whose letters kept rearranging themselves, either *The Drowned Bell* or *The Bellringer's Daughter* or possibly *The Drowned Daughter*, as though the sign itself were an echo unsure of its own loop.

Isolde slept between them, exhausted past dreaming. Tam kept the first watch, whittling a new stylus from a piece of driftwood that was probably not driftwood, since nothing here had ever floated anywhere.

Zephyrine sat awake and took inventory, the way they inventoried everything, because inventory was the last skill they trusted.

Remaining: the feel of ink drying under the hand. The weight of a good map-case against the hip. The sound of rain on a roof — any roof; the sound had detached itself from Vaelmoor and gone universal, and universal things, they were learning, were the survivors. The names Tam and Isolde, which were new, which had been earned, which were therefore *set*, dried ink, indelible. Those they would keep. Whatever else the erasure of a false life took, it could not take three days of salt road and a child's hand in the dark and a bitter man choosing, twice now, to stay.

Gone or going: the window. The rowan. The cat with the uncertain ears. The mother who was becoming a stranger in a portrait. Twenty-nine years of a life resolving, line by line, into the plausible, the typical, the stock.

And underneath it all, rising like groundwater, like floodwater, like the sea finally reclaiming a polder: the other life. Cael Adair. Serevan's terraces. The seven harbor bells. The thorn over every third door. A woman in a blue field asking after her son. A king's court, maybe. A guild of ward-keepers. A dragon. A map drawn as a weapon, in a hand that was their hand.

Zephyrine sat in the drowned bell of a dead inn and grieved — openly, silently, tears running into their beard, for a valley that had never existed, for a self that had never existed, for the particular red of berries they would never mix ink to match again. It was, they discovered, possible to mourn a lie sincerely. The lie had held their whole heart. Its falseness did not refund the love.

Near dawn, Isolde stirred against their side and murmured a word in her sleep. A word in the language the fishmonger had cried and the echoes prayed in — the language of Cael Adair, alive in her because she was the last vessel of it, the last true daughter of the unwritten country.

Zephyrine did not know what the word meant.

But their mouth shaped it perfectly, accentlessly, in the dark — and somewhere far off in the hollow city, faint as a bell across water, something answered.
