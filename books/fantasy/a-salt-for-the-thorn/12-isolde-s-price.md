## 12. Isolde's Price

Isolde had learned to count her memories the way other children counted coins.

She sat in the lee of a fallen archway in the hollow country, knees drawn up, and went through them one by one the way her mother had once gone through a purse before market day — turning each over, testing its weight, deciding what could be spared. There was the memory of gulls at Cael Adair's harbor, white-winged and loud as argument. The memory of dragon-priests swinging censers of salt-smoke through the dawn streets. The memory of her grandmother's hands, ringed in silver, teaching her to fold a paper boat that would sail on air instead of water.

And beneath all of them, wrapped around them like a stone at the bottom of a well, there was the last one. The heavy one. The true one.

She was running out. She could feel it the way you feel winter coming — not in any single cold morning but in the quality of the light, in the way the world had begun to seem slightly secondhand. Each mile of the Salt Road had cost her. Each step she had remembered into being had been paid for out of herself, and now the road behind them stretched long and pale and perfect, paved with everything she used to be.

The hollow country breathed around her. It was not wind, exactly, though it moved like wind. It was the sound of a place trying to remember itself — half-voices in the ruined streets, echoes of erased citizens drifting past the broken doorways like smoke looking for chimneys. Isolde had stopped flinching from them days ago. Now she only listened, hoping to catch a word of harbor-song, a scrap of the dragon-tongue, anything she could hold onto.

Mostly they said nothing. Mostly they were just shapes of almost-remembering, faces like reflections in disturbed water.

"Isolde."

Zephyrine's voice. She looked up. The cartographer stood at the edge of the archway with their map-case slung across their back and that look on their face they always wore lately — the look of someone reading a page that kept rewriting itself under their eyes. Behind Zephyrine, Tam Voss was crouched over a fire that burned without heat, feeding it shavings of ward-stone, his scarred hands moving with the automatic tenderness of a man tending something he no longer believed in.

"You should eat," Zephyrine said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You haven't been hungry for two days." They crouched beside her, and up close Isolde could see how thin the cartographer had gotten too, how the hollow country was drinking from everyone who walked it. "You're spending yourself on this road, little one. I've watched you do it. Every mile we've walked since the salt began, you've paid for it, and you think none of us have noticed."

Isolde looked down at her own hands. They seemed fainter than they used to be. Not transparent — nothing so dramatic as that — just *less*, the way a word repeated too many times becomes only a sound.

"I noticed," she said.

* * *

That night they camped in what had once been, Isolde thought, a temple to the tide. The walls still held carvings of waves, though the waves had been worn smooth by an erasure that did not erode so much as *forget*. Above the broken roof, where stars should have been, there was only the same flat darkness that had followed them out of Vaelmoor — a sky like a page with nothing written on it yet.

Tam took first watch. Zephyrine lay awake by the dead fire, and Isolde knew they were awake because their breathing had the careful rhythm of someone pretending.

She got up quietly and crossed to them.

"Zeph," she whispered. She had started calling them that somewhere on the Salt Road, and Zephyrine had gone very still the first time, and then had smiled in a way that made Isolde's chest hurt, and so she kept doing it. "I need to tell you something. Before I can't anymore."

Zephyrine sat up. In the dark their face was mostly shadow and the pale gleam of eyes. "What is it?"

Isolde sat down beside them and pulled her knees up again, and for a while she didn't speak, because the thing she had to say was enormous and she had carried it alone for so long that setting it down felt like setting down the sky.

"You know how I remember Cael Adair," she began. "The harbors. The priests. You know how the adults back home didn't, how even the words slid off them. You asked me once why I was different. Why a child remembered what whole countries forgot." She swallowed. "I lied to you. Not with my mouth. I just... never answered."

"Isolde—"

"Let me say it while I still can." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "There's a reason the erasure never finished. A reason this place is only *hollow* instead of *gone*. Erasure needs a seal, Zeph. Like your wards in Vaelmoor. And a seal needs a keystone. Something living, something true, something that carries the country inside it so completely that unmaking it would be like unmaking the idea of harbors, or the idea of home." She looked up at the blank sky. "They couldn't find a way to erase me. So they left me. And they hoped I'd forget."

Zephyrine had gone very still. Even the hollow country seemed to lean closer, its half-voices hushing.

"The royal line of Cael Adair," Isolde said, "ended the night of the erasure. Everyone says so. Everyone who remembers anything at all says so." She turned and met the cartographer's eyes. "It didn't end. It got small. It got seven years old. It got carried out of the burning city in a fish-cart under nets, by a woman who wasn't my mother but died for me anyway." Her voice cracked on the last of it, and she let it. "I'm the last daughter of the house of Adair, Zephyrine. I'm the keystone. That's why the echoes still linger. That's why Ashwing still burns, guttering, chained to a country that won't quite die. Because I'm alive, and I remember, and as long as both those things are true, Cael Adair cannot fully end."

The silence after was the kind of silence that has weight. Somewhere far off in the ruined city, one of the echoes moaned — a long, low sound like a bell underwater — and Isolde chose to believe it was mourning, or maybe rejoicing. With echoes you could never tell.

* * *

Zephyrine did not speak for a long time. When they finally did, their voice was rough.

"All this time. All these miles. You've been paying for the road out of a treasury I didn't know existed." Their hands curled closed on their knees. "Every memory you spent —"

"Was mine to spend." Isolde lifted her chin. "My grandmother told me once: a crown isn't a thing you wear. It's a thing you owe. I owe this country everything, Zeph. Its harbors are in my head. Its songs are in my head. If I hoard them, they die with me when the last flame gutters out, and nobody will ever smell salt on Cael Adair's wind again." She shrugged, aiming for casualness and missing by a mile. "Besides. Memories are like coins. They're only worth something when you spend them."

"That's not what coins are for either," Zephyrine said, and there was something breaking underneath the dryness of it. "Isolde, you're a child. You're supposed to keep your memories. That's what childhood *is*."

"I'm also a queen," Isolde said. "Or I would be, if my country existed. Which is rather the point."

Tam had come over sometime during the conversation without either of them noticing — ward-keepers moved quietly, even disgraced ones, even ones whose guild had betrayed everything. He stood at the edge of the firelight with his arms folded, and his face, usually so carefully bitter, had gone soft and stricken.

"How many do you have left?" he asked. "Memories. Truly."

Isolde considered lying. She was good at lying; queenship apparently came with the talent. But she was so tired, and these two had walked every mile of her road with her, and lies between family were just another kind of erasure.

"One," she said. "One big one."

The fireless fire crackled its soundless crackle. Tam sat down heavily on a fallen pillar, as if his legs had been quietly removed from the arrangement.

"And which one is that?" Zephyrine asked, and Isolde heard the fear in their voice, the fear of a person who had already lost one life to secrets and could not bear another.

"It's the first one," Isolde said. "The oldest one I have. Older than the harbor gulls, older than the priests, older than the fish-cart. It's the memory of the night the erasure was drawn." She looked directly at Zephyrine now, and her heart was pounding, because this was the part she had circled for days, the part that was a gift and a wound at once. "It's the memory of *you*, Zephyrine. Of who you were before Vaelmoor. Before the counterfeit life. I was there. I saw your face when you signed the map. I've been carrying the truth of you inside me since before I could write my own name."

Zephyrine's breath went out of them slowly, like air from a punctured lung.

"You're saying—" they started, and stopped, and started again. "You're saying you know what I did."

"I know what you *are*," Isolde corrected gently. "Those aren't the same thing. I learned that from watching you walk. The person who drew the Erasure Map wouldn't have carried Tam across the fray when his leg gave out. Wouldn't have slept sitting up against a wall so I could lie flat. Wouldn't have cried at night thinking neither of us could hear." She reached out and put her small hand over the cartographer's ink-stained fingers. "But that's not why I'm telling you. I'm telling you because I have one memory left, and I've decided what to do with it, and you don't get a vote."

Tam made a sound low in his throat. "Isolde. Girl. Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm going to give it to Zeph," she said.

* * *

The silence that followed had three different kinds of horror in it, and Isolde catalogued them all with the strange clarity that comes from having nothing left to lose.

Tam's horror was the simplest: the horror of a man watching a child propose to burn the last of herself. "You give away your final memory, you'll be—" He gestured helplessly at the hollow dark around them. "*This.* One of them. An echo. You'll linger a few years and then thin out into nothing, and the keystone dies with you, and the country goes with it. Do you understand what you'd be undoing? Everything you just told us. Everything you've paid. For *what*?"

"For the truth," Isolde said. "For the map."

Zephyrine's horror was quieter and worse. They had risen to their feet without seeming to decide to, and they stood with their back half-turned, shoulders rigid, staring out at the ruined temple walls as if the carved waves might offer counsel.

"No," they said finally.

"Yes."

"*No,* Isolde." They turned, and their eyes were wet and furious. "Do you know what I've been carrying since the confession? Since I found that map in my own hand? Not guilt — I haven't even earned real guilt yet, I don't remember the crime. What I've been carrying is the *not knowing*. Walking around inside a life that's a locked room. Every mirror a question. Every kindness anyone shows me suspect, because how could they love someone who did what I did?" Their voice shook. "And you want to make me carry the answer too. The truth of who I was. You want to hand me the worst thing I'll ever learn and pay for it with *everything you are*."

"Yes," Isolde said again. "Because you'll need it."

"Need it for *what*?"

"For the Door Out." She said it plainly, the way you tell someone the bridge is out. "Ashwing told us the price. The guild loyalist will tell us plainer. The Restoration Map has to be redrawn by the same hand that drew the Erasure Map — and that hand has to go into the ink. But Zeph, listen, *listen* — a hand can't redraw what it doesn't understand. You can't restore a country from guilt alone. Guilt only knows how to tear things down. You'll need to know what Cael Adair was. What it smelled like at dawn. What the dragons' vows sounded like. What *you* loved about it, once, when you were the person who drew maps to keep things instead of to kill them." Her voice dropped. "All of that is in my last memory. The whole night. The whole truth. The country and the crime and the person you were, all tangled together, because that's how truth works — it doesn't come in pieces you get to choose."

Zephyrine stared at her. "And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll spend it anyway," Isolde said. "On the road. On the next mile. Because that's what I've been doing, and I don't know how to stop, and honestly—" and here her composure, held together with spit and queenship, finally frayed — "honestly, Zeph, I'm so tired. I'm so tired of being the only one who remembers. I've been the whole country by myself for seven years. Seven years of being a nation of one. And I found you — I found *both* of you — and for the first time it doesn't feel like carrying the sky alone, and I thought, I thought, if I put the last of it into you, into your hands, into the map — then it won't be lost. It'll be *spent*. Spent on something. Grandmother said a crown is a thing you owe, and I'd rather owe it forward than bury it in a grave I dug out of my own head."

She was crying now, and she didn't try to hide it, because hiding things was what had gotten everyone killed in the first place.

Tam crossed to her and knelt, and the old bitterness in his face had burned away entirely, leaving something raw and young. "There has to be another way," he said hoarsely. "Ward-craft has transfer rites. Anchors. I was exiled for less than knowing them — let me think, let me work it—"

"There isn't time," Isolde said softly. "Ashwing's fire is guttering. You said it yourself, Tam. Days, maybe. And the Door seals when the last flame dies." She wiped her face with her sleeve, a gesture so ordinary and childish that it broke Zephyrine audibly — a small cracked sound, quickly smothered. "There's never another way. There's only the way, and who walks it with you."

* * *

They talked until the false dark thinned toward a false dawn. Tam argued rites and anchors and loopholes until his voice gave out. Zephyrine argued longer, and harder, and with a desperation that Isolde treasured even as she refused it — because being argued for was its own kind of gift, proof that she was not merely a keystone to these two, not merely a road-paver or a royal line, but a girl someone loved enough to fight.

In the end it was Zephyrine who stopped fighting. They sat back down beside her as the grey light came, and they were quiet for a long moment, and then they said, in a voice scraped clean:

"If I take it. If I take your last memory and learn what I was. Promise me something first."

"What?"

"When it's done — when the map is drawn, whatever happens to me — you'll be a queen worth remembering. Not a martyr. Not a coin somebody spent. A queen. With harbors." Zephyrine's jaw worked. "Promise me you'll live in one of them."

Isolde smiled through the last of her tears. "Only if you promise to come see it."

"I don't know what I'll be able to promise," Zephyrine said. "After."

"Then promise the part before," Isolde said. "Promise to take it. Promise to know. Because I can't stand the thought of you walking into that Door still guessing."

The cartographer closed their eyes. When they opened them, they nodded — once, slow, like a border stone tipping.

Tam turned away to stand watch, and his shoulders were shaking, though he would deny it forever.

Isolde took Zephyrine's hands in hers. They were bigger than hers, ink-stained, calloused from the road, and they trembled. Around them the hollow country hushed, every echo in the ruined city going still at once, as if the whole erased nation had gathered to witness.

"Close your eyes," Isolde whispered. "It's easier if you close your eyes."

And she reached into the deepest room of herself, into the well-bottom where the last true thing of Cael Adair had waited all these years, and she lifted it out — the burning city, the signing hand, the salt and the thorn and the terrible mercy of it — and she pressed it, whole and blazing, into the only hands she trusted to carry it home.

The last light of her country left her like a bird leaving a branch.

And far away, beneath a sky with no stars, the last dragon raised its head and *roared*.
