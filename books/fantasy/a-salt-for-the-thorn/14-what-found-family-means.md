## 14. What Found Family Means

Tam Voss had never been good at sleeping in the hollow country.

It wasn't the silence, though the silence was wrong — a hush with texture to it, like wool pressed over the ears of the world. It wasn't the light either, that grey pearlescence that came from no sun he could name, seeping up out of the ground itself as if the erased land remembered daylight only as an idea. It was the echoes. In the deep hours they gathered at the edges of whatever fire they'd built, standing just past the reach of the glow, wearing faces half-finished, mouths moving around words that had been taken from them before they could be spoken. Tam had learned, over three nights now, to close his eyes and let his ward-keeper's training do what it was made for: hold a boundary, keep a small space clean and true, and trust that morning would come.

Morning here did not come so much as accumulate.

He woke on the fourth day to the smell of salt and woodsmoke and, beneath both, something sweeter — Isolde's voice, low and steady, telling Zephyrine about the harbor markets of Cael Adair. The fish-sellers who sang their prices instead of shouting them. The bells of the tide-temples that rang not on the hour but on the turn of the current. He lay still inside his bedroll and listened, and thought: *this is how a country survives its own death. Not in stone. In a child's voice, offered freely, mile after mile.*

The thought hurt in a way he didn't examine. There had been a time when he'd have examined it. When he'd have taken it apart joint by joint the way the guild taught initiates to take apart a ward — find the flaw, name the flaw, report the flaw. That was before he'd learned what his order did with flaws. Before the mentor whose hands had taught him to draw a circle had used those same hands to sign the papers that put him out of it.

He got up. The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and Zephyrine sat beside it with their knees drawn up, the hidden map spread across their lap even though they couldn't look at it straight-on. Isolde was curled against their side like a cat against warmth, her eyes half-closed, her mouth still moving through the litany of harbors.

"—and the Salt Road started at the Gate of Gulls, and you could smell it before you saw it, like bread and lightning—"

"Like bread and lightning," Zephyrine repeated softly. Their hand moved without looking down, sketching in the margin of some old chart: a gate, two towers, gulls rendered as three strokes each. They drew the way other people breathed. Tam had watched them do it for years across a dozen border-stone inspections, back when their whole acquaintance was professional and slightly frosty, and he had never once seen them hesitate over a line.

*You knew,* he thought, watching them now. *Some part of you always knew these roads. You just thought you were inventing them.*

That was the thing he kept circling, the way a tongue keeps finding the gap where a tooth was. Ashwing's accusation had lodged in all of them differently. In Isolde it had become armor: *they're wrong, they have to be wrong, Zephyrine wouldn't.* In Zephyrine it had become a wound they carried openly now, having stopped denying it somewhere in the hollow country's second night, when the echoes had begun to flinch away from them — the erased recognizing, in Zephyrine's face, the hand that had erased them.

In Tam it had become a question he hadn't asked aloud yet, because he already knew the answer, and because the answer was going to cost him something to say.

He built the fire up. Put water on. Did the small domestic work that kept a camp from becoming a grave. And all the while he watched the two of them — the disgraced ward-keeper's instinct in him cataloguing threats, and finding, for once, almost nothing to catalogue. Just a cartographer with a stranger's crime stitched into their memory, and a girl spending herself coin by coin to pave a road home, and himself, Tam Voss, exiled for the crime of asking one honest question too many, sitting among them like a man warming his hands at a fire he had no right to.

Isolde looked up and caught him watching. She didn't smile — she hadn't smiled much since the salt road began taking its toll, since the memory of her mother's face had gone wherever spent memories go — but she tilted her head, the way she did when she wanted him to come sit down and stop hovering.

He came and sat down.

* * *

They ate. Oat porridge, the last of the dried plums, water that tasted faintly of chalk because everything in the hollow country tasted faintly of chalk, as if the land had been bleached down to its skeleton. Zephyrine ate mechanically, eyes on the map, and Tam recognized the look because he wore it himself during long vigils: the look of someone holding a hard thing at arm's length by keeping their body busy.

"We should talk about the Door Out," Zephyrine said finally.

"We should," Tam agreed.

"And we should talk about—" Their jaw worked. "About what I am. What I was."

"No," Isolde said.

Both adults turned to her. She was sitting very straight now, plums forgotten, and there was a royal set to her shoulders that she didn't know she had — or maybe she did know, maybe it was the last inheritance the erasure hadn't managed to pry out of her blood.

"You've said sorry eleven times," she said. "I counted. Eleven times since the dragon. You can't be sorry anymore. It doesn't help and it costs too much." Her chin trembled and she steadied it with visible effort, a girl hauling on ropes far too heavy for her, and succeeding anyway. "We don't have time for it. Ashwing's fire is dying. You told us. Every hour we spend being sad about the person you were is an hour the echoes spend screaming."

Tam felt something turn over in his chest. He had spent four days composing arguments — careful, guild-trained arguments, the kind you delivered standing, with your hands behind your back, in rooms where decisions got made about other people's lives. And a nine-year-old had just walked into the middle of all of them and swept the table clear with one sentence.

"She's right," he said.

Zephyrine looked at him. There was fear in their face — real fear, the naked kind, the kind people only showed when they'd stopped having the strength to hide it. "You haven't said anything. Since the dragon. Four days, Tam. You've checked the wards twice a night and you've said nothing about any of it." A breath. "Say it now. Whatever it is. I'd rather have it than the silence."

So this was the moment. He'd known it was coming. He set down his bowl, because his hands wanted something to betray, and he made himself meet their eyes.

"I believe the dragon," he said.

Isolde made a sound of protest and he raised a hand — not to silence her, never that, but to ask for the length of one breath, and she gave it to him, because she was generous in ways the world hadn't yet taught her to ration.

"I believe the dragon," he said again, "and I'm going to tell you why, and then I'm going to tell you why it doesn't matter, and you're going to want to argue with the second part, so listen to the first part properly." He held up fingers, the way he'd once held them up for initiates. "One. The wards around Vaelmoor were built to keep one person in. I learned that in my mentor's own archive, with my own eyes. Whoever that person was, the guild feared them enough to seal a valley over it. Two. Your maps bleed Cael Adair. Roads you never surveyed appear in your ink in a hand identical to your own. Three. You navigate the unwritten country by instinct — you found the ford at Hollowmere before I did, and I grew up on those charts. Four." His fourth finger stayed folded. He unfolded it slowly. "Four. When Ashwing said your other name, you flinched before you could stop yourself. Not like a person hearing a stranger's name. Like a person hearing their own."

The fire popped. Somewhere out past the light, an echo sighed — a long, empty sound, like wind through a house with no one in it.

Zephyrine's face had gone very still. "And the part where it doesn't matter?"

"The guild taught me that a ward is judged by what it does, not by what it was drawn to do," Tam said. "A ward drawn in malice that holds back a flood is a good ward. A ward drawn in love that cages a child is a bad one. It took me ten years and an exile to understand that the same is true of people." He leaned forward. "I have watched you since the stars went out. I watched you carry Isolde across the fray when her feet bled. I watched you map Tam Voss — *me*, a man you'd known for one sour season of border inspections — back into existence out of open sea, by will alone, because losing me was unacceptable to you. I watched you take the truth of what you were and not run from it, which is more than my entire order has ever done, and they weren't even the ones who paid for it." His voice roughened; he let it. "Whoever drew the Erasure Map, they are dead, Zephyrine. They died in that valley, living a borrowed life, and what woke up in their place when the wards fell is someone I have chosen to follow. That's the whole of it. That's the part that doesn't matter and the part that matters, both at once."

Silence. The grey light thickened by one degree, morning accumulating.

Zephyrine's eyes were wet. They didn't wipe them. "You could be wrong."

"I could," Tam said. "I've been wrong before. It's how I got exiled — I was right in the wrong room. But I'd rather be wrong about you than right about anyone else I've ever served." He glanced at Isolde. "And I notice the heir to Cael Adair reached the same verdict days ago, by simpler methods."

"He counts things," Isolde said, with tremendous dignity. "I just know."

"Yes," Tam said. "That's generally how it works."

* * *

They broke camp late, because none of them hurried anymore. Hurrying belonged to the world outside, the written world, where time ran in its grooves like water in a channel. Here, in the hollow interior of an unwritten nation, time pooled and eddied, and you moved through it at the speed of grief or grace, whichever was winning that day.

They walked. The salt road had ended miles back — Isolde's memories spent down to grains, the white paving thinning to scattered crystals that glittered in the grey light like a beach after tide — and now they followed the pull in Zephyrine's chest, the compass-needle of guilt and love that Tam had privately begun to think of as the truest instrument he'd ever navigated by. Around them the hollow country breathed its slow, damaged breath. Ruins stood in the middle distance, complete and impossible: a harbor with no sea, cranes hanging over dry stones; a temple whose bell still swung, ringing a note too high to hear, felt only in the teeth. Echoes drifted between the ruins. Some of them, lately, had begun to wave.

"They know," Isolde said quietly, the third time it happened. "The echoes. They know we're going to fix it. That's why they're getting closer."

"Or they're getting closer because they're dissolving," Tam said, "and dissolution looks like approach."

Isolde glared at him. But she took his hand, so he understood the glare was ceremonial.

They stopped at midday in the lee of a lighthouse that had never warned anyone home, and it was there — sitting on a dry dock with his boots dangling over a harbor that had forgotten water — that Tam did the thing he had been carrying since the fire, the thing heavier than the accusation, heavier than the confession.

He took out his knife. Not the working blade he used for cutting wick-cord and warding-chalk, but the other one, the one every ward-keeper carried and hoped never to use: the oath-knife, with the guild's sigil worn nearly smooth on the pommel. He'd meant to throw it into the unwritten sea. He'd carried it instead, all this way, unable to decide whether discarding it would be freedom or amputation.

Now he laid it flat on his palm and looked at it, and then at the two of them.

"When a ward-keeper is cast out," he said, "the guild takes back the oath. There's a formula. They cut the knot you tied at initiation and they burn the cord, and the presiding officer says the words: *what was sworn is unswn, what was bound is unbound.*" He turned the knife over. "They did that to me in front of the whole chapter-house. My mentor presided. He looked relieved." A breath. "But here's the thing they don't tell you, the thing I only understood out on the salt road, watching a nine-year-old buy us a highway out of her own head: an oath isn't in the cord. It's not in the knife or the sigil or the guild's permission. An oath is just a promise with your whole self behind it. And nobody — no chapter-house, no archive, no dragon — can take back a promise you're still keeping."

"What are you doing?" Zephyrine asked. Very carefully. As if he were handling something live.

"Re-swearing," Tam said. "Properly, this time. To the right people." He set the knife down on the dock between them, point toward himself, handle outward — the old gesture, the one that meant *my life is on the table, take it if you need it.* "Hear me, then. Both of you. Because I mean this formally, and formality is the only language my order ever taught me that turned out to be worth the learning."

Isolde had gone wide-eyed. Zephyrine had gone very still.

"This is a crew," Tam said. "Not a rescue party, not a penance detail, not three strangers walking in the same direction. A crew. Which means shared rations, shared watches, shared risk, and no member left behind — not in the fray, not in the deep, not in the dark at the end of the world. It means the smallest voice carries equal weight to the loudest, and I include myself in that, and I'll thank you both to hold me to it, because I have spent my whole life mistaking volume for authority." He looked at Isolde. "Crew means you don't spend yourself secretly. If the road asks a price, we see the bill together, and we decide together, and if the answer is no, it's no — even if you're the heir. *Especially* if you're the heir, because heirs get martyred by their own courtesy, and I have read enough history to know how countries mourn children."

"That's not fair," Isolde said, but her voice wobbled, and her hand found his sleeve and gripped it.

"It's completely unfair," Tam agreed. "Fairness is for symmetrical situations. This isn't one." He turned last to Zephyrine, and made himself say the hardest clause. "Crew means your past is cargo, not captain. It rides with us. It doesn't steer. When we reach the Door Out and the loyalist tells us the price — and they'll tell us, the guild always tells you the price, that's the one honesty they've never lost — we will hear it as a crew. We will weigh it as a crew. And if the price is you, Zephyrine Eriksen, then hear this now, in front of witnesses, under open sky, so it stands in the record of whatever records survive: *we will contest it.* We will fight the price. We will look for another way until there is no time left to look, and if there is truly no other way, then we will be there, both of us, holding your hands, so that whatever you give is given to people who love you and not to an abstraction called necessity." His throat closed. He pushed through it. "Nobody on this crew gets erased alone. That's the oath. That's the whole oath. Everything else is commentary."

The grey light lay on the dry harbor. Far off, the temple bell swung its silent note. An echo stood at the edge of the dock — an old man, maybe, or the shape an old man leaves behind — and he had his unfinished hand pressed over where a heart would be, and he was bowing, slowly, the way sailors bow to a flag.

Zephyrine picked up the knife.

Tam watched them turn it over, thumb finding the worn sigil, and saw something settle in their face — not peace, nothing so kind as peace, but resolve, the load-bearing kind, the kind you build a decision on. They held the knife out, handle first, offering it back to him.

"Then take it back," they said. "The oath. From my hand, not the guild's. If it's going to mean anything, let it be given, not inherited."

He took it. Their fingers touched around the pommel, and he felt the faint tremor in them, and he closed his own hands steady around the steel, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because some transactions complete themselves below the level of words.

"Me too," Isolde announced, scrambling up onto her knees. "I swear too. Shared rations, shared watches, no one left behind, and nobody gets erased alone." She screwed up her face with the effort of solemnity. "Also I'm the navigator, because I'm the only one who actually remembers where anything is, so you both have to do what I say about harbors."

"Within reason," Tam said.

"Within harbors," Isolde said.

Zephyrine laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound, the first laugh Tam had heard from them since the stars went out, and it did something to the air, he'd have sworn it did something to the air, because the nearest echo lifted its head like a flower turning toward sun.

"So witnessed," Tam said formally, and drove the oath-knife point-down into the boards of the dry dock, where it stood upright, quivering, a pin holding three lives to one purpose. "Crew of the Restoration. Three souls, one chart. Let the record show."

"Let it show," Isolde echoed.

"Let it show," Zephyrine said, and their voice was steady now, and their eyes, when they lifted them, were fixed not on the knife or the sky but southward, down the spine of the hollow country, toward the distant smudge on the horizon that Tam had been pretending not to see since dawn.

Because he had seen it. He'd been seeing it for hours, and hoping he was wrong, and knowing he wasn't.

Smoke. Or what passed for smoke here — a black column rising off the southern horizon, straight and dense, unwavering in a windless sky. Not the smoke of any fire the hollow country could make. The hollow country's fires burned pale, burnt-paper thin.

This smoke was the color of dragon-fire guttering out.

Ashwing was dying faster than the charts had promised, and the Door Out would seal with the last flame, and the column on the horizon was the clock now, ticking in black.

Tam pulled the knife from the dock, sheathed it, and stood.

"Crew," he said. "We move."
