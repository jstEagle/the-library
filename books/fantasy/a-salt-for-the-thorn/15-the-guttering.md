## 15. The Guttering

It began, as all endings do, with a light going out.

Zephyrine felt it before they saw it — a change in the pressure of the air, the way a sailor feels a storm three days off. They had been walking the salt road's last stretch through the hollow country, Isolde asleep against Tam's back in the sling of his coat, when the faint glow that had lit their way since the border simply *dimmed*. Not flickered. Dimmed, the way a hearth dims when someone lays a wet log across it: slowly, deliberately, dying by degrees.

They stopped. The road stopped with them.

Ahead, Ashwing lay coiled in the ruin of what had once been a temple to the dragon-priests — Zephyrine knew it was a temple because Isolde had told them so, and because the bones of the architecture still remembered being holy. Columns like broken ribs. A floor of white stone gone grey as ash. And at its center, the dragon: vast beyond sense, scales the color of old bronze gone green at the seams, wings folded like a cathedral closing for the last time.

Ashwing's fire was visible through the translucent plates of its chest. A furnace-heart, banked low. When they had first found the dragon, days ago — or hours; time moved strangely here, in the hollowed interior of an erased nation — that heart had burned steady as a lighthouse. Now it guttered. Now it *stuttered*, gaps opening in the flame like missing teeth, and each gap lasted a heartbeat longer than the one before.

"No," Isolde said.

Zephyrine turned. The girl was awake. She slid down from Tam's back and stood very still, staring at the dragon with an expression Zephyrine had learned to dread on her small face: not fear, but recognition. The look of someone watching a thing she had always known would happen finally begin to happen.

"Isolde—"

"It's starting." Her voice was flat. Too calm. "The fire's failing. That's how it works. That's how it *worked*." She corrected herself bitterly. "That's how it was supposed to work. The bargain said the erasure would hold as long as Ashwing burned. Burn slow, erase long. Burn out—" She swallowed. "Burn out, and everything unwritten comes unwritten all the way down."

Tam came up beside her, and Zephyrine saw him do the thing he did when he was frightened: straighten, square his shoulders, become the ward-keeper he no longer had any right to call himself. "How long?" he asked the dragon directly, because Tam Voss had never learned to be afraid of anything except himself.

Ashwing opened one eye.

It was the color of a struck match, that eye, and it took in the three of them — the disgraced keeper, the child of kings, the cartographer with two names and neither of them true — with an ancient, terrible patience.

"Long enough," the dragon said, "if you run."

* * *

They did not run. Not at first. First there were preparations, and preparations are the cruelest form of hope, because they force you to pretend the future is a place you will actually arrive in.

Ashwing raised its head — a labor that took the better part of a minute, muscles shifting under bronze scale like tectonic plates remembering motion — and breathed out. What came from its throat was not flame. It was a map.

Smoke, rather. Smoke that held its shape: a curling ribbon of grey hanging in the dead air of the temple, drawing itself into lines Zephyrine's hands itched to trace. A route. Through the collapsing interior of Cael Adair, down to the salt flats, across the unwritten sea, to the Door Out.

"The guild kept a door," Ashwing rumbled. "Your guild, ward-keeper. The Kept Doors. They built it into the world's spine, where even an erasure cannot reach without breaking something fundamental. It is the only way out of this country for those who were never meant to exist in it." The match-flare eye fixed on Tam. "And it seals."

"When?" Tam asked.

"When I die."

The word fell into the temple like a stone into a well. Zephyrine heard it land at the bottom of everything.

"You could tell us more," Isolde said suddenly, fiercely, stepping forward. "You're a *dragon*. You know things. You know about the Door, you know about the guild, you know about—" her voice cracked and she hated it, Zephyrine could hear her hating it, "—about my family. About Cael Adair. You can't just *die*, you have to *tell* us first—"

"Little queen." Ashwing's voice softened into something like tenderness, which was somehow worse than the accusation had been. "I have been telling your country's story for four hundred years to no one at all. Ask me anything while we walk. I will spend my last breaths as a teacher, if that is what you need." The great head swung toward Zephyrine. "But understand what is coming. When the fire fails entirely, the hollow country does not fade politely. It collapses. Inward. Everything the erasure left half-made — the echoes, the lingering ones, the almost-people — they will feel themselves going, and they will *panic*. And panic, in a place made of memory, is a weather."

"A weather," Zephyrine repeated.

"You will see."

* * *

They saw.

They had been walking for perhaps an hour along the smoke-map's route — Ashwing moving above them in slow, agonized flight, a bruise-colored shape against a sky that had never decided on a color — when the first scream came.

It rose out of the ground itself. Out of the white nothing between the remembered things. Zephyrine spun toward it and saw, at the edge of vision, a figure: a woman standing in a doorway that led nowhere, a doorway that existed only because the woman remembered standing in doorways. She was translucent at the edges, like paper held to a candle, and she was looking at her own hands with an expression of pure animal incomprehension as they thinned, and thinned—

The scream was not loud. That was the horror of it. It was quiet, domestic, the scream of someone calling for help inside a house where no one else has ever lived. Then the doorway went, then the woman went, and the space where she had been rushed inward like water filling a footprint.

"Keep moving," Ashwing said overhead, and its voice was strained now, fraying at the bottom edges. "Do not watch them go. It draws them to you — the fading ones seek anyone who still *holds*, anyone solid, and you are holding them here longer than mercy allows."

"How do you know that?" Isolde demanded, marching along with her fists balled, tears cutting clean tracks down her dusty face. "How do you know what mercy allows?"

"Because I have been the only solid thing in this country for four hundred years," the dragon said. "Everything that lingered came to me first. Every echo, every almost-person, every erased citizen who could not finish leaving. They gathered at my fire because I was warm. And I could not save a single one." A pause, filled only by the wind screaming through unmapped space somewhere behind them. "I am the last warm thing in Cael Adair, little queen. When I go cold, there will be nothing left to gather around. So we run."

And run they did.

* * *

The collapse accelerated the way rot accelerates: imperceptibly, then all at once.

By the second hour the horizon itself was unsteady. Zephyrine watched mountains — mountains Isolde named as they passed, the Grey Sisters, the Crown of Adair, each name costing the girl something Zephyrine refused to calculate — soften at their peaks like wax, like sugar in tea, like anything sweet given too long to the dark. Whole valleys folded shut. A river they crossed ran backwards for thirty paces, confused, trying to remember its own course, and then simply stopped existing mid-current, leaving them to pick their way across a bed of stones that had been underwater since before the erasure and now didn't know what they were.

The echoes came more frequently now. They emerged from the dissolving landscape in twos and threes, drawn to the party's solidity as Ashwing had warned, and they were not frightening, which was the worst part. They were *pleading*. An old man in the robes of a dragon-priest reached for Isolde with hands like smoke and said, in a voice worn thin as communion cloth, "*the line, the line must continue*" — and Isolde stopped dead and said, "Grandfather?" in a voice that broke Zephyrine's heart in a new place, a place they hadn't known was load-bearing.

An echo of a harbor-keeper pressed a rope into Tam's hands — a rope that dissolved before it finished touching him — and begged him to "mind the moorings, mind the moorings," over and over, walking backward into the white.

And one echo — a young man with ink-stained fingers and a face that made Zephyrine's chest seize with a grief they could not source — looked directly at Zephyrine and said, clearly, calmly, terribly:

"*You drew us out.*"

Then he was gone, and Zephyrine walked on with the words lodged in them like a splint of bone.

"We don't have time for this," Tam said quietly, at Zephyrine's shoulder. He wasn't being cruel. He was being kind, in the only way the situation allowed. "Whatever he was to you — whoever he was — we can't carry it right now. Later. Carry it later."

"There might not be a later," Zephyrine said.

"There might," Tam said. "If we move."

* * *

They reached the salt flats at dusk, or at what the hollow country offered as dusk: a dimming without a sun, a greying of the grey. Behind them, the interior of Cael Adair was visibly failing now — the mountains folding down like hands closing, the sky developing holes through which could be seen not stars but the *absence* of stars, black sockets in the firmament. Ahead lay the unwritten sea, flat and lightless and vast, and somewhere beyond it, the Door Out.

Ashwing landed badly.

There was no other word for it. The dragon came down in a long, skidding sprawl of bronze and dust, and when it lifted its head, Zephyrine saw that the fire in its chest had gone from a furnace to a handful of embers — bright spots winking in and out, fewer every minute, like a city losing power street by street.

"How far?" Tam asked. No preamble. Ward-keepers, Zephyrine was learning, saved their breath for essentials.

"To the Door? A day's sail, if the sea permits. To the end of my fire?" Ashwing's laugh was a sound like a landslide remembering gravity. "Less."

Isolde planted herself in front of the dragon's muzzle. She was so small against it. She was so *unreasonably* small, and so unreasonably fierce, and Zephyrine thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this is what a country looks like when it refuses to die.*

"Then we take you with us," she said. "Can you fly us? Can you swim? Can you—"

"I cannot cross the unwritten sea, little queen. Nothing written can. The sea unmakes what touches it — that is its purpose, that is why the guild let it stand. I am the most written thing left in this country. I would dissolve before the first mile." The ember-eye gentled. "This is where I leave you. This is where I have always been leaving you. I chained myself to this erasure so that it would burn *slowly* — so that someday, someone might find the way back. Four hundred years I have been a fuse, burning at the pace of mercy. My only regret is that the fuse is shorter than I promised myself it would be."

"Then give us more time," Isolde whispered. "You're a dragon. Dragons give gifts. Give us time."

Ashwing regarded her for a long moment. Then it lowered its great head until its brow nearly touched the girl's, and exhaled — and what came out was not smoke, not flame, but a single scale. Bronze-green, broad as a shield, warm as a living thing.

"A gift, then. When the fire dies and the collapse comes for you, press this to your heart and speak the name of your house. It will hold you together for a little while. Not forever. A little while." The eye swiveled to Zephyrine and Tam. "For the others — I have nothing. Only advice. The sea will ask you what you are made of. Answer it with something true. Lies sink."

"That's it?" Tam said. "Four hundred years of wisdom, and that's it? *Lies sink?*"

"The best advice always sounds obvious afterward, ward-keeper." Ashwing settled its chin onto the salt, and the ground shuddered. Far behind them, something enormous — a city, maybe, or the memory of a city — came down with a sound like a held breath released after centuries. "Now go. Take the boat at the tideline. The echoes keep it. They will let you pass, if you tell them the little queen sent you."

"And you?" Isolde's voice was very small now. All the fierceness spent. "What do we tell you? When it's done? If we make it?"

Ashwing closed its eyes.

"Tell me the stars came back," it said. "That's all. Tell me the stars came back, and that someone remembered my name."

* * *

The boat was realer than anything they'd found in the hollow country — real wood, real tar-smell, real rope — because the echoes who tended it had poured everything they had left into keeping it so. They stood along the tideline in a silent row, dozens of them, fishermen and sailors and harbor-wives, translucent and flickering, and as the three travelers boarded, they each touched the hull once, like a blessing, like a farewell.

Isolde told them she'd sent them. The echoes bowed.

Zephyrine took the tiller because their hands knew boats, though they could not remember learning, and Tam raised the sail because his hands knew knots, though the guild had stripped him of everything else, and Isolde stood at the prow with a dragon's scale clutched to her chest, watching the shore where the last warm thing in Cael Adair lay breathing in slower and slower intervals.

Behind them, the hollow country groaned.

Ahead, the unwritten sea stretched black and patient to the horizon, waiting to ask them what they were made of.

And above it all — Zephyrine looked up despite themselves, despite knowing — the sky was empty. No stars. Only the black sockets where stars should be, and one by one, as they watched, the distant glow of Ashwing's dying fire reflected off the underside of nothing, dimming, stuttering, guttering toward dark.

"Row," Tam said hoarsely. "Row like the world ends at dawn. Because it does."

The boat leapt forward into the dark, and the race began.
