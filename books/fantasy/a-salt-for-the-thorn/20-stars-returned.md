## 20. Stars, Returned

The first thing Cael Adair remembered was salt.

Not the salt of the road — that white, memory-paved ribbon that had carried them across the unwritten country — but the salt of the sea, old and patient, thrown up in a long grey breath against the harbor walls of Vael Adair. Isolde felt it land on her face before she understood that her face was still there to be landed on. She was standing on a quay that had not existed an hour ago, and the stone beneath her feet was warm, as if the city had only just finished dreaming it and the dream was still cooling into fact.

Above her, the stars came back.

They did not return all at once, the way the wards had fallen — no, the stars returned the way a held breath is released: in a long, shuddering, whole-body exhalation. One star, then ten, then a sky's worth, pouring back into the dark like water finding the shape of a cup. Isolde had lived her whole remembered life under a sky with holes in it, though she hadn't known they were holes. Now the holes were full, and the fullness was so complete it made a sound — a low, ringing hum, like a struck bell that never quite finished tolling — and she put her hands over her ears and wept, because she was nine years old, or perhaps ten, and the sky of her grandmothers' grandmothers had come home, and the woman who had bought it back with her own name was not there to see it.

"Isolde." Tam's voice, rough as rope. He was beside her on the quay, and he was crying too, though he would deny it later with the particular stubbornness of a man who had once been exiled from the only family he had. His hand hovered near her shoulder — he never quite knew what to do with comfort, her ward-keeper, her grumpy, loyal, half-mended Tam — and then settled, heavy and warm. "Look at the harbor."

She looked.

Vael Adair was rebuilding itself out of nothing, and the nothing was generous. Ships were rising from the water like leviathans deciding to be wooden. Ropes coiled themselves. Gulls — *gulls*, real gulls, with greedy yellow eyes and opinions — wheeled overhead, screaming the harbor back into being. Lantern-light bloomed along the terraced streets climbing the hill, and behind it, faint as breath on glass, the shapes of people: the echoes, the half-remembered ones who had lingered in the hollow country, now filling in with color and weight and the smell of bread. A woman at a fish stall turned and looked directly at Isolde, and her face was the face of every erased citizen Isolde had ever walked past in the grey interior, and she mouthed something — *thank you*, or perhaps *you're real*, or perhaps just the girl's name, spoken in a language that had been unwritten and was now, syllable by syllable, being written again.

Isolde was the last daughter of the royal line of Cael Adair. She had confessed it in the dark, when her memories were nearly spent, when she had traded them one by one to pave the salt road. She had thought, then, that she would die empty — a cup with nothing left, a name with no one left to say it. She had not imagined this. She had not imagined standing heir to a country that was remembering itself, feeling every restored memory settle into her like rain into drought-cracked earth. The harbors came back to her. The dragon-priests in their salt-white robes, their throats ink-stained from singing the maps. Her mother's hands. Her mother's *laugh*. Things she had sold on the road came flooding home with interest, because a country restored does not forget what its children paid.

And still. Still.

"Zephyrine," Isolde said, and the name cracked in the middle, the way a bell cracks, the way a heart does.

Tam's hand tightened on her shoulder. He didn't say *she's gone*. He didn't say *she faded mid-stroke, we all saw it, the ink took her*. He didn't say any of the true things, because Tam had learned, somewhere in the hollow country or on the unwritten sea or in the long grey years of his exile, that there are moments when the true things are a cruelty, and this — the first hour of a returned world, with the stars still ringing — was one of them.

Instead he said: "The dragon's waking."

* * *

Ashwing lay in the great salt-square at the heart of Vael Adair, where the dragon-priests had once sung their maps into being, and the last of the old fire was going out of her the way evening goes out of a western window — slowly, beautifully, irreversibly.

Isolde had never seen a dragon before the erasure. She had seen Ashwing chained to the erasure itself, guttering, ancient, furious, and she had seen Ashwing arrive at the Door Out in the final hour with her last flame cupped in her jaws like an offering, like an apology, like a gift. *Take my fire for the ink*, the dragon had said to Zephyrine. *I was the first to be spent. Let me be the last to be given.*

Now the giving was done, and Ashwing lay in the square with her scales gone the color of old ash, and the people of Vael Adair — the echoes made flesh, the returned, the remembering — had come out of their rebuilding houses to stand in a silent ring around her, because you do not let a dragon die unattended. Not in Cael Adair. Not ever.

Isolde walked to the great head and put her small hand against the vast, cooling cheek.

"You knew her," she said. "Before. You called her another name."

Ashwing's eye opened. It was the color of a coal that has stopped being fire and started being memory — deep orange going to grey at the rim. "I knew her," the dragon said, and her voice was wind through a canyon, vast and tired and gentle in a way that seemed to surprise even her. "I knew the cartographer. I do not know what Zephyrine became. Those are not the same thing, little heir, though the world will try to tell you they are."

"She erased you. She erased everything. She drew the map and we all went into the ink."

"She saved a valley she believed was empty," Ashwing said. "She believed it because she was made to believe it. And then the ink took her too, and made her small, and gave her a life with a border she never thought to cross, and she lived that life so *carefully*, little heir. I watched her through the wards for twenty years. I watched her map every road that led nowhere. Do you know what that is? That is a soul trying to draw its way out of a lie without knowing it's in one." The great eye closed, opened again. "I do not forgive her. Forgiveness is for the living to spend on each other. But I *witness* her. And in Cael Adair, witness is the oldest sacrament."

The fire in Ashwing's throat flickered — one last blue tongue of it, no bigger than a candle — and then settled into embers, and the embers settled into a glow, and the glow into a warmth, and Isolde understood with a grief that had no floor beneath it that the dragon was not dying tonight. The dragon was dying the way a country dies: slowly, over years, with witnesses. There would be time. There would be a few years of time, and the dragon would spend them teaching, and remembering aloud, and telling the dragon-priests — who were even now re-inking their throats in the temple above the square — everything that had been unwritten.

"You'll help me," Isolde said. It was not a question. Heirs of Cael Adair did not ask their elders for help; they informed them of the shape of the future.

Ashwing made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be the size of a mountain. "I will help you," she agreed. "Now go and do the thing you came here to do. I can smell the ink on you. You've been carrying the founding map for an hour and pretending you haven't."

* * *

The founding map was not a large thing.

Isolde had expected — had *wanted* — something grand. A table the length of a ship. Vellum from the last white calf of some sacred herd. Inks ground with pearl and saint's blood. That was how the old stories told it: the founding maps of Cael Adair were cathedral-things, drawn by nine cartographers over nine years, blessed by nine dragons.

But the map that had restored the country — Zephyrine's map, the Restoration Map, signed in salt and lit by a dying dragon's fire — had been drawn on the back of an old ward-chart, in ink that was mostly grief, by one tired hand in one terrible night. And so the founding map of the new Cael Adair, the one Isolde now unrolled on the long table in the map-hall above the harbor, was a modest thing too: good paper, honest ink, and room at the bottom for names.

The room for names was the whole point.

Around the table stood the people who had crossed the fray and the salt road and the unwritten sea. Tam, arms crossed, jaw set, wearing the grey-and-silver of the ward-keepers again — not the guild that had betrayed him, but a new order he was already, grudgingly, calling the Guild of Open Doors, because Tam Voss had never once in his life let a good grudge go to waste. The loyalist from the Door, who had dissolved with the erasure and been remade with it, and who stood at the edge of the lamplight looking like a man who had been forgiven something enormous and did not know how to hold it. The echo-woman from the fish stall, whose name was Maren and who had been Maren for three hundred years and one hour. Others. A growing crowd. A country's worth of shoulders, all leaning toward the table.

At the bottom of the map, beneath the coastline and the harbors and the valley of Vaelmoor — which was there, truly there, its wards become bridges, its borders become roads, its stars overhead — was a line of blank space, and above the blank space, in the old ceremonial script that Isolde's hands remembered even though her sold memories had been returned only in fragments, the words:

*Drawn closed by the hand of:*

And beneath it, a second line:

*Kept in memory by the people of:*

The first line, Isolde had already filled. She had written *Zephyrine Eriksen* in the old script, and the ink had taken it the way the sea takes a dropped coin — completely, with a small bright flash and then a settling. When she had finished, the salt road across the valley had glowed white for a full minute, visible from the harbor, and no one had spoken.

The second line was for the country. That would take years. That was meant to take years. Every citizen would come to the map-hall and add their name, one by one, generation after generation, so that the founding of Cael Adair would never be a finished thing, never a closed book, never again a country that could be unwritten because everyone had agreed to stop remembering it.

But there was a third line. Isolde had asked the dragon-priests about it, and they had gone pale, and then they had gone to their oldest books, and then they had come back and said: *there is a third line, little heir, and it has not been used since the first founding, and it is for the one the country itself must never forget.*

*And the land remembers the name of:*

Isolde dipped the pen.

Her hand shook. She was nine, or ten; she had spent nearly every memory she had paving a road for a woman who turned out to be the architect of her country's erasure, and who had then unmade herself to give it back, and Isolde had loved her — that was the thing no one said plainly, so Isolde said it plainly to herself now, over the ink, in the lamplight: *I loved her. I loved her on the salt road when I still thought she was only a lost cartographer with kind hands and a sad map-room, and I loved her after, when she was a monster with my country's blood in her inkwell, and I loved her at the Door when she chose the ink over herself. Love is not a reward for goodness. Love is a road. You pave it and you walk it and you do not ask it to be anything but what it is.*

She wrote:

*And the land remembers the name of Zephyrine Eriksen, who was not born of us and died for us, who drew our wound and then drew it closed, who is salt in the founding and star in the returning.*

The ink took the words. And then the ink did something ink should not do.

It *sang*.

A low note, the same note the returning stars had made, rising from the paper and out through the map-hall's open windows and down into the streets of Vael Adair, and every bell in every restored tower caught it and rang it back, and in the great square the embers of Ashwing flared once, briefly, like a heart remembering its work, and out in the valley — Isolde knew this without seeing it, knew it the way you know a wound is closing, by the easing — the salt road blazed white from border to border, every stone of it, every memory Isolde had spent, all of it lit at once like a name written in fire across the floor of the world.

The people in the map-hall wept. Tam did not pretend otherwise this time. He stood with his fists on the table and his head bowed and his shoulders shaking, the disgraced ward-keeper who had kept faith when faith was the only thing left to keep, and Isolde reached up and took his hand and he let her.

"She's in the land now," Isolde said. Her voice was small and did not sound like an heir's voice. It sounded like a child's, and she let it. "The land remembers her. The priests said — they said the land's remembering is the deepest one. Deeper than paper. Deeper than graves. If the land remembers, then she can't be gone-gone. She's just — somewhere the maps don't reach yet."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. She watched him decide something. She was learning to watch for it — the small settling of his jaw, the way his eyes went to the middle distance, the way a man who had spent his life maintaining wards looked when he felt, faintly, at the far edge of his craft, a ward that was not a ward. A door that was not a door.

"Isolde," he said slowly. "When the Restoration Map unwound — when everything erased came back — did anything come back that was never erased in the first place?"

She frowned. "What do you mean?"

"The valley made Zephyrine a whole life. Twenty years of it. A map-room. A house. A history. That life was a lie, but it was a *detailed* lie, and the erasure was thorough, and when the ink unwound—" His hand made a slow, uncertain gesture, the gesture of a man reaching for a shape in fog. "Everything that was unwritten got rewritten. But Zephyrine wasn't unwritten, Isolde. Zephyrine was *spent*. Poured into the ink. And ink that's been poured—" He stopped. Started again. "The priests say the land remembers her name. I felt something, just now, when the bells rang. Out past the valley's western border. On the salt road. A — a warmth. A *presence*. Like a ward waking. Like someone standing up."

The map-hall had gone very quiet. The lamplight leaned in.

Isolde looked down at the founding map, at the line she had just written, at the ink that was still, faintly, impossibly, singing — and far to the west, past the harbor, past the terraced hills, past the border that was no longer a border, she felt it too: not a memory, not an echo, not a ghost. A warmth. A small, new, unformed warmth, like a candle flame deciding whether to be a fire.

Like someone waking on the salt road with empty hands and no past at all.

Isolde closed her fist around the pen until her knuckles went white, and when she spoke, her voice was the heir's voice, and it was the child's voice, and they were, for the first time, the same voice.

"Tam," she said. "Saddle the horses. We're going back to the valley."
