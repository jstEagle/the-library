## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charm stall at the end of Fishgut Row sold three things: luck knots that worked about half the time, weather-glass pendants that worked never, and salt-bread from Brannoc's kitchen, which worked every time because it was just bread.

Ada Reyes had been selling all three for six years, and in that time had learned two great truths. The first was that people in outlawed Greyharbor would pay good coin to feel lucky, even if they knew better. The second was that a storm could be smelled coming an hour before it arrived — and this one smelled wrong.

It was wrong the way a chord is wrong. Ada had spent twelve years not listening to the weather, the way a reformed drunkard does not listen to the tavern door, and still the sky's dissonance crawled under their skin like ants. They tied off another luck knot with hands that were almost steady and told themselves: *You are a shopkeeper. You are nobody. The sky can do what it likes.*

"You've gone gray," said Old Pell, buying his weekly knot. "Storm coming?"

"Always is," Ada said. "That'll be two coppers."

He paid, squinted up at the sky, and said, "Funny night for it. Stars are out thick as herring."

They were. Ada did not look. Looking at stars had been forbidden to them by their own decree, back when decreeing things had still been within their power, and old habits kept a person alive.

The tide came in at dusk, which was normal. Then it kept coming, which was not.

Ada noticed first as water lipping over the cobblestones where it had no business being — the Row flooded only in true gales, and there was no wind at all. The sea lay flat as poured pewter and rose anyway, silent, obedient to nothing. Around them, Greyharbor began to notice too. A fishwife stood ankle-deep holding her baskets and staring south with her mouth open. Somewhere a dog began to bark and did not stop.

And then the wards went.

There was no sound a hearing ear could catch, but Ada caught it anyway, deep in the sternum — a snap like the world's largest harp string parting, followed by a silence so total it rang. Twelve years of pretending to be ordinary shattered in an instant, because there was no pretending through *that*. Every resonant within fifty miles felt the border wards die. Ada doubled over the stall, gripping its edge, tasting copper and lightning.

"No," they whispered. "No, no, I didn't — I haven't *called* anything—"

Because that was the terror of it. The last time the wards had shuddered, they'd blamed Ada. Fairly, mostly. But this — this was not them. This was something breaking the wards from the other side, or the top, or wherever one broke such things, and when the wards died, the covenant died with them, and when the covenant died—

The first star went out.

Not clouded. Not set. *Extinguished*, like a pinprick candle snuffed between wet fingers. Then its neighbor. Then a whole constellation, north to south, in a slow sweep that made the crowd on Fishgut Row make a sound Ada had never heard a crowd make before — a low, collective moan, the sound of a hundred people losing the same certainty at once.

"The Veil," someone said. "The stars were the Veil's anchors. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows stories," said someone else, without conviction.

The third truth Ada learned that night, standing in rising black water under a sky going dark hole by hole, was that you could spend twelve years building a small life and watch the sea take it in under an hour.

* * *

By full dark, half the stars were gone and the water stood knee-deep in the Row, and the wardens came.

They came in boats, which told Ada everything. Wardens did not boat through Greyharbor streets unless they expected to be sweeping houses floor by floor. Their lanterns bobbed down the drowned lane, and their voices carried flat and official across the water:

"—by order of the Coastward Compact, all residents will remain indoors. Resonants must present themselves for protective custody. Harbor-wide sweep begins now. Doors will be opened."

*Protective custody.* Ada had heard that phrase before, twelve years ago, in a courtroom with good acoustics. It had meant chains and a ship and a sister who wouldn't look at them.

They abandoned the stall — the luck knots, the useless glass, six years of small safe commerce — and waded for home with their heart hammering out a rhythm they refused to name. Storm-blood did not like fear. Fear fed it. They counted doorways, recited bread prices, did everything but feel, while behind them a warden's lantern flared against a doorway and a voice cried out and was silenced.

Their lodgings sat above Brannoc's salt-house, up a stair that had survived three fires and one divorce. Ada took the steps two at a time, burst through the door, and found Brannoc already waiting with a lamp, a loaded crossbow, and the expression of a woman whose evening plans had been ruined.

"Knew it," Brannoc said. "Knew it the day you moved in. Nobody sells charms that bad unless they're hiding real ones."

"I don't have time to be insulted—"

"Sit down, you soaked idiot, and bar the door."

Ada barred the door. Then, because terror makes honest creatures of us all, they turned and said, "How long have you known?"

Brannoc set the crossbow on the table, casual as laying out cutlery. "Since your second week. You hum when you think no one's listening, girl — boy — *you*, and when you hum, my kettle boils faster and my rheumatism quits complaining. I ran cargo past the border wards for twenty years. I know resonance when it's living under my roof eating my bread." She poured two cups of something amber and pushed one across. "Question was never *what* you are. Question was whether you'd bring trouble. And look at that. Trouble."

"They'll search house to house," Ada said. "If they find me—"

"If they find you, they find a woman named Ada Reyes, charm-seller, tenant, terrible at cards, who has paid her rent on time for six years." Brannoc's eyes, in the lamplight, were the flat grey of open water. "I hid priests during the Purging. I hid a duke's mistress in a barrel of pickled eels for nine days. I can hide one storm-touched charmer."

"Why?" Ada's voice cracked on it. Twelve years since anyone had chosen them for anything but their blood. "You could hand me over. There'd be a bounty. There's always a bounty."

"There is." Brannoc drank. "And there's a thing wearing a drowned sailor's face walking up Rope Walk right now, if my eyes are any good, and the wardens' boats are sitting awful low in the water for empty hulls." She nodded toward the window, and Ada looked despite themselves.

Out on the flooded street, beyond the warden-lanterns, something moved that was not a person and wore the face of a person the way a glove wears a hand. The sea had come up the street carrying its dead, or its dead's shapes, and the wardens hadn't seen it yet.

"The covenant's broken," Brannoc said quietly. "Whatever held the Veyl back is broken, and everything the old priests ever warned about is climbing out of the deep to collect. So no, I'm not selling you for a bounty. I'm asking what you know about fixing it — because I'd wager my whole rotten life that whatever broke those wards, a storm-born like you can feel the seam of it. Am I wrong?"

Ada wanted to say yes. Wanted to say *I'm just a shopkeeper*, wanted the small life back, the bread and the knots and the harmless gray days. Instead they closed their eyes and listened — really listened, for the first time in twelve years — and felt it plain as a scar: the torn edge of the Veil, far out past the harbor mouth, down where the drowned altar slept. Felt it the way you feel a missing tooth.

"You're not wrong," Ada said. "But Brannoc — if I go looking for that seam, the wardens won't be the worst thing hunting me. And neither will the Veyl." They hesitated, then said the rest, the thing they hadn't said aloud in twelve years. "My sister testified against me. She'll have felt the wards break too. And Marisol always did finish what she started."

Brannoc refilled both cups and grinned like a woman boarding a prize ship.

"Well then," she said. "Best get the boat out before the tide takes the stairs."
