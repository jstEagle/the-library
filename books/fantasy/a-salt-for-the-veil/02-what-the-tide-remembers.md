## 2. What the Tide Remembers

**Ada**

The boat was called the *Second Chance*, which Brannoc said was ironic, because she'd won it in a card game and renamed it from a much worse original title she refused to repeat.

"She called it the *Penitent Widow*," Sefa said, coiling rope with the brisk economy of someone who had spent thirty years arguing with weather. "Brannoc cried when she lost it. Then she cried again when she won it back, because by then she'd gotten attached to crying."

"I did not cry," Brannoc said from the tiller. "I have salt-water eyes. Occupational hazard."

Ada sat amidships with their hood up, watching Greyharbor shrink behind them into a smear of lamplight and chimney smoke. Three days since the stars had gone out. Three days since the wards had shattered like a dropped plate, and the tide had come up the streets to ankle-height, polite as a cat, as if testing whether anyone would object.

Father Anselmo—defrocked, self-described twice, once proudly—sat at the prow with a lead box on his knees. Inside it, he claimed, was the covenant's last true rite, written on vellum that had been underwater for four hundred years and had somehow stayed dry, which Ada felt was theologically suspicious but had decided not to press.

"You're staring at my box," Anselmo said without turning around.

"I'm staring at the sea," Ada said.

"The sea is behind you."

"Then I'm staring at your box."

Anselmo smiled, which made his weathered face look briefly younger and briefly more tired at the same time, a trick Ada hadn't known faces could do. "Good. Stare at it. Everything worth saving starts with someone staring at something they don't understand."

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa Ilo had kept the Gullwatch lamp lit for twenty-nine years, through two wars she'd been too stubborn to notice and one marriage too gentle to last. In all that time, she had never once seen what lived beneath the water.

Now, through the lamp's glass, she saw everything.

It had started the night the stars went out. She'd climbed to the gallery out of habit, struck the flame, and looked out—and the sea had *opened*, the way a book opens. Below the waves, far down where no light should reach, there was a city. Towers of pale coral grown into shapes like hands. Streets lit by fish that moved like drifting lanterns. And figures walking those streets, tall and slow, with faces like drowned sailors, except the faces were wrong in the way a memory is wrong—almost right, almost beloved, and hollow behind the eyes.

She hadn't told anyone for two days. Then Ada Reyes had come to her door with Brannoc at their shoulder, soaked to the knees, saying *the altar, we need to reach the altar,* and Sefa had looked at this exile who called storms without meaning to and thought: *well. Someone has to see where we're going.*

So now she kept the lamp burning in its cradle on the deck of the *Second Chance*, wrapped in oilcloth against spray, because the glass showed the way. The drowned city lay ahead of them, somewhere past the Teeth, and the lamp-glass turned toward it like a needle finding north.

"Left," she called. "A little left. There's a reef that wasn't there yesterday."

"There are reefs that weren't there yesterday," Brannoc muttered, but she turned the tiller.

Sefa watched the deep city slide beneath them. One of the tall figures had stopped walking. It stood in a street of white coral, face tilted up, and even through fathoms of water Sefa felt certain it was looking back at her.

Its mouth moved. No sound crossed the surface. But she read the shape of it anyway, the way you read lips across a crowded room:

*Turn around.*

* * *

**Ada**

They told stories to keep the fear small. That was Brannoc's rule, invented on the spot, enforced ruthlessly.

"Reyes. Story. Go."

"It's your rule. You tell one."

"My rule means I *collect* stories, not that I supply them. Anselmo?"

The priest was mending a net badly. He sighed with the patience of a man who had been defrocked and had therefore lost nothing but his patience's audience. "When I was young, I believed the Veil was a wall. Something between us and the sea-realm, keeping each side politely ignorant of the other. It took me forty years to learn it isn't a wall at all." He tugged a knot loose and frowned at it. "It's a promise. A promise is just a wall you make out of words, and words wear thin."

"What did the promise say?" Ada asked.

"*I will not drown you if you will not burn me.*" He said it simply, without ceremony, and somehow that made it worse. "Sworn at an altar that now sits under sixty feet of cold water, by a king who is probably dead and a queen who definitely is. Every generation, wardens renewed it. Until someone stopped renewing."

Ada pulled their cloak tighter. "Who would want it broken? The Veyl breaking it makes sense—they get the coast. But us?"

Anselmo set down the net. He looked at Ada for a long moment, and Ada recognized the look; it was the look people got right before they said the thing they'd been carrying.

"The warden records were sealed twelve years ago," he said. "But I was still ordained then. I read them. Your trial, Reyes—the storm you called, the deaths they pinned on it—" He paused. "There was no storm. Not a natural one, not one of yours. The wardens *made* it. Bound it to your blood so it would look like yours. They needed a resonant to blame, because a covenant breaks easier if the person who might renew it is exiled first."

The boat creaked. Somewhere below, the water slapped the hull in a rhythm almost like speech.

"They took my whole life," Ada said quietly, "to clear a path."

"They took twelve years," Brannoc said. Her voice was rough, but her hand on the tiller was steady. "We're taking them back. All of them. Starting with the part where you stop apologizing for existing. That's an order. I'm the captain."

"You won the ship in a card game."

"Which makes me captain by divine right."

And despite everything—the drowned faces in the water, the wardens' patrol-lamps sweeping the horizon astern, the hole in the world where the stars used to be—Ada laughed. It came out cracked and rusted, a laugh that hadn't been oiled in twelve years, but it was theirs, and Brannoc grinned like she'd just won another card game.

* * *

**Sefa**

The wardens found them at dusk, and the things wearing drowned faces found them an hour later.

The patrol sloop came first, three lamps burning green—warden-lights, covenant-light, light meant to say *we own the dark*. Sefa doused every lamp aboard except the little one in its oilcloth cradle, and Brannoc killed the sails and let them drift, quiet as driftwood.

Through the glass, Sefa watched the deep. The tall figures had risen from their city. They swam upward through the black in loose formation, unhurried, patient, faces tilted toward the hull. Toward her. One of them raised a hand—not threatening, she realized. *Inviting.* Like a host gesturing toward a door.

"They're not attacking," she whispered.

"Yet," said Brannoc.

"No. Look." Sefa held the glass out. Through it, the nearest figure resolved: a woman's face, bloated and silver-eyed, but wearing an expression Sefa knew from mirrors—from funerals—from long nights at the Gullwatch when the sea took someone and the village needed telling. It was an expression of warning.

The green lamps swept closer. On the sloop's deck, a figure in warden-grey raised something—a horn, or a focus, or worse.

Then the sea stood up.

Not a wave. The *sea*, a column of it, rising silent between the sloop and the *Second Chance*, and for one impossible heartbeat Sefa saw through it into the drowned city, blazing now with light, every tower lit, every drowned face turned upward. The column held for three heartbeats. Four.

Then it fell, and the sloop was gone—pushed a mile off course, mast stepped, crew scrambling, alive. Warned off. By the drowned. For them.

In the ringing silence after, Anselmo clutched his lead box and said, very softly, "The Veyl remember the promise. Even broken, they remember. They're not hunting us."

He looked at Ada.

"They're escorting you."

* * *

**Ada**

They sailed through the night with the dead for guides.

Ada stood at the bow while Sefa held the lamp, and beneath them the pale streets unspooled, turning always toward the place the drowned wanted them to go. Twelve years of running, and it ended here, on a smuggler's stolen boat, escorted by ghosts, toward an altar at the bottom of the sea.

"Why me?" Ada asked the water. No one answered, but Anselmo came to stand beside them, and Brannoc at the tiller, and Sefa with the lamp, and none of them left, and that was its own kind of answer.

Then the lamp-glass flared white, and Sefa said, in a voice gone strange:

"Ada. You need to see who's waiting at the altar."

Ada leaned over the rail, into the glass, into the deep—

And saw a figure standing on the drowned altar-stone, hair unbound, arms bare against the cold, wearing the grey of the Veyl court.

A figure Ada would know anywhere. A figure they'd spent twelve years trying to forget.

"Marisol," Ada breathed, and the sea went silent around the name.
