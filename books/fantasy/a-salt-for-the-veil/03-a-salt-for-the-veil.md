## 3. A Salt for the Veil

The drowned altar was not drowned anymore.

That was the first thing Ada noticed, standing on the deck of Brannoc's salt-barge as it glided over what the charts swore was forty feet of open water. Below them, visible through the impossible clarity of the sea, lay a ring of white stone, its carvings worn smooth by centuries of tide, and rising from its center—unbroken, unbothered, dry as bone—a single pillar with steps spiraling up its side into the air above.

"It's a stair," Sefa said, her voice hushed in a way Ada had only heard once before, when she'd spoken of her lamp. "It goes *up*."

"Everything about this place is upside down," Brannoc muttered, hands steady on the tiller. "Which suits me fine. I've never trusted a sea that behaved."

Father Emeric—defrocked, disgraced, and currently clutching his book of rites like a man holding a rope over a long drop—stood at the bow and read the water the way other men read scripture. "The altar surfaces for those who come to swear," he said. "Or to pay." He paused. "I should have said this sooner. I've been trying to find a kinder way to say it for three days, and there isn't one."

Ada's stomach dropped before he even spoke. Twelve years of exile had taught them the shape of bad news—the way it arrived dressed politely, apologetic, already halfway through the door.

"The covenant was sworn in blood freely given," Emeric said. "Not taken. Not spilled. Given. The veil between Veyl and our coast is woven from a willing death. That is the price. That has always been the price."

The silence that followed had weight. Brannoc stopped the barge. Somewhere below them, fish scattered like startled thoughts.

"No," Sefa said flatly. "No, absolutely not. We didn't sail all this way so you could—" She turned on Ada, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "Tell him he's wrong. Tell him there's another way."

"There isn't," Ada said quietly. And the terrible thing, the thing they would carry afterward like a stone under the tongue, was how much of them felt *relief*. Not at dying—at finally being told, plainly, what everything had cost all along. The storm they'd never meant to call. The sister who'd testified against them. The twelve years of salt-sellers' back rooms and flinching from wardens. It had all been leading here, hadn't it? Debts wanted settling. The sea kept excellent books.

"Well," said Brannoc, cracking her knuckles one by one, "I've out-argued customs men in four kingdoms, so I'll be having a go at the theology after we're done panicking. For now—" she nodded past Ada's shoulder, "—we've got company."

* * *

She came up out of the water without a splash, which was somehow worse than if she'd come in a warship.

Marisol Reyes stood on the white stone of the altar in robes the color of deep water, and she looked older than twelve years should have made her, and younger too, all at once. Her hair was braided in the Veyl fashion now, threaded with pearl. When her eyes found Ada across the water, she did not look away, and neither did Ada, and between them stretched every unsaid thing from a courtroom where a sixteen-year-old had raised her hand and said *yes, that's my sibling, yes, that's their storm.*

"You came," Marisol said. Her voice carried over the water without effort. "I prayed you wouldn't, and you came anyway. You always were the stubborn one."

"That's rich," Ada called back, "coming from the woman who swam here."

"I didn't swim. I was *brought*. There's a difference, and it matters less than you'd think." Marisol's composure flickered—just once, just enough for Ada to see the girl underneath, the one who used to hold their hand during thunderstorms because Ada's storms frightened her more than the sky's. "I've served the Veyl court for six years, Ada. I learned what broke the covenant. I learned who. And I learned that the veil can be restored—but only the way it was made."

"Don't," Ada said.

"Somebody has to give themselves. Willingly. Fully. That's the rite." Marisol spread her arms, and the sea around the altar went glass-still, listening. "And I have been offering myself for years, in small ways, preparing. I'm ready. I've *chosen*. That's what it requires—that's all it requires—and I am asking you, as the last person alive who knew me before all of this, to let me do this one true thing."

"You don't get to buy my life with yours!" Ada's voice cracked across the water like the storm they'd once called. "You testified against me. You took twelve years from me. You don't get to make us even by *dying*, Mari, that's not how—"

"That's exactly how!" And there she was, finally, Marisol angry and human and seventeen again. "You think I did it to hurt you? I did it because they told me if I didn't, they'd take you *that night*, and I thought—I was a child, I thought testimony would save you, and instead it exiled you, and I have spent every day since—" She stopped. Breathed. "I'm not making us even. There's no even. I'm just trying to give something back."

"Neither of you," Sefa said, stepping forward onto the gunwale like the deck might let her walk on water if she believed hard enough, "is dying today. I refuse. As lighthouse keeper, I formally refuse. It's practically a law."

* * *

They never got to argue further, because that was when Commander Haleth came down out of the sky on wings of pressed shadow, and the sea began to scream.

He landed on the altar's far edge with two dozen wardens behind him, and his face—his calm, bureaucratic, door-to-door face—was wrong now. Older than his body. Older than the coast itself. When he smiled, Ada saw the smile of something that had been wearing men like coats for a very long time.

"Do you know," Haleth said pleasantly, "how difficult it is to break a covenant? Not to violate—to *break*, to unwind, to make as though it never was?" He walked toward the pillar, and the white stone darkened under his heels. "You need a founder's blood. You need a resonant fool stupid enough to call a storm at the border. And you need a sister willing to say the words that send that fool away, so the wound never closes."

Ada went cold from the inside out.

"You," they whispered. "You made me call it. The voices on the wind—you *were* the voices. And Mari—"

"She was easier to bend than to break," Haleth agreed. "Children believe what fear tells them. But the covenant mended itself around your exile—barely—so I waited. And then the stars went out, and here we all are, and here is the altar, and I will simply kill everyone on it until the veil stays dead and Veyl drowns your coast and I inherit the wreckage." He raised a hand. "Nothing personal."

What followed was not a battle so much as a catastrophe with choreography. Brannoc threw the barge's whole powder locker into the water and the resulting geyser swallowed six wardens; Sefa stood at the pillar with her lamp held high and its light burned the drowned-faces off the things attacking her; Father Emeric chanted the rite at full volume like a man weaponizing liturgy. And Ada ran—not away, but *in*, through the chaos, toward the center of everything, toward the place where the air still remembered the first storm ever sworn upon.

Haleth saw them coming and laughed. "Yes! Give me your death, little resonant! A willing life, freely given—it works just as well for *unbinding!*"

"That's the thing," Ada said, reaching the pillar, pressing both palms against the cold white stone, feeling beneath it the vast sleeping shape of the storm they had called at nineteen and never stopped apologizing for. "Everyone keeps talking about giving a *life*. My sister. This monster. Even poor Emeric with his book." They closed their eyes. The storm rose in them, huge and homesick, the thing they had hated longest and knew best. "But it was never supposed to be a life. It was supposed to be a *self*. Everything you are, given over. So here's mine."

They seized the storm—all of it, every charged cell, every whispered word of weather, the gift and the curse and the twelve years of exile it had cost them—and poured it into the veil like wine into a wedding cup.

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest part. It felt like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had grown around it. It felt like the moment after crying. Light went up through the pillar and out along the seabed in veins of gold, and the stars—far above, beyond the smoke of Haleth's shadow-host—came back on, one by one, like windows relit in a house someone had finally come home to.

Haleth screamed, unraveling, his borrowed coat of commander burning away to reveal nothing underneath at all, nothing for centuries, and the last thing before he came apart entirely was not rage but something horribly like *relief*.

The sea settled. The altar sank gently, almost courteously, lowering Ada back toward the barge, where Sefa caught them under the arms and Marisol waded out and Brannoc swore with tremendous artistry, and Ada lay against the wet deck and felt—for the first time since childhood—nothing at all humming under their skin.

"It's gone," they said wonderingly. "All of it. I can't feel the weather. I can't feel *anything*."

Sefa brushed the hair from their forehead, and her hand was shaking, and her voice was not. "Good," she said. "Now you'll just have to feel things the ordinary way. Like the rest of us. It's terrible. You'll love it."

Marisol knelt beside them, dripping, pearl-braids ruined, and opened her mouth to say something enormous—

—and Ada, exhausted, magicless, alive, started laughing, and couldn't stop, and pulled their sister down into a hug that tasted of salt and had absolutely nothing left to prove.

Above Greyharbor, far away, the first clouds in weeks gathered with no help from anyone, and began, very quietly, to rain.
