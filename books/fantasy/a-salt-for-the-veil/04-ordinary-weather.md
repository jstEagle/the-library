## 4. Ordinary Weather

The stars came back on a Tuesday, which felt rude of them, somehow. As if they'd only stepped out for a smoke and expected no one to have worried.

Sefa Ilo noticed first, because noticing was her whole profession. She had spent twenty-three years keeping the Greyharbor light, and in that time she had learned that the sea told you everything if you were patient enough to listen: weather three days out, a hull gone soft in the ribs, a heart gone soft in the chest. The night the stars went out she had stood at the lamp room rail with her fists white on the iron and understood, with a certainty she couldn't explain to the harbormaster, that the world had been unplugged.

And now they were back. All of them. Arranged in their old constellations like nothing had happened, like the Veyl hadn't pressed its drowned face against the glass of the sky for six weeks.

Sefa looked at them a long while, then went downstairs and put the kettle on. That was the thing about miracles. Somebody still had to make tea.

* * *

Ada Reyes had taken to sleeping in the lamp room, which Sefa pretended not to notice and also did not mind.

They were there most mornings when she climbed the stairs with two mugs, sitting against the glass with their knees drawn up, watching the water with an expression Sefa recognized from mirrors. It was the look of someone taking inventory of what remained.

"You know," Sefa said one morning, handing over a mug, "most people who lose something sleep badly. You've turned insomnia into an art form."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"Your eyes are doing all the work of a harbor watch."

Ada almost smiled. It was a strange thing, watching them learn to be magicless — like watching a sailor learn to walk on land after twenty years at sea, that same careful planting of the feet, that distrust of ground that didn't move. They kept reaching for things that weren't there. Sefa had seen it a dozen times: Ada's hand twitching toward a storm cloud as if to turn it aside, toward a gull as if to ask it something, toward the tide itself as if it were a door they'd once had a key to. And each time, the hand closed on empty air, and Ada's face did the thing it did.

"Does it hurt?" Sefa asked. She'd been saving the question for six days.

"No." Ada considered the tea. "That's the worst part. It doesn't hurt. It's just *quiet*. I spent twelve years listening to the storm in my blood, and now there's — nothing. Just me in here." They tapped their sternum. "Turns out I don't much care for the company."

"You're decent company," Sefa said. "I'd know. I've been stuck with you for weeks."

"That's different. You're contractually obligated. Lighthouse keepers are legally required to tolerate shipwrecks."

"I'll have you checked for barnacles."

Ada laughed — short, surprised, real — and Sefa counted it a good morning's work. This was how it went, these days. Small salvages. You hauled up what you could and let the rest stay down in the dark where it belonged.

* * *

Marisol Reyes arrived on the Thursday packet boat with one bag, no warning, and the specific posture of someone who has rehearsed a speech on a long voyage and intends to deliver it whether or not anyone consents to hear it.

Sefa met her at the dock because Brannoc was busy arguing with customs officials about crates that officially contained salted cod and unofficially contained anything but, and because somebody in this found family had to handle arrivals.

"You're the lighthouse keeper," Marisol said. Not a question. Her eyes were different than Ada's — darker around the rim, Sefa thought, like water over deep places. Six years serving the Veyl court left marks even when the court let you go. Especially then.

"And you're the sister."

"I'm the sister." Marisol said it carefully, testing the weight of it, like a coin she wasn't sure was still legal tender.

"Well," Sefa said, jerking her thumb up the cliff path, "they're in the lamp room pretending to be furniture. Go on up. Fair warning: they'll pretend they aren't glad to see you. They do that. It's load-bearing."

* * *

Sefa did not eavesdrop. She want to be clear about that. She simply happened to be re-tarring the gallery rail directly beneath the lamp room windows, a task that required her complete attention and also allowed her to hear every word.

The first ten minutes were arguments. Salt-bread was involved — Marisol had brought a loaf, dense and grey and faintly bitter, the kind the Veyl's shore-folk baked, and Ada took one look at it and said, "You brought me *prison bread*," and Marisol said, "It's not prison bread, it's *mourning* bread, there's a difference, and you'd know that if you'd ever finished reading the letters I sent," and Ada said, "Your letters read like tax documents," and Marisol said, "Yours read like ransom notes," and so on, in the manner of siblings everywhere, circling the actual wound at high speed and throwing furniture at it from a safe distance.

Then it went quiet. Then Marisol said, low, "I offered my life for yours. Do you understand what that means? I finally chose you, after everything, and you wouldn't even let me have that."

And Ada said, quieter, "Love isn't supposed to be a payment, Mari. If I'd let you die for me, I'd have spent the rest of my life being a debt you paid. I couldn't stand being your altar." A pause. "So I became the salt instead. It seemed fair at the time. Less fair now, honestly. I miss setting things on fire with my mind."

"You never set anything on fire with your mind."

"One curtain. In Vellimont. Ask Brannoc."

The silence after that was a different kind — the kind where something is knitting itself back together, badly, with the wrong-colored thread, but knitting. When Sefa glanced up, the two of them were sitting shoulder to shoulder against the glass, passing the mourning bread back and forth, eating it anyway. It was terrible bread. They ate every crumb.

* * *

Brannoc reopened the smuggling routes the way other people reopen summer cottages: with relish, a fresh coat of paint, and a suspicious amount of inventory she claimed to have "had lying about."

"The wards are back," Sefa pointed out, watching the old woman nail a new sign to the *Kindly Tide* — *FREIGHT & PASSENGERS, DISCREET* — "which means the wardens are back, which means—"

"Which means the wardens are honest men again, doing honest work, checking honest manifests." Brannoc hammered the last nail with the satisfaction of a woman ringing a bell. "There's a difference between smuggling under a broken covenant and trading *adjacent to* a functioning one, girl. One's treason. The other's entrepreneurship."

"You're going to get arrested."

"I'm seventy-one. Let them try. My lawyer is a priest with no church and a grudge, and my crew includes the person who unmade the border magic. I like our odds." She squinted up at the lamp room. "Besides. Someone has to keep an eye on those two. They'll argue themselves into the sea otherwise. It's what the Reyes family does instead of hugging."

* * *

On the last night of the month, it rained.

Not a storm. Sefa would have known — she could feel storms coming the way she felt tides, in the teeth and the joints and the old scar on her knee. This was just rain: steady, gray, unremarkable, the kind of rain that meant nothing except that clouds had gotten heavy somewhere over the open water and decided to let go.

She found Ada standing out on the seawall in it, soaked through, face tipped up.

For a long moment Sefa just watched from the doorway of the boathouse. Then she walked out and stood beside them, because some silences were better shared.

"It used to answer me," Ada said at last. Rain ran off their chin. "Weather. I'd stand in it and it would... lean. Like a dog hearing its name." They laughed, wet and rough. "Now it's just falling on me. Doesn't know I exist. Twelve years ago I'd have given anything for that. Now—" They shook their head. "I don't know who I am if I'm not the storm."

Sefa thought about it. She was a woman who took her time with answers; the sea had taught her that haste sank ships.

"You're the person who poured yourself into a veil to save two worlds," she said. "You're the person who wouldn't let your sister pay for you. You're the person Brannoc calls 'the little disaster' with genuine affection, and the person Marisol crossed an ocean to argue with, and the person who sleeps in my lamp room because—" she paused, and decided, since they were already drenched, there was nothing left to protect, "—because you like the company. None of that needed magic. None of it ever did."

Ada looked at her. Rain between them, and the dark water beyond, ordinary as bread.

"Sefa Ilo," they said slowly, "are you saying what I think you're saying?"

"I'm saying you're decent company. Legally speaking, I'm required to."

"That's not a real law."

"It is now. I just wrote it." She held out her hand. "Come inside before you rust. There's soup. Marisol made it, which means it's terrible, which means we eat all of it."

Ada took her hand. And when they turned back toward the lights of Greyharbor — the lamp behind them, the town ahead, small and crooked and lit window by window against the rain — they didn't look at the sea the way you look at something you've lost.

They looked at it the way you look at a neighbor.

Just weather. Just water. Just home, finally, chosen freely, in the rain.
