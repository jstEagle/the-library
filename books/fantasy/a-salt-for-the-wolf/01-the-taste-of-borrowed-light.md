## 1. The Taste of Borrowed Light

The wedding of Aldous Verrin, wool merchant and third-richest man in Hollowmere, smelled of beeswax, roast pheasant, and the particular nervous sweat of a groom who had married above his station. Sable Zhou stood at the edge of the feast-hall with a borrowed serving coat over his shoulders and an empty tray in his hands, and he breathed it all in the way other thieves breathed in the layout of a house: the wax meant candles burned long enough for shadows to shift; the pheasant meant wine would follow and wine meant soft edges; the sweat meant Aldous Verrin was thinking about something other than his pockets.

He waited for the toasts.

They came late, as toasts did. The fiddler scraped his way into the third reel, the bride's mother wept into her second cup, and Verrin rose with his glass held high, flushed and beaming, and said, "To my Lira—may I remember this day until the day I die."

Sable touched the rim of the merchant's mind like a fingertip to still water.

It was not a grabbing, whatever the old stories said. It was a tasting. Memories had flavors, and wedding days tasted the way honey tasted if honey could also be sad—a brightness on the tongue with a long ache underneath, the ghost of every ceremony anyone had ever attended folded into one golden afternoon. Sable let it roll across him. The morning light through the chapel's east window, blue as river water. Lira's hands shaking so badly he'd had to steady the ring twice. His dead father's chair, empty, with a sprig of rosemary laid across it. The moment the priest said *husband* and the word landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Only what can be spared,* Sable reminded himself, and chose.

He took the taste but not the substance—the shine of the day rather than its bones. When he withdrew, Verrin would still remember his wedding. He would remember it slightly wrong, the way everyone remembered everything, details gone soft and secondhand, the light in the east window remembered as gold when it had been blue. He might wonder, years from now, why the memory felt worn at the edges, like a coin passed through too many hands. But he would have it. That was the covenant of Sable's trade: he took only what could be spared, and almost everything could spare a little of itself. Almost everything survived being forgotten once.

What he carried out of Verrin's mind sat behind his teeth like a pearl: the whole wedding, gleaming and intact, minus only its roots.

"To the bride," Sable murmured along with the room, lifting an empty glass he had no business holding, and Verrin beamed at him—at no one, at all the no ones pressed against the walls—and Sable slipped out through the kitchen while the fiddler began the fourth reel.

* * *

Maren's shop sat where the tanner's row bent away from the millrace, wedged between two buildings that had leaned toward each other for a century like conspirators sharing a secret. Above the door hung a sign with no writing on it, only a painted eye, closed. Those who knew, knew.

Sable came in through the back, as always, ducking under the drying racks of bundled herbs that were mostly just herbs—Maren sold chamomile and comfrey to half the valley, honest medicine, honest cover—and found the old woman at her counting table with a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, examining a memory vial against the lamplight.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"The cake was worth waiting for."

"Everything in that hall was worth waiting for, if you've got the stomach for joy." She set the loupe down and held out her hand, palm up, fingers curled like a falcon come to perch. "Show me."

He didn't hand her anything. There was nothing to hand her yet. Instead he took her weathered hand in both of his and breathed out, slow, letting the pearl rise from behind his teeth, and Maren closed her eyes.

Her face changed. It always changed. Seventy years of living had carved her features deep, but when she took a memory in, all those lines went smooth and young for the space of a breath, as though she were trying on the lives of everyone who had ever walked through her door. Her lips moved—repeating vows, maybe, or laughing at some joke told by a groomsman three hours dead drunk—and then her eyes opened and the smoothness drained away and she was Maren again, broker, appraiser, keeper of other people's best days.

"Verrin," she said. "The wool merchant."

"Aldous. Third-richest."

"There's no such thing as third-richest in a town this size; there's richest and there's everyone else." She turned the memory over in her mind the way she'd turned the vial under the lamp, checking its weight. "Clean work. You left him his day?"

"I left him everything but the color of the light."

"Mm." She reached beneath the table and came up with a small clay pot, sealed with wax, and pushed it across the boards. "Coin inside. And before you count it—I know you count it, you count every time—it's fair. Better than fair. Wedding memories run high this season."

"Why?" Sable asked, pocketing the pot. "Weddings happen every spring."

Maren looked at him then, really looked, the way she only did when she was deciding how much truth a twenty-year-old thief could carry without dropping it. She had a face built for withholding—flat grey eyes, a mouth that had practiced its line a thousand times in a thousand mirrors—but tonight the withholding had cracks in it.

"Buyers are asking strange questions lately," she said.

"What kind of strange?"

"The kind that pays too much." She took off her spectacles and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "For years it's been simple. Grief-memories for the rich who want to feel something they can't afford to earn. First kisses for the lonely. A mother's lullaby for a man whose mother never sang. Sweet things, small things, harmless things. Now—" she waved a hand at the shelves lining the back wall, rows and rows of stoppered vials glinting like a chapel of tiny saints, "—now they want childhoods. Whole childhoods. They want names. Naming-days. They ask me, do I have anything from before the charmline went up? Anything from the founding families. Anything *old*, Sable. And they don't tell me why, which is worse than any why they could say out loud."

Sable leaned against the doorframe and tried to make a joke of it. "Maybe rich folk in the low country have run out of their own lives to collect."

"Maybe." Maren did not laugh. She never laughed when it mattered. "Or maybe someone is looking for something specific and doesn't know what it looks like yet, so they're buying everything with roots in this valley and sifting. And when the sifting gets fine enough—" she looked at him, flat and grey and, for one unguarded instant, frightened, "—it finds people like us. People with holes in them."

"I don't have holes," Sable said. "I make holes. In other people."

"That's what you think." She said it lightly, brushing crumbs from the table, and the lightness was worse than any argument. "Go home. Eat something that isn't stolen. And Sable—stay away from the eastern farms for a month or two. There are men asking questions out that way, men with a writ of some kind. Royal paper, someone said."

"In Hollowmere?" He laughed before he could stop himself, and the laugh came out thin. "There's no magic here. There's been no magic here for three hundred years. What would royal hunters want with a valley full of sheep?"

"Three hundred years ago, something made that true," Maren said quietly. "Things that are made true can be made false again. Go home, boy."

* * *

He went home by the long way, along the millrace, because the night was warm and because he wanted, very badly, not to think about what Maren had said.

Hollowmere lay quiet under a fat spring moon. Water talked to itself in the race. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at something only dogs could see. The mountains stood around the valley like a congregation with bowed heads, and beyond their western shoulders the sky held a smudge of white—the last of the winter that should have ended weeks ago and somehow hadn't, the snowline creeping lower on the peaks each year while the elders blamed the wind and the children wore their coats into May.

This was his. This small lit valley, these crooked streets, this life balanced as delicately as a coin on its edge. By day he was nobody: a thin young man with ink-stained fingers who mended book bindings and copied letters for farmers who couldn't write. By night he was a thief of the gentlest kind, slipping into minds the way other men slipped into houses, taking only the shine, leaving only the wear. He ate well. He owed no one. He belonged to no one, which was a kind of freedom, or had felt like one at fourteen, and at seventeen, and even at nineteen.

At twenty, on a warm night in May, with a clay pot of fair wages in his pocket and a merchant's wedding-day dissolving sweetly somewhere in the world's appetite, it felt less like freedom and more like standing in a doorway with the wind rising behind him.

Because there was the thing he never let himself think, walking beside him now like a shadow cast by no visible object:

He made holes in other people. Maren had said it herself. And Sable Zhou could remember his life with perfect clarity, every year accounted for, every wound mapped—

Except the beginning.

He knew he'd been born; there was a record in the temple ledger, a date, a mother's name. He knew the name. He could not, no matter how many times he reached for it, remember her voice. He could not remember a single thing from before his sixth year—not a face, not a smell, not one scene. His childhood began abruptly, fully formed, like a play starting in the middle of the second act, and he had learned at a very young age never to mention this, first because he couldn't explain it, and later because he had begun to suspect that explanations existed, and that he would not like them.

*A hole shaped like a door,* whispered something in the back of his skull, in a voice that sounded unpleasantly like Maren's. *And doors open from both sides.*

He stopped at the little bridge over the race and looked down at the water, and the water showed him a thin young man with ink-stained fingers and a stranger's wedding shining behind his teeth, and for no reason he could name, Sable shivered.

Far to the west, over the mountains, over the snow that refused to melt, the sky flickered—once, faintly, green, like lightning seen through a closed lid.

Then it was gone, and the dog stopped barking, and the valley forgot it had ever made a sound.
