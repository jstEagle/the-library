## 2. What the Valley Forgets

The hunters came on a morning so gray the valley seemed to have been drawn in charcoal and left unfinished.

Sable saw them first from the roof of Maren's shop, where he had climbed to mend a slate that had slipped in the night wind. He crouched among the mossy tiles with the hammer still in his hand, and he watched three riders come down the mountain road at a pace no merchant ever used—deliberate, unhurried, the pace of men who did not expect to be stopped. Their cloaks were the color of wet slate, and even at that distance Sable could see the sigil stitched on their shoulders: a crown above crossed keys. The writ of the realm, carried into Hollowmere for the first time in anyone's living memory.

Below him, the town went about its business with the blind confidence of long habit. The baker's boy hauled his baskets through the square. Old Tam wept over his onions at market because they'd come up sweet this year, sweeter than onions had any right to be, and everyone laughed and said the soil was generous and thought nothing more of it. That was the covenant's great mercy, or its great trick: magic happened here the way weather happened, noticed only when it inconvenienced someone. A woman whose bread never burned. A man who could quiet a colicky infant with a hum. Sweet onions. Small sins against the natural order, absorbed by the valley like rain into loam.

Sable had built his whole life inside that absorption. He was twenty-three years old and he had been stealing memories since he was nine, and in fourteen years not one person in Hollowmere had ever looked at him twice. That was the gift's other face—the taker left no more impression than a draft through a door. He could stand in a crowded room and be forgotten before he finished leaving it. He had told himself for years that this was simply how the world worked, the way water worked, the way stone worked.

Watching the riders descend, he understood for the first time that it might have been something else entirely. Something granted. Something that could be revoked.

He climbed down too fast and split his palm on the ladder rail, and he barely felt it.

* * *

Maren was already awake when he came through the door, which was unusual; she kept broker's hours, rising late and working later, her back bent over the little brass apparatus she used to weigh memories like a spice merchant weighing saffron. This morning she stood at the window with her arms folded, watching the square, and her face wore an expression Sable had seen perhaps three times in ten years: fear, honestly worn.

"They've stopped at the boundary stones," she said without turning. "All three of them. Just sitting there."

"Testing," Sable said.

"You think?"

"The writ says the crown's law runs everywhere. But everyone knows magic doesn't cross the charmline. If they're smart, they'll want to know if that's still true before they walk in depending on it."

Maren turned then. She was sixty and looked older, her gray hair pinned up with whatever was nearest—a pencil today—and her eyes were pale as river ice. "And if it isn't true anymore? If their charms work past the line?" She let the question sit between them like something dead. "Then the valley has been protected all these years by a wall with a hole in it, and nobody told us."

Sable set the hammer down. His hand was still bleeding; he wrapped it in a rag without looking at it. "You've heard something."

"I hear everything. That's my trade." She came away from the window and began, with unnecessary force, straightening jars on her shelves—memories in suspension, each one glowing faintly through the glass like trapped fireflies. Wedding days. First snows. Dead mothers' voices. "A buyer in the low country wrote to me last month asking questions I didn't like. Not *do you have anything new*, but *how many gifted live in your valley*. Not *what do they remember*, but *what can they do*. Census questions, Sable. Inventory questions." She stopped, her hand flat on a jar of a farmer's memory of his wedding, and her voice dropped. "You don't inventory what you already own. You inventory what you intend to take."

Outside, a bell began to ring. Not the chapel bell—the watch bell, cracked and half-hearted, rung maybe once a year when a barn caught fire. It rang now with a thin, panicked stutter, and beneath it Sable could hear the sound of the town gathering, footsteps and voices converging on the square.

They went together. Neither of them suggested otherwise; there was safety in being part of a crowd, especially for two people whose whole existence depended on not being noticed. Maren locked the shop behind them with hands that were perfectly steady, which frightened Sable more than shaking would have.

* * *

The square was full by the time they reached it, the whole town pressed shoulder to shoulder around the well, and the three hunters sat their horses at its edge like a held breath. Up close they were worse than they'd been at a distance. They were young, all three—younger than Sable—and clean in a way that made the town look ragged, their gear oiled and matched, their faces smooth and unbothered. The one in front held a scroll case bearing the royal seal, but he didn't open it. He didn't need to. The sigil on his shoulder did the talking.

"We come under the king's writ," he said, and his voice carried without effort, pitched to reach every ear in the square. "By decree, all unlicensed workings are subject to examination. All practitioners are subject to registration." He paused, letting the words find their places. "We are aware that this valley believes itself exempt. We are here to verify."

A murmur ran through the crowd—not anger, not yet. Confusion. Exemption wasn't a thing anyone had ever had to believe in; it was simply the air they breathed. Old Tam, still holding his basket of too-sweet onions, called out in his cracking voice: "Exempt by covenant, lad. Older than any king. Your grandfathers' grandfathers knew better than to come up here."

Something flickered across the hunter's face—something that might have been pity, or hunger. He dismounted. And from his belt he took a small object on a chain: a charm, silver and plain, shaped like an eye with the pupil drilled through.

"Hollowmere sits behind a boundary," he said. "So does every place where the old workings survived. The crown learned, some centuries ago, that boundaries fail." He raised the charm so the whole square could see it. "This is how we test."

He walked, slowly, toward the edge of the square—to the old boundary marker, a waist-high stone most people used for tying off goats, worn featureless by centuries of weather. And as he crossed it, walking out beyond the charmline onto the bare road, he held the charm up.

It should have gone dark. Every child in Hollowmere learned that much before they learned their letters: past the stones, the old things stop working. Charms go dead. Gifts go quiet. The valley keeps its secrets and the world keeps its distance, and both are glad of it.

The charm stayed lit. Its drilled pupil glowed a steady, patient red, like a coal that would not die, and the hunter turned slowly in a circle to let the whole square see it burning.

"No," someone whispered. It might have been Maren.

The hunter walked back across the line, and the charm burned just as bright on the far side, and he smiled with terrible gentleness at the assembled town. "As I thought," he said. "The boundary is failing. Which means, good people, that you have been living outside the law for some time without knowing it. The king is merciful. Registration is not punishment. Examination is not punishment." His eyes moved across the crowd, unhurried, cataloguing. "But concealment is."

Sable stood very still in the press of bodies, and felt the crowd's fear move through him like cold water through cloth. Around him, people were doing arithmetic he could almost hear: *who among us? Who will they ask about? Whose bread never burns?* He watched neighbors glance at neighbors, quick and guilty, and he understood that the hunters would not need torture or truth-charms. The valley would inform on itself. Fear was the oldest charm of all, and it worked everywhere.

* * *

They took Bramwen Hale that same afternoon.

Sable saw it from the lane outside the smithy, where he'd gone ostensibly to return a borrowed tool and actually because the smithy sat at the corner where the roads met and he wanted to see everything. Bramwen was the cooper's daughter, nineteen, wide-hipped and loud-laughing, and her gift was so small it was nearly a joke: she could warm things. Milk, bathwater, cold hands, the stones of a windowsill on a winter night. She'd warmed Sable's tea once, laughing, pressing her palm flat against the cup until it steamed. "Useless witchery," she'd said. "I'm good for nothing but comfort."

Four hunters' men—there were more than three now; the riders had been scouts, and the rest had come up the mountain road behind them like water finding a crack—surrounded her in her father's yard. She hadn't even tried to run. She stood there with flour on her forearms, having been mid-bread when they came, and she said, "But I don't *do* anything. I just—it's not even magic, it's hardly anything—"

"That's exactly what we're looking for," said the officer, and his tone was almost kind, which was the worst thing about it. "Small gifts are the easiest to register. You'll be home by supper."

She was not home by supper. They walked her out of the yard with her wrists bound in iron-thread cord, and her father shouting, and her mother making a sound Sable had never heard a human being make before, a sound like a hinge tearing out of a doorframe. And as they passed, Bramwen turned her head and looked directly at Sable—looked at him, saw him, the way almost no one ever did—and her eyes were wide and wet and full of a question he couldn't answer.

*Why didn't you help me? Why doesn't anyone?*

He had no answer. He stood in the lane with the borrowed tool in his hand and let her be taken, because helping meant being seen, and being seen meant being taken too, and his whole life was built on the certainty that invisibility was safety. He had believed it the way children believe things: totally, without examination. Now, watching the hunters' backs recede down the road with their prisoner between them, he understood the shape of the lie. Invisibility was not a wall he had built. It was a mercy someone else had extended to him—an arrangement, a covenant term, a courtesy of powers he had never met—and courtesies could be withdrawn. Someone, somewhere, had decided the thief of Hollowmere was not worth noticing.

Someone could decide otherwise.

* * *

That night he could not sleep. He lay in his narrow room above Maren's shop listening to the wind work at the shutters, and the silence of the town below was worse than noise—no dogs barking at the strangers, no late voices, just the held breath of a hundred households each privately deciding what they would say tomorrow if asked about their neighbors.

Somewhere past midnight, unable to bear his own skull, he did the thing he almost never did alone: he went inward, into the archive of himself, the way he went into other people. It was a habit of his gift, self-examination, though he rarely found much worth seeing. His own memories were a modest collection. Maren's kitchen, the day she took him in. The shop, the trade, the small rituals of his hidden life. Years of them, layered and ordinary.

Except.

He found the hollow the way a tongue finds a missing tooth: suddenly, and with a shock of wrongness. There was a gap in him. Not a stolen memory—he knew what those looked like, knew the clean edges of an extraction, the faint scar-tissue shimmer where something had been lifted out. This was different. This was older. It sat at the very beginning of him, before Maren, before everything, and it was not empty the way a room is empty. It was empty the way a locked room is empty. Shaped. Deliberate.

Shaped like a doorway.

He lay in the dark with his heart going hard and slow, feeling the edges of the thing. A door-shaped absence at the root of his own mind, fitted with a precision no child could make and no accident explain. Someone had cut it. Or someone had closed it—from the other side. And standing at the threshold of that hollow, probing its outline the way he'd probe a mark's defenses before a theft, Sable felt—for the first time in fourteen years of taking other people's pasts—

something on the other side of the door, stir.

Faint. Patient. Like a wolf shifting its weight in the dark, waiting to be let in—or waiting to be let out.

Downstairs, in the street, hooves. Many of them. And a voice calling through the night, clear and carrying, naming names.

The first name was his.
