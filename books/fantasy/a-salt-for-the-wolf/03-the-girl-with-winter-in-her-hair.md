## 3. The Girl with Winter in Her Hair

The raid came at the hour when Hollowmere's lamps were lit and its lies were thickest—when every household drew its curtains against the dark and pretended, as it had pretended for two hundred years, that nothing in the valley needed hiding.

Sable was on Maren's roof when he heard the dogs.

Not the valley's own slow farm-dogs, but the lean, silent things that ran ahead of the crown's hunters, bred in kennels south of the mountains where they fed them something Sable did not want to imagine. He had heard them once before, years ago, from across the charmline—a sound like silk tearing. Now the sound was inside the valley. Inside his life.

He slid down the shingles and through the loft window of the dyer's house next door, where old Tam kept a jar of memories from his first wife that he could not bear to sell and could not bear to keep whole. Sable had promised to take only the sharpest edges someday, when Tam asked. Tam hadn't asked yet. Tam was screaming now, down in his yard, while three men in grey-and-crimson livery held him against his own fence post and a fourth held a charm of braided iron and bone against his throat.

The charm *sang*. That was the worst of it. Sable had always imagined the covenant as silence—the great hush laid over Hollowmere, magic pressed flat like flowers in a book. But the hunter's charm rang like a struck bell, and under the ringing, Tam's small gift—the way he could warm dye-water with a touch, nothing more, a trick so modest it was nearly kindness—rose up out of him like smoke and poured into the iron.

Tam went slack. The hunters wrote something in a ledger. One of them said, "Minor vessel. Adequate yield," in the bored tone of a man weighing flour.

Sable crouched in the loft's dark with his heart slamming and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that invisibility had never been his shield. It had been someone else's mercy. Someone else's patience. And the patience had run out.

He went over the back wall and ran.

* * *

The lanes of Hollowmere at night were a maze he'd mapped by theft: which windows glowed, which doors stuck, which alleys smelled of tannin or tallow. He took them at a dead sprint, boots skidding on frost-slick cobbles, and cut behind the chandlery toward the drainage culvert that let out past the sheep-pens—his route out of town, worn smooth by years of small crimes.

That was where he collided with her.

She came out of the shadow between the pens like something thrown, all elbows and terror, and they went down together onto the frozen mud. He caught a fistful of her coat—too big for her, a man's coat—and she bit him, hard, on the wrist, before either of them could speak.

Then the flood came.

It was nothing like taking a memory. Taking was gentle; taking was a thief's art, a sip drawn off the top of a life so the drinker never noticed the level drop. This was a dam breaking. The girl's memories poured into him unbidden, hot and cold at once, and Sable arched on the frozen ground with his mouth open around a scream he couldn't voice—

—*smoke, a village burning in green-tinged fire that left the snow unmarked, a woman shouting a word he didn't understand but his bones did, running, small hands in the snow, a door-shaped absence, and over it all a shadow moving against the flames, vast and four-legged, wolf-shaped, watching with eyes like winter stars, and a voice—not heard but remembered-in-the-blood—saying* ***not this one, she is salted, she is promised***—

It ended. He lay gasping in the culvert's reek with tears freezing on his face, and the girl crouched above him, feral and shaking, her hair streaked white at the temples like frost that had taken root and grown.

"Give them back," she said. Her voice was hoarse, unused. "You took them. Give them *back*."

"I didn't—" Sable coughed. "I don't take without asking. I don't take children. I swear it."

"You're lying. Everyone lies." But she was looking at him strangely now, head tilted, and he realized she had felt it too—the passage, the shape of what had moved between them. "You saw the wolf."

"Yes."

Her chin trembled, just once. Then she grabbed his collar with both fists. "Then you know they're coming. The grey men. They burned Ellowen. They want what I carry." She glanced over her shoulder at the dark. "They can *smell* it."

Dogs again, closer. Silk tearing.

Sable should have run alone. Every instinct honed by ten years of survival said so: one body moves faster than two, and a child is a weight, a liability, a grief waiting to happen. He got up anyway and pulled her with him.

"Can you run?"

"I've been running since autumn," she said.

"Good. Keep doing that."

* * *

Maren opened her door before he knocked, which meant she'd been watching the street, which meant she already knew everything. She looked at Sable, then at the girl half-hidden behind him, then at the bite on his wrist, and her face did the thing it always did—folded shut like a ledger closing.

"Inside. Quickly."

The memory-broker's house was narrow and deep, stuffed floor to rafters with jars, vials, wax-sealed phials, each holding a sliver of someone's life. In the back room, behind a false wall, was the archive proper: hundreds of memories shelved in the dark like preserves, glowing faintly with borrowed light. Maren lit no lamp. She never did, down here.

"Grey-and-crimson," Sable said. "Four of them working Tam's yard. There'll be more. They took his gift out through an iron charm—it *rang*, Maren. The covenant's supposed to stop that."

"The covenant is supposed to do a great many things." Maren knelt in front of the girl, unhurried, and studied her the way she studied merchandise—but Sable saw something else underneath, something almost like recognition. "What's your name, child?"

A long silence. "Wren," the girl said finally. "I think. Before, I had another one. It's gone soft."

"Before what?"

"Before the wolf salted me." Wren touched her white-streaked hair. "It put winter in me so the burning wouldn't take all of me. Just most." She looked up at Maren with terrible directness. "Are you going to sell me?"

"No one is selling anyone," Maren said, in the tone she used for closing deals, and Sable flinched at how much it sounded like a lie.

"She stays," Maren added, turning to him. "Whatever she carries, the hunters want it. Which means we want it more. Anything the crown fears is worth more than gold, Sable, and anything the crown wants *this badly*—"

"—is a reason to run," Sable finished. "Not a reason to keep."

"Those are the same thing tonight." Maren was already pulling jars from the shelves, packing a satchel with brisk, brutal efficiency. "Back room. Both of you. Don't touch anything."

* * *

Ilsa Vane found the trail at dawn, and hated how easy it was.

Two sets of prints leaving the chandlery district—one adult, long-strided, trained by habit if not by drill; one child's, light, quick, favoring the left foot. They led to the broker's house, and the broker's house had a cellar that didn't appear on any tax roll Ilsa had memorized, because she had memorized every cellar in Hollowmere three years ago when the crown first sent her here to wait.

Three years. Three years of drinking in the Woolpack and listening, of learning which farmers hummed to their crops and which midwife's hands ran warm, of writing names in a ledger she told herself she'd never submit. She'd thought she was protecting the valley by knowing it better than her masters did. Tonight her masters had come with writs and iron charms and taken old Tam Voss, who had once fixed her boot heel and refused payment, and the ledger had gone in the fire an hour after she watched them do it.

Now she stood in the broker's back room with her sword drawn and four soldiers' worth of authority in her pocket, and the thief and the child looked up at her from among the shelves like animals in a snare.

"Sable Zhou," she said. "And company."

The thief put himself between her and the girl. Of course he did. Ilsa had watched him for three years too—had reported, in her private ledger, that the memory-thief stole gently and sold cheap and never touched grief unless asked. She'd written *asset, low priority, possibly useful* beside his name and hated herself a little each time.

"You're the huntress," the girl said. Not afraid. Curious, in the flat way of children who have seen too much. "You smell like the grey men. But wrong. Like their dogs."

"Wren," Sable warned.

"It's true." Wren stepped out from behind him, chin up, white-streaked hair catching the archive's faint glow. "You've been near them so long you've soaked it up. But your heart isn't theirs. Hearts don't lie to me anymore. Nothing does." She tilted her head. "Yours is tired."

Ilsa's grip tightened on the sword. "You're going to come with me. All of you. Lord Aldery has orders—"

"I know what Aldery has orders for." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, three years of listening spilling over. "Gifted blood. Vessels. Yield. You heard what they called Tam Voss. You've read the requisitions. The covenant is failing and the crown means to feed it, and everyone in this valley is a meal, and you've known for *years*—"

"Ilsa." The thief's voice was quiet. "Your name's in my head. From the raid. One of them said it—Ilsa Vane, report the broker's cellar. They sent you to us because they trust you." He paused. "Do they trust you? Or do they just own you?"

The question landed like a blade between ribs, because both answers were true and neither was bearable.

Ilsa looked at the sword in her hand. At the child with winter in her hair, whose heart was tired, whose heart apparently spoke. At the shelves of stolen lives surrounding them, and the satchel packed for flight, and the thin false wall between this room and the world where her oath lived.

She had sworn an oath to a crown. She had also, quietly, without ceremony, sworn one to a valley—to its humming farmers and warm-handed midwives and stubborn old dyers—and only one of those oaths had ever asked her to watch children burn.

Ilsa reversed the sword and drove it point-down into the floorboards between them, letting go of the hilt. A surrender older than any court's forms.

"They'll have tracked me here by now," she said. "We have perhaps an hour before this house is surrounded. So either kill me, use me, or move."

The broker appeared in the doorway with a second satchel and an expression of profound annoyance, as though defection were a scheduling inconvenience.

"There's a culvert under the tannery," Maren said. "It comes out past the charmline. Beyond that—" she glanced at Wren, and her voice lost its broker's flatness for one unguarded moment, "—beyond that, I genuinely don't know. I've never been past it. None of us have. Not in living memory."

Wren was looking at the sword standing quivering in the boards. Then at Ilsa. Something passed over the child's face that might, in a less damaged creature, have been hope.

"Hearts don't lie to me," she repeated softly. "Yours stopped lying the moment you dropped it."

Outside, faint but unmistakable, the dogs began to sing.
