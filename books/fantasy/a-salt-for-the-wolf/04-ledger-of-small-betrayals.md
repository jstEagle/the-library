## 4. Ledger of Small Betrayals

The cellar beneath Maren's house smelled of tallow and cold earth, and it was deeper than the house above it had any right to allow. Sable had known about the archive for years—he had helped carry crates down its ladder-stairs in the dark seasons when Maren's joints betrayed her—but he had never understood what he was carrying. He had assumed ledgers. Bottles of brandy. The ordinary clutter of a woman who traded in things no one could see.

Now he stood at the bottom of the stairs with a feral girl asleep against his shoulder and understood that he had been hauling treasure all along.

Shelves lined every wall from floor to ceiling, and on them sat jars—not glass but something older, a smoked amber material that gave back the lamplight like the eyes of a patient animal. Each jar held a faint luminescence, dim or bright according to some logic of its own. Memories. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A whole valley's worth of afternoons and griefs and small triumphs, filed and shelved in the dark like preserves laid down against a famine.

"First kiss," Maren said behind him, her voice rough with the climb. She came down sideways, one hand pressed to her hip where the flight from the raid had left her bruised. "Third jar, second shelf. A baker's daughter, sixty years ago. Still sweet. Memory keeps better than peaches." She lifted the shutters on her lamp and the light spread, and for a moment the whole cellar glowed like the inside of a hive. "Put the child down on the cot. She'll sleep till morning. They always do, after."

"After what?" Sable asked quietly. "After you opened her up to see what she'd been carrying?"

Maren did not flinch. He had never seen her flinch. But something in her face closed, the way a shopkeeper's door closes at dusk—politely, completely. "After I read her," she said. "Which is what I do. Which is what you do, boy, only you're squeamish about the word."

He set Wren down. The child did not wake. Her hair lay across the pillow like frost on a windowsill, and in sleep her fingers curled loosely around a splinter of wood she'd been clutching since the burning village—the only relic, perhaps, of everything she'd lost. Sable pried it gently free and she let him, her breath evening out.

Ilsa stood at the foot of the stairs with her arms crossed, still wearing the hunter's grey she hadn't yet shed, as if the clothes were a question she hadn't answered. Her eyes moved over the shelves with the flat professional assessment Sable had come to dread. Counting exits, maybe. Or counting inventory.

"You knew about this place," he said to her.

"I suspected." Her voice was low. "The crown's records mention a broker in Hollowmere. They don't mention this." She nodded at the far wall, where the shelves gave way to a heavy curtain hung with iron rings. Behind it, Sable could just make out the gleam of a strongbox, and beside it—a crate. A shipping crate, stamped with the royal seal in black wax.

His stomach went cold before his mind caught up.

"Maren," he said. "What is that?"

* * *

She didn't lie to him. That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that kept the fury from ever quite finishing itself. She told him plainly, sitting on her stool with her hands folded in her lap like a woman confessing at a hearth-shrine, while the jars glowed around them and the sleeping child breathed slow and even three feet away.

"The crown has been my best customer for eleven years," Maren said. "Longer than you've had your gift. Longer than you've had your name, if it comes to that."

"What does the crown want with memories?"

"Names, mostly. First memories. The oldest stock in the valley." She gestured upward, toward the surface, toward the whole sleeping town. "You think Hollowmere stays quiet because nobody here has ever been extraordinary? There are gifted bloodlines in this valley older than the crown itself, boy. The covenant doesn't suppress magic—it *feeds* on it. Slowly. A trickle, drawn off through names and first memories, the root-memories, the ones that anchor a gift to a soul. Take enough of them and the gift goes dormant. Take enough from enough people and—" she spread her hands, indicating the cellar, the valley, the long unnatural chill that had crept into their summers these last years, "—you get peace. You get a valley where nothing burns down and no one turns into anything and everyone's grandmother swears there has never been such a thing as witchcraft."

Sable heard his own heartbeat in his ears. "And you sold them."

"I sold what people brought me to sell. Farmers who wanted to forget a dead child. Widowers who wanted to forget a wife's face so they could marry again without weeping into their porridge. And yes—" her eyes were steady and terrible, "—when the crown paid triple for root-memories, I asked my clients harder questions. I found the ones who wanted to forget more than pain. And I sold those."

"You fed the covenant." His voice came out strange, thin. "You've been feeding it for eleven years. That's why it's lasted this long. That's why the hunt didn't come until now."

"That's why the hunt *hasn't taken everyone yet*," Maren said, and for the first time her voice cracked, just at the seam. "Do you know what happens when the feeding stops, Sable? Do you know what the crown does when the trickle runs dry? They stop harvesting memories and start harvesting blood. Whole families. I have bought this valley eleven years of being merely *dull* instead of *dead*, and I have done it one stolen childhood at a time, and I would do it again, and I will not apologize to a boy who sells other people's weddings to buy his supper."

The silence after that was very long. Down on the cot, Wren turned in her sleep and murmured something in a language Sable almost recognized—from her memories, the flooded ones, the burned village under a sky full of crows.

It was Ilsa who broke the quiet. She had not sat down. She stood with her back to the curtain and the royal seal, and when she spoke it was in the tone of a woman laying out instruments before surgery.

"She's right about the blood. It's worse than she knows—I've seen the requisitions. The covenant isn't just failing because the harvests slowed. It's failing because it was badly made. Cracked from the start. Lord Aldery's family has been patching it for six generations, and each patch costs more than the last." She looked at Sable, and her grey eyes held something that might have been apology. "The hunt isn't for witches, Sable. We don't burn anyone. The gifted ones get taken east, alive. There's a keep in the mountains with vats in its foundations. I thought they were for rendering tallow when I was a recruit." She swallowed. "They're not for tallow."

"Then why take the neighbor woman? Old Sorrel, with her charm for settling milk—she couldn't feed a vat."

"Every drop counts now. The winter's coming faster." Ilsa finally sat, on an upturned crate, her hands hanging between her knees like a soldier's at rest. "I deserted two weeks ago. Before that I spent four years riding with the writ-bearers, and I'll tell you what I learned: Aldery means to seal a new covenant before the old one fails entirely. A big one. Sealed properly, the way the first one was sealed." She paused. "With a sacrifice freely given, they say in the old texts, though none of them believe it. In practice, given."

The lamplight shivered. Somewhere above them the house creaked, settling, and Sable thought of the hollow space in his own memory, the door-shaped absence he had found the night the hunters came, and felt it ache like a tooth.

"And Wren?" he asked. "What is she?"

Maren and Ilsa exchanged a look over his head, the look of people who had already spoken of this while he slept.

"Bound," Maren said. "Old blood. Possibly the old blood. When I read her, under the burned village and the wolf-shadow, there was something else. Something that wasn't hers. Something *lent* to her." She leaned forward. "Aldery will want that child, Sable. Not eventually. Immediately. Whatever she is, whatever was put in her, it's worth more to him than this whole valley's worth of jars."

Sable looked at the sleeping girl. Winter in her hair, the villagers would have said, if the villagers had dared say anything anymore. She had fallen into his arms out of a burning dark, poured six years of terror into his skull unasked, and now the most powerful man in the kingdom wanted to open her up and pour her out.

"We leave tomorrow," he said. "Over the charmline. East is the keep, so we go west. Over the mountains."

"To the Wolf Court," Maren said softly, and when he stared at her: "Boy, I'm a broker. You think I don't recognize goods when I see them? I've held a memory of the Wolf Court for thirty years." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the deepest shelf, where one jar sat apart from all the others in a wooden cradle lined with felt. Its glow was different—not amber but silver-white, cold, like moonlight trapped in smoke.

"Who did this one belong to?" Sable whispered.

"No one living remembers," Maren said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They worked through the hours before dawn, and the work was a strange kind of prayer. Maren chose what to carry and what to bury; Sable packed the cradled jars in wool and straw; Ilsa went up and down the ladder with supplies, and somewhere in the third hour she stopped wearing the hunter's grey. She came down last in a plain brown coat taken from Maren's dead husband's chest, and she would not meet Sable's eyes, and he understood that she had buried something of herself upstairs along with the uniform, and said nothing.

Near morning, while the others argued about the pass routes, Sable stood alone before the crate with the royal seal. Eleven years of payments. Eleven years of his mentor's hands sealing wax by candlelight while he slept upstairs believing himself clean.

He should have hated her. He tried. He summoned the fury and held it up to the lamplight, and it was real—it burned in his throat like swallowed glass—but underneath it, unavoidable, lay the plain arithmetic he had lived by all his thieving life. *Take only what can be spared.* Maren had taken what could be spared, and what could not, and the difference between them was thinner than he wanted to look at. He had stolen wedding days. She had stolen childhoods. Both of them had eaten well.

Survival was never clean. The valley had always pretended otherwise, had wrapped its dullness in the language of innocence, and the pretending had purchased them nothing in the end but time—and time, it turned out, had also been bought from someone.

He touched the black wax seal once, feeling the slight tackiness of it, the impression of the crown's teeth, and then he nailed the lid down over it and carried the crate to the corner where the shadows were deepest.

When he turned, Maren was watching him from across the cellar, the silver-white jar cradled against her chest like an infant.

"There's one more thing you should know before we climb," she said. "About the payments. The crown didn't pay me in gold, mostly."

"In what, then?"

"Information. Answers." She came closer, and in the cold light of the jar her face looked suddenly very old, and very afraid—the first fear he had ever seen on her. "Eleven years, Sable. I asked them many questions. But there was one I asked every single time, and every single time they answered, and every answer was the same." She stopped in front of him. Up close, the jar's light fell through her fingers and turned them to ice. "I asked them: *who was the thief who stole the first name?* And they said: *a daughter of Hollowmere, and her line endures, and when the covenant cracks we will know it because the line will start to remember.*"

The cellar seemed to tilt. The jars swayed in his vision, hundreds of small lights, and through all of them Sable heard the sound of a door-shaped hollow opening inside his own skull.

"Your mother," Maren said gently, "came from an old family. And last week, boy—the night the hunters arrived—you started remembering."

Above them, faint and far, the house's front door splintered inward, and boots came down through the rooms like hail.
