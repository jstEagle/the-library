## 5. The Road Beyond the Charmline

Ilsa had crossed the charmline twice in her life: once at nine, when her mother carried her over to see a fair in the low country, and once at nineteen, when she marched out with eleven other girls to be sworn into the crown's hunt. Both times she had felt it—a sensation like walking through a curtain of cold water, the valley letting go of her the way a hand lets go of a bird.

The third time, she was the one doing the carrying. Not literally; Wren ran light as a deer beside her. But Ilsa kept one hand on the child's shoulder anyway, because the night was black as a closed eye and because letting go had never come easily to her.

Behind them, Hollowmere lay under a thin haze of chimney smoke and lamplight, innocent-looking as any sleeping thing. Ahead, the world waited, and Ilsa knew—she had known since Aldery's writ arrived—that the world had changed while the valley slept.

"Steady," Maren murmured from the rear, her voice a dry rustle. The old broker carried her ledger-satchel strapped across her chest like a breastplate, and Sable walked between them all with his head down, listening to the dark the way other people listened to music.

They crossed where the boundary stone had cracked years ago, at the sheep-path no warden bothered to watch. Ilsa went first. She braced for the cold-water shiver.

It did not come.

She stood on the far side of the line that had defined her whole life and felt—nothing. No seam. No letting-go. Just air, and frost forming on the grass in midsummer, and her own heart banging against her ribs like a fist on a door.

"The line's dead," she said quietly. "Or dying. It didn't—" Her voice failed, and she hated that. Twenty years a huntress, and her voice still betrayed her when it mattered. "It used to push back. Like passing through a river."

Sable crouched and laid his palm flat on the ground, on the border itself, straddling two worlds. His face did something complicated.

"There's still something here," he said. "But it's thin. Like ice you can see the water through." He looked up at Wren, who had gone very still, her white-streaked hair lifting slightly in a wind that touched nothing else. "Like something's calling through it."

"Then we answer quickly and loudly," Maren said, "and we do it from very far away. Move."

* * *

Beyond the charmline, summer had been murdered.

Ilsa had seen the lowlands in every season; she knew their rhythms the way a soldier knows a barracks. What lay before them now obeyed none of them. Wheat fields stood silvered and stiff, every stalk furred with rime, though the sky overhead held only early stars and no cloud. A orchard of plum trees had bloomed and frozen in the same night—blossoms encased in glass-clear ice, hanging like lanterns that would never light. When they passed through, the blossoms chimed faintly against each other, a sound like distant bells rung by no hand.

The cold was not weather. That was what unsettled her most. Weather moved, shifted, broke. This cold simply *was*, settled into the land like a tenant with a lease older than any king's. It came up out of the ground through the soles of her boots. It sat in her lungs with each breath, patient and vast.

"It's the covenant," Sable said. He had said it three times since dusk, and each time his voice had dropped lower, as if the words themselves were heavy. "It isn't just failing inside the valley. The valley is the last place it's holding. Out here it's already gone—and the land is grieving."

"Grieving," Maren repeated, skeptical, but her breath fogged strangely, hanging in the air long after it should have dispersed, curling into shapes almost like script before unraveling.

Wren had stopped shivering an hour ago. Ilsa noticed it the way she noticed everything about the child—noticed, catalogued, worried at it in the back of her skull like a loose tooth. Wherever Wren walked, the frost retreated a pace or two, grass showing green and soft in a narrow corridor that closed behind her. She left a wake of thaw, small but unmistakable, the way a lit candle leaves warmth on skin.

"She's bound to it," Sable said softly, watching the green path close behind the girl's heels. "I thought—I hoped I was wrong. But she's woven into the covenant somehow. That's why her memories came into me so violently. They weren't just memories. They were *evidence*."

Ilsa said nothing. She had spent her career learning what happened to people the crown decided were evidence. It was why she was here, walking away from everything, with frost climbing her ankles.

* * *

They made camp at midnight in the lee of a fallen barn, its roof beams snapped like a neck, hay inside stiff and glittering. No fire. Ilsa took first watch because taking first watch was the one part of her old self she intended to keep.

She sat with her back to a beam, sword across her knees, and let the others sleep: Maren curled around her satchel like a mother around an infant, which, Ilsa supposed, after thirty years of trading in stolen lives, it essentially was. Sable on his side, one arm flung toward Wren even in sleep, guarding without touching. And Wren herself, asleep sitting upright at first, then slowly tipping sideways until her head found Sable's shoulder.

Ilsa watched them and grieved.

Not for the valley—Hollowmere had never loved her, had always known what she trained to become, had watched her march out with that careful, ashamed politeness valleys reserve for their own weapons. She grieved for the girl she had been at nineteen, standing in the crown's courtyard with eleven others, swearing on a relic of salted iron to guard the covenant's peace. She had believed it, then. Believed the hunt was mercy: find the gifted, take them gently, keep the balance. It took her four years to learn what the taking actually fed, and eight more to work up the courage to desert, and by then the girl in the courtyard had been buried under so many requisition orders and quiet graves that digging her out seemed pointless.

So instead she had become very good at her work and very good at drinking and very good at not thinking, and she had told herself that when the day came—the real day, the one that mattered—she would know it, and she would act.

This was the day. Here was the acting. Four strangers and a child, fleeing across a freezing land, hunted by the order she had served half her life.

And the terrible truth, the one she turned over in her chest like a stone with a warm side, was that she had never felt more certain of anything. Not the oath. Not the campaigns. Nothing. Grief and resolve had grown into the same knot in her, and she could no longer tell where one ended and the other began.

A twig snapped, two hundred paces north.

Ilsa's whole body changed shape without her deciding it. She rose silent, drew the sword slow so it wouldn't sing, and moved into the frozen orchard with twenty years of craft carrying her feet.

* * *

There were three of them. She smelled the hunt before she saw it: oiled leather, charm-ash, and the particular sourness of men who had ridden hard and eaten worse. They had posted themselves along the trail her group would take at dawn—good discipline, she noted bitterly, her own doctrine, from her own training manual—and they crouched around a shuttered signal-lantern, whispering.

She should have circled back. Woken the others. Slipped east through the plums and lost them in the hills. That was the plan. That was the sensible soldier's choice.

Instead she stood in the dark and counted reasons to stay: if these three were here, there were more behind, and the more behind would ride her trail until they found it. Wren left thaw in her footsteps like a signature. You could not hide a girl who un-wintered the ground. Sooner or later—sooner, if the frost kept spreading—the child would lead every hunter in the province straight to them.

There was another way. It was the way she had sworn off in a chapel in Marrowfield seven years ago, both hands flat on cold stone, after Kettering. *No more of that kind of work,* she had promised a god she wasn't sure was listening. *I'll take them alive or not at all.*

The men whispered on, oblivious, planning how gently they would take the child. One laughed.

Ilsa thought of Wren asleep against Sable's shoulder, frost retreating from her small green wake, and understood that promises made to gods were cheaper than promises made to children, and that she had never really stopped being a weapon—she had only been waiting to choose her target.

She gave the god her apologies and moved.

The first man never knew. She took him from behind, blade-turn, blood-pipe pressed shut, lowering him into the frozen wheat like setting down something breakable. Alive. Still alive. The second turned at the wrong moment and caught her elbow across his throat, dropping gasping and wheezing into the rime—alive, winded, done. The third was faster, younger, already shouting, and his sword came out bright in the lantern-light, and for six seconds it was Marrowfield all over again, steel and breath and the old arithmetic of angles, and Ilsa's body remembered everything she wished it could forget.

She disarmed him. Broke his sword-hand doing it. Put him down in the wheat with her knee on his chest and her blade resting against the pulse in his throat, and held him there while he wept, young enough to weep, and she said, "You saw nothing tonight but shadows. You got lost. Do you understand? Say it."

"I'm lost," he sobbed. "Shadows. Nothing."

"Good lad." She struck the edge of her scabbard against his helm, once, precisely, and let darkness take him.

Three men breathing. Three families spared a folded flag. Kettering's ghost could rest one more night.

When she returned to the barn, Sable was awake, sitting up, Wren's head cradled carefully in his lap. He looked at the blood on her cuff—one smear, the first man's, unavoidable—and at her face, and asked nothing, which was the kindest question anyone had ever asked her.

"They'll be found by noon," she said. "Their horses will carry word of where they're not. We have until midday, maybe less. There's a larger party behind them—I heard it in the way they talked. They're not chasing thieves, Sable." Her voice came out flat and steady, and she was grateful for it. "They're chasing *her*. They called her the vessel."

Wren stirred in her sleep and did not wake, and the frost outside the broken barn pulled back another inch from the doorway where she lay, as if the land itself were leaning toward her.

Sable looked down at the child, then at Ilsa, and when he spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"Then we don't go around the mountains anymore," he said. "We go through the pass. To the shrine. There's a keeper there who owes Maren a reading—and I need to know what's hollow in my head before they catch up to us."

Above the pass, far off, lightning flickered inside a cloudless sky—silent, white, and shaped, Ilsa thought with a chill no frost could account for, remarkably like teeth.
