## 6. Salt on the Threshold

The mountain pass announced itself first by sound. The wind changed its mind up there, Sable noticed—down in the lowlands it had howled and worried at them like a dog on a rope, but as they climbed it went quiet and wide, a held breath, as though the whole white world were listening for footsteps.

They climbed for two days. Ilsa took point, her old hunter's instincts useless and indispensable at once: useless because no one had passed this way in weeks, indispensable because she read the snow the way Sable read faces, finding the firm ground beneath, the hidden crevasse, the place where the drift would take a man to the knee and keep him. Wren rode on Sable's back in a sling Maren had rigged from a broker's satchel, and the child weighed almost nothing, and weighed everything. She had stopped speaking after the escape from the valley. Not stopped—Sable corrected himself—she had stopped *wasting* speech. When she did talk, it came out in short coins, spent carefully.

"Salt," she said, on the evening of the second day, her chin hooked over his shoulder. "Smell it."

He couldn't. But an hour later they smelled it too, sharp and mineral under the snow-smell, and around a shoulder of black rock the shrine appeared.

It was smaller than Sable had imagined, though he hadn't known he was imagining it until he saw it. A ring of standing stones no taller than a man, half-buried in drift, and at the center a low stone house with a turf roof furred white, a single chimney letting up a thread of smoke so thin it seemed ashamed of itself. The door was old iron, and set into it, at the height of a kneeling person's forehead, was a basin of gray salt worn smooth as a riverstone by centuries of thumbs.

"Threshold shrine," Ilsa said, and her voice did something he'd never heard it do—softened. "Hunters used to pass these on the old roads. Before the writs. We were taught they were superstition." She looked at the basin a long moment. "We were taught a great deal that was superstition, it turns out."

Maren stamped snow off her boots and squinted at the door with the expression she wore when appraising a memory she intended to buy low. "Someone lives here. Smoke's fresh. Salt's fresh too—see the crust, it's been renewed within the season." She glanced at Sable. "Keepers don't renew salt for travelers they mean to turn away."

The door opened before anyone knocked.

The keeper was old in a way that had nothing to do with years—a weatheredness, a worn-through quality, like a coin rubbed past its face. Her eyes were closed and had the look of eyes long closed, lids settled into the sockets like stones into a streambed. She wore gray wool, unbleached, and her hands were white with salt to the wrist, as though she had been kneading it like bread.

"You brought the cold in with you," she said. Her voice was dry and even, unhurried. "And the child. And the huntress. And the broker." Her head turned, precise as a needle finding north, and stopped on Sable. "And you. Come in before the wind finishes deciding about you."

Inside, the shrine was one round room, warm from a hearth that burned something that gave off no smoke, only a light the color of late afternoon. Shelves lined the walls, and on the shelves sat not books but jars—hundreds of them, thousands, each filled not with liquid but with a faint, slow luminescence, like fireflies drowned in honey. Sable's gift woke in him unbidden, the way it always did near memory, and he felt the room *touch back*. Every jar was a memory. The shrine was an archive older and vaster than Maren's cellar, and it was not stolen. He could feel that too, the way you can feel that a house is loved rather than merely occupied.

"Sit," the keeper said. "I am called Vesper. I was called other things once, but the salt took them, and that is the way of it here."

They sat. Wren slid off Sable's back and, without ceremony, walked to the hearth and held her hands to it, and the light leaned toward her. Vesper's closed eyes followed the motion.

"Ah," Vesper said softly. "There she is."

"You know her?" Ilsa's hand had gone to her knife. It stayed there.

"I know what she is. I have known she was coming since the winter began early, since the charms along the charmline began to fail, since the wolves started singing in a language the old ones used." Vesper lowered herself onto a bench with the care of someone whose body had been negotiated with, not commanded. "But it is not her I need to see. It's you, thief."

Sable's mouth went dry. "I haven't stolen anything here."

"No." Vesper smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was the smile of someone about to do surgery. "That is the tragedy of you. You are the most honest thief in Hollowmere. You take only what can be spared. You leave the shape behind. You even leave a little of yourself in the taking—did you know that? Every memory you pull, you pay for it with a sliver of your own. That is why your gift works so cleanly. You are not a cutpurse, boy. You are a trader. The only one of your line who ever traded fairly."

"My line," Sable said. The words came out flat. "You don't know my line."

"I know your hollow." Vesper rose. "Everyone in this room knows your hollow. It has been sitting in you like a missing tooth, and you have all been too polite to mention it. The child flinches when she looks at you because she can see it. The huntress dreams about it. Even the broker—" a dry glance at Maren, who did not flinch, which was itself an answer— "has wondered. A hole in a man's memory is not like other holes. It is not empty. It is *shaped*. Something was taken to fit in it, and something else is holding the walls apart."

"I don't want—" Sable started.

"Salt," Vesper said, "on the threshold. That is the old rite. You kneel, you press your brow to the basin, and the salt reads what the flesh has hidden. I will not lie to you about what it costs. It does not take. It only *shows*. But showing is its own price, sometimes." She held out her salt-white hand. "You have spent your whole life walking past doors, boy. Here is one that opens inward."

He should have refused. He knew that even as he knelt, even as Maren's hand hovered near his shoulder and did not land, even as Wren turned from the hearth with her winter-colored eyes wide and her small fists clenched. He should have refused the way he had refused everything for twenty years—politely, carefully, keeping the walls of his small hidden life intact around the hollow at its center.

He pressed his brow to the salt.

* * *

The salt was cold, then warm, then it was not salt at all but a door.

He fell through his own head.

He stood in a kitchen he had never seen and had always known: low beams, a black iron kettle, a window with frost ferns on the glass and beyond the frost a garden buried in snow that fell upward. His mother was there. He knew her the way you know a song you've forgotten the words to—by its shape, its rise and fall. She was singing. She was packing a bundle with hands that shook. And she was saying a name over and over, low, like a spell, like a prayer, like a *warning*—

The salt pulled him deeper.

He saw a hearth vaster than any hearth, a ruin before it was a ruin, and around it figures in gray with salt on their hands, and among them a man whose face was Sable's face grown old, and the man was *screaming*, and what he screamed was not a word but a name—his own, being pulled out of him like a splinter, like a tooth, like a soul—and around the name the air bent and hardened and became a wall, a covenant, a great gray quiet laid over the valley of Hollowmere like a bandage over a wound that had never been allowed to close.

The covenant was not made of prayer. It was made of a name. His family's name, the first of them, given up—or *taken*, the difference mattered, the difference was the whole of it—stolen at the sealing, so that the magic bound beneath would have no mouth to speak through. And the line that carried the hollow in their memory carried the name's absence too, generation to generation, a door-shaped hole passed down like an heirloom, like a debt.

And the crown knew. The crown had always known. The witch-hunters were not hunting magic. They were hunting *him*—his blood, his line—because as long as the heirs of the stolen name lived and did not know what they carried, the covenant held, and the crown's power held with it. Every hunter's writ, every raid, every neighbor dragged from his bed for a gift no larger than a candle flame—they were not purges. They were dredging. Searching the valley's silt for one specific face.

The salt showed him one thing more: the wolf. A shadow with eyes like winter, standing at the edge of the first sealing, watching the name being taken, and not stopping it, and *remembering*.

Then he was on the floor of the shrine, on his hands and knees, salt scattered around him like a burst seam, and someone was holding him—Wren, her small arms around his neck, her forehead pressed to his, and through her he could feel her memory of this moment already forming, already offering itself to him, and he almost took it, almost let it flood in and carry him away from what he knew.

He pulled back. He would not take from her. Not even comfort. Not even now.

* * *

They gave him tea that tasted of iron and pine. He sat with his hands around the cup and told them, because they had earned it, all of it: the kitchen, the mother, the hearth, the screaming man with his face. The name pulled out like a tooth. The covenant built from the absence. The hunt that had never been a hunt.

When he finished, the silence had a texture, like felt.

"So," Maren said at last, and her voice was carefully, deliberately light, the voice she used over a bargaining table when the stakes had gone past bargaining. "The crown has spent three hundred years looking for you."

"For my line." He heard his own voice as if from a distance. "For the name. They don't want to kill us, Maren. They want to *keep* us. Ignorant. Contained. A name in a vault doesn't work if the vault knows it's a vault." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I've spent my whole life being careful. Small. Hidden. I thought I was good at it." He looked at his hands, the thief's hands, quick and clever and suddenly foreign. "I wasn't hiding. I was being *kept*. The whole valley was the vault. The covenant was the lock. And I—" his voice failed and he started again— "I am the theft. That's what I am. Not a man with a hole in him. The hole *is* the inheritance. I'm what's left after they took the first of us."

"No," Wren said.

Everyone turned. She had not spoken in two days except in those short coins. Now she climbed onto the bench beside him, took his face in her small cold hands, and looked into his eyes with the terrible directness of children and the very old.

"You are not the hole," she said. "I know holes. I *am* one. A hole is what's left when something is taken." Her grip tightened. "You are what they couldn't take. That's why they're still looking."

Sable could not speak. Vesper saved him from trying.

"The child is right, which will annoy her when she's older." The keeper's closed eyes turned toward the door, toward the pass, toward the peaks beyond where the wind had begun to rise again, singing low in its throat. "But hear the rest, because the salt showed me your face while you slept in it, and I know what comes next. The covenant is failing. The winter is its death rattle. And there are two ways it ends. The crown's way, which is a new sealing with a new stolen name—there is a reason they want the child, boy, and it is not mercy—and there is the old way. The way of return. Give the name back. Give the first memories back. Freely, at the ruined hearth, before the wolf who witnessed the taking." She rose, salt sifting from her sleeves. "The Wolf Court is three days' climb. And you should know—the hunters are two days behind you, and they are not following your tracks."

Ilsa went very still. "Then what are they following?"

Vesper's head turned, needle-finding-north, and stopped on Wren.

"The child's singing," she said. "She has been singing in her sleep since the valley. And the wolves have been answering."
