## 7. Court of Teeth and Snow

They came for them at dusk, when the snow had turned the color of old bone and the wind had stopped pretending to be anything but a blade.

Sable heard the hunters before he saw them—not footsteps, which the snow swallowed, but the sound of their charms: a thin, insectile ringing, like glass teeth grinding. He had learned to hate that sound across three days of flight. It meant the charmline was far behind them. It meant the world outside Hollowmere had no intention of letting them go.

"Down," Ilsa breathed, and pulled Wren into the lee of a fallen pine.

Too late. The lanterns bloomed through the trees—six of them, then ten, cold white flames that cast no warmth and made no shadows. Sable felt his gift rise instinctively, reaching for the nearest mind the way a hand reaches for a banister in the dark—

—and met iron.

The memory of it would stay with him: the sensation of his fingers closing on a door that was not there. A hunter stepped from between the trees, young, freckled, almost apologetic, holding up a medallion of black salt and silver. "Nullstone," she said, as if apologizing for the weather. "You'll want to stop trying. It hurts less."

Ilsa moved anyway. Of course she did. Sable saw her weight shift, saw the knife leave her sleeve, saw the whole swift geometry of violence assemble itself in the space of a heartbeat—and then two hunters had her arms and a third pressed nullstone to her temple and she went slack as a cut marionette, breathing hard, eyes furious and alive.

"Don't," Sable said, to all of them, to no one. His hands were up. Wren's small fingers had found his coat hem and closed on it with terrible strength.

They bound his wrists with cord woven through with salt. They did not bind Wren at all. That frightened him more than anything else—the way they looked at her, the way they made a kind of loose ring around her, careful, reverent, the way men handle something already promised to someone else.

* * *

Wren did not cry. She had decided some time ago—Sable could see it in the set of her jaw, in the way she catalogued every lantern, every face—that crying was a door she had burned behind her in another village, in another life, in the life that lived now inside Sable's skull where it had no right to be.

She walked because they made her walk, but she counted things as she went. Forty-one paces between the trees where Ilsa fell and the treeline's end. Nine hunters. One wagon, drawn by horses whose breath steamed red in the lantern light. She counted because counting was a wall she could put between herself and the wolf-shaped shadow that had followed her out of her burning village and had never, in all her remembered years, left her alone.

And she watched Sable. Because he had her memories. Because when she closed her eyes at night she could feel them sitting inside him like stones in a riverbed, worn smooth by his keeping, and it was the strangest thing in the world to be lonely for your own past while it slept in someone else's chest.

The road climbed. The winter deepened until it stopped being weather and became architecture—snow walls taller than a man, blue-shadowed, singing faintly when the wind passed over them. And then the trees ended, and the court was there.

It had been a fortress once. Now it was mostly teeth: spires of ice-sheathed stone jutting at crooked angles, gargoyle faces melted and refrozen so many times they looked less carved than gnawed. Banners hung from the gatehouse, stiff with frost, bearing a sigil Wren didn't know—a grey tower on a white field—but which made Sable make a small sound beside her, low in his throat, like a man recognizing a debt.

Inside, the courtyard blazed with light. Fires in iron braziers, a long table laid with food no one ate, and at its head a chair that might once have been a throne, and in the chair a man who rose as they were brought in, unhurried, as though they were guests arriving late to dinner.

Lord Aldery was not what Wren expected. She had imagined cruelty wearing armor. Instead there was a tall, tired man in a scholar's grey robe, silver at his temples, with the kind of face that had once been kind and had been used too long for other purposes. His eyes found Wren first. Then Sable. Then, briefly, Ilsa—and something flickered there, recognition, regret, filed away.

"The Zhou boy," Aldery said. His voice was quiet, and the courtyard was quiet enough that everyone heard it. "Do you know how long I have looked for your line?"

"I'm not—" Sable started.

"You are." Aldery descended the dais steps. Up close, Wren could see the exhaustion in him, deep as mining shafts. "You have a hollow place in your memory, shaped like a door. You've carried it all your life and told yourself it was nothing. Am I wrong?"

Sable said nothing. Which was answer enough.

Aldery gestured, and the hunters brought Maren forward—from a separate wagon, Sable realized; she'd been taken hours before them, or days—and his heart lurched sideways in his chest. Her wrists were bound. Her eyes, when they found his, were dry and appraising even now, even here, taking inventory of the court, the guards, the exits, pricing everything.

"They held the gate," Aldery said. "She bought you time. I want you to understand that I know exactly what each of you is worth, and I am not your enemy, thief. I am the last man doing an ugly arithmetic honestly."

He led them—not dragged, led, which was worse—to the table and turned back the cloth covering something at its center.

Beneath lay a map of the valley, rendered in silver wire and crushed glass, and at its heart a shape that pulsed faintly, slowly, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"The covenant," Aldery said. "You've felt it dying. Don't pretend otherwise—the valley has forgotten how to be warm. Every year the winter comes earlier and leaves later. Every year another hearth goes cold that no fire will relight. Your brokers sell memories, your hunters harvest blood, and none of it matters, because the covenant was sealed wrong from the beginning. Sealed with a theft." His gaze settled on Sable, heavy as snowfall. "Sealed with a name that was taken rather than given. And a covenant built on stolen goods does not hold forever. It is failing, Zhou. This—" he touched the map, and the pulse stuttered, and every person in the courtyard flinched without knowing why— "is what failure looks like. Not fire. Winter. Slowly, and then all at once."

"And you mean to fix it," Sable said. His voice came out flat.

"I mean to seal a new one. Properly. Strongly." Aldery's eyes went to Wren, and Wren felt it like a hand on the back of her neck. "The child is bound to the old rite. Burned village, witnessed shadow, blood that quiets the cold. She is the key that fits the lock. With her freely—" a pause, delicate, obscene— "*given*, the new covenant could stand a thousand years."

"She's eight," Ilsa said, from between her guards. Her voice was gravel. "You're going to dress murder in consent."

"I am going to save a million lives with one," Aldery said, and for one vertiginous moment Wren believed he believed it, believed he had repeated it to himself so many times it had become true as stone. "You would spend her instead? Let the winter take valley after valley until there is nothing left to save? Choose, huntress. You of all people know arithmetic."

Wren did not look at Aldery. She looked at Sable, because he had her memories and therefore knew—knew about the wolf-shaped shadow, knew about the night the village burned, knew the thing she had never said aloud to anyone: that she had always suspected she was kept alive for a reason, and had feared, all her life, that the reason was this.

Sable's face was grey. But he met her eyes. And he did not look away.

"My lord," Sable said, turning to Aldery, and his voice did something clever—it shook, just enough, just enough to read as fear rather than performance. "You want the original sealing. The truth under the theft. I can get it for you."

Aldery went very still. "The hollow in your memory."

"A door," Sable agreed. "And I'm the only key that opens it. Kill me and it dies with me. Take the girl tonight and you get a covenant built on another theft—another failing thing, another winter waiting to happen. Or—" he let it hang, let the merchant's silence do its work, the silence Maren had taught him was worth more than any words— "you give us one night. Unbound. Fed. All of us. In the morning I open my own memory, and you'll have the original rite, the true terms, everything your scholars have killed for."

The courtyard held its breath. Somewhere a brazier popped and spat.

"And if you simply run?" Aldery asked softly.

"Then you lose the only living heir of the sealing line," Sable said, "and the winter takes everything, including your arithmetic." He spread his bound hands, an offer, a surrender, a lie. "One night, my lord. I don't run. I've spent my whole life learning what can be spared and what can't. I'm telling you—" and here, horribly, it stopped being a lie— "this is the one thing that can't."

Maren made a sound, half laugh, half sob. Aldery studied Sable for a long moment, and Wren understood suddenly that this man had spent years among thieves and brokers and liars, and recognized the craft when he saw it, and respected it, and was going to allow himself to be fooled because the alternative was admitting there was no honest choice left anywhere in the world.

"One night," Aldery said. "Guests' quarters. Guarded. At dawn, Zhou, you and I open that door together." He turned away, then paused. "For what it is worth—I am sorry it comes to this. It always comes to this. That is the lesson of covenants."

* * *

The room they gave them had a real fire, and bread, and a window of thick glass through which the snow fell upward sometimes, caught in the court's strange winds. The guards outside the door were polite. That was the worst part. Politeness meant certainty.

Ilsa sat against the wall working at her bonds with a patience that suggested she'd been untying knots since before Sable was born. Maren stood at the window, saying nothing, watching the upward snow. And Wren sat on the edge of the bed, swinging her feet, looking at Sable.

"You have them still," she said. "My memories."

Sable went still. "Yes."

"The burning part too."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, absorbing this, the way she absorbed everything—with a feral, unhurried gravity no eight-year-old should own. "Does it hurt? When you remember my village?"

"It hurts," Sable admitted.

"Good," Wren said. "Not—" quickly, seeing his face— "not because I want you hurt. Because it means you're keeping it properly. Like a grave. You don't build a grave that doesn't hurt to visit."

Sable laughed, or something near it, broken at the edges. "Wren. What Aldery wants to do—"

"I know what he wants to do." She looked at her hands, small and chapped in the firelight. "I've known since the wolves. Since before. Everyone keeps me like a thing that's going to be spent. You did too, at first. On the road. When you thought I was asleep, you and Maren talked about whether I was worth the trouble." She said it without accusation, which was somehow unbearable. "But you're the only one who ever gave anything back."

"Wren—"

"When we get out of here," she said, "you should give them back. My memories. All of them. Even the burning ones." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and grey as winter water. "Because then it won't be stealing anymore. Either direction. And maybe—" her voice wavered, just once, the only crack in her all night— "maybe then somebody will actually remember me, instead of just carrying me around."

Sable crossed the room and knelt, so their eyes were level, and took her small hands in his bound ones. Outside, the snow fell upward past the glass, and somewhere below them the covenant's pulse stuttered again, slower now, a heart making up its mind.

"I remember you," he said. "That's the first thing I'll give back. And Wren—when dawn comes, whatever happens, whatever Aldery thinks he's buying—one night is enough. It's always been enough. Watch me work."

From the wall, Ilsa's knife finally parted the last strand of salt-cord, and she flexed her freed fingers and smiled like a wolf.

"One night," she said. "Then we'd best not waste it."
