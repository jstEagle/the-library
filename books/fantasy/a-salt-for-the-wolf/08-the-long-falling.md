## 8. The Long Falling

The gate was a wound in the world.

Sable had thought of it as a door for so long—the hollow in his memory shaped like a door, the charmline like a threshold—but standing before it now, in the blue hour between midnight and the storm, he understood it was neither. It was a scar. The covenant had been cut into the valley's skin generations ago, and everything since had been the land trying to close around it and failing, failing, failing. The snow that fell here fell wrong: upward, sideways, hanging in the air like a held breath. Beyond the gate the true winter waited, deep and patient as a debt.

Maren stood at its edge with her ledger under one arm and a lantern in her hand, and she looked smaller than Sable had ever seen her. Smaller, and older, and utterly unafraid.

"You're stalling," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"You're stalling with extra steps." She pressed the ledger into his hands. It was heavier than it should have been—paper and leather and forty years of other people's yesterdays. "The north road to the Wolf Court is three days' walk if the weather holds, which it won't. The pass shrine is your first stop; the keeper will feed you whether you deserve it or not. And when you get there—"

"Maren."

"—when you get there, you do not bargain. You do not trade. You have spent your whole life selling pieces of yourself because you thought they were all you were worth. You are done selling. Do you hear me? What you give at that hearth, you give free, or it means nothing."

Snow gathered on her shoulders and did not melt. That was the covenant dying, he realized. Even inside the valley, even on hallowed ground, the cold had begun to reach through.

"Ilsa's watching the treeline," he said. "Wren's asleep against a root. We could still all go together. We could find another way through."

"There is no other way through. Aldery's riders hold the low passes, and the gate will only open once tonight—the old magic thins at moonset, one breath of an opening, no more." She smiled, and it was her merchant's smile, the one she used when the price was going to hurt. "One person holds the gate open from this side. One person walks through and closes it behind them. I've been doing arithmetic my whole life, boy. This sum comes out the way it comes out."

"Then let me stay. I'm faster than you. I know the gate's workings better—"

"You know nothing about the gate. You know what I taught you, which is what I needed you to know." Her voice gentled, which was worse than anger. "And you are the one who has to go. Whatever's sleeping in that hole in your head, it belongs on that mountain. I've carried it long enough. Carried it, and sold around its edges, and hated myself for both." She touched his cheek with a hand gone rough and cold. "You were never merchandise, Sable. I just couldn't afford to remember that out loud."

Behind them, up the slope, Wren stirred in her sleep and made a small sound—a child's sound, wordless—and Maren looked past him toward it, and something in her face broke and reformed into resolve.

"Go," she said. "Hold my lantern while I work."

* * *

He held the lantern. It was such a small thing to be asked, and it would be the last thing she ever asked of him, and he held it with both hands like a relic while she knelt at the gate's edge and began to speak words he had never heard her use—not the market cant of memory-brokers, not the valley dialect, but something older, syllables that made the snow shiver and the lantern flame lean toward her like a listener.

The gate opened.

Not visibly—that was the horror of it. There was no swinging of doors, no parting of mist. One moment the air beyond the charmline was a wall, dense and refusing, and the next it was simply *air*, and Sable could see the black pines of the outer dark, and the wind came through carrying a cold that bit down to the marrow of meaning itself.

"Go," Maren said again, and this time there was no smile.

Ilsa came down the slope with Wren half-carrying the girl, who woke confused and clutching the huntress's cloak, and they passed through the opening one by one—Ilsa first, blade drawn, scanning the dark with a soldier's flat attention; then Wren, who turned at the threshold and looked back at Maren with those winter-pale eyes and said, in her small cracked voice, "Come too."

"Soon," Maren lied, beautifully, effortlessly, the way she'd lied to buyers for decades. "Someone has to mind the shop."

Wren looked at her a moment longer. Children always knew. Then Ilsa's hand closed on the girl's shoulder and drew her into the dark, and Sable stepped backward over the line with the ledger hugged to his chest, and the last thing he saw on the valley side of his whole life was Maren Zhou—no relation, whatever she'd claimed—kneeling at the gate with her palms flat against the frozen ground, holding it open by main strength and old knowledge, her lips moving, her eyes finding his.

"Free," she mouthed. "Give it free."

Then the wind rose, and the storm arrived all at once like an army cresting a ridge, and the gate snapped shut behind them with a sound like a great book closing, and Sable was on the far side of the world he'd been born in, and Maren was on the near side of it, alone.

* * *

They made perhaps two miles before the horns found them.

The storm was Aldery's ally or Aldery's instrument—Sable no longer believed there was a difference—and it swallowed their tracks and their breath and their sense of direction in equal measure. Ilsa led by dead reckoning and instinct, one arm around Wren, shouting directions that the wind tore apart mid-word. Sable followed the sound of her voice and the weight of the ledger and nothing else. The world had contracted to white and roaring.

The horns came again, closer, and beneath them the baying of dogs that were not entirely dogs.

"They've flanked us," Ilsa shouted. "Riders on the ridge—they'll cut the road ahead. We can't outrun them in this."

"Then we hide—"

"There's nowhere to hide! There hasn't been anywhere to hide since we crossed the line!" She stopped, and Wren collided with her legs, and for a moment the three of them stood in the white roar like trees, and Sable watched Ilsa's face do the arithmetic Maren had done at the gate. He watched her arrive at the same answer.

"No," he said.

"The ravine back there—the one we crossed. If someone holds the far bank, makes noise, gives them a chase—" 

"Ilsa, no—"

"Someone," she said, over him, over the wind, "has to get this child to that mountain. That's the whole mission now. Everything else is decoration." She crouched, took Wren's face in her gloved hands, and said something to her that Sable couldn't hear—something that made the girl nod, solemn as a small judge. Then Ilsa pressed her knife into Sable's hand, handle-first, an act so intimate it hurt more than any blow. "You'll need this more than I will. Don't die cleverly. Die slowly, if you have to die, and make them regret the weather."

She kissed Wren's hair. She gripped Sable's shoulder hard enough to bruise. Then she turned downhill into the storm, toward the horns, drawing her second blade as she went, and the whiteness took her the way the sea takes a swimmer, and the baying shifted, and chased her, and faded.

Sable stood holding a knife and a child's hand and a dead woman's ledger, and the mountain waited above him through the snow, vast and indifferent, and he began to climb.

* * *

They found the shrine keeper's hut at dawn, or what passed for dawn—a thin brightening of the gray, the storm easing to a sullen fall. The hut was empty, the keeper gone or dead or never there at all; only the smell of old incense and a single lamp still burning, which should have been impossible and was therefore a kind of welcome. Wren curled on the stone floor and slept the way children sleep after terror: instantly, totally, her fist knotted in Sable's sleeve.

He sat against the wall with the ledger on his knees and tried not to think, and failed.

*Maren taken.* He had seen it in the storm's aftermath, in the way the horns had changed their note—from pursuit to triumph, from hunting to herding. They had her. Aldery had her. And every hour since the gate had closed, Sable had felt the hollow in his own memory ache like a tooth, like a wound, like a door rattling in wind.

Because that was the truth the blind keeper had given him at the pass shrine, days ago, in a voice like dry leaves: *the first covenant was sealed with a stolen name.* His ancestor's name. Stolen, and buried, and hunted ever after, generation upon generation, until the theft had trickled down the bloodline to a boy who grew up stealing memories because some part of him recognized the family trade.

He set down the ledger. He put his hands flat on his own temples, foolishly, as if the hollow were a place hands could reach.

And he opened the door.

It did not resist. That was the terrible grace of it—it had never been locked, only shut, and all these years he had kept it shut himself, the way you keep shut the door to a room where someone died. It swung inward on silence, and the silence filled him.

His mother's voice came first. Not words—just voice, the hum she made when she worked, weaving rush-lights by the window of a house he suddenly remembered room by room: the crooked shelf, the jar of river-stones, the wolf carved from black wood on the mantel that he had always thought was a toy. Her name surfaced like a body rising in still water. *Yeva.* Yeva Zhou, who had sung to him in the old syllables, the ones Maren had spoken at the gate, because theirs was the family that remembered what remembering cost.

Then the rest came, and it came like the storm.

He was four years old, maybe five, hiding under the table—why was he hiding?—and men in gray stood in the doorway, and their leader wore a crown's badge, and his mother stood between them and the mantel with her arms spread, and she was saying, *it ends with me, take me instead,* and they laughed, gently, horribly, the way powerful men laugh. Because it didn't end with her. It ended with whoever came after. The covenant was failing even then—had always been failing, a seal made of theft can only decay—and the crown's solution was not to repair it but to prune it, to keep the thief's line small and frightened and ignorant, one survivor each generation, raised blind, so that no one would ever grow strong enough to return what was taken.

They took his mother that night. He remembered the cart wheels in the lane. He remembered the wolf-shadow rising off the mantel carving, huge and silent, pressing against the window glass like a face against a sickroom window, unable to cross, bound to witness and forbidden to act—the covenant's own jailer, the covenant's own prisoner.

And he remembered the last thing, the thing the hollow had been shaped around, the thing he had been made to forget: his mother kneeling before they took her, holding his face, whispering the old syllables into his ear—not a spell but a message, folded small, addressed to whoever he might become:

*If you ever find your way home, give it back. Not sold. Not traded. Given free. A salt for the wolf, my love. Only a gift unbinds what a theft has bound.*

Sable came back to himself on the floor of the empty hut with tears freezing on his face and Wren's hand in his hair—when had she woken?—and the girl looking at him with her winter eyes, and she said, softly, "You cried out. You said a name. It wasn't yours."

"It was mine," he said. "It was my mother's. It's the same thing now."

Outside, the storm had stopped. In the ringing quiet, down the mountainside, torches were climbing—many torches, unhurried, in no formation a fleeing fugitive would use. Riders who already knew where he was. Riders who had let him run this far because Aldery wanted him at the Wolf Court, wanted the last of the thief's blood delivered to the ruined hearth for the new sealing, wanted Wren beside him as the sacrifice.

Ilsa wasn't coming back. Maren wasn't coming back. There was no one left between Sable and the court of teeth and snow but himself, a child, a knife, and a name he had only just learned how to say.

He wiped his face. He picked up Wren. He walked out to meet the torches, because the mountain lay beyond them, and the hearth lay beyond the mountain, and somewhere ahead a wolf who had watched his mother be taken was still waiting, patient as a debt, to see what he would give.
