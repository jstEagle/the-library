## 1. The Silent Tithe

There are three rules to guiding a caravan through the Thornfell passes, and I've broken all of them at least once. The first is: never trust a bridge you haven't walked yourself. The second is: never tell the merchants anything they don't need to know, because merchants talk the way water leaks—slowly, everywhere, and always downhill toward trouble. The third is: never let anyone see what you can do.

I was thinking about rule one when I stopped the column at the mouth of the Kestrel Narrows, because the bridge ahead had started lying to me.

Not out loud. Bridges don't talk. But every span in these mountains is anchored by an oath-stone—a block of grey granite cut with a covenant sigil, sworn into service by some mason's grandfather back when the god of oaths still bothered to answer people. A healthy stone hums. Not a sound you hear so much as feel, low in the teeth, like a promise being kept on schedule. A dying stone stutters.

This one was stuttering like a drunk swearing off drink.

"Problem, Eriksen?" Doss Halloway rode up beside me, all elbows and suspicion, the way he did whenever I stopped moving for longer than it took to spit. He's the kind of man who thinks competence is a trick someone's playing on him, which is fair, because in my case it usually is.

"Bridge looks tired," I said. "We'll take the riverbed."

"The riverbed?" He squinted past me at the crossing. It looked fine. That was the whole art of it—it always looked fine right up until the moment it dropped forty feet of mule, cargo, and screaming man into the white water below. "That bridge has stood since my grandfather ran this route."

"That's exactly how long bridges get to stand," I said. "They don't stand forever just because your grandfather liked them."

He chewed on that. Doss chews on everything, including tobacco he can't afford and insults he doesn't understand. "The riverbed adds half a day."

"Half a day beats a funeral. Funerals cost more in lost cargo than time." I turned my horse before he could argue further, which is most of leadership, if you're honest about it—the turning away part. "Kess! Take point down the shingle. Watch for soft ground where the water runs shallow."

Kess saluted with two fingers and a grin. She's nineteen and thinks I'm the best trail-guide in the Thornfells, which is the most dangerous thing about her. Give a young person a hero and they start expecting miracles. I've spent thirty years making sure nobody expects those from me.

Behind me, the caravan groaned into motion—six wagons, twenty-two mules, eleven men and women who smelled of tar and cheap brandy and the particular desperation of people smuggling wool past a crown tariff. Good people, mostly. Liars, all of them, but honest liars, the kind who lie only about cargo. I fit in perfectly. I lie about cargo too.

I also lie about myself, but that's not cargo. That's ballast.

* * *

Here's what I didn't tell Doss, because telling him would have meant explaining, and explaining would have meant the gallows by spring:

I put my hand flat against the oath-stone as we passed it, the way any pious traveler might touch it for luck, and I read it. Not the carving—anyone can read the carving, the old covenant-script that says *bear weight, keep faith, fail never*. I read the oath underneath. Every oath has a shape. Kept oaths are smooth, worn round like river stones. Broken ones have edges. This one had edges everywhere, hairline cracks running through the vow like frost through window glass, and at the center of it, a hollow where the binding voice should have been—and wasn't.

The stone hadn't failed yet. It was failing. There's a difference, and the difference was about four days, or one heavily loaded wagon train, whichever came first.

Any hedge-priest could have done the same reading, once. Any village elder with a blessing-book. The gift of hearing oaths isn't rare, exactly—it's just rare now, because the crown decided three years ago that gifts were treason, and everyone who could hear a promise being kept either registered, fled, or burned. Mostly burned. Registration is just burning with paperwork.

So I touched the stone like a man asking luck of it, nodded gravely at nothing, and told no one that I'd just saved their lives. That's the tithe I pay my silence. Every day, a small salvation, unclaimed.

You'd think it would add up to something. It doesn't. It adds up to nothing, same as coins thrown down a well. But I keep throwing them.

* * *

The riverbed route was ugly going. Shingle to the knees in places, the Kestrel running loud and cold on our left, the canyon walls leaning in like gossips. By midday the mules were miserable, the merchants were miserable, and Kess had found a dead elk wedged in a scree fan, which she declared "a sign of good luck," because nineteen-year-olds will declare anything good luck if it means avoiding the alternative interpretation.

I rode at the rear, which is where guides belong. Front is for glory. Rear is for counting heads and noticing things.

What I noticed was the sky.

Thornfell weather comes down the passes like an unpaid debt collector—you hear about it long before it arrives, and then suddenly it's standing in your doorway. There'd been cloud building over the western peaks since dawn, high and hard-edged, the kind that means snow above five thousand feet by nightfall. We'd be at the Greyford road by then if the riverbed held. If it didn't, we'd be camping under it, and the first rule of camping under early snow in the Thornfells is: don't.

I caught up with Doss at the head of the line. "Storm coming down the west faces. We push through the Narrows before dark or we winter here early."

"You said the riverbed costs half a day."

"It costs half a day in summer. In October it costs whatever the sky says it does." I let him do his chewing. "Move the heavy wagons up front. Lighter teams break through soft ground easier behind."

He grumbled but did it, because Doss understands arithmetic even when he distrusts everything else. And I fell back to the rear again, and did the thing I do every night, the thing that has become less a prayer than a habit, the way biting your nails is a habit, the way checking a lock you know is locked is a habit.

I prayed.

It goes like this: *Keeper of the Kept Word. You who hear every promise made under heaven. I have kept faith. Keep me.*

Thirty years ago, that prayer got answered. Twenty years ago, it got answered sometimes, faintly, like a voice calling from another room. Ten years ago it stopped entirely. No thunder, no rebuke, no sign of displeasure. Just—nothing. The particular nothing of an empty house. I've knocked on that door every day for ten years, and every day the silence answers, polite and total, and I say *thank you* anyway, because what else is there? You don't stop praying to a god just because he's stopped listening. That's not faith, exactly. It's more like loyalty to a friend who's moved away and left no forwarding address.

Some nights I wonder if he's dead. Gods die; ask any of the old river cults, ask the sun-temples of the coast, ask the empty shrines in every pass in the Thornfells where the covenant stones stand carved and cold. Some nights I wonder if he ever listened at all, if the early answers were coincidence wearing a divine coat. And some nights—the worst nights—I wonder if he heard me clearly all along and simply found me wanting, and the silence is not absence but judgment, delivered in the only language left to him.

Those nights I don't sleep much. But I'm very good at pretending I did. Invisibility is mostly theater, and I've had decades of practice.

* * *

We made the Narrows mouth by late afternoon, and that's where the second stone found me.

There's a wayshrine there—old work, older than the smuggler roads, older than the crown probably. A covenant arch straddling the trail, two pillars and a lintel, every surface crowded with oath-sigils: safe passage, true weights, shelter for the storm-bound, protection of children on the road. Whole generations of travelers had sworn themselves into that arch. It should have sung like a bell.

It whispered. Barely. One thread of sound left in it, thin as wire, and when I trailed my fingers along the pillar under the cover of steadying my saddlebag, I felt the shape of the failure—and it stopped me cold.

Oaths fail in patterns. A bridge fails from load. A boundary-stone fails from neglect. But this arch had been *unpicked*. Deliberately, carefully, the way you'd unpick a seam to steal what's sewn inside it. Someone—or something—had gone through the covenant-work and drawn the binding voices out of the stone, leaving the words behind like shed skins. Words without vows. Carvings without commitment. A shrine-shaped hole in the world.

And the one thread that remained, the last little vow still clinging to its groove, was a child-protection oath—*shelter the small, guard the young on the road*—and it was fraying too, unraveling even as I knelt there, its edges dissolving into something that felt less like decay and more like... harvest. Like someone had gotten to it and simply run out of time, or appetite.

I stood there with my hand on cold granite while the caravan clattered past me, and for the first time in ten years of silence, I felt something answer back. Not a voice. Nothing so kind. Just a pressure, deep under the mountain's bones, like a held breath. Like something enormous trying to speak through several feet of rock and a great deal of distance, and managing only a vibration, a hum in the marrow—

Then it was gone, and the silence closed over it, and I was a middle-aged man kneeling in the dirt of a smuggler road looking like a fool.

"Eriksen." Kess had doubled back. She was watching me with that open, worried face. "You all right?"

"Checking the shrine," I said. "Old stones. They settle."

"They're beautiful." She reached up and touched the lintel, and I almost grabbed her wrist, almost told her to stop—but there was nothing left in it to hurt her. That was the whole horror of it. Nothing left to hurt anyone. "My gran used to say the old god lived in these. That if you swore something under an arch, it stayed sworn."

"What do you say?"

She grinned. "I say gran liked a good story." She trotted back up the line, and I watched her go, and I thought: *so do I. So do I, girl. It's the only thing keeping any of us upright.*

But the pressure under the mountain didn't feel like a story. It felt like a prisoner tapping on a wall.

* * *

We cleared the Narrows as the light went copper, and the snow came down the west faces right on schedule, fat and silent, dusting the shale white. By full dark we were camped in a fold of the hills with the wagons circled and the mules hobbled, and the storm hissed against the canvas, and eleven liars slept around a low fire while I took first watch, because I always take first watch. First watch is where invisible men live.

I sat with my back to a wagon wheel and my hands around a cup of tea gone bitter, and I did the accounting I do every night. The bridge: saved. The reroute: held. The storm: outrun by an hour, maybe two. Nobody thanked me. Nobody knew. The caravan would reach Greyford tomorrow with its cargo and its skins intact, and Doss Halloway would pay me my fee and suspect me mildly the entire time, and I would take the money and vanish down the next road, and none of them would ever know how many times the quiet man at the rear had quietly kept them alive.

Loneliness disguised as competence. I've worn it so long I've forgotten if there's a face under it.

*Keeper of the Kept Word,* I prayed, into the snowfall, into the dark, into the ten-year silence. *Something down there is unpicking your work. Something under the mountain is harvesting oaths like a farmer harvests wheat, and today I felt you tap on the wall. If you can hear me—if you've ever been able to hear me—tap again.*

Nothing. Snow, wind, the mules shifting in their sleep. The fire popping its small dishonest comforts.

I poured out the tea and pulled my blanket up and told myself, as I do every night, that the silence was answer enough—that a god who needed his people deaf and blind and unmarked was a god worth staying quiet for. That invisibility was devotion. That the tithe was accepted.

In the morning we came down out of the high country onto the Greyford road, and the first thing we saw was smoke—not chimney smoke, but a black, greasy column rising from the town's market square, and riders in crown colors at the gate, and a crier's bell ringing out across the fields with a sound I felt in my teeth like a stuttering oath-stone.

Kess reined in beside me, squinting. "What's all that, then?"

I knew. Before I heard a single word of it, I knew, the way you know a storm's smell before the first drop lands. Three years the hunt had burned through the lowlands and the coastal towns, and I'd told myself the mountains were too poor, too far, too mean to bother with.

The bell rang again. The crown had finally come to Thornfell.

"Trouble," I said, and gave the mules my heels, and tried very hard not to think about the fact that the only person in the world whose letters I still answered lived in Greyford, and her name was Wren.
