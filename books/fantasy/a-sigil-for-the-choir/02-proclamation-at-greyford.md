## 2. Proclamation at Greyford

Greyford smelled the same as it always did: wet wool, woodsmoke, and the river's green rot, all of it layered over a base note of honest desperation. I'd been coming down out of the Thornfell passes into this town for eleven years, and in all that time the only thing that had ever changed was the price of ale and the names of the people pretending not to know me.

I told myself it was a good sign when nothing changed.

The caravan had dissolved at the pass mouth the way caravans do—Hesk taking his bolts of cloth toward the coast road, the two Vellish brothers vanishing before anyone could ask them to share the cost of a room, old Marta driving her cart of dried fish down the hill with the grim satisfaction of a woman who has successfully transported her cargo past every danger and now intends to enjoy complaining about the price of salt. I collected my fee from Hesk with the usual ritual: he counted it twice, complained about it once, and shook my hand like he was checking whether I'd stolen any fingers.

"Same time next season?" he asked.

"If the mountain allows."

"The mountain always allows. It's the men on it you have to worry about." He laughed at his own joke, which was the correct response, since no one else ever would.

I took a room at the Drowned Lamb because it was cheap, because the landlord owed me, and because its back stairs emptied into an alley wide enough for a man to leave without being remembered leaving. Old habits. You don't survive eleven years on the smuggler roads by developing new ones.

I had planned to stay three days. Sleep somewhere with a door that locked from the inside. Eat something that hadn't been cured, smoked, or carried up a mountainside by a mule with opinions. Maybe buy a new whetstone, since my current one had been worn down to something resembling a soap bar.

That was the plan. The criers came at midmorning on the second day.

* * *

There were four of them, which was two more than Greyford had ever bothered with for anything short of a royal wedding. They stood in the market square in front of the tithe-house, wearing crown blue with silver thread at the collar, and they unrolled their proclamation with the crisp ceremony of men who had done this in a dozen towns already this season and would do it in a dozen more.

I was buying bread when I heard the first bell. I stood there holding a loaf like an idiot while the square filled around me, because that's what crowds do—they gather toward the thing that should scatter them. Curiosity is the most reliable trait our species has. It will walk us straight into the fire every time, nodding politely to the neighbors on the way.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third," the lead crier read, his voice trained to carry, "of blessed memory and enduring law—" someone in the crowd snorted; even loyalists were getting tired of that particular formulation "—all persons possessing the gift of workings, sigils, hedge-craft, or any art beyond natural means, shall present themselves for registration within thirty days of this proclamation."

A pause. Criers are trained for pauses. It gives the important part room to land.

"Those who register will be assessed and, if found harmless, permitted to live under crown supervision. Those who do not register"—another pause, longer, a craftsman at work—"will be sought. And those who are sought and taken will burn."

He said it plainly. No drama, no relish. That was the worst part. In the old stories, persecution comes with thunder and black riders and burning eyes. In practice it arrives as a bored man reading off a scroll in a market square, while behind him soldiers stack firewood against the tithe-house wall in neat, efficient cords.

Firewood. In autumn. Stacked with care, split evenly, enough for a bonfire that would last an evening. Somebody had measured for it. Somewhere in some office in the capital, a clerk had calculated how much wood it takes to burn a person and had written that number down, and other clerks had checked his arithmetic, and now it was stacked in Greyford market square between the turnip stall and the fountain where children paddled in summer.

I put down the bread. I'm not sure why. Some animal instinct about not wanting things in your hands when you need them empty.

Around me the crowd did what crowds do. A woman clutched her son closer. Two farmers exchanged the flat look of men deciding not to discuss this in public. Someone near me whispered, "About time," and someone else whispered back, "Shut up, Gerda," and Gerda shut up, because even the people who approve of witch-hunts understand that approval sounds different out loud than it does in your head.

And I stood in the middle of it, gifted as a struck bell, and thought: *register*. The word sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Walk up to the tithe-house, give my name, let some crown assessor poke at the thing inside me—the gift I'd spent half my life smothering, the oath-reading sense I'd used last week to keep nine people and forty bales of cloth off a bridge that was quietly planning to kill them. Register. Be assessed. Be found—

Found harmless? There was no such finding. Not really. Registration wasn't mercy; it was inventory. You don't assess a knife for harmlessness. You just write down where it lives so you can collect it later, when the arithmetic changes or the mood does.

Thirty days. I'd be gone in three. Back up the passes, where the crown's writ ran thin and the smugglers' code ran thick, where nobody asked what you could do as long as you did it for the caravan. The mountains had been good to me precisely because they didn't want to know me.

So why did my feet not move?

Because across the square, the soldiers weren't stacking wood anymore. They were dragging someone out of the chandler's shop.

* * *

Her name was Sela Bren. I knew that much, and knowing it made everything after worse, because you can watch a stranger suffer and call it tragedy, but you cannot watch a neighbor suffer and call it anything but what it is.

Sela was the hedge-healer on Tannery Row. She was maybe sixty, small and round and permanently slightly out of breath, the kind of woman whose house always smelled of rosemary because she put rosemary in everything on the theory that it fixed whatever was wrong with the original dish. She had pulled my tooth once, years ago, charging me two coppers and a story. She had set Hesk's arm another winter. She had sat up three nights with the Vellish brothers' mule when it colicked, and the mule lived, and she'd refused payment because "animals don't get sick on purpose, poor things, unlike some people I could mention."

She was not a witch. She was what she was: a woman with a touch of the gift, the kind so small and domestic that most people never called it magic at all. Fevers broke easier under her hands. Bones knit straighter. That was all. She'd probably broken half the laws on that scroll without ever once doing anything a reasonable person could fear.

They brought her out of the chandler's shop by the arms, and she wasn't fighting. That was the detail that lodged in me like a splinter. She wasn't screaming or scratching or begging. She was walking as fast as her legs allowed, which given that her legs are sixty years old and the soldiers were not slowing down, meant she was mostly being carried, and she kept turning her head to look back at the shop, at her own front door, with the expression of a woman who has left the kettle on.

Someone had thrown a shawl over her shoulders. I don't know who. It might have been her own idea. Even being dragged to a pyre, she'd thought about the cold.

The square went quiet in that specific way squares go quiet—not silent, but hushed, the noise changing texture, everyone still talking but nobody saying anything. A sound like wind through wheat. Four hundred people watching a crime happen in daylight, in the open, by the book.

And then the officer turned around, and I stopped breathing.

* * *

She was older. We both were; that's what time does to mercenaries if we're lucky, and Ilse Varga had always been lucky in exactly the ways that mattered—fast, careful, and too mean to die young. But I'd have known her in a crowd of thousands. I'd have known her blind. There's a way she stands, weight slightly forward, like the world owes her money and she's come to discuss terms.

Captain now. Crown blue instead of company gray. Silver at the collar where we used to sew our pay-tally knots.

Eleven years ago we'd served together under Captain Renn at the Saltmarch garrison—she with a sword, me with a spear, and both of us with the shared understanding of professionals everywhere: the war is stupid, the pay is late, and the man beside you is the only fact you can rely on. She'd taught me a dice game that was mostly cheating. I'd taught her which of Renn's maps were lies. Once, at Fennick's Ford, she'd pulled me out of the river by my collar and said, "You owe me a drink, Eriksen, and I intend to collect with interest," and I'd laughed until my ribs hurt.

Then came the thing I don't think about. The thing with the ledger and the massacre and the name that got pinned to me like a medal awarded by cowards. I left the company in the night, and I never explained, and she never asked. That was Ilse. She understood desertion better than most priests understand grace.

Now she stood in Greyford market square with the crown's colors on and Sela Bren's arrest in her hands, and she looked—gods help me—she looked *correct*. Composed. Doing her job with the clean efficiency of a woman who believes in the job. That was always the frightening thing about Ilse: not cruelty, never cruelty. Conviction. She believed things, all the way down, and belief like hers doesn't bend. It just cuts.

She gave the order to move Sela toward the tithe-house. Her voice carried the way it always had, clipped, unhurried, the voice of someone who has never once needed to shout. Sela stumbled on the mounting block and Ilse caught her elbow—actually caught it, steadied her, an old soldier's reflex—and said something to her, low, that I couldn't hear. Sela nodded at whatever it was. Then they walked her inside, and the door closed, and the square exhaled all at once, four hundred people resuming their lives like a congregation sitting down after a hard sermon.

I stayed. I don't know how long. Long enough that the bread seller started giving me looks, long enough that the firewood pile stopped looking like firewood and started looking like what it was. I stood there in the crowd that had watched and murmured and done nothing, and I catalogued my own contribution to the morning's events, and here is what it came to:

Nothing.

I had done nothing. I had stood among the wheat-wind murmurers with a gift that could have—I don't even know what. That's the ugly truth of hiding for eleven years: you get so good at invisibility that you become invisible to yourself. What would I have done? Stepped forward? Announced myself? Swapped places with a sixty-year-old healer? I'm not a hero. Heroes are a fiction invented by people who've never had to eat in winter.

But complicity doesn't require heroics. That's the joke of it. All it requires is standing still. All it requires is a man telling himself, *thirty days, I'll be gone in three, this isn't my town, this isn't my war,* while a woman who once pulled his tooth for two coppers and a story is walked past a stack of measured firewood.

My god—the one I still pray to, out of habit, the way you scratch a dog's ear at an empty hearth—my god is a god of oaths. Of promises kept, debts paid, words that mean what they say. Eleven years of silence from that direction. Not a whisper, not a warmth, nothing. I'd told myself the silence was abandonment, then punishment, then simply the way of gods, and eventually I'd stopped telling myself anything at all.

But standing in that square, I finally heard the silence clearly for what it was. Because if my god had been listening—if anything had been listening—it would not have needed to speak. I already knew the verdict. I'd known it for eleven years, and I'd buried it under routes and ledgers and the small daily competence of keeping caravans alive.

An oath-breaker's god goes quiet. That's not abandonment. That's disappointment.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic he hates," said a voice beside me.

It was the bread seller, holding out the loaf I'd abandoned. I took it automatically. My hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Bad math?" she asked.

"The worst kind," I said. "The kind where you already know the answer."

* * *

I went back to the Drowned Lamb and packed in ten minutes. Old habit again: never sleep twice in a town after blood. I settled my account with the landlord through the back-alley window rather than the front door, because even guilt keeps its operational security, and I was halfway up the north road toward the pass before noon.

The road climbs out of Greyford through orchards going gold, and from the second bend you can see the whole town laid out below—river, roofs, market square, the tithe-house with its little curl of chimney smoke. Ordinary smoke. Bread smoke, maybe, or forge smoke. I looked at it anyway, because apparently I intended to punish myself thoroughly before leaving.

And then I made the mistake of looking at the road ahead instead, and there, posted on the mile-stone where every traveler leaving town would see it, was the proclamation nailed up in official copy. Crown blue wax seal. Thirty days. Register or burn.

Below the main text, smaller, in the cramped hand of some clerk who'd never met a hedge-healer in his life:

*Known practitioners in this district will be identified by record and testimony. Citizens are reminded that concealment of the gifted is itself an offense.*

Testimony. They wanted names. They'd get them, too—not from villains, but from neighbors, from debtors, from Gerda and everyone like Gerda, ordinary people doing ordinary cowardice at ordinary scale. Somewhere in a tithe-house ledger there was probably already a line item: *Greyford district—one (1) hedge practitioner, apprehended. Remaining: unknown.*

And I thought about Sela's hands. Fever-breaking, bone-knitting, rosemary-scented hands. And I thought about mine, which had read a dying bridge's oath last week and saved nine lives with the reading, and which had done nothing today at all.

Here is the arithmetic I hate, the sum I'd already worked out on the road's first bend: eleven years of silence had bought me exactly one thing. It had bought me a front-row seat. Every year I'd hidden, I'd traded a piece of my right to stand anywhere, object to anything, save anyone. Invisibility compounds. You start by hiding your gift and end by hiding yourself, and the final exchange rate is shameful: a whole man, converted entirely into a witness.

I couldn't fix that in Greyford. Sela was behind a locked door in a garrisoned tithe-house, and I am one man, and storming it would accomplish nothing except adding my corpse to the ledger. I knew that. Knowing things has always been my curse.

But thirty days. The proclamation said thirty days, and it was nailed to posts in every town from here to the coast, which meant the hunt was moving, and hunts move along roads, and roads run through mountains, and I knew those mountains the way Sela knew rosemary—which is to say, completely, and with strong opinions.

If the crown was going to sweep the Thornfell, then the smuggler roads I'd walked alone for eleven years were about to fill with other people running the same direction. Other marked ones. Other strays. People who'd also learned that hiding is a full-time occupation with no pension.

I stood at the mile-stone a long moment, reading the proclamation again like it might say something different the third time. It didn't. Proclamations rarely do.

Then I took out my knife, and—because I am, despite everything, still a man who respects the forms—I pressed my thumb briefly to the crown's blue seal, the way you'd touch iron to test whether it's hot, and I said the only prayer I'd said aloud in eleven years, to a god that hasn't answered since before Wren was born:

"Witness this. I'm done registering as nothing."

The stone said nothing back. The road said nothing. The mountains ahead said nothing, which from mountains is normal.

But I'd seen, on the second reading, the small line at the bottom of the proclamation I'd missed the first time—a clerk's note, an administrative footnote, the kind of thing only a professional reader of fine print would catch:

*Practitioners seeking registration may present themselves at designated offices. Inquiries regarding historical cases may be directed to the Office of Reckonings, Greyford annex.*

Historical cases. Old files. Old ledgers. Old massacres with somebody else's signature on them, sitting in some annex, waiting for anyone curious enough—or guilty enough—to come asking.

I turned south instead of north, toward the lowlands, toward trouble, toward whatever the Office of Reckonings kept in its drawers. It occurred to me, walking fast, that I had no plan worth the name, and that for the first time in eleven years I didn't much care.

Behind me, Greyford's chimneys smoked on. Ahead, the lowland road unrolled, flat and exposed and full of witnesses.

Somewhere on it, I'd find Maren Ostvik. I just didn't know it yet.
