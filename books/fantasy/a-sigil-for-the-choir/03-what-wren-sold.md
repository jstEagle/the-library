## 3. What Wren Sold

The fence's name was Aldous Fenwick, and he was dying the way old liars die: slowly, resentfully, and with one hand still clutching something he intended to sell.

I found him in a room above a tannery in Greyford's lowest ward, where the smell of curing hides was thick enough to chew and the rent was cheap because nobody wanted to admit they lived there. He'd been a fixture of my other life—back when I still ran goods through Thornfell instead of people-shaped secrets. Fences like Aldous were the grease in the machine. You never liked them. You trusted them exactly as far as their profit margin demanded.

He looked worse than his reputation now. Yellowed skin, hands like paper, and eyes that had gone bright with the particular fever of a man who knows his ledger is about to close unbalanced.

"Sebastian Eriksen," he rasped, when I ducked under the low doorframe. "They told me you'd come."

"Who told you?"

"The way rumors travel. A man asks after Wren Eriksen in three towns, and by the fourth town the asking arrives ahead of him." He coughed, wet and long, into a rag that had seen better decades. "Sit down. Standing there makes me feel like a customer, and I'm too poor to be one."

I sat on a stool near the window, where the light came in gray through greased parchment. "You know where she is."

"I know what she *did*." He said it with the relish of a man who had held a secret too long and found it heavier than any purse. "That's better, isn't it? Information's worth more than location. Location is just a place. What she did—" he tapped his temple with one trembling finger, "—that's a story. Stories keep their value."

"Aldous."

"Yes?"

"If you make me haggle for my daughter, I will put you out that window, and the fall will be shorter than your remaining time anyway."

He smiled. It was not a nice smile, but it was an honest one, which from a fence was nearly sacramental. "There he is. I always liked you, Eriksen. Terrible man. Very reliable." He settled back against his pillows. "Ask your question properly."

I made myself breathe. The room smelled of tallow and hide-scrap and the sweetish rot underneath both. Somewhere below us, a worker scraped a skin against a beam in slow, patient strokes, like the building itself was being flayed.

"Where is my daughter?"

"Gone below." He watched my face for the flinch and got it. Everyone in the mountain country knew what *below* meant. Not death—death was cheap and local. Below meant the court beneath Thornfell, the under-court, the place mothers invoked and priests refused to name except in the liturgy, where they named it constantly, because fear fills pews faster than hope ever did.

"When?" My voice came out flat. I'd learned flatness as a boy, when flatness was the difference between a passing soldier's glance and a second look. Flatness had kept me alive through two wars and one marriage. It failed me now. It felt like wearing a coat with no lining in winter.

"Three years past," Aldous said. "Winter of the deep snows. After the Kestrel Mark went up on you."

The Kestrel Mark. Of course. There was a bounty on my head older than my gray hairs—a fat one, crown-issued, for a massacre at a place called Harrow Bridge that I had not committed but had certainly been standing near, which in the arithmetic of warrants amounts to the same thing. I'd run south, changed my name twice, buried my gift so deep even I sometimes forgot it was there. I'd left—

I'd left her.

"You knew," I said. "All this time, you knew, and you let me walk these roads thinking she was—what did I think? Safe. I thought she was safe. In Lowmark, with her aunt. You let me believe that."

"I let you believe what you paid me to let you believe," Aldous said, without apology. "You sent letters through me for two years. Then you stopped sending them and started paying me to say they'd been delivered. That's not a correspondence, Eriksen. That's a subscription to absolution. And absolution is the one commodity I sell at full price."

The scraper below went on scraping. I gripped the stool's edge until the wood bit my palms.

"What did she do?" I asked. "How does a girl of nineteen buy off a crown bounty?"

"Not a girl of nineteen. She was seventeen." His eyes glittered. "And she didn't buy off the bounty. You can't buy off the crown; the crown doesn't sell, it only taxes. No—she went to the other market. The one beneath the mountain." He paused, savoring it, the old merchant to the last. "She sold her soul."

The words landed strangely, the way words do when they're too large for the room. I'd heard the phrase all my life as a curse, a joke, a sermon. *Sell your soul.* Nobody meant anything by it. It was what grandmothers said about fiddlers and what priests said about everyone.

"She sold it to whom?"

"To the Judges. The Magistrate-Judges of the under-court. They hold the debt on you now—the Kestrel bounty, the warrant, the whole bloody paper trail. Crown hunters who come looking find their writs... declined. Returned unread. There are men in this town with your description memorized who cannot, when asked, remember why." He shrugged, a small dry movement like paper folding. "The court bought your guilt. All of it. Every crime on your name, real or forged. And the price—"

"Wren's soul."

"Wren's soul," he agreed gently. "Payable at solstice. Three weeks from tomorrow, as it happens. The transaction completes then. Her soul goes into the court's keeping—permanently, irrevocably, all that—and whatever is left of her stays here." He spread his yellowed hands. "Or doesn't. Accounts differ on what remains afterward. The choir-servants, they call them. Half-souled. They serve below, most of them. Some few stay above, walking around with holes where the middle of them used to be."

I don't remember standing up. I remember the stool going over behind me, clattering, and Aldous not flinching, because a dying man has no use for alarm.

"Then we void it," I heard myself say. "Contracts have terms. Loopholes. You're a fence—you've broken tighter bargains than this over a bad night's dice."

"Oh, I've cheated widows and orphaned merchants, sure." He laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough again, longer this time, and when it passed he lay very still, spent. "But you can't cheat the court below, Eriksen. Their contracts aren't written in ink. They're written in *oath*. Spoken, sworn, sealed. The god of oaths himself used to enforce such things, in the old days, before he went quiet." Something moved across his face—not quite pity. Pity's cousin, the uglier one. "You'd know about that, wouldn't you? Your kind. The marked ones. The ones who hear oaths like music."

My blood went cold in the warm room. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed placidly. "Of course you don't. And I never said it, and you never heard it, and that exchange costs you nothing—which is the only free thing you'll get from me today." He closed his eyes. "Go home, Eriksen. Or go wherever it is men like you go. Light a candle. Grieve properly. It's cleaner than what you're thinking."

"And what am I thinking?"

"That you'll go down there." He opened his eyes again, and for a moment the merchant fell away and there was just an old man, tired all the way down to the marrow. "Everyone thinks that, at first. The fathers. The mothers. The lovers. They come down the mountain roads with their knives and their righteous anger, and the court lets them walk right in, because grief is honest work and the court respects honesty—it's the raw material of every oath ever sworn. And then solstice comes, and the contract completes, and the grieving one walks back out alone, having purchased nothing, changed nothing, and lost whatever weeks they might have had left with the half of her that remained." He shook his head slowly against the pillow. "Wren knew that. She made me swear to tell you exactly that, if you ever came asking. Those were her words: *tell him not to come. Tell him I knew what it cost. Tell him it was worth it.*"

"*Worth it.*"

"For you." He said it simply. "For a father who couldn't be bothered to send a letter that wasn't laundered through a fence. Yes. She thought you were worth it. Make of that whatever you can carry."

I stood in the doorway a long moment. The scraper below had stopped; the silence was worse.

"Why tell me the rest, then?" I asked. "The date. The terms. If her message was *don't come*, why hand me the map?"

Aldous smiled his terrible honest smile one last time.

"Because I'm a fence, Eriksen. And a fence's whole religion is that everything has a buyer." He turned his face to the wall. "Now get out. Dying is undignified enough without witnesses."

* * *

I walked.

Not toward anywhere. Greyford's streets in the hour before dusk, when the lamplighters were making their rounds and the criers' voices had gone hoarse from the morning's proclamations—*register or burn, register or burn*—and the town had that tight, listening quality of a place where everyone is deciding, all at once, whether to be brave.

Three years. She'd been seventeen. I'd been running so long and so well that I'd built a whole architecture of excuses, load-bearing walls of justification: *the letters would be traced; the less she knew of me the safer; a clean break is a kindness.* I'd told myself she was better off unclaimed, the way you tell yourself the dog you gave away is happier in the new yard.

And the whole time, she had been carrying me. Seventeen years old, carrying a grown man's sins on her back, down into the dark, to trade away the only thing that was truly hers to give—for a man who paid a fence to lie to her about receiving letters.

I stopped on a bridge over the millrace. The water ran black and quick below, and I did what I always do, what I've done since I was nine years old and first heard the hum in a boundary stone: I reached for my god.

Habit, really. Prayer without expectation, the way a man taps a barrel that's been empty for years, just to hear the same hollow note. My gift—the thing that lets me read the oaths soaked into things, the promises baked into stone and rope and wedding rings—had gone voiceless a decade ago. The god of oaths had simply stopped answering. Priests called it the Silence. Called it judgment, or mystery, or the end of days, depending on how much incense budget they had. I called it loneliness with liturgy.

*I'm here,* I said, into the water-noise. *She sold herself for me. Tell me what to do. Tell me if there's a term, a clause, a crack in the seal. You're the god of oaths. This is a contract. Contracts are yours.*

Nothing. The same nothing as always. The millrace roared its indifferent roar.

Except—

I stopped. Put my hand flat on the bridge rail, on the old oak worn smooth by a century of hands, and listened the way I listen, down under hearing, where oaths live.

The bridge carried an oath. Most old structures do—some mason's binding-word worked into the joints, some promise of safe passage murmured over the keel-stones. This one was ancient, frayed, barely a thread of the original covenant. But underneath the fraying, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, there was something else. Not an answer. Not a voice.

A direction. The way a compass needle isn't a road but points along one.

North. Into the mountains. Down.

I took my hand off the rail like it had burned me, and stood there feeling foolish and exposed, a grown man gripping a bridge in a mill-town at dusk because a dead god might have twitched in his sleep.

Coincidence, I told myself. Wish shaped into sound. A drowning man hears rescue in every ripple.

But I'd read enough failing oaths in my life to know the difference between a thread and a tremor. And that hadn't been wish. Wish doesn't have a bearing.

* * *

I went back once more, because there was one question left and Aldous owed me nothing but would answer it anyway, because cruelty and generosity are the same coin to a dying man.

He was asleep, or near it. I sat on the stool I'd knocked over and waited, and eventually his eyes opened, unfocused, finding me by voice alone.

"Eriksen. Still here. Stubborn as sin."

"One question. Then I'm gone."

"Everything's one question, at the end." He shifted painfully. "Ask."

"The letters. The ones I paid you to deliver and didn't. Did she write back?"

Something crossed his face then that I hadn't seen on it before—shame, maybe, surfacing late in a life that had never made room for it. He reached under his pillow with a hand that shook like a leaf in rain, and drew out a small bundle tied with string. Wax-sealed. Soft at the corners from handling.

"Thirteen of them," he said. "One for every letter you never sent. She wrote them anyway. Every month, for over a year, before she went below. She'd bring them here and ask, *has he written? Has he?* And I'd say soon, girl, soon, he's a careful man, careful men write slow." His breath caught. "I couldn't deliver them. I didn't know where you were either—that was the joke of it, see? Two liars, lying to each other through me, and me skimming the toll from both directions." He pushed the bundle across the blanket toward me. "Take them. They're worthless now. Worthless things are all I have left to give."

I took them. Thirteen letters, each addressed in a hand I almost didn't recognize—she'd been a child when I left, her writing all elbows—and each sealed with a little pressed sprig of rowan, because she knew I'd know what rowan meant. *Protection. Homecoming.* A sigil, in the only language we'd ever shared.

"Last one's dated a week before she descended," Aldous murmured, already sliding back under. "Read that one first. Or last. Read it however you like. It's yours. Everything's yours now. That's the trouble with dying—all your debts convert to gifts."

I stood in the doorway with the bundle against my chest, and behind me Aldous Fenwick began, very quietly, to snore—the rattling, broken snore of a man whose account was closing.

Outside, the first stars were up over Thornfell's black silhouette, and somewhere north of them, beneath the stone, a court of judges held my daughter's soul in escrow, twenty-one days from collection.

I untied the string right there on the tannery steps, because I could not have walked another step without knowing, and broke the seal on the last letter.

Her handwriting had steadied. Grown up in the space between lines. It said:

*Da. If you're reading this, then Aldous finally grew a conscience, which means one of us should be impressed. Don't be angry at him. Anger is just love with nowhere to put itself, and you've never been good at putting things anywhere—that's why you leave.*

*I'm not writing to ask you to save me. I'm writing so that somewhere, in some pocket or pack, there's a piece of paper that knows I existed. That's all anyone gets, in the end. A record. A witness.*

*It was worth it. Whatever you hear, whoever tells you the price—I need you to believe me on this one thing, because it's the only thing I've ever been certain of: you were worth it. Even the version of you that leaves. Especially him, maybe. Somebody has to love that man, and apparently it's going to be me, at a distance, permanently, and unpaid.*

*Don't come below. The court keeps what the court keeps.*

*But if you ignore me—and you will, you're my father, ignoring instructions is the family trade—then at least do it loudly. Do it in a way they'll sing about. I'd hate for my one great purchase to be redeemed quietly.*

*Rowan and return,
Wren*

I read it four times. The lamplighter came and went. The tannery stink rose around me like a tide, and I stood on the steps of a dying liar's room holding thirteen years of my daughter's voice, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives after the damage is done, exactly what kind of man I had been.

The kind worth selling a soul for. Which is another way of saying: the kind who wasn't there.

I folded the letter along its creases, put the bundle inside my coat, over my heart, where the weight of it could do its work, and looked north, to where the mountains stood up against the stars like a wall between two worlds.

Twenty-one days.

There was a woman in the high passes—an old hedge-witch, blind, half-legend, who was said to map magic by ear and to owe my healer neighbor favors of the kind that outlive death. I'd heard the name whispered on the smuggler roads for years: *Maren Ostvik, Maren of the Listening Road.* If anyone knew what lay beneath the mountain—if anyone had ever mapped a way down and back—

It was a thin thread. It was probably nothing. It was the only thing I had.

I started walking.
