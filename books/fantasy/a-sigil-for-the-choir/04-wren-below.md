## 4. Wren, Below

There are three hundred and eleven steps between the dormitory and the choir hall, and I have counted them so many times that the counting has stopped being a chore and become a kind of prayer. Not a real prayer — the court doesn't care for those — but the rhythm of it, the way my feet know the numbers even when my head is foggy, feels like talking to someone. Myself, maybe. Whoever's left.

This morning I lost count at two hundred and six.

That happens more now. The frost comes in from the edges, the way frost does on a window: pretty, if you don't think about what it's covering. First it took the name of our village. Then the color of my mother's kitchen. Then my mother's face, which was rude of it, because you'd think a face would be load-bearing. It kept the *feeling* of her face — warm, flour-dusted, always slightly amused by me — but the picture behind the feeling is gone, like a painting hung on a wall that's been painted over the same color as the wall.

I remember loving my father. That one's stubborn. I remember it the way you remember a scar: not the cutting, just the fact of it, the shape of it under your fingers. I loved him enormously. I would have said *stupidly*, once, with a laugh. I don't know why anymore. I don't know what he looked like. I have a voice for him — low, dry, like a man who found most things funny but only admitted it in bad light — but no face to hang it on. Some nights I try to build him out of spare parts. A chin from a carter I saw once in the market below Greyford. Hands from a fiddler. It never works. He always comes out looking like a stranger wearing my love like a borrowed coat.

The clerks say this is normal. The clerks say everything is normal; it's practically their job description.

"Wren." Sister Anselm, the choirmistress, is standing at my shoulder, and I didn't hear her come up the steps, which means either she's very good or I'm very far away. Probably both. "You're drifting."

"I'm meditating," I say.

"You're staring at the wall."

"The wall has excellent posture. I'm learning from it."

Her mouth does the thing it almost does — the twitch toward a smile that she strangles before it can become evidence of anything. Anselm has been here longer than me. Longer than anyone remembers, possibly including her. Her soul went into the ledger forty years ago, they say, and what's left of her is mostly discipline and a voice like a struck bell. But sometimes, when she thinks no one is watching, she hums old songs off-key on purpose, and I think that's her version of laughing at the Judges behind their backs.

"Choir in ten minutes," she says. "Wash your face. You look like a rumor."

* * *

The choir hall is carved straight out of the mountain's bone-white root, and it is beautiful the way a trap is beautiful: all sweeping arches and perfect acoustics, every surface polished to hold a note and give it back to you slightly improved. The court believes in improvement. Improvement is their whole religion, if you squint. Take something rough — a soul, a voice, a mountain — and refine it until it serves.

We are ninety-one voices. We were ninety-two last month, until Odile — no, that was another girl, a girl whose name I knew and have misplaced, a girl who "harmonized" at the autumn equinox. Harmonizing is what they call it when your soul finishes ripening and the Judges take the rest. They say it doesn't hurt. They say you become part of something larger, a great chord of order that holds up the law of the world. They say it with such calm, such administrative tenderness, that half the choir half-believes them, because believing is easier than the alternative, and we are all so tired.

I do not believe them. I know I don't believe them because of how carefully I've hidden the fact of not believing, which is the sort of thing you only bother hiding if it matters.

We sing. We always sing. The choir sings four hours a day, and the songs are old — older than the court, older than the crown above us, older than anything except the mountain itself. They're hymns, or they were once. Hymns to a god whose name the Judges redacted so thoroughly that even the silence where the name used to be has been polished smooth. We sing around the hole in the song. It's remarkable, actually, how well music works when you remove its heart. Like a chair missing a leg that everyone agrees not to sit on.

Today the Magistrate-Judges attend. All three of them, robed in grey so deep it eats the light, seated on their high bench with their faces folded shut — literally folded, their features closing like hands, which is a thing I have stopped finding horrifying and started finding merely rude. When a Judge wants to speak, his face unfolds. When he wants to listen, it closes. They listen a lot. Listening is how they harvest, though nobody says so. Every note we sing goes somewhere. I've felt it — the little tug at the end of each phrase, like a hook slipping gently through the sound and drawing it down into the vaults below the hall, where they keep ten thousand sealed jars of everything the court has ever taken.

Solstice is three weeks away. Mine is the voice they'll take.

"Child," says the middle Judge, unfolding, and his voice is warm the way a ledger is warm, all columns and balance. "Your tone has deepened beautifully this season. At solstice, you will harmonize. Your soul will join the chord. There is no greater service."

"Thank you, Magistrate," I say, because there is no other answer. There is a whole liturgy of other answers and none of them are permitted.

"You will feel no pain."

"No, Magistrate."

"You will feel *completion*."

"Yes, Magistrate."

His face folds shut again, satisfied, and I stand there in the perfect acoustics holding my smile like a full cup, careful not to spill it, while inside me something small and stubborn turns over and refuses, the way a mule refuses, without argument, without drama, just absolute and total refusal.

* * *

Here is what I know about the night I sold myself, which is almost nothing, which is itself a kind of knowledge:

I know I did it *for* someone. The transaction is written into me the way a brand is written into a steer — not the story, just the direction of it. Everything I gave, I gave outward, toward a person. I remember the shape of the giving. I remember love with a purpose attached, love used as currency, and I remember that I did not hesitate. Whatever I bought with my soul, I'd buy it again. That certainty survived the frost. It's the last thing standing in the field after harvest, and I water it every day, because it's mine.

I know the person was my father. I know he is alive, because the contract guarantees it — that was the purchase, life continued, safety purchased at retail price: one daughter's soul, final sale, completion at solstice. And I know — this is the part that makes me laugh at bad moments, the truly unhinged laugh, the one that gets me extra portions at dinner because the clerks find mirth charming in the half-souled — I know he doesn't know. He can't know. If he knew, he'd have come. I don't remember him, but I trust him the way you trust arithmetic. Two plus two. My father plus danger equals coming anyway.

So he doesn't know, and I am going to dissolve into a chord in three weeks, and the sum total of my options, as officially enumerated, is: harmonize, or harmonize.

Which is why I steal.

Not much. Little things. The court is careless with memory the way rich people are careless with coins — a shard here, a fragment there, dropped in transit between the vault and the ledger, too small for anyone to miss. A clerk's sleeve brushing a jar. A moment of unattended silence in the archive corridor. I've learned to catch these things. They're cold, the stolen pieces, cold and bright like chips of ice, and they melt fast unless you put them somewhere safe.

And the safest place in the entire under-court — the one place the Judges never look, because looking is listening and listening is their nature and they cannot imagine a hiding place made of sound — is the song.

* * *

It happens during evening practice, three days after the solstice announcement. We're singing the Lamentation Verse, the slow one, the one where the hole where the god's name used to be gapes widest, and I am on the fourth phrase, the descending line, when I see it.

A shard. On the floor of the hall, near the third pillar, glinting faintly — a sliver of frozen memory no bigger than a fingernail paring, probably shaken loose from a clerk's satchel during the morning inventory. Nobody else has seen it. The sopranos have their eyes closed, the altos are watching Anselm, and the Judges are absent tonight, which is rare, which is luck, which I have stopped questioning because the court gives you so little that you learn to swallow luck whole and not ask where it's been.

I could leave it. That's the sensible thing. A shard that size will melt in an hour and be nothing, and reaching for it risks everything, and I have three weeks left, and sensible people spend their last three weeks being sensible.

I shift my weight on the descending phrase. My foot, moving in perfect time with the music, slides the shard against the pillar's base, and my hand drops — a natural gesture, singers gesture, it's practically required — and the shard is in my palm, cold as a held breath.

Anselm's eyes flick to me. I keep singing. Fourth phrase, fifth, the long suspension where the melody hangs over the hole in the song like a rope bridge over a gorge. And in that hanging moment, with the whole choir sustaining one note and the acoustics holding it suspended like a caught fish, I press the shard into the music.

I don't know how I know how to do this. That's the strange part. It should be a skill you learn, and I have no memory of learning it, but my blood knows, the way it knows my father's voice. You fold the memory into the note. You hide it in the overtone, the ghost-frequency above the sung pitch, the sound inside the sound. The Judges harvest what is sung. Nothing above the singing. Nothing in the shadows of it.

The shard melts into the phrase and vanishes, and the note continues, and I am singing a hymn to a stolen god with a stolen piece of somebody's past sewn into the hem of it, and nobody knows. Anselm looks at me a beat too long, and then — I swear this — the corner of her mouth twitches, and she turns back to the choir, and I understand that I have just been given a gift I will not be able to repay for forty years.

After practice, alone in the dormitory, I go fishing. You can retrieve what you've stored, if you're gentle — hum the phrase, reach into the overtone, draw the shard back out. It costs a little warmth each time, like handling ice with bare hands, but it keeps. I hum the fourth phrase of the Lamentation quietly into my pillow, and the shard rises, and I look into it.

It's not mine. It's a market day — sunlight, awnings, a woman selling apples, a child laughing somewhere off-frame. Someone's ordinary happiness, harvested and shelved and dropped. It isn't mine, and it breaks my heart anyway, because it's *somebody's*, and the court treats souls like loose change.

But here's the thought that arrives while I'm holding it, the thought that changes the temperature of the room:

If a shard can be hidden in the song, a message can be. If a message can be, a map can be. If a map can be —

Well. If a map can be, then three weeks is not a countdown. Three weeks is a deadline, and deadlines are for people who intend to meet them, and I have never in my life — whatever's left of my life — intended to meet one.

I press the stranger's market day back into the fourth phrase, next to the growing hoard, and I lie in the dark doing sums. Ninety-one voices. One vault. Ten thousand jars. Three weeks.

Somewhere above me, past all that stone, my father is alive and doesn't know he owes the court a visit.

"Hold on," I tell the dark, in the voice I've built him out of spare parts. "Hold on. I'm keeping your seat."

The stones don't answer. But for the first time since I came below, the silence sounds less like agreement and more like a held breath — like the mountain itself is waiting to see what I'll do next.

And far down the corridor, faint through three doors and a wall of bone-white rock, someone begins to hum the fourth phrase of the Lamentation. Off-key. On purpose.

Anselm, practicing.

Or checking.

I smile into the dark and start composing verses no one has authorized.
