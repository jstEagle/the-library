## 5. The Hedge-Witch's Road

Three days out of Greyford, I'd learned two things about fleeing: it looks a great deal like walking, and it gives you nothing to do with your hands.

I missed the caravan work for exactly that reason. Give me a string of mules and a nervous factor and I know what I am—I'm the man who watches the bridge stones and counts the river's mood and gets everyone home. Alone on a forest track with a stolen horse I didn't even steal properly (I left coin; old habits), there was no one to be competent *for*. Just my own head, which has never been good company, and the silence where my god used to live, which is worse company still.

I prayed anyway. Out of habit, like a man scratching a scar. Nothing came back. Nothing had come back in eleven years, but you don't stop talking to someone just because they've stopped answering. You just stop doing it where people can see.

The healer's name had been Aldric. That was the thing that kept snagging in me like a burr under a saddle blanket. Not "the hedge-healer," not "a gifted." Aldric, who had set my arm when a mule kicked it sideways outside Greyford six winters back, who'd charged me half what it was worth because I made him laugh once. They dragged him through his own spilled cabbages while his neighbors watched from doorways with the particular blankness of people memorizing their own excuses.

And Ilse Varga had held the rope.

I tried to make that mean something different every few miles. She was following orders. She didn't recognize me. She recognized me and chose mercy by looking through me. None of the versions helped, because all of them ended at the same place: a man I'd broken bread with, hauled off to a pyre, and the only reason I wasn't beside him was that I'd gotten good at being nobody.

Being nobody, it turns out, is a skill. Like any skill, it can fail you at the worst possible time.

* * *

Maren Ostvik lived—or so the dying fence's directions went—past the ash wood, up where the Thornfell foothills started pretending to be mountains, in a house with no road to it. "You'll find her," Halvard had wheezed, blood on his teeth and a hunter's knife wound in him that no hedge-healer was left to close, "by listening. She'll hear you before you hear yourself. And boy—" he'd grabbed my wrist with the strength of the nearly dead, which is considerable, "—she doesn't suffer liars. Or cowards. You're at least one of those."

Charming man. I hoped the fever took him gently.

The ash wood announced itself by sound before sight—the leaves turning early, rustling silver-dry in a wind that hadn't reached the lowlands yet. My gift stirred without permission. It always does that near strong workings: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like struck flint, the sense of standing in a room where a conversation just stopped. Old oaths have a hum. New ones ring. Broken ones have a sound I won't describe except to say I've heard it twice and both times I dreamed about it after.

This place rang. Continuously. A whole hillside of small, patient promises, layered like sediment.

I left the horse tied at the treeline—if she heard everything, she'd certainly heard me coming, and there's no dignity in announcing yourself twice—and walked in on foot, hands visible, the way you approach a strange dog or a magistrate.

The house was smaller than I expected. Stone, turf-roofed, smoke rising thin and straight from a vent that should have scattered in the wind but didn't. A garden gone deliberately wild, herbs elbowing each other for space. And on the doorstep, facing away from me, sat an old woman shelling peas into a bowl, as if the end of the world were a rumor she'd declined to confirm.

"Maren Ostvik?" I said.

"You walk quiet," she said, without turning. Her voice was dry and unhurried, a stream over stones. "For a big man. But your heart walks loud. It's been shouting since the treeline. Sit down before you fall down; you look like a fence post that lost its argument with the weather."

I sat on the step below hers. Up close she was older than old—skin like paper that had been folded many times and creased where it mattered—and her eyes were wrong in the way blind eyes are wrong only if you expect them to be empty. Hers weren't. They looked at something. Just not anything in front of them.

"Aldric sent me," I said. Then, because lying to her felt like lying in church: "He didn't send me. He got taken. I'm here because he couldn't be."

"I know who sent you." Peas rattled into the bowl. "Aldric's been sending me his failures for thirty years. He thinks I don't notice they only arrive when he's already given up on fixing them himself." The shelling stopped. "You're the one he hid. All these years, right under Varga's nose in Greyford, and he never said a word to anyone, and now the crown burns the gifted and he's in a wagon headed south with his hands tied and you're sitting on my step smelling of guilt and cheap horse liniment."

The words went through me neater than any knife. "How do you—"

"Sound." She turned her face toward me at last. Whatever she looked at, it wasn't me; it was through and past and around me, some architecture I couldn't see. "Everything you do makes noise, whether you mean it or not. Oaths especially. An oath is the loudest thing a person ever says, and most people say theirs once and then spend the rest of their lives echoing." She tilted her head. "Yours echoes wrong. There's a hole in it. Big as a god-shaped hole, if I wanted to be crude about it."

My mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"There it is. The liar." But she said it almost fondly, and picked up another pod. "Boy, I've been mapping magic by ear since before your father was weaned. I heard the tithe-stones go quiet eleven years ago—one morning the whole covenant-web of the Thornfell passes just... stopped singing, mid-note, all at once, like a choir strangled between verses. Every oath-tender in the high country felt it. Most decided they'd imagined it. Imagining things is very popular. It's cheaper than the alternative."

"What's the alternative?"

"That somebody took our god." She said it the way you'd report a crop failure. Practical. Terrible. "And I've spent eleven years listening for where it went, and I'll tell you for free what I found: down. Everything that goes missing in this world and doesn't rot ends up beneath the mountain. So." Peas again. Rattle, rattle. "What did you come to ask me, Sebastian Eriksen? And mind—the truth rings differently than the lie. I'll hear whichever you give me."

She knew my name. Of course she did. I had the sudden vertiginous certainty that she'd known it since the treeline, maybe since Greyford, maybe since the day eleven years ago when the sky over the passes went silent and somewhere far below her, a girl I'd failed signed her soul away in my name.

"Wren," I said. "My daughter. They tell me she sold herself to the court under the mountain. To buy my safety. The sale completes at solstice. Three weeks." My voice did something embarrassing on the last word and I let it. There was no point performing competence for a woman who could hear my heartbeat lying. "Halvard thinks the transaction might be... unwound. Reversed. He also thought the moon was made of cheese and owed him money, so weigh that accordingly. I need to get below. I need a guide. Everyone says you're the only one who could do it."

"Everyone is right." She set down the bowl at last, and her blind eyes fixed on that not-place, and for a long moment the only sound was the ash leaves and the impossible straightness of the smoke. "I'll take you down."

Relief hit me so hard I nearly laughed. Then she raised one finger, and the relief curdled.

"One condition."

"Name it. Coin, service, my firstborn—"

"Your firstborn is spoken for, as I understand it." Wry as a blade. "No. Here's my price, Eriksen, and it's not negotiable, so save your bargaining voice. You will not go alone. You will gather the others. The marked ones, the strays, the ones the hunt is flushing out of the brush like pheasants. Tam of the ferry-crossing. Odile, who sells lies in Greyford's back streets. Brannoc, who used to be somebody's priest. You'll find them, and they'll come with us, or I don't take a single step toward that mountain."

"Why?" I asked. And then, because she could hear a lie at forty paces: "That's not why. Why *really*?"

"Because no one steals from the court alone." She said it flatly, a fact like the frost date. "I have listened to that place for years, boy, the way you'd listen to a river before crossing. It is not a fortress. Fortresses have walls, and walls have gates, and gates open. The under-court is a *harmony*. Every soul in it is a note in somebody else's chord. You cannot fight a chord with one voice. One voice going down there isn't a thief—it's an offering. It walks in and the court simply absorbs it. Adds it to the song." She finally looked at me directly, and I'd swear the blind eyes saw me whole, every hidden thing. "But a dissonance—a chord built wrong, built *against* them—that, they have no instrument for. They harvest obedience. They've forgotten what it sounds like when free voices disagree."

"So I'm gathering a band of fugitives to make ugly music at a god-eating court."

"I prefer 'choir.' Ugly choirs are still choirs." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the land where one might settle. "Besides. You need them more than the plan does. Don't argue. I can hear you deciding to argue."

I stood, mostly because sitting felt too much like losing. "I've spent eleven years making sure nobody knew what I was. Nobody traveled with me, nobody owed me, nobody could be tortured into naming me. It worked. It kept me breathing. And now you want me to collect every marked soul in the district and march them at the one place that eats people like us, because—what, because it's strategically sound?"

"Because it is strategically sound," Maren agreed placidly, "and because you're lonely, and those are the same sentence wearing different boots. You just haven't noticed yet. Give it a week."

"I'm not—"

"Your heartbeat just sped up. Argument lost." She rose, unhurried, joints cracking like green kindling, and stood level with me despite being a head shorter. Up close she smelled of ash leaves and pennyroyal and something older underneath, something like cold stone and candle smoke, like a crypt where something precious was kept. "Go find Tam first. He's young, he's terrified, and his gift frightens him worse than the hunters do—he needs to see an older fool be brave first. Then Odile, who will rob you, and then Brannoc, who will pray at you. In that order. Trust me on the order."

"And if they refuse?"

"They won't refuse. Fugitives are the easiest people in the world to recruit; you only have to offer them somewhere to *be*. That's the secret nobody tells you about belonging, Eriksen." She gathered her pea bowl and turned toward her crooked door. "It starts as tactics. It becomes necessity. And one morning you look around and it's neither—it's just the shape of your life, and you can't remember how you lived without it."

She paused at the threshold.

"The girl," she said, quieter. "Wren. What was she like?"

And that was the trap in the middle of the whole day, sprung gentle as a snare in grass. Because I opened my mouth to answer and discovered that eleven years of deliberate distance had done its work thoroughly. I remembered a small girl on a dock counting gulls. I remembered her mother's laugh and not her face. I remembered leaving, which is the memory I deserved, filed in perfect detail like a debt ledger.

"I don't know anymore," I said. "That's the truth. That's why it has to be me that goes."

Maren nodded slowly, as if I'd passed something.

"Good," she said. "Honest grief rings truest. Now get off my step. Solstice won't wait, and neither will I—I'll meet you at the split-oak above Greyford ford in four days, at moonrise. Bring your strays." The door began to close, then stopped. "Oh. And Eriksen?"

"Yes?"

"When you prayed at the treeline—and you did, don't bother denying it, prayer has a sound like no other foolishness—something answered. Very faint. Far down. You didn't hear it because you weren't listening with your whole self." Her blind eyes found me one last time. "But I heard it. And it said your daughter's name."

The door shut.

I stood on the step a long moment, ash leaves whispering overhead, the hillside's patient oaths humming around me like a congregation holding its breath. Three weeks until solstice. Four days until moonrise. Three strangers to find, each one a warrant waiting to happen, each one a risk I'd spent a decade refusing to take.

And somewhere far beneath my feet, fainter than memory, a voice that had been silent for eleven years had said *Wren*.

I walked back toward my horse faster than dignity allowed, and if my heart was loud, well.

For the first time in years, I found I didn't much care who heard it.
