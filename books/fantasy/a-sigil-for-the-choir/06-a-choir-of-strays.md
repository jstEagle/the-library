## 6. A Choir of Strays

### I.

Sebastian

Maren Ostvik had told me to gather others like me, and I'd told her I worked alone, and she'd laughed the way blind people laugh—straight at you, like they can see exactly how full of it you are.

"Alone," she'd said, turning her face toward the fire as if the flames were a view worth taking in. "You're a man who talks to a god that doesn't answer, and your plan is to steal from judges who eat souls. Alone. Yes. That'll go beautifully."

So here I was, three days out of Greyford, standing in the rain outside a tannery shed in the village of Hollowmere, about to recruit a boy who smelled like his own workplace and raised the dead for pocket change.

I want it on the record that I did not choose this life. It chose me by falling on my head repeatedly.

The shed door was latched with a rope. I knocked anyway, because manners cost nothing, and heard a scramble inside—the particular scramble of someone hiding something. Everyone in Thornfell was hiding something these days. The crown's criers had made sure of that: *register or burn*, sung out in that cheerful official cadence that made atrocity sound like a harvest festival.

The door cracked open on one eye, brown and suspicious, above a smear of tanning fat.

"You're not a hunter," the boy said.

"How do you know?"

"Hunters knock like they own the door." He looked me over. "You knock like you're apologizing."

"That's fair." I held up both hands, empty. "I'm looking for Tam Vossel."

His eye narrowed. "Who's asking?"

"A man who needs help going somewhere very stupid, and who can pay in a way that doesn't leave coin."

That got the second eye. "What kind of way?"

"The kind where nobody asks questions afterward." I leaned closer, lowered my voice. Rain ran off my hood between us. "I know what you did at the Fenlow burying-ground last month. The gravedigger's dog wouldn't stop barking at the fence, and the next morning three fresh graves stood empty and the bodies were found sitting neatly against the church wall, folded up like laundry. Nobody saw anything. Nobody ever sees anything, because you're careful."

The boy went very still. Underneath the tannery stink and the suspicion there was something else—fear, the old deep kind, the kind that lives in the spine.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Good. Keep saying that. It's served you well." I glanced down the muddy lane, empty except for a chicken with ambitions. "But here's my problem, Tam. There's a place under the mountain where they keep souls in jars. I need to steal one back before solstice. And I've been told—I was told by a woman who maps the world by sound and is rarely wrong—that no one steals from the under-court alone."

"You're mad," the boy said, with something dangerously close to admiration.

"Probably. But the dead listen to you. And down there, we may need listeners more than fighters." I straightened up. "You don't have to decide now. I'm sleeping in the loft over the smithy tonight. If you come, come quiet. If you don't—" I shrugged. "Then I hope the hunters never learn what the dog was barking at."

I turned to go, and his voice caught me like a hook.

"What's in the jar?"

I stopped. Rain ticked on my hood.

"My daughter," I said. "Or most of her. Whatever part they didn't already grind into law."

Silence behind me, long enough that I thought he'd shut the door. Then, quietly, the rope latch rattled loose.

"Give me an hour," Tam said. "The dead don't walk fast, but I keep some ready. You'll want them."

And that, children, is how a mercenary with a silent god acquired his first corpse-wrangler.

### II.

Tam

Here's what nobody tells you about raising the dead: they remember being bored.

Grandda Fenlow had been dead eleven days when Mr. Apologetic-Knock woke him, and the first thing out of his mouth—not literally, his jaw was wired shut with my thread—was a groan I'd learned to translate years ago. *This again. This mud again. This boy again.*

"Yeah, yeah," I muttered, packing my satchel. Bone needles, wax thread, the little clay bells that kept them calm. "One job. Then back to your dirt and your quiet."

He groaned again, lower. That one meant *liar.*

He wasn't wrong. I always said one job. There was always another job. Because a boy who can pull a grandmother out of her grave to scare off tax collectors doesn't get to be just a boy; he gets to be a rumor, and rumors get hunted, and hunters burn rumors.

The stranger was waiting by the smithy, hooded, patient in the way soldiers are patient—like patience is just killing time until the killing starts. I didn't trust him. I want that clear. I didn't trust him when he paid me, I didn't trust him when he explained the under-court, and I especially didn't trust him when he said *my daughter* in that voice people use when they've cut something out of themselves and are showing you the scar instead of the wound.

But Grandda groaned at him—a soft one, third-lowest—and I've learned to listen to the dead on this exact point. They don't care about warrants or crowns. They care about debts. And the way this man carried himself, all that weight balanced so carefully you'd miss it if you weren't looking—he owed somebody everything, and he meant to pay.

The dead respect that. The dead are all debtors.

"So," I said, catching up to him on the road out of Hollowmere, Grandda shuffling behind us with his arms folded like a man waiting in a very long queue. "This daughter of yours. She sold her soul for you?"

"For my safety. So they tell me."

"And you're getting it back."

"That's the plan."

Grandda groaned—second tone, the skeptical one—and I translated without thinking, the way you do. "He says plans are what graves are full of."

The stranger glanced back at the dead man walking behind us, and instead of flinching—which most do, even the ones who say they won't—he almost smiled.

"He's not wrong," he said.

"No," I agreed, and adjusted one of the clay bells on Grandda's wrist so it hummed soothing and low. "But he's coming anyway. We all come anyway. That's the whole problem with us."

### III.

Odile

The man came into my shop pretending to buy a love charm, which is how everyone buys things in Drossmark when they actually want forgery. Love charms, map cases, deed boxes—it's a whole language, and I speak it fluently, and I could tell within ten seconds that this one was fluent too, because he asked for a charm "to bind a promise," and only liars and lawyers use the word *bind* when they mean *protect.*

"Binding promises run dear," I said, sliding the display case across the counter. Real charms. Useless ones. "Depends how long the promise must hold."

"A season," he said. "Maybe less. Maybe forever, if I'm unlucky."

"Mm. And is the promise written, spoken, or sworn on stone?"

Something flickered in his eyes. He hadn't expected the third option. Most clients didn't know stones could hold oaths; that knowledge had died with the covenant roads, or been buried under them.

"Spoken," he said carefully. "By a god."

I set down the case. Now it was interesting.

Now, let me explain something about me, since apparently we're doing confessions this chapter. My name is Odile Ferrant, I am the best sigil-forger in the lowlands, and I say that with the modesty of a woman who has tested the claim against every rival and buried them all—professionally speaking. My gift is that sigils I draw *lie*. Beautifully. A lock-sigil of mine will believe it's been opened. A warrant of mine will believe it's real so hard that the paper blushes. I once forged a bishop's seal so convincing that the bishop bought it from me to use himself, because mine was better than his.

The crown wants my head for forty-one counts of fraud, two counts of treason, and one count—unfairly, I maintain—of impersonating a duchess. The duchess in question still writes me letters. Charming woman.

So when a rain-soaked mercenary stands in my shop asking about oath-stones in a voice like gravel learning gentleness, I do the arithmetic fast: either he's a hunter running a trap, or he's trouble of the profitable kind, or he's trouble of the fatal kind. In Drossmark those categories overlap considerably.

"Gods don't swear much anymore," I said. "They've gone quiet. All of them. Didn't you hear?"

"I heard," he said. "Mine included."

Ah. There it was. A marked man. They smell different after Greyford—like smoke that hasn't happened yet.

"What's the promise?" I asked, though I was starting to guess. Word travels along the smuggler roads faster than riders; word said a man was gathering strays to go below the mountain, which was the sort of word that got its tellers hanged, which meant it traveled all the faster.

"To bring my daughter home."

I looked at him for a long moment. He bore it well. Men usually squirm under my looking; I've cultivated a look that suggests I'm pricing them, because mostly I am.

Here's the thing. I'd been meaning to leave Drossmark anyway. The magistrate Ossler had started asking after me in a tone I didn't like—hungry, patient, the tone of a man building a case brick by brick. Forty-one counts of fraud make a tall tower, and towers fall on the person inside them. A smart forger knows when to let a signature go cold.

A smart forger also never works for free. But some jobs pay in something better than coin: a debt from a desperate man, owed under a mountain, redeemable in whatever currency I might someday need. Judges' credentials. Court seals. A favor from someone who walks where I cannot.

"Below the mountain," I said lightly, "the court uses sigil-credentials keyed to soul-signature. Very hard to forge." I smiled. "For anyone else."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "Can you do it?"

"I can do anything with ink and intent, darling. The question is price." I named it. He didn't blink, which told me he'd been expecting to pay worse. "Plus one thing unpriced: when this is done, you owe me. Not coin. A door opened, a name spoken, a lie believed—whatever I ask, once. Sworn on your god, silent or not."

"That's a dangerous oath to offer a stranger."

"All the best ones are." I pulled out fresh paper and my good pen—the one with the nib I'd shaped myself—and began sketching the credential-frame from memory, lines flowing like water finding a channel. My hands knew this work the way his probably knew killing: without consulting him. "Pack light, wear gray, and don't touch anything I draw unless I tell you to. Half my sigils bite."

"And the other half?"

"Worse."

### IV.

Brannoc

They found me in the ruin of Saint Aldhelm's-on-the-Crag, which is where I had gone to be useless in comfort.

I was a priest of the Oathbound for twenty-two years. I buried the drowned, married the stubborn, swore the frightened to things they mostly kept. And then the god went silent—not gradually, the way faith cools in most men, but all at once, mid-hymn, like a singer struck dumb on the high note. One morning the stones rang when I prayed. The next they were just stones. The synod called it a testing. I called it a burial, and I dug myself into the grave alongside, drinking mostly, preaching nothing, keeping the chapel roof patched out of habit because habit was all that remained.

Except.

Except that on certain nights—still, cold nights, nights when the mountain wind held its breath—I heard it. Not words. Echoes. Fragments of the old liturgy coming back wrong, like shouts returned off a cliff by someone imitating your voice slightly badly. A syllable of consecration. Half a verse of the Covenant Hymn. Once, terribly, what might have been my own name, pronounced the way the god used to pronounce it, with the weight of being known.

The synod would say madness. The synod, frankly, could say what it liked; it had stopped listening to me years before I stopped listening to it. Disgrace travels faster than grace.

So when the stranger came up the cliff path through the drizzle—soldier's stride, mourner's shoulders, followed by a boy leading a dead man on a rope like the world's most polite nightmare, and a woman whose boots said *cities* and whose smile said *run*—my first thought was that I'd finally drunk enough for hallucinations.

My second thought, when he spoke, was worse.

"You hear it too," he said. Not a question. He stood in the ruined doorway with rain steaming off him, and he said, quietly, "Don't you. The echoes."

I set down the bottle. Some conversations deserve sobriety, and this was going to be one of them, though I resented it.

"What I hear," I said, "is no concern of the crown's, if that's why you've come. I hold no office. I perform no rites. I am, officially, nothing."

"I'm not the crown." He came closer. Close up he had the look of a man carrying a debt too large for his frame. "I'm going under the mountain. There are judges down there who took something of mine. And Maren Ostvik says—" he paused, as if the name itself required handling— "she says the god's voice is down there. Stolen. Jarred. Harvested."

The wind moved through the broken windows of Saint Aldhelm's, and every shattered edge of glass hummed the same thin note, and I felt it in my teeth like a hymn remembered by the architecture alone.

"You think I can help," I said, "because I hear ghosts."

"I think you can help because you're the only priest I've met who still listens. Everyone else decided the silence meant the god left." His jaw tightened. "I'm starting to believe the silence means the god was *taken.* And if that's true, Father, then the liturgy isn't dead. It's imprisoned. And imprisoned songs have a way of wanting out."

I should tell you what I felt at that moment, because I've tried to describe it since and keep failing. It was the feeling of a man who has spent years mourning a wife hearing, from the next room, her cough. Not proof. Hope, weaponized. The cruelest and finest thing there is.

Behind the soldier, the dead man groaned—a low note—and the boy patted his arm absently, and the forger examined our crumbling saints with the eye of a woman appraising salvage, and I understood that I was being recruited into a company of the damned, and that the damned, at least, keep honest company.

"I'm disgraced," I warned him. "I drink. I doubt. I haven't said a true prayer in six years."

"Good," he said. "We've got enough believers. What we need is someone who remembers the words."

And God forgive me—or rather, God *hear* me, wherever They've been locked away—I laughed. First time in years. It came out rusty and it hurt my ribs, and it felt like the first note of a hymn.

### V.

Sebastian

We sat that night in the gutted nave of Saint Aldhelm's around a fire we could ill afford, five of us and one dead man arranged against the far wall with his hands folded, and I looked at what Maren's strategy had assembled, and I confess my heart sank like a stone in a well.

A boy who commanded corpses and flinched at loud voices. A forger who talked like a knife dressed for dinner. A defrocked priest who kept glancing at the broken windows as if expecting them to sing. And me: a smuggler with a silent god, a bounty on his head he'd never earned, and a daughter's soul sitting in a jar under a mountain, ticking down to solstice.

"If the crown could see us," Odile said, warming her hands at the fire, "they'd build a second pyre just for the variety."

"They'd need a bigger pyre," Tam said. "Grandda alone burns slow. All the embalming."

"Thank you for that, boy," said Brannoc, looking faintly green.

"His name isn't actually Grandda," Tam added, to no one in particular. "It's just what I call them. The dead like a name. Even a borrowed one."

I watched them—this ragged, warranted, wounded handful—and I thought about every mercenary company I'd ever served in, bands of hardened killers bound by pay and fear, and how none of them, not one, would have walked into the under-court for anything short of a king's ransom. And here sat a child, a cheat, and a drunk, willing to go for free, for each other, for a girl none of them had ever met.

There's a lesson in that. I didn't like it. Lessons about hope usually come disguised as risks, and I'd spent fifteen years avoiding both.

"We move tomorrow," I said. "North, through the Pass of Broken Oaths. Maren meets us on the far side to guide the descent." I fed a stick to the fire. "After that, we're past the point where any of us can turn around without the others hanging."

"Charming," said Odile.

"He means it kindly," said Brannoc.

"He means it *literally*," said Tam, and the dead man behind him groaned the long low groan that I was beginning to understand meant laughter, if the dead could laugh, and maybe they could, maybe that was the whole secret no one living had bothered to learn.

I lay back against a fallen pew and listened to them argue about pyre-weights and forged bishops, and above the ruined roof the clouds were tearing open, and for one moment—one treacherous, unguarded moment—I thought I felt it: far under the stone, far under the silence, something vast turning over in its sleep, like a voice clearing its throat at the bottom of a well.

Then it was gone, and the fire crackled, and Odile was telling Tam exactly how much a forged soul-credential would fetch on the black market, scandalizing him thoroughly.

Three weeks to solstice.

In three days, the pass. And in the pass—if Brannoc's echoes were true—we would finally learn what the silence had been trying to tell us all along:

that our god had not abandoned us.

That it had been *taken*.

And taken things, as Tam's dead could attest, remember exactly who put them there.
