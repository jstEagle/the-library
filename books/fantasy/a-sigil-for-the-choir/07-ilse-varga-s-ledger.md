## 7. Ilse Varga's Ledger

The cells under Greyford's tollhouse smelled of wet stone and old lies, which was to say they smelled like every other cell I'd ever worked in. I had learned long ago that the smell of a prison is the same everywhere—the crown builds them all from the same quarries, the same mortar, and apparently the same despair. There is a certain comfort in that, if you're the sort of person who finds comfort in uniformity.

I am, or I was. Ask me again at the end of this chapter.

The prisoner they brought me that morning was a hedge-healer named Aldous Fen, sixty-odd years old, hands like a farmer's, wrists already raw from the irons. They said he'd been caught treating a smuggler's fever without registering his gift. The smuggler got away. The healer did not. That is how the arithmetic of the hunt always works: we catch the ones who stop to help.

"Sit," I told him, though he was already sitting. It's a habit of mine, telling people things they've already done. It establishes who writes the record.

He sat. He looked at my uniform—the grey-and-gold of the Crown Inquisitors' Office, third rank, hunt-commissioned—and he looked at my ledger, and he looked at the brazier where the coals glowed their patient orange, and I watched him do the same arithmetic every gifted soul does when the door closes behind me. Not *will she burn me*. That comes later. First it's always *how long do I have*.

"Master Fen," I said. "You've been practicing the gift for how long?"

"Thirty-one years," he said. His voice was steady. Old men are steadier than young ones; they've had more time to make peace with dying, or less time to fear it, depending on how you want to read it. "Fever-breaks. Birthing charms. Nothing you'd call grand."

"Nothing you'd call registered."

"I registered once." His mouth twisted. "Under the old law. Before the Edict. The registry office in Carrowmere burned, and my name burned with it, and nobody ever wrote it down again."

"So you're unregistered through no fault of your own." I made a note. I always make notes; half of interrogation is making the prisoner watch you write, because a man will tell you anything to correct what he thinks you're writing down wrong. "And yet you treated a fugitive. A man carrying an unregistered gift himself, by your own account."

"He had a fever," Fen said simply. "I'm not in the business of asking men their paperwork before I break it."

There it was. That sentence. I have heard some version of it forty times in six years of the hunt, and every time it lands in the same place, just under the breastbone, where faith keeps its house. Because here is the thing about the Edict that the criers don't say in the square, the thing I only know because I sit on the other side of the desk: most of the people we burn are not monsters. Most of them are Aldous Fens. Fever-breakers. Charm-menders. People whose crime, reduced to its honest weight, is that they were born with something the crown decided to be afraid of.

I wrote it down anyway. *Treated known fugitive. Unrepentant.* The word "unrepentant" is a favorite of ours. It means: he would do it again. Which, for a healer, is less a confession than a job description.

"You understand," I said, "that the tribunal sits tomorrow. If you recant—if you swear the oath of registration and surrender yourself to the collars—"

"The collars kill the gift," he said. "They kill other things too. My wife died two winters back. I kept her comfortable at the end with a charm my grandmother taught me. You take the gift, you take her last three days from me. I'd sooner keep those days and lose the rest."

I looked at him for a long moment. He looked back, mild as milk.

"I'll note your statement," I said finally, and my pen scratched, and outside the little barred window a crier was shouting the Edict for the ten-thousandth time—*register or burn, register or burn*—in the cheerful cadence of a man selling fish.

* * *

That afternoon I went up to the records room, because the fever-man Fen had treated had left traces, and traces lead to names, and names are how I do my work. I am good at my work. I want that understood before anything else I say here. Whatever else happened in Greyford, whatever doubts took root in me that week like weeds in a wall, I was *good*. Six years, four hundred registrations, thirty-one executions, and not one wrongful conviction that anyone ever proved. In this trade, that passes for a clean conscience.

The trail was thin. A smuggler road through Thornfell, a bridge toll paid in coin minted twenty years ago, a witness—a drover—who described a tall man with a mercenary's stride who had looked at the bridge's oath-stone "like he was listening to it" and then turned the whole caravan around, saving eleven lives when the span collapsed three days later. The drover hadn't thought anything of it at the time. Now, with the Edict loose in the passes, he thought a great deal of it, and had come to collect the informant's fee.

A man who listens to stones. I knew that shape. You spend enough years hunting the gifted, you learn their silhouettes the way a shepherd learns his flock—not their faces first, but their *ways*. The way they stand near walls. The way their eyes flick to sigils carved over doorways, checking, always checking, whether the ward is live.

I pulled the cross-reference files. Thornfell region, mercenary companies, prior flags. And there he was.

**ERIKSEN, SEBASTIAN.** Flagged under case designation **ASHFORD HOLLOW — 19 YEARS PRIOR.**

Not flagged as gifted. Flagged as *wanted*.

I sat back in my chair. The records room was cold; it's always cold, paper likes the cold, and maybe the dead do too. I pulled the Ashford Hollow file, and the leather cover came apart in my hands the way old files do, soft as skin, and I read.

Ashford Hollow. Every hunter knows the name even if the younger ones don't know why. Nineteen years ago, a village in the eastern Thornfells, wiped out in a single night. Forty-one dead. The official finding—and I mean the finding sealed with the Privy Seal, the one they taught us at the academy as a lesson in what happens when the gifted go ungoverned—was that a rogue oath-bearer, a mercenary in crown employ, had turned his covenant-gift against the village that refused to pay his company's protection tithe. The oath-binder forced the villagers to swear oaths they could not survive breaking, and when they broke them—as starving people always break oaths—they died. Forty-one souls, killed by contract. The mercenary fled before the crown's investigators arrived. Name entered in the Red Ledger. Bounty standing.

The name in the Red Ledger was Sebastian Eriksen.

I remembered him. That's the thing I need you to understand, the thing that started the crack in me that week, though I didn't know yet it was a crack and not just a draught under the door. I *remembered* him. Eighteen years ago—I'd been a corporal then, border patrol, before the Inquisitors' Office bought me out of the mud—we'd shared a fire for three days during the Kestrel Pass action. Eriksen. Quiet man, competent, the kind of soldier who checks everyone's straps before a march without being asked. There was a girl with him sometimes, a little dark-haired thing, maybe five years old, that the company assumed was a camp-follower's daughter until someone asked and Eriksen said, in that flat way of his, "She's mine." And then he'd gone back to oiling his sword as if he'd announced the weather.

I remember one night specifically. A deserter had been caught stealing rations, and the captain wanted him flogged, and the man begged, and the captain asked Eriksen—he was our oath-bearer, our walking guarantee—to bind the man to an oath of restitution instead. Cheaper than a flogging, kinder, and the captain thought it clever. And Eriksen looked at the deserter for a long time and said, "An oath given under threat isn't binding, sir. It's just cruelty with paperwork." The captain didn't like that. But he let the man go with a fine, and Eriksen fed him from his own pack before he walked out of camp.

That was the man in the Red Ledger. The butcher of Ashford Hollow. Forty-one dead by forced oath.

I sat in the cold room with the file open in front of me and felt the two facts grind against each other like millstones with nothing between them.

Because here is what I know about forced oaths, professionally, better than almost anyone alive: they leave marks. An oath broken unto death doesn't kill cleanly—it kills *specifically*, along the lines of the sworn penalty, and the bodies show it. Drowned men who swore by water. Burned men who swore by flame. I worked the aftermath of two such incidents early in my career, before the Edict, when we still investigated these things instead of merely punishing them. The evidence has a signature. It is unmistakable, once you've seen it.

So I did what any thorough hunter does. I sent to the provincial archive for the Ashford Hollow examination reports. The bodies' condition. The coroner's findings. The forensic basis of the conviction.

Three weeks later—they are not fast, archives; they are where documents go to be forgotten politely—a packet arrived. Thin. Suspiciously thin, for a mass-casualty examination. I opened it at my desk by lamplight, alone, which I should not have done, and which I am glad I did.

The examination reports were missing. All of them. What remained was the summary judgment, the Privy Seal finding, and a single page of field notes in an investigator's hand, unsigned, undated except by reference: *"No oath-signatures present on remains. Wounds consistent with blade and fire, conventional. Recommend further inquiry into company movements that night."*

And beneath that, in different ink, in a hand I recognized because I have seen it on a hundred orders since: *"Inquiry closed. Finding stands as issued."*

Finding stands as issued. Forty-one dead, no oath-signature, wounds consistent with steel and torch—the plain tools of any massacre ever committed by any band of men anywhere—and the crown's answer was to seal the verdict anyway and hang it around the neck of one mercenary who had the poor judgment to be an oath-bearer, and therefore useful, and therefore guilty of the crime of being *the right kind of monster for the story we wanted to tell.*

I put the page down. My lamp guttered. Outside, Greyford slept the sleep of the righteous, the town that had dragged Aldous Fen through its streets that morning while its children threw cabbage stalks, because fear makes crowds cruel and the Edict had made fear fashionable.

Here is what I want recorded, since I am recording everything now, since the ledger is the only confessor I trust: I did not weep. I did not tear the file up or storm to my superiors. I am not that kind of story. What I did was smaller and worse. I sat there and I did the arithmetic I have spent six years refusing to do.

Thirty-one executions. How many of them had files like this? How many Privy Seals, how many findings that stood as issued, how many convenient monsters? I had built my career on the certainty that the crown's justice was slow but true—that we might be blunt, but we were not *bent*. And here was a bent thing, nineteen years old, sealed with the highest seal in the land, and I had been teaching its verdict to recruits as gospel.

And the man we'd framed. The man whose bounty had stood for nineteen years, driving him deeper into the smuggler roads, deeper into hiding, deeper into exactly the kind of desperate invisibility that the Edict now punished with fire—was somewhere in my mountains right now, running from a crime somebody else committed, while I hunted him for a gift he'd never once used against the crown.

Or so the evidence suggested. Evidence can lie. So can old memories. I know that better than most; memory is the first thing a good interrogator learns to distrust, especially her own.

But the field notes were in ink, and ink doesn't flinch.

* * *

Captain-Commander Rell received me the next morning in the tollhouse's upper room, where the maps hang. He is a broad, gray man, patient as a plow, and I have trusted him for nine years. I laid the Ashford file on his table and told him plainly: the examination reports are missing, the surviving field notes contradict the sealed finding, and the flagged party—Eriksen—is currently active in our operational area under the Edict. Requesting the case be reopened before we proceed against him.

Rell listened the way he always listens, completely, without blinking. Then he poured two cups of small beer and pushed one across to me, which he only does when the news is bad.

"Ilse," he said, "do you know who signed the closure order?"

"I assumed the Privy Council."

"It came down through the Office. Through the Office, Ilse. Our own chain." He drank. "Nineteen years ago the crown needed the Ashford Hollow massacre to be the work of a rogue gift-bearer. There was a treaty vote that month. Fear of the gifted was the argument that carried it. A village full of dead civilians and a monstrous oath-bearer at large was worth more to that vote than the truth was." He set down the cup. "The finding wasn't a mistake. It was policy."

"Then the policy convicted an innocent man."

"The policy convicted a *useful* man. Whether he was innocent besides—" Rell shrugged, and the shrug was the worst part, worse than the missing files, worse than the forged seal. It was the shrug of a decent man who has made his peace with the machinery. "Eriksen may be exactly what the ledger says. He may not. It no longer matters, because the Edict doesn't distinguish, and neither can we. Our commission is registration and suppression. Justice—" and here he smiled, tired and kind, the smile of a man handing you a stone and calling it bread, "—justice is a department we lost funding for years ago."

"Then I'm requesting transfer of the case to my authority regardless. I'll run him down, and I'll examine the evidence myself, in the field, before any tribunal sees him."

"No."

"Captain-Commander—"

"You'll run him down," Rell said, "and you'll take him alive if you can, and he goes straight to the tribunal at Carrowmere, unexamined, as the Red Ledger directs. Those are my orders, and above me they are the Office's orders, and I will not have a first-rank hunter of your record spending crown resources exhuming a nineteen-year-old village." He paused, and gentled his voice, which he does when he thinks he's delivering mercy. "Ilse. You found a rotten plank. Good hunters find them. The floor still holds. Go catch your fugitive."

I saluted. I took my orders. I walked out into the grey morning with the Ashford file burning a hole in my satchel like a coal I couldn't put down, and I want it noted that I intended, at that moment, to obey. I was a servant of the law. The law had been bent; very well, I would bend within it, I would take Eriksen alive, I would protect him from the worst of it, I would find some narrow mercy inside the machine—

And then the dispatch caught up with me at the gate. A rider, mud to the hips, from the regional office. New orders, superseding Rell's, superseding everything.

*Subject ERIKSEN, S., Red Ledger priority. On contact: no capture required. Execute on sight. Tribunal unnecessary.*

Execute on sight. Because somewhere above Rell, someone had done the same arithmetic I had—had realized that a living Eriksen, questioned by a thorough hunter, might start pulling threads, and that threads, pulled, unravel.

They weren't sending me to arrest a fugitive anymore. They were sending me to bury a witness.

I stood in the gate of Greyford with the dispatch in my hand and looked north, toward the Thornfells, where the peaks wore their winter white like a borrowed purity, and somewhere under all that stone a man was running from a murder he didn't commit, and a daughter—I remembered the dark-haired girl at the fireside; gods, the girl had a name, and I'd never asked it—was somewhere, or wasn't, or was sold, or was dead, because that's what nineteen years of a false bounty does to the people orbiting a marked man.

Rell thought the floor held. The rider's orders said otherwise. The floor didn't hold; the floor was the fire they lit the village with.

I folded the dispatch into my coat, next to the Ashford file, next to the small hard seed of doubt that had taken root under my breastbone where my faith used to sit. Then I called for my horse, and my warrant, and two riders I trusted, and gave the order that would decide everything after:

"We ride for Thornfell. We ride light, and we ride quiet—and we get to Eriksen before anyone who believes those orders does."

Even then, I told myself I was going to bring him in. Alive.

I have never lied well. It's why they made me an interrogator.
