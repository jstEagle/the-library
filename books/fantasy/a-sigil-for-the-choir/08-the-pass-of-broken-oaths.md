## 8. The Pass of Broken Oaths

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in high places. Not the silence of empty rooms or dead hearths—those are just absences, easy to name. I mean the silence above the treeline, where the wind has scraped everything soft off the stone and what's left is only the mountain thinking its long, mineral thoughts. I had walked through it a hundred times in my smuggling years, and every time it reminded me of my god.

Not because the mountain felt holy. Because the mountain felt *ignored*, the way I imagined my god must feel—a great old thing standing in the weather while everyone below got on with their small urgent business.

We crossed into the Pass of Broken Oaths on the fourth morning out of Maren Ostvik's hollow, and even Maren, who was blind and therefore had no business flinching at scenery, stopped walking when we reached the first of the stones.

"What is it?" Tam asked. The boy had been chattering about his feet for an hour. His feet were fine. His feet were the finest feet in Thornfell, according to Tam.

"The covenant stones," Brannoc said quietly. He'd gone ahead and now stood among them like a man visiting graves. "They used to run the whole length of this pass. Waymarkers. Every one carved with an oath—the traveler's oath, the shepherd's oath, the oath of safe passage sworn between the lowland clans and the high." He crouched by the nearest one. It had been a pillar once, taller than a man, and now it was a pillar-shaped argument with itself, split down the middle and scattered. "Someone broke all of them."

"Vandals?" Odile said. She was walking with her hands in her sleeves the way she did when she was memorizing something. I'd learned that about her: Odile's hands were idle only when her mind was working hardest.

"Vandals topple," Brannoc said. "This is *unmaking*. Look at the cut faces."

I looked. I wished I hadn't. The broken faces of the stones weren't rough. They were smooth as river ice, and across them ran lines of script—not carved, not written, but *drained*, the letters sunk into the rock like footprints in snow that someone had tried to sweep away. I knew that look. I had spent eleven years looking at the inside of my own skull and seeing that exact quality of nothing.

"That's harvest-work," I said.

Nobody answered, which was answer enough.

Maren turned her face toward the pass, listening to it the way other people read it. "The wind sounds wrong here," she said. "Like a room after the furniture's been sold."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a shattered waymarker, because there was nowhere else flat enough for Odile's bad hip and sheltered enough for a fire. Maren declared the acoustics acceptable, which from her was practically a blessing. Tam fed the mules. We had three mules, one of which was named Duchess and belonged, technically, to nobody, having been acquired in Greyford through means Odile described as "administrative."

Brannoc didn't eat. He sat with his back against the broken stone and his ear against it, which looked absurd and wasn't, because Brannoc heard things in stone that the rest of us needed hammers to find.

"You should sleep," I told him.

"I'm listening."

"To what? The stone's dead. You said so yourself—it's been drained."

"I said it's been *emptied*," Brannoc said. "Emptied isn't dead. A cup that's been emptied still remembers being full." He pressed his palm flat against the fracture. "Sebastian. When your god went silent—did it feel like dying?"

The question landed in the middle of the camp like a dropped coin. Everyone went still around it. Odile stopped pretending to mend her glove. Tam stopped feeding Duchess handfuls of oats he was stealing from Duchess's own bag.

"It felt like nothing," I said finally. "That was the whole horror of it. Not grief. Not absence-with-a-shape. Just... nothing, where something had always been. Like waking up one morning and finding you'd never had a brother."

"Mm." Brannoc closed his eyes. "And if I told you it might not have been nothing? If I told you the silence had a *cause*?"

"Every priest who loses his god invents a cause," I said. "It's kinder than the truth."

"The truth," Brannoc said, "is that I can hear fragments in this rubble. Words. Oath-fragments, the old covenant language, the kind they only spoke at consecrations. And Sebastian—they aren't prayers. They're *pleas*. This stone isn't remembering an oath. It's remembering being made to stop saying one."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, loose scree ticked down the mountainside, patient as a clock.

"Say it plainly," Maren said. She had a gift for cutting through the theological undergrowth; blind people develop a horror of wasted words.

Brannoc opened his eyes. In the firelight he looked older than his years and younger than his despair, both at once. "Your god didn't fall silent," he said to me. "It was taken. Silenced the way these stones were silenced—harvested, drained, its voice carried off somewhere to be *used*. The court beneath the mountain doesn't just buy souls, Sebastian. I think it bought the voice of God, and it's been speaking law with it ever since."

I want to tell you I received this revelation with dignity. What I actually did was stand up, walk twenty paces into the dark, and be sick over the edge of the trail, because eleven years of praying into a hole in the world will do that to a man when somebody hands him a reason.

When I came back, nobody commented, which was its own kindness. Odile poured me tea. Tam scooted over on the log without being asked. Maren said, "Well. Now we know why the Judges' writs carry such weight. They've been signed with a stolen hand."

"And it changes nothing," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Wren's soul is still in their vault. Solstice is still nine days off. Knowing *why* the god is quiet doesn't open a single door down there."

"No," Maren agreed. "But it tells us what kind of enemy we're robbing. An enemy that can rob a god will not be robbed cheaply." She smiled, thin and dry. "I do enjoy knowing the price in advance. It's the only honest thing about any bargain."

* * *

The ambush came two days later, at the narrows where the pass pinched between a cliff face and a drop that would teach you humility on the way down.

I smelled it before I saw it, or rather I smelled the *wrongness*: cold iron and lamp oil and the particular boiled-wool stink of crown-issue cloaks. Six years I'd run these roads without a hunter's nose finding me, and I'd like to say it was skill. Mostly it was that hunters chase rumors, and I had spent six years making sure no rumor had a face.

That was over. Varga had my name in her ledger now, and names travel faster than men.

"Halt!" The voice cracked off the cliff wall. Crossbows on the ledge above—I counted four glints, then five, then gave up counting because the number kept getting worse. Below us, where the trail widened into a shelf, eight soldiers in grey and black fanned out with the unhurried confidence of people who had done this many times and never once been interrupted.

Captain Ilse Varga stood at their center, and up close she was exactly as I remembered her from Greyford square: broad, weathered, with a scar through one eyebrow and the terrible calm of a woman who believes completely in what she's doing. Believers are the worst kind of hunter. Fanatics you can bait. Believers you can only survive.

"Sebastian Eriksen," she called up. We had the high ground, technically, but high ground is a loan, not a gift. "By warrant of the Crown, in the matter of unregistered gifts and the crime of sedition against the Court's lawful order. Surrender, and your companions will be registered, not burned."

"She's lying," Odile murmured. "Registration ends at the pyre nine times out of ten. I've forged enough registries to know how few names stay in them."

"I know," I said.

"You're outnumbered, Eriksen," Varga called. "You're on a dead-end trail. And whatever you think you're carrying down that mountain—" her eyes flicked to Maren, to Brannoc's priest's collar grown ragged, to Tam, whose face had gone the color of tallow— "it won't be worth five deaths."

Here is what I should have done: negotiated. Stalled. Given her Odile's forged credentials, which were very good and would have bought us an hour, and an hour is a country in the right terrain. That was the plan. That had always been the plan—we were soul-clerks, couriers, boring people with boring papers.

What happened instead was Duchess.

Duchess, our administratively acquired mule, had died in the night. Quietly, of age and altitude, in the manner of mules. We'd discussed burying her and decided the ground was too hard and the schedule too tight, and Tam had volunteered to "see to her," and I had been too busy staring at broken covenant stones to ask what that meant.

So when the crossbow bolt took Odile's pack strap off her shoulder—warning shot, precise, Varga's work—and the soldiers started up the slope, and Tam made a sound I had never heard a human boy make, a sound like a hinge giving way in his chest—

Duchess stood up.

Dead. Three days dead by morning, stiff as a gatepost, eyes gone white as the covenant stones' drained script. And she stood up anyway, because Tam's gift is not necromancy in the theatrical sense—he doesn't command the dead, he *asks* them, and apparently the dead trust him the way the living don't—and Duchess planted herself across the narrow shelf of the trail, a half-ton wall of dead mule, and screamed. Mules don't scream. Dead ones especially shouldn't. The sound came out of her like air forced through a broken organ pipe, and two soldiers sat down in the scree entirely of their own accord, and one of the crossbowmen on the ledge fired wide and hit his own officer's shield.

"*Gifted!*" someone shouted, and the word did more damage than the mule. The formation broke. Discipline is a story soldiers tell themselves, and the story had just acquired a plot hole the size of a resurrected donkey.

"Tam," I said, very calmly, because one of us had to be, "what did you do?"

"He said he was cold," Tam said. His voice was shaking. So were his hands, and yet Duchess stood there immovable, steam rising off her in the cold air, which was wrong, corpses shouldn't steam, except the steaming wasn't coming from her, it was coming from *him*, from the effort bleeding off him like heat off a forge. "In the night. He said he was cold. I couldn't just—leave him."

"You talked to the mule."

"He talks to everybody! He's very lonely!"

A bolt skipped off stone near Brannoc's head. Below, Varga was rebuilding her line with her voice alone, hauling her soldiers back into shape through sheer force of certainty, and I understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful that the plan was dead. The papers were dead. The fiction of five boring travelers was dead. There was a screaming dead mule blocking a mountain pass and a boy leaking heat like a kettle and a former priest shouting scripture at the sky and somewhere in it all, my secret—the last one, the one I'd guarded for eleven years—was about to become the least interesting thing on this mountainside.

Because Varga's men were regrouping, and Duchess could not hold forever, and Tam was already swaying.

"New plan," Odile said, tearing the bottom hem off her skirt to bind Tam's nosebleed. "Run?"

"Run," I agreed.

Maren caught my arm. Her blind eyes found my face with unnerving accuracy. "The narrows end in a scramble down to the old pilgrim stair, forty paces past the last stone. I can hear the gap in the wind. Go left at the cairn."

"Left at the cairn," I repeated.

"And Sebastian." Her grip tightened. "Whatever you're about to do instead of running—do it fast."

She knew me too well. Because here was the arithmetic: Duchess would fall, or Tam would, and either way eight soldiers and a believer with a ledger would come over that shelf after us, and they would follow us down the stair, and they would follow us to the gate, and everything Wren had bought with her soul would be spent for nothing.

Unless the road behind us stopped being a road.

I turned to face the last of the covenant stones—the big one, the traveler's oath, still standing though split to its heart. Eleven years I'd hidden my gift so well I'd half convinced myself I didn't have it. Reading oaths, hearing the promises soaked into things—that was all it was, a small sly talent, a smuggler's trick. But oaths are load-bearing. Any mason knows it. Half the walls in Thornfell stand because somebody swore on them once, and the swearing hasn't worn out yet.

I put my hand on the broken stone and listened.

The traveler's oath was still in there, gutted but present, the way Brannoc said a cup remembers being full. *Safe passage, sworn between high and low, until the mountain forgets its own name.* I read its failing architecture the way I'd read the bridge-stone a lifetime ago, and I found what I always found: the seam. The place where a promise holds.

Then I did the thing I had never once dared to do in front of witnesses.

I pulled.

The oath came apart in my grip like wet rope. I felt it go—felt the covenant that had held this pass for three hundred years unravel through my palm and out into the air as a sound like a struck bell dying—and the mountain noticed. The cliff face above the trail sighed, settled, and shed itself. Not a collapse. A *decision*. Tons of old grey stone sliding down across the narrows with the unhurried majesty of a curtain falling, sealing the pass behind us in a roar that swallowed Varga's shouts, swallowed the crossbow bolts, swallowed everything but the ringing in my ears and Duchess, bless her rotting heart, trotting gamely after us down the pilgrim stair because Tam had asked her to and she hadn't finished being asked.

We ran. All of us, laughing or crying or both—Tam sobbing into Odile's shoulder, Odile swearing with the fluency of a woman whose vocabulary had been forged in worse places than churches, Brannoc chanting something that might have been thanks and might have been apology, Maren navigating by the wind's gap with her hand in mine.

At the bottom, where the stair met the valley shadow, I stopped and looked back up. The pass was gone. Dust hung over it like a held breath. Somewhere behind that wall, Ilse Varga was standing in the wreckage of her warrant, learning what I had learned years ago: that the law's roads do not all lead where the law says.

"Well," Odile said, hands on hips, breathing hard. "No more papers. No more pretending. Every hunter between here and the lowlands will have our descriptions by week's end, and mine will be *flattering*, which is somehow worse." She looked around at the five of us—six, counting Duchess, who stood swaying at the trail's edge with her white eyes fixed on Tam like a dog waiting to be told it was a good girl. "Fugitives, then. Officially. Together."

"Together," Tam echoed, and grinned through the blood on his face, and Duchess made her broken-organ-pipe sound, which I choose to believe was agreement.

Brannoc was the last to turn away from the sealed pass. He touched the pilgrim stair's oldest step—an oath-stone too, worn nearly smooth by centuries of feet—and something crossed his face that I couldn't read.

"What?" I asked him.

"Back there. When you pulled the oath apart." He hesitated. "For half a heartbeat, Sebastian—in the second before it broke—I heard something in the seam. Underneath the covenant. A voice, trying to come through the gap you made." His eyes were bright and afraid. "It said *soon*."

The god's word, or an echo, or a hope wearing a voice. Nine days to solstice. I picked up my pack and started walking, and behind me, dead Duchess followed the living down the mountain, and none of us looked back again.
