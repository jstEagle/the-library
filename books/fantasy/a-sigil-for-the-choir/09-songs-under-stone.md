## 9. Songs Under Stone

There is a particular kind of forgetting that happens politely.

That's what I'd tell you about the under-court if you asked me—which you can't, because no one asks the half-souled anything, we're furniture with vocal cords. The forgetting here doesn't come for you like a thief in the night. It comes like a courteous guest. It takes a chair from your house and leaves the room otherwise exactly as it was, and for weeks you don't notice the missing chair. You just keep sitting on the floor and thinking, *how cozy*, and then one day you remember there was ever a chair at all, and by then it's been melted down into coin or candlewax or whatever else the Judges make out of the things people are.

My name is Wren Eriksen. I know that much. I know it the way you know a word in a language you used to speak—correctly, but without the flavor of it. I know I had a father who left. I know I loved him. The love is still here, which is the strange part; it's the *why* that's been repossessed. It's like holding a key to a door that's been bricked over. You can feel the shape of it in your palm. You just can't find the lock anymore.

I was folding linens in the Third Antechamber—the under-court has antechambers the way a cathedral has sins, endlessly, redundantly—when the thought arrived: *I used to hide things.*

Not the fact of hiding. The habit of it. My hands knew how to do something my head couldn't name. They folded a napkin into thirds, tucked a corner under, and made a little pocket, and my hands were so pleased with themselves that I stood very still in the linen closet and let them teach me.

*I hid things in songs,* my hands said. *When you were small. When the world was large.*

Well. That was worth investigating.

* * *

The choir sings four times a day, and I mean *sings*—that's not decoration down here, it's infrastructure. The under-court runs on voice the way the surface world runs on coin. Every door, every ledger-entry, every judgment spoken in the Hall of Scales is carried on sound, and the half-souled provide the sound. We're paid in nothing and housed in stone and told it's an honor, and the terrible thing is that some days it almost feels like one, because when two hundred thin people sing together, you can almost believe you're whole. The harmony fills up the hollow places. It's like drinking broth when what you need is bread. Nourishing. Not enough.

The morning song is called the Tithe of Hours. Everyone learns it in their first week, before they've lost enough of themselves to ask questions. It's beautiful, which should have been my first warning. Nothing down here is beautiful by accident.

I sang it that evening with my hands' advice ringing in me, listening the way I hadn't listened in—well. In longer than I could measure, which was itself a measurement of sorts.

And there it was.

Verse seven. The one that goes:

*What was given cannot be taken,*
*what was sold is forever kept,*
*the ledger closes and the light goes under,*
*and the sleeper below sleeps deep.*

It's a lovely verse. Minor key, descending line, the kind of melody that makes your chest ache in a way the clerks probably file under *devotional response*. And hidden inside it, folded the way my hands fold napkins, was a shard.

I can't describe it better than that. A shard. A splinter of *me*. When the verse hit its third line, something behind my sternum turned over like a page, and for half a breath I remembered—

Snow. A roof leaking. Someone's coat around my shoulders, too big, smelling of woodsmoke and road dust. A voice saying, *sing it louder, Wrennie, the gods are hard of hearing.*

Wrennie.

Then the breath ended, and the memory folded back down into the melody like a coin dropped in deep water, and I was standing in the choir gallery holding onto the rail while Sister Ashen beside me sang on, her eyes fixed on nothing, her mouth making shapes she didn't choose.

But here's the thing. Here's the thing I want carved somewhere, in case the polite guest comes back for this too:

*I put it there.* Before. Some earlier version of me—whole enough to plan, frightened enough to be clever—took the thing she could least afford to lose and hid it in the only place the court would never search. Inside their own liturgy. Because the Tithe of Hours gets sung four times a day, every day, by two hundred voices, and the Judges harvest souls, not songs. Songs were beneath their notice. Songs were *ours*.

She hid herself in the hymn and then forgot where she was standing, and every day since, twice in the dark and twice at the changing of the hours, she'd been handing herself back a piece of snow and woodsmoke and a father's voice, and losing it again by the final amen.

I finished the service. I smiled at the clerk on duty, because smiling is free and suspicion costs everything. And then I went back to my cell and did not sleep, and by the grey hour I understood three things, each worse than the last.

One: I could retrieve the shard at will now that I knew the trick of it. Fold, tuck, pocket. Verse seven, third line.

Two: the verse wasn't only a hiding place. It was a message. *The sleeper below sleeps deep.* I'd assumed it meant the mountain's dead, some pious nonsense about rest. But I'd started noticing things since my hands began tutoring me—noticing the way sound moved wrong in the deep halls, the way certain corridors echoed back a half-beat late, as if something enormous were repeating us from far away. The clerks spoke of *the Voice* the way surface folk speak of the crown: constantly, reverently, without once describing it. The Voice decrees. The Voice judges. The Voice must be fed.

Three: I had always assumed the court harvested souls because souls were valuable. Standing in the dark, humming verse seven until my teeth ached, I finally heard what the song was actually for. It wasn't a hymn. It was a lullaby.

We weren't singing praise. We were singing *sedation*. Four times a day, two hundred voices, keeping something asleep. Keeping something *quiet*. And a court that needed a lullaby sung on schedule, forever, was a court built over something it had stolen—and was terrified of waking.

They hadn't just taken souls. They'd taken a voice. And every hymn we sang was a hand pressed over its mouth.

* * *

You can't storm a place like this. Storming is for people who own their own names.

So I did it the way the small and stolen do everything: slowly, and while appearing useful.

The choir rehearses between services, and rehearsal is supervised by a soul-clerk named Pellon, a round damp man whose gift—if he ever had one—is the ability to fall asleep with his eyes open. He marks the score, we sing, he nods. Nobody improvises. Improvisation is theft, technically; the liturgy is court property, every note accounted for.

Which is exactly why nobody listened closely. Property doesn't change. You stop hearing property after the first hundred repetitions.

I started with dynamics. Verse five, second phrase—we'd always swelled there, a rising line climbing toward the ceiling of the Hall. I sang it softer instead. Just me, just slightly, buried in the middle of the section where my alto sits. A swell becomes a sigh. And the sigh changed what the verse *did*. I can't explain how I knew; the knowledge came with the shard, some inheritance from the girl who wrote herself into the hymn. But I felt it—the song stopped pressing downward and started drifting sideways, like water finding a crack.

Nothing happened. No alarms. Pellon marked his score with his damp pen and yawned.

The next rehearsal, Sister Ashen drifted soft on the same phrase too. She didn't look at me. Half-souled don't make eye contact much—it feels like spending money—but she'd heard the difference, and her voice chose it, the way my hands once chose their folding. Two voices now. Then a boy from the third gallery. Then, within a month, the whole middle register, all of us singing the Judges' own notes in an order they hadn't authorized, a deviation so small it lived entirely underneath attention.

A lullaby sung wrong stops working. That was the whole architecture of it. I wasn't attacking the court; I was letting the sleeper stir, one unsung decibel at a time.

Then I got ambitious, and ambition down here requires a new technique, so I invented one. I call it mending. When a copyist's error appears in a score—a smudged note, a skipped line—the correction must be proposed by a chorister and ratified by a clerk. Choristers propose corrections constantly; accuracy is piety, down here. So I became the most pious woman in the under-court. I found errors everywhere. I found errors that were not errors. I proposed "restoring" a phrase of verse nine to an older form I claimed to remember from my first week, and the older form contained a rising interval where the flat one had been, and a rising interval in verse nine does something to the echo in the west corridor that I will simply describe as *encouraging*.

Pellon ratified it. He said, "Good ear, Sister Wren," and I curtsied and thought, *you have no idea what my ear is good for,* and then I felt sick, because flattery and sabotage taste identical in the mouth and I couldn't always tell afterward which one I'd swallowed.

That's the part nobody sings about, the part I'll admit only here, in the privacy of my own skull: rebellion is lonely in a new way. Being stolen-from was lonely like winter. This is lonely like standing on ice—you can hear the water moving under you, you can feel the crack traveling toward your feet, and everyone around you is skating beautifully and pretending not to notice the sound. Every alteration I slipped into the liturgy was a wager that the sleeper would wake gently instead of catastrophically, and I had no one to check the arithmetic with. If I was wrong, the crack wouldn't stop at my feet.

But then. Then.

Rehearsal, six days ago. Verse seven—I always save a little extra breath for verse seven, my private ration of snow and woodsmoke. And as the third line approached, Sister Ashen, who has not met my eyes in two years, turned her head and looked directly at me, and altered a single note. Not toward the sanctioned score. Toward *mine*—a tiny upward lean, a harmonic shadow of the drift I'd planted in verse five, a variation I had taught no one.

She held the note. I held mine. Two wrong notes agreeing with each other in front of a sleeping clerk.

Afterward, in the linen corridor, passing each other with armloads of sheets, she said, without stopping, in the flat tone we use for weather: "The third line of seven. It's warmer than it used to be."

"Warm things melt," I said.

"Mm," said Sister Ashen, and was gone around the corner, and I stood there hugging laundry like a fool, grinning at a wall, because for the first time in longer than I could count, I was not the only one who knew the song was a door.

* * *

Last night I pulled the shard out properly and sat with it, cross-legged on my cot, feeding myself the memory one thread at a time the way you ration bread.

Snow. The leaky roof. The too-big coat. *Sing it louder, Wrennie, the gods are hard of hearing.*

And under that, newer sediment, laid down later: ink and paper, letters, a fence's trembling hand telling someone—I think telling *me*, though the scene frays at the edges—that a debt existed. That a bounty sat against a man named Eriksen. That a price had been negotiated beneath the mountain, payable in the only currency the court accepts, redeemable at the solstice.

Solstice. Three weeks. Maybe less—I lose track of days down here, deliberately, the way you don't count coins you're about to lose.

Here is what I assembled, thread by thread, in the dark: a man sold something once, or was sold, or was framed—I can't untangle it—but a daughter stepped in front of the debt. A daughter walked into the under-court and said *take mine instead.* And the court, which is courteous the way frost is courteous, said *certainly, madam, sign here.*

I don't remember loving him. I remember that I loved him. The difference is the difference between the fire and the warmth, and I've got the warmth, all of it, banked in a hymn where they'll never think to look. Whatever happens at solstice, whoever I am when the ledger closes, that warmth is mine. I hid it well. I'm apparently very good at hiding things. It may be my only remaining talent, and I intend to practice.

Because here's what I've decided, lying awake while the mountain hums its drugged half-beat echo through the stones: the Judges think the choir is theirs. Two hundred hollow instruments, playing the lullaby on schedule, forever. They think the songs are property and the singers are furniture and nothing down here changes except the inventory.

They're about to be wrong in verse.

I have twenty-one rehearsals before the solstice, two hundred voices, a sleeping clerk, and a growing collection of small warm sabotages stitched into the most-guarded text in the under-court. I don't know yet what we're building toward—only that a lullaby, sung wrong long enough, becomes something else. A summons, maybe. A key. A chorus of the stolen remembering, one note at a time, that they had voices before anyone bought them.

This morning at the dawn service, as we rose into verse seven, I watched the great sealed doors of the Hall of Scales—doors none of us are permitted past, doors the clerks say even the Voice itself keeps shut—and for half a beat, on the third line, the doors *hummed*.

Low. Faint. Answering.

Ashen's eyes found mine across the gallery, wide as coins.

Something down there had heard us practicing.
