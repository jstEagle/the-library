## 10. Odile's Bargain

The thing about honest work, I've always said, is that it pays badly and smells worse.

I'd spent four days in the back room of a dyer's shop in Lowmark, hunched over a table that wobbled like a drunk on ice, forging credentials for six people to walk into hell. Court writs of passage. Not the lowland crown's paper—that I could do in my sleep, and had—but under-court writs, which meant learning an entirely new alphabet of lies. The court beneath Thornfell didn't use ink the way we do. They used sigils pressed into vellum while it was still damp, sigils that *meant* things, and if your forgery meant the wrong thing even slightly, the gate-readers would taste it like a sommelier tasting vinegar.

So I'd stolen three authentic samples before we ever left Greyford territory—two from a dead soul-clerk's satchel, one off a magistrate's courier who was too busy drinking to notice his own belt-pouch lighter—and I'd spent four days copying them down to their scars. Every crease. Every watermark shaped like a closed eye. Every little flaw where the original clerk's hand had slipped, because the under-court apparently trusted imperfection the way bankers trust a scarred coin: proof it had passed through real hands.

"You're muttering again," Maren Ostvik said from the doorway.

"I'm *conversing*," I said. "With the document. It's a professional relationship."

"Does it talk back?"

"It will if I get it wrong." I held up the writ I was working on—a passage credential for one 'Brannoc Vael, lay brother, retired.' "This one has to say he's been dead for eleven years. Resurrected clerks get fast-tracked past the gates. Apparently the Judges find resurrection flattering."

Maren found her way to the table by sound, the way she did everything, her head tilting toward my breathing like a bird listening for worms. Her eyes were milk-white and fixed somewhere near my left ear, which she claimed was my most honest feature.

"And what does yours have to say about you?" she asked.

"Mine?"

"Your writ. Sebastian said you'd need papers too. Odile Ferren, journeyman sigil-clerk." She smiled, and there was something in it that made me want to check my pockets. "You've been avoiding making your own all week. I can hear you skip it. Every time you reach that stack, your hands go quiet."

Blind women shouldn't be allowed to be perceptive. It violates some natural order.

"My hands are fine," I said.

"Your hands are *loud*, girl. They've been loud since you got here. Now they've gone careful, and careful hands are hiding hands." She sat down across from me, folding herself onto the stool with the grace of a woman who'd been navigating darkness so long she'd made peace with it. "What aren't you telling us?"

I looked at the sixth blank writ. My name wasn't even on it yet. Just the empty space where a sigil would go, waiting to be filled with whatever lie I chose.

"The magistrate in Harrowgate," I said finally. "Corwin Hale. He's the one who processes descent permits for the southern approach. I knew him years ago, when I worked the river towns. He'll sell us legitimate writs—for a price."

"That sounds almost easy."

"It is easy. That's the problem. Corwin doesn't want coin." I turned the blank writ over, studying the watermarked eye on its reverse as though it might blink first. "He wants *craft*. A specific piece. There's a woman in Harrowgate, a merchant widow named Casselin, who's been trying to have her husband's trading charter authenticated for two years. The original burned in a warehouse fire that Corwin himself probably set. If someone could produce a forgery good enough to pass the guild's own verification—the deep verification, the kind they only run for charters over a certain value—he'd wave our whole company through the southern gate with real credentials. No risk at the checkpoints. Nothing to explain."

"And the price after?" Maren asked quietly.

I should have lied to her. I want that noted, somewhere, by whoever keeps accounts on such things: I *tried* to lie to her, and what came out instead was the truth, because blind women strip the varnish off you without ever lifting a brush.

"He wants my sigil-hand," I said. "After the job. He wants me bound into his service. Forged charters, forged wills, forged anything he needs, for as long as he needs it. And the binding isn't metaphorical—it's a contract-sigil, pressed into the skin. Break it and the hand goes numb. Permanently."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the dyer's vats gurgled like something digesting.

"You told Sebastian this?" Maren asked.

"No."

"Why not?"

Because Sebastian Eriksen looks at people the way a man looks at a fire he's afraid of needing. Because he'd already lost one person to a bargain made on his behalf, and I'd seen what the knowledge of that was doing to him—how he flinched now at every mention of debt or price or cost, how he'd started saying *we don't owe anyone anything* in that voice that meant he was praying to a god who never answered. Because if I told him, he'd refuse the deal, and then we'd go through the front gate with clever fakes, and clever fakes get people killed in the under-court, and I'd watched Tam bury his dead mule with more ceremony than most men give their brothers, and I was not going to be the reason any of these beautiful idiots died at a checkpoint.

"He doesn't need to know," I said. "It's a business arrangement. I've made worse."

"Have you?"

I thought about the Duke of Callowmere's second wife, whose death certificate I'd written eleven days before she died of natural causes. I thought about the bishop's seal I'd cut for a man who turned out to be a slaver, and the three weeks I'd spent afterward forging abolitionist pamphlets out of pure spite, free of charge, just to balance the ledger in my own conscience.

"Yes," I said. "And better ones too. This is right in the middle."

Maren reached across the table and found my wrist with fingers light as moth wings. She didn't grip. She just rested there, reading my pulse the way I read watermarks.

"When do you meet him?" she said.

"Tomorrow. Dusk. His house above the tannery row."

"And you'll sign?"

"I already signed," I said, and showed her the inside of my left forearm, where Corwin's clerk had pressed the provisional mark two nights ago, when I'd gone to Harrowgate alone to negotiate. A thin gray line, like a vein of lead under the skin. "The final binding happens after delivery. But the intent's already inked. He was very thorough about that part. Said he'd learned from experience not to trust artists' promises."

Maren was silent for a long moment. Then she laughed—not cruelly, but with the weary amusement of a woman who'd heard every variety of human stupidity and had stopped being surprised by any of them.

"You know what you remind me of?" she said. "A hedge-witch I trained under, forty years ago. She used to say the law is just a story rich people tell so they don't have to feel the knife. And you—you're out here writing your own stories. Better ones. Nicer knives."

"That's very flattering."

"It wasn't a compliment. She also went blind from reading curses by candlelight and died alone in a bog." She released my wrist. "But here's what I actually came to say. You're doing this because you think it's the cheapest price on the table. You've done the arithmetic. Six lives against one hand. Good trade, clean math, no one has to cry about it."

"Isn't that how trades work?"

"No," Maren said. "That's how *fences* work. Trades leave both parties standing. Fences leave one side richer and the other side smaller." She stood, finding her balance with a hand on the wobbling table. "You think you're spending a hand. Spend it if you must. But know this much: the moment you let them take it, you'll start believing you deserved it. That's how bindings work. Not the sigil—the belief. The sigil's just the receipt."

She found the door without touching the frame and left me alone with my forgery and my arithmetic.

* * *

Corwin Hale's house sat above the tannery row like a fat spider over a web of stink, all shuttered windows and imported glass. I arrived at dusk carrying the Casselin charter in a leather tube, wearing my best traveling dress and my second-best face—the one that says *I am competent and slightly bored and absolutely not desperate.*

He received me in a study lined with confiscated books. I recognized the spines. Half of them had come from the same purge that put the healer in irons at Greyford, and seeing them shelved and dusted and *kept* made my teeth ache in a way I hadn't anticipated. Collectors. The worst kind of thieves. At least I sold things back to people who wanted them.

"Odile Ferren." He poured wine without asking, which I counted as a small declaration of war. "Still pretty. Still punctual. I remember both."

"You remember the money," I said. "The rest is decoration."

He laughed and settled behind his desk, and I laid the tube down between us like a duelist laying down a blade. He unrolled the charter with hands that were softer than his reputation, and for a long moment he said nothing, just moved his magnifying lens across the vellum, checking the guild-marks, the fiber weave, the aging wash I'd applied with tea and iron-gall and a week of patience.

I'll be honest: watching another forger examine your work is a special kind of torture. You stand there cataloguing every shortcut you took, every place you hurried, and your mouth goes dry and your heart does arithmetic at speed. I stood there and remembered that I'd aged the wax seal using a technique I invented myself, and that if he recognized the technique he'd know it was mine, and if he knew it was mine—

"It's good," he said at last.

I exhaled through my nose, carefully.

"It's better than good. It's the finest piece of fraud I've seen in fifteen years of buying it." He rolled the charter up and slid it into his desk drawer, and the click of the lock sounded obscenely loud. "Widow Casselin will weep with joy, the guild verifiers will weep with shame, and I will collect a commission that would embarrass a bishop. Everyone profits. Isn't trade wonderful?"

"Our other arrangement," I said. "Six writs of descent. Legitimate. Sealed with your office."

"Already drafted." He produced them from the same drawer, fan-folded, each bearing the gray-green seal of the under-court's southern chancery. Real seals. Real authority. Documents that would open gates I couldn't have counterfeited in a year. "Passage for six pilgrims, retinue status, exemption from checkpoint inspection. They'll carry your friends past every ward between here and the vault doors." He smiled. "Assuming, of course, that the final item changes hands."

I pushed back my sleeve and laid my forearm on the desk, the provisional mark gray and gleaming.

"Then finish it," I said.

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Corwin Hale's face that wasn't greed. It might have been reluctance. It might have been indigestion. With men like Corwin, the two are indistinguishable.

"You understand the terms," he said. "Indefinite service. Exclusive craft. Any refusal of assigned work voids the contract, and the hand—" he gestured vaguely at my arm, "—goes quiet. Forever. You'll still have it. It just won't answer you anymore. Like a servant who's decided to stop listening."

"I understand."

"You could walk away," he said, and here was the strange thing: he said it gently. "Take the writs anyway. I wouldn't stop you—I never catch anyone, that's not my profession. But the mark would spread. By morning it'd be at your elbow. By week's end, your shoulder. It's patient work, the binding. It doesn't chase. It just waits for you to come back and finish what you started." He dipped a stylus in ink-black paste and held it over my arm. "Last chance to be a coward. Most people take it. Cowards live longer."

I thought about Tam, seventeen years old and grave-dirt under his nails, who talked to his dead like they were shy cousins. About Brannoc, who heard God in rubble and was ashamed of it. About Sebastian, who prayed every night to a silence and called it faith. About Wren, whom I'd never met, who'd sold the only thing she owned to save a father who didn't know—and whose soul was sitting in a jar under a mountain while we bickered about travel documents.

About Maren saying: *you'll start believing you deserved it.*

"Cowards live longer," I agreed. "But they die smaller."

I turned my arm over, palm up, and gave him the wrist.

The stylus bit. The paste went in cold, then hot, then cold again, tracing lines along the old provisional mark until the two became one design—a spiral, tight and deliberate, coiling from wrist to mid-forearm. It didn't hurt the way cuts hurt. It hurt the way oaths hurt, the way signatures hurt: a sense of something settling into place that cannot be unsettled. When he finished, the spiral pulsed once, faintly warm, and went still.

"There," Corwin said, capping his stylus. "Now you're mine."

"Now I'm *leased*," I corrected. "Ownership implies maintenance, and you strike me as a man who lets his assets rot."

He laughed again, and this time it was real, and somehow that was worse.

The writs went into my satchel. The charter stayed in his drawer. At the door, he paused with his hand on the frame and said, without turning around:

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I've bought a lot of hands over the years. Painters, accountants, poisoners. Yours is the first I've felt bad about." A pause. "Don't tell anyone. It ruins my rates."

* * *

I walked back through Harrowgate in the dark with my sleeve pulled down and six genuine miracles in my bag, and I felt exactly as terrible as I'd expected to feel, which was a small mercy. Anticipated misery weighs less than the surprise kind. Ask any con artist.

The others were camped in the ruin of a waystation chapel two miles out—Sebastian's idea, something about holy ground making hunters hesitant. Tam was teaching a dead crow to whistle. Brannoc was pretending not to listen. Sebastian sat sharpening a knife that was already sharp, because some men process fear through whetstones.

I handed him the writs. He examined the seals, the chancery marks, the inspection exemptions, and his eyebrows climbed slowly toward his hairline.

"These are real."

"Genuine article. Chancery-issued. I have connections."

"What did they cost?"

"A favor," I said. "An old debt. Nothing that concerns the mission."

He looked at me for a long moment. Sebastian has eyes like a well—you drop something in and wait ages for the splash, and sometimes it never comes. I met his gaze with my second-best face and my first-best smile, and I kept my sleeve down over the spiral, and I thought about Maren's voice: *the moment you let them take it, you'll start believing you deserved it.*

"Well," Sebastian said finally, tucking the writs away. "Whatever it was—you didn't pay it alone. That's not how this works anymore. None of us pays alone."

"Lovely sentiment," I said. "Completely untrue, but lovely."

He almost smiled. Then he went back to his knife, and I went to my bedroll, and I lay awake listening to the chapel's broken rafters tick as they cooled, feeling the spiral pulse faintly against my sleeve like a second heartbeat with someone else's rhythm.

Here's the thing about honor among thieves: nobody writes it down. There's no ledger, no charter, no sealed writ. It lives in the space between what you promise and what you deliver, and it's enforced by nothing except the terrible private knowledge of what you are. The crown has its laws. The court below has its judgments. I have my code, which fits on the back of a receipt:

*Deliver what you sold. Lie only upward—never to the small, the grieving, or the paying. And never, ever forge something true.*

I broke the third rule tonight, in a way. What I forged was true enough to pass for truth, and it freed six people, and it cost me a hand that still moves and always will, technically—just never again at my own direction.

Fair trade? No such thing. There's only the trade you can live with, and the trade you can't, and the long gray country in between where people like me keep our houses.

I fell asleep doing sums I already knew the answers to.

And in the morning, when we broke camp and turned south toward the mountain, the spiral warmed against my skin—once, twice, patient as a creditor—and somewhere far above us, on the high road, I swear I heard hooves.
