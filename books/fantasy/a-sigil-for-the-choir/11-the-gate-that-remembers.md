## 11. The Gate That Remembers

The mountain had a door, and the door had opinions.

We'd been climbing for six days when Maren stopped walking without stopping—her staff planted, her blind face tilted up like she was reading weather in a sky only she could see. Which, in fairness, was exactly what she was doing. She read everything that way. Sound, stone, the memory trapped in old mortar. I'd watched her navigate us through three days of fog by listening to the way water dripped off ledges, and I'd stopped doubting her somewhere around the second morning and started being quietly terrified of her instead.

"Here," she said. "This is where it stops pretending to be a mountain."

I looked at what she meant. The pass narrowed into a throat of black granite, and set into that granite was a gate. Not a gate like men build—no bars, no hinges, no wood anywhere—but a slab of seamless stone maybe twenty feet high, carved with lines so shallow you'd miss them unless the light hit wrong. When the afternoon sun slid across its face, the lines woke up. They weren't decoration. They were writing, and the writing moved, slow as honey, rearranging itself while we watched.

"That's not a lock," Tam said. He'd gone pale around the mouth, which for Tam was an achievement; the boy handled corpses with better appetite than most men handle breakfast. "Locks want keys. That wants—" he swallowed, "—that wants *reasons*."

"It wants a name," Maren said. She laid her palm flat against the stone, careful as a woman touching a sleeping animal. Her lips moved. Whatever she heard, it took a long time coming out.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the gate because there wasn't anywhere else. The wind came down off the high snowfields with knives in it, and Odile rigged a shelter from oiled canvas and two dead saplings, muttering the whole time about how in civilized country one built shelters *before* needing them. Brannoc sat apart, as he always did now, his ear turned toward the mountain like a man straining to hear a conversation in the next room.

I found him there at dusk. His hands were folded, his eyes closed, and when I sat down beside him he didn't open them but he did shift—six inches, no more—to make room. From Brannoc, that was a sermon on brotherhood.

"You hear something," I said.

"I hear a wall," he said. "A very large wall, thinking very hard." His mouth twitched. "You know what's strange, Sebastian? I served the Quiet God for thirty years. Thirty years of silence, and I told myself the silence was the point—that faith without answer was the purest kind. And then this past week, walking under these peaks, I feel Him like heat off a forge fire. Muffled. Straining." He opened his eyes. "He was never silent. He was *muzzled*. There's a difference, and the difference is a crime."

"And we're going to commit a few of our own," I said, "to undo it."

"Amen," Brannoc said, with feeling.

Behind us, Odile snorted. She'd come up quiet as she did everything, a woman whose footsteps apologized on their way out. "Careful, priest. The last man who preached justice to a magistrate got a lovely cell and a shorter career."

"The last man who forged a magistrate's seal got a bounty and a nickname," Brannoc returned mildly.

"'The Silver Hand' is a *dignified* nickname."

"Your hand is ink-stained."

"Dignified ink."

I let them go on. It had become a ritual of ours, the bickering—Tam called it evening prayers—and I understood why. Fear shared is fear halved, but fear *mocked*, given a silly hat and made to dance, is fear quartered. We were four days from the solstice and none of us said so, but all of us counted it in our sleep.

Maren saved me the trouble of finding her. She appeared at my shoulder with two cups of the barley tea Tam brewed, which tasted like warm dirt but kept a body moving, and pressed one into my hands.

"Tell me about her," she said.

I looked at the gate. "You already read its memory. You said so on the road."

"I read the gate's memory. I'm asking for yours." She settled onto the rock beside me with the economy of a woman who'd learned to sit precisely, because sitting imprecisely cost blind people bruises. "They're different things, yours and hers and mine. Three truths about the same night, if there ever was a night worth having three of."

"I don't have much of hers," I admitted. "That's the problem."

"Then tell me what little there is."

So I did. I told her about Wren at four years old, standing in a rain barrel because she'd decided she was a duck and would brook no argument. About her at seven, following me along the fence line of our holding, asking questions with the relentless precision of a small inquisitor: *Why does the oath-stone hum? Why do you flinch when it doesn't? Papa, is God loud?* I'd laughed then. I hadn't known how to answer. I still didn't.

"She asked once if the god could be caught," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Seven years old, mind you. Sitting cross-legged in the dirt, watching me mend a wall, and she says, 'If He talks to everyone, someone could put Him in a jar and own everything He says.' I told her not to be fanciful. I gave her bread and sent her to bed." I turned the cup in my hands. "Twenty years later I'm climbing a mountain to break into the place where somebody did exactly that."

Maren was quiet for a moment. "Children see the shape of things," she said finally. "Adults see the details. The details are usually lies."

* * *

In the morning, Maren stood before the gate and read its memory aloud.

She'd warned us it would take hours, that the gate remembered slowly, in the manner of very old things. But when she began, it didn't sound like recitation. It sounded like the mountain talking in its sleep.

"The maker was named Ossa Vael," she said, her sightless eyes fixed on nothing, her voice taking on a cadence that wasn't quite hers. "Ossa Vael loved a sister who went below, into the first court, and did not return. Ossa Vael built this door so that no one could follow who followed for any lesser reason. Gold opens nothing here. Force opens nothing. A stolen name opens nothing—the gate tastes the speaker's heart along with the word. It opens"—and here Maren paused, and even the wind seemed to lean in—"*only to a name spoken by someone who loves the one who enters.*"

Silence, except the wind.

"Well, that's convenient," Odile said at last, in the tone of a woman who did not believe in convenient. "And also impossible to counterfeit, which offends me professionally."

"Can't you forge it?" Tam asked. "You forge everything."

"I can forge a signature. I cannot forge twenty years of regret." Odile's grin was crooked. "Believe me, boy, I've tried. Regret has terrible penmanship."

Brannoc stepped forward. "Then it falls to the father."

Everyone looked at me. I looked at the gate, and the moving script looked back, patient as geology.

"There's a flaw in the plan," I said. "Possibly several. But here's the big one: I don't know her name."

"Not her name," Maren said gently. "Her *name*. The one nobody else used. The one she answered to before the world got its hands on her." She turned her face toward me. "You told me tonight about the duck and the questions and the wall-mending. Did she have another name, in those years? Something small and secret?"

I reached back through the years, and it was like reaching into cold water after something shiny—I could see it, I almost had it, and every time my fingers closed it slipped sideways. Wren. Wren at four, dripping rain-barrel water on the kitchen stones. Wren at nine, taller than her years, solemn as a magistrate. Wren at eleven, the last year, the year I left, when she'd stood in the doorway and—

And called herself something. Called herself something with her chin up, defiant, the way children armor themselves with nicknames when the world has hurt them and they refuse to show the bruise.

"I don't—" I started, and then the letters came back to me. Not my memory. The fence's letters. Old Corvin, dying in his chair in Greyford, pressing a bundle of correspondence into my hands with the last strength in his ruined lungs. Letters Wren had written him over the years—money owed, money paid, and in the margins, in a girl's careful hand, the small talk of two people keeping faith across distance. *The magpie came back,* she'd written once. *It knows me. It sits on my sill and screams until I feed it, and I've decided this is love.*

And signed—not Wren. Never Wren, in those letters. Signed:

*Little Magpie.*

"Oh," I said. The word came out of me like something exhaled after a long dive. "Oh, you stubborn bird. You brilliant, stubborn bird."

"You have it," Maren said. Not a question. She could hear it in my voice; she heard everything in voices.

"I have it." I laughed, and it cracked in the middle, because that was the thing about finding the key—it fit perfectly, and the perfection was the wound. She'd kept that name alive in letters to a smuggler-fence rather than send it to me. She'd hidden her softest self in the one correspondence I was never meant to see, because I was the reason it needed hiding. Every mile of this climb, I'd been telling myself I knew my daughter, that blood would carry me where memory failed. Standing at the gate, key in hand, I finally understood how much of her I'd surrendered the day I walked out. Nicknames are given by people who stay. Hers had survived anyway—passed hand to hand like contraband, like everything else worth keeping on the Thornfell roads.

"What is it?" Tam asked softly.

"Magpie," I said. "She called herself Little Magpie." I wiped my face with the back of my glove, pretending it was the wind. "Because a magpie screamed at her window until she fed it, and she decided that was love." I looked at the gate. "She was right, too. That's the worst part. That's exactly what love is. Something that shows up uninvited, makes a racket, and won't leave until you take care of it."

"Poetic," Odile said. "Get over there and open the door."

* * *

I stood before the gate alone. The others fell back to the camp's edge—Maren insisted; the gate tasted hearts, and too many hearts might muddy the flavor.

Up close, the moving script resolved into something almost like language, almost like music. I put my bare hand against the stone. It was warm. That undid me more than anything else had—all that black granite, all that buried dark behind it, and the surface warm as a living cheek.

I thought about what I was about to say. I thought about whether it was true.

Here is what I found, digging down: I did not remember her laugh. I could not picture her face anymore—I'd lost that years ago, the way you lose a path when you stop walking it, and I'd told myself it was mercy, forgetting what I'd abandoned. I didn't know her favorite food or whether she still fed birds or what she looked like grown. Twenty years of her life had happened without me, whole seasons of her, and no amount of wanting would give them back.

But I knew she'd sold herself to buy my safety. I knew she'd written *the magpie knows me* to a dying fence because she needed one person in the world to know her gently. I knew she was brave enough to bargain with judges and stubborn enough to hide her tenderness in margins. And I knew—bone-deep, oath-deep, deeper than the gift the god had ever given me—that if she walked out of that mountain tomorrow and didn't recognize me at all, I would follow her home anyway, at whatever distance she allowed, for whatever years remained to either of us.

Love, it turned out, wasn't remembering. Love was showing up uninvited and making a racket.

I leaned my forehead against the warm stone.

"Little Magpie," I said.

The gate went still.

Every line of script froze mid-flow, the way a stream freezes—not dead, but *listening*. Then, from deep inside the mountain, came a sound I will hear until I die: a single low note, like a bell struck underwater, like a held breath released. The seam of the door brightened. Cracked. Split.

Stone ground against stone, and light spilled out—pale, gold-veined, smelling of cold air and older cold—and beyond the opening lay a stairway descending into the dark, wide enough for ten, worn hollow in the center by feet that had gone down and, mostly, not come back.

"It opened," Tam whispered. "Sebastian, it actually—"

"I heard," I said. My voice didn't work right. "I heard."

Maren touched my arm. "Say the rest of it inside yourself," she murmured. "Keep saying it. The gate remembers what it admits, and things below have long ears."

We gathered our gear. Brannoc prayed, briefly, joyfully—a lapsed priest rediscovering that prayer was just talking to someone who'd been waiting. Odile checked her forged credentials for the eleventh time. Tam fed the last of the dried meat to a carrion crow that had followed us for two days, because Tam fed everything.

At the threshold, I looked back once at the daylight. Then down, at the stairs, at the dark where my daughter's soul sat sealed in a jar and my silent god sat muzzled beside it.

"Three days to solstice," I said. "Let's go make some noise."

The stair took us. Behind us, unhurried, the gate began to close—and it was only in the last sliver of fading light that I saw, carved small near the hinge-side where no one would ever look, a second inscription, fresh-chiseled, the stone-dust still bright in its grooves.

Maren couldn't read it. None of us could. But Odile went white as bone, and when I asked her what was wrong, she said, in a voice I'd never heard her use:

"That's my mark. My sigil-hand. Someone down there already knows I'm coming."
