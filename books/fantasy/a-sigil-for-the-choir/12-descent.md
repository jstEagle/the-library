## 12. Descent

I want it recorded, wherever the record of such things is kept now that my god has stopped taking dictation, that I said no to this expedition at least four times, and each time someone found a new way to make my refusal sound like cowardice.

The fourth time was Odile, which stung worst, because she's a forger and forgers are supposed to be liars with better handwriting.

"You're a priest," she said, not looking up from her inks, "who doesn't believe anything. That's the most honest kind. The rest of us lie for money. You do it for free."

"I was a priest," I corrected. "Past tense. Like a soldier who's lost a war, or a man who's lost a wife."

"Did you lose your god, or did you leave?"

"There's a difference?"

She looked up then, and there was something in her face I didn't like—pity, probably, or its uglier cousin, recognition. "Yes," she said. "There's a difference. Ask me how I know."

I didn't ask. That was three days ago, on the last warm ledge before the gate. Now the gate is behind us and the mountain is around us like a swallowed thing, and I have stopped saying no because there is nowhere left to say it *to* except the dark, and the dark, I am beginning to suspect, answers.

* * *

The halls are bone-white. I don't use that phrase lightly—I buried enough people in thirty years of ministry to know bone when I see it, even stone shaped like it. The under-court's corridors are ribbed like the inside of some enormous chest, arched overhead in pale curves, and the walls sweat a faint warmth that Maren says is blood-warm and I say is worse than blood-warm, because blood at least stays where you put it.

Maren walks ahead of us with one hand on Tam's shoulder and the other trailing along the wall, reading it. She reads with her fingers the way I used to read scripture—slowly, hungrily, with the constant suspicion that the text was lying to her.

"It remembers footsteps," she murmured, an hour or a year ago; time down here is a rumor. "Ten thousand years of them. All coming down." A pause. Her fingers stopped. "None going up."

"That's encouraging," said Sebastian, in the flat tone he uses for encouragement.

"Most things worth knowing aren't," Maren said.

Tam walked beside her, small and hunched, carrying his satchel of grave-goods like a boy carrying stolen sweets. He'd been quiet since the pass, quieter even than his usual quiet, and I knew why. In the ambush he'd raised the dead mule—raised it *well*, raised it standing, eyes rolling, hooves striking sparks—and he'd seen our faces when we saw what he could do. Sebastian had looked at him with something like gratitude. Odile had looked at him with something like hunger, already calculating what a gift like that was worth in forged papers. And I—

I had looked at him the way I'd been taught to look, thirty years ago, before the silencing: as a vessel. As a wonder. And Tam had caught me doing it, and flinched, and I have been trying to apologize ever since in the only currency I have left, which is silence offered rather than imposed.

The boy raises the dead. Somewhere in the courts of heaven, if there are still courts, there is a ledger with his name in it and a note beside it that says *misfiled*. That's all any of us are, I think. Misfilings. The god's filing clerk went blind, or died, or was dragged below this very mountain, and we've been falling through the cracks into the wrong drawers ever since.

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, not even in confession, especially not in confession: I did not lose my faith. My faith lost its voice, and I mistook the silence for an answer, and I spent eleven years treating an absence as a verdict.

Priests are not trained for silence. We are trained for response—the call and the answering call, the hymn and the amen, the prayer and whatever comes back, which in the old days came back as warmth in the sternum, as certainty, as the specific gravity of being *heard*. When the warmth went out of it—when every prayer began returning to sender, unopened—I did what most men do with grief. I dressed it up as doctrine. I preached that the god had withdrawn to test us, that silence was the final discipline, that a faith untested by emptiness wasn't faith at all but appetite. I preached it beautifully. I got promoted.

And then the day came—it was a Tuesday, gods forgive me, it should at least have been a holy day—when I stood up to preach about the eloquence of divine absence and heard my own voice come back off the cathedral stones changed. Not echoed. *Edited*. The same words, slightly altered. Slightly *improved*. And I understood, in the way you understand falling—that is, entirely too late and all at once—that something was listening after all. Something that was not my god.

I sat down in the pulpit. I did not finish the sermon. I walked out of my own church mid-liturgy, past the shocked faces, past the incense, out into a rain that felt like the first honest weather in years, and I did not go back.

They defrocked me within the season. Desertion of post, they called it. I called it desertion too. We agreed on the word and nothing else.

* * *

Now, in the white ribs of the under-mountain, my voice does something I have not heard it do in twenty years.

It comes back different.

Not edited—down here the echoes don't improve you, thank every absent saint—but *delayed*, and *layered*, and once, just once, when Sebastian asked me to check the count—"Seven, still seven?"—my answer came back as *seven*, yes, but beneath it, half a breath later and an octave lower, something else returned too. Something that was not my voice at all.

Something that hummed.

"What was that?" Odile whispered, grabbing my sleeve. Forger though she is, she knows authentic work when she hears it.

"Echo," I said.

"That wasn't your echo."

"No," I admitted. "It wasn't."

Sebastian had gone very still. Of all of us, he has the most practice with silence—he wears it like armor plate, this smuggler with a dead god and a living debt—and I watched him do the thing I'd watched him do at the broken covenant stones on the pass: tilt his head, slightly, the way a man listens for a child breathing in the next room.

"You hear it too," I said to him.

"Don't," he said.

"Sebastian—"

"*Don't.*" And then, softer, because he is not cruel, only careful: "Twenty years, Brannoc. Twenty years of talking to a wall. If there's something humming down here, I'll believe it when it says my name. Not before." He adjusted the strap of his pack. "Hope's just another oath you can break. I've stopped making them."

I wanted to tell him that I understood, and I did understand, and understanding is worthless, which is why priests exist—to hand out the worthless thing and charge for delivery. Instead I said, "Fair," which is the layman's amen, and we walked on.

But the hum stayed with me. It stayed under the floor of the world, under my boots, under my ribs, and it was muffled the way a voice is muffled through a wall—not absent, never absent, *present and prevented*—and every step deeper made it clearer, and every step clearer made it more unbearable, because I recognized it.

God help me, I recognized it.

You spend eleven years listening to a voice. Even after it stops speaking, you know its timbre the way a widow knows a coat. The warmth in the sternum, the specific gravity of being heard—that was *this*. That was the frequency under everything, the drone beneath the hymns, the thing I'd been preaching toward and preaching about and finally, shamefully, preaching *instead of*. It hadn't withdrawn. It hadn't tested us. It had been *taken*, carried down into this white throat of stone, and muffled, and harvested, and I had stood in a pulpit for eleven years telling the starving that famine was a spiritual exercise.

My knees nearly went. I put a hand on the ribbed wall to steady myself, and the wall was warm, and the warmth pulsed once, slow, like something enormous breathing through sleep.

"Well?" Maren asked, without turning. Blind woman, sound-reader; she hears my heartbeat change pitch the way I hear weather in a knee. "You've gone strange, priest."

"I think," I said, and had to stop and start again, "I think we're walking inside the answer."

"To what?"

"To every prayer I ever filed under *unanswered*."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she reached back without looking and found my hand on her shoulder, and squeezed it, hard, the way you grip a rope.

"Then walk faster," she said.

* * *

We camped—or tried to—in a side chamber where the ribs opened into something like a nave. I say *something like a nave* because my profession leaves marks on my perception the way sin leaves marks on the soul, but the resemblance was not mine alone: vaulted ceiling, rows of pale columns, a raised dais at the far end worn smooth as if by centuries of kneeling. Whatever built this place built it to be worshipped in, or worshipped *from*, and the distinction between those two things has apparently been the whole history of religion.

Odile lit no fire. Down here, light draws attention, and attention draws the court's clerks—we were traveling as soul-clerks now, in Odile's forged vestments, gray robes with sigils stitched so beautifully false that I kept wanting to confess to them. So we ate cold provisions in the dark, and Tam fed crumbs to a dead rat he'd raised somewhere in the second corridor and couldn't bring himself to put down, and I sat apart, as is my habit, and failed to pray, as is my deeper habit.

Except.

Except that for the first time in twenty years, the failure had texture. I knelt—out of habit, out of spite, out of something I refuse to name while anyone might overhear—and I addressed the dark in the old formal cadences, the ones carved into my bones during novitiate, and I got nothing, the same nothing, the familiar nothing.

But the *nothing hummed*.

It hummed underneath my words like a drone string under a melody, and where my prayer ended the hum continued, patient, vast, muffled—straining against whatever seals it the way water strains at ice. And I realized I was doing something I had not done since I was a young man with a whole heart and a working god: I was praying *with* someone instead of *at* them. The hum took my poor dead words and held them the way you hold a stranger's luggage. It could not speak. But it could listen. Oh, it could listen. It listened the way the drowned would drink.

I came back to myself with wet eyes and a dry throat and Tam sitting cross-legged in front of me, watching, the dead rat asleep in his lap.

"You were crying," he said. Children and the dead: neither has learned tact.

"Priests cry," I said. "It's in the job description. Right after 'hypocrisy.'"

"Why'd you stop being one? A priest."

Ah, the directness of the young. I considered several answers—the funny one, the bitter one, the long one—and chose, for once, the true one.

"I heard something in my church that wasn't God," I said, "and I ran. And I told myself for twenty years that running was integrity. And now I'm walking straight toward the thing I ran from, because it turns out the something wasn't a demon or a fraud or a test." I looked at the white ribs above us, at the warm dark between them. "It was a *cage*. And the thing in it has been listening to every prayer I ever pretended not to pray."

Tam absorbed this with the seriousness children reserve for matters of death and dinner. Then he said, "So God's down here. Like, actually."

"Muffled. Chained. Something. But near. Nearer than it's ever been."

"Is it angry?"

I thought about the hum. About the patience in it, the terrible gentle patience of a thing that has been silenced for centuries and has chosen, out of everything it might feel, to keep listening.

"No," I said. "That's the frightening part. I'd know what to do with anger. Anger I could repent from." I rubbed my face. "This is grace, boy. Unconditional, unearned, undiminished grace, showing up in the worst possible place, in the worst possible company, at the worst possible time. Grace always did have terrible timing. It waits until you've built your whole life on its absence, and then it knocks."

Tam considered. The rat's whiskers twitched in its sleep, dead nerves remembering.

"Sebastian won't like it," he said at last.

"Sebastian," I said, "has spent twenty years knocking on a door and calling the silence an answer. When he finds out what was really behind the door—" I shook my head. "Pray for him, Tam. You raise the dead. Pray for the living. It's harder."

* * *

We slept in watches. I took the middle watch, naturally—old clergy habits, keeping vigil—and sat on the dais steps with my back against a column, listening to the mountain.

And that was when the echo came back wrong in the other direction.

Footsteps. Above us, or ahead of us, in the deep corridors—soft, measured, many. I counted them the way Sebastian counts us: *seven, still seven*. Except we were six. Six souls, one dead rat, and a hum. The footsteps were seven. No—more. They multiplied as I listened, or divided; sound down here can't do simple arithmetic, and neither, increasingly, can I. Every echo returns slightly changed. Every footfall comes home wearing someone else's shoes.

And threaded through the footsteps, thin and clear as wire, came singing.

A choir. Distant, descending, drawing nearer with the unhurried confidence of those who own every stone they walk on. The song was beautiful in the way taxes are lawful—technically correct, structurally flawless, and utterly without mercy. I knew that melody. Gods forgive me, I knew it. It was a covenant hymn, one of the oldest, one I had sung in seminary with a full heart in front of a full congregation—a hymn of gathering, of voices joining into one.

Except the version floating down the white halls toward us had verses I'd never learned. Verses with gaps in them. Verses where the melody bent around absences the way a river bends around stones, and I understood, with the cold clarity that is grace's least comfortable gift, that the missing parts were *people*. The song was sung by the half-souled, and their hollows showed.

The Judges' choir, descending. Toward us, or past us, or through us.

I woke the others without a word—hand on shoulder, finger to lips; Maren read my urgency through the floor before I touched her. Sebastian was awake instantly, knife already palmed, moving like a man who has been ambushed so often that surprise is just a formality to him now. Odile was smoothing her forged vestments. Tam clutched the rat.

"Company," I breathed. "Singing. Coming down the main gallery."

"How far?" Sebastian whispered.

I listened. The hum beneath us rose, just slightly, just enough—a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep. And in that rising I heard something else, something that turned my marrow to winter water: beneath the Judges' choir, beneath the harvested hymn, one voice among the many was *off*. Not wrong. Off. Bent, deliberately, a quarter-tone from true, in a pattern that repeated, that insisted, that was not drift but *message*—a sabotage stitched into the song the way Odile stitches lies into vellum.

Someone down there was hiding something in the music. Someone in the choir was already fighting.

"They'll reach this gallery in minutes," I whispered to Sebastian. "And there's something else." I swallowed. "One of them is singing against the song."

Sebastian's eyes found mine in the dark, and I saw the thought land in him like an arrow in a target: a name he hasn't spoken aloud in three weeks, a daughter whose voice he'd know anywhere, even changed, even hollowed, even coming back to him the way everything down here comes back—

*Slightly changed.*

"Brannoc," he said, very quietly, "how well do we hide?"
