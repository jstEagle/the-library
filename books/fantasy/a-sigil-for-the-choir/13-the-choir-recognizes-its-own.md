## 13. The Choir Recognizes Its Own

The dead do not knock.

That was the first thing I learned in the under-court: everything down here arrives announced or not at all. The Judges announce themselves with a sound like a door closing in your chest. The soul-clerks announce themselves with ledgers and ink-stained cuffs and the particular shuffle of people who have been tired for so long they've forgotten it's a choice. Even the silence announces itself, down here, pressing on your ears like a hand.

So when four strangers walked into the choir's counting-hall wearing clerk's grey and carrying forged credentials, I knew within a breath that they were wrong. Not because their papers were bad—though I'd learn later they were very good papers, just not good enough for what came after—but because they knocked. One of them, a boy with dirt under his nails and terror behind his eyes like a second set of pupils, actually rapped his knuckles on the doorframe before entering, out of some surface-world habit he couldn't kill.

Nobody knocks in the court beneath the mountain. You are summoned, or you are already here.

I kept my eyes on my ledger and my voice in the proper register—the flat, sweet nothing the choir is trained to sing when we count souls aloud—and I thought: *run, you idiots.*

* * *

Let me back up, because you deserve to understand what I understood, which was almost nothing.

My name is Wren. I know this the way you know your own hands—by use, not by memory. Three years ago, or maybe five; time below the mountain is measured in tithes, not seasons—I sold something to this place. I don't remember what exactly. That's rather the point of selling it. What I have instead is a shape where the thing used to be, an ache with no object, like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

I remember loving my father. I want to be clear about that, because everyone down here assumes the sale takes the love too, and it doesn't. It takes the *why*. I have the love the way you have a scar: proof something happened, no account of how. I remember his hands being large. I remember a smell like pine tar and iron. I remember a nickname that lives in me like a coal banked under ash—*little wren*, someone called me, in a voice I can't put a face to—and I guard that ember the way the poor guard anything, secretly and with both hands.

The Magistrate-Judges tell us our souls will "harmonize" at solstice. They say it the way a butcher says *humane*. There are forty-one of us in the choir, half-souled servants who traded pieces of ourselves for bargains struck above ground, and every day we sing the Reckoning Song while the clerks tally what remains of us, and every day there is slightly less to tally, and nobody screams, because screaming requires a whole soul and most of us are running on fumes and stubbornness.

I hide things in the song. Small things. A verse bent half a tone so it carries a word the Judges don't listen for. A rhythm borrowed from a lullaby I don't remember learning. It's not rebellion, exactly. Rebellion needs a plan. This is more like—smuggling. Keeping contraband memories sewn into the lining of a hymn, hoping someday to cross a border with them intact.

I mention this because it matters later. Everything about that day matters later.

* * *

The four false clerks were processed through the counting-hall at mid-tithe, which meant I stood close enough to touch them and had to pretend otherwise.

The tall one—grey at the temples, a face like a map of somewhere hard to reach—presented credentials signed by a magistrate whose seal Odile (though I didn't know her name yet) had forged so beautifully that even the hall's truth-stone hesitated before accepting it. I watched the stone flicker amber, amber again, then settle grudgingly green, and I thought: whoever made these papers loves her work the way I love my stolen verses.

But it was the tall one's voice that did it.

He spoke only twice. Once to answer the registrar—"Soul-clerk, third circuit, audit rotation"—in a voice pitched low and bored, professionally forgettable. And once, when the boy beside him fumbled a ledger and nearly dropped it, he said, quietly, "Steady," and something in my chest turned over like a boat in weather.

Not the word. Words are cheap. The *grain* of it. There was iron in it, and pine tar, and underneath both, a roughness like a man who has spent years talking to no one and has gotten good at it. My body knew that voice before I did. My blood answered it—a stupid animal recognition, the kind dogs have, the kind that bypasses every locked door in a person and walks straight in through the cellar.

I sang the next line of the Reckoning Song half a tone sharp, which is the equivalent of a civilian screaming, and covered it by coughing, and the registrar didn't look up, and I stood there with my heart going like a hammer on an anvil thinking: *that is either my father or I am finally going mad, and honestly the odds favor madness.*

Because here's the arithmetic of my ruin: I sold my soul partly to buy his safety. I know this the way I know the tooth-ache—there's a debt-shaped hole in me with his name on the invoice. So if he were here, it would mean he'd come for me, which would mean he knew, which would mean the world above had finally done one decent thing, and the world above does not do decent things. The world above sells daughters and burns hedge-witches and calls it order.

And besides—he didn't recognize me. He looked directly at me, clerk to clerk, one professional to another, and his eyes went over me like water over stone. No snag. No stumble. Whatever I'd been to him, the sale had taken it from both ends. We were two strangers standing three feet apart, connected by a debt neither could name.

I have never felt anything so much like grief and so little like surprise.

* * *

They made their mistake at the fourth hour, and it was beautiful in its stupidity.

The audit rotation requires the visiting clerk to verify the choir's soul-sigils against the master registry—which means touching each of us, briefly, palm to sternum, reading the sigil's depth. Standard procedure. Degrading by design. The Judges believe shame keeps the half-souled docile, and mostly they're right.

The tall one worked down the line. I watched him approach each of my fellows with careful, gloved hands, and I watched him flinch—microscopically, a man swallowing a scream—at each sigil he read. He wasn't disgusted. That's what I noticed. Clerks get disgusted; it's part of the training, a callus against pity. This man was *grieving*. Each sigil he touched, he grieved, quietly, like a man walking a battlefield after the war's over, identifying faces.

Then he reached me, and I made my own mistake, which was looking up.

Our eyes met. And his face did something extraordinary—it performed perfect blankness while everything behind it caught fire. I saw it happen. I saw a man recognize someone and decide, in the space of half a heartbeat, not to. I saw hope and horror arm-wrestling behind his eyes, and horror won, because horror always wins in this place, and he said, "Sigil verification," in that voice like iron wrapped in wool, and pressed his palm to my sternum.

Warmth. Through the glove. Stupid, impossible warmth.

And the sigil—that black knot the Judges tied into whatever's left of me—*sang*. Just a note, just a thread of sound, but it harmonized with the ember I keep banked under my ash, with *little wren*, with pine tar and iron, and for one full second I was five years old in a house I cannot picture, safe, held, known—

"Your sigil resonates irregularly," said a voice from the doorway, and the temperature of the room dropped ten degrees.

A Magistrate-Judge stood in the arch. I never hear them arrive. They're built of law and cold, robed in grey so deep it eats light, and where a face should be there's a smoothness like the inside of a bell. This one's attention settled on me the way frost settles on a window.

"The resonance," it said. "Again."

The tall man's hand was still on my chest. I felt his pulse through the glove—fast, fast, a rabbit's drumbeat—and I understood two things simultaneously. First: he was going to die here if I didn't act. Second: I had been rehearsing for this moment my entire half-life without knowing it, because hiding contraband inside sanctioned things is the one art I have mastered.

I bowed. Choir-deep, properly humble, eyes down.

"Beg pardon, Magnitude," I said, in my flattest, sweetest register. "It's the audit itself. Foreign hands on the sigils make them ring strange—we're not used to being touched by anyone but our own registrars. Like striking a bell with a stranger's hammer." I let my voice go small and technical, the drone of a girl who has said ten thousand boring true things so that this one beautiful lie could slip through unnoticed. "The resonance is mine, not his. I'm told my sigil sits shallow. It catches drafts."

Silence. The Judge's bell-face inclined toward me, and I felt it listening—not to my words but to the space between them, hunting for the harmonic of a lie. Down here, lying is less a crime than a physics problem: the truth-stones taste intent, and intent has a flavor.

So I gave it flavor. I thought about the Reckoning Song. I thought about the ache with no object. I thought, deliberately, *this stranger means nothing to me,* and let the thought be true—because it was, it was horribly, perfectly true, he meant nothing to my mind, only to whatever's left of my blood—and the Judge tasted that truth and found it clean.

"Audit rotations will wear gloves of court-make going forward," the Judge said at last, to the room, to no one. "Continue."

It left the way it had arrived: without arriving, simply ceasing to be there.

The tall man withdrew his hand. For a moment—one moment, no longer—his fingers curled, almost catching mine, a gesture so old and so human it hurt worse than any sigil ever tied into me. Then he stepped back, clerk-blank, and moved down the line to the next soul, and I went back to my ledger, and neither of us looked at the other again, because we were both professionals, and professionals know that the most dangerous thing in any heist is a face that wants something.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I want it noted that I did it alone, without plan, without permission, against every survival instinct the sale left me.

I followed them.

Not immediately. Immediately would be suicide. I finished my shift. I sang the closing verses with my contraband verses folded inside them. I ate the grey porridge and slept the grey sleep in the dormitory cell where the forty-one of us lie in rows like spoons in a drawer. And then, at the change of tithe, when the halls run quiet and the bone-white corridors echo strangely—every echo down here comes back slightly wrong, slightly *edited*, as though the mountain is taking notes—I slipped out through the laundry passage that I'd mapped months ago for reasons I couldn't explain and now understood perfectly, and I went hunting.

They weren't hard to find. Amateurs leave wakes. The boy—Tam, I'd learn, though names came later—had raised a dead rat in the lower gallery to scout ahead, and its little corpse-clicking echoed like a typewriter. The priest-shaped one hummed under his breath as they walked, some hymn from above, and the mountain's edited echoes carried it to me down three turnings. The woman with the ink-stained fingers argued in whispers about seals and tolerances. And the tall man walked last, silent, and every few dozen paces he stopped and put his hand flat against the wall, as though listening for something in the stone.

Listening for something in the stone. As though he expected the stone to answer.

I trailed them through galleries of sealed jars—ten thousand of them, I'd guess, ranked on shelves to the ceiling, each jar humming faintly with a voice trying to get out. I'd walked those galleries a hundred times and never heard the humming. But walking behind these four, hearing the priest's hymn bounce off the shelves and come back changed—richer, angrier, more *alive*—I heard it. The jars were singing back. The whole vault was a choir being forced to hold one note forever, and something in my chest, in the tooth-ache, in the ash-covered coal, recognized the tune.

The tall man stopped. Put his hand on a jar. And whispered a name—not mine, not anyone's—a god's name, maybe, spoken like a man calling into a well he'd long ago given up expecting echoes from.

And the echo that came back was not edited.

It came back whole. It came back *his name*, spoken in a voice like a landslide learning tenderness, and all four of them froze, and I froze behind a shelf of souls, and for three heartbeats the entire under-mountain rang like a struck bell.

Then the echoes resumed their editing, the humming dimmed, the mountain took its note back, and the tall man stood there shaking.

I should have gone back. Every survival instinct the sale left me said go back, report nothing, survive to solstice, keep your ember hidden. That had been the whole of my strategy for years: hide, endure, keep the coal alive.

But the coal had just heard a voice answer its own, and embers are not famous for their caution.

I pressed my palm against the shelf, against the humming glass, and I mouthed the only prayer I remembered, the one stitched into my stolen verse, the one whose meaning I lost in the sale but whose shape still fits my mouth like a key fits a lock it no longer remembers opening.

Above me, faint as frost forming, the tall man's voice said: "We move on solstice eve. Whatever it costs."

And I thought: *so do I.*

Whatever it costs. Blood answering blood across the void the sale carved—I didn't have the words for it yet, wouldn't have them until much later, standing in a vault full of voices with my father's face finally wearing recognition like a wound. All I had that night was the certainty, sudden and total as sunrise, that these four doomed amateurs were going to break this place open, and that I had three weeks to make sure my song was ready when they needed it.

I slipped back through the laundry passage before the tithe-bell. I lay in my drawer among the spoons. And I began, silently, in the dark, to rewrite the Reckoning Song.

The Judges would notice by solstice. I intended for them to notice too late.
