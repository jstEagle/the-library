## 14. Judgment Deferred

I have been captured before. Twice by rival crews, once by a customs magistrate with a fondness for hot irons, and once—memorably—by a goat that knocked me off a ledge into a ravine I then had to climb out of while concussed. So I know the genre. There is a routine to it: the rough hands, the blindfold, the indignity of being marched like luggage.

This was not that. This was worse, because nobody touched me at all.

The Judge simply looked at me across the bone-white hall, and the hall itself did the rest. The floor rose like a tide of pale stone, gentle as a mother's hand, and folded around my ankles, my wrists, my throat—not tight enough to hurt, which was somehow more insulting than pain would have been—and lifted me into an alcove that hadn't been there a moment before. My companions were gone. Whether they'd fled or been swallowed too, I couldn't say. Maren's warning about sound down here rang in my ears: *every echo comes back changed.* Right now mine came back as silence, which I took personally.

The Judge stood at the center of the hall, if standing is what you'd call it. The Judges of the under-court wear robes the color of old parchment and faces like unfinished sculpture—the features suggested rather than committed to, as though whoever made them ran out of conviction halfway through. This one's voice, when it came, came from everywhere: from the walls, from my own chest, from the stone holding my jaw shut.

"Sebastian Eriksen," it said. "Oath-sworn. Voice-claimed. You are in violation of four hundred and eleven statutes."

"Is that all?" My mouth worked even though my head barely could. "I've been slacking."

"You find this amusing."

"I find most things amusing. It's a character flaw. My daughter told me so." A lie. Wren had never said any such thing, because I hadn't been there for her to say it to. But the Judge didn't need to know my inventory of regrets.

"The amusement is noted. It will be weighed."

"Weighed against what?"

"Everything."

* * *

They took me somewhere quieter. Quieter, in a place where quiet was already the native language, meant somewhere the echoes stopped pretending. A chamber with no corners—or corners so many they canceled each other out—and a table of black glass between two chairs. One chair was mine. The other was empty until the Judge sat in it, and even then it seemed mostly empty, the way a glove is empty without a hand.

"You are here for the soul registered as Wren Eriksen," the Judge said. "Transaction number—" and it recited a string of numbers so long and precise that I understood, viscerally, how much bookkeeping goes into damnation. "You intend to steal her back."

"Yes."

"You understand that theft from the court is impossible."

"I've been called impossible before. Usually by people who owed me money."

The Judge regarded me. Its face did something that on a human would have been a sigh. "We are aware of your reputation, Eriksen. Smuggler. Oath-reader. The man who walks roads no one else can see. We are also aware of your gift, which you have hidden for nineteen years with a diligence we almost admire. Do you know why your god went silent?"

My heart did something complicated. "I assumed it abandoned me. Gods do that. It's practically their hobby."

"It did not abandon you." The Judge leaned forward, and its unfinished face caught light it shouldn't have been able to catch. "It was taken. As were its laws, its covenants, its voice. Every oath ever sworn upon Thornfell stone passed through its mouth, and every one of those oaths is now filed in our vaults. Your god has been singing in our choir for nineteen years, Eriksen. It sings beautifully. It sings *compliantly*."

Something in my chest cracked along an old fault line. Nineteen years I had prayed into a silence and read that silence as abandonment—had built a whole life on the assumption that I was unworthy of answer, or that the god was dead, or that faith was just a habit the body kept after the soul left. And the whole time—

"You're lying," I said, because someone had to.

"We do not lie. Lying requires effort. We archive." The Judge spread its hands, and the air above the glass table filled with images—no, not images. *Records.* I saw covenant stones from the Pass of Broken Oaths, whole and gleaming, decades ago. I heard—I *heard*, in a chamber where sound behaved like a well-behaved dog—a voice like winter thunder reciting the terms of a bridge-oath, a hearth-oath, a marriage-oath. The god's voice. Rich and vast and patient, the voice I'd spent half my life talking to and getting nothing back.

Then I watched it be taken. I won't describe it. Some things should stay undescribed.

"So now you know," the Judge said softly. "Your grief is fresh. Use it. Grief makes men honest."

"What do you want?"

"To offer you a trade. But first, education. You cannot evaluate an offer without understanding the market." It gestured, and the records changed. Now I saw souls—or their sigils, the written shapes of them, glowing faintly in ranks upon ranks, shelved like jars of preserves. "You believe we are monsters. Most surface-dwellers do. Monsters take. We *manage*. Consider: before the court, what happened to a gifted soul when its owner died? It scattered. It leaked into the world as hauntings, curses, wild magic that burned villages and soured wells. Chaos. Suffering. We ended that. We collect souls at the moment of transaction, we hold them in trust, we harmonize them into the great order. Nothing is wasted. Nothing wanders. Every gift accounted for, every voice given its proper place in the choir."

"People sold their souls to you," I said. "Willingly?"

"Some. Many. Desperation is a fine salesman. But consider what they received. Protection. Provision. Order. The woman who sold her soul to save her child did not lose—she *invested*. Her love became law. Her sacrifice became structure." The Judge's voice took on the warm cadence of a merchant who genuinely believes his own pitch. "You have lived outside order, Eriksen. You know what the roads are like without law. You smuggle through them."

"I smuggle *because* of laws like yours," I said. "Laws that decide some people are cargo."

"And yet you benefit from them. Every road you walk was made safe by someone's enforced order. Every bridge stands on an oath someone was compelled to keep. Freedom is a lovely word, but freedom does not hold up bridges." The Judge tilted its unfinished face. "Order requires harvest. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic."

I wanted to argue. God knows I wanted to. But the terrible thing about sitting across from the under-court's logic is that it isn't stupid. It's the stupidest clever thing there is—every step reasonable, every step sane, and the sum of them a machine that grinds up daughters and calls it bookkeeping.

"Now," the Judge said. "The trade."

* * *

It laid it out the way a moneylender lays out terms: gently, clearly, with every kindness short of honesty.

"Wren Eriksen's transaction completes at solstice. Eleven days. At that time her soul will be harmonized—integrated into the choir, her memories distributed as raw material, her gifts assigned where the court finds them useful. She will not suffer. She will not even, strictly speaking, end. She will become part of something larger and permanent. This is what she purchased with herself: your safety, Eriksen. The bounty on your head was bought and burned. The hunters who sought you forgot your name. She paid for your life with her self, and the purchase was lawful, final, and *hers*."

"She was sixteen," I said. "She was a child buying her father out of a mess he didn't even know he was in."

"She was of age by the court's reckoning. And she knew precisely what she was doing. Would you dishonor her choice by undoing it?"

That landed harder than it should have. Because I'd asked myself the same question on the way down, in the dark, in Maren's careful silence: who was I to overturn the one big decision Wren had ever gotten to make about me?

But then I thought of her at six, solemn as a little judge herself, refusing to eat her supper until I'd eaten mine, because she'd decided we starved together or not at all. I thought of her at nine, teaching herself letters off my smuggling ledgers because I was too poor and too proud to buy her a tutor. I thought of the fence's dying words: *she wrote to me every year, asking if you were safe. Every year, Eriksen. Even after you stopped writing back.*

"No," I said. "I wouldn't undo her choice. I'd finish it. She bought my safety. Fine. I'm safe. Now I'm spending it on her. That's not dishonoring the deal—that's honoring it at compound interest."

The Judge paused. I think, in its way, it appreciated the accounting.

"Then hear our counter-offer," it said. "We will release Wren Eriksen. Whole. Soul intact, memory intact, debt dissolved, free to leave this mountain and live whatever life she chooses. Effective immediately. No solstice. No harmonization."

The stone holding me loosened, just slightly, like a fist unclenching one finger. Hope is a dangerous thing to feel in a room with no corners; it echoes strangely.

"In exchange," the Judge continued, "you will surrender your memories. Not your life. Not your soul. Your *remembered past*—the accumulated record of your experience, forty-three years of it. Raw material. Memory is the richest substance in the harmonic ledger, Eriksen. A soul is a shape; memory is the clay. Forty years of a life like yours—oaths witnessed, roads walked, betrayals survived—is worth more to the choir than ten ordinary souls."

"And what happens to me? The me that's left?"

"You remain. Functional. Competent. You will know how to walk, fight, read, lie, survive—all the skills persist, as skills. You will simply have no past to consult. No reasons. You will be a man with excellent instincts and no idea where they came from." The Judge folded its hands. "You will not remember Wren. You will not remember selling anything. You will not remember this bargain. You will wake on the mountainside with a full purse and a mild sense of loss, and she will be free, and the ledger will balance."

"That's not mercy," I said. "That's a butcher explaining cuts."

"It is mercy," the Judge said, with such calm certainty that I almost envied it. "Mercy is a price paid by someone. We are offering to let *her* go free and have *you* pay. That is the only kind of mercy that has ever existed. Everything else is poetry." It leaned closer, and its unfinished face was, for a moment, almost kind. "Consider what you actually are, Eriksen. A man who hid his gift for nineteen years. Who left his daughter to keep her safe, and stayed away so long the keeping became forgetting. Who prays to a silent god out of habit. What is he, if not already a collection of memories wearing a competent body? We are offering to formalize the arrangement. And she lives."

The worst part—the part I'll carry to whatever grave I get—was the pause. The long, honest pause in which I actually considered it. Because the math was right. One man's past against a girl's whole future. Any fool could run that sum.

Any fool except the man whose past it was.

Because here's what the Judge missed, sitting there with its archived god and its balanced ledgers: it thought my memories were data. Entries. Clay. But my memories weren't a record of Wren—they were the only place she still existed for me. The supper-refusing. The ledger-letters. The weight of her asleep on my shoulder on a night coach when she was small enough to fit there. If I gave those up, I wouldn't be sacrificing the *memory* of loving her. I'd be sacrificing the love itself, and leaving behind a stranger with my hands who might pass her on the road and feel nothing at all.

And she'd know. Wren, sharp as a new blade, would look at that stranger and see exactly what her father had chosen: a version of himself that had finally, truly, left her. The sale she'd made to protect me would be completed twice over—once by her, once by me—and the second time I'd be the one signing.

"No," I said.

"You have not heard the final term—"

"There's always a final term. No." The stone tightened around my wrists again, and I laughed, because the alternative was screaming. "You want to know something funny? Nineteen years I prayed to a god that wasn't ignoring me—it was gagged. And the whole time, the thing answering my prayers was *you*. Every road that stayed safe, every bounty that dried up, every bit of luck that kept me breathing—her coin, your ledger. I've been living on my daughter's soul this whole time and calling it providence." I looked the Judge in its unfinished face. "So no. I won't sell her anything else. Not my memories, not my compliance, not my arithmetic. You can keep the silence. I'm done renting it."

The Judge sat back. Somewhere far above us—or below; direction was negotiable down here—a bell tolled once, deep and slow.

"A pity," it said. "You would have harmonized well." It rose, and the chamber began to fold itself away like a letter being closed. "Then we proceed by statute. Theft from the court. The penalty is forfeiture of everything—soul, memory, and voice. Your companions will be catalogued as accessories. The girl you came for will watch, as is traditional, so that she understands the cost of hope."

"Traditional," I said. "Of course. You'd have nothing without tradition."

The last thing I saw before the stone closed over my eyes was the Judge's face, smoothing itself toward completion at last—as if my refusal had supplied the missing feature.

As if hatred finished it.

The dark took me, and in the dark, for the first time in nineteen years, I heard something answer when I called. Faint. Muffled. Coming from the direction of the vault.

It sounded like a god clearing its throat.

And beneath it—thinner, braver, unmistakable—someone was singing a song I almost knew, in a voice I'd have known anywhere, if I'd ever been allowed to keep it.
