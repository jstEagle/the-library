## 15. Varga Descends

The trouble with serving the law for twenty years is that you learn its handwriting. I don't mean the script on warrants and writs—though I know that too, every clerk's cramped little flourishes. I mean the handwriting underneath. The way a true order sits square on the page, and the way a rotten one leans.

The order in my hand leaned.

*Pursue fugitives to the mountain gate. Execute upon capture. No investigation required.*

No investigation required. In twenty years of service to the Crown's Office of Purity, I had never once seen those four words written down. Investigation was the spine of the work. You investigated because the law was worth investigating for; you dug through filth and heresy and worse because at the bottom of every hole there was supposed to be truth, clean as a washed stone. That was the bargain. That was why a woman like me got up before dawn and rode into cold country after people whose only crime, half the time, was being born wrong.

I read the order three times by lamplight in the waystation at Greyford's edge, while my hunters slept around me like tired dogs, and then I did what any loyal officer would do.

I folded it up small and put it in my breast pocket, over my heart, where it could keep lying to me.

* * *

The village was called Hesselmere, or had been. It sat two days off the smuggler roads, close enough to Thornfell that the mountains watched it, far enough that the mountains hadn't bothered saving it. It had burned eleven years ago. Everyone knew the story: gifted fanatics, a massacre, one survivor—a mercenary named Sebastian Eriksen—who walked out of the ashes and vanished into the passes. His name went into the ledger. Mine went in beside it eventually, though mine was earned honestly, warrant by warrant, year by year.

I'd come to Hesselmere because Eriksen had come this way. My trackers found his fire-circles, his careful trailcraft, the marks of six travelers where he'd been alone before. He was gathering people. Gifted people. And he was moving toward the mountain, which meant he knew something about the under-court that the Office didn't put in its own reports, and that interested me more than the order in my pocket wanted it to.

But mostly I came because of the ledger.

It had bothered me since Greyford. Not his name—I'd expected his name. What bothered me was the entry itself. Twenty years of reading warrants teaches you what evidence looks like when it's been handled, and Eriksen's file had been handled. The testimony was too consistent. Witnesses contradict each other; that's what witnesses do. Six survivors of a massacre describing the same man, the same words, the same sequence of events, word for word across three different interviews—that wasn't memory. That was composition.

So I rode to Hesselmere, ostensibly to trace the fugitives' route, actually to dig.

The records house still stood, or the shell of it. Stone walls, charred roof, the county archive inside half-eaten by the same fire that ate everything else. I expected ash and disappointment. What I found, in an iron strongbox that had done its one job well, was paper.

Village ledgers. Tithe rolls. A militia roster. And letters.

I sat on a fallen beam in the cold and read until my hands stopped feeling like my hands.

Here is what eleven-year-old paper said: Hesselmere had no gifted fanatics. Hesselmere had a tithe dispute. The village elders had refused a levy imposed by a crown factor named Aldous Renk—one of those men who collect taxes the way tumors collect blood—and Renk had written, in his own tidy hand, to someone above him, proposing a solution. *Let the incident be attributed to gift-work,* the letter said. *The Office requires examples. The village requires punishment. Both requirements may be served at once, and the cost borne by no one who matters.*

There were three such letters. Then a fourth, acknowledging receipt. Then a fifth, authorizing "the operation," signed with a seal I knew. I'd sworn my oath under that seal. I'd had it pressed into wax on my commission like a brand on a calf.

And beneath all of it, the last document, the one that made me sit very still for a long time: a report from the Office itself, dated three months after the fire. An internal review. It concluded that the evidence against Sebastian Eriksen was, quote, *insufficient and possibly fabricated*, and recommended reopening the case.

Someone had written *Denied — do not pursue* across it in red ink. Below that, in the same hand: *He saw the letters. He cannot be permitted to speak.*

Eriksen hadn't just survived a massacre. He'd survived being framed for one, and then spent eleven years running from the proof of it, hunted by the very institution that knew he was innocent, because he'd had the bad luck to see something he wasn't meant to see.

I built my career on that fire. I'd told recruits about Hesselmere. *This is why we hunt,* I'd say, standing over the ledger with its neat black entry. *Because of what the gifted did here.* I'd pointed at names. I'd made them memorize dates.

I had been pointing at a lie so long I'd worn the pointing finger smooth.

* * *

My sergeant found me still sitting there at dusk. Halvard's a good man—solid, unimaginative, the kind of soldier who'd follow you into a burning building and complain only about the temperature. He looked at the papers spread around me, and then at my face, and I watched him decide not to ask.

"Captain. There's a rider from the Office."

"Of course there is."

"He wouldn't give me the message. Said it was for your eyes only." Halvard hesitated, which he almost never did. "Captain, are we—should we be moving? The trail's fresh. Another day and they're at the gate."

"We'll move tomorrow."

"Ma'am, the order says—"

"I know what the order says, Sergeant."

He knew too. That was the thing about Halvard; he always knew. He looked at the papers again—at the red ink, at the seal—and something moved behind his eyes that I recognized, because it was moving behind mine. He saluted and left without another word, which was its own kind of answer.

The rider's message came sealed and lean. I read it once by torchlight outside the records house, because it seemed fitting to read rot among ruins.

*Captain Varga. Your pursuit is noted. Reiterate: upon capture, execute all fugitives immediately. Do not detain. Do not question. Do not investigate anomalies in prior records pertaining to subject Eriksen. Compliance is expected. Deviation will be answered.*

Do not investigate anomalies in prior records. They knew I'd find them. They'd let me ride here anyway—or sent me here anyway—and they were watching to see what I did next. This wasn't an order anymore. It was a test with a blade at the end of it.

I thought about the hedge-healer in Greyford. Old Tomas, gentle as bread, who'd set my broken wrist when I was nine years old and living rough, and who'd asked nothing for it but that I be kind to somebody later. They dragged him through the street in chains while I stood there in my captain's coat and did nothing, because the warrant was valid, because the law was the law, because a woman has to believe in something or she's just meat walking around.

I'd believed. That was my whole self, folded up small: I believed the law was clean. I believed that if I did the ugly work faithfully, the ugly work meant something. Every prisoner I'd taken, every pyre I'd lit—it all balanced on the idea that somewhere above me, someone honest was checking the sums.

Nobody was checking the sums. The sums were the fraud. The whole great ledger of the Office of Purity was written in the same tidy hand as Aldous Renk's letters, and I had spent twenty years as a line item in it.

Halvard reappeared with two horses saddled and a third loaded. "Thought you might want provisions," he said. "For the mountain road."

"Halvard."

"Captain."

"If I deviate, they'll answer it. You understand that. Whatever happens to me happens to whoever rides with me."

He swung up into the saddle and looked down at me with the expression of a man who has followed someone into enough burning buildings to have opinions about exits. "With respect, ma'am, I've read the order too. *Execute upon capture, no investigation required.* I've executed on orders before. I can't execute on that one. So either you arrest me for insubordination, or—" he shrugged, vast and unbothered, "—we ride."

We rode.

* * *

The mountain gate was exactly where the old maps said it would be and nothing like anything else I'd ever seen. A seam in the rock, tall as a cathedral door, carved over with sigils that hurt to look at directly—the kind of craft the Office called heresy and the Office's founders had apparently practiced fluently, but that was a thought for a braver night than this one.

We reached it at moonrise and found the trail ended at a campsite: six bedrolls, a dead fire, signs of a hurried departure downward. And something else. A dead mule, standing upright in the middle of the pass road, arranged with a certain grim comedy across the path like a toll barrier—but the mule had been gone days, and whatever animated it had moved on, and I chose, consciously and forever, not to write that part in my report.

"They went below," Halvard said quietly. "Into the under-court. On purpose." He crossed himself, an old habit from a faith the Office had also outlawed, back when the Office cared about consistency. "That's a grave, Captain. Volunteering for one."

"Then we'd best not let them die alone."

"Ma'am?"

I dismounted and stood at the seam in the rock. Cold air breathed out of it, smelling of deep stone and old incense, and underneath that—faint, so faint I might have invented it—something like singing. Distant. Many voices. Wrong somehow, the way a bell is wrong when it's cracked.

Twenty years ago I'd have ridden away and filed a report. Ten years ago I'd have sent Halvard down alone and waited at the top to arrest whoever crawled out. A year ago I'd have gone down with drawn steel and a warrant in my pocket, certain to the marrow that I was the law's hand reaching into the dark.

Now I took out the order—the leaning, rotten order—and read it one last time by moonlight. *Deviation will be answered.* Yes. I imagined it would be. But there are answers and answers, and I had finally learned the difference between obeying a law and obeying the men who'd stolen its name.

I drew my sword—not against anyone, just to hear it, just to remind myself what I was—and drove it point-first into the earth at the threshold. A captain's sword stays with her commission. I was resigning both.

"Halvard," I said, "get the lanterns. We're going down."

"And if we meet them? The fugitives?"

"Then we apologize," I said, stepping through the gate into the dark, "and ask if they're hiring."

Behind us, far above, the wind moved over Hesselmere's ashes, and ahead of us the cracked-bell singing rose to meet us out of the deep—and somewhere between the two, I understood, waited the truth I should have been hunting all along.
