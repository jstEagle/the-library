## 16. The Vault of Voices

### Sebastian

The vault smelled like a church that had drowned.

That was my first thought, and I'm not proud of it, but twenty years of smuggling teaches you to catalog smells before anything else—tallow, wet stone, old wax, the copper tang of something recently bled. The vault had all of those, plus one more: a hum so low I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it. Like standing inside a struck bell that never finished ringing.

Ten thousand jars lined the walls. Not figuratively—I counted the nearest shelf and did the arithmetic and wished I hadn't. Each jar was sealed with black wax stamped by a sigil I didn't recognize, and each held a faint light, the color varying: amber, blue, one sickly green near the door that Odile pointedly did not look at.

"That's hers," Maren said. She couldn't see—she'd been blind since before I met her—but her head turned with the certainty of a compass needle. "Third shelf. The blue one. It sings off-key."

"Off-key?"

"Everything in here is in tune with everything else, boy. Except that one." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Souls that were sold rather than taken always are. They remember they had a choice."

I crossed the floor. My boots made no sound, which should have warned me—in Thornfell, silence is either a blessing or an ambush, and down here it was neither. It was architecture. The under-court had built its whole house out of hush.

The jar sat where she said it would. Blue light, steady as a lamp. And around its neck, on a cord of braided hair I refused to think too hard about, hung a small leaden sigil stamped with a name.

*Wren Eriksen.*

Not Wren Okonkwo. She'd taken her mother's family name at some point, after I left, and I learned this standing in front of her soul in a jar and it hit me the way grief always hits me: late, sideways, and all at once.

"Sebastian," Brannoc murmured behind me. The disgraced priest had his hand flat against the wall, eyes closed, lips moving. "It's here. Under us. Under *everything*. The voice—it's not in the jars. The jars are the *cage*."

"The jars hold souls," Tam said. He'd brought two of his dead down the mountain—a smuggler's mule and, gods forgive me, a smuggler—and both stood in the doorway with the patient blankness of hired porters. "Brannoc said the voice is separate. Where?"

"Below. Beneath the floor." Brannoc opened his eyes. "We're standing on its throat."

Odile whistled softly. "Charming real estate. Very them."

I touched the jar. Cold glass, warmer than it should have been, like skin. Somewhere behind me Maren said my name in warning, but I'd already stopped listening, because the moment my fingers met the seal, the hum changed. It didn't get louder. It got *interested*.

And for half a heartbeat—one breath, no more—I heard it. A voice like a river deciding to be a man. It said nothing I could parse. It said everything, all at once, the way a god talks when it hasn't been allowed to talk in centuries and has been saving up.

Then footsteps in the corridor, measured and many, and Maren's hand closed on my wrist like a shackle.

"Clerks," she breathed. "Play your part, boy. You're a soul-clerk from the Third Terrace, you've done inventory since dawn, and you are *bored*. Boredom is the best disguise there is. Nobody suspects a bored man."

I arranged my face into the expression I'd worn through every customs inspection of my life—the face of a man who would rather be anywhere else—and turned to greet whatever came through that door.

What came through the door was my daughter.

She wore the grey robes of the choir, hemmed short for service, her hair bound back in a style I'd last seen on her mother. She carried a ledger. She looked at me the way you look at furniture.

"This shelf is due for rotation," she said to the clerks flanking her. Then, to me, without a flicker: "You. Clerk. When was this row last audited?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Since dawn," I said, because it was the only true thing available.

"Then it's overdue." She set her ledger down on the shelf beside her own soul and began checking seals, jar by jar, her back to me, and I understood—some animal part of me understood—that she was giving me time to look at her without being caught looking.

She didn't know me. Of course she didn't. The fence had told me what the sale cost her: memories thinning like frost, love persisting without its reasons. But knowing a thing and watching your daughter audit the shelf where her soul sits in a jar, while she calls you *clerk*—those are different weathers entirely.

I have smuggled contraband past kings' men. I have lied to priests with better lies than their scriptures. Nothing in my life prepared me for saying "yes, mistress" to Wren Eriksen in a voice I kept level through sheer professional spite.

### Wren

There was a clerk on the third terrace who stood wrong.

I want to be clear about what that means, because in the court beneath the mountain, everyone stands wrong eventually. The Judges stand wrong on purpose, all angles and ceremony. The choir stands wrong from exhaustion—we sleep in shifts and the shifts are lies. Even the dead porters stand wrong, though that's less a posture than a condition.

This man stood wrong the way a song stands wrong. Familiar. Off-key in a direction I knew.

He was broad through the shoulders, scarred across the knuckles in the pattern of a man who'd held weapons and then put them down and picked up rope instead. He checked the seals properly—he'd been taught, somewhere, by someone patient—but he checked the blue jar twice. Three times. His fingers hovered over it like it might bite him, or like he might bite it.

"You'll smudge the wax," I told him, and my own voice surprised me. It came out gentler than the choir allows. Softer than I've spoken to anyone since the frost started taking things.

He snatched his hand back. "Apologies, mistress."

"Don't call me that." The words were out before I could weigh them. In the court, *mistress* is what clerks call choir-servants who outrank them, and I don't outrank anyone; I'm property with a ledger number. But hearing it from him felt like wearing someone else's coat. "Just—Wren is fine."

Something moved across his face. Grief has many shapes; I've catalogued most of them from the inside. This one looked like a man taking a blow he'd been expecting for years and finding it hurt worse anyway.

"Wren," he repeated. He said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "A good name."

"It means a small bird that sings in winter." I don't know why I told him that. I don't know why I know it. Half my memories are like that now—facts with the furniture removed. I know I loved a story once about a soldier who lost everything and kept walking. I can't tell you the story. I can't tell you his name in it. But I know I loved it, the way you know a room you dreamed.

"Your mother had taste," the clerk said quietly.

"My mother died when I was nine." Frost takes and takes. "How did you—"

"Inventories list next of kin," he said smoothly, and it was such a good lie, delivered with such a straight face, that I almost believed it myself. Almost. There was something underneath it, some current, and I thought: *this man has lied to people he loves, and hated it, and gotten good at it.*

The other clerks drifted toward the far wall, where Odile—for that was her name, I'd learn later, though at the time she was just a woman whose forged credentials smelled faintly of beeswax and ambition—was loudly complaining about the shelving taxonomy. That left him and me and ten thousand jars and the hum.

"Do you hear it?" I asked him. Nodding at the floor. "The humming."

"Hard not to."

"The Judges say it's the sound of order. Harmony." I ran my thumb along the edge of my ledger. "I say it's the sound of something trying to finish a sentence. Have you ever had a word stuck in your mouth? Whole years of that. That's what it sounds like to me."

He looked at me then—really looked, the way nobody in the court looks at anybody, because looking costs—and he said, "What was the story? The one you loved."

I blinked. "I haven't told you any—"

"You hum when you work. Little scraps of songs. One of them sounds like a story-song, the kind mothers sing." His ears went red, actually red, a grown man blushing in a vault of stolen souls. "I have a good ear. Occupational hazard."

I should have reported him. Curiosity in a clerk is a flag; the Judges harvest curiosity along with everything else. Instead I heard myself say: "A soldier loses everything. He keeps walking. He—" The rest was gone. Just the shape where it used to be, the way a cup leaves a ring on wood long after the cup is moved. "That's all I have. Isn't that ridiculous? Loving a story you can't remember."

"No," he said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle, and he covered it with a cough. "No, I don't think it's ridiculous at all. I think—" He steadied himself. "I think maybe the loving is the part worth keeping. The details are just packaging."

Somewhere in my chest, in the hollow space where my soul used to sit, something turned over. Like a bird shifting on a nest.

"I have to log this row," I said. "You're in my way, clerk."

"Yes, mistress."

"*Wren.*"

"Yes... Wren."

I wrote numbers in the ledger that meant nothing and signed the row complete, and as I turned to go, I did something I have never done in the court. I broke procedure. I leaned close, close enough to smell road dust and pine tar under the clerk's borrowed robes, and I whispered:

"The green jar on the east wall is mislabeled. The Judges check it at midnight. If I were a clerk who valued his hands, I'd be elsewhere at midnight."

I don't know why I helped him. I still don't, fully. But the frost has never taken the singing, and his voice snagged in my songs like a burr in wool, and blood—whatever the sale left of it—answered blood.

### Sebastian

Midnight found us crouched behind the east wall's shelving while two Judges in bone-white masks unsealed the mislabeled jar and argued, in voices like dry paper, about whether a soul harvested from a tax evader could legally be re-catalogued as contraband. Bureaucracy, it turns out, is the same below the mountain as above it. This was the only comfort available, and I took it.

"She warned us," Brannoc whispered, awed. "Why would she warn us?"

"Because she's kind," I said, "and the court hasn't managed to tax that yet."

Odile sketched in the air with her sigil-hand, tracing the seal patterns she'd memorized from the forged credentials. "Here's the shape of it. The vault locks at solstice eve—everything gets moved below, to the throat, for the harmonizing. Tonight's our window. We need three things working together." She ticked them off. "One: a clerk's seal good enough to open the transfer cage. Mine will hold for exactly as long as nobody looks closely, so pray nobody looks closely. Two: a distraction with weight. Tam's dead are strong but slow, and if the Judges raise the alarm, the whole choir gets locked down—including your girl."

"And three?" Tam asked.

"Three," Brannoc said slowly, "is the hymn." He'd been muttering fragments for hours, pulling them out of the rubble of his faith like splinters from a wound. "The god's voice is caged under the floor, but sound travels. If I sing the old covenant verses—*his* verses, the ones from the broken stones—at the right pitch, the jars will resonate. Every soul in this room will start ringing. Ten thousand bells going off at once. No Judge can think through that. No lock holds against it."

"And the risk?" Maren asked dryly. "There's always a risk. Usually several, wearing hats."

"The hymn needs a counter-voice," Brannoc said. "The covenant was always sung in harmony. Call and answer. I have the call." He paused, and I watched the lapsed priest wrestle with the believer underneath. "But the answer has to come from someone the court owns. Someone whose soul is in a jar. The resonance has to run *through* the theft, do you understand? Through the thing being stolen. Otherwise it's just noise."

The vault went quiet except for the hum, and the hum, for once, seemed to lean in and listen.

"She'd have to sing," I said. "Against the Judges. While her own soul sits three feet away in glass."

"She'd have to *want* to," Maren corrected. "Wanting is the instrument, boy. Everything else is technique."

I thought about the way she'd said *just Wren is fine*. About the whisper, the mislabeled jar, the kindness spent on a stranger because kindness was all the sale had left her. I thought about a story she loved and couldn't remember, about a soldier who lost everything and kept walking, and I wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time with hope—whether the story had been about me, and whether it mattered, and whether love without its reasons was somehow purer or just poorer, and whether I'd ever stop asking questions I couldn't afford answers to.

"I'll ask her," I said.

"You?" Odile raised an eyebrow. "She knows you as a clerk with smudgy fingers."

"I'll manage."

Maren's blind eyes found me with unnerving precision. "You'll tell her the truth, is what you mean. All of it. That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan I've got that doesn't make me sick."

"Hm." She considered. "Those are usually the plans that work. Gods have a sense of humor like that."

### Wren

He found me in the choir corridor during the change of shift, when the frost is thickest and even the overseers stop counting heads.

"You shouldn't be here," I said.

"Probably not." He fell into step beside me, matching my pace exactly, and there it was again—that wrongness, that familiarity, a rhythm my body knew and my ledger-brain couldn't source. "I need to tell you a story."

"I don't like stories."

"Liar. You hum them."

I stopped walking. The corridor stretched away in both directions, bone-white and humming, and I realized I was afraid—not of him, strangely, but of what he was about to break. Some things survive the frost precisely because nobody touches them.

"There was a soldier," he said quietly. "Who lost everything. His god went silent, his family scattered, his name got nailed to a crime he didn't commit. He kept walking. For years. He got very good at being invisible." His voice did something complicated on the word *invisible*, folded it around a knife. "And he had a daughter. And he left her, because he thought leaving was protecting, which is the lie cowardly men tell themselves so they can sleep. And the daughter grew up. And when the world came for him, she paid his debt herself—with the only coin she had."

The frost stirred. Something behind my sternum, in the hollow, shifted like ice on a thawing lake.

"That's not a story," I said. "That's a confession."

"It's both. Best ones usually are." He turned to face me, and up close his eyes were wet and furious and hopeful, an ugly wonderful combination. "Wren. Your soul is in a jar on the third shelf. I've seen it. I've come to steal it back, and I need your voice to do it, and I need you to trust a man you don't remember, and I know how that sounds—"

"How do you know my name?" The question came out small. Smaller than I wanted. "I never told you my name."

"You did. Twenty years ago. You were four, and you made me swear it was a secret, because you'd decided names were powerful." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "You were right."

I should have called the overseers. Every rule of the court, every survival instinct the frost had left me, screamed it. Instead I stood in the humming corridor and listened to the hollow in my chest, and the hollow sang—one clear note, off-key, stubborn, the note Maren Ostvik would later say marked every soul that chose its own chains.

"The soldier in the story," I said. "Did he come back for her?"

"He's here, isn't he?"

The bell in the vault began to ring—solstice eve, the transfer, everything moving below—and somewhere beneath my feet, beneath ten thousand jars, beneath the whole lying architecture of the court, something vast and muffled and patient turned over in its sleep and almost, almost spoke my father's name.
