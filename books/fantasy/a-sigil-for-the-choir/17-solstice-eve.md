## 17. Solstice Eve

### Odile

There is a particular flavor of dread that comes from a debt you agreed to while feeling clever. I know it well. It tastes like copper and bad decisions, and on the morning of solstice eve it arrived at my elbow in the form of Magistrate Corvel's messenger—a gray little man with gray little hands who set a gray little note beside my cup of tea as though he were leaving a tip.

*The hand is owed. The hand is due. Come alone.*

I had known this was coming. Knowing has never once stopped me from hoping otherwise; hope is the one forgery I've never managed to perfect.

The others were busy. Sebastian stood at the mouth of the tunnel with Maren, going over the route for the hundredth time, his voice low and patient in the way of men who have learned that patience is cheaper than panic. Tam was in the corner arguing with a dead rat, which is less mad than it sounds—the rat was being uncooperative. Brannoc murmured hymn-fragments under his breath like a man counting coins he wasn't sure were still legal tender.

Nobody looked up when I slipped out. That was the plan. That was always the plan: be forgettable until forgetting becomes fatal.

Corvel received me in a side chamber off the upper galleries, all velvet and poor lighting, the way small men with large appetites prefer their transactions. He held out his palm like a priest offering communion.

"The sigil-hand," he said. "Left or right, your choice. I'm told the left does finer work."

"You're told correctly," I said. "Which is why you'll want to reconsider."

"Odile." He said my name the way one says the name of a horse one intends to geld. "You forged credentials into the under-court of the Judges themselves. Do you imagine there is any reconsidering left in the world for either of us?"

And here is the thing about honor among con artists: it isn't honor, exactly. It's bookkeeping. I had signed. The signature was real even if everything around it was not. A woman whose entire trade is making false things true cannot then complain when her own true things turn false.

"I'll need both hands tonight," I said. "For the work. You can have whichever you like tomorrow."

Corvel smiled, and I saw that behind the smile he was doing arithmetic, and that the arithmetic frightened him more than I did. "Tomorrow," he allowed, "the court may not exist. Come back after solstice, and we will settle accounts in whatever world remains." He waved me out like smoke. "Go. Forge beautifully. It would be a shame to maim an artist before her masterpiece."

I walked out with both hands and no relief whatsoever, because I understood what I'd just been given: a stay, not a pardon. Debts deferred accrue interest. Somewhere in the dark beneath my feet, the mountain was already counting.

When I got back, Sebastian looked at me—just once, just long enough—and I gave him the smile I give customs officers. He didn't buy it. He didn't press, either. There is a decency in that which I intend to exploit for as long as it lasts.

### Tam

The problem with dead things is that they don't listen. Living things don't either, but living things have the excuse of having something else to do. Dead things are just stubborn.

I needed twelve. Brannoc had done the figuring—if the vault doors opened, the guards would come from three directions, and three directions meant twelve bodies standing between us and them, or at least stumbling toward them in a convincing manner. Twelve sounded easy when Brannoc said it. Brannoc says everything like scripture, so it all sounds easy.

By sundown I had seven.

The ossuary galleries below the old waystation gave up their dead grudgingly. Old bones are tired bones. They've been holding still for decades and they resent being asked to commute. I sat cross-legged on the cold floor with my hands flat against the stone, sweating, shaking, doing the thing nobody likes to watch, and seven skeletons stood in a ragged line and swayed like drunks at a funeral.

"That's not enough," Odile said, appearing at my shoulder the way she does, smelling of ink and secrets.

"Thank you," I said. "I hadn't counted."

"I'm just saying, if you could find five more—"

"They're not *findable*, they're—" I stopped, because shouting at Odile is like shouting at a locked door; satisfying, but the door doesn't care. "They're tired," I finished, quieter. "Everything down here is tired."

She crouched next to me. For a moment she didn't say anything clever, which from Odile is practically a eulogy.

"My grandmother," she said, "used to say that the dead aren't gone, they're just unpaid. Somebody owes them something—a burial, an apology, a story—and until it's paid they can't leave." She tapped my knee with one ink-stained finger. "So pay them. Tell them what they're walking toward. Dead folk hate surprises same as anyone."

It was nonsense. It was probably nonsense.

I did it anyway. I knelt in front of my seven swaying soldiers and I told them: there's a vault below full of stolen voices, and a god in a jar, and a girl who sold herself so her father could keep breathing, and tomorrow we're going to break it open, and they could come or not, but if they came they wouldn't be guarding anything anymore—they'd be part of the breaking. First useful thing any of them would have done since they died.

The line straightened. All seven of them, spine by spine, like a choir finding its pitch.

And then, from the gallery behind me, footsteps. Five sets. Slow, deliberate, dragging slightly on the left.

I hadn't called them.

Odile's hand found my shoulder. "Tam," she said softly, "I think you just recruited."

### Maren

Sound tells you everything, if you let it. Stone speaks. Water gossips. Air carries grudges. I have spent sixty years listening to a world that stopped showing itself to me, and I will tell you what I have learned: silence is never empty. Silence is a room with the door held shut.

That evening, with the company gathered and the plan spread out in Odile's beautiful lies upon the table, I put my hand flat on the stone and listened to the mountain underneath us, and what I heard made my stomach drop through the floor and keep falling.

"It's quiet down there," I said. "Too quiet. You all understand that the vault doesn't just hold voices—it *eats* them. Every echo that goes into that place comes back thinner. Sound goes in and doesn't come out whole."

"So guide us another way," Sebastian said. "Feel along the walls. You've done it before."

"In the halls, yes. In the gate, maybe. But the vault itself—" I shook my head, hating every word, hating more that they were true. "Child, I am blind because my eyes quit. My ears are all I am. And in that room, my ears will be taken from me too. I'll be nothing. Less than nothing—I'll be a burden in a place where burdens get people killed."

The silence that followed had its own texture. I know silences the way other women know faces. This one was guilt and love braided together, and nobody wanted to be the first to cut it.

It was Wren who spoke.

"Then you won't need your ears," she said. "Because you'll have mine."

I turned toward her voice—young, steady, with a crack running through it that she probably thought was hidden. "Explain."

"The choir-song. The one the Judges make us sing. I've been changing it for weeks—small things, wrong notes, verses that shouldn't fit. The song remembers where it's been. If I sing it in the vault, every wall it's ever touched will answer it. I can't hear those answers. But you can." Her voice steadied as she went, the way ice thickens as it freezes. "You'll be deaf in there. So I'll be your ears, and you'll be mine. We'll each carry half the map."

"And if the Judges hear the corruption in your song?" Brannoc asked gently. "If they realize what you've been doing?"

Wren laughed—a short, startled sound, like she'd surprised herself. "Then they'll learn what I've been doing at the worst possible moment for them. Sir, I sold my soul to these people. I'd like very much to ruin their solstice."

I reached across the table and found her hand. Small. Cold. Gripping like someone twice her size.

"Half a map," I said, "is better than none. But hear me, girl: when I tell you to run, you run, and you don't wait for the half I'm carrying. Promise me."

She promised. I felt the oath go through her and out into the stone, and somewhere far below, something vast and muffled shifted in its sleep, the way a dog shifts when it hears its name.

### Wren

Here is what nobody tells you about volunteering: the moment after you do it, when everyone is looking at you with gratitude, and you realize that gratitude is just fear wearing its good coat.

We spent the last hours before solstice the way soldiers spend the night before a battle—not sleeping, not quite talking, doing small necessary things with exaggerated care. Odile re-inked our forged credentials for the third time, muttering that the seal on Tam's clerk-papers was "a tragedy" and fixing it anyway. Brannoc sewed a hymnal into the lining of his coat, page by page, like a man burying treasure he intended to dig up himself. Sebastian checked and rechecked the route until Maren told him, with great kindness, that the walls were getting bored of him.

I sat apart and practiced.

The corrupted song is a strange creature. It began as the choir's obedience—an anthem the Judges wrote to keep us harmonized, our half-souls humming in approved intervals. But I had been feeding it wrongness for weeks. A flattened third here. A verse reversed there. A phrase from a lullaby my father used to sing, smuggled in where a doxology should be, because I couldn't remember why that lullaby mattered, only that it burned in me like a coal that refused to go out.

Now the song was a lie with a spine. Now it was a key learning to think of itself as a blade.

I sang it through, quietly, in the dark of the staging gallery, and heard the stones around me hum it back with tiny variations—each wall adding its own memory, its own small treason. When I finished, the silence afterward was different. Fuller. Like a room that had been holding its breath and finally exhaled.

"You sing like someone getting away with something," said a voice, and I didn't jump, because I'd heard him coming—everyone's father walks the same way, heavy on the heels, apologizing to the floorboards.

Sebastian lowered himself onto the bench beside me. Up close he smelled of road dust and lamp oil, and there was a crease between his brows that I suspected lived there permanently.

"Does it bother you?" I asked. "What I'm about to do? Singing rebellion in their own chapel?"

He considered it honestly, which I was learning was his way—he considered everything honestly, as though honesty were a muscle he refused to let atrophy. "It bothers me that you have to," he said. "The singing itself seems fine."

"It's a terrible song."

"It's a terrible song," he agreed solemnly, "sung by the right person."

We sat there while the mountain ticked toward midnight. Somewhere above, the last true sunset of autumn was happening without us. Somewhere below, ten thousand jars waited with their stolen mouths shut.

"Whatever happens tomorrow," Sebastian said at last, "I want you to know—"

"Don't." I said it fast, before the feeling could arrive and wreck me. "Don't make it a speech. Speeches are for funerals and we're not attending one. We're attending a robbery."

A breath moved through him that might have been a laugh. "A robbery," he said. "With a choir."

"Every good crime needs music. Ask anyone."

He stood, and paused, and I felt him wanting to say the thing anyway—fathers always want to say the thing—but he let it go, and patted my shoulder once, awkwardly, like a man handling something he was afraid of breaking.

"Get some sleep, Wren."

I didn't sleep. I sat in the dark and sang the corrupted verse one more time, soft as breath, and at the very bottom of the song, beneath the lullaby, beneath the lies, I heard—for the first time, faint as a pulse through a wall—something answering.

Not a wall. Not an echo.

A voice. Deep and vast and muffled, like a god speaking through ten thousand closed jars, and it was singing harmony.

### Sebastian

Midnight came, and with it the solstice, and with it the hour we had built ourselves toward out of forgery and grave-dirt and borrowed courage.

We stood at the threshold of the deep stair, twelve risen dead at our backs, four liars and a priest and a blind woman and a half-souled girl at our front, and I looked at each of them in turn and understood, with a clarity I had not earned, that every one of them was about to pay for someone else's rescue. Odile with her hands. Tam with his strength. Maren with her hearing. Brannoc with the faith he'd sworn off. Wren with the last of herself.

And me with whatever the vault decided to take.

Maren tilted her head, listening to the mountain's slow clockwork. "The gates change at first bell," she said. "After that, the court seals for the rite, and nothing goes in or out that the Judges don't invite."

Brannoc unwound the hymnal from his coat. Odile flexed her ink-stained fingers. Tam whispered something to his dead, and they rattled forward like a tide deciding.

Wren stepped past us all to the head of the stair, took a breath that shook only a little, and began to sing.

The corrupted song went down into the dark ahead of us like a lit fuse.

And the mountain—every mile of it, every hollow bone of it—began, very softly, to sing back.
