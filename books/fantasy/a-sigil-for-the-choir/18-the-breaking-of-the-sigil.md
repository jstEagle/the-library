## 18. The Breaking of the Sigil

Every plan I've ever been part of has had the same shape: a long stretch of careful nothing, then a moment where everything goes wrong at once, and then whatever survives of you picks itself up off the floor. I'd told myself this one would be different because we had a forged writ, a dead man walking, a blind woman who navigated by echo, and a priest who hadn't believed in anything in twenty years. That is not a list that inspires confidence. That is a list that gets read out at an inquest.

But for about four hundred heartbeats, it worked.

We moved through the vault of voices in our soul-clerk grays, Odile's forgery humming against my chest like a second heartbeat—her sigils always did that, lied so beautifully they almost convinced themselves. Ten thousand jars lined the walls from floor to unseen ceiling, each one glowing faintly, each one holding a voice or a soul or some currency between the two that the Judges had spent centuries convincing everyone was theirs to collect. The air tasted like candle smoke and cold iron. Somewhere above us, Wren was singing the corrupted verse she'd spent weeks threading through the choir's ritual song, her voice coming down through the stone like water through a crack.

It was working. The guards on the vault door were swaying. Brannoc's hymn under it, low as breath, was doing its work on the jars themselves—a hairline tremor running along the nearest shelf, dust sifting down like the mountain was shedding skin.

"Third row," Maren murmured beside me, her blind eyes aimed at nothing and everything. "Left side. It sings wrong, your daughter's jar. Out of tune with all the rest."

Of course it did. She'd been out of tune with every choir they'd ever put her in. That was my girl.

I found it between a judge's harvested oath and a farmer's dying prayer—the sigil-seal, black wax over white bone, Wren's name cut into it in the court's angular script. My hands were steady. Twenty years of hiding my gift had taught me that much: the steadier the storm inside, the steadier the hands have to look.

I pressed my palm flat against the seal and reached for it the way I'd reached for bridge-stones and oath-rings my whole quiet life, reading the binding, looking for the flaw.

There wasn't one.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the court beneath the mountain: they are not sloppy. Every other power I'd ever robbed had been built by men, and men leave gaps—you can drive a smuggler's cart through the space between what a law says and what its enforcers bother to check. But this seal had no gap. It wasn't locked. It was *woven*, thread over thread over thread, and every thread was paid for. A soul given freely cannot be stolen back by force. That was the whole architecture of the place. They didn't take. They made taking look like giving.

"It won't break," I said. My voice came out flat and strange.

"Everything breaks," Tam said behind me. He had his hand on the shoulder of the dead clerk we'd brought along for door-duty, a gray-faced man named Hodd whose corpse was proving more reliable than most living colleagues. "You just have to find what it loves more than holding together."

"I know what it loves." I did. That was the problem. "It loves being paid."

And then everything went wrong at once, right on schedule.

The singing stopped.

Not faded—stopped, mid-phrase, the way a bird stops when the cat's shadow crosses the window. And in the silence that followed, down the long throat of the corridor, came footsteps. Not the shuffling sway of charmed guards. Measured boots. Many. And a voice I knew, carrying ahead of them the way her voice always had, across parade grounds and prison yards and one bad night in Greyford that I'd spent three years trying to forget:

"Clerks! Away from the shelves!"

Odile swore with the fluency of a woman who'd been hanged twice in absentia. "The writ," she hissed, "the writ will hold, watch—" and she stepped forward, forgery raised, beautiful and lying, as the torchlight came around the corner and fell on Captain Ilse Varga and six of the crown's hunters, swords already drawn.

Varga looked at the writ. I watched her look at it—watched the hunter's stillness in her, the same stillness she'd had at nineteen when we'd stood watch together and she'd caught a smuggler's lie in the space of a breath. Her eyes went from the seal to Odile's face to mine, and something moved behind them that wasn't duty. It was arithmetic. I'd seen her do this math before, over ledgers, over evidence that didn't match her memory of me. I could only pray the sum came out the way it had been trending.

"The writ is forged," she said.

"It is immaculate," Odile said.

"It is forged," Varga repeated, "and I don't care." She turned to her hunters. "These clerks are under crown escort. We're taking custody of the vault inventory."

One of them—a young one, faithful, the dangerous kind—hesitated. "Captain, the Judges said—"

"The Judges," Varga said, "sent me down here to execute fugitives without trial. I've read the village records from Thornfell. I've read who signed the orders. If the Judges want to discuss chain of command, they can climb down here and discuss it with me." Then the young one moved anyway, sword up, faith outrunning sense, and the vault went to noise.

I stopped watching after the first exchange of steel. Not from courage—from arithmetic of my own. Six hunters, one deserter, one boy who'd already turned two corpses loose among the guard, one forger, one priest chanting, one blind woman standing perfectly calm in the middle of it because sound had told her exactly where every blade was before it moved. We would win or lose this room without me. But there was only one person in the world who could open that seal, and she was currently pouring herself into it.

I put both hands on Wren's sigil and pushed.

Nothing. The weave held, patient as tax law. Paid for. Given freely. Sealed with consent, and consent, the court had learned, was the strongest mortar ever mixed—it couldn't be broken from outside because outside was exactly where it expected thieves to stand.

"You have to be inside it," Maren said quietly. She hadn't turned from the fight; her head was cocked toward me, listening to my failure. "That's how their law works. Everything taken must be given. You can't crack it, Sebastian. You can only pay it."

"With what? I don't carry coin down here. None of us do."

"No," she said. "But you're carrying something heavier than coin. I've heard it in your voice since Greyford. All those years of silence, and the one thing you never stopped talking about, even when you said nothing at all."

And I understood.

Memory. The Judge in the corridor had offered it to me days ago—or hours; time ran strange down here—framed as mercy: give us your memories as raw material and walk away with your daughter. I'd refused because refusing felt like the last piece of myself I owned. Now there were no Judges offering terms. There was just a seal woven from freely-given things, and the only freely-given thing I had left was every moment I'd ever hoarded of her.

The fight was going badly at the edges—I heard Tam shout, heard a body drop that might have been friend or corpse, heard Brannoc's hymn crack and recover—and I shut all of it out. This is the mercenary's oldest trick, the one they never teach in the songs: when the battle is too big, shrink the world. Shrink it to the width of your hands.

I thought of Wren at four, asleep in the crook of my arm on a ferry crossing, drooling on my good coat, and how I'd let her rather than wake her, and how my arm went numb and stayed numb for a day and I'd told no one because telling meant explaining why I was smiling.

I fed it into the seal. The first thread loosened.

I thought of her at seven, teaching herself to whistle with two fingers, failing for a month, and the afternoon she finally got it—one ragged, glorious note—and how she whistled it at everything for a week, at the kettle, at the mule, at me, and how I pretended to be annoyed and would have fought anyone who actually was.

Second thread. The weave groaned.

Her at nine, furious at me, drawing a line down the middle of the cottage floor in chalk and declaring half of it hers, and how I honored the border for three days until she got lonely and erased it herself and never mentioned it again, and neither did I, because some treaties are best left unratified.

Third thread. Something deep in the vault changed pitch. The jars were rattling now, all ten thousand of them, Brannoc's hymn and Wren's silenced song and the god's imprisoned voice all leaning toward the sound of a lock coming apart.

Her at twelve, standing in the doorway with a packed bag, saying she was leaving, and me saying fine, and meaning come back, and her knowing I meant come back, and neither of us having the language for it—that was the night I broke us, and I carried the shape of that doorway in my chest for fifteen years like a swallowed stone.

Fourth thread. Fifth. The seal was screaming now, thin and high, and I could feel what it was doing to me—could feel the memories going *through* me instead of *out* of me, the difference between paying with coin and paying with blood. Each one passed into the weave and came out the other side lighter and thinner, like cloth worn through. I could still see the ferry. I could still hear the whistle. But the edges were going soft, the way a face goes soft in a dream you're waking from.

I didn't stop. That's the part I want written down somewhere, if anyone ever asks: I knew exactly what it cost, and I didn't stop.

Her laugh. Her terrible handwriting. The way she said my name when she wanted something versus when she was angry—two different words wearing the same letters. The chalk border. The ferry. The whistle. The doorway. Her whole childhood, which was also my whole redemption, poured thread by thread into a lock built by judges who believed everything had a price and had simply never imagined someone would pay this one gladly.

The sigil shattered.

Not cracked—shattered, black wax bursting into ash, and the sound it made was not a sound at all but a *voice*, one note, high and clear and unmistakably hers, the ragged glorious whistle-note grown up and perfected, ringing through the vault like dawn through a broken roof. The nearest jars split in answer. Then the next shelf. Then ten thousand seals letting go at once, and the vault filled with voices—harvested oaths, stolen prayers, souls unbound, and underneath all of it, rising like a tide finding its shore, something vast and old and *glad*.

The god's voice. Unmuzzled. Filling the under-court from wall to wall, and every jar it passed through it set free.

I was on my knees. I don't remember getting there. My hands were empty and my chest was empty and I tried to think of her face and found—found the shape where a face should be, warm and beloved and utterly blank, like a window lit from behind with the curtain drawn.

"Sebastian." Maren's hand on my shoulder, steady. "It's done. Look up."

At the far end of the vault, past the wreckage of the fight, past Varga standing bloodied over her own former hunters with her sword still drawn and her face full of a decision she'd already made, a girl was running toward me. Brown hair escaping its braid. Court-gray robes torn at the hem. Whole—visibly, radiantly whole, her eyes lit from inside with everything the sale had taken and returned.

She was calling my name. I knew the word. I knew I loved the word. I could not, for the life of me, remember whose voice it had been before tonight.

She reached me and caught my face in both hands, laughing and crying at once, and said, "You did it, you impossible stubborn man, you actually—" and then she stopped, because she saw it. Whatever was missing. Daughters always see.

"It's all right," I said, and found that it mostly was. "Tell me your name. I feel like I should know it."

Behind her, in the ruined vault, the god's voice gathered itself out of ten thousand freed jars and spoke—not in thunder, not in law, but in a tone I recognized the way you recognize home from a great distance—with terrible gentleness, it said my name, and asked me what I wished remembered.
