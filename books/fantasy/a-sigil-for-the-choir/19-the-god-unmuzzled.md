## 19. The God Unmuzzled

I had been a priest for eleven years and a lapsed one for nine, and in all that time I had learned exactly one theological truth worth carrying: grace does not knock. It kicks the door off its hinges while you're standing in the wrong room holding the wrong end of a very bad plan.

I was standing in the Vault of Voices, holding a hymn I'd rewritten three times that night, when the sigil broke.

You should understand what a vault of ten thousand sealed jars sounds like before it breaks. It doesn't sound like anything. That's the horror of it. Ten thousand jars, each one holding a piece of a god's voice—or a soul's voice, or both; down here the distinction had been sanded away by centuries of careful theft—and every single one silent. Not empty. *Silenced.* There is a difference, and the difference has weight. An empty jar rings when you tap it. A silenced one swallows the sound of your knuckle like it owes you money.

Maren had gone deaf to her own sound-mapping the moment we entered—she'd warned us she would—and so she stood with her hand on Odile's shoulder, listening through another woman's bones, which is either beautiful or blasphemous depending on how many sermons you've slept through. Tam's dead were arranged along the walls like an honor guard nobody had asked for. Wren was singing.

And Sebastian Eriksen—the man who had spent twenty years hiding his gift so well he'd half-convinced himself he didn't have one—was pouring his memories of his daughter into her own soul-sigil like a man emptying his purse into a beggar's cup, knowing the beggar was a lie and the cup was bottomless.

The sigil cracked.

I want to tell you I felt it coming. I didn't. What I felt was my teeth ache, then my fillings, then the stone under my boots, and then the world turned inside out like a glove.

* * *

Here is what happens when a god who has been muzzled for two hundred years gets its voice back inside a mountain built to amplify sound:

Everything sings whether it wants to or not.

The jars went first. All ten thousand at once—not breaking so much as *opening*, seams splitting like eyes waking, and what came out wasn't noise. Noise is chaos. This was worse. This was *order*. Every stolen syllable, every harvested oath, every whispered prayer the Judges had bottled as raw material for their law came out at once, and each one was still trying to be what it had been: a marriage vow, a deathbed confession, a child's blessing over a sick lamb, a smuggler's promise on a bridge-stone. They collided. They harmonized anyway, because voices are stubborn that way, and the resulting chord went through the vault, through the halls, through the bone-white architecture of the under-court like a bell struck from the inside.

I fell to my knees. So did everyone else, including the dead, which I found professionally embarrassing—I'm the one who raises them, they should follow my lead.

But here's the thing about falling to your knees after nine years of insisting you don't believe anymore: your knees remember. Your knees have been waiting. And when the chord hit me—when the god's actual voice, unmuzzled, unfiltered, poured through the vault like light through cracked glass—I did not pray. Prayer implies you're asking for something. I just *received*, the way a dry riverbed receives rain, without dignity, without negotiation, groaning in every channel at once.

"Brannoc," Maren said somewhere above me, her hand finding my shoulder by sound alone. "Your face."

"What about it?"

"It's doing something honest. Try to survive it."

* * *

The Judges' authority died faster than any empire in history, and it died of exactly what it was made of: words.

I saw it happen in the great hall beyond the vault, because the vault doors had blown open and none of us were fool enough to close them. The Magistrate-Judges stood in their tiers, robed in grey, and every law they spoke came out as noise. Their authority was built on stolen speech—you cannot legislate with someone else's voice once that voice has gone home—and so when the god spoke, their verdicts dissolved mid-word. I watched a Judge pronounce sentence on a soul-clerk and the sentence unravel into static, into birdsong, into somebody's grandmother saying *eat something, you're too thin*. The clerk looked at the Judge the way you look at a clock that's stopped: not angry, just done pretending.

One of them drew a blade. The blade hummed a lullaby. He dropped it.

Law built on theft cannot survive the theft being reversed. I'd have wept for them if they hadn't spent two centuries selling other people's souls by the measure. Instead I laughed, which surprised me, and which Varga—who had arrived mid-chaos with her sword still wet from cutting down guards who'd been pursuing us, a sentence I never expected to compose—heard and did not comment on. She stood at the vault threshold with the expression of a woman watching her whole belief system burn and discovering the fire is warm.

"The crown sent me to execute fugitives," she said, mostly to herself. "Nobody said anything about witnessing a resurrection."

"Get used to disappointment," Odile said. Her left sleeve was dark where the sigil-hand debt had come due early; she'd paid something down there in the scuffle, something I suspect we'll learn about when she's good and ready, which is to say never. She was grinning anyway. Forgers love nothing more than watching an authentic document get destroyed.

* * *

Then the god spoke a name, and everything got complicated again.

Not my name. I want that on the record. Nine years of lapsed faith, one night of grace, and the god's first word in the free air was *Sebastian*.

It didn't say it the way people say names. It said it the way a bell says noon. It came from everywhere—from the split jars, from the stones, from the marrow of the mountain itself—and it landed on that scarred, secretive, self-punishing man kneeling beside his daughter's emptied sigil-shard, and the whole under-court leaned in to listen.

*Sebastian Eriksen,* the god said, and I felt the words in my back teeth. *You gave.*

He didn't answer. He couldn't, probably. When a god addresses you directly, your tongue files for leave.

*You poured your remembering into the breaking, and the breaking held. She is whole. You are less. This is the oldest arithmetic there is, and I have never liked it.*

Wren was standing beside him. Whole—I could see it, see it the way you see color return to a gray face. Whatever the sale had thinned in her had flooded back, and she was looking at her father with the terrible newness of a person meeting a stranger she has apparently loved for years. Because that was the price, and we all knew it, and none of us had said it aloud: the memories he'd fed the sigil were gone. Not stored. *Spent.* Fuel burns.

"I'd do it again," Sebastian said hoarsely. "Don't offer me a refund."

*I am not offering a refund,* the god said, and there was something in its voice that I recognized, gods help me, from my own sermons—the thing a preacher does when the congregation expects thunder and you give them gentleness instead, and it lands harder than thunder ever could. *I am offering a restoration. My voice is mine again. And a voice can carry what was given away, if the giver consents to receive it back.*

The silence in the vault changed texture. Even the dead shifted.

"You can give him back what he spent," Maren said quietly. Not a question. She reads sound the way I used to read scripture, and she'd heard the shape of the offer before it finished forming.

*I can,* the god said. *Memory is only a song the mind sings to itself. I have ten thousand songs returned to me tonight. One more is nothing.*

"And the cost?" Odile asked, because Odile always asks the cost out loud, on principle, having built a career on people who don't. "There's always a cost. Free miracles are how temples fund their second buildings."

The god was quiet for a moment—a real quiet, a considering quiet, and I realized with a jolt that this was the first time in two hundred years anyone had asked it a question it hadn't already been forced to answer.

*The cost is choice,* it said. *A memory restored by force is a haunting. A memory restored by consent is a gift. So I will not decide for him. Sebastian Eriksen: I can return what you gave. But a man cannot hold two versions of a life. If I restore your memories of your daughter as they were—all the years you carried, the good and the shameful, the lullabies and the leaving—then the man who chose to forget them, the man who bought her freedom with them, ceases. He becomes a story you once told. Do you understand? You may have her past back, but you will lose the self who loved her enough to pay for it.*

And the vault held its breath, all of us, even Tam's dead, even Varga, even the last Judge cowering behind his dissolving podium.

Because I understood, and I wished to God I didn't. This was the trap inside the mercy. Grace arriving with a price tag isn't rare—that's most grace, honestly—but usually the price is paid in suffering, and suffering at least leaves you intact. This price was paid in *selfhood*. Take back the memories, lose the man who sacrificed them. Keep the sacrifice, stay a stranger to your own daughter forever.

"That's not a choice," Sebastian said. His voice was wrecked. "That's a riddle."

*All choices are riddles,* the god said. *That is why I gave you voices instead of answers.*

* * *

I should say what I was doing during all this, since this is supposedly my account. I was praying. First time in nine years. Not eloquently—I'd forgotten most of the liturgy and improvised the rest, which the god presumably found either charming or heretical. But I knelt there in the wreckage of the vault with ten thousand freed voices humming in the stones around me, and I prayed the only prayer I had left, which was: *don't let him choose wrong, and don't let me watch if he does.*

He knelt there a long time. Wren knelt beside him. She didn't touch him—she'd been reaching for him earlier and he'd flinched, not from her, from the gap where twenty years of her should have been—and now she sat with her hands folded, waiting, patient as frost, this woman who had sold her soul for a father who couldn't remember why she looked at him like that.

"I don't want them back," he said finally.

"*Sebastian*—" Wren started.

"Let me finish." His voice steadied. "If I take the old memories, I get the man who left you. I know him. I've met him. He was very good at hiding and very bad at staying." He laughed, wet and awful. "The man sitting here right now—he's new. He's got nothing but today. But everything he has, he has *because of you*. I'd rather be a stranger you saved than a father who remembers leaving."

The god was silent. Then:

*Brannoc.*

My spine went rigid. Of course. Of course it would turn to the lapsed priest for the benediction, because gods are cruel in the small ways even when they're merciful in the large ones.

*You kept fragments of my voice in your hymns for nine years. Broken verses, altered lines. You thought this was doubt. It was custody. Say the words over him. Make the restoration—or refuse it—holy.*

So there it was. Nine years of sleeping in hedgerows and drinking too much and telling myself the silence meant the god had died, and it turns out I'd been a courier the whole time, carrying contraband scripture in my throat, waiting for a delivery address.

I stood up. My knees objected. I ignored them.

I put my hand on Sebastian Eriksen's head, and I said the old words—the ones from before the court, before the muzzle, the ones Brannoc-the-priest knew and Brannoc-the-drunk had somehow never managed to spit out—and I altered the ending, because the old ending was about obedience and this moment deserved better:

*"What was given in love is not lost. What was spent for another is not spent. Let the keeping go, and the keeping-of-him remain. Amen, and also: thank you."*

Light, or something like it—the acoustic equivalent, a brightness you heard rather than saw—moved through the vault. The jars sang. Somewhere far above, I swear on what's left of my faith, the mountain itself exhaled.

And Sebastian Eriksen stayed exactly as he was: a man who loves his daughter completely and cannot picture her childhood at all.

*It is done,* the god said softly. *He chose. It stands.*

Wren made a sound I'll hear until I die—half grief, half laughter, the exact sound of a woman getting back everything except the one thing she wanted for him. She took his hand. He let her. He didn't know her, not really, not yet. He held on anyway.

That's when the god spoke again, and its voice had changed—lighter, almost amused, a thing stretching muscles it hadn't used in two centuries.

*Now. There remains the matter of the road up, the hunters above, and the solstice dawn, at which point every soul I have freed must walk out of my mountain and into a world that burned witches last month. They will need oaths to hold them together. Real ones. Freely given.* A pause, and I could have sworn the old stone chuckled. *Brannoc. You're ordained, aren't you?*

I looked at the ragged, bleeding, brilliant lot of them—the smuggler, the singer, the forger, the boy with his dead, the blind mapmaker, the deserter with a crown's blood on her sword—and I heard myself say, before my nine years of lapsed wisdom could stop my mouth:

"Everyone who's staying together, raise your right hand."

They did. Every last one of them. Even the dead, which Tam swore afterward was not his doing.

I took a breath, and began the only sermon I remembered entire: the one about what we owe each other.
