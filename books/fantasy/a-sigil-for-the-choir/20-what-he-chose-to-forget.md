## 20. What He Chose to Forget

The god asked me a question, and I have hated questions my whole life, so naturally this was the worst one ever put to a man.

*What do you want remembered?*

The vault was coming apart around us in slow motion—ten thousand jars splitting like ice on a spring pond, the god's voice pouring out of them in a flood that wasn't sound so much as *permission*, and every Judge in the under-court was discovering at once that law built on stolen speech makes very poor footing. Somewhere behind me Odile was laughing the way she laughed when a forgery finally worked, which is to say like a woman getting away with something. Brannoc was weeping and singing at the same time, doing both badly. Tam had his dead arranged in a defensive line and looked embarrassed about it.

And Wren stood in front of me, whole.

That was the thing. The sigil was shattered, her soul unbound, and she stood there in the vault's white light looking at me with eyes I knew and did not know, because the payment had already begun coming out of me. I could feel it going. Not pain—worse than pain. Pain you can grip. This was more like watching snow melt off a roof: nothing dramatic, just one warm drip after another, and each drip a piece of her.

Her at four years old, standing on a fence rail with her arms out, daring gravity to try something. Gone.

Her at nine, teaching herself letters off smuggled broadsheets because I was too proud or too poor or too cowardly to send her to a proper tutor. Dripping away.

Her at twelve, furious at me—I remembered being the object of the fury, and then even the shape of what she'd been furious *about* went soft and grey, like ink left in rain.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the only currency I've got left: for one moment—one long, coward's heartbeat—I considered keeping it. The god was offering. That was the horror of it. It wasn't demanding; it was *asking*, politely, the way no power has ever asked me anything. Restore yourself, it said, or restore her. You may keep what is yours.

But none of it was mine. That was the whole lesson of my life and I'd been a slow student. Her soul wasn't mine. Her childhood wasn't mine. Even my memories of her weren't really mine—they were hers, loaned to me by proximity and love, and I had already defaulted on that loan once, years ago, when I walked out of Thornfell and told myself it was for her safety. I'd spent twenty years paying interest on that lie. I wasn't going to take collateral now, at the end, when the debt had finally come due in full.

"Restore her," I said. "All of her. Every piece."

*It will cost—*

"I know what it costs. I'm the one holding the bill." My voice cracked on it, which was fine, because everyone in that vault was cracking somewhere. "Take the memories. All of them. I don't want a discount. I don't want to remember that I forgot—that seems crueler than clean forgetting. If she comes back whole, let me meet her like a stranger who happens to know he loves her. That's enough. That's more than I deserve."

The god was silent for a moment—which, given that it had just spent centuries being silenced against its will, felt like a joke it was allowing itself—and then it said, in a voice like a bell rung underwater:

*You understand you are choosing her over yourself.*

"I've been choosing myself for twenty years," I said. "Look how that went."

And then it took them.

I won't describe it, because there's nothing to describe. That's the trick of losing memories—you can't stand at the door and watch them leave. There was a warmth behind my eyes, and a sense of something vast and gentle passing through me like wind through an open window, and then—

Then there was a girl standing in front of me, and I loved her.

That's all I can tell you about the before. I knew it the way you know your own hands. Whatever the god took, it left the love itself—it couldn't take that, or wouldn't, or maybe love isn't stored in memory at all but somewhere deeper, in the marrow, in the oath-stone of the body. I looked at this young woman with frost-pale hair and her mother's stubborn chin and I thought: *there she is.* And I thought, immediately after: *who is she?*

And then I thought, third and most urgent: *why am I crying?*

* * *

I woke on the mountainside.

I don't remember the climb out. None of us do, I think—the god carried us up the way water carries leaves, without asking permission, and set us down in the high pasture below the Pass of Broken Oaths where the morning sun was doing its best work on the snow. Someone had put a blanket over me. Someone had built a fire. There was a dead mule grazing nearby with an air of profound confusion, and Tam sitting beside it apologizing to it in a low voice, which raised questions I decided to defer.

My head was quiet. That was the first thing I noticed. For twenty years my skull had been a room with a silent god rattling around inside it, and now the room was empty—not godless, exactly, because I could feel the voice humming up through the mountain stones beneath me, vast and content, taking oaths from anyone who cared to swear them—but empty of *me*. There were holes in me shaped like a person, and I didn't know the person's name.

I sat up. Brannoc was nearby, asleep sitting upright with his hymnal open on his chest, smiling in his sleep like a man who'd gotten good news in a dream. Maren sat with her back against a stone, face turned toward the sun she couldn't see, listening to something. Odile was wrapping her right hand in bandages with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd made a bargain she hadn't mentioned to anyone and intended to keep mentioning to no one. I noted that. Filed it. A man with holes in his memory learns to file things fast.

"You're awake," Maren said, without turning. "Good. You slept like the dead, which around Tam is a professional hazard."

"How long?"

"Half a day. The mountain spat us out at dawn yesterday. You've been snoring on holy ground since." She tilted her head. "How much did it take?"

I reached inside myself, carefully, the way you probe a missing tooth. "Everything," I said. "There's a girl. I know I love her. I don't know her name, her face, why, when, any of it. Just—" I gestured helplessly at my own chest. "This. Like a scar with no wound attached."

Maren was quiet a moment. Blind as she was, she had a way of looking at you anyway, straight through the eyes into whatever was underneath. "You chose that," she said. Not a question.

"It seemed rude to haggle."

"It usually does," she said, "right up until the haggling starts." Then, softer: "She'll come find you, you know. They always do. Souls that were bought back don't stay lost."

"I don't even know what to call her."

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest and kindest smile I'd seen in a book with no happy chapters. "Then you'll get to hear it for the first time twice. Some men wait their whole lives for that."

* * *

She came at dusk.

I saw her coming down the slope from the pass road, and my heart did something embarrassing and involuntary, lurching like a bridge-stone under bad weight, and I still didn't know why. She walked like someone carrying something fragile and precious and newly repaired—carefully, testing each step. The light caught her hair and turned it to frost-fire, and I thought, absurdly, *I have seen that exact light before,* and then thought, *no, you haven't, you'd remember,* and then understood that remembering was precisely the thing I couldn't do anymore, and sat down hard on a rock.

She stopped ten feet away. We looked at each other.

Up close she had a small scar on her jaw I didn't recognize and a way of holding her shoulders that suggested a person who had spent a long time braced for blows that had finally, blessedly, stopped coming. Her eyes were wet. So were mine. We were, between us, producing a remarkable amount of moisture for two strangers on a mountaintop.

"Hello," she said.

"Hello."

"My name is Wren."

The name went through me like a bell through water. Not recognition—recognition requires something to recognize *with*. It was more like hearing a song you've never heard and knowing every note. "Wren," I repeated, and my voice broke in the middle of it, and I didn't try to fix it.

"That's me." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, the way people do when they've decided crying is happening *to* them rather than *by* them. "Wren Eriksen. And you're Sebastian Eriksen, mercenary, oath-reader, the man who broke the sigil and gave—" she stopped. Started again. "They told me what you paid. The god told me, actually, which was strange, because I'd only just met it, and it talks like a judge and cries like a grandmother, if a grandmother could be a mountain."

"I don't remember any of it," I said. "The vault. The choice. You." I tapped my temple. "Clean sweep. Apparently I asked for it that way."

"I know." She came closer, slowly, and sat on the rock across from me, close enough that I could see her hands shaking. "I've been trying to decide whether to be angry about that. The not wanting to remember that you forgot. Because part of me thinks it would be easier if you grieved me properly, you know? If you knew what was missing. And the other part of me—" her breath hitched— "the other part thinks it's the most generous thing anyone's ever done, and I don't know what to do with that except sit here and tell you my name like we're meeting at a market."

"We could pretend we're meeting at a market," I offered. "I'm told I'm good at pretending. Professionally, in fact."

A laugh escaped her, wet and startled, and something in my chest turned over at the sound of it, some lock clicking open that had no business opening for a stranger's laugh. "You're terrible," she said. "Brannoc says you're terrible. He says you once rerouted an entire caravan around a bridge because you read the oath-stone wrong on purpose to avoid sharing a campfire with a man who talked too much."

"That sounds like a slander I would absolutely commit."

"He says you hum when you think no one's listening. Off-key."

"Now that's a lie. I hum beautifully. Ask anyone whose opinion doesn't matter."

She was crying again, and laughing again, doing both at once like Brannoc with his hymn, and I realized I was memorizing her—not the past, I had no past to hang her on, but the present, furiously, greedily, the way a man drinks after a long thirst. The scar on her jaw. The way she pushed her hair back with two fingers. The gap in her smile, lower left, that I suspected came from a story I would now never know and would have to ask about, and ask again, and ask until she got tired of telling it, because that was the arrangement now. She was a country I'd never visited, and I had the rest of my life for the tour.

"Why?" she asked suddenly. "Why did you choose me over yourself? You didn't even know—if you'd kept your memories, you'd have known what you were giving up, and you'd have known it was worth it, but you didn't. You just... trusted the love without the evidence."

I thought about it. Honestly. Because she deserved an honest answer and I was apparently the kind of man who gave those now, having tried everything else.

"When the god asked," I said slowly, "I don't think I was deciding with my memories. I think the memories were already leaving. I think what was left was just—" I put my hand flat on my chest. "This. And this said: her. Whole. No matter what. And I've spent my whole life trusting oaths, sworn ones, carved ones, the kind you can hold in your hand. But the truest oath I ever swore, I swore without words, to a girl whose name I can't remember, and it held. It held through twenty years of silence and a war and a court under a mountain. So when the choice came, I figured the oath knew what it was doing better than I did."

Wren looked at me for a long time. The sun was nearly gone and the first stars were coming out over Thornfell, and somewhere below us the god's voice hummed in the stones, patient as bedrock.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" she said at last. "Or the best part? They're the same part."

"Hit me."

"I spent three years down there forgetting you on purpose-adjacent. The court thinned me out, drip by drip, and I hid what was left in a song, and by the end I remembered loving you without remembering *you*. Exactly what you have now. We're matching set, you and I. Two people who loved each other across a hole." She smiled, and it wobbled. "So when you look at me like that—like I'm a stranger you'd burn down a kingdom for—I'm not confused by it. I know exactly what that looks like from the inside."

* * *

Night settled in properly. The others drifted toward the fire—Tam first, herding his confused dead mule, then Odile cradling her bandaged hand, then Brannoc, waking mid-snore and joining the world with the grace of a man falling downstairs. Maren followed the sound of our voices and installed herself near us like a cat claiming a warm spot.

"So," Wren said, settling beside me with the firelight on her face. "You don't know anything about me."

"Nothing. I know I love you. I know your name as of about an hour ago. I know you have a scar on your jaw and a gap in your smile and you cry and laugh at the same time, which I suspect runs in the family."

"It does," she said solemnly. "Mother's side."

"Tell me something. Anything. Start anywhere."

She thought about it, head tipped, firelight moving in her eyes. Then she smiled—the gap-toothed smile, the one that undid me on schedule—and said:

"Well. Once upon a time there was a man who could read the oaths in old stones. And he had a daughter who used to stand on fences just to worry him..."

And she began. And I listened. And when she got a detail wrong—she had me taller, nobler, braver than any man has a right to be—I corrected her, gently, and she corrected me back, harder, and we argued by the fire while the mountain hummed and the stars wheeled and the freed choir sang quietly around us, songs with new verses in them now, songs that belonged to themselves.

I don't remember her childhood. I never will. But she was telling me stories, and I was making new ones, and somewhere in the dark below us a god who had gotten its voice back was listening to every word—and I could have sworn, in the stones under my hand, that it was laughing.
