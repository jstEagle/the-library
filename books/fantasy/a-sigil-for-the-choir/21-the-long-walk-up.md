## 21. The Long Walk Up

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it's been holding too long—slowly, finger by finger, with a certain offended reluctance.

I say that with authority because I spent three years inside it. You develop opinions about a place when it's been digesting your soul by spoonfuls. The under-court had a texture I will never fully describe to anyone who hasn't lived it: stone that listened, air that tasted faintly of ledger ink, silence so thick you could lean against it. When Brannoc's hymn cracked open the vault and ten thousand jars split like seed pods, all that stolen voice came rushing out at once, and for one impossible moment the whole mountain *sang*.

Then it stopped singing and started arguing with itself, which is what happens when a god gets back several centuries of its own speech in a single swallow. The Judges' law dissolved into noise—their authority had been built on stolen words, and stolen words, once returned, take their thefts personally. We ran. Running seemed wise.

Now we climb.

* * *

Day three above ground, if you count the half-day we spent lying in the scree field just outside the gate, breathing like people who'd forgotten how. Tam counted heads twice because he didn't trust himself not to have lost someone without noticing. He hadn't. We were eleven: the six of us who'd gone down, the five choir-souls who'd followed the sound of freedom out of the dark like moths who'd finally decided the candle was worth the risk.

Varga made eleven. She caught up to us on day two, alone, no colors, her hunter's coat scorched across one shoulder from whatever she'd cut down on her way out. She walked into our camp with her hands visible and said, "I have nowhere else to be," in the tone of a woman who has recently audited every place she could be and found them all rotten.

Sebastian looked at her a long time. She looked back.

"He's the captain," Tam whispered to me. "The one from the pass."

"I know who she is." I did, in the way you know a story you've been told but never seen performed. "He knows too."

They stared at each other for the better part of a minute, two people conducting an entire negotiation in eyebrow language. Then Sebastian jerked his chin at the fire, and Varga sat down, and that was that. Later I learned he remembered her—remembered Greyford, the criers, the healer dragged through the street—but memory is a strange currency down here in the world after the vault. He was spending what he had left carefully.

Me, I had everything back. Every year of my life reinstalled whole, including the ones I'd sold, including the ones I'd hidden inside a chorus verse about winter barley. The Judges had taken my memories thin as frost and I'd smuggled the important ones out in plain sight, humming them twice a week to a room full of half-empty people, and none of us had known what we were carrying.

My father carried nothing. That was the arithmetic of the vault: his memories of me were the fuel that broke my sigil, and the god offered to give them back, and he said no—or rather, he said *give her everything instead*, and the god, who takes oaths freely given, took him at his word.

So now I walk beside a man who loves me visibly, helplessly, and cannot pick my face out of a crowd of two.

It should be unbearable. Mostly it isn't. Mostly it's like this:

* * *

We make camp in a hollow below the tree line, and Odile builds a fire with the efficiency of a woman who has forged documents in worse conditions, and Tam's dead mule—which he insists on calling Sergeant Bramble, which nobody asked him to do—stands at the edge of the light doing the slow swaying thing dead things do when they're bored.

I sit next to my father. This is a choice I get to make now, and I intend to make it often, possibly to excess.

"You're staring," he says.

"I'm memorizing. It's different."

"Is it."

"It's much more flattering to you." I tuck my knees up. "Tell me something true about yourself. Anything. I'll tell you if it matches the records."

"The records," he repeats. "You mean the stories you've reconstructed about me from secondhand sources."

"I mean the fence in Lowmark who kept your letters for eight years and read them aloud to me like scripture. I mean Maren, who's known you since before I was born and has opinions. I mean the general archive of people who've met Sebastian Eriksen and survived the experience." I count on my fingers. "Current holdings: you're left-handed but trained yourself right-handed so opponents would misread your sword work. You can't cook anything except porridge and one fish dish involving too much butter. You hum when you're nervous, badly, and you don't know you do it. And you once won a bet against three caravan men by swearing you could tell a bridge's oath-stone was failing just from how the birds avoided it."

His mouth does something complicated. "That last one. How—"

"You wrote it to me. When I was nine. You signed it 'a friend of your father's,' which fooled absolutely no one, because the handwriting matched the birthday letter you also sent as 'a friend,' and because no other man alive uses that many semicolons."

"I use semicolons responsibly."

"You use semicolons like a man building a barricade out of punctuation." I'm grinning. I can't stop. Three years of rationed feeling and now it all comes out sideways as jokes; the healers among us—I suppose that's Brannoc now, in his lapsed-priest way—say that's normal. Grief and joy both need somewhere to go, and mine goes out through my mouth.

He's quiet a moment, watching the fire. Then: "The left-handed thing is wrong. I'm genuinely right-handed. Everything else—" A pause. "Everything else stands."

"Sergeant Bramble sways approvingly," I note.

"Don't encourage the mule."

"He's the only member of this company who never lied to anyone."

"He's also dead, Wren."

"And yet more honest than most living men. Your daughter notices these things."

Something flickers across his face when I say "your daughter"—not pain exactly. More like a man touching a scar to check whether it still hurts, and finding instead that it hums. He knows what he paid. The god told him the terms plainly; grace arrived with a receipt. But knowing a cost and feeling its shape are different trades, and he's still learning the weight of the empty pocket.

"Ask me something," he says suddenly. "Anything you want to know. About you, about—before. Whatever the letters didn't cover."

I consider this seriously, because it deserves seriousness. There are gaps in me—not sold gaps anymore, but ordinary human ones, the kind everyone has. I was nine when he went away for good. There are nine years of material in him that I experienced only from child-height.

"What was my mother's laugh like?"

He doesn't answer right away. When he does, his voice has gone careful, the way it does around anything precious. "Like water going over rocks. Quick, and then gone, and then you realized you'd been smiling the whole time."

I write that down. Literally—I keep a little book now, cheap paper, charcoal stub. Not because I fear losing things again. Because he gave up his version of me and I intend to build him a new one, accurate and generous, and a builder keeps plans.

* * *

Maren walks at the front of the column with her staff clicking, reading the mountain by sound the way other people read by sight. On the fourth morning she stops us all with a raised hand.

"Listen," she says, though we hear nothing.

Then we do.

It comes up through the boot-soles first—a hum in the stone, low and patient, like a hearth-fire deciding to be a choir. The god's voice, moving through the rock of Thornfell the way it must have moved before the Judges bottled it. Where it passes, things happen. An old waystone that Brannoc had pronounced dead beyond resurrection flickers with fresh sigil-light. A raven on a dead branch opens its beak and produces, unmistakably, one perfect note of a hymn no raven should know, then looks embarrassed.

"It's taking oaths again," Brannoc says. His voice cracks on the word oaths. He's been quietly coming apart and reassembling since the vault, a priest finding his faith alive where he'd buried it, and I think the reassembly is harder than the collapse. "Freely given. Listen—it's not commanding. It's *asking*."

The hum resolves, briefly, into something almost like words. Not quite. More like the shape of words felt through a floor. And then, clear as a bell over water:

*Who will swear?*

Nobody moves. This is, I want to be clear, a god asking for volunteers, which is not the sort of thing you rush into. Even Tam looks thoughtful, and Tam once animated a tax collector.

Brannoc kneels. Of course he does. He puts both palms flat on the stone like a man returning to a house he'd burned down himself, and says, "I swore to you once with rules attached. Conditions. Interpretations. I'd like to try again without those."

The hum deepens. The waystone flares. And Brannoc makes a sound I've only heard from the freed choir members in their first hours above ground—the sound of someone getting back a piece of themselves they'd stopped believing existed.

When he sits back up, he's crying without any apparent distress about it. "It says yes," he reports, thickly. "It says it never wanted the rules either. Those came later. From us."

One by one, the others go to the stone. Odile swears something short and self-protective—"I'll tell the truth when it matters, and no more often than that"—and the stone accepts it with what I'd swear was amusement. Tam swears to look after the dead in his care, "even the annoying ones," and Sergeant Bramble sways solemnly. Varga hesitates longest. In the end she swears only this: "I will not obey an unjust order again. Test me whenever you like."

The god takes that oath with a resonance that rattles pebbles downhill, and Varga flinches, then straightens, looking like a woman whose ledger has finally balanced.

My father goes last. He crouches at the stone for a long time, silent, and I watch him decide. When he speaks, it's quiet enough that only the stone and I catch it.

"I have nothing left to give that I haven't already given," he says. "Except this: I'll stay. Whatever she's building, whoever I am to her—I'll stay and find out."

The stone lights up gold.

The god says, in that floor-of-the-world voice, and I would know it anywhere because I spent three years being hollowed out to the sound of its absence:

*Welcome home, Sebastian Eriksen.*

He flinches at his own name spoken in that voice. I put my hand on his shoulder. He covers it with his own without looking, automatically, the way you reach for a railing in the dark.

* * *

The stories become our evening ritual somewhere around day five, and I maintain that I invented it for his benefit, though Odile claims she saw me enjoying myself and therefore disputes my authorship.

The format is simple. I tell him a story about Sebastian Eriksen—some episode from his past, sourced from letters, from Maren, from the fence's gossipy archive—and he adjudicates. True, false, or true-with-corrections. Points are awarded. Nobody has explained what the points are for. Tam is keeping score anyway.

"Item," I say, on the sixth night. "In the winter of your twenty-ninth year, you guided a wool merchant's caravan through Whitefall Pass during a storm so bad the crows were walking, and you did it blindfolded to prove a point to a rival guide."

"False," he says immediately. "Mostly false. There was a storm. There was a rival guide, a dreadful man named Corwin Hale who deserved humiliation. But I wasn't blindfolded—that was Hale's suggestion, meant to mock me, and I declined it, and instead I simply navigated correctly while he got lost twice and blamed his mule."

"That's less impressive."

"It's more impressive. Anyone can be lucky blindfolded. Navigating correctly while your rival curses a mule requires character." He pauses, prods the fire. "Also there was a bridge out. I read the oath-stone. Rerouted everyone. Hale followed the official map and lost his cargo in the gorge."

I stop mid-note in my book. "You read the stone. With the gift. In front of witnesses?"

"No." A beat. "No, I told them I'd heard ice in the river at the wrong pitch. Which was even true, incidentally. But I knew about the stone first."

"You've been hiding it that long."

"I've been hiding it longer than you've been alive." He says it lightly, but the lightness has that barricade-of-semicolons quality, load-bearing. "Thirty years of being competent instead of honest. You get good at it. You get so good at it that when they finally come to burn you for it anyway, you realize the hiding bought nothing but practice."

Around the fire, the company has gone quiet in the particular way of people listening while pretending to tend their boots. Varga is watching him with an expression I'm starting to recognize as her conscience doing calisthenics.

"But here's the thing," he continues, and now he turns to look at me directly, and I brace myself, because direct attention from this man hits like weather. "Here's the thing I keep turning over. The hiding worked exactly once. It kept me safe, and it cost me—" his hand makes a small helpless gesture that encompasses me, the fire, the whole last decade, "—it cost me the one thing I actually wanted to protect. So the new policy is honesty. Aggressive honesty. Possibly tactless quantities of it. You should be warned."

"Noted," I say. My throat is tight. I make a show of writing it down: *New policy. Aggressive honesty.* "For the record, item adjudicated: true-with-corrections, and the corrections improve the story. One point."

"Two," says Tam. "There was a mule."

"There is always a mule with you," Odile tells him.

* * *

On the eighth day we pass the ruins of a shrine—one of the god's old high places, smashed in some purge or another, its covenant stones toppled into a stream. The stream has spent years running over them, and here's the miracle, here's the thing I'll tell until I die: the stones aren't broken anymore. The water wore them smooth, but the sigils cut into them have filled back in with light, line by line, the way a wound closes. The god has been repairing itself all along, patiently, downstream of its own destruction, using nothing but runoff and time.

Brannoc stands in the stream reading the restored verses aloud, weeping freely, and this time nobody pretends not to notice.

I crouch at the bank and touch one of the stones. The hum travels up my arm and settles somewhere behind my ribs, in the exact spot where my soul-sigil used to sit, and the god says—not in words, in warmth—

*You hid them well, little singer. All those years. I heard every verse.*

"You were in there with us," I say out loud. "All that time. Bottled. And you heard the choir anyway."

*I heard everything. It is remarkable what a god can hear through its own stolen voice, when the singers love each other.* The warmth shifts, acquires something like humor. *Your corrupted verses were terrible, by the way. Musically. Subversively, they were excellent.*

"I had limited resources."

*Mm. And yet you built a rebellion out of a folk song about barley. I have ruled nations with less.* A pause, and then, gentler: *He is walking behind you. He is wondering whether to ask you something. Put the poor man out of his misery.*

I turn around. My father is indeed standing there wearing the expression of a man rehearsing a sentence he hasn't approved for delivery.

"She talks to you now?" he asks.

"When she feels like it. Gods are like cats. Or grandmothers."

"Ah." He shifts his weight. "I wanted to ask—when we get down. To the valley. To wherever we're going, the waystation, the—whatever we're building." He's doing the thing where he uses semicolons out loud, clauses stacking up like sandbags. "I don't know what I am to you. Legally. Structurally. The letters called me a friend of your father's, and apparently I fooled no one even then, and I—"

"Dad."

The word lands between us and stops him cold. I've used it maybe four times since the vault, carefully, like spending the last coins of something. His face does the humming-scar thing again.

"You're my father," I say. "That part isn't in dispute. It survived a soul-sale, a harvest, a heist, and your entire memory. It's the single most durable fact I own. What's still under construction is everything else—the daily architecture. Whether you like your porridge salted. Whether you snore. Whether you'll embarrass me by crying at festivals, which based on current evidence, yes."

"I don't cry at festivals."

"Brannoc cries at festivals and you cried harder than Brannoc at the shrine an hour ago. I have witnesses."

"That was a god, Wren. That's different. There's a theological exemption."

"Mm-hmm." I hook my arm through his, and we start walking again, and behind us the restored shrine hums contentedly, and ahead of us the trail bends down through pines toward a valley where the light is doing something golden and unremarkable and completely free. "Anyway. Yes. Come home. Build the daily architecture with me. It's mostly chores and arguments about music. You'll be terrible at it for a while and then you'll be fine."

"That might be the kindest thing anyone's ever promised me."

"It's the only thing I've ever promised you. First of many. I'm establishing a precedent."

He laughs—really laughs, surprised out of himself—and the sound goes up the mountainside and the god catches it and gives it back a half-second late, slightly amplified, unmistakably pleased with itself.

Below us, the valley waits. Above us, the mountain lets us go, all the way this time, finger by open finger, until it releases us entirely into the light.
