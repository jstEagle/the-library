## 22. A Sigil for the Choir

The waystation at Greyford Pass had been a ruin when we found it. Now it was a ruin with a roof, which in the Thornfells counts as luxury.

I stood in the yard that first morning of the thaw and looked at it the way a man looks at a debt he's decided to be glad about. The stone walls still bore scorch marks from the crown's hunters—the old owners had been gifted, a pair of sisters who kept a beacon-lamp burning for lost travelers, and the hunt had come for them two winters back. The sisters hadn't come back either. But the walls had held, and Brannoc said that mattered. *A building that remembers kindness keeps some of it in the mortar,* he told me, in the tone of a man who had spent thirty years being wrong about his god and intended to spend the rest of his life overcorrecting.

So we fixed the roof. Then the well. Then the stable, which Tam insisted on doing himself because, as he put it, "the last thing this place needs is me raising anything else." He was fourteen and had spent most of the winter teaching himself to sleep without dreaming of the dead things that answered him. Some nights it worked. We didn't ask on the nights it didn't. We just left the lamp lit outside his door, and he stopped mentioning that too, which was its own kind of thanks.

That was how the waystation worked, I came to learn. Nobody said much. Everybody paid attention.

* * *

The refugees started arriving with the mud.

First it was a family of three out of the lowlands—a tanner whose son had floated a dropped hammer back to his hand without touching it, and who'd run rather than register. Then an old woman who could make bread rise twice as fast as it should, which sounds like nothing until you've watched a witch-hunter decide it's something. Then a pair of brothers who could talk to crows, and a girl who dreamed true, and a man who swore up and down he had no gift at all and only wanted soup, and whom Odile took one look at and pronounced "a liar of no talent whatsoever," which made us all like him immediately.

By midsummer there were twenty-three of us. By harvest, forty. Varga drilled a watch rotation out of the ones who could fight and taught the rest to run in directions she'd chosen, which she claimed was the whole art of war. Maren, blind as ever, mapped the mountain paths by their echoes and could tell you who was coming up the road by the sound of their boots before they cleared the treeline. She was never once wrong. When I asked her how, she said, "The frightened walk like they're apologizing to the ground. You'd know if you listened to yourself."

"I don't apologize to the ground."

"You do. Constantly. It's very humble of you. It's also why you trip."

She wasn't wrong about that either. Maren rarely was, and she'd developed a way of saying so that made you grateful instead of annoyed, which I suspect is the actual gift she never registered with anyone.

* * *

The door was my project.

Not fixing it—it hung fine, after Odile forged a new set of hinges and Tam's dead mule (the same one from the pass; he'd grown fond of it, and it of him, in whatever way a dead mule is fond of anything) hauled the timber for the frame. No: the door needed a sigil.

Every door in the Thornfells used to carry one. Oath-stones, covenant-marks, the old script of the god who had gone silent—or been taken, which we now knew—and every one of those sigils was a chain dressed up as a blessing. *I swear. I am bound. I am owned by this promise.* I'd spent my whole life reading them, feeling the weight of them through my gift like cold iron through wet cloth. Even now, with the god's voice loose in the world again, humming in the stones, humming in *me*, the old marks made my teeth ache.

So I sat on the step one evening in late autumn with a chisel and a scrap of slate, and I tried to design a sigil that wasn't a chain.

It went badly for a long time. Every shape I drew wanted to close. A circle closes. A knot closes. An oath, by its nature, shuts a door behind you and swallows the key. I filled three slates with failures and threw them into the yard, where Wren collected them later and, I learned afterward, kept the worst one pinned above her bed because she liked "watching you be bad at things."

"You're thinking of it as a promise," she said, appearing beside me with two cups of cider and handing me one, the way she did, without asking whether I wanted it, because she'd somehow already decided I did. She was right. I always did.

"Aren't sigils promises?"

"Yours were. Yours were chains with nice lettering." She sat down on the step, tucked her boots under her, and looked at my fourth failed slate. "But you're not the same man who wrote those. Ask the door what it wants."

"Doors want things?"

"This one does. It's been burned. It's been empty for two years. It's been full of strangers for six months." She sipped her cider. "If I were that door, I wouldn't want another oath. I'd want to stop bracing."

I looked at the door for a while. It was good oak, scarred, recently mended, holding up a roof for people who had nowhere else. It wasn't bracing anymore. It was just... open, most hours, with a wooden latch worn smooth by forty different hands.

So I stopped drawing circles.

What I finally carved was a line that began at the threshold and ran outward, off the edge of the wood, unfinished—deliberately unfinished, trailing off the doorframe and onto the wall and stopping only because stone runs out. Around it I set small marks, not binding-words but naming-words: *warmth. water. lamp. leave or stay.* And at the start of the line, where an oath-sigil would name its owner, I cut nothing at all. Blank space. Room for whoever walked in to write themselves in.

An invitation. Not *you are bound* but *you are welcome*. Not *this door owns your word* but *this door would like to hear it, if you feel like giving it.*

When I finished, the god hummed. Not words—just that low warmth in the stones, the sound of a voice remembering it had one. Brannoc, who felt these things the way other men feel weather, came out onto the step, went very still, and then laughed until he had to sit down.

"What?" I said.

"It likes it." He wiped his eyes. "Thirty years I prayed for a sign, and the sign turns out to be *carpentry*."

"Welcome to faith," Maren called from inside. "It's mostly chores."

* * *

Wren added her verse a week later.

I came down at dawn and found her on a ladder with the smallest chisel, working along the unfinished line where it trailed across the doorframe. She'd cut words into the tail of it—not sigil-script but plain letters, the lowland alphabet, the kind any refugee could read:

*and if you go, go knowing the door remembers you kindly*

I stood there longer than was dignified.

"That ruins the symmetry," I said, when I could talk.

"Symmetry is for judges." She climbed down and looked at her work with her head tilted, the exact tilt she'd had since the day I met her again on the mountainside, though I couldn't have told you where I knew it from, because I couldn't have told you I knew it at all. "Besides, it's not finished. That's the point of yours, isn't it? It goes on. Someone should say what happens when people leave."

"And what happens?"

"The same thing that happens when they stay. They're still ours." She said it lightly, but her ears went pink, and she busied herself with the chisel-roll, and I let her, because I was learning her present, and lesson one was: *she deflects with jokes when she means something, and the pinker the ears, the truer the joke.*

Here is what I will tell you plainly, since I've earned the right to say hard things simply: I do not remember her childhood.

The god offered it back to me, that night in the vault. Asked me what I wanted remembered. And I asked it to give her everything instead—to pour every drop of what I'd fed into the breaking of her sigil back into her, whole, even if the price was that I'd stand in front of my own daughter and see a stranger. It was the easiest terrible choice I ever made. I'd do it again before the sentence finished. But easy doesn't mean painless, and there are mornings—I won't pretend otherwise—when I watch her laugh at Tam's mule impressions and think, *there is a version of me who knows exactly where she learned to laugh like that,* and the not-knowing sits in my chest like a stone I chose, which is somehow both worse and better than a stone I didn't.

But here is the other thing I'll tell you: memory is not the only way to know someone.

I know she takes her cider hot and her mornings cold. I know she hums when she works and stops humming when she's worried, so the whole waystation can gauge her mood by the silence, like a barometer. I know she taught the choir corrupted verses under the Judges' noses for months, which means she is brave in the specific way that looks like patience. I know she keeps the fence's old letters in a tin under her bed and reads them on solstice eve, and comes out after with red eyes and dares anyone to mention it. I know she corrects my stories—"it was the *left* bridge, Da, honestly"—with a possessiveness over my past that I've decided to find charming rather than alarming.

And I know that every night, before she goes up to bed, she touches the blank space at the start of the door-sigil. Just briefly. Two fingers, like a salute, or a question, or a thank-you. I've never asked which. Some things you don't ask. You just keep the lamp lit.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it does in the Thornfells, with a week of grey wind and then snow deep enough to swallow fences. The road closed. The waystation filled.

Forty became fifty-three. There was soup, which the untalented liar made, magnificently. There were lessons—Maren teaching echo-craft to anyone who'd hold still, Odile running a forgery class she insisted was "calligraphy, purely calligraphy, officer," Varga drilling children in running-in-chosen-directions to their enormous delight. Brannoc held services in the common room that were less services than arguments, which he maintained was the traditional format. The god attended all of them, insofar as a god attends anything: a warmth in the stones, a hum under the floor, and occasionally—if Brannoc is to be believed, and these days I mostly do—an actual opinion, delivered during the arguments, usually siding with whoever was losing.

Tam raised the mule once, in February, to show a frightened seven-year-old that dead things weren't automatically scary. The child ended up riding it around the yard shrieking with joy. Tam watched from the fence with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it for years in the mirror: the look of a man discovering that the thing he hated about himself might be the thing somebody needs. He slept better after that. Mostly.

And the hunt? The hunt was dying. Word came down the mountain in scraps, carried by crows and peddlers and one extremely smug crow-brother: registries burning in Greyford, ledgers seized, a tribunal in the capital calling the whole enterprise *irregular*. Varga read each report aloud at supper in her flat captain's voice, and each time, the room got a little lighter. She never smiled at the news. But she started sleeping without her boots on, which from Ilse Varga was practically fireworks.

"They'll come asking questions eventually," she warned us, one such night. "About all of you. About what happened under the mountain. Governments hate unanswered questions worse than they hate witches."

"Let them ask," said the girl who dreamed true, who was eleven and fearless in the way that makes adults nervous.

"They'll get answers," Wren said. "Just maybe not the ones they ordered."

Everyone laughed. I laughed too. And underneath the laughter I caught myself looking at her—at my daughter, my stranger, my daily lesson—and thinking that the Judges had understood one true thing before we broke them: that a soul is a story. They'd just believed stories could be owned. Sealed in jars. Filed and harvested and spent.

They were wrong. Stories can't be owned. They can only be told, and retold, and corrected—"it was the *left* bridge, Da"—and added to, verse by verse, hand by hand, by everyone who walks through a door that leaves room for their name.

* * *

On the first day of spring, I carved the last mark.

Not on the door—beside it, low, where a child could reach. A small square of blank slate, framed in oak, with a chisel hanging from a leather loop next to it. Anyone passing through could add a line. Their name, their thanks, their grievance, their joke. Odile signed first, obviously, with a signature so beautiful it was probably legally binding somewhere. Then Tam. Then Varga, after standing in front of it for a full minute like it might arrest her, wrote only: *V. — stayed.* Maren pressed her palm to it and asked me to carve for her, "since one of us should be accurate," and dictated a single line: *the blind woman heard the door before it existed.*

Wren went last, at dusk, with the snowmelt running loud off the eaves. She thought for a long time. Then she cut four short words beneath everything else, straightened up, and handed me the chisel without explanation.

I read it after she'd gone inside.

*the choir keeps singing.*

Below the mountain, ten thousand jars had split open, and the souls in them had gone home or here or wherever they chose, and the god's voice ran free in the stone of the world, taking oaths freely given and refusing all the others. Somewhere in that flood, I am told, was a song—a corrupted verse a half-souled girl hid inside a ritual hymn, small sabotages stacked into rebellion, rebellion stacked into rescue. I don't remember hearing it. I'm told I was busy.

But the waystation hears it. On quiet nights, when the fire's low and the snow ticks against the shutters, the stones hum it back—her melody, her mischief, the tune she stole from the Judges and gave to the dark. Brannoc says the god is learning it. Maren says the god has known it all along and is simply being polite. Wren says nothing at all, just touches the blank space by the door, two fingers, and goes up to bed.

Me, I've stopped needing to know which story is true. That's what the year taught me, if it taught me anything: belonging isn't a memory you recover or an oath you're bound by. It's a door you carve open, and a verse somebody adds, and the choosing of it, daily, in the cold, with your hands.

Tomorrow there'll be chores. The roof leaks at the north corner, the soup needs bones, and a peddler swears there's a family stranded at the pass who'll reach us by dark. I'll listen for their boots—apologizing to the ground, probably, the way mine do—and I'll put the lamp in the window, and I'll wait to learn them.

It's what the door is for.
