## 3. Wren at the Well

The road down from Thornfell was a debt of its own—four hours of switchbacks that took the morning and gave back nothing but a view of the valley Wren lived in, green and small as a coin at the bottom of a purse. Juniper made the descent twice a season, no more, because every visit had to be paid for in candle-hours afterward: Master Ellery counted his absences the way other men counted sins, and each one cost Juniper a week of copying penance, hunched over psalters until his wrist sang.

He went anyway. He always went.

By midday he could smell the village before he saw it—woodsmoke and wet loam and the particular sweetness of the cider press running autumn's first apples. Lowmarsh sat in its bowl of hills like something the mountains had coughed up and then decided to keep: forty roofs, one crooked bell tower, and the well at the center of everything, older than any record the temple kept, ringed with stones worn smooth by three hundred years of hands hauling rope.

Wren was at the well when he came down the last stretch of lane. She was drawing water, which was wrong already, because it was Tansy Miller's day for the well, and Wren never took another woman's turn—not his sister, who kept accounts the way their mother had, who remembered what everyone owed everyone down to the half-copper. But there she stood, rope in her hands, hauling with slow, even pulls like someone working in a dream, and she was so pale that for one bad heartbeat Juniper thought she was someone else entirely.

"Wren."

She turned. The paleness held, but the smile was hers, wide and crooked on the left side, and some knot behind his ribs loosened enough to let him breathe.

"Junie." She set the bucket down and crossed the lane and hugged him hard enough to creak. She smelled of tallow and dried lavender and underneath it something faintly mineral, like the air inside a cave. He told himself it was the well water. Wells were like that. "You're thin. The temple starves you."

"The temple feeds me exactly as much as I've earned, which is the temple's whole theology in a single meal." He held her at arm's length. Up close the pallor was worse—not the gray of sickness but a kind of translucence, as if she'd been drawn in good ink and then someone had come along with bread and lifted away the top layer of her. "Are you sleeping?"

"Sleeping fine." The smile flickered. "Eating fine. Working too much, same as you. You look terrible, by the way. Temple gray. They should put it on their crest."

They walked together toward her cottage at the village edge, past the smithy where Hobb raised a hand without stopping his hammering, past the shrine niche where a carved Aldermother watched the road with her stone patience. Wren talked about the harvest, about the Hendersons' new goat that ate a christening gown, about the price of salt—and twice, in the space of twenty minutes, she lost a word.

The first time it was small. She was saying the miller's boy had been *something*—promoted, apprenticed, blessed—and the word simply wasn't there. She stopped in the lane, mouth open, hand making a small grasping motion in the air, as though the word were a moth she could catch if she moved quickly enough.

"Apprenticed," Juniper offered.

"Apprenticed." Relief washed her face, and then embarrassment, quick and hot. "I'm tired. Harvest does this."

Harvest didn't do this. Harvest made people tired; it did not make them stand in lanes fishing for common words out of empty air. But he said nothing, because he had spent thirteen years learning the discipline of saying nothing, and silence had become the shape of his love.

The second time was worse. They were in her kitchen, and she was setting out bread and hard cheese and the plum preserves she made every year and sent up the mountain with him wrapped in oilcloth, and she said, "You'll want to see Mother's—" and stopped.

Her hands went still on the table. Not searching, this time. Just still, the way a clock goes still.

"Mother's what?" Juniper asked gently.

Wren looked at the table. Then at him. And for a moment—a long moment, long enough for the kettle to begin its low complaint on the fire—she looked at him the way you look at a stranger who has used your name correctly. Recognition without memory. Affection with nothing underneath it.

"Junie," she said slowly, "why are you here?"

"I came to see you. I come every season. I brought you cinnamon." He produced the twist of paper from his coat, because ritual was a rope and he needed to hold onto something. "And you were going to show me Mother's something. Her quilt? Her recipe book? Her pressing iron?"

Her face unclenched. Whatever had gone missing came swimming back, or something close enough to it wore its clothes. "Her recipe book. For the preserves. I keep forgetting I gave it to Marta Hale after the funeral, and every time I go to reach for it, it's—" She laughed, a little wildly. "It's like reaching into a pocket and finding a hole where the lining should be."

"You gave it away," Juniper said. "That's all. Things get given away. That doesn't mean anything's wrong."

But he was watching her hands now, and her hands were trembling, very slightly, the way water trembles before it decides which way to run.

* * *

He stayed through the afternoon because leaving would have meant admitting what he'd seen. He chopped wood for her, more than she needed, because chopping wood was an answer to dread the body understood even when the mind refused it. Wren sat on the step and shelled beans and talked, and mostly she talked normally, and twice more she didn't.

Once she called the dog—the neighbor's dog, visiting for scraps—by a name that belonged to no dog Juniper had ever heard of, some soft liquid name like *Sennet*, and when he asked she frowned and said, "Did I? I don't know where that came from." Once she paused in the middle of describing the church renovation and said, with perfect calm, "There's a word for what the priest is doing to the roof and I have known that word my whole life," and then she looked at her own hands as if they had betrayed her, and said, "Re-slating. Re-slating the roof. There."

"There," Juniper agreed, and swung the axe, and split the log clean through, and pretended his heart wasn't doing arithmetic.

Because here was the thing he could not say aloud, the thing he had carried since the archive, since the sealed page with the Whitlock name and the transaction mark dated thirteen years past, since Brother Cassian's boot had taught him what questions cost: *thirteen years ago*. Wren was fourteen. Thirteen years ago she had been an infant, and their parents had been alive, and something had been bought or sold or traded with a mark the ledgers used for soul-contracts, and now his sister was pale and translucent and losing words like coins through a torn pocket, and the two facts sat next to each other in his chest and refused, absolutely refused, to be strangers.

He had told himself, walking down the mountain, that he would not think about it. That the page might mean anything. That debts were debts and souls were a scribe's metaphor and Master Ellery would laugh his dry dust laugh and say, *Whitlock, you've been reading allegory into accounting again.* But Master Ellery hadn't seen Wren's face go smooth as a locked door. Master Ellery hadn't heard her call a dog *Sennet* in a voice full of longing, as if the name had been waiting in her mouth for years, belonging to something she couldn't see.

At dusk they walked back to the well together, because Wren insisted on drawing the evening water herself, and Juniper didn't argue because arguing would have required explaining, and there was a crowd at the well anyway—a knot of villagers around a figure sitting on the well's rim, and the crowd had the particular hush of people listening to something they don't quite believe but can't stop hearing.

The figure was a woman, old in the way of travelers, old the way rivers are old, with a coat patched in a dozen colors and a staff laid across her knees. A star-reader. Juniper knew the type from the passes—wandering sky-priests the temple tolerated the way you tolerate weather, reading omens off the night for copper coins. This one's eyes were milk-pale, clouded or blind, and yet she turned her head toward Wren before Wren made a sound, the way a compass turns north.

"—and I tell you," the star-reader was saying, her voice dry as chaff, "the charts don't lie. The stars are dimming. Not setting—dimming. Veshal, the Shepherd's Eye, burned blue-white when I was a girl. Now look at it." She gestured upward at the darkening east, where the first stars were pricking through. "Look at it and tell me what color it burns."

"White," someone said doubtfully.

"Gray-white. Gray-white with a brown edge, like paper going near a flame. I have read skies in nine kingdoms and I am telling you the sky is *thinning*." She rapped her staff on the well-stones. "Thinning like cloth wearing through. Like broth stretched too far. Something is drinking the light up there, and none of your priests will say what, because your priests count candles and not stars."

A nervous ripple of laughter. The miller's wife clutched her shawl. Someone said, "Old woman, you'll frighten the children," and the star-reader smiled with all the warmth of a knife left out overnight.

"Good," she said. "Frightened children live longer."

Juniper would have pulled Wren away—he was already touching her elbow—but the star-reader's blind head swiveled and fixed on them both, and her voice changed entirely, dropping its market-barker roughness into something quiet and precise and terrible.

"There," she said softly. "Come here, the pair of you. Come here."

"We're just passing," Juniper said.

"No one is ever just passing." She beckoned with two fingers. "Your sister first. Sister, give me your hand."

Every instinct Juniper owned—the smuggler's instinct, the debtor's instinct, the scribe's instinct for what happens to people who attract attention—screamed at him to walk away. Instead Wren stepped forward, obedient to something older than caution, and laid her hand in the star-reader's palm.

The old woman's fingers closed around it. Her clouded eyes widened. And then, very quietly, she began to shake.

"What?" Juniper said. His voice came out flat and strange. "What is it?"

"Cold," the star-reader whispered. "Her hand is cold as deep water. Child, child, when did you last feel warm? Truly warm, down in the bone?"

"I'm warm," Wren said, laughing uncertainly. "It's autumn. Everyone's cold in autumn."

"Everyone's cool in autumn." The star-reader turned Wren's hand over, tracing the lines of the palm with one cracked fingernail, and whatever she found there made her lips press white. "This isn't chill. This is *withdrawal*. Something in this girl is being drawn off, slow as tallow rendering, and what's left behind is cooling." Her blind gaze lifted to Juniper, and it was unbearable, being seen by eyes that couldn't see, being known by them anyway. "You feel it too. Don't you. You felt it the moment you saw her today."

The axe-work, the woodsmoke, the whole careful ordinary architecture of the afternoon collapsed in his chest. "She's been forgetting words," he heard himself say. "Losing—small things. Calling things by names that aren't theirs."

"Names go first." The star-reader released Wren's hand almost tenderly, the way you release something you're afraid of breaking. "Names are the thinnest part of us. The lightest. They go out of a person the way smoke goes out of a room—under the door, when you're not watching." She gathered her staff and stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and looked up—not at the few early stars, but higher, at the deepening violet where the rest of the sky's population would soon appear, and her face was the face of a woman counting a debt she already knew could not be paid.

"Thirteen years," she murmured. "Something has been feeding for thirteen years, patient as winter. And now the sky above this whole country is going hungry, and the hungry sky will start calling in its debts, and it will start with the ones already marked." Her head turned back toward them. Toward Wren. Toward him. "Go home, children. Bolt your doors. And you, boy—" the cracked fingernail rose and pointed at Juniper's chest, precisely, as if touching something written there that only she could read, "—whatever ledger you belong to, find your page in it. Before the ink finds you."

Then she shouldered her pack and walked out of the village westward, into the darkening, and the crowd broke apart around her wake like water around a prow, muttering, relieved, frightened, already halfway to deciding she was mad.

Juniper stood at the well with his sister's cold hand in his and did not move for a long time.

"Junie?" Wren said at last. "What did she mean, a ledger?"

"I don't know," he said, which was true, and also the most complete lie he had ever told anyone he loved.

* * *

He stayed the night because he could not make himself leave. He lay on the pallet by her hearth listening to her breathe in the next room, and somewhere past midnight he heard her sit up, and heard her voice drift through the wall, soft and wondering and aimed at no one:

"Sennet. Sennet, where are you?"

Calling the name into the dark like a prayer. Or like a summons. Or like a child calling for a dog that had never existed, in a house that had never been hers, in a life that was, word by word, degree by degree, quietly draining out of her while she slept.

Juniper got up and stood in her doorway and watched her turn back to her pillow, and above the little window, through the glass, he could see the sky. Veshal hung there over the eastern ridge, the Shepherd's Eye, the steadiest star in the northern heavens, the one every traveler in Thornfell navigated by.

It was burning gray-white. With a brown edge. Like paper going near a flame.

He packed his bag in the dark and was on the mountain road before dawn, climbing back toward Thornfell, toward the archives, toward the sealed page with his family's name on it—because the star-reader had told him to find his page in the ledger, and for the first time in thirteen years of careful invisibility, Juniper Whitlock intended to break into the one place in the world where asking questions got you broken.

Behind him, below, the valley slept on under a thinning sky, and somewhere in it, a girl dreamed of a name that was not hers, and reached for it, and could not hold on.
