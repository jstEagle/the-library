## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

The caravan moved by starlight, as it always did, and so when the stars went out, Juniper Whitlock was one of perhaps a dozen living souls positioned to see it happen.

He had been walking point on the Salt Road spur above Thornfell Pass, three lengths ahead of the mules, reading the mountain's dark shapes the way other men read scripture. He knew this route the way he knew his own hands: the leaning cairn at the second switchback, the place where the scree sang underfoot if you took the curve too wide, the hollow where wind pooled like cold water and carried sound from the valley below. He had guided smugglers through these passes for six years now, and in all that time he had learned one law above all others: the sky was honest. The stars did not lie about position, did not shift for bribes or fear. Whatever else in Juniper's borrowed life was uncertain—his debts, his name, the slow erosion of everything he might have been—the stars held still.

At the hour the temple bells would have rung compline, if there had been a temple near enough to hear, Juniper stopped walking.

He could not have said why, at first. There was no sound out of place, no smell of men or mules gone wrong. The night was ordinary: cold enough to bite through wool, dry, the wind moving east to west along the ridge. Behind him, Doss the carter muttered to his beasts, and old Sefa struck no light because lights were forbidden on the road, and somewhere below, the river made its endless argument with stone.

But the hair on Juniper's arms had risen, and the back of his neck felt the way it did before a storm broke—a pressure without weight, a listening.

Then he looked up, and understood.

The stars were going out.

Not all at once. That would have been terrible enough, but this was worse, because it happened in sequence, like candles being snuffed by an unseen hand moving across a vast and patient room. First the bright ones went—the Shepherd's Eye above the eastern peak, then the Three Sisters strung low over the southern ridge—and the darkness where they had been was not empty but *absent*, a hole in the fabric of the night that his eyes kept trying to fill and failing. Then the middle stars, the ones sailors and ward-keepers trusted, winking out in ragged rows, north to south, as though something enormous were drawing a shutter across the heavens.

"Doss," Juniper said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as if the darkness were already pressing on it. "Doss, stop the line."

"What in the nine hells—" Doss came up beside him, and Juniper heard the man's breath catch and hold. "Juniper. Juniper, what is that. What *is* that."

"I don't know."

It was the truth, and the truth was unbearable. Six years of careful competence, of being the man who knew every pass and every shadow, and here was a thing he did not know, hanging over all of them.

Sefa reached them last, her breathing thin from the climb. She was the oldest of the caravan, a woman who had run salt and stolen silk through three wars, and she looked up at the dying sky with an expression Juniper had never seen on her face before. It took him a moment to recognize it, because he had only ever seen it in mirrors on bad mornings.

It was recognition.

"No," she said softly. "No, no. Not again. Not *again*."

"Sefa—"

"My grandmother told stories." The old woman's hand found his sleeve and gripped it with surprising strength. "Stories from before the Concord, girl-stories, wet-nurse stories. She said the sky hadn't always been full. She said once, long ago, the stars went out over the mountains, all together, in a single night, and what walked down out of the passes afterward—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Nothing good. Nothing good came down, and nothing good stayed up."

Above them, the last stars went out—not the faint ones at the zenith, which had seemed safe in their smallness, but all of them, everywhere, in the same instant, as if the sequence had never mattered at all and the whole slow extinguishing had merely been a courtesy, a warning given to those who knew how to read it.

The darkness that followed was total.

Juniper had known dark before. Cave-dark, cellar-dark, the blind black of a new moon caught in fog. This was none of those. Those darknesses were *places*; this was a *thing*, thick and present, pressing against his open eyes until he raised a hand and could not see the hand. He heard Doss swearing, heard a mule scream and begin to pull against its tether, heard the whole fragile order of the caravan dissolving into animal panic around him.

"The wards," someone shouted from behind—it was Pellin, the young one, the one who talked too much on the road. "The border wards! Look at the wards!"

And Juniper turned south, toward the Thornfell line, and saw them die.

* * *

He had never seen the wards. No one had, not truly—they were old beyond memory, older than the temple, older than the Concord that named them, a lattice of quiet power laid over the passes like frost on glass. Ward-keepers tended them, or had once, in the days before the temple absorbed their order and reduced them to caretakers of stones nobody visited. Travelers crossed the border a thousand times a day and never felt a thing, which was precisely the point. A ward, Halvard had once told him—young Juniper, curious, asking why certain boundary-stones were dressed in ribbons—was like a heartbeat. You noticed it only when it stopped.

Juniper saw them now. In the last instant before they failed, the entire southern horizon lit with a web of pale silver lines, geometric, vast, beautiful in the way that a bone breaking through skin is beautiful—structure revealed by catastrophe. For half a heartbeat the mountains wore their hidden skeleton, every pass and ford and ancient standing stone connected in a single luminous net stretched from the western crags to the salt flats.

Then the net shattered.

There was no other word for it. The lines cracked like ice on a winter pond, and the cracks ran and branched and ran, and the sound reached them a full breath later—a sound Juniper felt in his teeth and the roots of his hair, a high crystalline shriek that rose and rose and then cut off into silence so complete that his ears rang with the absence. Shards of light, actual shards, visible fragments of something that should not have been breakable, rained down along the border and faded before they touched earth.

After that: nothing. Dark mountains. Dark sky. The mules screaming, the men shouting, Sefa's grip crushing his wrist.

"Off the road," Juniper heard himself say, and was distantly impressed by how steady his voice came out, because inside he was nothing but a small boy again, standing in the temple yard at seven years old while Brother Aldous explained the terms of his family's debt in words he almost understood. "Everyone off the road, into the rocks, now. If anything comes up the pass, we don't want to be silhouetted against the ridgeline."

"Against *what*?" Doss demanded wildly. "There's nothing to silhouette against! There's nothing left!"

"That's rather my point."

They got the mules off the road and into a fold of rock below the third switchback, and Juniper stood at the edge of the group with his heart hammering and did the thing he was best at, the thing the temple had trained into him alongside the copying of prayers: he counted what he knew, and set aside what he didn't.

What he knew: the wards had failed all at once, along their whole length, which meant it was not decay. Decay was local, gradual, the business of centuries. This was deliberate or catastrophic, and either way it had been coordinated across two hundred miles of border. What he knew: the stars had gone out in sequence, and sequences implied intention. What he knew: Sefa's grandmother's story, and the shape of the silence underneath it.

What he didn't know: why, in the middle of all that terror, some cold and certain part of him had whispered, *of course*, as if he had always known this was coming, as if he had spent thirteen years waiting for exactly this night without ever being told to wait.

Thirteen years. The number arrived unbidden and sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"Juniper." Sefa's voice, low, at his shoulder. "The temple will lock its gates tonight. You know that. Whatever's coming down those passes, the brothers will bar the doors and pray, and anyone outside stays outside."

"I know."

"You're outside."

"I know that too."

She was quiet a moment. Below them, far down the valley, a bell began to ring—Thornfell's alarm bell, the one reserved for invasion—and then another, and another, a chain of panic rolling through the lowland villages like fire through dry grass.

"Then go," she said. "Whatever you are to those brothers—debtor, scribe, spy, I've never asked and I'm not asking now—go and be it indoors. The rest of us will scatter the cargo and be gone by dawn. We're smugglers, boy. Darkness is our trade. But you—" Her old eyes, the only things still clearly visible in the starless night, fixed on him. "You've got a look on you like a man who's just remembered something he paid good coin to forget. And I've seen that look kill people who stood still."

* * *

He did not go to the temple.

He told himself, walking fast down the eastern trail with the mule-path crumbling under his boots, that he was going because the temple gates would take an hour to reach and the pass was closer, because the wards had broken *here*, in Thornfell, and whatever had broken them might have left evidence, and evidence was the currency of a man whose life depended on knowing things first. He told himself it was strategy.

It was a lie, and some part of him knew it even as his feet made the choice. The wards had broken here. And thirteen years ago, a page in a sealed ledger had recorded a transaction bearing his family's name, and he had been beaten for asking about it, and he had never stopped asking, not really, not in the secret ledger-room of his own heart where he kept the questions the head scribe's cane could not reach. The wards and the ledger and the number thirteen were threads of the same knot. He could feel it now, feel it pulling tight, and a man does not walk away from the knot his own life is tied in.

The pass was a wound of absolute dark. He navigated by memory and by touch, one hand on the rock wall, counting switchbacks, and twice he nearly walked off the path where the mountain fell away into a blackness so complete it seemed solid. The wind had died entirely, which was wrong—wind did not die in the passes, wind was the pass's permanent tenant—and the silence pressed against his eardrums like deep water.

He smelled blood before he found the man.

Iron and salt, sharp on the dead air, and beneath it another smell he knew from the archive rooms: burnt parchment, the scent of wards written on vellum and consumed. He turned the final bend, where the shrine-stone stood—the old boundary-marker with its faded ribbons, the one Halvard had shown him years ago—and the smell thickened, and he knelt, and his fingers found cloth, and the cloth found flesh.

"Halvard?"

A wet sound that might have been laughter. "The little scribe. Come to copy out my dying confession?"

"Keep talking. Talking means alive." Juniper's hands moved over the old man with the brisk efficiency of someone who had bandaged smugglers after ambushes and could not afford to shake. Ribs, certainly—two, maybe three, splintered. A gash along the scalp leaking warmth. And his hands—Juniper's breath caught. Halvard's hands were burned to the wrist, the skin split and weeping, the fingertips charred black as if he had plunged them into a furnace.

"The wards," Halvard rasped. "I was on the line. Checking the anchor-stones. They were *singing*, boy—do you understand? Thirteen years of silence and suddenly they were singing, all of them at once, a chord I hadn't heard since—" He coughed, and the cough cost him visibly. "Since the night your parents signed."

The world tilted. Juniper gripped the old man's shoulder to steady himself and felt the bones grind and forced himself not to flinch. "What do you mean, singing? Wards don't sing."

"They do when they're being *paid*." Halvard's hand—ruined, blistered—found Juniper's wrist and clamped it with shocking strength. "They do when the payment comes due. I fed those wards for forty years, boy. I know their appetite. And tonight they weren't hungry anymore. Tonight they were *satisfied*. Do you hear me? Something settled the account. Something paid in full, all at once, and the wards let go like a fist opening, and the sky—" His voice broke, and what came through the break was not pain but something far more frightening. It was grief. "The sky was collateral. All of it. Every star you see from Thornfell to the sea is anchored to the border lattice, has been for nine hundred years, and when the lattice fell—"

"The stars went with it."

"Now you're doing sums." The ruined mouth twisted. "You always were quick. Your mother was quick too. Quick enough to strike a bargain with the Undercourt to keep her children breathing, and clever enough never to tell either of you what she'd put up as surety."

Juniper could not breathe. The pass, the dark, the dead air—all of it receded, and he was kneeling in a void holding a dying man's wrist, and the number thirteen rang in him like a struck bell.

"Wren," he said. His sister's name came out of him like blood from a wound. "Halvard. What did she put up? What did she *sell*?"

Above them, in the starless black, something moved.

Not wind. Not falling stone. A presence, vast and unhurried, passing along the ridgeline where the wards had sung, and where it passed, Juniper felt the temperature drop and the darkness deepen, as though the night itself were being folded closed behind it, page by page, like a ledger whose accounts were finally, at long last, balanced.

Halvard's grip tightened to agony. "Boy," he whispered, "we need to be anywhere but here."

But Juniper did not move. He was looking up into the absolute dark, and the cold certainty that had whispered *of course* when the stars began to die was whispering again, clearer now, unmistakable:

*They've come to collect what your family owes.*

And the thirteenth moon was rising nowhere at all.
