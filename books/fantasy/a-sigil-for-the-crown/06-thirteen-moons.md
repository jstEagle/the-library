## 6. Thirteen Moons

The cave Halvard had chosen smelled of cold stone and old smoke, the way all high places in Thornfell smelled now that the sky had gone blind above them. Juniper sat with his back against the wall and watched the ward-keeper work at a fire no larger than a cupped hand—a fire fed not by wood but by something else, something that burned without crackling and gave off light the color of deep water.

"Emberstone," Halvard said without looking up. "Temple issue, once. Before they decided ward-keepers were more useful dead than equipped." He turned the stone in his fingers, and the little flame leaned toward Juniper like a curious animal. "You keep looking at it like it's going to bite you."

"Everything in this mountain bites," Juniper said. "I've learned to check first."

Halvard laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years—and then stopped laughing as though he'd remembered he had no right to it. He set the emberstone on a flat shelf of rock between them, and its light pooled there, and in that pool Juniper could see the old man's hands: scarred across every knuckle, the fingers of someone who had spent decades drawing lines in the air and binding them shut.

"You want to know what we're walking toward," Halvard said.

"I want to know if there's anything worth walking toward."

"There is." Halvard looked up, and in the emberlight his eyes were pale as frost on a windowpane. "But you're not going to like the shape of it."

* * *

Juniper had carried questions since the pass—since the night the stars went out like snuffed candles and the wards shattered into falling glass, and he had found this broken old man bleeding among the wreckage of everything Juniper had believed was permanent. But questions were dangerous things. He had spent thirteen years learning that. In the temple scriptorium, under Master Aldous's candle-counting eye, curiosity was a debt like any other, and debts compounded. So he had held his tongue through two days of climbing, through the frozen passes where nothing moved and even the smugglers' secret cairns seemed to have forgotten their own purpose, down into this cave below the tree line where the dark felt less like absence and more like weight.

Now he let one question out, carefully, the way you'd release a bird you weren't sure wouldn't turn on you.

"The wards," he said. "You said they were fed by souls."

"I said worse than that." Halvard drew a breath that seemed to cost him. "I said I helped build them."

The fire flickered. Somewhere deeper in the cave, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"Thornfell has always been a border," Halvard went on. "Not just between kingdoms—between what's above and what's beneath. The Undercourt is old, boy. Older than the temple, older than the crown, older than any name your family ever carried. And it has an appetite. For three hundred years the wards kept that appetite pointed downward. And do you know what the wards were made of?"

"Ink and prayer," Juniper said. It was what the temple taught. It was what he had copied ten thousand times in his careful, invisible hand.

"Ink and prayer and *payment*." Halvard's mouth twisted. "Every ward-stone in the border line sits on a contract. A soul, sold freely—or sold *convincingly*—to the Undercourt, held in trust, burning slow as fuel. That's what keeps the dark down there and the stars up here. Not faith. Arithmetic. The temple knows. The temple has always known. Why do you think they keep their ledgers so carefully?"

Juniper thought of the sealed page in the archive. The Whitlock name. The transaction mark dated thirteen years past. The beating Brother Cassian's predecessors-in-office had given him for asking about it—the head scribe's ring cutting his lip, the lesson delivered in the flat voice of a man reciting scripture: *some accounts are closed, novice, and reopening them is theft.*

"My family," Juniper said quietly.

"Your family." Halvard nodded slowly. "Your parents came to me thirteen years ago. You'd have been—what, five? Six?"

"Five."

"They had a sick child. A boy who wasn't going to see another winter. Fever in the blood, the healers called it. Nothing to be done." Halvard's voice went very level, the way voices go when a man is walking across ice and doesn't want to look down. "So they did what desperate people have always done at the border of the deep places. They went to the Undercourt's brokers. And they bought back your life."

The cave was very cold. Juniper found that he could feel each individual place where the stone touched him through his coat, as if his body had decided to inventory every sensation except the one spreading through his chest.

"And the price—"

"Was never yours. That's the cruelty of it. The bargain required a soul of equal weight from the same bloodline, but not the same person. They paid with the only other thing they had." Halvard met his eyes. "They paid with Wren. Held in trust. Protected—truly protected, the wards around her were strong ones, I wrote them myself—but held. And the sale would complete when the thirteenth new moon after the signing rose over Thornfell."

Juniper's hands had gone numb. He looked down at them and saw them flexing without his permission, opening and closing, as if trying to grasp something that wasn't there.

"Thirteen years," he said. "She's thirteen years old."

"She'll be fourteen the day the moon fails to rise."

* * *

He didn't remember standing up. He was at the cave mouth suddenly, staring out at a sky with nothing in it—not clouds, not darkness proper, but a *thinness*, a blankness, as if the whole dome of the world had been wiped clean while no one watched. His breath came out in white plumes and his heart was slamming against his ribs and somewhere very far away Halvard was saying his name—saying it gently, which somehow made it worse, because gentle was not a tone Juniper had earned or trusted in a long time.

"Sit down, boy."

"How long?" His voice came out scraped raw. "How long until—"

"Twelve turns. Twelve nights until the thirteenth new moon. Which would be bad enough if the wards still stood." Halvard's voice hardened. "But the wards are broken, and broken wards mean the Undercourt isn't waiting politely anymore. When the stars went out, that was the court calling in its paper. Every soul held in trust along the border is being drawn down early. Wren's among them. If she crosses the threshold before the moonrise—if she's pulled under before the term completes—then the sale seals wherever she stands, and no kin on earth can contest it."

Juniper turned. "Then we go now. Tonight. We go get her and we run—south, east, anywhere—"

"Run where? The sky is empty, boy. There is no *away* from a creditor who owns the ground beneath the ground." Halvard pushed himself upright with a grunt, one hand pressed to the wound in his side that Juniper had bound two days ago and that had already soaked through its dressing once. "There's exactly one way to stop a soul-sale, and it's written into every contract the court has ever signed, because the court is ancient but it is also *legal*, and legality is its prison as much as ours. A sale may be contested before completion by blood kin, at the Undercourt's own threshold, in open court."

"At the threshold," Juniper repeated. "Under the mountain."

"Under the mountain. Where they make the rules. Where every word you speak can be weighed and bought and turned against you." Halvard limped to the shelf and took up the emberstone again, and its light threw his shadow huge and crooked against the far wall. "And understand what contesting means. You don't walk in and ask nicely. You walk in and *bid*. The court will hear a counter-claim only if the claimant offers payment of equal weight to what's already contracted. Your parents offered a soul. What will you offer?"

Juniper opened his mouth and found nothing in it. He had nothing. He had a scribe's fine hand and a smuggler's knowledge of hidden roads and a body that had learned to be small and quiet and unremarkable, and none of it weighed anything against a soul.

"I don't know," he said, and hated how young it sounded.

"No," Halvard agreed. "Neither did I, the first time I stood at that threshold. But here's what I know now, and it's why I'm coming with you, half-dead as I am." He crossed the space between them and gripped Juniper's shoulder, and his hand was iron despite the ruin of him. "The court values strange things. Not just souls. Names. Regrets. Years unlived. Promises unkept. I have seen a man buy his daughter back with the memory of his own mother's face. I have seen a woman pay with her talent for joy. The ledger accepts whatever weighs true. So you'll go down there carrying everything you are, and you'll find out which part of you weighs the most."

* * *

They slept in shifts, though Juniper suspected neither of them truly slept. He lay watching the emberstone gutter and flare and thought of Wren at the well, three weeks ago now—an eternity ago—pale in the morning light, losing words mid-sentence, looking at him with those moments of blank incomprehension that he had told himself were nothing, were girlhood, were nothing at all. *Juniper, I—* and the sentence trailing off like thread slipping off a spindle. She had been forgetting herself already. The court had been drawing on her account for months, maybe years, small withdrawals no one thought to audit.

And his parents. Dead six years now—fever again, the same fever that had nearly taken him, as if the family blood carried both the sickness and the bargain. They had traded their daughter's soul to save their son, and they had never told him, and he had spent his whole borrowed life resenting the weight of a debt he couldn't name while Wren grew up bright and quick and slowly, imperceptibly, being emptied.

He should have seen it. He had copied ledgers for thirteen years; he knew better than anyone how numbers lie by telling the truth in the wrong order. The temple's charity that placed him in the scriptorium. The stipend that kept food in Wren's village kitchen. All of it interest payments. All of it the temple keeping its collateral comfortable.

*Some accounts are closed,* the head scribe had said, breaking his lip. Closed accounts. Sealed pages. And Brother Cassian hunting him through the foothills now, burning villages, because a living witness to the Whitlock page was a threat to three hundred years of careful arithmetic.

Hope arrived in him then, sitting alone in the dark with a dying man's fire—it arrived the way Halvard had perhaps intended, wrapped tight in a deadline, wrapped so tightly in the twelve remaining turns that he could hardly tell it apart from terror. But it was hope. Wren could be saved. There was a door, and doors had keys, and keys had prices, and he had spent his whole life learning to count costs.

Whatever the court wanted, he would pay it. Whatever part of him weighed most, he would put it on the scale himself.

* * *

They broke camp before what passed for dawn—a gray suggestion of light that the starless sky permitted grudgingly, like a merchant releasing coin a copper at a time. Halvard moved badly but moved, and Juniper learned to set the pace just fast enough to be merciful and just slow enough to be honest.

At the treeline, where the path bent south toward the lowlands and Wren, Halvard stopped.

"We don't go to her first," the old man said, before Juniper could even angle his body that direction. "I know what you're thinking. Get her, run her to the mountain, present her at the threshold safe. But the court's pull is on her now, boy, and growing. Every hour you spend near her, you spend inside the radius of that pull—and the enforcers know it too. Cassian will watch the village. He'll expect exactly that."

"So we abandon her?"

"We go *around* her. We go to the mountain, we learn the terms, we prepare the claim. And on the twelfth turn—" Halvard's frost-pale eyes held his. "On the twelfth turn, we bring her to the threshold ourselves, ahead of the pull, ahead of the hounds, with the counter-payment already weighed. It's the only road where she arrives free instead of delivered."

Juniper looked south, toward the lowlands, toward the well, toward the sister who might already be forgetting his face. Then north, toward the black mass of the mountain, whose roots went down further than any map admitted.

"Twelve turns," he said.

"Eleven and a half, if we waste the morning." Halvard started walking.

Juniper followed. Behind them the empty sky pressed down like a held breath, and ahead the mountain waited with its ledgers open, and somewhere in the arithmetic of souls and names and equal weights, a page was turning toward the thirteenth moon.

He only hoped that when he found what weighed the most in him, there would be enough left afterward to recognize himself.
