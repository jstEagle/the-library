## 7. The Temple's Hounds

Brother Cassian did not sleep. Sleep was a concession to the body, and the body was merely the inkwell of the soul — a vessel to be kept full and dark and ready for the brush of divine purpose. So while the foothill villages banked their fires and drew their shutters against the starless dark, he sat upright in the temple wagon with the ledgers open across his knees, reading by the light of a lamp that burned rendered tallow mixed with consecrated ash.

The pages before him were not scripture. They were better than scripture. Scripture told a man what to believe; these pages told him what had been *done* — every candle Juniper Whitlock had ever signed out of the scriptorium stores, every coin of his indenture repaid at three coppers over face value, every night unaccounted for in eleven years of meticulous temple bookkeeping. The gaps in the record were as legible to Cassian as the entries. A man who owed the Thornfell temple four hundred marks did not simply *vanish* on the night the wards fell unless he had somewhere to be.

And now the man had taken something. That was the part that made Cassian's hands tremble slightly as he turned the pages — not fear, never fear, but the fine vibration of a tuning fork struck against truth. The sealed folio from the archive of names was missing from its cradle. The lock had not been forced. It had been opened with a key, or with knowledge, and there were perhaps six living souls who knew where the archive kept its keys.

Cassian intended to reduce that number considerably.

"Brother." Deacon Ossler stood at the wagon's tail, a lantern swinging from one fist, his round face slick with the humidity that had crept into the foothills since the sky went dead. "The village ahead. Millbrook, they call it. The headman says no one's passed through."

"The headman is lying."

"You can't know that, Brother."

"I can." Cassian closed the ledger with a soft, deliberate sound, like a mouth closing on a secret. "Because lying is what people do when they have seen a fugitive and been paid in smuggler's silver to forget him. Because the millpond wheel turned all night — I heard it from the ridge — and a mill does not grind grain for a village that expects no guests. And because, Ossler—" he stepped down from the wagon, straightening his scribe's cassock, its hem stiff with dried wax, "—the dog is watching the north fence."

Ossler looked. A lean yellow dog stood at the corner of the nearest paddock, ears up, gaze fixed on the tree line above the village with the absolute attention of an animal that had recently been fed by someone it did not know.

"Being seen," Cassian said quietly, "is now lethal. For him. For anyone who sees him. For anyone who *helped* him see or be seen. That is the doctrine of the erasure, Ossler, and you would do well to stop flinching from it. The wards are down. The stars are gone. Do you imagine that happened because the heavens grew tired? Somewhere, a debt has come due, and the Whitlock name is written in the margin of that debt, and we are going to burn the margin clean."

He walked toward the village with his ledger under his arm like a breviary, and behind him, at his signal, two of the enforcer-scribes began unbundling the firepots.

* * *

Juniper learned about Millbrook from the smoke.

He saw it at dawn from a ledge half a mile up the smugglers' road — a gray feather rising off the valley floor, too thin for a hearth-fire, too steady for an accident. He stood very still among the pines, a sack of barley bread and hard cheese against his spine, and watched the feather thicken, and understood.

Millbrook had fed him. The miller's wife had pressed a heel of cheese into his hand and looked at his face — really looked, which no one had done in eleven years — and said, "You've got your mother's chin, Whitlock boy. God keep whoever's keeping you." She had not asked questions. She had simply seen him.

Now she was paying for the seeing.

He did not let himself think about her face in the smoke. He filed it away instead, in the same cold drawer where he kept the memory of the head scribe's boot against his ribs, the same drawer where he kept Wren's name, and Halvard's confession, and the arithmetic of twelve remaining turns until the thirteenth moon. The drawer did not make the grief smaller. It only made it portable.

"They'll work outward from the roads," Halvard rasped behind him. The old ward-keeper came up the trail leaning on a cut staff, his ruined hip dragging, his breath whistling through whatever the ward-collapse had left of his lungs. Maren — no, Maren wasn't with them yet; that came later, on the salt — Halvard alone, then, gray-faced and stubborn. "They'll assume you'll run for the lowlands. Open country. Where a man can be tracked by his own hunger."

"Then we don't go to the lowlands."

"We go *up*?" Halvard laughed, and the laugh cost him a coughing fit. "Boy, the high passes are dead ground now. No stars, no wards, no sun worth the name through this murk. You'd be navigating by—"

"By memory." Juniper shouldered the bread sack. "I ran these passes for eleven years with lanterns shuttered and temple patrols on the ridgelines. I know which goat trails hold a man's weight in fog and which ones drop you onto the scree. I know where the smugglers cached rope and where the springs run even in drought. The temple's hounds hunt by paper, Halvard — by ledgers and lists and the assumption that everyone travels the way the maps say. I spent my whole life learning how to be a gap in the map."

He heard the words leave his mouth and felt their strange, bitter weight. All those years of careful invisibility — the bowed head in the scriptorium, the unremarkable face, the debts calculated to the copper so that no one would ever look twice at him — he had thought of them as a cage. Now they were armor. Being unseen had been lethal in its own slow way; being *seen*, he understood now, was lethal much faster. But knowing how not to be seen — that was a craft, and he was, gods help him, a master of it.

Halvard studied him for a long moment. In the flat, starless light, the old man's eyes looked like water holes in slate.

"You hate them," Halvard said. Not the temple — he meant the hounds, the burning, the whole apparatus. "But you don't hate them blindly. You know exactly how they'll move. That's worse, isn't it? Knowing the machine because you were a gear in it."

"I was never a gear." Juniper started up the trail. "I was the oil. Come on. There's a shepherd's bothy above the treeline where the wind covers tracks by noon. We can be two ridges away before Cassian finishes interrogating the survivors."

"And the survivors?"

Juniper didn't answer. The silence answered for him, and Halvard, to his credit, let it lie.

They climbed. The world above the smoke was a country without a ceiling — no stars, no moon, only a low bruised-gray dome that pressed down like a lid on a pot. Juniper navigated the way he'd promised: by the lean of the trees against the prevailing wind, by the smell of water in a hollow, by counting his own paces and holding the count against the terrain in his head like a second set of eyes. Twice he stopped Halvard with a raised hand and took them off the trail entirely, cutting across bare granite where no footprint could live, rejoining the path a quarter mile on. Once, near midday, they heard bells below — the temple wagons, moving along the valley road with their escort of mounted scribes, the sound carrying strangely in the dead air — and they lay flat among the boulders for an hour while the bells passed, and Juniper memorized their heading, and adjusted his own.

It was during that hour, cheek against cold stone, that he allowed himself to open the drawer.

The miller's wife's hands, flour to the wrist. *You've got your mother's chin.* His mother, who had signed something in an archive before he was born. His mother, whose signature Lady Vess would surely show him if he lived long enough to stand in front of her. Everyone who touched the Whitlock name got burned — the miller's wife, the head scribe who beat him and was perhaps already regretting it, Halvard who had helped draft the wards and was coughing his life out on a mountainside, Wren most of all. The name itself was the contagion.

Which meant, some cold and growing part of him observed, that the fastest way to save everyone the name had touched might be to stop carrying the name at all.

He didn't understand the thought yet. He only felt it settle into him like a seed into winter soil, patient, waiting for its season.

"Bells are gone," Halvard whispered. "Move?"

"Move."

* * *

The interlude of Brother Cassian, recorded here as he himself would have wished it recorded: plainly, and without apology.

On the third day of the hunt, Cassian burned the village of Harrow's Bend, and it was there that he found the proof he had been praying for.

Not the man himself — the man was a shadow, a smuggler's ghost, and Cassian had expected nothing less. What he found was in the headman's house, beneath a loose floorboard: a scrap of temple vellum, torn from a ledger, bearing a column of figures in a hand Cassian knew intimately. He had supervised that hand for eleven years. He had praised its neatness. He had held it up to the novices as an example of disciplined penmanship.

Juniper Whitlock had copied out a page of accounts — why, Cassian could not yet say, but the page concerned temple disbursements to the ward-keepers' fund, and the ward-keepers' fund was a subject the archive of names kept locked — and had left the copy behind, deliberately, in a village he must have known the hounds would search.

A message. The fugitive was leaving messages.

"He wants to be followed," said Deacon Ossler, turning the scrap in fingers gone gray with ash. "Brother, this is bait. He knows we read everything. He's leading us somewhere."

"Yes," Cassian said.

Ossler waited. The village smoked around them. A woman knelt in the road with her arms around two children, and Cassian noted, with the detachment he brought to all acts of necessary correction, that she was not looking at the fires but at him, with an expression he had catalogued long ago and filed under *the hatred of the uncomprehending*. It was the same expression sinners gave the flood, the plague, the honest tax. It did not move him. Pity was a luxury of men who believed the world was supposed to be kind. Cassian had read the ledgers. He knew what the world actually was: a balance sheet, and the balance sheet was bleeding.

"You're certain, Brother? If it's a trap—"

"It is a trap," Cassian agreed. "And we will walk into it. That is what makes us different from him, Ossler. He hides in gaps and guesses. We walk in the open, in daylight, in the sight of God and the temple, and we take every trap set for us and spring it empty, because a trap only works on a man who fears what's inside it." He took the scrap of vellum and held it to the light, studying the figures, the small careful numerals, the way the penstrokes tightened at the ends of lines the way they always did when Juniper Whitlock was copying something he was not supposed to understand. "He thinks he is showing me a discrepancy in the ward-keepers' fund. He thinks the numbers will frighten me into doubt."

"And won't they?"

Cassian smiled. It was, by all accounts, a gentle smile; novices had once called it kind.

"No," he said. "Because I have read the sealed folios, Ossler. I have read the *whole* ledger. I know precisely what the ward-keepers' fund purchased, and I know what the Whitlock family sold to purchase it, and I know that the transaction mark on that contract is the same mark that appears in the margins of the oldest ward-plans in the deep archive. The boy has found one thread. I am holding the entire garment. Let him lead us. Every step he runs, he spends strength; every village that shelters him teaches the next village silence; and at the end of the thread is not his sister's salvation but ours. The Undercourt holds what it holds. The temple's duty is to ensure no surface hand ever reaches it. Whatever bargain his parents struck, it ends with him. Ash leaves no signature."

He gave the order to march, and the hounds went on.

* * *

They nearly died at the Stone Ladder.

It was a smuggler route Juniper had used a dozen times — a chimney of stacked granite where the mountain had cracked and leaned, climbable with rope and nerve, bypassing three miles of open switchback that any tracker could read like print. He went first, setting the line, testing each hold the way he'd tested a thousand holds in the dark with contraband on his back and temple lanterns sweeping the slopes below.

Halfway up, he found the anchor cut.

Not weathered. Cut — the old iron ring sheared clean, the rock around it scarred with fresh chisel work. Someone had come through ahead of them. Someone who knew the smuggler routes, who knew which routes *he* would use, who wanted the Stone Ladder to fail exactly at the height where failure meant the bottom of the couloir.

"Halvard." His voice came out very quiet. "Get off the wall. Slowly."

Below him, the old man froze, one hand on the rope. Above, the gray sky sat on the peaks like a lid. The wind moved through the crack with a sound almost like breathing.

"They're not following us," Juniper said. "They're *ahead* of us. Cassian isn't hunting our trail — he's reading it faster than we write it. He knows the smuggler routes because the temple bought a smuggler. Someone talked."

"Rennick," Halvard said grimly. "Or the Corvane brothers. Half the guild would sell the passes for absolution and a pardon."

Juniper clung to the cold stone and felt, for the first time since the stars went out, the true shape of the thing hunting him. It was not dogs and firepots. It was not even Cassian, with his terrible gentle smile. It was the fact that he had spent eleven years building a self designed to be overlooked — and the moment he became visible, every piece of that self became a weapon pointed back at him. His routes. His habits. His handwriting left in a village floorboard like a fool. Being seen was lethal, yes. But worse: being *known*. And the temple had known him longer than he had ever known himself.

He finished the climb on uncut anchors, ancient pitons rusted into seams, hands bleeding freely by the top. He hauled Halvard up the last stretch by main strength, the old man white-lipped and silent, and they lay together on the summit shelf gasping while somewhere below, faint as a memory, the temple bells changed direction.

Toward the lowlands. Toward Wren.

Juniper sat up so fast the world tilted.

"They're not trying to catch me anymore," he breathed. "That's why the trap at the Ladder — slow us down, bleed us, buy a day. They don't need to catch me on the mountain. They need to reach the lowland villages first. Wren. Halvard, they'll take Wren, and they'll call it protective custody, and by the time I get down there she'll be behind temple walls where no counter-claim at any court can ever reach her—"

"Then you can't go down." Halvard pushed himself upright, wincing. "If you turn back, you lose the moons. Twelve turns, boy. The threshold doesn't wait, and neither does the sale."

"Then I send word." Juniper was already digging in his pack, pulling out the stub of graphite and the folded scraps of vellum he'd hoarded like a miser. His hands shook, and he hated them, and he made them work anyway. "There's a woman in Coldharrow, a dyer, who owes the guild a favor she can never repay publicly. She can reach Wren's village in two days if she rides. Warn her. Move her. Get her off the temple's map."

"And after that?"

"After that—" Juniper looked east, toward the salt flats he had crossed a hundred times by starlight that no longer existed, toward the mountain whose roots held his sister's soul in a lantern. The dead sky gave nothing back. No compass. No sign. Only the memory in his skull and the twelve turns ticking down like water through a cracked jar.

"After that we cross the salt," he said, "and I navigate by the only stars I have left."

He wrote the warning fast, in a cipher the guild had taught him and the temple had never cracked, and tucked it into the lining of his coat against his ribs, where the beating scars were, where the debt lived, where everything that mattered had always been carried.

Behind them, far below, a plume of smoke rose from the valley where the Stone Ladder's foot met the road — the hounds, finding the trap sprung empty, burning the nearest shelter on principle.

Juniper watched it for exactly as long as it took to memorize the direction, and then he turned his back on it, and walked into the white.
