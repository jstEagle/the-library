## 8. The Salt Road

The salt flats began where the foothills gave up pretending to be anything but a wound.

Juniper felt the change before he saw it—the ground going hard and pale beneath his boots, the scrub pines thinning to skeletons, the air taking on a mineral bite that tasted of old tears. Behind them, the last of the sheltering hills folded away like a hand withdrawing its protection. Ahead, nothing. A white plain stretching to the base of the dark mountains, cracked into hexagonal plates like the shell of some enormous dead thing.

And above it all, the sky.

He had stopped looking up two days ago, because every time he did, his body betrayed him with the same lurching expectation: *stars should be there.* The eye reached for them out of habit older than language, found only a smooth black ceiling like the inside of a closed lid, and something in his chest twisted. He'd learned to keep his gaze on the horizon instead, on Halvard's broad back plodding ahead of him, on the grey smear of dawn that never quite resolved into morning.

"No stars," Halvard said now, without turning. "No sun worth naming. No moon since the wards fell." The old ward-keeper's voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "You know what that means for crossing this?"

"That we're lost?"

"That we're *blind.*" Halvard spat into the salt. "Every road ever cut through these flats was cut by sight. Travelers steered by the Sister Peaks—" he lifted a scarred hand toward the north, where two jagged silhouettes should have been visible even in full dark, "—or by the stars, or by the moon on the brine pools. Take away the sky and the flats become a maze with no walls. Men have walked in circles here until their legs gave out. The salt keeps what dies on it. You can find them still, standing upright, preserved like saints."

"Cheerful," Juniper said.

"I'm not paid to be cheerful. I'm not paid at all anymore, if you'll recall." But there was no real bitterness in it. Since the pass, since Juniper had hauled the half-dead ward-keeper out of the rockfall and stitched his scalp with smuggler's gut-thread, something had shifted between them—not trust, exactly, but the grudging arithmetic of men who owed each other their lives and hadn't finished counting.

Halvard stopped at the edge of the first white plate and crouched, pressing his palm flat against the crust. He stayed that way a long moment, head bowed, like a man listening to a confession through a wall.

"Brine's close under here," he murmured. "Feel it? The whole flat is a skin over deep water. Step wrong, put your weight on a hollow plate, and you go through into slurry that pulls like a drowning woman's arms." He looked back at Juniper, and in the grey half-light his ruined face—burn-scarred on one side from whatever had happened when the wards shattered—looked almost gentle. "So. Master guide. Your roads got us past Cassian's pickets at Harrow Ford. Got us through the ash villages without stepping on a single pyre-stake. Can your memory carry us across sixty miles of nothing?"

Juniper crouched beside him and laid his own hand on the salt. Cold. Hard as fired clay. And beneath the hardness, faint as a heartbeat, a shifting wet sound.

*You learned the passes at night,* his old master Vetch used to say, rapping Juniper's knuckles with a ledger-rule whenever the boy misremembered a switchback. *Because night is when the honest world sleeps and the true work happens. A guide who needs light is a guide who's already lost.*

He had spent seven years memorizing Thornfell's veins of shadow. Smuggler routes were passed mouth to mouth, never written—Brother Cassian's enforcer-scribes burned written route-maps along with the villages that kept them—and so they lived in the minds of guides like scripture lived in the minds of heretics: recited, tested, punished into permanence. Juniper could walk the Salt Road in his sleep. He had, once, actually, as an apprentice, drugged on poppy-wine by a caravan master who wanted to see if the boy's knowledge would hold without his wits. It had held.

But that had been under a living sky.

"The road runs southwest by south," he said slowly, feeling his way through the memory like a man counting coins in the dark. "There are markers, but they won't help us—cairns of black stone, and in this light everything's black. So we steer by the ground instead. The plates crack wider as you go south; the brine rises; the footing gets worse. We want to follow the seam where the crust stays firm longest—that's the old riverbed underneath, running down from the mountains. Where the plates ring hollow, we swing east. Where they ring solid, we hold our line." He paused. "It'll be slow. And I'll be wrong sometimes. Maybe often."

Halvard grunted. "Wrong and moving beats right and frozen. Lead on, scribe."

"I'm not a scribe anymore."

"You're whatever keeps us alive. Lead on."

* * *

They walked.

There was no other word for it, no romance in it, none of the glory the temple hymns assigned to pilgrimage. They walked through a darkness that was neither day nor night, across a plain that reflected nothing, in silence broken only by the tap-tap-tap of Halvard's staff testing each plate before he trusted it, and the crunch-crunch of Juniper's boots following the line the staff approved.

Hours passed, or what they decided to call hours, marking time by meals eaten standing up and by the small stubborn rituals Juniper imposed on himself. Every thousand steps, he recited the route backward from memory, end to beginning, the way Vetch had drilled him. Every rest, he scratched a small mark into his forearm with a pin—a smuggler's tally, one for each leg of the crossing—because a man who couldn't see the sky needed *some* ledger, even if it was written on his own skin.

Somewhere in the middle distance of that endless white, his mind began doing what minds do in desolation: it wandered. To Wren, pale at the well, forgetting the word for *bucket*, her eyes going glassy when he said their mother's name. To the sealed page in the archive, the Whitlock name inked in a brown-black script that seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at it. To Brother Cassian's voice, carrying across a burning village three nights ago: *"The debt must be balanced, Juniper Whitlock. Balance is holy."*

"They'll follow us onto the flats," he said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice. "Cassian. He won't stop at the shoreline."

"He'll follow," Halvard agreed. "But the flats will slow him. Zealots make poor travelers—they think suffering is a sign they're on the correct path, so they never take the easy road even when it's offered." The old man's staff rang against a hollow plate, and they swung east, forty paces, before the ground firmed again. "Besides. He's hunting a name. And out here—" Halvard gestured at the blind white immensity around them, "—names don't weigh much."

Juniper thought about that more than he wanted to. About how much of him was name. *Juniper Whitlock, debtor, second-copyist of the Third Scriptorium, son of Aldous and Maren-of-the-lowlands—*

He stumbled.

Not on the ground. On the thought. Because for half a heartbeat, reaching for his mother's name, he had come up with *Maren* and then a blank, a smooth white nothing like the flats themselves, and terror had gone through him like cold water through a cracked plate.

Then it came back. Maren. His mother had been Maren. Of course she had. He shook himself, laughed shakily.

"Tired," he muttered. "Seeing things."

Halvard gave him a long look. "The flats do that. Eat at the edges of a man. Keep talking. Names, places, prayers, dirty songs, I don't care. Just keep the words coming. A mind that goes quiet out here doesn't always come back loud."

So Juniper talked. He told Halvard about the scriptorium, about Master Ellery-voth who counted candles the way other men counted sins. He told him about Wren learning to whistle through a blade of grass, about the taste of well-water in high summer, about the smell of the temple incense that he'd hated and now, absurdly, missed. And Halvard listened, and occasionally answered, and the white miles went by beneath them like pages turning in a book neither of them had chosen to read.

* * *

It was near the end of the third walking-shift—Juniper knew because his tally-pin had left six fresh marks on his arm—that he heard the sound.

At first he took it for the flats themselves: the wind worrying at the crust, the deep brine shifting below. But wind didn't cry. Wind didn't repeat itself, thin and cracked, in a rhythm too much like words.

"Halvard."

"I hear it." The old man had gone very still, head cocked, and for a moment the burn-scarred side of his face looked like something carved. "East. Half a mile, maybe less. Small voice." His jaw tightened. "Could be bait."

"Bait for what? Who'd know we—"

"Cassian knows you were a guide. He knows guides can't pass up a cry for help—it's bred into the trade. Or it's the flats themselves. They've been known to sing with dead men's voices, lure the grieving off the firm ground." Halvard's grip shifted on his staff. "Or it's a child."

The cry came again. Higher. Angrier, almost—as if whoever made it had passed beyond fear into fury at being abandoned.

"It's a child," Juniper said.

"It might be a trap."

"It's a *child*, Halvard."

For a moment the old ward-keeper just looked at him, and something moved behind his eyes—something old and guilty, the look of a man who had helped build the machine that ate souls and had been running from the sound of it ever since. Then he sighed, a sound like a landslide deciding against itself.

"Firm ground holds toward the sound for about two hundred paces, then it goes soft. Stay behind my staff. If I say run, you run and don't argue."

They went east, fast now, Juniper calling out at intervals—"We hear you! Keep crying! Keep crying so we can find you!"—and the voice answering, thinner each time, until they crested a low rise of crusted salt and saw it.

A hollow. A place where a brine pool had drained and left a bowl maybe thirty feet across, its sides slick and steep, and at the bottom, half-buried in slurry to the waist, a girl.

She was small—eight years old, nine at most—with dark hair stiff with salt, standing out from her head in wild spikes. Her clothes had once been good: a wool coat with silver buttons, the kind lowland merchants' children wore. She had stopped crying the moment she saw them and was now regarding them with an expression of profound, offended suspicion, arms crossed over the slurry, as though *she* had discovered *them* stranded and was considering whether rescue was worth the indignity.

"Well?" she demanded. Her voice was hoarse as a crow's. "Are you going to stand there all day?"

Juniper blinked. "Are you—are you alone? Where are your parents?"

"Where are *yours*?" she shot back. Then, quickly, before the question could land anywhere: "The caravan went on. They said the crossing was safe. They said a child slows everyone down." Her chin lifted. "I'm not slow. The ground broke."

Halvard was already at the rim, driving his staff down into the crust at the hollow's edge, testing, circling, muttering measurements under his breath like a man pricing lumber. "Crust is rotten all around," he called back. "Can't come at her straight. Scribe—there's a coil in my pack. Rope, and the iron pin. Move."

Juniper moved. While he dug for the rope, Halvard talked the girl through it, his gravel voice going strangely gentle. "What's your name, little one?"

A pause. A long one. When she answered, the offense had drained out of her voice, leaving something flatter underneath. "Maren."

The name went through Juniper like a hook. *His mother's name.* The one that had slipped away from him hours ago and come back. He nearly dropped the coil.

"Maren," Halvard repeated, unbothered, anchoring the pin. "Good strong name. Now listen, Maren. We're going to throw you the rope. You wrap it twice around your wrists—twice, not once, the salt's slick—and you lean back, and you kick at the slurry like you're angry at it. Are you angry at it?"

"Yes."

"Good. Angry kicks float better than scared ones. Ready?"

She was ready. She was, it turned out, ferociously ready. The moment the rope hit her hands she wrapped it with quick, practiced competence that no eight-year-old should have owned, and when Halvard and Juniper hauled, she kicked and clawed and swore—actual curses, filthy ones, delivered in a piping voice that made Halvard bark a laugh despite everything—and came up out of the slurry like a cork from deep water, streaming grey mud, furious, alive.

She stood on the firm crust shaking herself like a dog, and Juniper saw her clearly for the first time in the flat grey light. Small face, sharp-chinned, salt-crusted. Eyes a strange pale color, almost silver, that seemed to catch light the flat world refused to give. And on the back of one grimy hand, briefly visible before she tugged her sleeve down over it, a mark: a small dark sigil, round as a coin, that Juniper's guide-trained eye catalogued before his mind could decide what it meant.

He decided, for now, to say nothing.

"You're bleeding," Maren informed him, pointing at his forearm where the tally-pins had raised welts. "That's stupid."

"It's a ledger," Juniper said.

"A ledger of *what?*"

"Of steps between me and my sister."

Maren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Ledgers are traps," she said finally. "My grandmother said so. Everything written down can be collected later." Then she held out her hand, imperious. "I'm coming with you."

Halvard snorted. "You're going to the nearest village, little one, as soon as we—"

"There aren't any villages. Not for sixty miles. You said so yourselves—I heard you from the hollow, sound carries on the flats." She planted her feet. "And the caravan that left me is heading the way you came, which means the men who burned the smoking village are behind me somewhere, and I'm not going back. I'm coming with you." Her silver eyes fixed on Juniper with unsettling directness. "You're going to the mountain. Everyone who walks this road is going to the mountain. I want to see it."

"How could you possibly know—"

"I know things," Maren said simply, and pulled her sleeve down further over the sigil, and said nothing else.

* * *

They argued about it for a mile. Halvard wanted to turn back toward the shoreline on the chance of finding her people; Maren dismantled this plan with a series of flat, factual observations about distances and pursuit that suggested a mind far older than her coat. Juniper caught himself watching her walk—tireless, sure-footed, avoiding soft ground as if she could *hear* it—and catching himself wondering, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the flats, what manner of thing could leave a mark like that on a child's hand, and what manner of parents named her Maren and let caravans leave her behind.

But when she stumbled, once, near the end of the shift—just once, exhaustion breaking through the armor for a single step—he caught her arm without thinking, and she leaned into him for exactly three seconds, no more, with her salt-stiff hair against his ribs, before pulling away and pretending it hadn't happened.

"We keep her," Juniper said quietly to Halvard, in the pause while the girl walked ahead, out of earshot. "Whatever she is. Whatever she's carrying. We keep her."

Halvard studied him. "You realize what you sound like? A man collecting obligations on the road to pay a debt. That's how the Undercourt thinks, scribe. Everything gathered is weighed."

"Then let it be weighed." Juniper looked at the small figure trudging ahead of them through the blind white world, upright and furious and alive. "I've spent my whole life being careful, being invisible, being *owed*. She's the first thing on this entire road I chose." He flexed his marked forearm. "Let the ledger show it."

Behind them, far back along the pale immensity of the flats, something glinted. Once. Twice. Like lamplight on a scribe's blade, or the polished tips of a hundred pens marching in formation.

Halvard saw it too. He didn't speak. He only lengthened his stride, set his staff ringing against the firm plates, and said, in a voice pitched for Juniper alone:

"Walk faster, scribe. The hounds have found the scent—and the mountain is still three days away."
