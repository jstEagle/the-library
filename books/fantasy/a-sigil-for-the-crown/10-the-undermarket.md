## 10. The Undermarket

The Gatekeeper's toll had taken the well from him.

Not all of it—Juniper still knew that water existed, still knew a girl named Wren had once knelt beside it—but the texture was gone. He could not remember how the cold of the rope had bitten his palms in winter, or the particular green-black smell of the water in August, or the sound Wren made when she dropped a coin in and listened for it to land. Those things had been paid out like coins from his own purse, and now there was only a smooth blankness where they had been, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to live.

He kept reaching for them anyway. That was the cruelest part.

"Stop doing that," Halvard murmured at his shoulder as they passed beneath the last arch of the mountain's throat. The old ward-keeper walked with a staff he'd cut from a dead pine on the surface, and it clicked against stone that was not entirely stone—softer, almost, as if the mountain had flesh somewhere under its skin. "Reaching for what you sold. The Gatekeeper counts every attempt. Every reach is interest."

"Interest on what? I paid."

"You paid with memory. The mountain keeps the change." Halvard's face was gray in the light of lamps that burned without flame—pale globes of something like moonglass, strung along the cavern walls on chains of braided hair. "Down here, nothing is ever fully paid. You'll learn."

Maren walked between them, small hand hooked into the strap of Juniper's pack, and said nothing. She had been quiet since the threshold, quieter than a child should be, watching everything with eyes that reflected the lamp-glow back too cleanly, like polished river stones. Twice Juniper had caught her mouthing words to herself—not prayers. Prices.

Then the passage opened, and Juniper forgot to be afraid, because wonder came first.

* * *

The Undermarket filled a cavern so vast that its ceiling was a rumor. Lamps hung in tiers descending into blue distance, thousands of them, and beneath their light sprawled a city of stalls and tents and terraced shops built from materials Juniper's scribe-trained eye could not classify: walls of pressed shadow, counters of fossilized song, awnings stitched from what looked like shed skin but shimmered when it moved. And the crowd—the crowd was worse than wonderful. Figures robed and hooded, figures with too many joints, figures that were mostly voice, vendors whose faces were mirrors showing each customer someone they had failed to become.

And everywhere, the trade.

Juniper heard it before he understood it. A woman at a stall of tiny glass vials crying softly while she drank one down—"my grief for my son, I can't carry it, I *can't*"—and the vendor weighing her tears on a scale of black iron before handing her a slip of paper that would let her sleep. A man selling his left-handedness to a fiddler with ruined fingers, shaking hands afterward and flexing his right hand as if greeting a stranger. Children—no, not children, Juniper realized with a lurch; they were *unlived years*, given temporary shape so they could be browsed—a whole pen of them, laughing and playing games no one had ever gotten to play, waiting for a buyer rich enough to purchase a decade that had never happened.

"It's a market," Maren said, and her voice had gone flat and old. "Everything's a market if you're hungry enough."

Halvard pulled his hood lower. "Keep your hands in your pockets. Keep your name behind your teeth. Down here a name spoken aloud is an invitation, and an invitation is a contract, and a contract is a chain. Say nothing. Buy nothing. Touch nothing."

"And Wren?" Juniper kept his voice low, though the market's roar could have swallowed a shout. "Her soul is here somewhere. Sold thirteen years ago. There has to be a record, a registry, a—"

"There is." Halvard's jaw worked. "The Archive of Sold Things. But no one walks into the Archive, boy. Not without an audience first. Debts this old, this large—they're held by someone. Someone who will want to look at you before you're allowed to look at anything."

"Who?"

Halvard didn't answer. He was staring across the market at a tower of white stone that rose from the cavern floor like a splinter driven upward through the world's palm, and at the banner hanging from it: a silver circle, open at the bottom, like a purse emptied forever.

Juniper had seen that sigil before. It was stamped on the sealed page in the temple ledger, the page that had cost him three ribs and a week of silence. The transaction mark.

"Lady Vess," he said.

"Creditor-in-chief," Halvard confirmed grimly. "The Undercourt lends. Vess collects. If your parents' bargain touched this place—and it did, or your sister wouldn't be fading up there like breath off glass—then it touched her ledger. Which means we need an audience. Which means we need standing. Which means—"

"Which means we need money," Maren said, "and we don't have any."

"We have stories," Juniper said. "The Gatekeeper took mine, but apparently that's currency down here."

Halvard gave him a long look. "Stories are small coin. Enough for bread. Not enough for an audience with Vess." He turned, scanning the market tiers, and Juniper watched the old man's face do arithmetic with ruin. "We find the cheap quarter first. Learn the layout. And Juniper—"

"I know. Invisible."

"No." Halvard's hand closed on his shoulder, hard. "Invisible is what got your sister sold. Down here, invisible just means unguarded. Stay *seen*. Stay next to me. And whatever you see being sold—whatever you see anyone buying—do not want it. Wanting is how they hook you."

* * *

They descended through the market's middle tiers, and Juniper learned what survival cost.

It was not paid in coin, though coin changed hands too—iron pennies stamped with moons, and Juniper counted at least four different currencies in the first hundred yards. But the real economy ran on the intangible, and the prices were written in ways that made his scribe's soul recoil and his scribe's eye keep reading anyway.

A stall of bottled regrets, arranged by vintage. *Cheap ones*—the regret of an unkind word, a door not held—went for a few coppers. In a locked case at the back sat a single dark vial labeled in elegant script: *the regret of a father who chose the wrong child.* The price tag was not a number. It was a name, and a date, and the words *payable upon death.*

A memory-broker who dealt in firsts: first snows, first kisses, first lies. His display case held a jar of first steps, dozens of them, toddling faintly against the glass.

A fortune-teller whose cards were unlived years laid face-down, and Juniper watched a hollow-eyed miner turn over *the year you spent at sea* and begin to weep with joy at a life he'd never had, even as the fortune-teller quietly pocketed the miner's ability to dream.

An apothecary selling courage by the dram, hope by the ounce, and—in a dusty corner jar, marked down—forgiveness, nearly expired.

And everywhere, threaded through it all, the poor. The ones who had already sold too much. They sat in alcoves with signs around their necks describing what remained: *WILL TRADE SENSE OF SMELL. WILL TRADE THE MEMORY OF MY MOTHER'S FACE, PARTIAL ONLY. WILL TRADE MY ABILITY TO WEep—* and that seller had crossed out the last word because he'd already sold it, and the sign just read *WILL TRADE,* and people stepped around him the way surface-folk stepped around beggars, except these beggars were missing pieces Juniper couldn't see, and some of them smiled, because whatever they'd sold had been hurting them, and the peace was worth it, wasn't it, wasn't it—

"Juniper." Maren tugged his strap. "You're staring."

"I'm a scribe," he heard himself say. "I've spent ten years copying ledgers about grain tithes and candle-wax. I thought debt was numbers." His voice came out strange. "Debt is *this*. Debt is a man selling his mother's face so he can eat."

"That's why they build it deep underground," Maren said. "So the sun never sees it."

They bought bread—actual bread, baked from actual grain hauled down from surface farms in trade, because even the Undercourt needed bodies fed—and ate it in the lee of a fountain that did not hold water but held names, hundreds of them, murmuring up from the basin in a soft braided stream. People knelt at its edge and cupped the sounds in their hands and drank, and Juniper understood with a sick lurch that these were forgotten names, orphaned names, names surrendered in contracts like the one he was beginning to fear he would have to sign, and the fountain was where the mountain kept its spare parts.

One of the names in the water sounded almost like *Whitlock.*

He stood up too fast. Halvard caught his elbow. "Later," the old man said. "First we find the Hall of Petition. Vess grants audiences on the third bell of every turning—but only to petitioners with collateral. We need something to put on her scale."

"We have nothing."

"We have the truth," Halvard said. "Which down here is worth more than it is up top, and less than you'd hope."

* * *

The third bell rang through the caverns like a struck tooth, and the sound of it rearranged something in Juniper's chest—he felt his heartbeat stumble and resume in the bell's rhythm, and saw by Halvard's wince that the old man felt it too. Everything down here wanted to set your tempo. Everything wanted to own your time.

The Hall of Petition was a colonnade of pillars carved from pale stone, each pillar wrapped in chains of contracts—literal chains, links of parchment fused to iron, thousands of agreements hanging like ivy. Petitioners queued between them: a merchant offering his daughter's talent for music against a loan; a widow asking to buy back her husband's last words, which she'd sold in grief and regretted in the morning; a young man, barely older than Juniper, trying to pawn his future, which the clerk behind the desk examined with a jeweler's louche and pronounced "overleveraged."

And at the head of the hall, on a dais of black glass, sat Lady Vess.

She was smaller than Juniper expected. That was the first horror—that the creature who held his sister's soul in her ledger was a slight woman in dove-gray silk, with silver-streaked black hair pinned in a severe coil and hands folded neatly in her lap, hands like a schoolmistress's, like a scribe's. Her face was beautiful the way a locked door is beautiful: flawless, finished, and closed. Only her eyes moved, tracking each petitioner as they approached, and her eyes were the color of the space between stars—which was, Juniper thought with a fresh wave of nausea, a color that no longer existed anywhere above ground.

"She doesn't look like a monster," Maren whispered.

"They never do," Halvard said. "Monsters are made by what they hold, not what they wear. Look at her hands."

Juniper looked. Beneath the silk cuffs, at each wrist, were scars—old, deliberate, parallel. The marks of someone who had once been bound. Or collared.

"One at a time," said the clerk, a gray person of indeterminate age whose voice sounded like pages turning. "State your business and your collateral."

The queue crawled. Juniper used the time the way he'd learned to use all waiting: observing. He watched Vess grant the merchant his loan with a single nod, and watched a ribbon of faint golden light unwind from the daughter's distant direction—from *upward*, from the surface—and coil away into the pillar-chains. He watched the widow leave empty-handed, and saw something flicker across Vess's closed face that might, in a softer creature, have been regret. He watched the young man with the overleveraged future argue, and then stop arguing, and then sign, and Juniper's fingers curled into fists because the signature the boy pressed into the page was shaky and wet and *young*, and no one in the hall reacted at all, because this was Tuesday, this was commerce, this was the price of staying alive down here where the stars had been sold out from under everyone.

That was when it truly curdled for him—not the market, not the fountain of names, but the *ordinariness*. The clerks' bored efficiency. The way horror had been processed into paperwork. Ten years in the temple archives had taught Juniper that the worst cruelties never wore masks. They wore ledgers.

"Next."

They stepped forward together, and Vess's gaze landed on Juniper and stopped.

It was not dramatic. That was the second horror. She simply looked at him the way he imagined an appraiser looks at a ring brought in from the street—cataloguing, weighing, arriving at a figure. And then, very slightly, she tilted her head, and the corner of her mouth moved by a fraction, and she said, in a voice like cool water over slate:

"A Whitlock. How punctual the blood is, after all these years. Thirteen turns, and the family finally comes to call—two days after my wards go dark and my collateral starts to *wander*." Her folded hands unfolded, smoothed her skirt, refolded. "You've come about the girl."

"Wren," Juniper said. His voice held. He was proud of that, later. "My sister. I'm told her soul is held here as collateral against a bargain made before I was born. I've come to see the contract."

"Of course you have." Vess glanced past him to Halvard, and something colder entered her expression. "Master Halvard. The ward-keeper who helped draft those same wards. Who helped draft the *sales* that fed them. How brave of you to show your face below." Her eyes returned to Juniper, and softened by a degree that was somehow worse than the coldness. "The contract exists. It is valid. It is *perfected*, which is a legal term meaning that in eleven days it completes, and your sister becomes, in full and final settlement, the property of the Undercourt." She paused. "You may contest it. Petitioners with standing may always contest. But standing requires collateral, Master Whitlock, and I confess I am curious what a fugitive copyist with no name worth mentioning and no coin worth counting believes he can put on my scale."

The hall had gone quiet around them. Even the chained contracts seemed to hang stiller.

Juniper opened his mouth—to plead, to bargain, to say *anything*—and found nothing there but the smooth blank spaces where his memories of Wren used to be, and the fading echo of a name in a fountain, and the terrible new understanding of what this place considered valuable.

Vess watched him search himself, and her closed face did something almost gentle.

"Take your time," she said. "Everyone does. And when you find what you're willing to pay—come to my tower. Ask for me by name." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "I've been expecting you since before you were born."

Behind the clerk's desk, a lantern began to glow—one of the moth-lanterns, Juniper would learn later, the living contracts—and inside it, faintly, something with wings beat against the glass in a rhythm exactly like a heartbeat.
