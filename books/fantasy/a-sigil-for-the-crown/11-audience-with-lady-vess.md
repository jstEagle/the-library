## 11. Audience with Lady Vess

The invitation came as a moth.

It landed on Juniper's shoulder in the Undermarket's third gallery, while he stood pretending to study a stall that sold secondhand courage by the dram. The moth was white as unmarked paper, and when it opened its wings against his collarbone he saw the script folded there, fine as hair: *Lady Vess will see the Whitlock heir at the turning of the hour. Come alone. Come willing.*

*Heir.* He almost laughed. Thirteen years of copying other people's prayers in a temple scriptorium, thirteen years of being no one so thoroughly that even the head scribe forgot to beat him on feast days—and now the Undercourt itself called him *heir*, as if the word were an honor instead of a hook.

Maren appeared at his elbow before he could decide anything, her dark eyes tracking the moth as it climbed into the lamplight and dissolved into smoke. "That's a summons," she said. "Not an invitation. There's a difference down here."

"I noticed it didn't ask."

"They never do." She glanced up at him, and for a moment her face did something complicated—fear, maybe, or its close cousin. "Juniper. Lady Vess doesn't waste audiences. If she's calling you, it's because she already knows how she wants you to leave."

"Then I'll disappoint her," he said, and was surprised at how steady it came out. Weary competence. It was the only thing he'd ever owned outright.

* * *

Her hall was not what he expected. He had imagined something like the temple's great sanctum—stone and severity, incense thick enough to drown thought. Instead Vess received him in a room that felt like the inside of a memory: walls of soft grey silk that moved faintly, though there was no wind, floors of pale wood worn smooth as river stone, and light that came from nowhere and everywhere at once, the color of late afternoon in a childhood summer.

A table stood between them. Two chairs. Tea, already poured, still steaming.

"You're punctual," said Lady Vess. "Your mother wasn't."

She sat across from him, and Juniper made himself look at her properly. She was beautiful the way a ledger is beautiful—all balance and precision, every line accounted for. Silver hair pinned with black lacquer combs. A gown the color of deep water. Her hands, folded on the table, bore rings on every finger except one: the fourth finger of her left hand, bare and slightly paler than the rest, as if something had been removed from it recently and often.

"Lady Vess." He did not sit until she gestured. "You know why I've come."

"I know why you think you've come." She pushed the teacup toward him. "Drink. It's real tea, grown above ground, traded down here at obscene cost. You'll want to remember what the surface tastes like, where you're going."

"Where I'm going is your vault. Wren's lantern—"

"—is exactly where it has been for thirteen years, safe, untouched, accruing interest." Vess smiled, and it was a terrible smile because it was warm. "Sit back, Master Whitlock. Or may I call you Juniper? I find given names make negotiations honest."

"You can call me whatever gets me out of here faster."

She laughed—a low, genuine sound that unsettled him more than any threat would have. "Oh, you are your father's son. He said nearly the same thing, sitting where you're sitting. Different chair. Same table." She turned her teacup slowly on its saucer. "Very well. Let us be honest together, since honesty is the rarest commodity in my market. I am going to offer you a gift, Juniper Whitlock, and then I am going to show you a piece of paper, and one of those two things is going to save your life. Guess which."

"The gift."

"Clever. No—the paper. But the gift first, so you understand the shape of your good fortune." She laced her fingers. "Walk away. That is the offer. Walk away tonight, through the Gatekeeper's door, up the mountain, back into the world of stars—or the memory of stars—and live. Comfortably. I will settle your temple debts myself; Brother Cassian will receive orders to stand down; Thornfell will forget your face within a season. You will have a house. An income. A life that belongs to no ledger but your own. Everything you have wanted since you were old enough to want."

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. Not from fear—from the horrible accuracy of it. A life that wasn't borrowed. Wasn't that the exact shape of the longing he carried under his ribs like a coal? She had reached into his chest and read it aloud.

"And Wren?"

"Wren is collateral," Vess said gently. "Collateral is not cruelty. It is arithmetic. Your sister sleeps in a lantern, dreamless, painless, preserved. She does not suffer, Juniper. She does not age. When the thirteenth moonrise comes, she passes wholly into the Court's keeping, and she will spend eternity as a ward-light in the deep galleries—honored, treasured, *used*, yes, but used the way a cathedral uses a bell. Is that so much worse than the life she had? Pale, forgetting, fading at a well in a village that barely remembers her name?" She tilted her head. "I have watched surface lives, scribe. They end. Collateral doesn't."

"You're trying to make me grateful."

"I'm trying to make you *rational*. Gratitude would be a bonus." She rose, smooth as poured ink, and went to the silk wall. Where her hand touched it, the fabric parted like water, revealing a shallow niche, and in the niche a lantern of green glass, and inside the lantern—

Juniper was on his feet without deciding to be. Inside the lantern, folded small and bright and struggling, was a soul. He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. It pressed against the glass in quick, birdlike pulses, and each pulse struck somewhere behind his sternum.

"That's not hers," he said hoarsely. "That's a decoy. You wouldn't keep her here."

"No," Vess agreed, and the wall closed over the lantern. "But you flinched for it anyway. Remember that. Remember that you would have traded everything in this room for a moth in a jar, if you believed it was her. That is the weakness your enemies will use, and I am not yet your enemy." She returned to the table and drew from her sleeve a single sheet of vellum, laying it flat between the teacups. "Now. The paper."

It was old. Old the way the temple archives were old, but warmer—handled, loved once, creased along lines where someone had folded and unfolded it many times before sealing it away. The script was formal Undercourt hand, dense with clauses, and Juniper's trained eye began moving down it before he gave it permission.

*Contract of Exchange, sealed beneath the mountain in the Year of the Leaning Bell.*

*Party of the first part: the Undercourt of the Deep, its officers and assigns.*

*Party of the second part:* —and there his breath stopped— *Aldous and Merrow Whitlock of Thornfell, freeholders, of sound mind and full consent.*

The signature block was at the bottom. Two names, two hands. His father's writing he knew instantly—he had grown up tracing it on the flyleaves of smugglers' route-books, the strong downstrokes, the little hook on the *d*. His mother's beside it, smaller, careful, pressing hard enough to emboss the page.

And between the names, in the clause he had somehow skipped, the words arranged themselves with the slow inevitability of a landslide:

*...in exchange for the preservation and protection of the child JUNIPER WHITLOCK, whose life is forfeit to the wasting fever ere the season turns, the parties herein surrender unto the Court one (1) soul of equivalent weight from their issue, said soul to be selected by the Court at its discretion...*

The room went very quiet. The silk walls stopped moving. Even the light seemed to hold its breath.

"No," Juniper said.

"It was a bad winter," Vess said softly. "The fever came up the valley with the salt caravans. Your parents carried you to three healers and a hedge-priest, and all three told them to prepare a small grave. And then someone told them about the mountain." She turned the vellum toward him, tapping one lacquered nail beside the clause. "The Court required a soul of equal weight. A child's soul for a child's life. The Court chose. Not them—they begged to choose, they offered themselves, but adult souls weigh wrong for a child's debt. So the Court chose the daughter. The healthy one. The one who wasn't dying."

"She doesn't know." His voice sounded far away, someone else's voice. "All these years, she doesn't know it was for *me*."

"She knows nothing. That is the mercy of lanterns." Vess sat again, folding her hands. "Your parents signed to save you, Juniper. You are alive because your sister has been dying by inches for thirteen years, so slowly and so gently that no one ever had to tell her. Every word she's lost at that well. Every winter she's gone paler. That is the interest on your life, paid quarterly."

The guilt did not detonate so much as it *arrived*—complete, total, having been waiting inside him all along for exactly this document to give it permission. He saw it whole in a single instant: every candle he'd ever resented burning in the scriptorium, every night in the cold passes guiding smugglers, every careful invisible year—it had all been spent money. Borrowed. Financed by a girl who forgot words mid-sentence and smiled at him anyway, who had never once been told what her smile was costing.

His knees hit the floor before he understood he'd knelt. The stone was warm. Everything down here was warm, like the inside of a body.

"Get up," Vess said. "Kneeling is for the temple. Down here we prefer debtors upright—it's easier to take things from them standing."

"Why—" His voice cracked. He started over. "Why show me this? If you wanted me to walk away, why hand me the one thing guaranteed to make me stay?"

"Because I am honest, and because walking away is genuinely the better bargain, and because I have learned that most people need to see the price tag before they believe the thing is real." She leaned forward, and for just a moment something moved behind her beauty—something tired, something very old. "Contest the claim, and here is what happens. You will need payment of equal weight to void a sealed contract. Do you know what weighs the same as a soul in this place? Nothing you own. Nothing you'll ever earn. You are a scribe with debts and a dead man's name, and the thirteenth moonrise is coming whether you kneel or stand. Walk away, Juniper. Live well. Let your sister become something eternal. Grieve, and garden, and grow old. It is not nothing, the life I'm offering. I would have taken it, at your age."

Something in the way she said it—*at your age*—snagged on him through the roaring of his own guilt. He looked up at her from the floor. "Who sold you?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the silk walls, a faint shifting, like the room itself inhaling.

"That," Vess said at last, rising, "is not for sale today." She extended her hand to help him up, and after a moment he took it. Her grip was cool and precise. "You have twelve days. The offer stands until the twelfth moonrise. After that, the contract completes, and I stop being able to help you—even if I wanted to. Which," she added, releasing his hand, "I advise you strongly not to assume."

At the door—and there had been no door before, the silk simply parted—she spoke once more, and her voice had gone brisk and businesslike, the mask fully restored.

"One courtesy, scribe to creditor. Your parents' copy of this contract was destroyed, per the terms. What you saw tonight exists nowhere else. Whatever you choose—walk or contest—Brother Cassian's masters in Thornfell must never learn the terms of the original bargain. The temple believes the Whitlock sale fed the border wards. If they learn it bought a fevered boy's life instead, they will not send enforcers next time." Her eyes held his, flat and silver as coins. "They will send fire."

* * *

The corridor outside was dim and endless, lit by lanterns of every color, each one humming faintly with something alive. Juniper walked until the door was long gone behind him, then stopped, put his hand against the warm stone, and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

When it was done he knelt there, forehead nearly touching the floor, breathing the mineral smell of the mountain's bones. Above him, impossibly far above, was a sky with no stars in it, and a village with a well, and a girl who was going to ask him someday—when he brought her home, *if* he brought her home—why he looked at her the way he did.

*It was always supposed to be me,* he thought. *The fever. The grave. The small plot behind the meetinghouse. She's living my death.*

He laughed, and it came out broken, and somewhere down the corridor a lantern flickered in sympathy, or mockery.

Footsteps. Small ones, quick ones, deliberately audible—which down here meant trustworthy, or meant someone who wanted to seem so. Maren rounded the corner with Halvard limping behind her, and her face did the complicated thing again, worse this time.

"You met her," Maren said. It wasn't a question. "She showed you the contract."

"How do you know that?"

"Because everyone who meets her comes out looking like that." Maren crouched in front of him, searching his face. "Like they've been handed their own bill. What did she show you, Juniper?"

Halvard leaned against the wall, grey-faced, saying nothing, but his eyes were kind and that was somehow unbearable too.

Juniper wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. When he spoke, his voice came out level—the weary-competent voice, the only inheritance he'd ever earned honestly.

"She showed me that the debt was mine. It always was." He got to his feet, steadying himself against the warm stone. "Wren was sold to save my life. My parents signed for *me*." He looked down the corridor, toward the deep galleries where a green-glass lantern might or might not hold a moth-shaped lie, and felt the last of his hesitation burn away like fog off the salt flats. "So the counter-payment is mine too. Whatever it costs. Whatever it *weighs*."

Maren exchanged a glance with Halvard. "Juniper," she said carefully, "payment of equal weight for a soul—that's not coin. That's not years, not memories, not anything you can carry in a pack. People who go looking for what weighs that much don't come back the same. Some don't come back at all."

"Then I'd better start looking," Juniper said, "because I just realized something Lady Vess didn't plan on." He pushed past them, down the humming corridor, toward the archive district where contracts lived as moths in lanterns. "My parents couldn't choose which soul the Court took. But I'm not signing as a parent begging for a child's life. I'm signing as the debtor." Over his shoulder, grim and certain: "And I know exactly what I'm worth."
