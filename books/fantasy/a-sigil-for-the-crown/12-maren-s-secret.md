## 12. Maren's Secret

Maren had learned to count in the Undermarket, where everything was counted and nothing was free. She counted her steps now—two hundred and eleven since leaving Juniper asleep against the salt-crusted wall of the waystation—and she counted the lanterns swinging ahead of her, three of them, hung on hooks of black iron that grew out of the mountain's throat like teeth.

She counted because counting kept her from thinking about what she was about to do.

The tunnel narrowed, then opened into a chamber that smelled of cold wax and older things. Lady Vess did not keep an office; she kept a parlor, absurdly, down here beneath the world—a room with a low velvet settee, a table of polished bone, and a fire that burned without heat, casting shadows that moved a half-second behind the things that made them. Vess sat with her ankles crossed, turning a small brass instrument over in her fingers: a spyglass no longer than a pen, its lens dark.

"You're late," Vess said, without looking up. "Two hundred and thirteen steps. You dawdled."

"I counted two hundred and eleven."

"Then you miscounted, or you lied to yourself. Both are useful habits." Vess raised her eyes. They were gray as slate and just as warm. "Report."

Maren stood at the edge of the firelight, hands folded the way she'd been taught—folded so the old scars on her knuckles wouldn't show. She had been taught many things in this parlor. How to cry convincingly. How to be found. How to be a stranded child on a salt flat with chapped lips and a story about a dead grandmother in the eastern villages, a story that would make a soft-hearted smuggler stop his cart.

Juniper hadn't stopped for the story. He'd stopped because Maren had been humming. A market tune, one of the Undercourt's own counting songs, and he'd known it—known it the way a man knows a word from his childhood language—and that recognition had frightened him more than any lie could have. So she had amended the plan on the spot. She had let him believe he'd rescued *her*.

"The scribe is moving toward the mountain," she said. "Halvard believes there's an old ore road through the third gallery that bypasses the Gatekeeper's toll entirely. He means to use it when they descend."

"And Juniper?"

"He trusts Halvard. He doesn't trust me yet, but he will. He feeds me first, every meal. He checks my boots for blisters." Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that. "He hums when he thinks no one is listening. Temple hymns, but wrong—changed, like he's been correcting them in his head for years."

Something flickered across Vess's face—not warmth, nothing so generous, but interest, the way a moneylender looks at collateral. "And the sister?"

"Wren Whitlock is held in the vault galleries. The soul-lantern dims. I've seen it. It burns like a candle in a draft."

"It would." Vess set down the spyglass. "You've done well, little wren."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Maren flinched before she could stop herself.

"Don't," Vess said softly. "Don't perform hurt for me. I invented that trick, remember? I taught it to you in this very room, six years ago, when you were brought down from the surface half-frozen and full of names you couldn't pronounce. I gave you a trade: your ears and your face, in exchange for a place at my table. You have been my best investment." She rose, and the shadow-fire stretched her silhouette long and thin against the rock. "When the Whitlock contract completes, you'll be freed of it. Full settlement. A name of your own, registered in the archive, unencumbered. Isn't that what you've wanted since you were seven years old?"

A name. Maren touched the thought the way she once touched the temple offerings through the grate—carefully, hungrily, knowing it wasn't hers. Down here, children without names were furniture. Up there, they were people. She had spent six years being furniture that walked.

"Yes," she said.

"Liar," said Vess, almost fondly. "But a useful liar. Go back. The moon turns in nine days. When Halvard leads them onto the ore road, signal the vault wardens with this." She pressed something into Maren's palm: a sliver of mirror, thin as a fingernail. "Catch the firelight with it twice. The wardens will do the rest. Halvard will be taken alive—he owes the court years, and we collect—and the scribe boy will be delivered to me unspoiled. Do you understand? *Unspoiled.* If he's harmed, your settlement is forfeit."

Maren closed her hand around the mirror sliver. Its edge bit her skin, a small bright pain, and she was grateful for it. Pain was honest. Pain didn't negotiate.

"And if I'm caught?" she asked.

Vess smiled, and it was the worst thing in the room, worse than the heatless fire. "Then you were never mine, and I never knew you, and the archive will show that Maren of the Salt Road died six years ago of exposure. Grief is also a currency, child. I trade in all of it."

* * *

She went back up the tunnels counting again, and somewhere past step ninety the counting failed her, because she began instead to remember.

Not the parlor. Not the lessons. She remembered the salt flats—the real ones, the ones above, under a sky that used to have stars in it. She remembered being seven, and her mother's hands cracking ice off the water barrel, and a song her mother sang that had no words anyone taught her, just a rising line like a bird climbing wind. She remembered dying, supposedly. Exposure, the ledger said. But she hadn't died. She had wandered down into the Undermarket following a light she thought was a lantern, and by the time she understood what kind of market it was, her name had already been weighed, appraised, and entered into a column.

Six years of columns. Six years of being sent up into the thinning world to be found by desperate travelers, to learn their routes and their fears and their songs. She had been a mirror sliver herself, catching other people's fires and flashing them back to the dark.

And then Juniper Whitlock had crouched down in the salt crust, looked at her not with pity but with *recognition*—the look of a man who knew what it was to be owned by a ledger—and had said, "Can you walk? Good. Then walk beside me, not behind me. Behind me you're cargo. Beside me you're company."

No one had ever made her company before.

She reached the waystation and paused outside, listening. Halvard's voice, low and rough, arguing geography with himself. Juniper's silence, which was its own kind of voice—the silence of a man memorizing exits. And then, faintly, Juniper humming. That broken hymn again, corrected note by note, and Maren realized with a lurch what he was doing: he was tuning the hymn to the counting song. To *her* song. He'd heard it once on the flats and had been quietly rebuilding his prayers around it ever since, the way he rebuilt everything—ledgers, routes, escape plans—as if the world were a text that could be fixed if you copied it carefully enough.

Her eyes stung. She wiped them on her sleeve, angry at them. Crying convincingly was a skill; crying honestly was a liability.

She slipped inside. Juniper looked up, and his whole face changed—softened, opened—in a way that made her chest hurt worse than the mirror sliver had.

"There's stew," he said. "It's mostly salt pork and regret, but Halvard claims regret adds body."

"It does," Halvard grunted from his corner, not opening his eyes. "I've been marinating in it for thirty years."

Maren took the bowl. Their fingers brushed. Juniper didn't pull away fast, the way people did down below, checking for pickpockets. He just went back to studying his map, trusting her at his elbow, and that trust sat in Maren's stomach like a swallowed coal.

Nine days. She had nine days to decide what she was.

* * *

On the fourth night, the decision was taken partly out of her hands.

They had moved to the mouth of the third gallery, where the old ore road began its descent—a ribcage of rotten timbers and rusted rails spiraling down into the dark. Halvard stood at the edge with a hooded lamp, reading the mountain the way he read everything: reluctantly, expertly, with the expression of a man rereading his own confession.

"This road predates the wards," he said. "Predates the sales. The first ward-keepers cut it when the mountain still belonged to itself. If it holds, we come up inside the court, past the Gatekeeper, past the tolls. If it doesn't hold—" he shrugged, "—we find out quickly."

Juniper was checking the lashings on their packs, and Maren was holding the lamp for him, and that was when she saw it: far below, deep in the gallery's throat, a light answering theirs. Two flashes. Paused. Two flashes again.

The signal. But not hers—she still had the mirror sliver in her boot, untouched. Which meant Vess hadn't trusted her alone with the task. Which meant there was a second spy, or a warden patrol already in position, which meant the ore road was a trap whether Maren betrayed them or not.

Her heart went strange and slow, like a clock underwater.

"Halvard," she said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, and she hated it, and she used the crack. "I have to tell you something. Both of you. Now, before we take another step."

Juniper turned. In the lamplight his face was hollowed and sleepless and utterly open to her, and she thought: *he feeds me first.* She thought: *beside me, not behind me.*

"I know the Undermarket," she said. "Not from stories. From inside. I was sold down when I was seven—I fell through the wrong door on the flats, and the court registered me before my mother even knew I was missing. Vess owns my settlement. She sent me up to be found by you. I was supposed to lead you onto this road and signal the wardens." She pulled the mirror sliver from her boot and held it out, glinting. "There's your proof. There's my confession. The road is trapped. There's a light down there right now, waiting for a signal I never gave."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far below, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

Halvard moved first. Not toward her—with a speed that belied his ruined body he stepped *between* her and Juniper, facing outward, toward the dark, toward whatever else might be listening. Thirty years of guilt had built him into a wall, and here, finally, was something worth being a wall for.

"How long have you known?" he asked quietly.

"That they were watching? Since the waystation. That I'd choose differently?" Maren's throat worked. "Since the stew."

Juniper stared at the mirror sliver in her palm. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a scribe reading a contract aloud to be sure of every clause. "Why tell us now?"

"Because you hummed my song," she said, and immediately wished she'd found a better reason, a braver reason, anything less naked than that. "Because you check my boots for blisters. Because down there I'm a column in a ledger and up here you made me *company*, and I am so tired of being a column, Juniper. I am so tired."

He took the mirror sliver from her hand. He didn't throw it away. He turned it over, caught the lamplight with it—one flash, two—and then closed his fist around it, keeping it, keeping her secret the way he kept everything: filed, weighted, accounted for.

"Then we don't take the ore road," he said. "We let them think we're taking it."

* * *

The plan they built in the next six hours was Juniper's, drawn in the margins of his map with a scribe's precision: let the false signal flash. Let the wardens gather at the ore road's mouth and wait in the dark for travelers who never come. Meanwhile the three of them would go back to the main passage, pay the Gatekeeper's toll properly, enter the court through the front door like honest debtors—because, Juniper argued, the one thing the Undercourt never expected was to be approached by the rules.

"They watch every crack," he said, scratching lines, "because cracks are how *they* move. Nobody watches the front gate. It's beneath their dignity."

"It's beneath everyone's dignity," Halvard muttered. "That's why it works."

So they waited for full dark, and Maren climbed partway down the ore road alone, heart hammering, and flashed the mirror twice into the deep. Two flashes. Pause. Two flashes. The answering light winked acknowledgment, and wardens began to move—she heard them, boots on timber, dozens of them, converging on a trap that would stay empty—and she climbed back up with her legs shaking and her chin high.

It almost worked.

It worked until the main passage, until the last bend before the Gatekeeper's bridge, where the air went cold and still as held breath—and the lamps came on. All of them at once, a dozen hooded lanterns ringing the passage, and out of their circle stepped a figure in gray silk, unhurried, as though she had merely been out walking and found the evening disappointing.

Lady Vess looked at Maren first. Not with anger. With arithmetic.

"One flash too many," Vess said. "Your second signal came from a different angle than your first. You always did hold your left hand higher than your right, little wren. Sentiment makes sloppy tradecraft." Her gaze slid to Juniper, traveled him slowly, cataloguing. "Scribe Whitlock. You've cost me a good agent. Do you know what replacement costs, down here? Nothing replaces an ear that's already trusted."

"She chose," Juniper said, stepping slightly in front of Maren, and Maren's whole body went hot at the word. *Chose.*

"They always say that." Vess sighed. "Bind them."

The wardens moved. What followed was short and ugly and nothing like the clean choreography of Juniper's plans. Halvard fought like a man settling a thirty-year account, taking two wardens down with him into the rail dust, roaring something about wards and souls and *I helped build them, I'll see them broken*—and then a chain of cold iron took him across the throat, and he went down, and did not get up.

"Halvard!" Juniper lunged; a warden caught his arms; another took his legs; they folded him to the stone with practiced cruelty, pinning him, and Maren screamed and grabbed the nearest warden's coat and was thrown aside, skidding, her palms torn on the rock.

"Alive," Vess said idly. "Both of them. The old man goes to the cells—he has years outstanding, and we'll begin collection tonight. The scribe comes to my parlor. We have business, he and I, about his parents' signature." She turned at last fully to Maren, who lay bleeding on the stones, and her voice softened into something almost gentle, which was the cruelest register she owned. "As for you. I told you what happens to agents who are caught. You were never mine. Maren of the Salt Road died six years ago."

"No," Maren said. She pushed herself up on her torn palms, blood threading between her fingers, and faced the woman who had named her, trained her, erased her. "She did die. Six years ago. On the flats. And what got up was yours." She lifted her chin. "But I'm not hers either. I'm not yours. I'm nobody's column. I'm standing *with* them."

Vess studied her for a long moment, head tilted, and then—worst of all—she smiled, small and genuine, the smile of a moneylender watching an asset appreciate beyond projection.

"Oh, *little wren*," she said. "Do you know what loyalty is worth in this market? More than souls. More than names. I should know; I sold mine." She gestured, and the wardens hauled Juniper upright between them, his map fluttering loose from his pack, pages scattering across the stone like pale moths. "Take them both to the holding galleries. Separate cells. And someone collect those pages—they're the only honest accounting anyone's brought into my court in a century."

They dragged Juniper past her, and he twisted in the wardens' grip, and his eyes found hers, and he didn't look betrayed. He looked *furious on her behalf*, which was somehow infinitely worse, and he mouthed one word before they wrenched him away:

"*Run.*"

She didn't run.

They put her in a cell of smooth black stone with no door she could find and a single seam of light along the floor, and she sat with her back against the wall and her torn palms open in her lap, and she counted. Her own heartbeat, this time. One, two, three. It was the only currency she had left that no one had appraised.

Above her, muffled through a hundred feet of mountain, she imagined the twelfth new moon rising over a starless sky. Two turns left. Somewhere in these galleries, Halvard was paying in years. Somewhere in a parlor of heatless fire, Juniper was learning what his parents' signature really bought.

And somewhere in the vault, a soul-lantern burned low in its draft, dimming, dimming, waiting for someone to remember it was a person.

Maren pressed her bleeding palms flat against the cold floor and began, very quietly, to sing the counting song—the market's song, her mother's song, the one Juniper had rebuilt his hymns around—and she sang it not as a signal this time, but as a promise, letting the words climb the black stone like a bird climbing wind, certain that somewhere in this mountain of ledgers, if sound could carry at all, it would carry to him.

One, two, three. Two turns left. She had chosen her side, and the mountain had heard her choose.

Now she only had to survive long enough to make it matter.
