## 14. Brother Cassian Descends

Brother Cassian had never once doubted the Ledger, and this was precisely why he made such an excellent instrument for it.

He stood at the mouth of the mountain with two hundred temple enforcers behind him and the dark pressing against his face like a living thing. The descent road—no, not a road, a *throat*—yawned before them, cut into the rock in spirals too regular to be natural and too old to be holy. Torches guttered in the wind that breathed up from below, a wind that smelled of ink and cold iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft.

"Light the censer," Cassian said.

Behind him, Brother Odo swung the great silver thurible on its chains, and the smoke of consecrated myrrh rolled forward into the dark. Where it touched the stone, the rock *flinched*. Cassian watched the shadow recoil from the smoke and felt the familiar warmth of certainty settle over him, the warmth he had carried since he was nine years old and first learned to copy the Ledger's opening verse: *All debts are sacred. All debtors are known.*

Somewhere beneath them was the boy Juniper Whitlock—the scribe who had stolen from the archives, who had fled his indenture, who had asked questions about a sealed page. Somewhere beneath them were the records of thirteen years of soul-sales, the heresy the temple had spent generations pretending did not exist while its own hands fed the wards below. And somewhere beneath them was the truth that could unmake everything: that the border wards had not been miracles but *mortgages*, paid in souls, and that the temple had been the broker.

Cassian knew all of this now. He had read Halvard's confession in the burned village of Ashenmere, pulled from the coals of a hearth where the ward-keeper had tried to destroy it. He had read every word, and he had not wept, because weeping was for men who believed the world could be other than it was.

The world was the Ledger. The Ledger was the world. And what was written must be either honored or erased.

"We descend," he said. "No one speaks. No one touches anything that glitters. If a voice calls your name—and it will—you do not answer."

Odo shifted beside him. "Brother, the scouts say the market below trades in—" He stopped, as if the word itself might cost him.

"I know what it trades in," Cassian said. "That is why we burn it."

* * *

The Undermarket announced itself long before they reached it, by sound.

It was a sound like a library drowning—a vast susurrus of whispers and rustling pages and coins that chimed in registers no surface coin could reach. Then the tunnel opened, all at once, into a cavern so large that the ceiling was a rumor, hung with ten thousand lanterns of colored glass, each one glowing with a light that had no flame behind it. And inside those lanterns—

Cassian did not let himself look closely. Looking closely was how they got you.

The market spread below in terraces carved from the mountain's bones: stalls of black wood and bone-white silk, merchants whose faces were smooth as eggs or crowded with more features than faces should hold, and everywhere the goods—bottled regrets stacked like preserves, spools of unlived years, name-plates polished to mirrors, and lantern after lantern after lantern, each one holding a moth that beat its wings in slow, patient panic.

A soul, Halvard's confession had said, looks like a moth because a moth is a thing that spends itself against the light and calls it purpose.

"Form ranks," Cassian said quietly. "We go down the central stair. We do not stop. We do not trade. When we reach the archive terrace, we fire it."

They went down. And the market *noticed*.

It happened the way weather happens—gradually, then all at once. First the whispers changed pitch, bending toward them like grass toward wind. Then the merchants turned, one terrace at a time, hundreds of smooth faces and crowded faces tilting in unison, and Cassian understood that the Undermarket had no guards because it had never needed any. The market itself was watching.

At the third terrace, a merchant stepped into their path. She was small and gray as ash, wearing a dress stitched from ledger-pages, and she smiled at Cassian with a mouth full of neat black teeth.

"Pilgrim," she said. "What brings you down? We sell everything. Even forgiveness. Especially forgiveness—it keeps poorly, you know, so it's always fresh."

"We are not here to buy," Cassian said.

"No one ever is." Her smile widened. "But everyone pays. That's the whole architecture of the place, dear. You came down here carrying something, whether you know it or not. Shall I tell you what it is? It's very heavy. I can see it dragging behind you like a bride's train."

"Stand aside."

"Oh, gladly." She stepped back and swept a page-stitched sleeve toward the stair. "But you'll pay on the way through. Everyone does. The toll is a true story, freely given—or didn't your Gatekeeper tell you? Ah." The black teeth gleamed. "You came down the war-road instead. The one the mountain opens for people who intend harm. Yes, I can smell it on you. Incense and kerosene." She leaned close, and her whisper was almost tender. "Then your story will be taken from you later, brother. With interest. The mountain is patient about its accounts."

She melted back into the stalls, and Cassian led his men onward, and behind him he heard—not heard, *felt*—the whispers change again, spreading outward through the terraces like a stain through water:

*Temple. Temple-men. They've come to burn the archive. They've come to burn the archive.*

By the fifth terrace, the stalls were closing. By the sixth, the merchants had begun to gather along the edges of the great cavern, silent, watching, hundreds of them, and among the crowd Cassian saw things that tightened his grip on his censer-chain: children with old eyes. A woman whose shadow moved a half-second behind her body. A man holding a lantern in which a moth beat frantically, and the man was whispering to it, *soon, soon, I'm saving up to buy you back.*

These were the sold. These were the collateral. And every one of them was looking at Cassian with an expression he recognized from the villages he had burned: not hatred.

*Hope.* Horrible, misplaced hope—that the fire might be a mercy.

He crushed the feeling. Feelings were interest accruing on nothing.

* * *

The archive terrace was a cathedral of glass.

Lanterns by the tens of thousands hung in ranked rows from chains anchored in the living rock, each one labeled with a lead seal, each one holding its moth, and the combined light of them turned the whole vault gold. At its center stood the great contract-racks, and there—Cassian found it without being shown—the Whitlock lantern, larger than the others, its moth battering itself against the glass in rhythms that were almost words.

Beside it, another lantern, freshly sealed, its label still wet with ink: *HALVARD, WARD-KEEPER — PAYMENT: REMAINING YEARS (17)*.

And standing between Cassian and both lanterns, breathing hard, soot-streaked, with a girl clutching his sleeve and a stolen soul-lantern already under his arm, was Juniper Whitlock.

"You're late," Whitlock said. His voice shook, but he held his ground. "The sale completes tomorrow night. You're two hundred men and a torch, and you think that makes you righteous. It doesn't. It just makes you *arsonists with paperwork*."

"The records are heresy," Cassian said. "Every contract in this room is a wound in the world. The temple erases wounds."

"The temple *made* these wounds!" Whitlock's voice cracked across the vault, and the moths in a thousand lanterns stirred at once, wings drumming like rain. "You read Halvard's confession—I know you did, you burned three villages to get it. Your own high scribes drafted the first sales. Your wards ran on souls. You aren't here to cleanse anything. You're here to destroy the receipts!"

For one moment—one treacherous, human moment—Cassian wavered. Because the boy was right, and Cassian had known he was right since Ashenmere, and he had chosen the fire anyway, because the alternative was a world where everything he had served was a lie, and a man cannot live inside that world. Better a short world than a false one.

"Yes," Cassian said. "And so we burn them."

He raised the censer. "Ignite the racks."

* * *

The fire caught strangely.

Consecrated flame should have leapt greedily through dry paper and old wax, but the contracts fought back. Each lantern, when the fire touched it, screamed—a thin, glassy note—and the moths beat harder, and the flames bent *away*, curling off the glass like water off oiled skin. Only the racks themselves burned, black wood charring, and the heat built and built with nowhere to go, and the whole golden vault began to fill with smoke that was not smoke but *ink*, boiling off burning labels in black clouds.

In that chaos, everything shifted at once.

The Undermarket's watchers surged forward—not at the temple men, but at the racks, hundreds of desperate hands reaching for lanterns, tearing them from melting chains, running with their own souls or their children's souls clutched to their chests. Merchants who had traded in intangibles for centuries suddenly became thieves in their own shop, and the enforcers' ranks broke apart as the crowd flooded between them.

"Hold formation!" Cassian roared. "Hold—"

Brother Odo went down under three of the sold, who did not strike him—they simply *took*, wrenching the censer from his hands, scattering consecrated coals, and vanishing into the smoke with their prizes. On the far side of the vault, Cassian saw temple men fighting not the crowd but each other, brothers turning on brothers as some dropped their torches and joined the looting, thirty years of doctrine dissolving in firelight when a man held his mother's moth in his hands and read the temple's own seal on the label.

That was the moment zealotry met its consequence, though Cassian would not understand it until much later: you cannot send believers to burn a lie when the lie has their families written in it.

"Whitlock!" Cassian drew his blade—not to kill, but to clear a path—and shoved through the crush. The boy was moving toward the exit tunnel with the girl and the lantern, and Lady Vess's white-robed retainers were converging from the opposite side, and between all three forces the vault floor had become a churned sea of fire and glass and screaming moths freed from shattered lanterns, souls loose in the air, blundering against faces, seeking bodies, seeking *home*.

One landed on Cassian's shoulder.

He felt it like a word spoken directly into his blood: a name. *His* name, the one the temple registry held, the one his mother gave him before the Order took it and reissued him a title. The moth rested there a moment, weighing him, and then it lifted away, uninterested, and Cassian stood in the burning heart of everything he had come to destroy and felt—for the first time in his life—*unwritten*.

Whitlock was three paces away, the girl pulling his arm, Vess's retainers closing in. Cassian raised his blade.

And then the mountain spoke.

Not a sound—a *pressure*, rolling up through the rock, through the soles of every boot in the vault, a single vast pulse like the heartbeat of something turning over in its sleep. Every lantern still hanging shivered. Every moth still captive beat once, in perfect unison. And in the sudden silence, every soul in the Undermarket heard the same sentence arrive inside their skulls, in a voice like a ledger closing:

*Thirteenth moonrise approaches. All accounts come to threshold.*

The crowd broke. The temple broke. Vess's retainers broke. And in the breaking, Juniper Whitlock looked back across the chaos, straight at Cassian, and shouted the last thing Cassian expected:

"The stairs behind the archive—Halvard's old ward-passage—it goes straight to the threshold! If you want to burn something, brother, burn the *contract*, not the boy who's trying to end it!"

Then the smoke took him, and he was gone, and Cassian stood alone in the fire with his blade lowered and his certainty cracked clean through, hearing the words repeat in the ruin of his mind like a verse he'd never noticed in scripture:

*Trying to end it.*

Around him, his two hundred men were scattered, converted, or dead. Behind him, the archive burned with only half its records, the rest already fleeing into a hundred hiding places through the mountain. Ahead, the passage mouth gaped, lit by falling embers.

Brother Cassian sheathed his sword, picked up a fallen torch, and followed the heretic down.
