## 15. Halvard's Bargain

The cell had no door.

That was the first thing Halvard understood when the numbness finally let him think past his own body: the Undercourt did not need doors. The walls of his prison were grown, not built—pale stone veined with something that pulsed faintly, slow as a sleeping heart, and where a doorway should have been there was simply a seam in the rock that had never been opened. He had been a ward-keeper for thirty years. He knew containment when he lay inside it. This room had been *grown around him*, shaped to his measure, and it would hold until the mountain decided otherwise or until whatever remained of his life ran out, whichever came first.

He lay on a slab of the same pale stone and counted what he had left. It was an old habit, counting. In the passes above Thornfell he had counted lanterns, counted breaths between gusts of wind, counted the seconds a ward held after its anchor-stone cracked. Counting was how a careful man survived. And Halvard had been, once, a very careful man—which was precisely how all of this had happened.

His ribs were broken. Two, maybe three, on the left side, from the moment the Undercourt's grey-clad collectors had taken him at the archive stairs. His right hand was worse. They had not tortured him—they hadn't needed to—but the binding they'd used to stop him working any craft had settled into the bones like frost settling into earth, and now his fingers ached with a cold that had nothing to do with temperature. A ward-keeper's hands were his livelihood. Whoever designed these bindings had known exactly what to take.

He wondered, distantly, if he had trained them. Thirty years ago. In another life. In the life where he still believed the work was holy.

* * *

She came on the second day—or what he took for the second day, because the cell kept no hours and his body was becoming unreliable as a clock. The seam in the wall simply opened, the way a mouth opens, and Lady Vess stood in it with a lantern she did not need. Down here, he suspected, light was a courtesy one extended to guests. Or to prisoners one wanted to see clearly.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. Dying men make poor conversation."

"I'm not dying." He tried to sit up and managed it on the third attempt, breath sawing through the broken ribs. "Not quickly."

"No." She regarded him with an expression he could not read—not cruelty, not pity. Something more like an appraiser looking over goods long stored in a cellar. "You're spending, though. I can hear it. Every hour down here costs you. You surface-folk are so *thin*, Halvard. So little reserve. You burn yourselves down to a wick and call it discipline."

"What do you want?"

"To talk. Is that so strange?" She drifted into the cell without seeming to walk; the distance simply closed around her, the way distances do underground. She set the lantern on the floor between them, and its light made the veins in the walls glow faintly amber. "An old colleague comes beneath my mountain after thirteen years of hiding above it, carrying a forged pilgrim's seal and a boy who smells of temple ink and smuggler tar. Of course I want to talk."

"I'm not your colleague."

"No?" Her head tilted. "Then who drafted the Thornfell border wards? Who laid the anchor-stones at the nine passes? Who sat at the table in the winter of the famine year and negotiated terms with the Undercourt on behalf of a temple too proud to come itself?" She smiled, small and terrible. "I keep excellent records. That is rather the entire point of me."

Halvard said nothing. There was nothing to say that she didn't already know better than he did.

"Your boy is in my market," Vess went on, settling herself onto the slab opposite him with the ease of someone visiting a garden. "Juniper. A copyist. Debtor. Brother to the collateral on the Whitlock contract. Do you know what he's been doing since you were taken? He's been reading. Sitting in my archive galleries with that ink-stained look, reading contract law by lantern-light, taking notes like a good little scribe." Something moved across her face then, brief as a fish under ice—something that might, in a creature with more warmth in it, have been amusement. "He thinks he can find a loophole. Thirteen years of temple training and he thinks salvation is a matter of finding the right clause."

"He's cleverer than you think."

"Oh, I don't doubt it. Cleverness is cheap down here. Everyone who arrives is clever—it's practically the toll." She leaned forward. "But cleverness doesn't change arithmetic. The contract completes on the thirteenth moonrise. The girl's soul comes down, the ledger balances, and the wards stay fed for another cycle. Unless—" and here her voice acquired a certain silk, the silk of a trap being offered as a gift, "—unless someone contests the claim at the threshold, in blood, with payment of equal weight. Which is what he's going to try. And which will destroy him. You know it. I know it. He doesn't, because he's twenty-three and in love with his own sister, which is a kind of blindness the surface specializes in."

"If you've come to gloat—"

"I've come to make *you* an offer."

That stopped him. Vess watched the stoppage land, catalogued it, filed it away—he could see her doing it, the way a moneylender watches a debtor flinch at the figure.

"The boy cannot pay equal weight," she said. "Not yet. Not in two turns. He would need to surrender something the Court values as much as a soul, and he hasn't understood yet what that is, and even if he did, the drafting alone—the precision required, the binding language—he'd need months and a lifetime's practice and a hand steadier than any scribe the temples produce. He'll stand at the threshold on the thirteenth moonrise with a document full of good intentions and insufficient clauses, and I will be forced to honor the original contract, and the girl comes down, and the boy breaks, and everyone has wasted a season." She sighed. "Unless the timeline changes."

Halvard's heart was beating hard against his broken ribs. He made himself keep still. "What kind of change?"

"A stall. A delay. Two moons, granted formally, entered into the record—a lawful adjournment, not a mercy. During those two moons the contract is frozen, the girl stays as she is—half-here already, poor thing, forgetting words mid-sentence—and the boy has time to draft something real." She spread her hands. "I am, whatever else I am, a creature of proper procedure. If he produces valid counter-payment within the adjournment, the Whitlock contract dissolves and I lose my collateral and the wards go hungry and the world above gets to go on pretending. If he fails, everything completes anyway and I am two moons richer in interest. Either way, the record is clean. I am offering him time. What I want in exchange—" her eyes came up to his, "—is years."

The word hung in the cold air of the cell.

"Yours," Vess said gently. "You are fifty-eight years old, Halvard Wardwright, formerly First Keeper of the Thornfell Passes, and by my reckoning—and my reckoning is the only one that counts down here—you have eleven years left in you. Eleven years of breath, of ache, of mornings. I will take them. All of them. Now. In this room. And in exchange I enter the adjournment into the great ledger, sealed and witnessed, and Juniper Whitlock has two moons instead of none."

"And if he succeeds," Halvard said slowly, "you get nothing back. You spend my years and lose the soul regardless."

"Yes." She said it without flinching. "That is why the offer stands only tonight. Tomorrow I reconsider, and tomorrow's price will be higher, and the day after higher still, because that is how markets work, and because—" the fish-under-ice flicker again, "—I confess a certain curiosity about how this ends. Sentiment. Don't tell anyone. It's very bad for business."

He looked at her for a long time. The lantern between them burned without flickering; down here even fire obeyed procedure.

"Why?" he asked. "Why offer anything? Freeze the contract. Take the girl. Let the boy break. You win either way if you do nothing."

"Do I?" For just a moment, the creditor's polish dropped and something older looked out of her face—something tired, something that remembered sky. "Thirteen years ago a woman sat across a table from me with a newborn in her arms and signed away her daughter's future to buy her son's life, and I told myself it was commerce, it was procedure, the ledger must balance. And every year since, I have watched that girl up there grow paler and quieter and forget more words, and part of me—the part that was sold once myself, before I climbed high enough to forget it—has been waiting to see whether anyone up there would come down and fight for her." She rose, smoothing robes that needed no smoothing. "So far, it's been a crippled old man and a boy with ink on his fingers. Hardly inspiring. But the night is young, as we say down here, though we say it ironically."

* * *

After she left—if leaving was the word; the seam closed and the light stayed, which he suspected meant she wanted him to be able to see what he was deciding—Halvard sat with his back against the pulsing wall and did the arithmetic honestly, because he owed himself that much.

Eleven years. He had assumed fewer. The bindings in his hand, the broken ribs, the thin surface-flesh of him—eleven years felt almost generous. He thought about what eleven years looked like from inside. Mornings. He had always loved mornings above the tree line, the way the first light came off the snow before the sun itself cleared the ridge, turning the whole pass the color of new copper. Eleven years of that. Eleven springs of the meltwater running loud in the gullies. Eleven autumns of guiding caravans down through the narrows with a lantern in his fist, feeling the wards hum under his boots, humming *because of him*, because he kept them, because keeping things was the only prayer he had ever truly believed in.

Except.

And here was the arithmetic that mattered, the column he had spent thirty years refusing to add.

The wards hummed because souls fed them. He had learned that in his second year as First Keeper, when the old keeper before him—dying, unafraid, wanting absolution more than comfort—had told him the truth of the founding: that the border wards of Thornfell were not anchored in stone but in *payment*, that the mountain below held a court and the court held contracts, and that every generation the temple sent down names, and every generation the wards drank deep and the passes stayed safe and the harvests came and nobody above asked what the safety cost or whose child paid it. Halvard had been twenty-eight. He had gone to his superiors. He had been told, kindly, firmly, that this was the price of civilization, that the alternative was plague and raiders and the dark, that a man of his talents should concern himself with maintenance rather than morality.

And he had concerned himself with maintenance.

Worse. In the famine year, when the temple needed a negotiator who spoke the Undercourt's language, they had sent *him*, because he was careful and precise and trusted, and he had sat at that table and helped draft the standard terms—the clauses, the rollovers, the collateral structures—that made the sales clean and repeatable. He had invented half the machinery of it. Somewhere in the deep archive of this very mountain, in a lantern among ten thousand lanterns, there was probably a moth with his handwriting folded into its wings.

And thirteen years ago, when the Whitlocks came—when a desperate mother named her newborn son as the debtor and her six-year-old daughter as the payment, because the bargain was structured so that the debt deferred and the soul secured, and Halvard had *checked the clauses*, had confirmed them sound, had initialled the schedule—thirteen years ago he had done his job carefully, and a girl had begun to fade, and a boy had begun to copy prayers to cover a debt he never chose.

Atonement, he thought, was supposed to feel like relief. Everyone said so. Lay down your burden, unburden your soul, confess and be washed. But lying on the cold slab of a grown cell, Halvard found it felt nothing like relief. It felt like paying. Slowly, expensively, in the exact coin of the harm—because what had his sin been, really? Not cruelty. Carelessness dressed as duty. He had kept the lamps lit without asking what burned in them. And so the payment now was not his death—he could have died anywhere, uselessly, and death was cheap—but his *time*. The one thing he had always hoarded, always budgeted, always counted. Eleven years, poured out so a boy he'd known for a handful of weeks could have two moons to attempt something impossible.

It wasn't enough. He knew it wasn't enough. Nothing would be enough; that was the nature of what he'd done; you couldn't unfeed a ward. But it was *proportionate*, and proportion was all a man in his position had left to offer, and he clung to that with both aching hands.

There was one other thing. He turned it over as the cell's pulse slowed toward what might have been night. He knew the wards. He had *built* them, or their grandfathers. And if Juniper was going to stand at the threshold in two moons and offer a counter-contract, the boy would need to understand what he was negotiating against—needed the founding terms, the original architecture, the flaws Halvard had baked into the system because even careful men grow tired. Halvard had spent the last day and a half, in the gaps between pain, composing it in his head: a confession, yes, but a *technical* one. Where the anchors were soft. Which clauses had escape hatches and who had put them there and why. What the Court valued and why a name outweighed a soul. Everything Juniper Whitlock would need to fight, delivered in the only currency Halvard had ever been fluent in: precise, documented, complete.

He would need Vess to carry it. That was the bitter joke—his confession would travel to the boy in the hands of the creditor herself. But she wanted a contest; he'd seen it in her, the hunger under the procedure. She would deliver it. She would want her opponent armed.

Or perhaps he was flattering himself. Perhaps it was simpler than that. Perhaps, somewhere under the ledgers and the silk, Lady Vess remembered being sold, and wanted—one last time, one small time—to watch somebody choose differently than she had.

* * *

She came again before he called for her, which told him she had known his answer before he gave it. The seam opened. The lantern-light leaned in.

"Well?" she said.

"Two moons," Halvard said. "Formal adjournment, entered in the great ledger, witnessed, sealed. The contract frozen. The girl neither worsens nor completes during the term. The boy gets his time uncontested—your collectors leave him alone, your spies recall, no interference in the drafting. Those are my terms for the sale, and I want them written into the adjournment itself, because you taught me down here that only what's written survives."

Vess's eyebrows rose a fraction—an archivist's appreciation of good drafting. "You've been studying."

"I had a good teacher. Terrible woman. Never let me round a number in my life." He pushed himself upright against the wall, gasping once at the ribs, and made himself meet her eyes. "Eleven years. All of them, now, today. And one condition more, and it's the important one, so attend: you carry a message to Juniper Whitlock. Word for word. Everything I'm about to tell you—the founding of the wards, the famine-year terms, the Whitlock contract, the flaws, the hatch-clauses, the true weight of a name—all of it. You'll swear it into the same ledger. Because if that boy walks into the threshold knowing only half the truth, then my years bought nothing but a longer execution, and I will have sold my mornings for nothing, and I will spend whatever's left of this evening hating you efficiently."

For a long moment Lady Vess said nothing at all. Then she drew from her sleeve a slim black book that seemed to drink the lantern-light, and a pen of grey bone, and she said, "Dictate."

And Halvard Wardwright, First Keeper of the Thornfell Passes, traitor to no one and accessory to everyone, began to dictate his confession to the underworld of his own design—slowly, precisely, in the voice he had once used to bless the anchor-stones—while somewhere above him a boy bent over borrowed paper in a gallery of moths, and somewhere nearer than that a girl forgot another word, and overhead, beyond leagues of living rock, the empty sky wheeled on, starless, patient, waiting to see what two moons would buy.

When he finished, near what he chose to believe was dawn, Vess closed the black book and stood. At the seam she paused, and without turning said, quietly:

"For what it's worth, Keeper—I remember the famine-year table. You were the only one there who looked ashamed."

"That wasn't worth much then."

"No," she agreed. "But it's worth more now. Shame is the only honest currency the surface ever sends us." The seam began to close. "Two moons, Halvard. Tell your boy to write well. I should hate for your mornings to be wasted."

The wall sealed. The lantern guttered once and steadied. And in the pulsing dark, an old man with empty hands and an emptied future leaned his head back against the heartbeat of the mountain, and for the first time in thirty years, slept without counting anything at all.
