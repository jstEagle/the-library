## 17. Twelfth Moon Rising

The twelfth new moon came up over the mountain like a bruise beneath the skin of the world—a darkness with weight, a darkness that pressed. Juniper felt it in his teeth before Halvard's old almanac confirmed it: one turn left, and Wren's soul still sat in a lantern in Lady Vess's vault, burning on borrowed time.

He had spent eleven turns learning the Undermarket's rhythms the way he'd once learned temple ledgers—by candle-count and repetition, by noticing what was counted and what was overlooked. Vess's vault stood at the heart of her holdings above the Undercourt proper, a tower of black glass where contracts went to sleep. Its doors were warded with names, not iron. Names could be stolen. But towers, Juniper had learned, were built by people who feared tunnels.

And tunnels, he knew like prayers.

* * *

"The ore roads," Maren said, crouched over the map Halvard had scratched into a slate before his capture. She'd kept it hidden inside her coat for two moons, sleeping against it like a talisman. "Halvard marked them from memory. He helped cut half of them."

"He told me." Juniper traced the lines with one finger. "The mountain used to bleed silver. The Undercourt built its first fortunes on it, back when the courts still pretended to honor old bargains. Then the veins failed, and they sealed the shafts rather than let surface-folk see what lived down here." He looked up. The vault-tower rose above them even now, its black glass drinking the lamplight of the market below. "Sealed things have seams."

Maren grinned—the sharp, feral grin she'd brought up from whatever gutter of the world had made her, softened only lately, only for him. "You sound like him. Halvard. All *seams* this and *pressure points* that."

"I sound like a man who's about to do something stupid."

"You sound like my brother," Maren said, and then stopped, hearing herself. The word hung between them—*brother*, freely given, unearned by blood—and Juniper felt it land somewhere the Undercourt couldn't tax.

"Then let's be stupid together," he said.

* * *

The seam Halvard remembered was behind the tannery row, where the market's smell turned to acid and salt. Juniper found it by touch alone: a line in the wall where the mortar changed age, where someone long dead had patched stone over stone. The smuggler's pry-bar he'd carried across the salt flats sang quietly against the seam, and a slab no wider than a coffin swung inward on hinges gone green with centuries.

Cold air breathed out. Old air. Air that remembered silver and sweat.

"Ladies first," Maren said, and shoved him through.

The ore road ran straight as a plumb-line into the mountain's dark, ribbed with rotted support beams. Their lamps—shuttered to slits, per smuggler custom—threw blades of light along walls glittering faintly with mineral ghost-light, the last breath of the failed veins. Juniper walked with his hand trailing the wall, counting paces the way Brother Cassian had once taught him to count candle-burns in the scriptorium. Every skill the temple had ground into him, it seemed, would serve him best in betraying everything the temple loved.

"How far?" Maren whispered.

"Halvard's marks say six hundred paces to the junction, then left, then up. The shaft climbs under the vault's foundations. There's an old ore-lift. Counterweighted." He paused. "It'll groan. Everything down here groans. That's our cover—the mountain is always complaining. No one listens anymore."

"No one listened to me either," Maren said lightly, "until I started screaming."

They climbed. The lift-shaft was a throat of rust and rope, and Juniper went first, wedging himself into the counterweight channel, finding handholds in beams that should have crumbled and didn't—ward-kept, probably, Halvard's fingerprints still on the wood after thirty years. Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone, they could hear the vault-tower humming. Not silence. A hum, deep and patient, like a hive of sleeping bees made of paper.

"Contracts snoring," Maren murmured. "That's grim."

"That's the point of a vault. Things in there aren't stored. They're *kept*. There's a difference." He thought of the archive, the moth-lanterns beating softly against their glass. "Kept things dream about getting out."

"Like us."

"Like everyone I've ever met."

The beam ended. Above Juniper's head, a hatch of lead-sheathed wood, banded in iron, and set into the iron a seal that made his eyes water to look at directly: a knot of interlocking letters, none of them his, all of them legal. Vess's mark. Her name worn as armor.

He'd prepared for this. Three nights drafting in Maren's safehouse, using the penmanship the temple had beaten into him until it was flawless, flawless, flawless. He pressed his palm flat against the seal and wrote—not with ink but with intention, tracing in his mind the counter-signature he'd composed: a clause of inspection, dry as dust, bureaucratic as scripture. *By right of audit, the undersigned requests entry for verification of holdings.* It was a lie wearing a clerk's robe. The Undercourt respected nothing so much as paperwork.

The seal considered. The seal, horribly, *read*. Juniper held still while something vast and patient skimmed the surface of his mind like a finger down a ledger column.

Then the bands loosened, and the hatch opened on a room full of light.

* * *

Vess's vault was not what smugglers' tales promised. No mountains of gold, no thrones of bone. It was a library that had decided to become a cathedral—or the reverse; down here the distinction blurred. Shelves spiraled upward into darkness, and on every shelf, lanterns. Hundreds. Thousands. Each one glowing with its own private weather: some warm as hearth-coal, some cold as the stars that had gone out, some flickering arrhythmically like hearts afraid of stopping.

Wren's was on the third gallery. Halvard had sworn it. *Third gallery, western face, between the red lamp and the blue.* And there it was: a lantern of plain glass, humble among the splendors, holding a flame the color of juniper berries and woodsmoke and a well in a lowland village where a girl had once laughed so hard she forgot the word for *bread*.

Juniper's hands shook. He made them stop.

"Talk to me," Maren breathed beside him. "What do you see?"

"Wren." His voice cracked on it. "I see Wren."

"Then take her and let's—"

The hum changed.

It was subtle—a shift in pitch, the way a room changes when someone opens a door far away—but Juniper had spent thirteen years surviving on exactly such subtleties. He froze. Maren froze. Below them, in the cathedral dark, something began to climb.

Not footsteps. *Pages.* The sound of pages turning, thousands of them, rising through the spiral shelves. And with the pages, a smell: hot wax, hot metal, the specific scorched stink of wards burning too fast.

"Cassian," Juniper whispered. He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way you know a storm by the taste of copper. "He's in the vault. He's *here*—he didn't come to burn records, he came for the master-holdings—" And then, worse, arriving whole in his head: *or for me. The temple always intended to collect both Whitlocks.*

Maren's hand closed on the lantern shelf's rail. "We take the light and we run. Now. Regret later."

He lifted Wren's lantern down.

The moment it left the shelf, every flame in the cathedral flared at once—all those sold souls, all those kept dreams, turning toward him like faces toward a shout. And the hum became a bell. Somewhere above, in the tower of black glass, an alarm that had waited decades for a reason to exist finally spoke: a single tone, pure and enormous, that Juniper felt in his marrow, in the scar tissue where the head scribe's cane had landed, in the hollow where his name was already starting to thin.

"Counterweight shaft," he said. "Go."

They ran. Behind them the pages turned faster, and a voice came down the spiral—Cassian's voice, transformed by the Undercourt's acoustics into something with too many harmonics, a zealot's voice given a choir: *"THIEF. The debt walks. The debt WALKS."*

Maren hit the hatch first and dropped through, catching the beam, swinging into the shaft like she'd been born in one. Juniper followed with the lantern hugged to his chest, its small warm light pressing through his ribs like a second heartbeat. The lead hatch slammed above them. It wouldn't hold. Nothing down here held for long; that was the mountain's whole theology.

The counterweight lift groaned under them, exactly as predicted. Groaning was cover. Groaning was nothing.

But as they descended, Juniper looked up once, through the widening gap of the receding hatch, and saw a face at the edge of the third gallery. Not Cassian. A woman in grey, standing very still amid the flare of ten thousand souls, watching him go with an expression he couldn't read at this distance—except that it wasn't rage.

It was recognition. Lady Vess, looking up at a thief carrying a light out of her vault, and seeing, perhaps, a child who once carried a light the other way.

Then Cassian's fire bloomed above, and the gallery went white, and Juniper fell into the dark with his sister's soul against his chest, running toward a moonrise that would forgive nothing.
