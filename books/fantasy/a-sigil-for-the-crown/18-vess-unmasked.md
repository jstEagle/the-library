## 18. Vess Unmasked

The vault doors had been old when Vess was young.

She remembered that with a strange, drifting clarity as she stood in the wreckage of her own sanctum, the alarm-bells still tolling somewhere above like a heart that refused to quiet. The doors were older than the Undermarket itself, hewn from the mountain's first bones, and for three hundred years nothing had opened them that did not carry her seal. Tonight a girl of perhaps fifteen years and a nameless scribe had slipped through them on roads Vess herself had ordered sealed twice over.

Old ore tunnels. Of course. She should have guessed. She *had* guessed — she had posted watchers at every mouth of them — and yet here she stood among shattered lantern-glass and drifting motes of spent light, and the Whitlock soul-lantern was gone from its cradle.

Gone, and the thief gone with it, and Halvard's borrowed years burning down in her ledger like a candle measured to the last inch.

Vess did not rage. Rage was for creatures who still believed the world could be other than it was. Instead she walked the long aisle between the vaulted shelves where the collateral of the Undercourt slept in their glass and brass — regrets coiled tight as sleeping serpents, unlived years humming faintly, names folded into paper wings — and she let the silence tell her what the bells could not.

They would run for the threshold. The thirteenth moonrise was close now; she could feel it in the stone the way an old woman feels weather in her joints. Two turns remained of the stall Halvard had purchased with his dying. Two turns for a smuggler, a child, and a scribe who did not officially exist to cross half the dark of the mountain and stand at the Court's threshold with a counter-contract in hand.

She could stop them. That was the bitter joke of it, the joke no one down here ever laughed at: she could stop them with a word. Half the market owed her. The other half feared her more than they loved their own reflections, which in the Undercourt was the deeper devotion. One word from Lady Vess and the ore roads would fill with hired knives, the gates would remember their old hungers, the very lamps would lean their flames toward the fugitives like flowers toward a sun that wanted to eat them.

And yet she found herself giving no such order.

She found herself instead descending alone to the deep gallery beneath the market, where the Court kept the one archive no one traded in: the record of things given back. It was a short shelf. In three centuries it held perhaps forty entries, each one a soul bought as a child, reclaimed by blood or by mercy, carried up into the thin air and the indifferent stars. Forty children out of thousands. She had read every entry so many times the ink might as well have been carved into her.

She stood before the shelf for a long time without reading any of it, because she did not need to. She knew what Juniper Whitlock intended. He intended to make his family forty-one.

*Fool*, she thought, and the word came out soft, almost tender, and she hated how much of herself was in it.

* * *

The Undercourt assembled at the turning of the twelfth moon, as it always did, to count what the dark had taken in.

They gathered in the Hall of Weights — a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, lit by a thousand soul-lanterns strung on chains of black iron, each one a small captive sun. The magnates sat in their crescent of seats carved from flowstone: the Broker of Years with his face like a ledger page, blank and ruled; the Twins of Debt, who spoke only in alternation and never finished each other's sentences so much as devoured them; the Gatekeeper, who had sold Vess her first toll and never quite stopped watching her afterward, as if waiting to see whether she would ever come back to pay the rest.

Vess stood at the center, as she had stood for a hundred years, creditor-in-chief, keeper of the great book. And she gave her report in a voice like poured honey over iron: the wards above were failing, the surface world stumbling blind, the harvest richer than it had been in living memory. She did not mention the missing lantern until the end, because mentioning it early would be to admit it could be missed.

"The Whitlock contract," she said. "The soul-collateral has been removed from my vault."

A stirring went through the crescent. The Broker of Years lifted one finger, and the hall quieted.

"By whom?"

"A brother," Vess said. "Come to reclaim what was sold before his birth. He intends a counter-payment." She paused, and heard herself add, against every instinct of a century: "His name."

The silence that followed had texture. Down here, where everything had a price and most things had several, the surrender of a whole identity — lineage, memory, place in the world's remembering — was not unheard of. But it was rare enough that the lanterns themselves seemed to dim and lean closer, the way crowds do at an execution.

"He cannot pay it," said one Twin.

"Or rather," said the other, "he can, but there will be nothing left to walk away."

"That is rather the nature of payment," Vess said dryly, and something that was almost laughter moved through the crescent, cold as groundwater.

It was the Gatekeeper who leaned forward, her ancient eyes finding Vess with uncomfortable precision. "You could refuse him. You could void the exchange. The claim is yours; the collateral is yours; the law bends where you hold it."

"I know what I hold," Vess said.

"Then why do you sound," the Gatekeeper said slowly, "like a woman asking permission?"

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Around them the thousand small suns burned in their cages, and in the oldest of them — the ones at the very top of the hall, bought before even the Gatekeeper's time — the light had gone strange and dim, souls worn down by centuries of being looked at and never once looked *for*.

"Because I am going to offer him a choice," Vess said, "and I want the Court to witness that I offered it fairly."

The Broker's blank face did not change, but his pen scratched. "What choice?"

Vess turned toward the great doors at the far end of the hall, beyond which the mountain's throat climbed toward the surface, toward the threshold, toward the moonrise that would settle everything.

"The same choice someone once offered me," she said. "I intend to find out if he is wiser than I was."

* * *

She caught him in the Gallery of Echoes, two turns' walking below the threshold, where the mountain's old mining ways narrowed and the walls had been carved, ages ago, with the faces of everyone the Undercourt had ever ruined. It was a corridor Vess avoided, ordinarily. Tonight it seemed fitting.

Juniper Whitlock came around the bend with Maren a step behind him and the soul-lantern wrapped in sackcloth against his chest, and when he saw her he did exactly what she expected of him: he stopped, he put his body between her and the light under the cloth, and he reached for whatever weapon a temple-trained scribe reached for, which was to say nothing at all except a certain set of the shoulders that said he would die badly before he moved aside.

"You're alone," he said. It wasn't a question. His eyes went past her, counting shadows. Smuggler's habit, or survivor's. "Where are your people?"

"Selling you out to whoever pays best, most likely," Vess said. "But I didn't bring them. I wanted to talk to you myself, one creditor to one debtor, before the law makes us something less conversational."

Maren made a small sound — half warning, half recognition — and Vess glanced at her, and the girl flinched. Yes. The girl remembered her. Vess had chosen her young, from a border village, for her quick hands and her forgettable face, and had spent years teaching her that loyalty was just another currency with a poor exchange rate. And then the girl had turned, in the firelight of the vault, and chosen a nameless scribe instead.

Children, Vess thought. They broke your heart precisely by refusing to become what you made them.

"You have it," Vess said, nodding at the bundled lantern. Inside, Wren Whitlock's soul burned the way all stolen things burn: brightly, uneasily, waiting to be either claimed or forgotten. "Cleverly done. The ore roads were closed twice. You found the third way, the one even I'd stopped watching, because the mountain forgot it and I trusted the mountain's forgetting." She smiled thinly. "That's the trouble with this place. Everything down here is for sale, including our mistakes."

"If you've come to take it back," Juniper said, "you'll need to go through me."

"Yes," Vess said. "I imagine I would. And I imagine you'd make me regret the inconvenience, at least for a moment. Halvard trained you poorly and you've learned quickly anyway — that's always the dangerous combination." She stepped closer, and the lantern-light from the walls' carved faces moved over her, and she saw him see her properly for the first time: not the creditor-in-chief of rumor, not the lady of a thousand caged suns, but simply a woman, tall and gray and precise, with eyes the color of banked fires.

"But I haven't come for the lantern," she said. "I've come to make you an offer. The last one. After this, the thirteenth moon rises and we are only law to each other."

* * *

She told it plainly, because plainly was the only honest coin left to her.

"I was sold," she said. "Like her. Like Wren Whitlock. Before the wards, before the Court had its pretty ledgers, in the bad years when the mountains ate children and called it tribute. I was six years old. My mother signed me away for a winter's grain and the promise that the debt wouldn't follow my brothers." Her voice did not waver; she had had centuries to sand the edges off it. "There was a woman then, the way there is a woman now. Creditor-in-chief. And when my turn came — when someone came down for me, blood kin, too late by half — she offered me a bargain. Walk free, poor and forgotten, into a world that had already sold me once. Or stay. Learn the weights. Learn the ledgers. Become the hand that holds the scales, so that no one ever sells you again."

Juniper had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear — she knew all its flavors — but the stillness of a man listening to his own possible future described in another woman's voice.

"I chose to stay," Vess said. "Of course I chose to stay. I was six years old and the surface had already taught me what it thought I was worth: one winter's grain. Up there I would have been a charity, a curiosity, a girl with a gap in her soul that everyone politely ignored. Down here I became inevitable. I learned every price, and then I learned the better lesson — that the one who sets the price never pays it." She spread her hands, and the rings on her fingers caught the wall-light, and for a moment she let him see them for what they were: not ornaments but trophies, each one a contract closed, a century of closing them.

"And now?" Juniper said quietly. "Do you never pay it?"

"Every day," Vess said, "and never in coin anyone can see. That is what power is, boy. A debt you owe forever to a version of yourself that stayed up in the cold and waited for someone to come." She let that sit in the echoing dark between them. Then:

"So here is my offer, and I give you my word — and my word, whatever else I am, has never once been broken — that it is freely given and fairly weighed. Take the lantern. Take the girl. Go up tonight, ahead of the Court, ahead of the moonrise. I will mark the contract *paid in part* — a part-payment releases collateral under the old law, and I am old enough to use the old law. Your sister wakes on the mountainside with her soul her own again. The sale is broken. No counter-contract. No thirteenth-turn gamble. No erasure."

"And the cost," Juniper said. There it was already, the ledger-keeper's reflex, the terrible gift his masters had beaten into him and life had refined: he heard an offer and reached instantly for the hidden column. "Nothing is released down here without cost. What's the part?"

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Behind him, Maren had gone white as salt.

"The cost," Vess said gently, "is that the wards stay dark. The cost is that the next child sold to feed them is sold to *me*. The cost is that you take your sister home to a world still going blind, and you live, and you are kind, and you are safe, and every night you look up at a sky with no stars in it and know that you could have been the man who relit them — and chose the smaller rescue because it was the one you could survive." She took a slow breath. "That is the cost. I know its exact weight. I have been paying it for four hundred years, and I am offering you the chance to owe it too, because misery down here keeps such excellent company."

The echo of her voice came back off the carved faces, off the ruined and the ransomed, and died in the dark.

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Maren shifted, long enough that somewhere far above, water found its patient way through stone.

Then Juniper Whitlock unslung the lantern from his chest, and Vess felt the old hunger rise in her — the collector's hunger, the creditor's certainty that all things flow downhill to the one who holds the book —

— and he knelt, and unwrapped the sackcloth, and checked the flame inside with the tenderness of a man checking a sleeping child, and wrapped it again, and stood, and slung it back where it had been. Against his heart. Where it had been the whole time.

"No," he said.

Vess had prepared herself for bargaining, for weeping, for hatred, for the trembling greed of a man offered safety at someone else's expense. She had not quite prepared herself for *no* said so simply, so without drama, the way one declines a second helping of bread.

"You don't understand what you're refusing," she said, and heard the edge come into her voice despite herself. "I'm offering you your sister. Whole. Alive. Free. I'm offering you the thing you crawled across a dead sky to get, gift-wrapped in my own ruin, and you're telling me *no* like I've offered to sell you a bootlace."

"I understand exactly what I'm refusing," Juniper said. "You're offering me Wren in exchange for every Wren who comes after her. You're offering me the deal that made you." He met her eyes, and his were not hard; that was the unbearable part. They were tired, and kind, and absolutely clear. "You said the woman who ran this place before you offered you a choice. Do you know what I think? I think she offered it because someone once offered it to her, and she took it, and she's been handing it down the line ever since — each of us selling the next one the same winter's grain. Somebody has to drop the coin. Somebody has to say the chain ends with them." He touched the lantern, lightly, through the cloth. "I'm not brave, Lady Vess. I'm a copyist. I copy things. And what I've learned, copying prayers for ten years, is that the words only mean anything when they cost the scribe something. So. No."

"*Fool*," Vess said — and this time it did not come out soft. This time it came out with four hundred years behind it, with the Gallery of Echoes drinking it in and throwing it back in the voices of the ruined faces on the walls, and for one naked moment she was not the creditor-in-chief of the Undercourt but a six-year-old girl standing at the bottom of a stair, listening to her own mother's footsteps recede above her, choosing, always choosing, the warm dark over the cold light because the cold light had already failed her once.

Maren stepped forward then, and put herself squarely between them, small and stubborn, and Vess looked at the girl she had made and understood — finally, absurdly, at the very end of it — that she was looking at the only thing she had ever successfully given away.

"You should go," Maren said quietly. To Vess. To her maker, her owner, her mirror. "Both turns are nearly spent. He's going to beat you. Let it at least be clean."

For a moment longer Vess stood in the echoing gallery, in the sight of all the faces the mountain remembered ruining, and something moved behind her eyes that in a surface creature might have been called grief, and in her could only be called inventory — the final tallying of a debt so old the original creditor was dust.

"Pity," she said at last, straightening, the mask sliding home, the lady of the thousand caged suns looking out again from behind the banked-fire eyes. "That is what I feel for you, Juniper Whitlock. Not anger. Pity. Because you're about to learn the last secret of this place, the one they don't carve on the walls." She turned away, her skirts whispering against the stone like a ledger's pages turning. "The mountain doesn't care what you pay. Name, soul, love, years — it takes everything equally and remembers none of it. You're going to give it everything you are, and it will not so much as note your handwriting in its margins. At least when I sold myself, I got to remain to regret it."

She walked away down the Gallery of Echoes, and the carved faces watched her go, and she did not look back — she had stopped looking back four hundred years ago, and had almost succeeded in forgetting why she'd started.

But at the bend of the corridor she paused, one hand on the stone, and spoke without turning, and her voice was level and cold and perfectly audible:

"The Court convenes at the threshold on the thirteenth moonrise, and I will stand in my place and do my duty, and if your counter-contract is one ounce light, I will weigh it and reject it and take them both — you and her. Bring your full weight, scribe. Bring everything." A pause, thin as wire. "And pray the mountain finds you worth the ink."

Then she was gone into the dark, and the echoes settled, and high overhead — impossibly far, impossibly close — the last turn began to run.
