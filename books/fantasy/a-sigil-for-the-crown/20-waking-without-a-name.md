## 20. Waking Without a Name

The stars came back the night Wren Whitlock stopped being able to remember her brother.

She was standing at the well when it happened—standing there with a bucket she had no memory of carrying down, in a village that had spent thirteen days under a sky like a shuttered window. The whole of Thornfell had lived those days in a strange, flattened dusk. Candles were hoarded. Children were kept indoors. The temple's bell rang the hours with a sound that seemed to fall dead out of the air, as if even sound needed light to travel by.

And then, just past moonrise—or where moonrise should have been, for the moon itself was a rumor now, a thin absence rolling through the dark—one star kindled above the eastern ridge. Then another. Then a dozen, and a hundred, until the sky tore open like a sack of grain and poured itself out across the world, and the whole village came stumbling from their doorways weeping and shouting and falling to their knees in the frozen mud.

Wren did not kneel. Wren stood at the well with an empty bucket and felt something leave her.

It did not hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like setting down a load she hadn't known she was carrying—a bundle set gently on the ground behind her, a debt forgiven—and she should have felt light. She should have felt free. Instead she stood in the sudden silver wash of the returned stars and grieved, hugely and without object, the way one grieves in dreams: knowing only that something beloved had been here, in her arms, and was gone, and that the shape of its absence was the exact shape of something she could not name.

She said, aloud, to no one: *Juniper.*

The word came out strange. Half-remembered. Like the first note of a song whose rest had been lost.

She frowned at herself. The well water shivered in the dark below, reflecting stars it had not seen in nearly two weeks. She looked down into it and saw her own face, pale and thin and older than twenty-two, and beneath her face, faintly, as though the water remembered what she did not—the suggestion of a second reflection standing close beside hers. A shoulder almost touching her shoulder. When she blinked, it was gone.

*Juniper,* she thought again, and this time the name meant nothing at all, and she began, quietly and helplessly, to cry.

* * *

In the morning the village remembered how to be a village.

Doors opened. Bread was baked. The temple of Thornfell rang its bells with something almost like joy, and old Mother Ilsa swept her step and told anyone who'd listen that she'd known the stars would come back, she'd *said* so, hadn't she told them all—

Wren went about the business of living as though her hands knew how, because they did. Feed the hens. Draw the water. Mend the fence wire where the frost had heaved it. Her body moved through its rounds with a competence that surprised her; there was money hidden in the crook of the chimney she didn't remember hiding, coins wrapped in oilcloth, enough to carry her through a hard month, and she stood holding them for a long time trying to understand why her chest ached.

There was a second bowl in her cupboard. She noticed it on the third day.

A chipped clay bowl, glazed brown, with a hairline crack running down one side—the sort of thing a person keeps because throwing it away would be admitting something. She held it under the pump and washed it and dried it and put it back on the shelf, and could not tell herself why, and did it anyway. There was a coat on the peg by the door that fit no one in the house. Too long in the sleeve. She wore it sometimes, when she walked out past the fields toward the mountains, which she found herself doing more and more often, though she had never been a walker, never been drawn to the high cold country, never—

Except that she had. She was almost sure she had. Someone had taught her the names of the passes. Someone had shown her which lichens burned clean and which smoked foul, someone had laughed at her over a fire and stolen the last of the honey-bread off her plate and—

She sat down on a stone in the shadow of the foothills and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw sparks.

"Someone," she whispered. "There was someone."

But the mountain wind came down and took the words, and the stars wheeled overhead, indifferent and beautiful, burning again as if they had never once gone out.

* * *

The strangers came a week later: a girl of perhaps fourteen with ash-grey eyes and a way of moving that made the dogs of the village go silent instead of barking, and a man who limped and leaned on a staff of black rowan and looked at everything—walls, wells, children—as if reading fine print written on them all.

They came asking questions, or rather the girl asked questions while the man watched, and the questions were odd ones.

"Has anyone come through," the girl said, sitting uninvited at Wren's table, turning Wren's salt cellar in her fingers as if checking whether it was real, "that nobody remembers? Anyone who might have passed through and left no mark? A name missing from a ledger. A place set for a guest who never arrived."

"No one," Wren said. "We've had no travelers since the sky came back. The roads are—" She paused. Why did she know the state of the roads? Why did she know that the high pass was closed with rockfall two miles up, that the smuggler trails along the ridge were passable but cold? "I don't know why I know that," she said slowly. "I've never traveled."

The grey-eyed girl and the limping man exchanged a look so long and loaded that Wren's skin prickled.

"You're Maren," Wren said suddenly. "And you're—" The name rose in her like a bubble through deep water. "*Halvard.* You're Halvard, the ward-keeper. You were disgraced. You—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking. "Why do I know you? I have never seen either of you in my life."

"You have," Maren said softly.

"I haven't."

"You have," Maren said again, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and for a moment she looked much younger than fourteen, much more tired, as though she had been walking a very long road carrying something heavy and had finally set it down in front of the wrong person's table. "You have, Wren. You just can't hold on to it. Nobody can. That was the price. That was how it was paid." She swallowed. "He paid it for you."

"Who?" Wren's voice came out very small. "Who paid *what*?"

Halvard lowered himself onto her bench with the groan of a man whose bones had opinions, and laid his black staff across his knees, and looked at her with eyes that held an entire ruined career's worth of guilt.

"The Undercourt sold your soul," he said, "and your brother bought it back. And the price the court demanded—the price equal to a soul, freely given, blood kin, sealed before witnesses—was his name."

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. Outside, a hen complained about the cold.

"My brother," Wren repeated.

"You had a brother," Halvard said. "Juniper. He copied prayers in the Thornfell temple by day and guided smugglers by night, and he carried your family's debts like a stone around his neck, and he loved you so stubbornly that he crossed the darkened mountains and walked into the court beneath the world and gave away every piece of himself that made him himself, so that you could sit here this morning eating bread under a sky full of stars." His voice roughened. "He signed the counter-contract on the thirteenth moonrise. I watched him do it. The mountain took his name out of the world even as he spoke it. By the time he reached the surface, the records in the temple were blank where he'd been written, the ledgers had no line for him, and the debt-marks on his hands were gone. Every trace. He is alive. He walked down out of the hills three nights ago. But no one remembers that Juniper Whitlock ever existed."

"Then—" Wren's mind grasped at it, slipped, grasped again. "Then where is he? If he's alive, where—"

"He's in the village," Maren said. "He's been in the village the whole time. Staying at the inn under the name 'Corren.' Working the forge because Brenn threw his back out. He helped rebuild the east fence yesterday. You thanked him for it."

Wren stared at her.

"You *talked* to him," Maren said, and now her grey eyes were wet. "Yesterday. At the fence. He fixed the post you couldn't get straight and you thanked him and offered him cider and he said—" Her voice broke entirely. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "He said he'd like that. He said you seemed kind. He doesn't know why he wanted to stay in this village. He doesn't know why the well draws him, or why he keeps almost writing certain names and stopping, or why he woke up crying the first night and couldn't say about what. The mountain left him nothing. Except—" She reached across the table and took Wren's wrist, turned it over, and pushed back her sleeve.

On the inside of Wren's forearm, faint as a scar left by a childhood burn, was a small inked sigil: a circle broken by a single descending line, the mark of a debt discharged. She had noticed it days ago and assumed she'd always had it.

"You both have it," Halvard said quietly, showing his own wrist, where an older, faded version of the same mark sat among the other scars. "Every soul the Undercourt has ever released carries the discharge-mark. His will fade in time, as mine has. Yours already has nearly. But his—" He hesitated. "His is still dark. Fresh. Because he paid it himself, in full, with everything he had."

Wren looked at the sigil and understood, all at once and incompletely, the way one understands thunder after lightning—that the grief she had been carrying since the stars returned had a name, and the name had been taken out of her mouth, and the hole it left was exactly the shape of her brother.

"I want to see him," she said.

"Wren—" Halvard began.

"I want to *see* him."

"He wouldn't know you," Maren said miserably. "That's the worst part. We tried. Corren—Juniper—he looks at you like you're a friendly stranger. He likes you. He said you reminded him of someone he couldn't place and then he laughed like it was a joke, but he wasn't laughing, Wren. He goes to the well every evening. He stands there and stares into the water and doesn't know why. Last night he said, *I keep feeling like I'm forgetting something important, except I'm already forgotten, so what's left to forget?*" She wiped her face fiercely. "Half of me is underworld-born. Court-touched. That's the only reason any thread of him holds in me at all. You're surface-true, clean-blooded. Whatever the mountain took, it took completely."

Wren stood up. The chipped brown bowl sat on its shelf behind her, waiting for a person who would never come to eat from it, and she looked at it, and something in her set itself like bone.

"Then we'll give it back to him," she said.

Halvard's eyebrows rose. "Child, the contract is sealed. Witnessed by the assembled court. What's given to the Undercourt does not—"

"I don't care what the Undercourt does." She was surprised by her own voice—hard and bright, a blade she hadn't known she owned. "Thirteen years ago my parents sold my soul to save my brother, and everyone let him believe the debt was his, and he carried it alone his whole life, and then he paid it with his *name*, and you're telling me that's simply the end of it? That we all stand around being grateful?" She looked between them—the disgraced ward-keeper and the half-court girl with tears drying on her cheeks—and saw, in the way neither of them looked away, that they had already been thinking it too. "Stars came back. Stars *can come back*. Things that were lost can be lost differently." She pulled the too-long coat from its peg and shrugged it on; the sleeves hung past her fingertips, and she did not roll them up. "Tell me everything. From the beginning. Tell me about the bargain, and the wards, and Lady Vess, and the archive of moths, and the Gatekeeper and her tolls. Tell me every term of every contract ever spoken in that mountain. Because somewhere in all that fine print, there is a loophole, and I am going to find it, and I am going to walk into the Undercourt and pay whatever it costs to buy my brother back—his name, his memories, his place in the world's ledger—all of it, returned."

"And what will you pay?" Halvard asked quietly. "Equal weight, Wren. It's always equal weight. That's the whole cruelty of the place."

Wren looked down at her own wrist, at the fading sigil of a debt discharged, at the life that had been purchased for her at a price she was only beginning to count.

"Whatever's equal," she said. "I'll find out what I weigh."

Outside, far down the lane, a broad-shouldered stranger with ink-stained fingers and no history was helping Brenn's boy carry firewood, and pausing, as he did every evening now, at the well—looking down into the starlit water at a second reflection that was never quite there, grieving without a reason, waiting without knowing for what.

Maren followed Wren's gaze and smiled through the last of her tears.

"Start with the Gatekeeper," she said. "She takes stories. And Wren—I think yours might be worth more than you know. He gave away his memories of you to pass her gate." Her ash-grey eyes gleamed. "Which means the only complete telling of your childhood together is locked inside the mountain. And stories, freely given—" she tapped the table, once, like a knock on a door, "—are the one coin the Undercourt cannot mint itself. It has to *buy* them."

Wren picked up the chipped brown bowl and tucked it, deliberately, into her satchel.

"Then we'd better make mine expensive," she said, and stepped out into the starlight, toward the mountain, toward the well, toward the brother the world had agreed to forget.
