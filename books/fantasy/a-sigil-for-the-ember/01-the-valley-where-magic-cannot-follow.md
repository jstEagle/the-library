## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The bees woke before Elodie did, as they always did, humming their low gold hymn through the walls of the cottage — a sound she had come to think of as the valley's true heartbeat. Not the dragon's slow breathing from the crater in the north, which some nights shook the teacups in their saucers and which she had learned to sleep through the way one sleeps through surf. The bees were better than surf. The bees asked nothing of her except smoke and patience, and neither of those had ever burned down a village.

She dressed in the dark by feel: wool stockings darned at both knees, the gray smock with the veil-cloth sewn into the hood, her mother's old knife on her belt because a decade of habit dies harder than any god. Outside, the Hollow Valley lay in that particular blue-black stillness it kept for the hour before dawn, when the mist came down off the rim cliffs like something being poured, and the world was small enough to hold in two hands.

Ten years. She counted them the way other people counted coins or grudges — not out of bitterness, exactly, but because the counting was the only ritual exile had left her. Ten years since the trial. Ten years since Maren stood up in the white courtroom at Caelmere's heart and said, in a voice like a blade being laid down flat on a table, *I saw her call it.* Ten years since the wardens had walked her to the valley rim and the ward had closed behind her with a sound she still heard sometimes in dreams — not thunder, though everyone expected thunder from her; a soft wet click, like a door latching in a house where no one was left awake.

Magic could not follow her here. That was the sentence's mercy and its cruelty braided into one rope. No storm-calling, no weather-sense, none of the Eastwick gift that had made her family's name for nine generations and unmade hers in an afternoon. The Hollow Valley drank magic the way sand drank rain. Whatever she had been — storm-daughter, caller, the girl whose temper once turned the sky black over three parishes — had been left at the rim like a coat too heavy to carry.

What remained was a woman who kept bees.

She walked the path down to the hives with the lantern hooded, because bees disliked sudden light almost as much as they disliked sudden grief, and Elodie had learned to move through mornings as if the whole world were a hive she might disturb. The grass was cold and wet through her boots. Somewhere upslope, an owl said its one word and thought better of it.

There were six hives now, ranged along the south-facing bank below the cottage, each one painted a different faded color because Wren — no. Not Wren. Because *she* had painted them, in her first year, when her hands needed work more desperate than her heart needed quiet, and she had chosen colors from a memory of Caelmere's market stalls: madder-red, woad-blue, a yellow so pale it was nearly an apology. The hives hummed. She pressed her ear to the third one, the red one, the temperamental one, and listened to the sound of ten thousand small creatures agreeing to be a single thing.

*That,* she thought, as she did most mornings, *is the only kind of power I want anymore.*

It was a lie she had gotten very good at telling. The kind of lie that smooths its collar and shows up to work every day and almost believes itself by noon.

* * *

The morning's work was ordinary, which was the whole point of mornings here. She fed the weak colony sugar-water laced with chamomile. She scraped propolis off her gloves and smelled it on her fingers all day afterward, resinous and dark, the smell of things sealed shut. She checked the comb the red hive had built crooked against its frame — bees always built crooked in this valley, and she'd stopped correcting them years ago; there was something in the geometry of the place that even insects noticed, a wrongness in the angles, as if the land itself had been folded up carelessly and put away.

That was the ward. She knew that now, though it had taken her five years to understand what the wardens' maps had shown and their mouths had never said. The Hollow Valley wasn't a valley the way other valleys were valleys. It was a pocket, a fold pressed into the living land by hands that had known exactly what they were doing. Nothing magical entered. Nothing magical left. Rain fell downward. Rivers ran downhill. Birds migrated in sensible seasons. And at the center-north, sunk into the earth like a coin dropped into bread dough, slept the last dragon in the world, breathing so slowly that his exhalations came once every quarter hour and fogged the whole northern meadow.

Vaskar. Even the name felt like handling a hot coal with bare fingers. She avoided the crater. Everyone in the valley's scattered homesteads avoided the crater — the few dozen exiles and eccentrics and debt-fleers who shared this pocket of nowhere with her, farming its stubborn soil and speaking in lowered voices about the mountain-that-breathed. Elodie avoided it more thoroughly than most, because the first month of her exile, nineteen years old and furious and drowning in the silence where her power used to live, she had marched up to the crater's rim intending — she had never fully admitted intending *what* — and had looked down into the dark at the vast coiled shape of him, ember-light guttering along his ribs like a fire one gust from dead, and he had opened one eye the size of a cartwheel and looked at her.

He hadn't spoken. Dragons, she'd learned later from Tam the fisher-heretic, spoke perhaps once a decade and considered conversation a form of expenditure. He had simply looked at her, and in that look she had read something so old and so tired and so precisely, devastatingly familiar — the look of a thing waiting to end — that she had walked back down the slope and sat in the grass and cried until dark, and had never gone back.

Ten years of not going back. She was very good at it. She could walk the northern fence line and keep her eyes on her boots the entire time, could hear his breathing roll across the meadows like distant tide and feel nothing but the ordinary dread any farmer feels for the enormous sleeping thing that shares her valley.

Almost nothing. Almost ordinary dread.

* * *

It was midmorning, the sun finally burning the mist off the low fields, when she saw the rain fall upward.

Later — in the terrible weeks that followed, in the long road and the drowned cathedral and everything after — she would return to this moment again and again, trying to reconstruct exactly what she had seen and in what order, and the memory would always come apart at the same seam, the way a dream does when you try to carry it into daylight. But the bones of it were these:

She was at the far southern fence, mending a gap where a badger had pushed through, when the light changed. Not dimmed — *changed*, the way light changes when something enormous passes between you and the sun, except nothing passed; the sky was high and hard and blue. She straightened, hammer in hand, and looked north, because everything in her looked north first, always, a compass needle she couldn't pull out.

Over the crater, over the northern rim where the valley met the cliff-wall and the cliff-wall met the sky, it was raining.

That alone would have been strange enough. It was late summer, dry as tinder; the last rain had been six weeks ago and had fallen, as all rain fell here, in a sober vertical line, in and down, obeying. But this rain — she could see it glittering, a bright curtain hanging off the rim cliffs like a veil off a bride — this rain was falling *up*. Droplets lifting off the grass of the high meadow, off the standing stones, off the black lip of the crater itself, rising in long silver threads into the sky and vanishing. Falling upward. Water remembering, perhaps, some older law. Water being *called*.

Elodie stood very still. The hammer hung loose in her hand. A bee landed on her shoulder, investigated the wool, found nothing worth reporting, and left.

Here is what she felt, and she would spend a long time refusing to rank the feelings, because ranking them meant admitting which one won: fear, first, cold and clean as well-water. Then — and this was the shameful one, the one she kept in the locked room behind her ribs where she kept all the shameful ones — *longing*. Because upward-falling rain was impossible in the Hollow Valley, and impossibility here had only one meaning, and the meaning was that somewhere beyond the rim, in the wide world she had been carved out of, magic was moving again. Magic was close enough to lean against the ward. Magic was knocking.

And under the longing, under even the fear, a third thing, quiet as an egg under a brooding hen: *the outside world has found me.*

She told herself, walking fast back toward the cottage, that it might mean nothing. Weather systems broke. Storms wandered. Perhaps some freak cell had parked itself over the rim cliffs beyond the ward, outside the valley proper, and she was seeing only its edge, distorted by the fold in the land. People saw impossible things at the edges of places. Sailors saw sea-serpents; shepherds saw lights. The eye wanted patterns the way the lonely wanted letters.

But the rain kept falling upward. All afternoon, while she pretended to sort frames and clean the settling tank, the bright curtain hung off the northern rim, threads of silver climbing into a sky that gave nothing back. And twice — twice — she felt it. Felt it in her teeth, in the old scar-tissue where her power used to live, a sensation like a tuning fork struck against a bone: a faint, rhythmic pressure against the inside of her skull, patient and vast, the way water presses against a dam. Not attacking the ward. Testing it. The way you press your thumb against ice to learn how long winter intends to last.

By evening she had a name for the feeling, and the name frightened her more than the rain did.

*A crack.*

* * *

Tam Osgar came at dusk, as he came every seventh day, carrying smoked trout and gossip in roughly equal quantities. He was seventy if he was a day, a former seminarian who'd fled some scandal in a city he refused to name, and he was the closest thing the valley had to a neighbor, a priest, and a friend, which is to say he was irritating, wise, and loyal in unequal measure.

"Rain," he said, standing in her doorway with the fish wrapped in cloth, nodding north without looking at it, the way people in the valley nodded at the crater — acknowledging the impossible without granting it the dignity of attention. "Falling the wrong direction. Saw it from the ridge."

"You saw it too." Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

"Whole eastern homesteads saw it. Old Marta says it's the dragon dreaming." He set the fish on her table, uninvited, permanent. "Marta says a lot of things. Marta also says the wardens put a curse on her turnips in '41, so I weight her opinions accordingly." He lowered himself into her second chair, which creaked its annual complaint. "You're the one who'd know, though, wouldn't you. What it means. When the sky starts behaving like the sky doesn't."

"I don't know anything anymore, Tam. That was rather the point of the arrangement."

"Mm." He watched her with eyes gone pale and shrewd as frost. "You've got your knife on indoors. You never wear your knife indoors unless the bees have upset you or the world has. Bees are calm. I heard them from the gate. Singing like it's a festival day."

Elodie sat down across from him. For a while neither spoke. The fire ticked. Far to the north, so faint it might have been imagination, the ground breathed — Vaskar's long exhale rolling out over the meadows, fogging the starlight.

"Something's pressing on the ward," she said at last. It cost her something to say it aloud; saying it made it real, and real things required decisions, and decisions were how a person ended up on a scaffold. "From outside. Gently. Like someone testing a door."

"Who'd test a door to the Hollow Valley? We're nobody, girl. This is where the kingdom puts the things it wants to forget." He paused, and she watched the thought arrive in his face the way weather arrives on a hilltop. "Unless," he said slowly, "the kingdom has started wanting its forgotten things back."

"The gods are dead," she said. "That's what they taught us. The old gods died in the God-War, the god-emperor wears what's left of divinity like a stolen coat, and there are no miracles, only weather, and weather has laws." She recited it in the cadence of the schoolroom, because the cadence was armor. "I believe it. I have to believe it. If the gods can answer prayers, then prayers can be *answered wrong*, and then—"

"Then forty people burn in a church square on a cloudless day," Tam finished quietly, "and a nineteen-year-old girl takes the blame, because someone has to, and the empire cannot very well arrest the sky."

The words landed in the room like dropped glass. Neither moved to sweep them up.

"That's not what happened," Elodie said. It was the sentence she had said ten thousand times in ten years, to judges and wardens and the dark ceiling of her cottage at 3 a.m., and it had never once sounded convincing, least of all to her. "I didn't call that storm."

"No," Tam agreed mildly. "Though I notice you've never once managed to say what *did*."

"Because I don't know!" It came out louder than she meant, and the bees outside, hearing it through the wall, shifted their hum a half-tone higher, and she breathed until they settled. "Because I was there, and my hands were up, and the sky came apart, and I remember *wanting* — I remember being angry enough to want it — and I have spent ten years not knowing whether I'm a woman who was wrongly condemned or a monster who got a light sentence. Do you know what that does to a person? Not knowing? It's worse than either. Either one you could *do* something with."

Tam was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its teasing edge entirely.

"And now the sky over the rim is running backward," he said, "and something is leaning on the door of the world, and you're sitting here telling me the gods are dead." He rose, joints crackling like green wood, and gathered his hat. At the door he paused, silhouetted against the last violet light, and said, not unkindly:

"The rain's getting closer, girl. Marta timed it. Each hour, the edge of it comes a mile south. By tomorrow nightfall it'll be over your hives." He settled the hat on his head. "Whatever's testing the door — I'd decide soon whether you're going to answer it. Doors don't stay tested forever. They open, or they hold. And either way, the person who decides usually isn't the person leaning."

He let himself out. She listened to his footsteps fade down the path, uneven and unhurried, a man who had already survived whatever the world could throw and intended to meet the rest of it at a stroll.

* * *

She did not sleep. She tried — banked the fire, lay down, performed the whole liturgy of rest — but the pressure against her skull had grown teeth in the night hours, a slow rhythmic gnawing at the edges of the ward, and around midnight the rain reached the northern meadows. She stood at her window and watched it fall upward over Vaskar's crater, silver threads climbing out of the dark, and beneath the rain she could see — or believed she could see, and belief was a country she'd sworn off entering — a glow. Ember-light. Deep in the crater, along the vast sleeping ribs, the old dragon's fire flickering in a rhythm that no longer matched his breathing.

Quickening. Or guttering. From this distance, in this light, she couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Toward dawn, exhausted past the reach of fear, she dreamed — the first dream in ten years that wasn't the trial. In it she stood in her mother's apiary in Caelmere, before the exile, before everything, and the hives were singing a wordless song, and her mother's hands were in the frame beside hers, steady, honey-golden, and her mother said — in the voice of someone stating a fact about the weather —

*When they come for you, girl, and they will come, remember: the storm was never yours. You were only the tallest tree in the field.*

She woke with tears drying on her face and the smell of ozone in the room, sharp and green and electric, the smell of a sky deciding. It had no business being in a sealed cottage in a valley where magic could not follow. It had no business anywhere.

Outside, the eastern horizon had gone the color of a bruise, and the rain — the impossible, climbing rain — had crossed the northern meadows in the night and now stood in a glittering wall half a mile from her hives, hanging off the air like a curtain in a doorway, and through it, faint but unmistakable, carried on no wind she could feel, came a sound she had not heard in ten years and had spent those ten years pretending she'd forgotten.

Bells. A bronze bell, rung once, deep and resonant, humming like a struck sigil.

Someone was coming down out of the broken sky toward her door.
