## 2. A Prayer Answered by Something Old

The rain came on the third day after the sky broke over the rim, and it did not fall so much as arrive — all at once, everywhere, the way a verdict arrives.

Elodie Eastwick was in the east meadow with her hands buried to the wrist in a hive when she felt it: a pressure change, a taste of iron at the back of her throat, the particular hush that falls over the world a half-second before water. Ten years of exile had not dulled that sense. Nothing dulled it. It lived in her like a splinter too deep to draw, and it told her, plainly and without mercy, that rain was coming.

Not valley rain. Valley rain was polite. It drifted down from the Hollow's low grey ceiling in fine veils, barely enough to keep the heather honest, and it never — *never* — fell upward first.

She pulled her hands free, left the frame half-inspected, and stood in the meadow grass with her heart going hard against her ribs. Above her, the clouds that were not clouds — the permanent ceiling of the ward, that soft pearl-grey dome that had hung over the valley for ten years like a lid on a pot — were moving. Churning. Darkening from the center outward, as though something beneath them had reached up and stirred them with a finger.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, because there was never anyone to say things to. "No, you don't."

The first drop hit her knuckle and she flinched as if struck.

It was warm. That was the wrongness of it, the thing that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world. Rain should be cold, or at least cool, or at the very least indifferent. This was warm as bathwater, warm as blood left standing, and when she caught a second drop on her tongue it tasted of salt.

Salt. In the Hollow Valley, three hundred miles from any coast, under a sky that magic could not touch.

She ran.

* * *

The village of Ember's Rest — forty souls, one shrine, one well, and one girl who talked to bees — sat in the crook of the valley where the river Mirel slowed itself down to think. Elodie had learned its rhythms the way she'd once learned the rhythms of storms: which gutters flooded first, which doors needed propping, which families brought their animals up to the high pasture at the first smell of wet stone. She had spent ten years learning to be useful in small ways precisely because she could not be trusted with large ones.

She came down the hill road at a dead run, skirts hauled up, hair whipping loose from its braid, and saw the river before she saw anything else.

The Mirel had risen. Not crept — risen, all at once, the way a sleeper sits bolt upright. Brown water that had been ankle-deep over its gravel beds yesterday now lapped at the top of the stone bridge, and it was moving wrong. Rivers moved downstream. This water moved in slow spirals, great lazy coils of current turning against the grain, as if the river remembered being something else and was trying to remember harder.

And the water was rising still. She could see it climb — actually see it, a handspan, another — up the bridge stones, silent, without spray or sound, the way water rises in a glass.

"Tam!" she shouted, hammering on the first door. "Tam Broadhurst, get your family up, the river's—" But Tam was already out, already waist-deep in his own yard hauling a bleating goat toward higher ground, and beyond him the whole village was waking to the same wrongness. Doors opening. Voices. A child crying somewhere, thin and high.

Elodie stood in the middle of the lane with rain running warm down her face and did the arithmetic of disaster the way she'd been taught to do it long ago, by people who were mostly dead now. The river would take the low houses first. The mill. The Widow Cetch's cottage, set so close to the bank you could fish from her window. And the shrine — the little stone shrine by the ford where the village children went to whisper at the old gods nobody believed in anymore, because believing in things was dangerous and the empire had made sure everyone knew it.

The shrine where children prayed.

She was moving before the thought finished forming.

* * *

She found Wren knee-deep at the ford.

The girl was nine years old, orphaned twice over — once by fever, once by the empire's habit of making orphans of anyone whose parents asked questions — and she had adopted Elodie with the ruthless, unappeasable devotion of her kind. Elodie had spent two years trying to keep the child at arm's length and had failed utterly, and knew it, and pretended not to.

Wren was standing in the floodwater with her arms spread wide and her face turned up into the warm salt rain, and she was laughing.

"Wren!" Elodie's voice cracked across the water. "Wren, get out of there!"

"I prayed!" The girl spun, arms still wide, and Elodie saw that the water around her was glowing — faintly, phosphorescent, the way deep ocean water glows, green-blue light curling off Wren's ankles in ribbons. "I prayed at the shrine, I prayed for rain for the barley, everybody said the gods were dead but I prayed anyway, and they *answered*, Elodie, they answered *me*—"

The river took a step.

There was no other word for it. The whole body of the flood surged forward at once, upstream, against every law Elodie had ever broken or obeyed, and it rose as it came — waist-high, chest-high, a wall of brown water threaded with that impossible sea-light — and it gathered behind Wren like a hand cupping a candleflame it meant to snuff.

Later, Elodie would try to reconstruct what happened next and fail. There was no decision in it. There was no thought. There was only the oldest reflex she owned, older than exile, older than guilt: *the water is rising and someone is in it.*

She hit the flood at a run.

The cold — no, not cold, warm, horribly warm, warm as a drowned man's bath — closed over her thighs, her waist, and the current seized her like a living thing. Because it was a living thing. She could feel it thinking. She could feel it *remembering* — vast, slow memories pressing against her skin through the water, memories of trenches and whales and the black mile-down silence where light has never been. This water had been ocean once. Every drop of it. It had been called home into a riverbed too small for it, into a valley warded shut against exactly this, and it was homesick, and it was grieving, and it wanted, with the whole dumb enormous want of the sea, to be deep again.

And underneath all of that, threaded through it like wire through wax:

*Answered. Answered. Answered.*

A prayer, being answered. Still being answered. Somewhere above the valley, past the cracked ward, something old and starving had heard a nine-year-old ask for rain and had poured out an ocean in reply, because the dying grasp, and the drowning pull, and gods who have not been listened to in a hundred years do not know how to give small gifts.

"Take my hand!" Elodie got an arm around Wren's chest and the girl clung to her, all elbows and terror now, the laughter gone as fast as it had come. The water was at Elodie's collarbones. The spiral current dragged at her legs like hands. Downstream — no, *upstream*, everything was upstream now — the mill bridge loomed, and the flood was carrying them both toward the gap beneath it, a gap that was filling with churning light.

She planted her feet on something — a fencepost, a saint, a miracle — and pushed.

And her power woke.

* * *

It had been ten years. She had counted every one of them the way prisoners count, which is to say obsessively and in secret and always in the dark. Ten years since the storm. Ten years since forty names read out over forty coffins while she stood in the rain in chains and Maren Voss, her best friend since they were seven years old, testified in a clear steady voice about what she had seen Elodie do.

Ten years of nothing. Of numbness. Of reaching inside herself and finding the room where her magic had lived swept bare, the hearth cold, the windows painted over. Exile in the Hollow Valley was supposed to work that way — the ward didn't just keep magic out, it kept it *asleep*, and she had told herself for a decade that the silence in her chest was peace.

It was not peace. It had never been peace. It was a held breath.

Now, with salt water in her mouth and a child's heartbeat hammering against her own, the breath came out.

It started in her palms. A tingling, then an ache, then a *pull* — the air around her thickening, leaning toward her, every drop of suspended rain suddenly aware of her the way iron filings are aware of a magnet. She felt the storm overhead turn its attention downward like a vast eye opening. She felt the flood hesitate, the spiral currents stuttering, the whole remembering ocean pausing mid-grief to look at *her*.

Because it knew her. Water always knew her. Storm-calling was in her blood four generations deep, and the rain had missed her the way a dog misses its person.

*Call us*, said the water, without words, without sound, in the language she had sworn off forever. *Call us and we will part. Call us and we will carry you both to the hills on our backs. You remember how. You have always known how. One word. One word, storm-child.*

For one heartbeat — one single, perfect, terrible heartbeat — she wanted it.

Not the rescue. The rescue she could have refused. She wanted the *calling*. She wanted the feeling that came with it, the feeling she had spent ten years refusing to name even in the privacy of her own skull: that moment when the sky leaned down and listened to *her*, when the most enormous and indifferent forces in the world bent themselves around her will like iron around a lodestone, when she was, for as long as the storm lasted, not a small exiled woman with chapped hands and bee stings, but the axis on which the weather turned. She had told herself she missed it the way one misses a lost limb. That was a lie. She missed it the way one misses a lover who ruined her life. She missed it *specifically*, in detail, at night, with her whole traitorous body.

Her lips parted. Her lungs filled. Somewhere below her sternum, in the swept-bare room, in the cold hearth, an ember that was not an ember began, very quietly, to glow.

One word. She could feel the word assembling itself behind her teeth, a word in no human language, a word made of pressure and lightning. The flood waited for it, quivering, obedient, adoring. The storm above the valley waited for it. Ten years of silence waited for it, and every night of that decade she had told herself the silence was imposed, was the ward's doing, was exile's chains — and here, now, in the water, she understood with sickening clarity that the silence had also been hers. That some part of her had kept the door barred from the inside. And that part of her was, right now, throwing the bar aside with both hands.

"Elodie." Wren's voice, tiny, against her ear. The girl's fingers were digging into her shoulders hard enough to bruise. "Elodie, your eyes are doing the thing."

The thing. She didn't know what the thing looked like. She'd never seen it herself. But she had seen it in mirrors once, long ago, in another life: eyes gone silver as hail, lit from behind.

Elodie Eastwick bit down on the word until she tasted blood.

And then she did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the trial, harder than the wagon ride into the valley, harder than ten years of mornings: she picked up a child and walked out of a miracle without answering it.

* * *

She got them to the high ground of the churchyard on legs that shook, through water that let them go grudgingly, sullenly, the spiral currents plucking at their ankles all the way like fingers reluctant to release a toy. Behind them the flood kept rising, kept coiling, kept glowing its homesick ocean-green, and the warm salt rain kept falling, and over the valley rim — she saw it clearly, once, between the churn of cloud — something vast turned over in the sky like a sleeper disturbed by a name.

The village gathered on the hill. Tam Broadhurst put a blanket around Elodie's shoulders and she couldn't stop shaking long enough to be ashamed of it. Wren wrapped herself around Elodie's arm and would not be pried loose, and Elodie found, to her horror, that she was holding on just as tightly.

"You went in after me," Wren said. "Nobody goes in after anybody."

"Somebody has to," Elodie said. Her voice came out rough. "That's the whole job of somebody."

"The water glowed where you touched it." The girl looked up at her with the terrible directness of children, of orphans, of people who have nothing left to lose by seeing clearly. "It glowed *before* you touched it. It was waiting for you. Wasn't it."

The rain fell warm on them both. Somewhere down in the drowned lanes, the Mirel turned its slow remembering coils, and far above, past the wound in the ward, whatever had answered a child's prayer went on answering, generous and blind and hungry, pouring an ocean into a valley that had been built to keep such things out.

"It was waiting for the rain," Elodie said, and knew, as she said it, that it was the first lie she had ever told the girl, and that it would not be the last.

She looked north, toward the crater at the valley's heart where the last dragon slept out his final centuries, where the ground was warm year-round and nothing grew and she had never once, in ten years, dared to walk. The ember-glow that sometimes seeped from that crater on winter nights seemed, tonight, to pulse. Once. Twice. Like something breathing faster in its sleep.

Like something dreaming of smoke.

"We need to talk to Vaskar," she said, before she could stop herself, before the sensible part of her could clamp a hand over her own mouth.

Wren's eyes went round. "You've never even *gone* there."

"I know," said Elodie, and the rain fell warm and salt upon them both, and far away in the dark of the crater, the last dragon opened one ancient eye.
