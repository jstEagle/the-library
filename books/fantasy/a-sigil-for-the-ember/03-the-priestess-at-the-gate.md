## 3. The Priestess at the Gate

Sera Ivane had been burning for three days.

Not the clean burn of a votive candle, nor the honest fire of a hearth left untended. This was the other kind — the kind the inquisitors carried in their censers, the kind that did not consume wood but faith itself, that ate through ward-marks and blessing-wards and the layered protections of an ordained priestess as though they were parchment. It had found her at the river ford outside Vellin, and it had followed her across two provinces, and now, on the third night, it lived in her like a tenant who had stopped paying rent.

She climbed anyway.

The slope up to the valley rim was steep and stupid with scree, the sort of ground that turned ankles and broke promises, and Sera's boots had been ruined since the second day when she'd waded the marshes north of Grayford to throw off her trackers. Her feet were numb past feeling, which was a mercy. Her hands were not. The burns ran from her right palm up beneath the sleeve of her cassock, and every time she shifted her grip on the strap of her satchel, the pain announced itself again, fresh and articulate, as if it were delivering a sermon.

She let it preach. Pain meant she was still alive, and being alive meant the sigil was still unclaimed by anything worse than her.

In the satchel, against her hip, the bronze disc hummed.

It had been humming for eleven days now — since the night in the reliquary vault beneath the Chapel of Tides when she'd pried it from its leaden cradle and felt it wake against her palms like something surfacing from deep water. Not a sound, exactly. A vibration that lived in bone rather than air, that she could feel in her teeth, in the plates of her skull, in the hollow behind her sternum where her god used to speak. It hummed the way a struck bell hums, one long diminishing note that never quite diminished, and everything it touched — stone, water, flesh — remembered being rung.

*Come,* it said, without words, in the language of bells. *Come home.*

And so she came. Up through the dead lands, past villages whose names had been scraped off the milestone stones, past shrines where desperate people had tied ribbons and left bread and prayed to gods that officially did not exist — and had begun, horribly, wonderfully, to answer. She had seen what an answered prayer looked like when the answering thing was starving. She carried the memory of a well in Duncarreg that had filled overnight with water that whispered. She carried worse memories than that. They all weighed less than the satchel.

The moon was a thin nail-paring overhead, and the stars here were wrong — or rather, they were right, and that was the wrongness. For eleven days she had traveled through country where the sky sat close and heavy, where the constellations of Caelmere had been renamed and re-charted by the emperor's cartographers until the old patterns drowned under new ones. But ahead of her, rising against the star-field like the shoulder of a sleeping animal, lay the Hollow Valley — and above the Hollow Valley, the stars were the old stars. The ones from before. The ones her grandmother had named for her when Sera was small enough to still believe names were prayers.

Because magic could not follow into the valley. Everyone knew that. It was the one law even the god-emperor's censors had never managed to erase, because no censor could explain it: whatever power had carved the Hollow Valley out of the world had done so absolutely, and nothing that worked elsewhere worked there. No blessings. No hexes. No answered prayers. No gods.

Which meant no inquisitors' fire either.

That was the arithmetic she had bet three days of burning on.

* * *

She smelled the ward before she reached it.

Not smelled, precisely — but the senses blur when you're this far gone, and what came to her was a scent like rain on hot slate, a tang of ozone and old copper that she recognized from the reliquary vault, from the moment the sigil had first woken. The air above the valley rim was *thick*, though there was nothing visible to thicken it. She put out her burned hand and felt resistance — a pressure like wading chest-deep into a current, invisible water standing on its head, holding the shape of a wall.

The ward of the Hollow Valley. Ten years old, if the rumors held true. Older than that at its roots — the rumor-mongers only knew about the exile, the storm-caller girl sent down into the place where her own power could not reach her, but wards like this were not built in an afternoon. Wards like this were grown, over generations, and someone had merely... closed a door in it. Sealed a daughter of Caelmere inside a garden whose gate had been locked from the outside.

Sera pressed her palm flat against the nothing.

The hum in her satchel swelled. She felt it climb her ribs like ivy, felt the bell-note find the frequency of the ward and match it, the way two strings on a lute will shiver together if you pluck one hard enough. Sympathy. Resonance. The oldest magic there was, older than gods, maybe — the simple truth that things which are alike want to touch.

The ward shivered.

For one heartbeat, nothing. Then, with a sound like a great sheet being snapped out in the wind — a sound she felt in her back teeth, in the fluid of her eyes — a seam opened in the air before her. Not light. An absence of resistance. An invitation shaped like a doorway, hanging in the dark above the scree.

Sera Ivane had been a priestess for twenty-two years. She had knelt before altars, walked coals, spoken the ninety-nine names of the Drowned God into the mouths of the dying. She had never once seen a door open where no door was, and she thought, distantly, that she should be afraid.

She was too tired to be afraid. Fear required fuel, and hers was spent.

She stepped through.

* * *

The change was immediate and total, and it nearly killed her.

It wasn't dramatic — that was the horror of it. There was no thunderclap, no flash, none of the theater the liturgies promised. She simply crossed the threshold and the burning *stopped*. All of it. Three days of inquisitor's fire that had eaten through her wards and her flesh and her reserves of grace — gone, snuffed, like a hand closing around a candle flame. What remained was the damage the fire had already done, and the damage, suddenly unfought, unmedicated by the constant low-grade war between her ordination and the poison, arrived all at once like a bill come due.

Her knees went. She caught herself on the scree and felt skin tear along her forearm and barely registered it. The world tilted, righted, tilted again. Somewhere below her, downslope, there was grass — real grass, silvered by moonlight, sloping away toward darker shapes that might have been orchards or woods, toward a thread of paleness that might have been a stream. And beyond all of it, at the bottom of the valley like a coin dropped in a bowl, a smear of warm light. Lamplight. A window.

A house.

*Someone lives here.* The thought arrived with absurd clarity, floating up through the fog of her exhaustion. *Ten years of exile, and someone is keeping a lamp lit.*

She got her feet under her. She took one step, then another, and discovered that her body would walk if she gave it nowhere else to go, the way a mule will pull a cart if the cart is all there is. Down through the wet grass. Past a fence post with bee boxes stacked along it, their hives silent and dark, breathing that faint warm smell of wax and honey that survived even here, even now, even at the end of everything. Past a garden gone half-wild. Toward the light.

The sigil hummed against her hip, softer now, contented almost, like a child finally home. *Here,* it sang. *Here, here, here.*

"Yes," she croaked, to it, to herself, to the valley. "Here."

The last hundred yards took everything she had left. Twice she fell; twice she rose. The lamp grew. The house resolved itself out of the dark — low, stone, practical, with smoke-blackened eaves and a roof sagging gently at one corner like a tired shoulder. Smoke curled from the chimney despite the hour. Someone awake, then. Someone tending a fire.

She reached the door. It was plain oak, unpainted, worn smooth at shoulder height where years of hands had pushed it. There was no knocker. There was a horseshoe nailed above the lintel — iron, unadorned, the old country way, the way that predated every god including hers — and Sera looked at it and felt something crack open in her chest, some dam she'd built out of doctrine and duty, and to her horror she understood that she was going to weep.

Not yet. Not yet. First the door.

She raised her fist to knock, and her arm refused the order. She tried again. Her hand trembled against the oak, touching it, unable to strike it. Of course. Twenty-two years a priestess, and she could not perform so basic a human act as knocking on a stranger's door, because knocking was asking and asking meant admitting need and admitting need meant—

The door opened.

* * *

Later — much later, when she could bear to reconstruct it — Sera would remember the woman in the doorway in fragments, because consciousness was failing her even then, going down in stages like a ship settling into shallows.

Honey-brown hair, escaping its braid, silvered early at the temples. Eyes the grey-green of the sea before weather, wide now, startled, ringed with sleeplessness. A shawl thrown hastily over a linen shift. Beekeeping gloves tucked under one arm, as if she'd been doing midnight chores, as if sleep and she were old estranged friends who no longer pretended to visit. Younger than Sera expected, older than her years, and wearing an expression Sera recognized with a jolt because she saw it in her own mirror every morning:

the look of someone waiting, without admitting it, for the world to come find them.

"Please," Sera said. The word cost more than the climb had. It came out broken, a syllable with the middle missing. She tried again. "I'm looking for—" Her voice failed entirely. She swallowed blood-tasting spit and dragged the rest of it up from somewhere below her ribs. "The Dragonkeeper. I was told— they said—"

*They said the last dragon sleeps in the Hollow Valley, and dragons keep keepers, and keepers know doors.* That was what the whisperers in Ondrel's unregistered dark had told her, passing the sigil's location hand to hand like contraband. *Find the Dragonkeeper.*

The woman in the doorway went very still. Not frightened-still. Something else. The stillness of a person hearing a name they'd buried.

"There's no dragon," the woman said. Her voice was low, roughened, careful. "There's no keeper. You've been misinformed. Whatever you were sold, whoever sold it to you—"

"You're her." It was not a question. Sera didn't have the strength for questions. Certainty had settled into her the way the cold settles into wet cloth, complete and undeniable. "Elodie Eastwick. The storm-caller." She watched the name land like a blow, watched the woman flinch from her own history as if from a raised hand. "I know what it costs, hearing that name. I'm sorry. I wouldn't be here if the whole world hadn't run out of anywhere else to send me."

The silence stretched. Crickets stitched the dark. Somewhere upslope, water moved in the stream, patient as it had been for ten thousand years, asking nothing of anyone.

Then Elodie Eastwick saw the blood.

Sera felt it happen — felt the other woman's gaze travel down from her face to the ruin of her right arm, the burned cassock sleeve stuck to the wound beneath, the scorch-marks that had eaten through wool and silk and blessed linen as if they were all the same thing, which, to inquisitor's fire, they were. Felt the gaze continue to the satchel clutched against her ribs like a second heart.

"The fire followed me across two provinces," Sera heard herself say. Delirium had loosened her tongue; the truth was coming out in pieces, unordered, like cargo spilled from a broken crate. "It will follow me here eventually. It always finds what it's hunting. But not tonight. Tonight it can't cross your line. Your beautiful, terrible line." She laughed, and it hurt, and she laughed anyway, because the alternative was sobbing and she had one function left and it was not that. "Do you know what it's worth, a place the fire can't follow? Do you know what I would have given, ten years ago, for one night in a valley like this?"

"Who are you?" Elodie asked. Quiet. Steady. The steadiness of someone who has practiced being steady the way soldiers practice drill.

"Sera." She tried to bow, or nod, or do whatever it was exiled priests did instead of curtsying, and her knees made the decision final. "Sera Ivane. Priestess of the Drowned God. Last of—" She stopped. Corrected. The truth, all the way down, while she still owned a tongue to tell it. "Last of nine. The others are ash. I'm what's left of an order that the empire erased, carrying the one thing they'll burn kingdoms to retrieve."

Her legs folded. The ground came up — grass, dew, the smell of beeswax and night — and hands caught her before she finished falling, strong hands, gardener's hands, beekeeper's hands, hauling her upright with a strength that surprised them both.

"I can't take you in," Elodie said, and her voice cracked on it, and Sera, fading, catalogued the crack the way she'd once catalogued cracks in altar-stones: as evidence of something holy straining at its seams. "I can't. You don't understand what it means, someone coming here, what it costs—"

"I understand better than you could dream." The darkness was coming in earnest now, folding over her vision like a tide. She fought it. She had one more thing to say and she would say it if she had to spend her last heartbeat saying it. "Elodie Eastwick. Listen to me. Listen—"

She was dimly aware of being lowered, of grass and a shoulder beneath her head, of a heartbeat under her ear that was not her own, quick and human and afraid.

"The gods are answering prayers," Sera whispered. "All over Caelmere. Every god the empire buried. And they're answering *wrong*. Because they're not gods anymore, not really. They're dying, and they're hungry, and they're grasping at us like drowning men grasp at—" Her breath hitched. "I have seen what an answered prayer does. I have a thing in my bag that can open the door between the living land and the dead one, and the emperor knows, and his fire knows my name, and there is exactly one person in this world the texts say can stand at that door and survive the standing."

The tide rose. She let it. There was nothing left to hold it back with.

"They called me Dragonkeeper," she murmured, as the dark closed. "But the texts call you storm-born. They call you the hinge."

The last thing she felt, before the black took her, was the sigil in her satchel ringing — one long, clear, joyful note against her hip, like a bell that has waited centuries to be struck — and the woman above her breathing one word into the dark, so quietly it might have been a prayer, might have been a curse, might have been both:

"Hinge."

* * *

Sera dreamed of the cathedral.

She dreamed it as it had been — white towers above the harbor at Vellin, bells rolling their bronze voices across the water at dawn, the congregation kneeling in ranks like a field of pale flowers. She dreamed it as it was now — flooded to the clerestory windows, silent, its nave a drowned hall where fish drifted through shafts of green light and the dead still knelt in their pews, patient as furniture, waiting for a service that had been postponed twenty years.

And in the dream, as always, her god was there. Vast. Below. A presence the size of weather, the size of famine, filling the drowned nave the way silt fills a harbor, pressing at the stained glass with the patience of water, which never hurries because it always wins.

*You brought it,* the dream-god said. Its voice was not a voice. It was the sensation of depth — of looking down into water too dark to see the bottom of. *You brought my key to my door, little priestess.*

"It isn't your door," Sera said, in the dream, as she always said. "It was never only yours."

*Everything is mine,* said the drowned god, kindly, terribly, the way a flood is kind, the way a famine is terrible. *The sea proves it daily. Bring me the key, Sera Ivane. Bring it alone, as you swore. Bring it, and I will give back everything the empire drowned.*

And Sera, in the dream, as always, opened her mouth to answer —

— and woke instead, gasping, to lamplight and the smell of melted beeswax and the sound of someone moving quietly around a small stone room, banking a fire, setting a kettle on, making room in a life that had been carefully, deliberately empty for ten years.

Her burns were dressed. Clean linen, honey-salve, the work of competent hands. Her satchel sat beside her cot, untouched, still humming its one long note — but softer now, patient, as if it too understood that the door it wanted would not be opened tonight.

From somewhere beyond the wall came the murmur of voices. Elodie Eastwick, speaking low and urgent — not to her.

To someone else.

"—and I'm telling you she's real, the sigil's real, I *felt* it through the door, and if she's telling the truth then the ward won't hold them forever, so you can sulk about it or you can help me, but either way, old one, the world has found us again—"

A pause. Then a voice answered, and Sera lay very still in the dark, because the voice was vast and dry and sardonic and old beyond any reckoning she had words for, and it rumbled through the floor of the cottage the way thunder rolls through the floor of the sky, and it said:

"*Entertaining priests at midnight. Your mother kept bees and knew when to leave the world alone. You, apparently, collect the end of it on your doorstep.*"

Sera closed her eyes and smiled, despite everything, despite the burns and the dream and the drowning god waiting patiently below the tide of the world.

The Dragonkeeper was not a myth after all.

Neither, it seemed, was the dragon.
