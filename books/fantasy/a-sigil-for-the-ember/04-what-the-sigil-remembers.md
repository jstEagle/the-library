## 4. What the Sigil Remembers

The sigil did not sleep.

Elodie had known that within an hour of bringing it inside, though she could not have said how. It sat on her table between the honey jars and the salt dish, a disc of bronze no wider than her palm, and it *listened*. She felt it the way one feels a stranger standing too close behind them in an empty room — not a sound, not a warmth, but a pressure at the back of the neck, a certainty of attention.

Sera slept in the chair by the hearth, or tried to. Her burns had been dressed with comfrey and honey, and the fever that came with them came honestly, burning through her in waves that made her murmur names Elodie didn't recognize — names of gods, mostly, and once, heartbreakingly, a name that sounded like a sister's. Wren had curled up on the floor beside the priestess like a cat adopting a wounded bird, one small hand resting on Sera's ankle as if touch alone could hold her to the world.

Elodie sat across from the sigil and refused to touch it.

That was the bargain she'd made with herself: she would keep it safe because a dying woman had carried it over the ward with half her skin scorched away, but she would not *touch* it. Touching things was how they got into you. She had learned that lesson at nineteen, in a courtyard full of screaming, with rain falling so hard it broke the flagstones, and she had never once needed to learn it again.

But the sigil did not need touching.

It waited until the fire had burned low and Sera's breathing had gone deep and even, until the only light in the room was ember-light and the strange pale glow off the bronze — and then it reached for her anyway, the way water reaches for a low place.

*Look*, it said, without words, without sound. *Look what you forgot.*

* * *

She was standing in Caelmere.

Not the Caelmere of maps — she had seen those, years ago, before exile, before the god-emperor's cartographers scraped the old kingdom off every chart in the realm. This was Caelmere whole: white-walled Ondrel gleaming on its river bend, the terraced vineyards of the south coast, the mountain shrines where pilgrims climbed barefoot in the snow because the old gods liked to be met halfway. A country so alive it seemed to hum. She could smell bread baking. She could hear a market two streets over, the bright argument of prices.

And then it began to burn.

There was no warning in it. One heartbeat the city stood; the next, fire walked down the great avenue like a dignitary taking an inspection, unhurried, thorough, *interested*. Elodie tried to run toward it and found she could not move — this was memory, she realized, memory wearing her eyes, and whoever remembered it had been made to stand still and watch. The heat came anyway. It came through the remembering like truth comes through a confession: total, and too late to matter.

She saw the temples go. Not toppled — *emptied*, first, their bells cut down and dragged away on carts while priests knelt in the doorways singing hymns that grew thinner and thinner, like voices heard through closing doors. She saw the shrines in the mountains filled with stones. She saw a woman in a drowned-god's vestments wade into the sea carrying her own altar on her back, walking out past the breakers, walking out past the light, and not coming back.

*This is what was done*, the sigil said, and its voice was not cruel. That was the worst part. It said it the way a ledger says it, without appetite. *This is what was decided about us.*

The vision turned, and Elodie understood with a lurch that they were leaving the ground, rising above the burning capital, rising until the whole kingdom lay beneath them like a body under a sheet — and then the sheet was pulled back, and she saw what the maps no longer showed.

Beneath Ondrel. Beneath the palace and the granaries and the emperor's new marble avenue laid over the bones of older streets. There was a door.

It was not a door as she understood doors. It was a seam — a hairline fracture in the world itself, running down and down beneath the capital's foundations, and through the seam came light. Not firelight. Not sunlight. Ember-light, deep red-gold, patient, ancient, the color of the last coal in a hearth that everyone has forgotten to feed. The seam ran further than the eye could follow, and behind it, pressed against it like faces against frosted glass, were—

*Us*, said the sigil. *We are behind it. What is left of us.*

Elodie saw them then, or almost saw them — shapes vast and starved moving in the ember-dark, the way fish move under winter ice. They were not monstrous. That was the horror of it. They looked *hungry*, the way a dog looks hungry, the way a child looks hungry, with a patience that had gone on so long it had curdled into something else. And threaded through the dark around them, thin as spider silk, she saw the prayers. Thousands of them. Rising from the living world above like heat-shimmer, passing through the seam, and being caught — caught and held and *answered*.

A farmer praying for rain. A mother praying for her fevered son. A girl in a valley that magic could not reach, praying for rain over a garden that would not grow.

Elodie's stomach dropped through the vision like a stone through still water.

*Oh*, she thought. *Oh, no. It wasn't answered by accident.*

* * *

She came back to herself with her hands flat on the table and her chair pushed back and her heart going like a trapped bird. The fire had burned to grey. Outside, the false dawn was seeping into the sky, that hour when the valley looked least like itself and most like a promise.

Sera was awake. Elodie didn't know how long she'd been awake — long enough, from the look of her, to have watched the whole thing happen in Elodie's face. The priestess had propped herself up despite the burns, and her grey eyes were clear with fever-brightness and something underneath it that Elodie recognized, bitterly, as pity.

"You saw," Sera said. Not a question.

"I saw a city burn." Elodie's voice came out rougher than she intended. "I saw a door under the capital. I saw—" She stopped. Started again. "I saw my neighbor's daughter's prayer being *caught*. Like a fly in amber."

"Tam." Sera said the girl's name quietly. "Her name is Tam. She prays to Ashen-Who-Waits, the hearth god, though her grandmother taught her the older names. Rain-prayers went to Veyra, in the old days. The storm-mother." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You would have known that, once."

"Don't." Elodie stood, put the table between herself and the priestess, and immediately hated how much that gesture gave away. "Don't do that. Don't come into my valley and start handing me back pieces of myself like I dropped them on the road."

"Didn't you?" Sera asked gently. "Drop them?"

The silence stretched. In the hearth, a log settled. On the floor, Wren stirred but did not wake, her hand still curled around Sera's ankle.

"Explain it to me," Elodie said finally, because the not-knowing was worse than any answer could be. "The visions. The prayers getting answered. Ten years, nothing in this valley answers anything, and now suddenly the whole world's gods are playing favorites. Why now? What changed?"

"Nothing changed." Sera shifted, winced, kept her voice low for the sleeping girl. "That's what no one understands. Nothing has changed for ten years except that we stopped looking. The god-emperor unmade Caelmere from the records, yes. He cut down the bells, he renamed the months, he put his own face where the old gods' faces used to go in every shrine from here to the coast. But you cannot kill a god by renaming it. You can only starve it." Her hands moved as she spoke, restless, sketching shapes in the air — Elodie realized with a distant chill that they were old liturgical gestures, the ones priests used when speaking of the dead. "Prayer is food. Belief is food. Memory is food. For three hundred years the old gods ate from a kingdom's worth of altars, and then in a single generation they were given nothing. No temples. No festivals. No names spoken over cradles. The god-emperor thought he had won a war. He had only started a famine."

"And starving things get desperate," Elodie said.

"Starving things get *grasping*." Sera leaned forward, and the fever made her intensity feverish too, so that she glowed in the dim room like something lit from inside. "A fed god answers prayer out of abundance. It has rain to spare, health to spare, luck to spend. But a starving god answers out of its own flesh. Every miracle costs it. Every prayer caught and granted takes something it cannot make more of. They are eating themselves to answer us, Elodie Eastwick. Can you understand what that means? Somewhere right now, a dying god is pouring the last of itself into a good harvest for a farmer who will never know its name — and the god does it *anyway*, because being answered-for is the only worship left to it. Because if it stops answering, it stops existing, and it is not ready to stop existing. None of them are ready. They were never taught how."

Elodie looked at the sigil on the table. In the grey dawn light it looked smaller, somehow. More pitiable. A bronze disc humming faintly, like a struck bell that would not finish fading.

"That girl," she said. "Wren. Her prayer for rain. It flooded the valley floor. Water that remembered being ocean. If a god paid for that out of its own—"

"It nearly emptied itself for a child's garden," Sera said simply. "Yes. I traced the answering. It was Veyra. Or what's left of Veyra." Something moved through the priestess's face then, quick and painful, there and gone — grief with nowhere to put itself. "My own god holds on differently. The Drowned One stopped answering years ago. We tell ourselves it is dignity. I am no longer sure it isn't despair."

Elodie turned away and stood at the window. Below, the valley lay in its morning colors, the bee-fields silvered with dew, the crater of the sleeping dragon steaming faintly in the north as it always did, breath of a thing dreaming through its final centuries. Her valley. Ten years of her valley, where nothing impossible had ever been allowed to happen, where she had built a life out of honey and distance and careful forgetting.

"The door," she said, not turning around. "Under the capital. The visions showed me it's real. Showed me what's behind it. What is it you want, exactly? You crossed a ward that burns, carrying that thing, half-dead, to find a Dragonkeeper who hasn't done magic in ten years. Nobody does that for a history lesson. What do you want?"

Behind her, the chair creaked. When Sera answered, her voice had lost its liturgy. It was just a tired, burned woman's voice, honest as an open wound.

"I want to open it."

Elodie closed her eyes.

"Not to let them out," Sera went on quickly. "I'm not mad. I've seen what a starving god does with a granted prayer — I watched Veyra drown a valley for a child's cabbages. If the old gods came back into the world as they are now, famished and grasping, they wouldn't reign. They'd *feed*. They'd take everything the god-emperor took from them, with interest, and the interest would be paid in us. I know that. My order knew that, and it's why my order is dead." A breath. "No. The door opens both ways, Elodie. Things can come through. But things can also be *let go* through. The old rites — the true ones, the ones burned with everything else — say the door was never a prison. It was a hospice. A place where gods go to die well, tended, honored, *fed* at the end instead of starved. The god-emperor turned a hospice into a cell and called it victory. I want to open the door so they can die properly. So they can stop grasping. So Tam's rain-prayer gets answered by weather instead of by a god spending its last coin." Another pause, longer. "And so my god can stop waiting for us at the bottom of the sea, too proud to ask and too stubborn to leave."

Elodie opened her eyes. The valley was fully awake now. Smoke rose from the village chimneys; somewhere a dog was barking at nothing; the bees would be stirring in the hives, doing their small, honest, mortal work. Ordinary life, all of it, balanced on top of a seam of ember-light running down beneath a country that officially did not exist.

"You're asking me to help you open a door between the living land and the dead one," she said. "With a bronze disc and a storm-caller who hasn't called weather in ten years. And you crossed a burning ward to find me because—"

"Because the door doesn't open for faith anymore. Faith is what the god-emperor burned." Sera's voice was very quiet now. "It opens for blood and for storm. Storm-blood, the old rites say. A caller in the threshold, holding the way open." A pause, and Elodie heard her choose the next words carefully, laying each one down like a foot on ice. "There is exactly one storm-caller left in the world, Elodie. The god-emperor's purges were thorough. And she lives in a valley where magic cannot follow — which means, I think, that she chose a prison where no one could ever again ask her to open doors."

Elodie laughed. It came out wrong — too short, too sharp, nothing like laughter really, just the shape of it with nothing inside.

"You don't know anything about me."

"I know your storm killed forty people." No flinch in it. Sera had clearly decided that mercy, today, meant precision. "I know you swore then and swear now that you never called it. And I believe you — which should frighten you more than accusation does. Because if you didn't call it, Elodie, then something else did. Something reached through a nineteen-year-old girl and spent her like a coin. And the same something has just spent a god's last strength flooding this valley for a child's prayer." The priestess rose, slowly, paying for every inch of movement in pain, and crossed the room until she stood beside Elodie at the window, close enough that Elodie could smell the comfrey and the burn-salve and underneath it the sea, still on her, sea-salt that would never wash out. "The famine is ending. One way or another. Either the gods die grasping and take this world down clawing, or someone opens the hospice door and lets them go with honor. There is no third path where we all politely forget about it. I checked. I spent eleven years checking."

Outside, far to the north, the steam over Vaskar's crater bent sideways in a wind that had no business existing in the Hollow Valley — a wind blowing against the morning, a wind with intention in it. Elodie watched it and felt the old dread settle over her shoulders like a familiar coat.

And beneath the dread, quieter, shamefully warm, the other thing she would not name. The feeling of the storm stirring in her chest when she'd pulled Wren from the flood. Ten years buried and it had come awake at the first taste of rain, hungry, *grateful*, hers again if she only said yes.

Reluctantly, terribly, she understood. Understood the starving gods reaching through the seam. Understood the priestess crossing fire. Understood even, in a way that made her feel ill, the god-emperor sealing the door and scraping the maps — because some truths are so heavy that the only mercy is to pretend the country they happened in never existed.

She understood all of it, and understanding, she was discovering, was not the same as forgiving, and neither was the same as agreeing.

"Get some sleep," she said at last, turning from the window. "Heal. Eat. And then you're going to tell me every rite you remember, every word of those burned texts, starting with the part about what happens to the person standing in the threshold." She picked up the sigil from the table — finally, deliberately, letting it feel her hand — and it hummed against her palm like a living thing recognizing kin. "Because I noticed, priestess, that you told me what the door opens for. You haven't yet told me what it *costs*."

For just a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — Sera's fever-bright eyes flickered toward the north, toward the crater, toward the slow breath of the sleeping dragon.

And far beneath the valley, deeper than roots, deeper than memory, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, for the first time in three hundred years, to dream of waking.
