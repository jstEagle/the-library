## 5. Vaskar Wakes

The first thing Vaskar knew was the taste of iron going out of the world.

He had slept through the death of his kind, through the felling of the great roosts, through the long slow forgetting of his name — and he had learned, in that long dark, to sleep the way old things sleep: with one ear open, one claw curled around the warm stone of his own heart, listening for the sound that would mean the world had finally finished with him.

What woke him was not thunder. It was smaller than thunder. It was the sound of a bell that had been struck once, very far away, inside a thing that should not have been able to ring.

Vaskar opened one eye.

The valley had changed in his absence — or rather, he had changed in his absence, and the valley had stayed exactly, insultingly the same. The same crooked pines. The same grey scree. The same bees, descendants of descendants of descendants of the hives his keeper had tended, droning their small golden industry over a meadow that had never once known a storm it did not deserve. Magic could not follow a soul into the Hollow Valley. That was the point of it. That was the mercy and the punishment both, and for ten years the silence had been a kind of anesthesia, and Vaskar had slept in it like an old hound by a cold hearth.

But something had rung.

He uncurled, joint by joint, and the uncurling took a while. He was not a young dragon. He was not even a middle-aged dragon. He was the last page of a book whose other pages had been torn out and burned, and his body had begun, in these final centuries, to make its own arrangements — scales gone dull as old pewter along his flanks, the great furnace of his chest burning lower each decade, the ember at his center shrinking from a bonfire to a hearth to, lately, a coal that a careless breath might scatter. Dragons did not die of old age, precisely. They died of running out of reasons, and the reasons had been running out of him like sand through a cracked jar.

Still. He could smell the valley. And the valley smelled wrong.

Rain, he thought, and then corrected himself: *rain that is not rain.* Water falling upward over the rim of the Hollow, in threads, in ropes, in long silver stitches sewing the sky to the earth the wrong way. He could taste it on his tongue even from the deep of his crater — old water, salt water, water that remembered being ocean when this whole country was a sea-floor and the gods were young and fat and cruel. Water that had been *prayed for.*

"Someone," Vaskar said to the empty crater, and his voice came out like a landslide remembering how to be a voice, "has been *answering prayers.*"

The crater, as usual, did not answer. The crater was a hole in the earth the size of a fallen cathedral, and Vaskar had made it himself, four hundred years ago, when he had come to the Hollow Valley to die in private and then, finding death slow to arrive, had simply stayed. His keeper had found him the following spring. A storm-caller with soot on her knuckles and no fear in her whatsoever, who had looked up at the last dragon in the world and said, "You're in my bees' flight path."

He had eaten no one since. It seemed rude, after that.

* * *

He climbed out of the crater in stages, because dignity required it. An old dragon does not scramble. An old dragon *ascends*, slowly, with the gravity of mountains, and if his hips protest, the protests are between him and the rock.

The valley floor was flooded.

Not deeply — a hand's depth, perhaps two in the low places — but it lay across the meadows like spilled mercury, and it was *moving wrong*, sliding in slow coils against the wind, and when the light touched it, it did not glitter. It *watched.* Water that remembers being ocean has opinions. Vaskar had flown over oceans when they were young and full of leviathans and the drowned gods' first temples, and he knew that look.

And there, on the dry rise where the keeper's cottage stood — the cottage Elodie Eastwick had built with her own hands on the exact footprint of her grandmother's, because sentiment was the one magic that survived the ward — there were lights. Two of them. Lamplight and firelight, and the small dark shapes of people moving behind them, and one shape that was neither lamp nor fire nor person but a *hum*, a bronze-colored sound, a bell-note that sat in Vaskar's oldest teeth and would not stop.

He knew that sound.

He had hoped, with the particular hopeless hope of the very old, never to know it again.

"The sigil," he said, and the word came out of him in smoke. "Someone is carrying the sigil. Into my valley. To my keeper's granddaughter. While I sleep." He considered this. "I am going to be *furious* in about an hour. First I need to finish waking up."

* * *

He did not announce himself. Announcing oneself was for young dragons, who needed the attention the way fires needed air. Instead he came around the western shoulder of the valley on the old paths, the ones worn smooth by his own belly in the first decades, and he settled on the ridge above the cottage with the patience of geology, and he listened.

The keeper's descendant was arguing with a priestess.

He could hear them through the shuttered window, their voices rising and falling like weather. The girl — Elodie, little Elodie Eastwick, who had been nineteen and furious and terrified the last time he'd bothered to look at her, who had thrown a stone at his crater once and shouted that if he was going to haunt her valley he could at least be useful — the girl was saying, in a voice scraped raw with ten years of talking only to bees:

"—and I'm telling you, I didn't *call* anything. I don't call anything. I *can't* call anything. There is no magic here, that's the entire—"

"I'm not saying you called it." A second voice. Wrecked, low, burned at the edges the way a voice is burned when its owner has crossed something that should not be crossed. "I'm saying it came *anyway.* The gods don't wait for permission anymore, Storm-Caller. That's rather the point. They're dying, and dying things grasp."

Vaskar's tail-tip curled with something that in a younger dragon would have been delight and in him was merely a dry, rusted amusement. *Storm-Caller.* So the priestess knew. So the priestess had come all this way, half-cooked, across a ward that ate magic like a fire eats paper, to find a storm-caller who had spent a decade pretending to be a beekeeper. That took a certain magnificent stupidity, or a certain magnificent faith, and in Vaskar's long experience the two were indistinguishable at this range.

He waited until the argument wore itself down into the exhausted silence that follows all true arguments, and then he leaned his head over the cottage roof, so that his shadow fell across the garden, and he said:

"Elodie Eastwick. You are harboring a priestess, a drowned god's sigil, and a child, in the last valley in the world where none of those things are supposed to exist. Your grandmother would have had opinions. Your grandmother had opinions about *weeds.*"

The screaming was, he had to admit, satisfying. It had been a long time since anyone had screamed at him. It meant he still mattered enough to be frightening, and a dragon hoards whatever he can get.

* * *

They came out to him, of course. They always came out. The girl first, arms crossed, chin up, wearing fury like a borrowed coat two sizes too big; then the priestess, swaying, bandaged in what looked like torn altar-cloth, cradling against her chest a bronze disc the size of a dinner plate; then the child — a small girl, dark-eyed, hollow-cheeked, standing slightly behind Elodie with the particular ferocity of a child who has decided that this adult is hers now and will be defended accordingly.

Vaskar looked at all of them for a long moment.

"Well," he said. "The last dragon, the last priestess, the last storm-caller, and the last child in the Hollow Valley. If we stand very close together we might almost make one whole person."

"You're awake," Elodie said. Not a question. Her voice had gone flat, which he recognized as her fighting voice, or her frightened voice; in her they had always been the same voice.

"I am awake," Vaskar agreed, "because someone struck a bell inside my sleep. Do you know what that is, priestess? Do you know what you've been carrying?"

Sera Ivane — for he knew her order's faces, he had burned her order's temples once, in another life, on the other side, when he was still the emperor's beast and proud of it — Sera lifted the sigil.

It was worse than he remembered. He had seen it last at the coronation of the god-emperor, in the talon of a dragon who had not yet learned to regret anything, and then it had been bright, arrogant, whole. Now it was scarred, green with age, its ring of names worn nearly smooth — and it was *humming*, that bell-note, that grave-bell note, and beneath the hum Vaskar could hear, faint and vast and terrible, something breathing.

"It's real," Sera said. Her voice did not waver, which cost her; he could see what it cost her. "The Door of Caelmere is real. The old gods are real, and they are dying, and they have begun answering prayers because they are starving for worship, and this—" she lifted it higher, and the hum climbed into Vaskar's molars, "—this opens the door between the living land and the dead one. I need the Dragonkeeper to carry it where it must go."

"I'm a beekeeper," Elodie said.

"You're the Dragonkeeper's blood."

"I'm a *beekeeper*," Elodie said, with the exact intonation of a woman who has had this argument with the universe before and lost it every time, "and I would like everyone in this valley to stop assuming that the sins of my grandmother are a family business I've inherited. There is no magic here. There is no power here. There is me, and my hives, and a very old lizard with opinions—"

"*Lizard*," said Vaskar.

"—and I want to know," Elodie finished, stepping forward, one finger jabbing up at a snout the size of a fishing boat, "why a priestess who says the gods are answering prayers everywhere in Caelmere has walked into the one valley where they *can't*, and why she needs *me*, and what exactly it is that you're not telling us, because I've been lied to by better liars than either of you, and I survived it, and I am *tired.*"

The silence after was very good. Vaskar let it sit. Old dragons understand the architecture of silence the way masons understand arches.

Then he laughed. It was not a kind laugh; it came up from the coal at the center of him and it shook loose grit from the ridge and it made the child grab the hem of Elodie's shirt, but it was, at least, an honest laugh, the laugh of a creature who has run out of everything except the joke at the end of the world.

"Oh, keeper's-get," he said, when he had finished. "You want the truth. From a dragon and a priestess, in the same evening, after the day you've had. Very well. The truth is cheap tonight. The truth is *free.*" He lowered his head until one vast eye, gold going grey at the rim, was level with her. "The sigil is real. The Door is real. The gods are real, and they are dying, and they are grasping, and every prayer answered anywhere in Caelmere is a drowning man pulling swimmers under. And the Door — the Door your priestess wants opened —"

He paused. Not for effect. For grief, though he dressed the grief up in the only clothes he had left, which were jokes.

"—the Door will kill whatever opens it."

The wind moved through the pines. Down in the flooded meadow, the water that remembered being ocean went very still, listening.

"That's not true," Sera said.

"It is entirely true," Vaskar said, "which is why it's so inconvenient. The Door is not a door, priestess, it is a *wound*, and a wound does not care who stitches it shut. It needs a caller — someone with storm in their blood, someone who can stand in the threshold and hold the seam open against the weight of two worlds pressing together. And the weight of two worlds," he said gently, "is not a thing a body survives. Your order knew. Why do you think the Door was sealed and the maps burned and the order scattered? Not mercy. *Arithmetic.* One caller per Door, and the Door only needs opening once."

Sera's face did something complicated. Elodie's face did something simpler: it went white, and then it went hard, and Vaskar, who had known three generations of Eastwicks and had liked all of them and been lied to by all of them, watched the girl do the arithmetic herself, faster than the priestess wanted.

"Someone with storm in their blood," Elodie repeated.

"Elodie," Sera said.

"You walked into a valley with no magic," Elodie said, very quietly, "carrying a door-killer, looking for a storm-caller. You walked into the *only valley* — you crossed the ward half-burned — and you asked for the Dragonkeeper." Her voice cracked down the middle, and she hated it, and Vaskar watched her hate it. "You didn't come here because I'm the last of anything. You came here because I'm *expendable.* Because I've already lost everything, so what's one more—"

"That is not why I came."

"It's *exactly* why you came. It's why *everyone* comes. The emperor sent me here so I'd be easy to forget. The gods are going to dig me up so I'll be easy to *spend.*" She turned away, both hands pressed flat over her face, and the child — Wren, the priestess had called her, and Vaskar filed the name away the way he filed everything now, carefully, because he had less and less room — the child put one small hand on Elodie's arm, and Elodie flinched and then didn't.

Vaskar watched, and the coal in his chest ached in the old way, the way it had ached when the roosts burned, the way it ached every time he woke and counted the world's dragons and arrived, as always, at one.

* * *

Later — much later, when the priestess slept her burned sleep by the fire and the child slept curled against the girl's side like a comma against a sentence — Elodie came out into the garden alone, and stood in the wet grass, and looked up at the ridge where he lay.

"You knew my grandmother," she said. It was not a question either. Everything she said to him was not a question; questions required believing the answer might be kind.

"I knew your grandmother," Vaskar said. "She lied to me for eleven years about the whereabouts of my favorite honey. She was the finest storm-caller Caelmere ever produced, and the last thing she did with that power was seal herself in a valley where it was worthless, so that the emperor could never use her to open the Door. She chose the bees, girl. She chose them over the storm. I never understood it until tonight."

"Tonight."

"Tonight I watched you stand in front of a dragon, a priestess, and the end of the world, and ask about *bees.*" His smoke curled upward into the dark. "She would have been proud. She would also have lied to you, so be grateful for the improvement."

Elodie was quiet a long time. The upward rain had stopped; the valley's stolen ocean was draining back through the ward-crack in slow silver threads, going home to whatever starving god had borrowed it. Above the rim, far off, the sky over Caelmere glowed faintly wrong, the color of a fire seen through too many hills.

"If I open it," she said at last. "If it came to that. Would it hurt?"

Vaskar considered lying. He was, after all, very good at it; he had been the god-emperor's beast, and beasts of empires learn that truth is a luxury of the unowned. But the coal in his chest was small tonight, and small things cannot afford luxuries.

"Yes," he said. "All of it. All at once. And then nothing, forever, which is either the worst part or the best part, depending on how tired you are."

Elodie nodded, slowly, the way people nod at verdicts. Then she said, "You're dying too. Aren't you. I could hear it in your voice. It sounds like a fire going down to coals."

The old dragon said nothing, which was, from him, a confession.

"And you'll tell me to open it anyway," she said. "Won't you. Because you're the last, and I'm the last, and the priestess is the last, and everything last is supposed to throw itself into the fire for everyone else. That's the story. That's always the story."

"No," Vaskar said, and the word came out of him heavier than he intended, heavier than smoke, heavier than grief. "No, keeper's-get. I will tell you what I told your grandmother on the last night I ever spoke to her, when she asked me the same question about herself. I told her: *spend the fire. Don't hoard it.* A coal kept in the dark is only a coal. But a coal given away—" he paused, and the pause cost him, and he paid it, "—a coal given away can light something that outlives the giving."

He looked north, past the rim of the valley, past the wards, to the drowned cathedral he could smell on the far wind — salt and bells and old death — and he thought: *soon.* The bell had been struck. The door would be found. The world was finishing with him at last, and it was going to need his last coal to do it.

"Sleep, Elodie Eastwick," he said. "Tomorrow the valley burns. I can smell the inquisitors on the wind, and they are three days out, and you are going to need to be rested when I teach you how to hold a flame that isn't yours."
