## 6. Inquisitor Ash

The road to the Hollow Valley was not a road at all anymore.

Corvin Ashe had known that before he set out — the maps in the Imperial Archive showed it as a smudge of gray hatching, a cartographer's shrug, *uninhabited* written in a clerk's hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. But knowing a thing from a map and knowing it with your boots were two different knowings, and by the fourth day of riding through country that seemed to have forgotten it was ever part of anything, his boots had taught him what the map could not: that the world simply thinned out here, like breath fading on cold glass.

He rode alone. That was unusual. Inquisitors traveled in threes as doctrine demanded — one to question, one to record, one to burn — but the god-emperor's writ for this particular errand had come sealed in black wax, and black wax meant *alone*. Black wax meant the kind of work you did not want witnesses for, even the ones sworn beside you.

He checked the resonance-needle for the hundredth time that morning. It sat in its padded case like a sleeping adder: a sliver of consecrated glass suspended in quicksilver, trembling now — always trembling now — toward the northeast, toward the valley rim he could just begin to see, a blue-gray wall of hills stacked against the horizon like folded laundry. The needle had been still for six years. Then, eleven days ago, in the reliquary beneath the Basilica of the Ascension, it had begun to hum.

Corvin remembered the sound. Everyone in the archive had. A low bronze note, like a bell struck underwater, and every consecrated instrument in the building answering it, one after another, until the whole undercroft rang like a struck harp and the archivists stood pale among their relics wondering which god had knocked.

The orders were simple. They were always simple; that was the horror of them. *Burn what answers. Question nothing.* Written in the emperor's own hand, or close enough to it — that cramped, ascetic script that looked less like writing than like a row of nails driven into paper.

Corvin had read the writ four times on the road and each time his eye snagged on the same phrase, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. *Burn what answers.* Not *who*. What. As if whatever was answering prayers across Caelmere had already been demoted from personhood in advance. As if the emperor had looked at the reports — villages flooded by rain that fell upward, crops blooming overnight into monstrous abundance, a well in Grayford that wept salt water and spoke in a dead woman's voice — and seen none of it as miracle. Only as infestation.

He closed the case. The needle's hum faded to nothing, though he could have sworn he felt it through the leather, through his saddle, through the bones of his own hands, faint as a second pulse.

Eleven days ago the instruments had rung. And Corvin Ashe, who had spent ten years learning to feel nothing in particular about anything, had stood in that ringing dark and thought one thought, unwelcome and immediate as a slap:

*She's alive.*

* * *

He made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone — one of the old boundary markers of dead Caelmere, carved with the face of some minor harvest god whose name no living tongue remembered. The emperor's surveyors had chiseled the face smooth decades ago, during the Unwriting, when every altar in the kingdom was defaced and every divine name struck from every ledger. What remained was an egg-shaped blankness, a stone that had once smiled and now did not.

Corvin built a small fire because doctrine required it — an inquisitor's fire was a statement, a little hearth of the true faith burning in the wilderness — and then sat apart from it, because he had never liked statements.

He ate hardtack and dried mutton and drank water that tasted of iron, and he let himself do the thing he never let himself do within sight of another inquisitor: he remembered.

Her name had been Elodie Eastwick. She had been nineteen years old and she had called a storm that killed forty people, and Corvin Ashe had stood in the gallery of the Assize Hall in Vaelport and watched her stand trial with her wrists bound in lead-thread and her chin up, up, always up, as if she could see over the top of her own condemnation to something better on the far side of it.

He had loved her. He had been twenty-two and stupid with it, the kind of love that lives entirely in glances across a training yard and letters burned unread because keeping them was evidence. She had known. He'd never said it and she had known anyway, the way she knew weather three days off, the way she knew which bees were about to sting. Some people simply perceived things others had to be told.

And then the storm came down on the harbor district like the fist of something enormous, and forty people died in the flood, and Elodie Eastwick stood in the dock and swore — swore on her mother's grave, on her own life, on every god the empire had since unwritten — that she had never called it. That she had felt it coming and reached for it and *missed*, the way you reach for a door handle in the dark, and that the storm had answered someone else's hand entirely.

Corvin had believed her. That was the wound that never closed cleanly: he had believed her, completely, with the whole dumb animal certainty of a young man in love, and belief was not testimony, and testimony was all the law would take.

Maren Voss had given the testimony instead. Maren, who had known Elodie since they were children throwing stones at crows. Maren, who stood before the assizes in her brand-new warden's gray and said, in a voice like a drawn blade being sheathed slowly, that Elodie Eastwick had the power, and the temperament, and the history of instability, and that the court must weigh the possibility of innocence against the certainty of capability.

*Must weigh the possibility of innocence against the certainty of capability.* Corvin had heard those words a thousand times since, in his own mouth, delivering verdicts of his own. He had made them the cornerstone of his career. He had never once stopped hearing them in Maren's voice.

Exile, not execution. That was what Maren's testimony bought — everyone said so afterward, in the careful tones people used around a mercy they didn't quite trust. Exile to the Hollow Valley, where magic could not follow, where a storm-caller would live out her life as ordinary as dirt. Forty people dead, and the storm-caller sent to keep bees.

Corvin had requested transfer to the inquisitorial service the same month. He told himself it was duty. He told himself a great many things that year.

* * *

On the sixth day he crossed the last of the hill country and saw the Hollow Valley spread below him, and understood in one long look why the empire had chosen it for exile.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. A bowl of green miles wide, ringed with birch and pine, threaded with a river that moved slow and silver as poured cream. Terraced gardens climbed the near slopes. Smoke rose from chimneys — a dozen small holdings, maybe twenty, a village that had grown up around the exiles the way moss grows around a stone. He could see, absurdly, rows of white boxes that his mind took a moment to identify: beehives. Dozens of them, ranked along a south-facing slope like a choir.

And in the valley's northern quarter, a crater.

Even at this distance, even through the heat-haze of late afternoon, the crater was wrong. No grass grew to its rim. The earth around it was fused glass-black, smooth as a lake at evening, and at its center — sunk so deep he could only see the suggestion of it, a curve like the shoulder of something vast — slept the last dragon.

Vaskar. Every child in Caelmere learned the name before they learned their prayers, back when children still learned prayers. The dragon who had fought at the Breaking and refused the gods' reward and crawled here to die slowly, alone, in a land where magic could not follow him — which was, Corvin supposed, the only mercy the old world had ever shown anything. To die somewhere the gods couldn't watch.

The needle in its case was humming audibly now. He could hear it through the leather, a thin bronze whine like a mosquito made of church-bells, and it pulled toward the valley floor with an insistence that had stopped being subtle somewhere in the last mile.

Corvin sat his horse on the ridgeline for a long time and did not go down.

Doctrine said go down. Doctrine said ride to the nearest holding, present the writ, question the residents, burn what answers. The writ itself said *question nothing* — a contradiction he had noted and declined to resolve, because resolving contradictions in imperial writs was how inquisitors ended up in the quicklime pits behind the Basilica. You carried the contradiction. You let it ride double behind your saddle like a second man.

But there was a problem, and the problem was arithmetic.

The resonance had begun eleven days ago. Whatever had woken the instruments in the archive was already here, already moving, already — if the reports from the border provinces were accurate — *answering*. A village girl's prayer for rain answered too well. A widow's prayer for her drowned son answered with the son himself walking out of the sea, dripping, smiling wrong. Each report worse than the last, each answered prayer a small apocalypse with a human face.

And the needle pointed down into a valley full of farmers and beekeepers and one sleeping mountain of ancient fire, and the writ said *burn what answers*, and Corvin Ashe sat on his horse doing the arithmetic that doctrine forbade:

If the thing answering prayers was in this valley — if it had come here, to the one place magic could not follow, the one place in all the world where an answered prayer should be impossible — then either the ward had failed, or the thing answering was stronger than the ward, and either way, one inquisitor with a torch and a writ was not going to accomplish anything except becoming a report in someone else's file.

Or.

Or the thing answering was *from* here. Had always been from here. Had lived here ten years among the bees, keeping its head down, keeping its hands empty, and had woken eleven days ago to a world suddenly full of voices calling its name.

He did not let himself finish that thought. He dismounted instead, and hobbled the horse, and walked to the edge of the ridge where the grass gave out and the fused black glass began, and looked down at the crater with the sun going red behind him.

"Vaskar," he said aloud, because there was no one to hear, and saying names aloud was the oldest habit of a faith he officially did not practice. "You're still down there, aren't you. Still listening."

The crater said nothing. Craters generally didn't. But the needle in its case sang one clear note, sweet and terrible, and Corvin chose — privately, without witnesses, in the small courtroom of himself — to count it as an answer.

* * *

He went down into the valley at dusk, on foot, leading the horse, because a rider on the ridge road announced himself for miles and he wanted — he told himself — to assess before engaging. Assess. A good doctrinal word. He wore no colors; he had left the ash-gray cloak of the inquisitoriate rolled in his saddlebag, and in his plain travel leathers he was just a man, a traveler, a shape in the failing light.

The first holding he passed was a bee-farm, and the farmer was outside in the last light, working his hives bare-handed, no veil, no smoke, humming to himself. The bees moved around him in a slow golden weather. Corvin watched from the fence line, and the farmer looked up and nodded at him the way men nod at strangers on roads — easy, unafraid — and said, "Storm tomorrow. Big one. You'll want shelter past the mill."

"You read clouds?" Corvin asked.

The farmer laughed. "No need. Bees tell me." He patted the nearest hive box the way you'd pat a dog. "Everything tells me, these days. Whole valley's gotten talkative." He said it cheerfully, without any sense at all that he had said something impossible, and went back to his hives, and Corvin stood at the fence with the hair rising on his arms.

*Everything tells me, these days.*

The ward was cracked. He hadn't needed the needle to tell him. Ten years of silence and now the whole valley was waking up like a limb remembering it has nerves, and somewhere down in that green bowl was the epicenter, the place the resonance pooled and sang, and the needle swung in his case like a compass finding north:

Toward a cottage on the valley's western slope. Toward smoke from a chimney and lamplight in a window and, when he crested the rise close enough to see, toward a woman in the garden.

She was older. Of course she was; ten years is ten years everywhere. Her hair was longer than he remembered, bound back with a strip of cloth, and she moved among her hive-boxes with the unhurried certainty of someone who has made peace with a place. There was a girl with her — small, dark-haired, maybe eight, carrying a smoker and chattering, and the woman laughed at something the girl said, and the laugh crossed the distance between them and went through Corvin Ashe like a spear through wet paper.

Elodie Eastwick. Alive. Keeping bees. Laughing.

He had rehearsed this moment for ten years without knowing it. In the rehearsal he always dismounted, always presented the writ, always said the words — *by authority of the god-emperor, I place you under inquiry* — and she always looked at him with those storm-gray eyes and asked the question he could never answer in the rehearsal either: *Did you come all this way for me, Corvin, or for the thing you think I am?*

In reality he stood in the shadow of a drystone wall with the needle singing against his ribs and did nothing at all.

Because that was when he saw the other smoke.

Not chimney smoke. It rose from the valley's eastern rim, where the hills dipped toward the pass — a thin black column against the last violet of the sky, too straight, too deliberate. Signal smoke. And beneath it, catching the dying light, the glint of metal on the ridge road: riders. Six of them, maybe eight, in armor that threw back the sunset in dull orange, moving down toward the valley at a canter, and at their head a banner he knew the way you know a scar.

The ash-gray of the inquisitoriate. Someone had not come alone after all. Someone had followed the same needle he had, or been sent on the same writ, and they had not stopped on the ridge to do arithmetic.

They were going to burn what answers. And everything in this valley was starting to answer.

The girl saw the riders first — children always saw things first — and pointed, and Elodie Eastwick turned and looked east, and even across the whole width of the valley Corvin saw her go still, absolutely still, the stillness of a woman listening to weather three days off and not liking the forecast.

Then she looked west. Straight at the wall where he stood. Straight at him, across forty yards of dusk, and he saw her eyes narrow — not recognition, not yet, but the animal knowledge of being watched, the old perception he had loved her for, waking up after ten years of sleep.

The needle in its case sang one long, clear note.

Corvin Ashe stepped back from the wall, heart hammering, and began — for the first time in ten years of perfect obedience — to decide which side of his own writ he was standing on.
