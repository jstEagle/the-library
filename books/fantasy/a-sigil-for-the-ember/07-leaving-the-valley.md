## 7. Leaving the Valley

The ward broke at dusk.

Elodie knew it the way she knew weather — not with her eyes but in her teeth, in the old scar along her forearm that had ached for ten years whenever a storm came near. The valley's silence, that thick wool-wrapped hush she had learned to live inside like a second skin, tore open all at once, and the world came pouring through the wound.

She was in the apiary when it happened, smoke-board in hand, about to lift the first hive of autumn honey. The bees went mad against their combs — not the ordinary fury of a robbed colony but something older and stranger, a sound like a thousand small wings reciting one word over and over. *Run,* said the hive. *Run, run, run.*

Elodie set down the smoke-board very carefully, as if the carefulness of it could hold the moment still.

Overhead, the rain was falling upward.

It had been doing strange things since morning — pooling in the air like held breath, drifting sideways against no wind — but this was different. This was the sky itself failing, the water climbing back toward clouds that were already gone, and beyond the rim of the valley, where the ward should have stood between here and everywhere else, the evening light bent wrong. It folded. It gathered into a seam of white-gold fire, thin as a knife cut, and through that seam Elodie saw torches. Dozens of them. Descending the switchback road that no traveler had walked in ten years because no traveler could.

"Inquisitors," Sera said from behind her.

Elodie hadn't heard the priestess come up the path. She was still half-burned, bandages gone gray at the edges, and she moved like a woman carrying something breakable inside her chest — which, Elodie supposed, she was. The bronze sigil hung at her throat, humming its low struck-bell note, and now the hum changed pitch, rising, eager, like a dog scenting its own kind.

"You said they'd take days," Elodie said. "You said we'd have time."

"I said I hoped." Sera's voice was flat with exhaustion. "Hope isn't a map, Dragonkeeper."

Wren came pelting down from the cottage, barefoot, hair unbound, the girl's face white around her eyes. She skidded to a stop between them, breathing hard, and pointed back the way she'd come without managing any words at all.

"The well," she got out finally. "The well water's standing straight up. Like a rope. Like a — like a finger pointing."

*The valley is answering them,* Elodie thought. *Everything in it is answering them.* The sigil's resonance had soaked into the land for three days now, and the land, starved of magic for a decade, had drunk it down greedily, and now every spring and stone and sleeping root was glowing like a lamp in a dark field, visible from anywhere, visible from the rim, visible from the road where men with torches and iron questions were already descending.

"Vaskar," Elodie said.

Sera blinked. "The dragon? He sleeps. You told me he sleeps out his last centuries."

"He does." Elodie pulled off her veil, dropped it on the grass. Her hands were steady; she made them be steady. "But he also dug. A hundred years before I was born, when the ward was raised, he carved tunnels under the eastern ridge — ways out that don't cross the ward so much as go beneath it, the way a river goes under a wall. He showed me once. I was nineteen and furious and I swore I'd never use them." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I swore a lot of things at nineteen."

"And now?"

"Now there are three of us and one of me, and I can't fight inquisitors with a smoke-board." She looked east, toward the crater, toward the long dark throat of stone she had avoided for ten years the way you avoid a grave. "Get Wren. Take nothing you can't carry in one hand. Meet me at the old kiln by the bee-meadow. If I'm not there in a quarter hour—"

"What do we do?"

"Leave anyway." She said it simply, and watched Sera understand what it cost, and hated her a little for understanding so quickly.

* * *

The kiln was a moss-eaten dome of brick half-swallowed by bramble, and behind it, hidden under a curtain of ivy Vaskar had grown himself out of sheer vanity, the tunnel mouth breathed cold air into Elodie's face like an animal's exhale.

She had brought a lantern, a jar of honey, and the storm-scarred cloak she'd worn the day of her trial, though she couldn't have said why. Some things you carry even when carrying them hurts.

Sera arrived first, Wren's hand locked in hers, the girl clutching a bundle that clinked — jars, Elodie realized. The child had packed jars of preserved plums. Something in Elodie's chest turned over gently and did not right itself.

"Is it true," Wren whispered, staring at the dark mouth in the hillside, "that a dragon lives down there?"

"He lives above it," Elodie said. "He sleeps in the crater. The tunnels are just — his cellar."

"Dragons have cellars?"

"This one has opinions about architecture. Come on."

They went in single file, Sera's sigil providing a faint bronze glow that made the lantern almost unnecessary, and the dark closed over them like water. Behind them, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, the valley began to burn.

Elodie felt it before she heard it — the heat pressing through rock, the particular wrongness of green things burning, hives and hedgerows and the cottage with its blue door and the bed she'd slept in for ten years. The inquisitors didn't loot first. They burned first and looted after; everyone knew that; she'd sat through enough trials to know how the god-emperor's people worked. They would find the empty hives. They would find the abandoned cottage. They would stand in the ashes of her life and ask each other where the Dragonkeeper had gone, and the answer would be: down, and east, and away.

She kept walking. One foot, then the other. The tunnel sloped gently downward, then turned, then forked, and at the fork Vaskar's claw-marks still scored the wall — a directional sign, absurdly, scratched in elegant High Draconic that translated roughly to *this way, fool.* Ten years ago she had traced those marks with her fingers and sworn never to follow them. Now she followed them. That was exile, she thought. Exile was a promise you made to your own suffering, and suffering always broke its word eventually.

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked quietly. She had fallen into step beside Elodie, matching her stride with the unconscious precision children have. "Leaving. Does it hurt like a wound or like a bruise?"

Elodie looked down at her. In the sigil-light the girl's eyes were enormous and entirely serious.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because you're walking like my mother walked when we left Marrowfield." Wren's voice didn't waver. "Like it hurt but she didn't want anyone to see. So I'm asking so you don't have to pretend."

For a moment Elodie couldn't speak at all. She put her hand on the girl's head, briefly, clumsily — she had never been good at gentleness, it was the one magic she'd never had — and said, "Like a wound. But wounds close."

"Mama's didn't."

"No," Elodie said. "Some don't. We walk anyway."

Behind them, far off, something vast collapsed — the cottage roof, maybe, or the apiary, or the whole shape of the life she had built out of bees and distance and deliberate forgetting. She did not look back. Looking back, she had learned at nineteen, was how you lost people: you looked back and the thing you loved became the thing you carried, and the weight of it slowed your feet until the fire caught you too.

So she looked forward, into the dark, and walked.

* * *

They had been underground perhaps two hours when the tunnel began to change.

The rough-hewn walls smoothed. Claw-marks gave way to surfaces that gleamed faintly, as if polished by breath over centuries, and the air warmed and took on a smell Elodie remembered from her childhood in the capital — hot stone and sulfur and something underneath both, something like cinnamon left too long near a flame. Dragon-smell. The smell of a creature whose body was a slow furnace that had never fully gone out.

The passage opened without warning into a gallery vast enough that the lantern light failed to reach either wall. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some buried organ. And the floor — the floor was scattered with gold.

Not treasure, exactly. Not coins and crowns. Bones, Elodie realized, her stomach dropping — no. Not bones. Teeth. Shed teeth, hundreds of them, each longer than her forearm, yellowed with age, lying among drifts of scale like autumn leaves. A dragon's whole life, molted and discarded across centuries of sleep.

"Don't touch," said a voice like a landslide learning to speak.

Elodie froze. Sera made a small sound. Wren stepped in front of both of them, arms spread, which would have been funny if Elodie's heart weren't hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird.

At the far end of the gallery, where the tunnel narrowed again, a head the size of a hay wagon lifted from the floor. One eye opened — amber, slit-pupiled, ancient, and dimmer than any eye should be, guttering like a candle in a draft. Vaskar regarded them for a long moment with the weary patience of someone who has seen everything twice.

"The keeper returns," he rumbled. "With a priestess who smells of drowning, and a child who smells of plums, and the stink of imperial fire on the wind. I sleep four hundred years and wake to a house fire. Typical."

"They're burning the valley," Elodie said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Inquisitors. They breached the ward."

"I know. I felt it. Do you know what it feels like, little storm-caller, to feel a ward you helped raise crack like an egg? Like losing a tooth." His nostrils flared, twin furnaces breathing. "You're taking my tunnels east."

"Yes."

"Hm." The great head swung toward Sera, and the sigil at her throat sang louder, a clear ringing note that filled the gallery. Vaskar's eye fixed on it, and something moved through the dim amber — recognition, and grief, and a bitterness so old it had fossilized into humor. "And that. Of course. That thing again. It called to me in my sleep for three days, priestess. Whispering. Asking. Doors are rude that way."

"Can we pass?" Sera asked.

"You're already passing. My tunnels don't ask permission; that's rather the point of them." He lowered his head closer, and the heat of his breath washed over them, and Elodie saw up close how thin he had become — scales dull, the great wing-frames sharp beneath skin, a monument to himself slowly eroding. When he spoke again, the sardonic note had dropped away, and what remained was quieter and worse. "East, the tunnels surface in the ghost-hills, past the saltmarsh. Three days' walk. The wardens will have dogs on the roads within a day. Keep to the dead places — the roads that aren't on maps. Maps are how the empire thinks. Confuse its thinking and you confuse its hands."

"Thank you," Elodie said.

"Don't thank me yet." The eye swiveled back to her, and held her, and she felt the old weight of it, the weight of being known by something that had watched her whole life from the bottom of a crater. "You're leaving the valley, Elodie Eastwick. Do you understand what that means? For ten years the ward has kept more than magic out. It kept you small. Out there, the sky is enormous, and storms remember you, and you will have to decide, every hour, whether to be the woman who tends bees or the woman who calls lightning. Both of them are you. Only one of them survives contact with the world."

"That's not fair," Wren said indignantly, from in front of Elodie, fists on hips. "She's both. She can be both."

Vaskar's gaze slid down to the child. Something ancient and amused flickered in the amber depths.

"Well," he said. "Perhaps the plum-hoarder knows best." He settled his head back onto the floor with a grinding sigh that shook dust from the ceiling. "Go. And keeper — when you stand under open sky tonight, and it feels like drowning—" the eye was closing, the voice fading toward sleep, "—remember that drowning is only water deciding to hold you. Some of us call that belonging."

* * *

They surfaced at moonrise.

The tunnel ended in a screen of gorse on a hillside, and Elodie pushed through it into night air that hit her like a wave — huge, moving, alive with scents she had forgotten existed: woodsmoke from a distant farm, wet earth, sheep, the mineral tang of the saltmarsh miles to the south, and under everything the sea, invisible but present, breathing on the horizon.

Ten years. Ten years of valley air, filtered and tamed and warded, and now this — the whole unguarded atmosphere of the world pouring into her lungs, and with it, faint and far and unmistakable, the taste of a coming rain. Not a storm. Just rain, somewhere over the hills, ordinary weather, and still her skin prickled and her pulse jumped and something in her blood turned toward it like a flower toward sun.

*There you are,* said the old hunger, warm and horrible. *There you are, and no one is watching.*

"Elodie." Sera's hand on her arm. "Breathe."

"I am breathing."

"You're gasping. There's a difference." The priestess eased her down onto a flat stone, and Wren pressed a jar of plums into her hands with tremendous gravity, as if this were medicine, and perhaps it was. Elodie ate one. It was too sweet and slightly bruised and the best thing she had ever tasted, because she was free and terrified and grieving and the sky was so large it made her dizzy.

Below them, far behind, the Hollow Valley glowed. From this distance the fires were almost beautiful — a ring of orange light cradled in black hills, like a hearth seen through a keyhole. Her home. Her bees. The grave-markers she'd never carved for the forty dead, the penance she'd tended instead, all of it burning because a bronze disc had hummed three days too loudly.

"I spent ten years learning to live small," she said. Nobody had asked; she said it anyway, to the night, to the plums, to the two figures beside her. "Small garden. Small life. Small weather. I thought if I made myself quiet enough, the past would forget my name." She laughed, and it came out wet. "Turns out the past doesn't forget. It just waits for you to leave the house."

Wren climbed onto the stone beside her and leaned against her shoulder, small and solid and smelling of smoke and honey.

"We'll build another house," the girl said, with the absolute confidence of someone who had lost three homes already and survived all three losses. "A better one. With room for the dragon's opinion about architecture."

Elodie put her arm around her and looked east, where the ghost-hills waited, and the dead roads, and whatever came next.

Far to the north, past the black line of the ridges, a red glow was rising that had nothing to do with fire — a stain on the underside of the clouds, pulsing slowly, like a heartbeat. Sera saw it too. Her hand went to the sigil, and the sigil answered, ringing soft and urgent in the dark.

"That's not the valley burning," Sera whispered.

"No," Elodie said, and stood, and felt the whole enormous sky lean in around her. "That's the direction of the capital."

The rain she had tasted earlier chose that moment to arrive — first drops cool on her upturned face, ordinary, harmless, hers to ignore if she wanted. She wanted. She let them fall, and turned her back on the burning valley, and led her strange small family down into the dark hills, toward the glow on the horizon and the door that waited beneath a city that no longer existed.
