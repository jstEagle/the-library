## 8. The Road That Isn't on Maps

Wren counted the strangers the way she used to count her teeth: carefully, and with a small worry that one of them might go missing.

There was Elodie, who had pulled her out of the flood by the collar of her dress like a cat lifting a kitten, and who talked to bees when she thought no one was listening. There was Sera, who was burned down one whole side of her body and never once complained about it, which made Wren think she must be very brave or very stupid, and Sera did not seem stupid. And there was the dragon.

The dragon was not walking with them. The dragon was a warm feeling in the ground under their feet sometimes, and a glow far off in the north at night, and once — only once — a voice in Wren's head that had tasted of smoke and old honey and said, *So you're the small one. Mind the priestess; she leaks.* Wren had decided she liked him enormously.

Four, then. Four was a good number. Four fit around a fire. Four could hold hands across a table if there were ever a table, which so far there had not been.

They walked the road that wasn't on maps because, according to Elodie, all the other roads belonged to people who wanted to burn them.

* * *

The road itself was strange in a way Wren couldn't stop noticing, like a tooth with a rough edge your tongue kept finding. It was paved — really paved, with fitted stones worn smooth as river pebbles — but nothing traveled it. No carts. No hoofprints. Weeds grew up through the seams in long green stitches, and here and there a birch tree had pushed its way right through the middle of the road and stood there, white and smug, as if to say the road had been its idea all along.

"Nobody uses this anymore?" Wren asked.

"Nobody remembers it," said Elodie.

"That's different."

Elodie looked down at her for a moment. She had gray eyes, storm-colored, and when she looked at you it felt like weather deciding something about you. "Yes," she said finally. "It is."

Wren decided then that she liked being agreed with by Elodie Eastwick more than almost anything else in the world, except honey, and the moment just after you stopped crying.

Sera walked behind them, favoring her burned side, humming under her breath. Her humming wasn't a tune exactly. It was more like the sound a bell makes after it stops ringing, if the bell couldn't quite let go. Wren had noticed that things near Sera hummed too. The bronze thing she carried — wrapped in three layers of cloth and tied against her chest like a baby — hummed loudest of all. Sometimes at night Wren could feel it through the ground, a slow pulse, like something enormous asleep on the other side of a wall.

"What's the bell-lady's name?" Wren whispered to Elodie, though Sera was close enough to hear every word. Whispering loudly was one of Wren's talents.

"Sera Ivane," said Sera, without opening her eyes. "And I'm not a lady. I'm a priestess. Ladies have shoes."

Wren looked down at Sera's feet. They were wrapped in rags. She looked down at her own feet, also wrapped in rags. She felt a sudden fierce kinship.

"We're both shoeless," Wren announced. "That means we're family."

Sera opened her eyes then, and something crossed her face — quick, like a fish under ice — before she smiled. "Is that how it works?"

"That's how everything works," said Wren. "You share bread, you're family. You share a blanket, you're family. You're both shoeless—" she spread her hands, *obviously* "—that's practically blood."

Behind them, Elodie made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed halfway, like a bird changing its mind mid-flight.

* * *

They came to the first milestone on the second day.

It stood alone where the road bent around a hill, taller than Wren, carved from pale stone gone soft-edged with rain. Someone had cut words into it long ago, deep and careful, and someone else — later, angrier — had chiseled them out again. All that remained were shallow scars where letters had been, a name erased so thoroughly that even the shape of it was gone.

Wren put her hand flat against the stone. It was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm like a hand.

"There was a town here," Sera said quietly. "Ondrel way, maybe. Or one of the river towns." She didn't touch the milestone. She stood a little apart from it, the way you stand apart from a sickbed.

"Why did they scratch the names off?"

"Because the god-emperor said those towns never happened," said Elodie. "And the empire is very good at making true things behave like lies."

Wren frowned at the stone. "That doesn't work. Things happen whether or not anyone writes them down. I happened, and nobody wrote me down anywhere, and I'm still happening *all over the place*."

There was a silence. Then Elodie crouched down beside her, eye level, and her storm-gray eyes had gone strange and soft. "No," she said. "No, they don't get to unmake you, little one. You're quite correct."

Wren hadn't known she'd needed correcting about anything, but the warmth of it went through her anyway, all the way down, like the first swallow of soup on a cold day. She memorized the feeling. She was building a collection of them.

* * *

At night they slept in the ruins of what Sera called a waystation — a travelers' house from the old kingdom, roof fallen in, walls still standing waist-high and shoulder-high in places, a hearth like a black mouth. Elodie built a fire with a flint and a lot of swearing, because, she explained when Wren asked, magic couldn't follow her here and besides, "fire you make with your hands teaches you patience."

"Does it?"

"No," said Elodie. "It mostly teaches you swearing."

Wren laughed until her ribs hurt, and Elodie looked startled, as if she'd forgotten the sound existed, and then she laughed too, low and rusty, and Wren added that to the collection: *the sound Elodie makes when she forgets to be sad.*

They shared bread that night. Hard travel-bread, half stale, split four ways — well, three ways, plus the piece Wren crumbled onto the hearthstone for the dragon, because it seemed polite, and because she had decided the dragon was the fourth member of the family whether he knew it yet or not.

"You know he can't eat that," Elodie said.

"It's not for eating. It's for knowing we thought of him."

Elodie stared at her for a long moment. "He's going to be insufferable about this," she said, but softly, and Wren understood that she had passed some kind of test whose rules nobody had told her.

Under the broken roof, with the stars coming through the gaps like holes punched in dark cloth, Wren lay between the two women and listened to them breathe. Sera breathed carefully, guarding her burned side even in sleep. Elodie breathed like someone who had spent ten years listening for footsteps and couldn't stop now that there weren't any.

Wren had been alone since the flood took the village. Before that she'd been alone since the fever took her mother, and before that she'd been small and not alone, which she barely remembered, more like a story someone had told her about another girl. She had learned that families were not the people who shared your blood. Families were the people who heard you coming and didn't hide.

These two heard her coming. They left room for her by the fire without being asked. When she talked too much — and she always talked too much; her mother had said she had a mouth that ran ahead of her feet — neither of them told her to be quiet. Sera answered her questions, even the ones about the drowned god, slowly and seriously, as if a seven-year-old's questions deserved real answers, which Wren had privately suspected for years and was delighted to have confirmed.

So she made the decision there, in the dark, between the breathing of two strangers: these were hers now. Whatever came. If the empire came over the hills, she would bite it. If the gods came back, she would stand in front of these two and be extremely loud at them. Nobody had asked her to decide this. That was what made it a real family, she figured — the ones you got given could be taken away, but the ones you chose, you chose with your whole chest.

She fell asleep smiling, with ash from the fire dusting her hair like gray snow.

* * *

The ghost-road grew stranger the farther they went.

On the fourth day they passed a bridge over a dry riverbed, and the bridge was perfect — not one stone missing — while everything around it had rotted back into grass. On the fifth day they passed an orchard of trees still bearing fruit, small and hard and sweet, that no one had picked in ten years. Sera said a blessing over the apples before they ate them, and Elodie said nothing at all, but Wren saw her looking at the orchard the way you look at a grave.

And everywhere, the milestones.

Some were chiseled blank like the first. Some had been toppled face-down into the dirt, and Elodie would not let Wren turn them over. One had been smashed to gravel, deliberately, thoroughly, the way you break a thing when breaking it isn't enough and you keep hitting it anyway. Wren knelt among the fragments and found a single surviving corner of lettering — three strokes of a *K*, maybe, or a *T* — and held it in her palm until it warmed, and then set it gently back down.

"Who were they?" she asked, walking again. "The towns."

"People," said Sera. "Bakers and boatmen and children who talked too much."

"Like me."

"Just like you."

"And the emperor erased them because he was scared of them?"

Sera was quiet for a few steps. "Because they prayed to the old gods," she said at last. "And when he crowned himself with a new god's light, he couldn't bear a country full of people who remembered older ones. So he unwrote them. Roads, records, names. He called it mercy." Her voice did something complicated on the word *mercy*. "I was ordained in one of those towns. I think. I've started to forget which."

Wren reached up and took Sera's rag-wrapped hand. Sera's fingers closed around hers, careful of their own strength, and stayed there the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

On the sixth day, the road began to sing.

Not loudly. Wren noticed it first as a feeling in her back teeth, then in the soles of her feet, and then, when she pressed her ear to the paving stones, unmistakably: a note, low and sustained, rising up out of the earth itself, as if the road were a string someone had plucked a hundred years ago and it was still ringing.

"The stones remember being walked on," Sera said. "Enough feet, enough years, and the road holds the shape of the going. The old kingdom's roads were laid with songs in them. Blessing-songs. So travelers wouldn't arrive lonely."

"Did it work?"

"I don't know. I never walked one until now." Sera's good hand rested on the wrapped sigil at her chest, and Wren saw that the humming of the bronze thing had changed to match the road — the same note, the same pulse, two voices finding each other. Sera's face went pale under the burn-scar. "Elodie," she said. "It knows where it's going."

Elodie had stopped walking. Ahead of them the road crested a low rise, and beyond the rise the land fell away into a wide gray valley, and in the middle of the valley, faint with distance, stood the silhouette of a bridge — vast, arched, older than anything Wren had ever seen, spanning a gorge that held fog instead of water.

"That's not on my map either," Elodie said slowly. "And my map has roads on it that haven't existed since my grandmother's grandmother."

"The ghost-roads all lead somewhere," Sera said. "We've been following the song without choosing to. This is the Tollbridge at Grayford." She swallowed. "In the old days, you paid it a memory to cross."

Wren looked from one woman's face to the other. Elodie's jaw had gone tight as a knot. Sera's hand hadn't left the sigil.

"A memory," Wren repeated. "Like — you give it one and it keeps it? Forever?"

"So the stories say."

Wren thought about her collection. The sound of Elodie laughing. The weight of Sera's hand around hers. The taste of orchard apples eaten sitting in a dead king's road. The warm feeling of the dragon under the ground, and the piece of bread on the hearthstone, and the moment Elodie had crouched down and told her that she was quite correct and no one got to unmake her.

Her chest went tight and hot.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted, so she planted her feet and made it bigger. "Well, then we don't cross it. We go around. Rivers have bottoms. I've waded worse."

"Wrens don't decide the route," Elodie said, but her hand came down on Wren's head, heavy and warm, and stayed there.

Ahead, the fog in the gorge shifted, and for one heartbeat Wren would swear — though she never told anyone this, not for years — the fog shaped itself briefly into faces. Hundreds of them. Travelers, maybe. Or tolls already paid.

The road sang on beneath their feet, patient as stone, and it sounded, Wren thought, horribly like it was asking them a question.
