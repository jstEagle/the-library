## 9. Tolls of the Ghost-Road

The bridge appeared on the third morning, exactly where the mapmaker's daughter had said it would be, which was the first thing about the ghost-road that frightened Sera Ivane: it kept its promises.

She had walked roads that lied before. Roads in Caelmere proper, under the god-emperor's sun, where milestones pointed to cities that had been renamed and rivers that had been dammed and a cathedral that officially had never existed at all. She had learned to distrust every honest-looking signpost. But this road — the old road, the dead road, the one that ran beneath the skin of the new kingdom like a vein beneath skin — this one told the truth. The milestones bore names no one spoke anymore. *Ondrel, four leagues. The Weeping Ford, two.* And now, rising out of the mist where the marsh gave way to higher ground, a bridge of grey stone with arches like folded hands.

It should not have been there. Sera knew the geography of this country better than any living priestess; she had memorized the drowned god's parishes the way other children memorized their prayers. There was no river here. There had been no river here for three hundred years, not since the flood that took the lowlands and gave her order its name and its god and, eventually, its grave. And yet the bridge stood over water — dark, slow, patient water that had no business existing and looked as though it knew it.

"Stop," Elodie said quietly.

Sera stopped. Behind her, Wren bumped into her back and grabbed a fistful of her robe, the way she always did when the world did something wrong. The girl had not let go of either of them for more than a few minutes since the valley burned. She held on now with both hands, small fingers twisting into the scorched hem, and peered around Sera's hip at the bridge with the flat, assessing stare of a child who had already learned that beautiful things were often traps.

"It's just a bridge," Wren said.

"It's never just a bridge," Elodie said.

* * *

They made camp within sight of it but not too near, because Elodie insisted, and because Sera had learned in ten days of travel that Elodie Eastwick's instincts were worth more than doctrine. She built the fire herself — small, shielded, smokeless, the fire of someone who had spent a decade being hunted by nothing and had somehow retained all the habits of prey. Sera watched her work and thought, not for the first time, that exile had not broken this woman so much as folded her up very small, the way you fold a letter you intend to keep but cannot bear to read.

Wren fell asleep first, curled against Sera's side with her thumb in her mouth, though she was surely too old for it and would deny it fiercely if anyone mentioned it. The firelight moved on Elodie's face. Sera found herself studying the storm-caller the way she studied scripture: looking for the verse she'd missed, the line that would make the whole text cohere.

"You're staring," Elodie said, without looking up from the blade she was sharpening against a whetstone she'd produced from somewhere impossible.

"I'm thinking."

"About the bridge."

"About what it will ask."

Elodie set down the stone. "You know what it asks."

"I know what the stories say." Sera drew her robe tighter. The night was warm, but she had been cold since the ward burned through her crossing — cold in a way that sat in the bones rather than the skin, and that no fire seemed to reach. "A memory. One memory, freely given, to cross. The toll-takers of the ghost-road were the drowned god's servants once. Ferrymen for souls. When the order died, the roads kept their habits, the way dogs keep the habits of dead masters."

"And which memory will you give it?"

The question landed cleanly, the way all of Elodie's questions landed — no cruelty in it, only precision. Sera had prepared an answer. She had prepared it on the second day of travel, when she'd realized the bridges would come, and she had polished it every night since, the way you polish a lie: until it shone, until it felt smooth in the mouth, until you could almost believe it yourself.

"My ordination," she said. "That's what I told you I'd pay. The day I took my vows. It's the right shape for it — big enough to satisfy the toll, small enough to survive losing. What is one ceremony to a woman who has already lost her whole church?"

Elodie looked at her then, directly, with those grey eyes that seemed to hold weather in them even here, even magicless, even exiled. She looked for a long moment. Then she nodded and said, "All right," and Sera understood that she had passed some examination she hadn't known she was sitting, and that the passing meant less than she wanted it to.

Because it was a lie. Not the ordination itself — that part was true; she had decided, truly decided, that the ordination was the price she could afford. But underneath the decision, in the place where decisions are actually made, she had known for two days that when the bridge asked, she would give it something else. Something older. Something she could not afford.

She was a priestess. Lying was supposed to have been carved out of her like a rotten tooth. Instead the lies had grown in the socket, filling the space, and she had fed them, and they had teeth of their own now.

She slept badly, and dreamed of bells.

* * *

Morning came up white and slow out of the marsh, and the bridge stood waiting, and the water beneath it stood stiller than still, and Wren said, "I don't like it," and nobody argued with her.

They approached on foot, single file, Elodie in front with her staff — a plain thing of ash wood, unremarkable, except that Sera had seen her sleep holding it the way soldiers sleep holding weapons. Halfway across the first span, the mist thickened. Not drifted — *thickened*, deliberately, the way a room goes dark when someone closes a door. And in the middle of the bridge, where the arches met the sky, something stood up out of the stone.

It had been part of the bridge all along; that was the horror of it. A figure the same grey as the masonry, robed the same way the arches were curved, faceless in the way that worn carvings are faceless — features rubbed away not by time but by use, by centuries of leaning close to travelers to listen. It did not move. It did not need to move. The bridge moved around it, or the mist did, or the morning did.

When it spoke, the voice came up through the soles of their feet, through the stone, through the water.

*A toll.*

"We know," Elodie said. Her voice was steady. Sera marveled at it, and hated that she marveled, and marveled that she hated. "One memory. Freely given. We've heard."

*Not yours,* the figure said, and the blankness of its face turned — there was no other word for it — toward Sera. Toward the scorched ruin of her robes, toward the sigil wrapped and hidden against her heart, toward whatever it was that the drowned god's servants recognized in one another across three hundred years of silence. *Hers. The bearer pays. That is the old law. The road remembers the old law even if no one else does.*

"Why her?" Elodie's hand had shifted on the staff. "We carry it together."

*The road does not care how you carry it. Only who bears it.* The voice softened, or seemed to — became, horribly, gentle, the gentleness of deep water. *And the bearer has been deciding for two nights which memory to bring me. I have listened. I am patient. Decide now.*

Sera's mouth went dry. *Two nights.* It had heard everything — every rehearsal, every polishing of the lie, every moment she'd stood watch alone and told herself the ordination was enough, was fair, was survivable. Had it heard the truth underneath? Had it heard the name?

She knelt, because kneeling was the oldest reflex she had, older than lying. She pressed her palms to the cold stone and closed her eyes and reached into herself, into the archive of her own life that every priestess builds and tends and keeps locked, and she stood for a moment on the threshold of the two memories, weighing them.

The ordination: incense and bronze bells, the choir of the flooded nave, the god's presence like a hand laid on the crown of her head. Losing it would hurt like losing a photograph of a house that burned. She would know it happened. She would feel the absence shaped like it. But she would keep everything the ordination had *made* her.

The other memory: she was nine years old. Her mother was alive. They were standing on the sea-wall at Ondrel — Ondrel, before it unhappened — and her mother had put a cowrie shell in her palm and closed her fingers over it and said, *the sea gives back what it loves, little fish, remember that when it takes things.* And Sera had believed her, wholly, the way children believe, and that belief had been the foundation stone of everything — the vocation, the faith, the long devotion to a god of return. Take away the ordination and you take away the office. Take away the shell and the sea-wall and her mother's living voice, and you take away the reason there was ever an office at all. You take away the root. Everything above it stays green for a while, and then it doesn't.

The bridge waited. The mist waited. Behind her, she heard Wren breathing fast and small, and Elodie saying nothing, giving her nothing, letting her choose — and that silence was its own weight, heavier than any demand.

Sera paid the toll.

She did not tell them which memory she gave. That was the second lie, and it was easier than the first, because by then she was practiced. She rose from the stone lighter — genuinely, physically lighter, the way an amputated limb is lighter — and the faceless figure inclined its head, and the mist opened, and the far side of the bridge stood clear and ordinary in the morning light.

"What did it take?" Elodie asked, when they were across. Wren was looking up at her with those enormous eyes, and Sera smiled the smile she used for congregations, warm and complete and load-bearing.

"The ordination," she said. "As I promised. Incense and bells and a bishop's cold hands. Honestly, I'm relieved. I never liked the incense."

Wren laughed, uncertain, checking Elodie's face to see whether laughing was safe. Elodie did not laugh. Elodie watched her for a long, quiet moment with those weather-grey eyes, and then she turned and led on down the ghost-road, and said nothing at all.

* * *

They walked. The milestones counted down to Ondrel. Wren collected snail shells off the roadside and lined them along the top of a milestone like an offering to no one, and Sera caught herself explaining, before she could stop, that shells were the drowned god's currency, and then caught the look that passed over Elodie's face — quick, analytical, gone — and understood that she had just given something away.

Because here was the crack, and here was the terrible arithmetic of it: a woman who had lost her ordination should have felt the hole where it was. Should have flinched at the shells, or reached for ritual that wasn't there, or stood bewildered before the milestone-shrine of a god whose vows she could no longer name. But Sera's ordination was intact. She could feel it, whole and humming, somewhere behind her sternum — the vows, the bells, the hand on her crown. What was missing was earlier. What was missing was *before*. She kept reaching for the beginning of her own story and finding a wall where the sea-wall should be, a smooth blank place with her mother's voice on the far side of it, audible only as rhythm, as tide, as something loved and gone.

She knew what she had paid. The bridge knew. And Elodie Eastwick — who had spent ten years learning to read the weather in people's faces because the actual weather had been taken from her — was starting to read hers.

At dusk they sheltered in the hollow of a fallen milestone, and Wren slept, and Elodie sat across the small fire and looked at Sera for a long time before she spoke.

"When we get to Ondrel," she said, "you'll want to see the sea-wall. You'll feel like you have to. Everyone who loses something feels the pull of the place where they lost it."

"All right," Sera said carefully.

"You told us your mother died in the flooding of the cathedral. You told us that story the first night, on the road. You told it well." Elodie poked the fire; sparks went up and died. "But you told it from the outside. Like a story you'd been handed. And today, when Wren lined up those shells, you didn't grieve. You *taught*. A woman who'd just paid her ordination wouldn't teach. She'd ache."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, the marsh made its small wet sounds, patient as the bridge had been patient.

"So I'll ask you once," Elodie said, "and then I won't ask again, because it's yours and I have no right to it. What did the bridge take?"

And Sera Ivane, last priestess of the drowned god, bearer of the sigil, keeper of the door's key, looked at the woman she was beginning — dangerously, against all her training and all her caution — to love, and reached for the polished lie, and found that the lie had a hole in it now too, a smooth blank place shaped like a cowrie shell, and nothing left to fill it.

"I told you," she said. "The ordination."

Elodie held her gaze a moment longer. Then she nodded slowly, and lay back, and pulled her cloak around her, and said, into the dark, in a voice with no accusation in it at all — which was somehow worse than any accusation:

"All right, Sera. Goodnight."

And Sera lay awake between the sleeping child and the sleeping storm-caller, listening to the marsh breathe, feeling the smooth blank place inside herself where her mother's voice used to live, and understanding that she had crossed the bridge twice today — once over the water, and once over something else entirely — and that the second crossing had no far side.

Far to the north, past the dark rim of the world, something vast and old turned over in its sleep, and the ember-light of a dying dragon guttered once, like a candle feeling a draft — and Sera, who could no longer remember why she loved the sea, dreamed of bells ringing underwater, and woke with salt on her face that had not come from her eyes.
