## 11. Ash Finds Them

Corvin Ashe had been in Ondrel for three days before he understood that the city was watching him back.

It was not the watching of wardens or informants — he knew that kind, had been that kind, and could feel its absence here like a missing tooth. This was something older and stranger: a city of people who did not exist, keeping watch over their own nonexistence. Shopkeepers who sold bread to no registered name. Children whose births had never been inked into any ledger. A whole municipality unmade by decree twenty years ago, struck from the rolls and the maps and the tax records, and yet still breathing, still trading, still lighting lamps at dusk — a city practicing the art of being nowhere, together.

He hated it immediately, because it was working.

He sat in the corner of a dye-house converted to a tavern, hood up despite the heat, a cup of small beer going warm in front of him, and let the room teach him its rhythms. The god-emperor's writ ran everywhere else in the world. Here it simply stopped, like a tide meeting a seawall no one remembered building. Corvin had crossed half of dead Caelmere following the sigil's resonance — a hum beneath his hearing, a pressure behind his eyes that sharpened as he closed on it, the way a storm-sharp sky smells before rain — and the trail had led him here, into this city that unhappened itself, and then the trail had done something no trail had ever done to him.

It had gone quiet.

Not faded. Quiet. As if the sigil had stepped behind a door and was holding its breath.

*They know I'm coming,* he thought, not for the first time. *And they're waiting to see what I do.*

The tavern filled with the evening shift of dyers, hands stained blue to the wrist, voices low out of habit even though there were no ears here but theirs. A woman argued cheerfully with her son about the price of madder root. Two old men played a board game with pieces carved from broom handles. Ordinary life, stacked thick and careful, all of it balanced on the single secret of not being written down anywhere.

Corvin thought about orders. He was good at thinking about orders; it was most of what he did. *Burn what answers,* the emperor's seal had said, in the flat gold script that meant the words came from the throne itself and not merely from a man wearing it. *Question nothing.* He had carried those words through nine provinces. He had burned a shrine in Marrowfield where an old woman's prayer for her sick grandson had been answered with a harvest of fish from a dry well — fish that should not have existed, glistening and wrong-eyed, and the whole village weeping with gratitude while the well stank of deep water. He had questioned nothing. He had stood in the smoke and felt the resonance of the sigil pull northward like a hook set in his own chest, and he had followed it, because following it was the only thing left that his life still knew how to do.

Except now the hook had led him to Elodie Eastwick.

He had known it for six days. Since the saltmarsh, since the drowned chapel where someone had prayed a storm out of a clear sky and every warden's weather-glass in the province had shattered at once. He had stood among the broken glass and felt the shape of that storm the way you feel the shape of a voice through a wall, and there had been no doubt in him at all. Ten years, and she still called weather like a signature.

*Burn what answers.*

He drank the warm beer and watched the blue-handed dyers and tried to imagine writing the report. *Subject located. Subject traveling with fugitive priestess of the drowned god and one child, age approximately ten, unregistered. Subject neutralized per directive.* The words arranged themselves easily enough; words always did. It was everything behind the words that refused.

Because he had loved her. That was the fact under all the other facts, the stone at the bottom of the well, and no amount of drawn water ever quite cleared it. He had loved her at nineteen, when she was all elbows and lightning and laughter, when she could read a cloud the way scholars read scripture and thought this made her ordinary. He had loved her badly, quietly, in the way of a boy raised in the Inquisition's dormitories who had learned early that wanting things was how you got them taken away. And when the storm came down on Grayford Vale and killed forty people in eleven minutes — forty names he had never let himself forget, because forgetting them would have been the first step toward forgetting her — Corvin Ashe had stood in the great hall of the Citadel and given testimony.

*She called it,* he had said. His voice had not shaken. He had practiced. *I saw her raise her hands. I heard her say the words.*

Both true. Both lies. He had spent ten years not taking that sentence apart, because he suspected that if he ever did, there would be nothing left of him inside it.

* * *

He found her on the fourth morning, in the market square, and the finding was so easy it frightened him.

That was the thing about Ondrel — the city hid its people from emperors, but it could not hide them from him, because he was not looking for a sigil anymore. He was looking for the way she stood. Ten years should have erased it. Ten years of exile, of bees and valley mud and a magicless life, should have sanded her down into someone he'd have to study to recognize. But she stood at a fruit stall with her weight on her left foot and her head tipped slightly back when she laughed, exactly as she had at nineteen, and Corvin felt the recognition go through him like a blade sliding between ribs — clean, deep, and slow to bleed.

She was haggling over plums. There was a girl beside her — dark-haired, sharp-chinned, maybe ten, holding the basket with both arms like it contained state secrets — and the girl was laughing too, at whatever outrageous thing Elodie was saying to the fruit-seller, and the fruit-seller was laughing, and none of them knew, none of them could know, that the man three stalls away with a cup of cider he wasn't drinking had come to burn them.

Sera Ivane was not with them. He'd find her separately; the priestess moved like a woman used to being hunted, checking sightlines, never standing twice in the same place. But the sigil — the sigil was near. He could feel it now, faint again, like warmth through a wall. On the girl? No. Close to the girl. In the basket, perhaps, wrapped in cloth under the plums. Carried openly through a market by a child, because the safest place to hide a thing everyone feared was in plain sight among people who had already erased themselves.

*Burn what answers.*

Elodie turned, and saw him.

He watched it happen — watched the laughter die in stages, watched her body understand before her mind did, the way prey understands. The basket shifted behind the girl. Elodie's hand came down onto the child's shoulder, light as a bird landing, and Corvin understood that gesture too: not comfort. Positioning. Putting herself between.

"Corvin." She said his name the way you'd spit out a pit. Ten years, and no softness in it at all. Good. He deserved the lack of it. "You're a long way from your master's fire."

"The emperor's reach is long."

"So they keep telling me." Her eyes went past him, quick, cataloguing — counting the exits, counting his escorts, finding none. That puzzled her; he saw it land. An inquisitor alone, in a city that shouldn't exist, walking up to her in broad daylight with no soldiers at his back. "Where's your retinue, Ash? Did the god-emperor finally decide you weren't worth the expense?"

"I came alone."

"That's either mercy or a trap."

"It's neither." He set the cider down on a barrel. His hands wanted to shake and he forbade them. "Walk with me. Ten minutes. Just talk."

"No."

"Maren Voss is four days behind me with two hundred wardens." He said it flatly, without theater, because theater would have told her he was lying and she had always been able to taste a lie on him like copper. "She has a description of Sera Ivane, a resonance-tracker, and a signed order that doesn't distinguish between the priestess, the child, and anyone who shelters them. When she gets here, Ondrel stops being invisible. You understand what that means. Every person in this square who isn't written down anywhere gets written down somewhere very bad."

Around them the market went on — plums changing hands, a fiddler tuning, the blue-stained dyers buying their supper — all of it oblivious, all of it mortal. Elodie's face did something complicated. He watched her weigh him, weigh the claim, weigh the girl's hand tightening on her skirt.

"You're lying," she said, but her voice had lost its edge.

"I've lied to you once in my life," Corvin said, "and it cost forty people their lives and me everything after. Ask me if I'd risk another."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softening, nothing so kind, but a recalculation. She looked down at the girl. "Wren. Take the basket home. The blue door with the fish painted on it. Don't wait for me."

"But—"

"*Don't wait.*" Gentle, and iron underneath. The girl went, backing away three steps before turning, and Corvin noted that she ran not toward safety but toward where Sera had last been seen — the child was trained, or had learned training the hard way. He filed it and hated himself for filing it.

Then it was just the two of them in the moving crowd, and Elodie Eastwick looked at him with ten years of exile in her face and said, "Ten minutes. Talk."

* * *

They walked. Ondrel's streets were narrow and leaned toward each other overhead like conspirators, and the canals smelled of salt and dye, and Corvin kept his hands visible and his pace unhurried because he knew — he had always known — that she could kill him before he finished a single syllable of a binding word. Not with magic. The valley had burned that out of her, or buried it, or she had buried it herself. With a fish knife from any of these stalls, and her elbows, and the particular fury of a woman who had already lost everything once.

"You testified against me," she said, before he'd chosen which truth to open with. Of course she started there. Ten years, and it was still the first stone in her shoe. "At my trial. You stood up in front of the assembled court with your pretty inquisitor's collar and you said—" her voice changed, dropped into a cold imitation of his younger self, "*I saw her raise her hands. I heard her say the words.* Do you have any idea how many nights I've spent in that valley hearing your voice say that?"

"Yes," Corvin said.

That stopped her mid-stride. She turned. "What do you mean, *yes*?"

"I mean I've heard it too. Every night for ten years. I was there." He kept walking; if he stopped he would not start again. "You want the ten minutes to be about whether I'm sorry. I'm not going to give you that, because sorry is cheap and you'd know it was cheap, and you'd be right. I'm going to give you something better. I'm going to tell you what actually happened in that hall, and then you can decide what I am."

The canal curved. Gulls argued over something floating. He spoke to the water, because he could not have spoken to her face.

"There were two testimonies that day. Mine and Maren Voss's. Everyone remembers mine because I was the dramatic one — the lover turned witness, the boy who betrayed his sweetheart for the crown. Good theater. The court ate it. But Maren's testimony is the one that exiled you instead of executing you, and nobody ever asks why, because Maren made sure nobody asked." His throat was dry. "I said what I said because I believed it. That's the truth and I'll die on it — I believed I saw you call that storm. I was half a mile off, in the rain, and I saw a figure on the ridge with her hands up, and I loved you, and I knew what you could do, and grief does arithmetic very fast. Forty dead. Who else but the storm-caller?"

"And now?" Elodie's voice was very quiet. "Ten years older and wiser. Was it me, Corvin?"

He stopped walking. The question hung between them over the black canal water, and underneath it, unspoken, lay the other question, the one he had carried since the saltmarsh, since the shattered weather-glasses: *if it wasn't you, then what did I help bury?*

"I don't know," he said. "I have never known. That's the honest answer and I know it's worthless to you. But I'll tell you what I do know." He turned to face her at last, and let her see whatever was in his face, because hiding it had cost him enough. "Maren stood up after me. And Maren — who was your closest friend in the world, who slept in your shadow for fifteen years — Maren stood up and said, *I was beside her when the storm broke, and I swear by the old law that Elodie Eastwick did not will it.* Sworn testimony, contradicting mine, from a witness closer than I was. And then, in the same breath, before the court could seize on it — Maren said, *but she is the only storm-caller in Caelmere, and the emperor's new law says a caller who cannot prove innocence dies, and I cannot prove it for her, only that I believe it.* And the court heard a loyal friend testifying to uncertainty, and gave you exile instead of the axe, and everyone called Maren merciful."

Elodie had gone very still. "That's not how I remember it."

"No," Corvin said. "I don't expect it is. Memory keeps what it needs. You needed a betrayer, and I volunteered. It was easier for both of us than the alternative."

"Which is?"

"Which is that I don't know why Maren said both of those things in the same breath, and neither do you, and it has been eating me alive for ten years." The words came out rougher than he intended. "Because I've read that transcript a hundred times, Elodie. A hundred. I keep it in my kit with my writs. And every time I read it, I hear a woman trying to save your life in the only language a purge-court is allowed to hear — and failing, and settling for exile, and knowing that exile to the Hollow Valley was the one place in the world the emperor's law couldn't follow. The valley where magic cannot follow. Where *you* couldn't be reached. Tell me that's coincidence. Tell me the High Warden didn't check."

The market noise seemed to recede. Elodie's face had gone white around the mouth. "You're saying she—"

"I'm saying I don't know. I'm saying I've had ten years and a hundred readings and I still don't know, and I hate it, and I hate that I handed the court my certainty when I had none, because my certainty is what made Maren's doubt necessary." He breathed. "I testified at your trial. That's the first thing you need from me and I've said it. Here's the second. Four days. Maybe less. Maren Voss is coming to Ondrel with two hundred wardens and a signed order, and she is coming for the sigil, and she will burn this city's invisibility to get it, and every unregistered soul here will pay for our war. So you have a choice, and I can't make it for you, and gods forgive me, I'm about to make it worse."

"Don't," Elodie said.

"One hour." He said it quickly, before his courage curdled. "I report a false trail south, toward Grayford. It buys you until tomorrow's dusk — no more, Maren's tracker is better than my lie. One hour from now I walk out of Ondrel and file that report, and whatever you do with the hour is yours. Run. Hide deeper. Go north to the cathedral, go wherever the dead god is pulling you — I know it's pulling you, I can feel the sigil humming from here like a struck bell that won't stop ringing. But if you're still in this city when Maren arrives, I won't be able to save you. Do you understand? Whatever I feel, whatever I owed you, whatever I—"

"Stop." Her voice cracked across the word. She was staring at him with an expression he had no map for — fury and grief and something older than both, something from before the trial, from a summer when they'd been nineteen and invincible and the worst thing in the world was weather. "Why? Why would you do this? You burned Marrowfield. You burned the shrine and the old woman's fish and you questioned nothing, that's what you are now, that's what you told me you were. Why is this different?"

Because, he almost said. Because it's you. And he saw that she knew that was the answer, and saw that it disgusted her — that his love, which had once been the best thing in his life, had become just one more corruption, one more reason a man in power bends the rules for the few and lets the many burn. He watched her arrive at that understanding in real time, watched it land, watched her decide not to say it out loud because saying it would be a cruelty and she had carried enough cruelties for one lifetime.

"Because I was wrong once," he said instead, "at enormous cost, and I have been paying installments ever since. Consider this a payment."

"And if Maren finds out you lied?"

"She won't. And if she does—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost more than the words, "—then Ash burns too. I've been ash for ten years anyway. It's a comfortable rank. Nothing left to catch."

For a long moment she said nothing. A barge went by on the canal, laden with dye-vats, the boatman singing something tuneless. Somewhere behind them, faint as a memory, the sigil hummed on — and Corvin realized Elodie could probably feel it too, and that she was choosing, right now, in this wet narrow street, between running and the Door, between the found family in the fish-door house and the power that had ruined her once.

"Thank you," she said finally, and it came out like a wound being cleaned. "I hate that I'm saying it. I hate that you're making me say it. But thank you."

"Don't thank me." He stepped back. "Hate me. It's cleaner. Hate me and use the hour."

He turned and walked toward the south gate, and she let him go, and neither of them looked back, because looking back was a language they had both sworn off and neither had ever learned to stop speaking.

* * *

The report took him until midnight. False trail, Grayford, resonance fading — clean work, plausible, the kind of lie he'd once caught lesser men in. He sealed it with his own wax and sent it by the relay post, and then he sat in his rented room above the chandlery with the lamp unlit and listened to Ondrel settle into its unwritten night.

One hour, he had promised. It had been nearly seven. He had walked the south gate road, turned back twice, stood in the rain at the city's edge like a fool in a ballad, and come back to an empty room and an empty bottle and the knowledge that she was gone — north, surely, always north, toward the drowned cathedral and the Door and the ending of everything — and that he had bought her the road with a lie stamped in his own seal.

Both choices. That was the shape of his life now, he thought. Every choice he made contained both choices, the loyal man and the traitor sharing one body like the two faces of a coin worn smooth. He had testified and he had testified falsely; he had hunted her and he had freed her; he had burned what answered and he was, tonight, in the deepest part of the night, beginning to wonder what exactly he had been burning — and whether the thing that answered prayers in Marrowfield and the thing that had torn Grayford Vale apart ten years ago were the same starving thing, and whether the emperor's gold script had ever once told the truth about anything.

*Question nothing,* the writ said.

He took out the trial transcript. Read it again by moonlight, the hundred-and-first time. Maren's testimony, both halves of it, the belief and the betrayal braided into a single breath. And for the first time in ten years, Corvin Ashe did the thing the writ forbade.

He questioned.

He questioned until dawn grayed the window, and when the relay horn sounded from the south gate — two notes, urgent, the signal for a high warden's column sighted on the road — Corvin was already dressed, already armed, already moving toward the gate with the transcript in his coat and his heart going like a drum, because the column was a day early, the tracker was better than his lie, and somewhere ahead of the wardens' dust, riding hard through the gray morning, came Maren Voss.

And Corvin Ashe had one choice left to make, and both faces of it were already bleeding.
