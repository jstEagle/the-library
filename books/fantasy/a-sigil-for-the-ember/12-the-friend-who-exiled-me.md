## 12. The Friend Who Exiled Me

The rain on the saltmarsh that night came down soft and ordinary, and Elodie hated it a little for that.

She sat up in the wagon while the others slept — Sera curled against the wheel with her burned arm wrapped in linen, Wren a small warm weight under a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's childhood. The fire had been banked to embers. Somewhere out in the dark, Vaskar breathed his slow, guttering breath, and the marsh answered him with frogsong.

Ordinary rain. It fell when it fell, for no one, answering nothing. Ten years ago she would have said that was all rain ever was.

Ten years ago she had believed she knew exactly what her storm was, too.

Sleep wouldn't come, so she let the memory come instead, the way it always did on wet nights, rising like groundwater. She didn't fight it anymore. Fighting it was like arguing with tide.

* * *

The trial was held in the Hall of Scales in Caelmere's capital, though of course no map had carried that name for years now, and the Hall itself was probably rubble or a granary or a temple to the god-emperor's shining face. But memory kept its own cartography, and so in Elodie's head the Hall still stood: vaulted ceiling painted with the four old gods at the corners — Stormfather, Green Mother, the Twin Flames, and in the north arch, half-faded even then, the Drowned One with his patient open mouth.

She remembered the ceiling better than the faces. She had spent most of the trial staring up at it, because looking down meant looking at Maren.

They dressed her in white for the trial. That was the custom for accused storm-callers — white so that any residual charge in the blood would show as a blue tracery beneath the skin, visible proof of guilt or innocence. She had knelt on the stone floor in her white dress for three days while the arbiters argued, and every hour a warden walked the length of the hall inspecting her arms, her throat, the backs of her knees.

Her skin stayed clean. No blue. Not one thread of lightning left in her.

It should have been enough. It was not enough, because forty people were dead, and the dead do not care what color your veins are.

* * *

The storm had come on the eve of the Harvesting, over the town of Grayford — the same Grayford, though she hadn't known it yet, where years later a bridge would wait for her with a trade and a betrayal folded inside it. She had been nineteen, newly sworn to the Court as a storm-caller of the third circle, which meant she could call cloud and calm squall but could not, by law and by training both, raise anything worse than a hard rain.

She remembered that evening perfectly. That was the cruelest part. She remembered *everything*, and none of it helped.

Maren had come to her rooms before the festival, still in her cadet grays, laughing about something — the drillmaster's wig, the way it lifted in wind like a startled bird. They had shared a bottle of plum wine stolen from the officers' cellar, sitting on the window ledge with their boots dangling over the courtyard, because they were nineteen and invincible and best friends since the age of seven, when Maren had broken a boy's nose for mocking Elodie's stammer and never once mentioned it again.

"I'll get posted to the coast by spring," Maren had said. "Warden training. You'll be calling weather for the Court, and I'll come home with salt in my hair and we'll drink real wine and laugh about the wig."

"You hate the coast."

"I'll love the coast." She had knocked her shoulder against Elodie's. "I'll love anywhere I'm pointed."

And then the sky over Grayford had opened like a wound.

Elodie had felt it from forty miles away — not called it, *felt* it, the way you feel a scream through a wall. A pressure behind her eyes, a taste of copper and deep water, and then the connection snapped taut between her chest and that black boiling column of cloud as if someone had hooked a line into her sternum and hauled.

She had fought it. She wanted, desperately, after everything, to believe she had fought it. She remembered gripping the window ledge until the wood split, remember saying — saying aloud, in an empty room — *"not mine, not mine, let go, let go."*

And the storm had obeyed her anyway. Or used her anyway. The arbiters' scholars later explained it in careful language: resonance, sympathetic channeling, an untrained third-circle caller caught in a feedback loop beyond her grade. The storm had needed a voice, and hers was the nearest trained throat in forty miles, and it had spoken through her whether she consented or not.

Forty people. Fishermen mostly, and their families, drowned in their own cellars while the festival lights still burned above. The wave that came off the river was later measured against the church tower. The tower lost its top two floors. The church had stood four hundred years.

She had woken two days later in a warden infirmary, her hands bandaged where the split window ledge had cut them, and Maren had been sitting beside the bed. Not touching her. Just sitting, very straight, very pale, with the look of someone who had been awake the whole time.

"Tell me it wasn't you," Maren had whispered. "Tell me, and I'll believe you. Whatever anyone says, whatever they show me — I'll believe you."

And Elodie had opened her mouth and found that she did not know.

That was the thing she had never told anyone, not Sera, not Vaskar, not the bees who asked for nothing. In the moment the storm took Grayford, there had been one heartbeat — one single heartbeat out of all the thousands of that night — where the pressure in her chest had not felt like invasion. Where it had felt like *hunger answered*. Like a door in her swinging open onto a room she'd always known was there.

One heartbeat. She could not swear to what lived inside one heartbeat. And so she had looked at her best friend and said, "I don't know," and watched something die in Maren's face.

* * *

In the Hall of Scales, on the third day, they brought in Maren to testify.

Elodie had expected her to lie. God help her, some traitorous part of her had hoped for it — hoped Maren would stand before the arbiters and say, *I was with her, she fought it, she screamed at it, I saw.* It wasn't true; Maren hadn't been there; but testimony was testimony, and wardens covered for their own, everyone knew that, and love covered deeper than wardens.

Maren put her hand on the scale-stone and gave them the truth instead.

"She told me she didn't know if she called it," Maren said, in a voice like a blade being drawn slowly. "I asked her directly, at her bedside, and she said: *I don't know.*" Her eyes had found Elodie's across the hall, and they were dry, and they were terrible. "A storm-caller who cannot answer for her own storm is a storm-caller the Court cannot trust. Forty people are dead. I loved her my whole life. I am telling you the truth anyway."

The arbiters had murmured. The scale-stone had done whatever scale-stones did. And the sentence, when it came, was almost gentle — exile rather than execution, banishment to the Hollow Valley where magic cannot follow, her name struck from the rolls of the Court, her power sealed away with iron and ink and distance. Merciful, people said afterward. High Warden Aldous himself had argued for mercy, people said.

Elodie had understood, even then, standing in her clean white dress with her clean blueless veins, that the mercy was not mercy. Exile to the Valley was how the Court destroyed a storm-caller without spilling blood that might remember whose it was. Cut the root, bury it alive, and tell everyone you were kind.

But it was Maren who made the motion. Maren who stood before the assembled court and proposed the banishment herself, formally, in the language of law, her cadet's braid crisp, her spine straight enough to draw a line against.

*I am doing this to save your life,* Maren had mouthed across the floor, when no one else could see.

And Elodie, nineteen years old and hollowed out, had mouthed back the only honest thing left in her: *Then why does it feel like dying.*

* * *

The wagon jolted over a rut, and the present came back: marsh dark, ember-light, Wren's slow breathing. Elodie realized her hands were clenched around the wagon's rail hard enough to ache, and loosened them finger by finger.

Ten years of bees and quiet. Ten years of learning the valley's small honest weathers, the ones that needed nobody's permission. She had built a life in the place designed to unmake her, and she had almost — *almost* — stopped waking at night with the taste of copper water in her mouth.

And now Maren Voss was out there in the dark with imperial writ in her pocket and the sigil's scent in her nose, hunting them, and the girl who had testified against her was the same woman who had exiled her was the same woman who had broken a boy's nose for a seven-year-old's stammer, and Elodie could not make those three people fit into one shape no matter how she turned them in the light.

Because here was the wound underneath the wound, the one she kept wrapped in silence the way Sera kept her arm wrapped in linen:

She still did not know if she was innocent.

Not in the way the arbiters ruled it — the scholars had settled that question well enough, resonance and feedback, a storm wearing her voice like a stolen coat. But innocence was bigger than verdicts. Somewhere in her, in that single damning heartbeat, something had *wanted*. She had felt the storm's vast attention settle on her and some buried door in her had creaked with joy, and joy like that doesn't come from nowhere. You don't hunger for a thing you've never tasted.

Maybe she had called it and forgotten calling. Maybe wanting was calling, in the grammar of storms. Maybe the difference between a victim and a monster was one heartbeat wide, and she had spent ten years unable to measure the distance.

Vaskar had asked her once, early in her valley years, in his sardonic gravel: "Do you miss it, little keeper? The thunder?"

"No," she had lied.

"Hm," he had said, and closed one ancient eye, and let the lie stand between them like a fence post neither of them would move.

* * *

Sera stirred by the wheel. Elodie heard the rustle of linen, the soft catch of breath that meant the burns were hurting her even in sleep, and then the priestess's voice, low, not quite awake: "Elodie? The water — is the water—"

"It's just rain," Elodie said gently. "Plain rain. Go back to sleep."

Sera slept. Wren slept on, one fist curled near her mouth, trusting utterly in the way only children and the very desperate can trust. Out past the rim of the marsh, far to the south and east, lay Grayford, and its bridge, and its dead who had never once visited her dreams without bringing their cellars full of black water.

Elodie thought about Maren's face in the Hall of Scales. Dry-eyed, terrible, truthful.

Everyone in this story thought they knew what Maren was. The emperor's wardens thought she was the empire's sharpest blade. Corvin, she suspected, feared her more than he feared the god-emperor. And Elodie had spent ten years letting the word *betrayer* sit between them like a locked door, because a locked door was easier than the truth.

The truth was that Maren had testified honestly, moved for exile formally, and mouthed *I'm saving your life* — and that all three things could be true at once, and that the worst possibility of all was not that Maren had condemned an innocent woman.

The worst possibility was that Maren had seen clearly, in a way Elodie herself never could, what lived in her friend's heart. That the person who knew Elodie best in all the world had looked at the evidence of her one uncertain heartbeat and concluded: *this much is dangerous, and danger must be buried alive.*

If that was true, then the exile wasn't betrayal. It was diagnosis.

And if it was diagnosis — if Maren had been right —

Then somewhere in Elodie Eastwick there was still, after ten years of bees and honey and Wren's laughter, the thing that had tasted the storm and been glad.

The fire shifted. An ember popped, bright and brief.

Far off to the southeast, where the marsh met the ghost-roads, lightning flickered once against the underside of the clouds — silent, distant, directionless. Ordinary lightning. It fell where it fell, for no one, answering nothing.

Elodie watched it anyway, counting heartbeats, and did not sleep.
