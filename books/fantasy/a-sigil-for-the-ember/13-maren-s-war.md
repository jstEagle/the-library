## 13. Maren's War

The war room of Grayford Keep smelled of tallow and old iron, and Maren Voss had learned to love it the way other people loved their mothers' kitchens.

She stood over the map table with her palms flat on the wood, the way her father used to stand when he was thinking, though she had never once admitted to herself that she'd copied him. The map spread beneath her hands was not the map the emperor's court used. The court's maps were clean — rivers like silver thread, roads like stitched seams, every town a tidy black mark with a tidy name. This one was different. This one had been drawn by hand, by her own hand, over eleven years of nights, and it showed Caelmere as Caelmere actually was: scarred. Burned. Half its towns inked out in careful strokes of grey wash, the way you'd shade out a tooth that had been pulled. Ondrel. Vessing. The Saltmarsh parishes. A whole constellation of places that no longer appeared on any document bearing the imperial seal, because documents bearing the imperial seal were written by people who believed that what the emperor decreed had always been true.

Maren knew better. She had been nine years old when the gods last walked openly in Caelmere, and she remembered what their walking looked like from underneath.

"High Warden." Captain Iles stood at the door, rain still dripping off his oilskin. He was a good man, Iles — steady, unimaginative, the kind of soldier who would hold a line until the line held him. "The outriders are back from the valley road."

"And?"

"Nothing. Again." He hesitated, which he only did when the news was worse than nothing. "Except — Brannic says the bees are wrong."

Maren looked up. "The *what*?"

"The bees, m'lady. In the hives along the valley rim. They've all gone quiet. Not dead — quiet. Brannic's father kept bees; he says it's what they do before a hard winter or an earthquake." Iles rubbed the back of his neck. "Or before something they can hear that we can't."

Maren straightened slowly. Outside the arrow-slit window, the rain came down in long grey sheets over the warden compound, and somewhere below, a sentry called the hour — two bells past midnight, the dead watch, when sensible people slept and she did not.

Bees going quiet. She thought of the reports stacked on her desk, the ones she'd read three times each: prayers answered in villages where no priest had passed in a decade. Rain falling upward at the Hollow Valley's rim. A fugitive priestess of the drowned god crossing wards that had held for eleven years without so much as scorching her boots — except that she *had* been scorched, half-burned, which meant the ward had fought her, which meant the ward was still trying, which meant the ward was failing.

And under all of it, threaded through every report like a wire through wax: the sigil. Bronze. Old. Humming like a struck bell. Carried east toward the valley by a woman who should have died twice on the road and hadn't.

"Double the watches on the valley approaches," Maren said. "And send word to the saltmarsh stations. If the sigil-bearer turns south, I want to know before her boots touch the mud."

"Yes, High Warden." Iles paused at the door. "Begging pardon, but — the men are asking questions. About why we're hunting a priestess and a beekeeper. Some of them remember the Eastwick trial. Some of them remember her *fondly*."

"I remember her fondly," Maren said, and the words came out flat as slate. "That has never once stopped me doing my duty. Dismissed."

He went. The door closed. Maren stood alone with her map and her candle and the rain, and let herself do the thing she permitted exactly once a year, on the anniversary, in the dark, where no one could see: she remembered.

* * *

Her family had died on a Tuesday.

That was the detail that had broken something in her, afterward — not the fire, not the screaming, not the way the sky over Vessing turned the color of a bruise for three days after — but the sheer ordinary fact of it. Tuesdays were market days. Her mother had gone to market. Her little brother Tam had gone along because he wanted honey-cakes and because he was seven and seven-year-olds got to want things. Her father had stayed home with the accounts, because someone had to, and so her father had died in the house when the temple of the storm-god came down, and her mother and Tam had died in the square among four hundred others, and none of it — none of it — had anything to do with anything any of them had done.

They said afterward it was the war. The Godswar, the histories called it now, though at the time it hadn't felt like a war; wars had armies and fronts and reasons. This had just been the old gods getting louder. Hungrier. Their priests calling down power the human body was never built to channel, whole congregations burning out like lamps, rival temples leveling each other's districts with weather and plague and things that came up out of the ground wearing the faces of the drowned. Nine-year-old Maren had crouched in a root cellar for two days with the baker's family while the sky fought above them, and when she came out, Vessing was gone, and the emperor-to-be was already marching his new doctrine through the ashes: *no more gods. No more temples. No more magic in human hands.* And the people, hollow-eyed and ash-grey, had cheered, because it was easier to cheer than to grieve.

Maren had cheered too. She'd meant it.

Eleven years later she commanded the wardens of the eastern reach, and she still meant it. That was the thing Corvin Ashe had never understood, back when he'd been worth talking to — back before the valley, before the exile, before he'd started looking at her across briefing tables like she was something he'd failed to save. Corvin thought her hatred of the returning gods was grief wearing armor. He wasn't entirely wrong. But grief cooled. What Maren carried had never cooled once in eleven years, because it wasn't only grief. It was arithmetic.

Four hundred and twelve dead in Vessing. Sixty thousand dead in the Godswar, by the honest count — the count the empire didn't publish. And against that, one girl in exile, keeping bees in a valley where magic couldn't follow. One girl whose power had been taken from her before it could be spent again. Maren had run the sum a thousand times and it never came out differently. Whatever it cost Elodie Eastwick — and Maren knew it had cost her everything, knew it every time she let herself think about the girl's face at the trial, nineteen years old and white as paper, searching the gallery for a friend who wouldn't meet her eyes — whatever it cost her, the ledger balanced. The valley was mercy. The valley was the only mercy the empire had left in it, and Maren had made sure Elodie received it, and if Elodie hated her forever for it, then Elodie could hate her alive.

Because the alternative — the alternative was what was happening right now, in the reports on her desk. Prayers answering themselves. Gods grasping. A bronze sigil moving through the world like a lit fuse.

The last time the old gods had come back from silence, Vessing had burned.

* * *

The summons came at dawn, and it was signed with the seal she hated most: the sunburst-and-chain of the god-emperor's own chancery.

*By grace divine and law imperial: the bearer of the relic known as the Sigil of the Door is to be apprehended or destroyed. All who shelter her share her sentence. Question nothing. Burn what answers.*

Burn what answers. She read the phrase twice, tasting it. It was the emperor's favorite instruction lately, and it told her more than any dossier: he was afraid. Aurelian III, the man who had worn divinity like a borrowed coat for eleven years, who performed miracles on feast days with stagecraft and siphoned worship into whatever thin power he actually possessed — he was afraid of the genuine article coming home. Maren had met him once, at court, five years ago. She had watched him bless a banner and seen, for half a heartbeat, the flicker behind his eyes of a man checking whether anyone had noticed the trick. She had knelt and sworn and said nothing, because the empire needed its god the way a drowning man needs driftwood, true or not.

But driftwood that panics sinks everyone holding onto it.

She rode out with thirty picked wardens an hour after dawn, east along the valley road, into country that grew stranger the closer they came to the Hollow Valley. The land there had been exiled along with the girl — wrung dry, magic-bleak, grass the color of dishwater — and eleven years of bleakness had given it a particular silence. No birds sang near the rim. Even the wind seemed to arrive already tired.

It was on the second day, at the shrine of Saint Aldric — a roadside alcove, pre-imperial, officially condemned, unofficially dusted clean by travelers — that Maren saw the first answered prayer with her own eyes.

A farmer's wife was kneeling there. Middle-aged, mud to the knee, a basket of eggs beside her. As the wardens drew rein, the woman was finishing a prayer — Maren caught only the end of it, the old cadence, the one they'd all been taught as children before the teaching became a hanging offense — *and bring him safe home, that is all, that is all I ask* — and then the air over the shrine moved.

Not wind. Movement. Like heat-shimmer in cold air, like a curtain stirring in a closed room. And the woman gasped, and pressed both hands to her mouth, and began to laugh and cry at once, and said, to no one, to the empty alcove, "*Thank you. Oh — thank you, thank you—*" and Maren sat her horse and felt the hair rise on her arms, because she had heard that tone before. Once. In a root cellar under Vessing, from a priestess of the harvest-god, minutes before the ceiling came down.

"Dismount," she said quietly. "Wardens — dismount."

They took the woman's name — Tessa Marrow, husband conscripted to the salt works, missing eight months — and they took her testimony, and she gave it gladly, radiant, unafraid, telling them how she'd prayed every day for a year and this morning, this very morning, a rider had come up the south road saying the works had released the autumn conscripts early, saying her Tom was on the list, saying he'd be home by the feast day. Coincidence, said the rational part of Maren's mind. Coincidence, coincidence, coincidence.

But the woman's eggs, in their basket, had gone cold as river stones in the space of a prayer. And the shimmer over the shrine had bent *toward* her, Maren was almost sure, the way flowers bend toward light.

"What will happen to me?" the woman asked at the end, suddenly fearful, clutching her basket. "For praying? You're wardens. Will you — my Tom's coming home—"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Thirty armed riders around a kneeling farmwife, and the woman's only crime was asking the sky for her husband back, and the sky, apparently, had listened.

"Go home," Maren said. "Pray for whoever you like. But if anything answers you again — anything you can see, anything that speaks — you send for the wardens. Do you understand me? However kind it seems. *Especially* if it seems kind."

The woman nodded, bewildered, and gathered her eggs, and hurried off down the road toward a homecoming that might be real and might be bait.

Sergeant Ovist fell into step beside Maren as they remounted. "You think it was really answered," he said. Not a question.

"I think a god with nothing left to give is giving away crumbs," Maren said, "and I think crumbs are how you teach dogs to follow you." She wheeled her horse east. "The last time they fed us, we forgot they eat."

* * *

They reached the valley rim on the fourth day, and Maren stood at the edge of the ward-line and felt it like a toothache.

The Hollow Valley's ban was the oldest working in the empire — older than the emperor, older than the Godswar's end, laid down by the first conclave of the purges when they'd needed somewhere to put the dangerous ones. Magic could not cross it. Power unraveled at the rim like thread pulled from cloth. For eleven years it had been the most silent place in Caelmere, and Maren had found a grim comfort in that silence, in knowing that somewhere in her jurisdiction there existed a hole in the world where none of it — the gods, the miracles, the burning — could reach.

Now she stood at the rim and felt the hole filling in.

It was subtle. A pressure, rather than a sound. The sense of a held breath beginning to release. The ward was still there — she could feel its edges, worn smooth as sea glass — but it had cracked somewhere, deep in its structure, and through the crack the world's magic was seeping back like water into a cellar. Slowly. Inevitably. The bees had known. The upward rain had known. And somewhere down in that green bowl, sleeping out his final centuries, the last dragon had certainly known longest of all.

"High Warden." Ovist again, at her elbow, holding out a leather dispatch case. "Rider from Grayford. Marked urgent."

She broke the seal. Read it once. Read it again.

*Inquisitor Ashe requests audience. States he has located the sigil-bearer's trail. States further that the trail leads into the Hollow Valley, and that he will enter with or without warden sanction. Requests — quote — 'the High Warden's presence, as she knows the exile.' End quote.*

Maren lowered the paper. Below her, the valley lay green and quiet in the afternoon light, smoke rising from a single chimney near the western slope — the beekeeper's cottage, the exile's cottage, small and domestic and utterly unremarkable, except that Maren knew to the inch what it cost to keep that chimney smoking.

Corvin. Of course Corvin had found the trail; Corvin had been chasing ghosts of that girl for eleven years, and ghosts were the one quarry he'd never learned to stop hunting. And now he wanted her to come down into the valley with him, into the place where her oldest wound lived, and stand face to face with the person she had condemned to keep alive.

"She knows the exile," Maren repeated softly, reading the words again. Yes. She knew the exile. She had built it, stone by stone, testimony by testimony, out of the wreckage of a friendship and a burning city and a lie she had told a tribunal with her heart hammering and her voice perfectly level — the lie being not that the storm was Elodie's, but that Maren believed it was. Eleven years she had carried that lie the way soldiers carry shrapnel: too deep to cut out, close enough to ache in cold weather.

Elodie Eastwick thought Maren Voss had betrayed her. Let her. Hatred was armor too, and it had kept the girl alive longer than love ever could have.

But the sigil was real, and the gods were waking, and the ward was cracking, and the arithmetic that had balanced for eleven years was starting, very slightly, to tilt.

"Send back to Grayford," Maren said. "I'm riding to meet Inquisitor Ashe at the valley gate. And Ovist—" she folded the dispatch away, and her voice came out quieter than she intended, "—have the men pray tonight. Quietly, if they still remember how. Because if the old gods are truly coming back, I want to know which of my wardens they'll find listening."

She turned her horse toward the valley, toward the green silence and the single thread of chimney-smoke, toward the friend she had saved by ruining and the war she had sworn would never happen twice.

Behind her, far to the north, over the drowned hills where no church remained, thunder rolled — long, low, and patient, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
