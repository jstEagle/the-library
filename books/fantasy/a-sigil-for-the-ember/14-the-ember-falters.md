## 14. The Ember Falters

Vaskar had counted ten thousand dawns in this valley, and he had hated most of them.

Not with any great passion — passion was for the young, and for the burning, and he was neither — but with the low, grinding contempt of an old king deposed by time itself. The sun came up over the crater's rim the way a creditor comes to the door: punctual, unwelcome, inevitable. It found him coiled in the deep hollow where the mountain had folded in on itself a thousand years ago, wings folded like a closed ledger, scales the color of cooled slag. It found him listening.

That was the thing no one understood about sleeping dragons. Sleep was the wrong word. He *listened*. He had listened to the valley's heartbeat for three centuries — the slow pulse of roots, the patient traffic of worms, the small frantic industry of Elodie Eastwick's bees. He had listened to the girl herself, when she came near the crater's edge on her rare, guilty visits, her footsteps always a little too loud, her breath always carrying that thread of storm-smell she didn't know she had. She thought she was sneaking. She thought she was being respectful of a sleeping monster's rest. It was, he had to admit, rather charming, in the way that mice are charming when they believe the cat is decorative furniture.

And now, for six days, he had been listening to the sigil.

It had come into his valley like a bell rung underwater. Sera Ivane's bronze disc did not sing, exactly. It *pulled*. Somewhere beneath the world — beneath the drowned cathedral, beneath the dead city, beneath the seam where the living land pressed against the dead one — a door had noticed that a key existed, and the door was hungry, and the key was humming its name.

And Vaskar's fire, his ancient, hoarded, miserly fire, had begun to answer.

He felt it the way an old man feels spring: as a summons he could no longer politely decline. Something in his chest — the ember, the last coal of the first fire, the thing that had made him a dragon rather than merely a very large lizard with opinions — leaned toward that pull like a flower toward light. And each time it leaned, it guttered.

On the first day he had lost the color of his left foreclaw. The scales there had gone from slag-black to ash-grey, and the heat beneath them, which had run steady as a buried river for nine hundred years, had flickered. On the third day his dreams — the long amber dreams of a dragon at rest, full of remembered skies — had begun to fray at the edges, and he had woken twice with smoke in his throat and no memory of breathing it.

On the sixth day, the girl came to see him.

* * *

Elodie Eastwick climbed the crater's rim the way she did everything: reluctantly, competently, and with her jaw set like a woman arguing with the world and losing on points. Vaskar heard her from a mile off. He always did. Ten years in the valley had taught her the terrain but not the silence; she moved through it like a guest, not an animal, and the valley knew the difference.

He opened one eye. It was a performance. He had been awake for hours, watching the sigil's pull drag at the ember in his chest like a tide going out.

"You look terrible," Elodie announced, from the rim.

"You look like a woman who has been arguing with a priestess for three days," Vaskar rumbled. His voice came out of the hollow like a landslide deciding to be polite. "How goes the war?"

"We're not at war. We're—" She picked her way down the scree with the sure feet the valley had given her, and stopped at a respectful distance, which was to say, at the distance where his breath could no longer lift her hair. She had learned that boundary early and never tested it. Another point in her favor. "Sera wants to leave. She says the god is calling her. Wren cries at night. And I can't—" She stopped. Started again. "I came to ask you about the sigil."

"Ask, then."

"What is it *doing* to you?"

Ah. Vaskar closed the eye and considered the question from several angles, the way one considers a chess problem, or a lie. The girl had sharp eyes. She had noticed the ash-grey claw, then. Or she had noticed the way the air above his hollow shimmered wrong, heat without source, a fire burning thin.

"The sigil," he said at last, "is a bell. And I am — regrettably — the bell's oldest acquaintance. It rings, and things that once answered bells begin to remember that they have legs." He let a curl of smoke escape, theatrical, a distraction. "It is doing nothing to me that age was not already doing, more slowly."

"You're lying."

"Of course I'm lying. I'm a dragon. Lying is one of the four things we do well, and the other three are hoarding, burning, and dying alone." He opened the eye again and found her standing her ground, arms crossed, storm-grey eyes fixed on him with a stubbornness he recognized because he had once seen it in a mirror the size of a lake. "But I am also, in this instance, telling you a truth wearing a lie's clothing, which is the traditional dragonish compromise. The sigil pulls, child. It pulls at everything old. I am the oldest thing in this valley, and so the pull arrives at my door first. It is an inconvenience. It is not a crisis."

"What happens when it stops being an inconvenience?"

He could have told her. He could have told her that the door beneath the world was not merely hungry but *drinking*, that it had found in his ember the last unspent fire of the old age, and that it was drawing it out of him thread by thread the way a spider drinks a fly. He could have told her that the ember in his chest — the ember she would one day, if the priestess's mad errand came to its mad conclusion, be asked to carry — was guttering early, that he had budgeted his death in centuries and found the ledger suddenly, catastrophically overdrawn.

He looked at her instead. Nineteen when they exiled her, twenty-nine now, and the valley had done its slow work: she had hands that knew hives and bread ovens and the weight of a frightened child, and she had no idea — none — that she was the most dangerous creature in Caelmere. That was the tragedy of Elodie Eastwick. Everyone had told her she was a weapon for so long that she had come to believe weapons were all she was, and she had spent ten years trying to be a woman instead, and the world had come to collect the weapon anyway.

She deserved, he decided, a few more months of not knowing. The knowing would come soon enough, and it would come with a bill attached.

"It becomes a crisis," he said, "when someone is fool enough to open the door. Which is why I am going to teach you something, and you are going to learn it, and neither of us will enjoy the process."

"I didn't come here for lessons."

"No one ever does. That is what makes them lessons." He uncoiled, slowly, the way glaciers move — an acre and a half of dragon rearranging itself, joints cracking like felled timber, until his head hung above her at a conversational altitude. Up close, he knew, she could see what the sigil's pull had done: the dullness of his scales, the way the heat-haze above his back came in stutters now, like a candle in a draft. She saw. Her face did the thing faces do when they decide not to have seen.

"Show me," she said, because she was Elodie Eastwick, and courage was the only currency she had never learned to hoard.

* * *

The lesson took place in the shallow bowl at the crater's heart, where the ground was warm and nothing grew, and where the last fire of the old world sat just beneath the stone like a heart beneath a breastbone.

"Hold out your hands," Vaskar said.

"I don't have any fire."

"Neither does a wick. That has never stopped a candle." He exhaled, gently — gently, for him, which still flattened the grass in a ten-yard radius — and let a tongue of flame curl down from his jaws. Not his true fire. Not the ember. A surface flame, a courtesy flame, the kind he could strike from the air itself the way a man strikes a match. It hovered above her palms, the size of an egg, orange and ordinary and obedient.

"Now hold it."

"Vaskar—"

"*Hold* it, storm-caller. It will not burn you. Fire knows its own, and you — whatever else you are, whatever they said at your trial — you are fire's cousin. Your storms are only fire that learned to weep." He watched her flinch at the word *trial* and did not apologize. Dragons did not apologize. It was in the charter. "Hold it. And when it flickers, do not feed it your fear. Fear is wet wood. It will smother."

She held it. And here was the thing that made his old heart — his cooling, guttering, overdrawn old heart — turn over in his chest like a stone in a stream: she *could*. The flame sat in her cupped hands like a tame bird, and her face, which had been set in its usual defensive arrangement, went slowly, wonderingly open. He watched ten years fall off her. He watched the exile become, for one moment, a girl in a tower room who had once been told she was chosen.

"It's warm," she whispered.

"Fire generally is. That is the first lesson and the last one, and everything between is commentary." He lowered his great head until his eye was level with hers, one vast gold iris reflecting a small woman holding a small flame. "Now listen, because this is the part that matters, and I will say it once, and I am old, and old things mumble. That flame is not yours. It came from me. It lives on my breath and my will. And yet it burns in *your* hands, and it would burn as gladly in a beggar's, because fire does not care whose hands — it only cares that the hands do not close."

"What are you teaching me?"

"That when the day comes — and it is coming, girl, do not make that face, I have watched the priestess pray and I have heard the bell ring, the day is coming — when the day comes that you are asked to hold a flame that is not yours, you must not try to own it. You must not try to deserve it. You must not stand in the threshold of anything and think *this is my right*." The ember in his chest flickered as he spoke, and he felt the door pull at it, and he kept his voice level the way one keeps a face level at a funeral. "You must hold it the way you are holding that one. Open hands. Open hands, Elodie. The closed fist is how gods are made, and I have known gods, and they were all, without exception, terrible dinner company and worse endings."

She looked down at the flame. When she spoke again her voice had gone quiet in a different way. "The sigil's pull. It's taking something from you. That's why your fire looks—" She gestured at his flank, at the ash-grey patch he had not managed to hide. "Sera's god is dying. The old gods are all dying. And the door is hungry, and you're the oldest fire left, and it's drinking from you."

Silence, then, of the kind that has weight. Somewhere above the crater, a hawk turned on the wind, and Vaskar envied it with his whole ancient soul.

"You are too clever by half," he said finally, "which is a family trait, I believe, though your branch of the family has always been too clever by three-quarters."

"How long?"

"Dragons do not answer that question."

"How *long*, Vaskar."

He considered, once more, the lie. He was, after all, a dragon; it was one of the four things. But there was a limit even to the tradition, and the limit was a woman standing in a warm bowl of stone with a borrowed flame cupped in her open hands, looking at him with the particular ferocious tenderness that she had spent ten years pretending she did not feel for anything, and that she felt, he knew, for a broken priestess, an orphaned child, a valley full of bees, and — gods help them all — a dying dragon.

"Longer than you need," he said. "Shorter than I promised myself. Now close your hands."

She closed them, and the flame went out, and the going-out of it was gentle, the way a guest leaves — and Vaskar felt the ember in his chest gutter once, hard, a coal in a wind, and felt the door far beneath the world drink what the little flame had carried up out of him, and kept his face level, because it was a funeral, and the deceased was him.

"Again," he said. "And this time, when it flickers, breathe."

* * *

They practiced until the light went long and gold across the crater, and then they practiced in the dusk, and by the time the stars came out Elodie could hold a flame that was not hers for a full hundred heartbeats without it wavering, and her face was smudged with soot and lit from below, and she looked, Vaskar thought, like every painting ever made of every doomed hero, which was not a comfort.

She built a small fire at the crater's edge — her own fire, wood-gathered, mortal, ordinary — and sat beside it wrapped in her cloak, and he lowered himself into his hollow nearby, because he was old and tired and the pretense of sleep had become indistinguishable from the thing itself.

"Vaskar," she said, into the dark. "When the door opens. If it has to be opened. What happens to the one who opens it?"

Ah. So she had been listening too. She had heard the shape of the thing beneath his lessons, the way you hear the shape of a bone beneath a blanket.

"That," said Vaskar, "depends on what the opener is holding, and how open her hands are, and whether she has learned to spend a fire instead of hoarding it." He let his eye drift half-closed, gold light guttering. "Spend the fire, child. Whatever fire you are ever given — spend it. Hoarded fire only burns its keeper. Spent fire lights something. That is the whole of dragonish wisdom, and it took me nine hundred years to learn it, and I learned it too late to be of any use to anyone, which is why I am teaching it to you now, while there is still time."

"Time for what?"

He did not answer. Far beneath them, beneath the stone and the roots and the drowned cathedral and the dead city, the door pulled at his ember again, a long slow drink, and he felt the ash-grey spread another inch along his flank like frost claiming a window, and he thought: *not yet. Not until she is ready. Not until her hands are open.*

And then, drifting up from the valley below on the night wind, he heard the thing that ended the evening's peace: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast along the western ridge, where no one rode, where nothing had ridden in ten years — and threaded through the hoofbeats, faint as a dying man's pulse, the cold iron smell of the god-emperor's inquisitors.

Elodie heard it a heartbeat later. She stood, and the firelight caught her face, and Vaskar watched the storm rise in her eyes like weather over open water.

"They've found the valley," she said.

"Yes," said Vaskar, and got, painfully, wearily, to his feet. "I believe, storm-caller, that your lesson is about to be examined."
