## 15. Sanctuary of the Unregistered

The council chamber of Ondrel had once been a counting-house, and it still smelled of ink and old coin — a smell Sera found more honest than incense. Nine elders sat behind a crescent of mismatched desks: a fishwife with hands like knotted rope, a one-eyed map-seller, a woman who might have been a noble in another life and dressed like a beggar to prove she'd chosen it. None of them wore the emperor's colors. None of them could afford to.

They were unregistered people. That word again. Sera had been hearing it for two days now, spoken in Ondrel's low, careful voices — *unregistered* — as though the absence of a name in some imperial ledger were a kind of wealth. In a way, she supposed, it was. A person the empire did not know existed was a person the empire could not burn.

She stood before the crescent alone. Elodie, Wren, and Vaskar's distant rumble from the harbor caves were all somewhere behind her, but the council had asked for the priestess specifically, and so the priestess had come, her burned half still wrapped in linen that smelled of marsh-moss and honey.

"You carry it," said the eldest, a man whose voice sounded like a door left open too long. "We can hear it from here. Like a bell under water."

Sera did not deny it. The sigil hung against her breastbone beneath her coat, and even through bronze and cloth she could feel it warming, the way a hearthstone warms an hour after the fire dies. It had been warmer every day since the ghost-road. Since the bridge. Since she'd paid her toll and lied about which memory she'd given.

*You paid the ordination,* the drowned god murmured, in the voice like water filling lungs. *You paid the wrong one.*

*I paid what I chose,* she answered, and kept her face still for the council.

"The city offers sanctuary," the fishwife said. "Real sanctuary. Food, roofs, names that aren't yours — we're good at names. We've hidden smugglers, deserters, a prince once, though he cried." A ripple of dry laughter around the crescent. "In return you give us the sigil. It goes into the deep vaults below the salt-cisterns where nothing answers prayers and nothing hears bells. And your friends stay. All of them. The girl grows up safe. The storm-caller—" the fishwife's eyes flicked toward the door, as if Elodie might be listening, and perhaps she was "—the storm-caller gets what none of us ever got. A whole life, unwatched."

It was generous. It was rational. It was everything Sera had spent three weeks bleeding across a kingdom to prevent, and she heard herself say, calmly, clearly:

"We accept."

The lie came out of her smooth as oiled rope. She felt it leave her mouth and settle into the room like a coin on a counter, and she hated how easy it was. Ordained liars, her order used to call themselves, back when her order existed. We lie the way other priests sing. She had never understood the joke until now.

"Then it's done," said the eldest, and something in his shoulders loosened — nine old people relaxing all at once, the sound of a city exhaling. "Bring it at moonrise. Alone, if you please. Vaults are small."

"I'll bring it," Sera said. "Alone."

* * *

Elodie was waiting for her in the courtyard of the safehouse, sitting on the rim of a dry fountain with her sleeves rolled and her forearms beekeeper-brown, looking — Sera thought not for the first time — like a woman who belonged in sunlight and had been exiled into shadow by clerical error. Wren sat beside her, braiding straw into rings, and the sight of the two of them together did something painful to Sera's chest. They looked like family. They weren't, quite, yet. That was the tragedy she was about to make worse.

"Well?" Elodie said. "What did they want?"

"Sanctuary. Real one. Roofs, food, new names."

Wren's head came up. "New names? Do I get to pick?"

"You already picked one," Elodie said gently. "Wren."

"That's the name I made up. I want a better one now."

"Wren." Sera's voice came out strange, flattened. She had planned this conversation in the stairwell — planned the phrasing, the softening, the small omissions that keep a family functional — and now that she stood here, every plan dissolved like salt in rain. Because Elodie was looking at her with those gray eyes that had survived exile and inquisitors and ten years of bees, eyes that missed nothing, and Sera knew — knew with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading the silences between prayers — that the storm-caller would see through any softening she attempted.

So she told the truth about the offer. And then she told the lie about the answer.

"They want the sigil for it," Sera said. "Which is why we're leaving tonight. I told them no."

Something eased in Elodie's shoulders. "Good. Thank you for—"

"I told them we'd decided together," Sera went on, because apparently she intended to dig her own grave with a silver spoon. "That we'd talked it over and refused as a group. Unanimous."

The easing stopped. Elodie went very still, the way water goes still before it remembers it's a storm. Beside her, Wren looked up from her braiding with the sharp animal attention children have when they smell weather changing.

"We never discussed it," Elodie said.

"No."

"You decided for us."

"Yes."

"And you lied to a council of desperate people to do it."

"They're desperate enough to be reasonable," Sera said. "That's what makes them dangerous."

Elodie rose from the fountain rim. She wasn't tall; she didn't need to be. Ten years of valley silence had taught her a stillness that filled space the way shouting couldn't. "Sera. Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Sit down anyway." And there it was — not anger, exactly, or not only. Something older and wearier underneath, the tone of a woman who had been managed before, by experts, by her best friend, by an entire imperial court that had decided what she could bear to know. Sera sat down on the fountain's cold stone because refusing would have proven something true and terrible, and she wasn't ready to prove it yet.

Wren, wisely, gathered her straw rings and retreated to the doorway, though she did not leave. Of course she didn't. Nobody left this family; that was the whole point of it, and the whole problem.

"Talk," Elodie said.

* * *

What do you say, when you've been caught? Sera had rehearsed confessions her whole life — the liturgy of contrition, kneel here, speak these words, be absolved — but there was no liturgy for this, no prescribed form for *I lied to protect you from your own mercy*, and so she simply began, and the beginning came out wrong.

"It's not what you think."

"It never is," Elodie said, without heat. "Try me."

"The sigil isn't cargo." Sera pulled it out — she shouldn't have, indoors, in daylight, but the argument needed evidence — and set it on the fountain stone between them. Bronze, palm-sized, a circle of waves swallowing a star, humming its low struck-bell note that made the dry fountain dust shiver. "It's a mouth. And something has been talking into it since the night I crossed your ward half-burned, and it isn't saying the same things to me it would say to you."

"What does it say to you?"

There it was. The question. Sera had known it was coming since Grayford Bridge, since the toll-bridge, since the moment Elodie had watched her pay a memory and seen — hadn't she seen — the shape of the lie.

"It says," Sera said, and her ordinaried voice failed her, cracked down the middle like river ice, "*come home.*"

Silence. The sigil hummed. Somewhere above them a gull screamed its one opinion about everything.

"My god is dying," Sera said. "I told you that on the road — the old gods are starving, grasping, answering prayers they should ignore because answering is the only thing left they can feel. I told you that like it was geography. Neutral fact. What I didn't tell you—" she made herself look up "—is that mine talks to me. Only me. Not through dreams, not through omens. Talks. Every day. Every hour. He has been narrating my life to me since the cathedral fell, and lately he has stopped asking and started telling."

"Telling you what?"

"To bring him the sigil. To bring him to the Door. To bring myself." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong too. "He's very clear about the last part. He frames it as an honor."

"And Ondrel's vault," Elodie said slowly, "where nothing hears bells—"

"Would be the first quiet I've had in six years. Yes. I know. You think I don't know?" Sera stood, because sitting had become unbearable, and paced the length of the dry fountain with the sigil's hum following her like a loyal, awful dog. "I turned down silence, Elodie. I turned down safety for a child and a roof for all of us and the first quiet in six years, and I did it in thirty seconds, without asking any of you, because he asked me to. Do you understand what that means? I am not loyal to this family. I am loyal to a drowning thing wearing my father's voice, and I cannot always tell which loyalty is moving my mouth."

The honesty landed between them like something dropped from a height. Sera stood panting slightly, surprised at herself — she hadn't meant to say the last part, the true part, the part that made her monstrous. But there it was, lying on the courtyard stones, and Elodie was looking at it.

"You should have let them take it," Elodie said quietly.

"Don't."

"If the vault is quiet — if you could sleep, if you could be free of—"

"*Don't.*" Sharper than she meant. The sigil flared warm against her palm where she'd gripped it without noticing. "You want to know why I lied to the council? Not why I refused — why I lied about the unanimity?" She held Elodie's gaze. "Because if I'd told them the truth — *one mad priestess says no* — they'd have taken it anyway. Nine old smugglers versus one burned woman. They'd have taken it from me, and they'd have been right to, and their vault would have held a god's mouth shut for a generation, and he would have gone quiet, and I would have—" Her voice broke again. She hated it. "I would have been alone in my own head for the first time since I was nineteen years old. And I am more afraid of that quiet than I am of him."

Elodie said nothing for a long time. The gull screamed again. Wren, in the doorway, had gone very carefully still, the stillness of a child pretending not to listen who is listening with her entire skeleton.

"So you lied," Elodie finally said, "to keep us in your war."

"I lied to keep you in *my* war. It's the only one I've got." Sera pressed the heel of her hand against her eye, against the burn-scarred side, where the skin pulled wrong. "And before you say it — yes. I know what Maren did to you. I know what it costs, being decided for. Being managed by someone who loves you and thinks they know better. I have watched you carry that wound for three hundred miles and I just drove it deeper with my own hands. I know."

"Do you?" Elodie asked, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow worse. "Because here's what I see, Sera. I see a woman who pays memory-tolls and lies about which ones. Who refuses sanctuaries and lies about why. Who carries a talking god under her coat and tells us it's cargo." She stood, slowly, joints stiff with old exile. "Every lie you tell us makes him stronger and us weaker. Don't you see that? He doesn't need to take the sigil anywhere. He just needs you to keep deciding things alone until there's nothing left of us to decide with."

The words went in under Sera's ribs and found the bone.

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" Elodie's voice stayed gentle. Gentle as floodwater. "When did you last make a choice he didn't comment on? When did you last want something — anything — without hearing his voice fold itself around the wanting? Can you even find the seam anymore?"

And Sera opened her mouth to say *of course, obviously, yesterday, the bread, I wanted bread* — and found nothing. No seam. No clean edge where Sera Ivane ended and the drowned god's long narration began. She had paid a memory at the bridge and could not fully remember which one, and the gap in her own history had a voice in it now, filling the space, explaining, always explaining, *you gave the ordination, you gave the ordination, you are my last priestess and you gave the ordination—*

"Sera." Elodie's hand, on her arm. Warm. Human. Calloused from bees. "Breathe."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. And you're not fine, and you haven't been, and we've been carrying you and calling it fellowship." Elodie took a breath of her own. "Here's what happens now. Not because I'm deciding for you — I'm done watching people do that, including myself. Here's what I'm *offering*. Tonight, we leave Ondrel. All four of us, sigil included, before moonrise, before the council learns their unanimity was fiction. We go where the god wants least to go. And you tell me — truthfully, on the road, however long it takes — everything he says. Every hour. Every whisper. Not because I'll fix it. Because a secret shared is a secret halved, and right now you're carrying a god in a body built for grief."

"Why?" It came out of Sera small, almost childlike. "Why help me? I lied to you. I decided for you. I'm everything you ran from."

Elodie almost smiled. It was a tired, valley-worn expression, and it transformed her face the way sun transforms a field.

"Because ten years ago someone decided for me too, and everyone called it betrayal, and I built my whole self on hating it." She picked up the sigil from the fountain stone — it hissed faintly at her touch, displeased, like a cat lifted — and held it out. "And because I've recently begun to suspect that the truth was kinder and stranger than I knew. Maybe yours will be too. Take your god, priestess. Let's go make him miserable."

* * *

They left by the cistern gate at dusk, four shadows and a hum, while above them the unregistered city lit its illegal lamps one by one — a constellation of small defiant fires that no empire would ever chart. Sera walked at the rear, as always, and felt the drowned god stir against her ribs like something rolling over in shallow water.

*You told her,* he said, in the voice of her father, in the voice of the tide.

"Some of it."

*Liar.*

"Priestess," she corrected, and was surprised to find that the word still fit, that somewhere under the narration and the noise and the six years of drowning there was still a seam, thin as wire, and it was hers.

Ahead, Wren skipped between flagstones. Elodie walked with her hood back, letting the wind find her face like a woman greeting weather she'd been promised. And far off, past the harbor caves, something vast and guttering exhaled smoke into the dark — patient, ancient, waiting for them all.

Behind them, in the counting-house, nine elders waited for a moonrise delivery that would never come.

Ahead, the road bent south, toward saltmarsh and rumor and a cathedral full of drowned bells.

And in Sera's ear, gentle as tide through a wreck, her god began — for the first time in six years — to sing.
