## 16. Storm Over Saltmarsh

The saltmarsh taught you its weather the way an old woman teaches a granddaughter to read: slowly, patiently, and only if you kept still long enough to listen.

Elodie had learned it in three days. The marsh breathed in tides — inhaled brine and exhaling rot-sweet mud when the water withdrew, then swallowed its own breath again as the sea crept back through channels silvered like veins of mercury. By midmorning the herons went silent before any human eye could see a reason, and by late afternoon the light turned the color of weak tea, and the wind came off the water carrying that particular thin coldness that meant rain was thinking about arriving but hadn't committed.

She knew weather without magic. She had spent ten years learning to know it that way, deliberately, the way a man who has lost a limb learns to know the room by touch alone. It had become almost a devotion: this reading of clouds with nothing behind her eyes but attention. No storm-calling. No reaching. Just a woman standing in reeds taller than she was, watching the sky behave itself.

"Elodie." Wren's voice came from somewhere to her left, muffled by the reeds. "There's a boat."

"There's always a boat. Marsh people have boats the way other people have shoes."

"Not like this one. This one is *sneaky*."

Elodie smiled despite herself and pushed through the reeds toward the girl's voice. Wren crouched at the edge of a channel, mud to her knees, pointing with the fierce certainty of a child who had survived by noticing things. And there — half-hidden under a screen of alder branches — was a boat. Low, dark-hulled, oars shipped and wrapped in cloth to keep them quiet. Not a marsh boat at all. Marsh boats sat high in the water and were ugly on purpose.

That one was built for silence.

Behind them, further up the shingle where the marsh gave way to grey grass, Sera stood talking with Vaskar — or rather, standing near the great shape of him, half-buried in the dunes like a hill that had decided to rest there. Even diminished, even wounded from his fall into the sea, he was enormous; gulls perched along his spine and he permitted it, which was more courtesy than he showed most living things. He could not fly yet. His left wing dragged when he moved, and every hour that passed seemed to take something else out of him — another scale gone dull, another inch of the ember-glow beneath his hide fading toward ash. He had stopped pretending otherwise around the third day. That frightened Elodie more than anything the wardens or inquisitors could do.

Sera saw her looking and lifted a hand — *all well* — and Elodie turned back to the channel and the sneaky boat, and let the smile die out of her face entirely.

"They've found us," she said quietly.

"You don't know that."

"I know that boat didn't grow here. I know Maren Voss doesn't give up. And I know—" She hesitated, because the last thing felt like superstition even though she no longer believed in coincidences. "I know my hands have been itching since dawn. Both of them. Right where the storm used to live."

Wren looked down at Elodie's hands as though they might be visibly itching, and then up at her face, and whatever she saw there made her stop arguing. Ten years old, and she already understood that some kinds of knowledge weren't worth disputing.

They were halfway back to the others when the first horn sounded.

It came from the seaward edge of the marsh, low and flat, a sound like the marsh itself clearing its throat. Then another answered from the landward side. Then a third, further north, and Elodie's stomach dropped through the mud of the world because she understood the pattern instantly: they were not chasing. They were closing a circle. Wardens didn't hunt like inquisitors — Corvin Ashe's people burned what answered and asked questions after — wardens *herded*. They wanted the sigil intact and the bearers alive, or at least two of them alive, and they wanted it done cleanly, and High Warden Maren Voss had never in her life done anything uncleanly.

Except once. Except me, Elodie thought, and did not let herself finish the thought.

"*Vaskar!*" Her voice cracked across the marsh. "*Sera!* Circle closing — we move now, east, into the deep channels—"

She heard rather than saw the response: the vast slow grinding of the dragon rising from his rest, sand sheeting off his flanks, Sera's voice pitched high and urgent. And she heard the other thing too, the thing she'd been dreading — boots in the shallows, many of them, disciplined, wading through water that should have been too soft to hold a charge. They had come across the flats at low tide. Of course they had. Maren would have waited for low tide the way she'd once waited for Elodie's seventeenth birthday to tell her a secret.

The reeds ahead of them exploded.

Not fire — the wardens' discipline held even here — but bodies, six of them in grey-and-white salt-cloaks, spears leveled, faces grim beneath hoods crusted with dried spray. Elodie pulled Wren against her so hard the girl squeaked, and put herself between the spears and the child with a speed that surprised them both, her body remembering things her mind had spent a decade teaching itself to forget.

"High Warden's compliments," said the nearest warden, a woman with a scar splitting her chin. "The sigil. The priestess. You come with us, Eastwick, and the girl walks free. Those are the terms."

"And if I refuse?"

The woman's eyes flicked — just once — upward. To the sky.

To the bruising cloud that was gathering over the sea beyond the marsh, black-green and wrong, stacked in towers, moving against the wind.

*Moving toward me*, Elodie realized, with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear. *Gathering. Answering.*

Because that was the truth she had been refusing to look at since the valley: the itch in her hands wasn't dread. It was appetite. The storms of Caelmere remembered her. Ten years of exile, ten years of the ward pressing magic flat as hammered tin, and now she was outside it, in a country of dead roads and dying gods where everything old was waking and grasping — and the sky above the saltmarsh had caught her scent the way a hound catches blood.

The wardens began to advance. Slowly, professionally, giving her time to be sensible.

And Elodie Eastwick, who had not called a storm in ten years, who had sworn on Vaskar's own name that she never would, reached into the sky and *pulled.*

* * *

It was not like the valley. In the valley, magic couldn't follow; here, magic fell on her like an ocean falling into a cup.

Later she would try to describe it to Sera and fail. The nearest she ever got was this: imagine being deaf for ten years, and then hearing not one sound but every sound at once, all the sounds in the world, each of them calling your name. The storm was already there — had been building over the cold water for hours, a natural thing, an honest thing — and the moment she touched it, it became hers, wholly and gratefully, a starving animal finally offered a hand to lick. She felt its weight in megatons of suspended water. She felt its hunger in the static crawling down her arms and lifting the fine hairs there. She felt its *joy*, and that was the worst part, because joy was contagious, and some sealed cellar door inside her flew open and she was nineteen again and the whole sky loved her and she had never once, in ten years of bees and honey and careful smallness, actually wanted to be small.

The first lightning strike took the lead warden's spear and turned it to a blackened fork. The second hit the channel where two more wardens were wading, and the thunderclap arrived so fast the sound and the flash were one event, and men were simply *gone* from the water, and the water itself leapt white and steaming into the air.

"STOP," Wren screamed. "ELodie, STOP—"

But Elodie was somewhere else. She stood in the middle of the marsh with her arms raised and rain beginning to fall in sheets that fell sideways, and the storm poured through her, and it was — gods forgive her — *magnificent.* Every prayer she had ever swallowed, every night she had lain awake in the Hollow Valley feeling the absence of power like the absence of a limb, every envious glance at thunderclouds she'd refused to claim — all of it was being repaid with interest, and the interest was delicious. She could feel the whole storm system now, hundreds of square miles of it, and it would do anything. Anything. She could drown the wardens. She could drown the county. She could call the lightning down in the shape of her own name and write it burning across the sky for every mapless mile—

Something small slammed into her legs.

Wren. Arms locked around her thighs, face buried against the wet wool of her coat, shouting something Elodie couldn't hear over the wind — and then the wind shifted, and she heard it:

"—you're going to kill us, you're going to kill US—"

And Elodie looked down, past the girl clinging to her, and saw the lightning leave her own hand.

She hadn't decided to throw it. That was the horror of it, the thing she would carry for the rest of her life: she had not *decided.* The storm was moving through her and she was enjoying it, luxuriating in it, drunk on it, and the storm — loyal, eager, terrible — was doing what storms did, which was strike at anything that moved. A warden had broken from the line. Small. Fast. Running not toward them but away, toward the dunes, a young one, panicking — and the bolt leapt from Elodie's fingers with the easy affection of a hand patting a shoulder, and the world went white—

—and Wren was between her and it. Had been, for one heartbeat, exactly in the path, dragging on her legs, pulling her off-balance, turning her aim by accident into miracle.

The bolt struck the mud three feet from the running warden and blew a crater in the marsh, and the shockwave picked Wren up like a doll and threw her.

Everything after that happened very slowly and very quickly at once. Elodie heard herself screaming — heard her own voice say *no, no, no, put it DOWN,* and felt the storm resist, felt it sulk, felt it like a great dog that didn't understand why the game had ended — and she tore her arms down and *shoved*, with everything in her, not at the wardens but at the sky, *away, go away, I release you, I RELEASE you* — and the storm peeled off her like a bandage off a burn, taking its joy with it, leaving her hollow and shaking in the sudden unnatural quiet.

The rain kept falling, but it was only rain now. Honest rain. Nobody's rain.

"Wren." She was on her knees in the mud. "Wren, Wren—"

The girl lay on her side in the shallows, and for three heartbeats — three heartbeats that Elodie would count for the rest of her life, one, two, three, the longest units of time ever measured — she didn't move.

Then she coughed, spat out marsh water, and started to cry. Not loudly. The small, furious crying of someone who has been scared worse than she wants anyone to know.

Elodie gathered her up and held on, and the girl let her, which was somehow worse than if she hadn't. Over Wren's shoulder Elodie could see the wardens — the survivors — regrouping far across the flats, staring at her the way sailors stare at a reef they've just learned the location of. The scarred woman made a hand-sign, and they withdrew, unhurried, into the rain. Called off. Or sent to report. With Maren it was always both.

Sera reached them first, skirts plastered to her legs, the bronze sigil clutched against her chest and humming faintly, as it had hummed since the marsh, since the storm began — drinking, Elodie realized numbly. It had been drinking the storm's echo. Everything in this country drank now; everything was starving. Behind Sera came Vaskar, laboring through the soft ground, and his shadow fell over them both like a mountain's grief.

"She's breathing," Sera said. "She's whole. Elodie — she's whole."

"I nearly—" Elodie's voice broke apart. She pressed her mouth to the top of Wren's head, to the smell of mud and rain and child. "I aimed at nothing. I wasn't aiming. I was *enjoying it*, Sera. Do you understand? The storm was killing people and I was standing in the middle of it feeling like a god, and if Wren hadn't grabbed me—"

"But she did." Sera knelt in the mud beside them, and her voice was gentle and absolutely steady, the voice of a woman who had drowned once already and refused to be hurried by anything again. "She grabbed you, and you stopped. Listen to me. You stopped."

"It shouldn't have taken her grabbing me."

"No," Vaskar said.

His voice rolled over the marsh like a landslide deciding to be polite about it. Elodie looked up at him. Rain ran off his jaw in rivers. His ember-dimmed eyes regarded her with something she had never seen in them before: not sardonic amusement, not ancient patience, but a kind of raw, unguarded sorrow, as if she had shown him a wound he recognized because it matched his own.

"No, it should not have," he said. "Now you know what I have known for four hundred years. The fire does not care whom it loves. It loves everyone equally, the way a flood does." He lowered his great head until his breath stirred her soaked hair. "In the valley, little keeper, I told you the door will kill whatever opens it. I lied by omission. It is not the opening that kills. It is the *wanting*. Whatever stands in that threshold must want the power less than it wants what stands behind it. Today you learned the size of your wanting. Grieve it. Then thank the child who measured it for you."

Wren, still sniffling against Elodie's coat, lifted her head and looked at the dragon with red-rimmed, ferocious eyes.

"You're welcome," she said thickly.

A sound escaped Elodie that was half laugh and half sob and entirely involuntary. She held the girl tighter.

But underneath the laughter, underneath the relief, something else was settling into place — cold and clear as the rain itself. Because she had understood, in those three heartbeats while Wren didn't move, why the valley existed. Why exile. Why mercy. She had spent ten years believing she'd been punished for a crime, and then believing she'd been punished for someone else's crime, and both beliefs had let her keep a small clean flame of grievance burning to warm herself by. But it wasn't punishment. It had never been punishment. The ward around the Hollow Valley wasn't a wall to keep her in.

It was a wall to keep the storm out. To keep *her* out of the storm. Someone — Maren, the council, whoever — had looked at what lived inside her and understood that the kindest thing, the only survivable thing, was to lock the hungry thing away from its food.

Exile hadn't stolen her power. Exile had been the only reason she still had a self left to lose.

"Elodie?" Sera's hand was on her arm. "The tide's turning. We need to move — deeper channels, east. There'll be more of them."

Elodie nodded. She rose, keeping Wren's hand locked in hers, and did not let go even when the girl squirmed, even when Vaskar turned his bulk toward the eastern flats with a groan of strained wing-joints. She walked with her free hand closed in a fist at her side, nails biting her palm, holding onto the pain because the pain was proof of a boundary, and the boundary was proof she was still a person and not a weather system wearing one.

The rain followed them east across the marsh, gentle, ordinary, falling on the dead roads and the nameless milestones and the grey grass. And far behind them, at the seaward rim of the world, the bruised clouds she had summoned and dismissed were gathering themselves again — slowly, patiently, the way an addict's craving gathers — waiting to see whether she would reach for them one more time.

She could still feel them there. That was the hook the storm had left in her: not the lightning, not the power, but the *knowing* — that somewhere over the cold water, everything she had ever been owed was waiting, and it would come whenever she called, and it would love her completely, and it would burn down anything she forgot to protect.

Three days later, at the edge of the saltmarsh, they met an old cartographer who took one look at the sigil in Sera's hands and went white as bone — and told them where the Door of Caelmere truly lay.

But that evening, walking the last miles of the marsh with Wren's hand in hers, Elodie knew one thing already, with a certainty that felt like the first honest thing in ten years:

If the door needed a storm-caller, it would get one. But the storm would never again get *her.*
