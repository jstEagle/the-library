## 17. What Wren Saw

Wren woke to the smell of rosemary and burnt feathers.

The burnt feathers were her own hair — a patch above her left ear where the lightning had passed close enough to curl it, close enough that when she touched the spot her fingertips came away smelling of storms. The rosemary was hanging from the beam of the low room where they'd carried her, because Sera said rosemary kept bad dreams out, and Wren had not had the heart to tell her that the bad dreams were already inside and had signed a lease.

She lay still for a while and took inventory, the way Elodie had taught her during the first week on the ghost-roads. *When you wake up somewhere new, count what you have before you count what you've lost.* Ten toes. Ten fingers. Two ears, both working, though the left one held a faint ringing like a struck spoon. One whole body, bruised from shoulder to hip where the water had thrown her against a fence post that was no longer a fence post but a memory of one, since the storm had unmade half the marsh.

One family, she counted next, and then stopped, because counting them felt like tempting something.

She could hear them through the wall. That was how she knew she was safe, actually — not the walls, which were peat and reed and would burn like a festival if anyone cared to light them, but the voices. Sera's voice, low and even, saying something about fever-breaking. Vaskar's voice — and this still made Wren grin into the dark ceiling, a dragon's voice coming through a cottage wall like a grandfather grumbling about his knees — saying something about *foolish weather* in a tone that meant he'd been awake all night worrying and would deny it under torture. And underneath both, quieter, a voice Wren knew better than her own by now: Elodie, not saying much of anything. Elodie hadn't said much of anything in two days.

Wren knew why. She'd seen it. She'd been standing right there when the sky opened.

* * *

Here is what Wren saw, and she had been putting it in order ever since, the way you straighten beads on a string:

The wardens came out of the reeds at dusk, six of them, and there was no time to run and no cover worth the name, and Elodie had gone very still in the way she went still before doing anything important, the way she went still before telling the truth. And then Elodie had looked up at the clouds — flat, gray, harmless clouds, the kind that only know how to rain politely — and she had *asked.*

That was the part people would get wrong later, Wren thought. They'd say she summoned it, commanded it, whipped the sky like a mule. But Wren had heard the actual words, because she'd been close enough, holding the pack straps with both hands the way Elodie told her to whenever things got sharp. Elodie had said, almost gently, almost apologetically: *"I need you."*

And the sky had come down like a lover.

Not rain. Not a storm, even, not really — Wren had seen storms on the coast before the valley, real ones, ones with manners and shapes. This had been the sea deciding it wanted to be the sky instead. The wind didn't blow so much as arrive, all at once, everywhere, and the lightning came sideways and in colors Wren didn't have names for, green and violet and one flash that was white the way bones are white. The wardens' horses screamed and bolted. A warden's spear turned to splinters in his hands without touching anything. And the floodwater — the floodwater had picked Wren up like a mother picking up a child, except mothers are careful, and this water was not careful, this water was *joyful*, and joy is the most dangerous thing in the world when it is bigger than you.

She remembered going under. She remembered thinking, with strange clarity, *oh, I'm going to die in water that remembers being ocean,* and being more embarrassed than frightened, because it seemed like a stupid way for a girl who'd survived Ondrel and Grayford Bridge and the toll-bridge to go.

And then — hands. Elodie's hands, hooked under her arms, dragging her up through water that fought the whole way, water that did not want to give her back, and Elodie shouting at the storm — not words, just sound, raw sound, like tearing cloth — and the storm letting go. All at once. Like a hand opening.

Then the quiet after, which was worse. Rain falling politely again. Six wardens scattered and groaning and alive, mostly, though one would walk crooked forever. And Elodie kneeling in the shallows with Wren's head in her lap, pressing on the gash above Wren's ear, and Elodie's face —

Wren had seen a lot of faces in her nine years. Dead faces, mostly, early on; then hungry faces, then guarded faces, then the slow thawing of three specific faces that had decided, for reasons Wren never questioned and never intended to question, to keep her. She was good at faces. It was survival.

Elodie's face in the shallows had been the worst face Wren had ever seen. Not grief. Grief she knew. It was the face of someone who has found something they buried on purpose, dug it up, and recognized it as their own.

* * *

On the third day, they let her get up.

Sera fussed — Sera fussed beautifully, with compresses and bitter teas and a running commentary on the moral failings of marsh water — and Vaskar pretended not to fuss, which for a dragon meant coiling himself around the outside of the cottage in the rain and refusing to leave, on the grounds that the mud was *interesting.* He was too big for the yard now; he'd grown since Grayford, or maybe Wren was only noticing. His scales had gone duller, though. She'd noticed that too, and filed it away in the place where she kept things that scared her, which was getting crowded.

Elodie was in the garden, of course. Elodie was always in the garden when her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. She was tying back bean runners that the storm had flattened, with strips of rag, very carefully, as if the beans were also recovering from something.

Wren let herself out the door, stood a moment testing her legs — fine, mostly, a little sea-drunk — and walked across the wet grass to stand beside her.

"You're supposed to be resting," Elodie said, not looking up.

"I rested three days. I'm a professional."

Something flickered at the corner of Elodie's mouth. Not quite a smile. Wren watched for it anyway, catalogued it, held onto it. Then she crouched by the bean row and started handing up rag strips before they were asked for, which was her whole strategy in life: be useful enough that nobody remembers to send you away.

They worked in silence for a while. The marsh had begun its repairs — reeds standing back up, birds returning to argue about territory, the flooded low field draining with a long musical gurgle. Somewhere out there, the water that remembered being ocean was seeping back toward the sea, carrying whatever it had learned about them.

"Does it hurt?" Elodie asked finally. "Your head."

"Only when I think." Wren handed up another strip. "So mostly no."

That got the almost-smile again. Wren gathered her courage the way she gathered everything: quickly, greedily, before it could be taken back.

"Elodie."

"Mm."

"I'm not afraid of you."

The rag strip went taut in Elodie's hands. She didn't stop tying, exactly, but the tying changed — became the kind of knot you tie when your mind is elsewhere, the kind that has to be cut off later. Wren pressed on, because she had learned from watching Sera that some things, once started, have to be finished or they rot.

"I want you to know that first. Before the other thing. I'm not afraid of you. Everyone thinks I should be, probably. Vaskar keeps looking at me like he's waiting for me to start crying, and Sera keeps asking if I'm having dreams about the water, and I'm not, I dreamt about bees last night, actual bees, they were spelling words." She made herself breathe. "But I'm not afraid of you. Even now. Especially now."

Elodie set down the rag. Her hands hung empty at her sides, and Wren saw them trembling, very slightly, and understood suddenly that the beans had never been the point.

"Then what," Elodie said quietly, "are you afraid of?"

And here was the thing Wren had been turning over for three days in the dark behind her eyes, the thing that had finally come out the right shape, and it hurt to say because she could see, even before she said it, that it was going to land like the fence post had landed — but she was nine years old and she had buried everyone else she loved, and she had learned that the only unforgivable thing was leaving the true thing unsaid.

"I'm afraid you'll leave us," Wren said. "To be powerful again."

The words came out smaller than they'd sounded in her head. They always did. But they went in deep — she watched them go in, watched them pass through Elodie's ribs the way the lightning had passed over hers, and for a moment neither of them breathed.

"The storm obeyed you," Wren went on, because once the door was open everything fell out at once, the way it does. "It *loved* you. Did you see it? It wasn't angry, it was — it was happy, it was like a dog that's been shut in a house for ten years, and you called it and it came, and it nearly—" Her voice cracked and she hated that and pushed through. "It nearly killed me and it was *happy*. And you stood in the middle of it and part of your face was terrified but part of it wasn't, part of it was home, and I saw that part, and I keep seeing it."

"Wren—"

"And here's the bad part." Wren stood up, because she couldn't say the rest crouching among beans like a coward. "The bad part is I understand it. If I had something like that in me — something that big, that missed me that much — I don't know that I'd want to give it up either. So I'm not mad. I'm not even asking you to promise. I just needed you to know that I *saw*. Because everybody's going to ask you if you're sorry, and you're going to say yes, and it'll even be true. But I saw the other thing too. And I wanted you to know somebody saw it and stayed anyway."

The silence after was enormous. A heron flapped up out of the drainage ditch. The beans dripped.

Elodie sat down. Not gracefully — she just folded, right there in the mud between the rows, knees up, arms around them, a woman thirty years old sitting in a garden like a child sent to think about what she'd done. Wren had never seen her do that. Nobody who'd known her ten days would have believed it.

"When I was nineteen," Elodie said, to the bean row rather than to Wren, "a storm killed forty people, and everyone said I called it, and I said I didn't, and the truth is I genuinely don't remember. Isn't that a terrible thing? Not whether I did it — whether I *would know*. There's a room in me where that night used to be and the door to it won't open, and every day I have to decide whether the door is locked from the outside or the inside." She laughed, a small terrible sound. "Ten years in exile and I still don't know if I'm being punished or hidden."

"You're being kept," Wren said. "By us. It's different."

Elodie looked up at her then — really looked, the way she almost never let herself, because looking at people directly was apparently one of the many things exile had taught her to ration. And Wren saw the exact moment the words finished landing, deeper than any blade, deeper than the fence post, all the way down to wherever Elodie kept the girl she'd been before the trial.

"You're right," Elodie said. "That's the worst part. You're nine and you're right and I hate it."

"I'm very wise. It's the head wound."

That startled a real laugh out of her — short, wet, surprised — and Elodie scrubbed a hand across her face and pulled Wren down into the mud beside her without asking, which was its own kind of answer, and put an arm around her shoulders, careful of the bruises, careful of everything, always so careful, and Wren leaned in and let herself be careful-with.

"If I start to go," Elodie said into her hair, "you tell me. Not Sera, not Vaskar. You. You're the only one who'll see it early, because you're the only one watching for it."

"That's a lot of responsibility for a professional rest-taker."

"You can handle it."

They sat like that while the marsh drained and the light went long and gold, and Wren thought: *there. Whatever happens next, we had this. Put it in the keeping-place with the bees.*

* * *

It was full dark when Vaskar's shadow crossed the window.

Wren saw it first — she was getting good at reading him, at reading the particular quality of his silences — and she nudged Elodie, and they came out into the yard together, and found the dragon standing at the north edge of the garden with his head raised and his nostrils flared, looking at nothing, looking at everything.

"What is it?" Sera asked from the doorway, tea towel still in her hands.

Vaskar was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost all its sardonic furniture. It was bare wood underneath.

"My fire," he said, "just guttered. Like a candle in a draft. And there is no draft." His great head turned slowly, and his old eyes caught the moonlight and threw it back the color of dying embers. "Something is drinking. Something far away is drinking, little priestess, and I am beginning to suspect I know what the cup is."

Behind them, in the cottage, on the table where Sera had wrapped it in linen against the damp, the bronze sigil began — very softly, like a struck bell heard through a mountain — to hum.
