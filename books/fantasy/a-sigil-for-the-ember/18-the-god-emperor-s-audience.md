## 18. The God-Emperor's Audience

The palace of Aurelth had a smell that Corvin Ashe had never learned to stop noticing: cold incense and hot metal, like a blade being quenched in a censer. It was the smell of divinity as the god-emperor practiced it — something holy forced through something industrial — and it met him at the doors of the Hall of Ascension before the guards even touched their halberds.

He had been riding for eleven days. He had not slept properly since Ondrel, and his left arm still carried the stiffness of the wound he'd told no one about, the one he'd taken from a fishmonger's hook when the market crowd turned ugly and he'd chosen, for one long heartbeat, not to draw steel on civilians who were only frightened of him. The wound was shallow. The choice was not. He could feel it sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Inquisitors did not report failure often. There was a protocol for it, though; there was a protocol for everything in the Inquisition. You surrendered your sidearm at the bronze doors. You knelt on the seventh flagstone from the threshold, the one worn hollow by two hundred years of knees. You spoke your report aloud, plainly, without ornament, and you did not look above the emperor's collar unless commanded.

Corvin had performed this ritual four times in nine years of service. Three times, the news had been good. Once, it had been this.

The hall was vast and mostly empty, which was its own kind of statement. A lesser ruler would have filled it — courtiers, sycophants, the machinery of flattery — but Emperor Savel the Second, Called Divine, Called Undying, kept his audiences small and his silences enormous. His throne stood on a dais of black glass at the far end, beneath a dome painted with a sky that never changed: golden, cloudless, fixed. No weather in the imperial heaven. Corvin thought, absurdly, of rain falling upward over a valley rim, and pressed the thought down like a coal under a boot.

Savel sat slumped. That was the first wrongness, always the first wrongness, and Corvin catalogued it the way he'd been trained to catalogue everything: the emperor did not sit so much as pool, one shoulder lower than the other, chin propped on knuckles, a man wearing his own body the way a tired actor wears a costume an hour past curtain. He was not old. That was the official miracle — the god-emperor had ascended forty years ago at thirty-two and had not aged a day, and the portraits in every government office proved it, row upon row of the same strong jaw, the same dark eyes, the same confident half-smile. The portraits were accurate. The man they depicted had simply drifted out from behind them, the way a hand withdraws from a glove.

"Corvin Ashe." The voice came out flat and slightly too slow, like a bell rung underwater. "You have failed."

"I have, Your Radiance."

"Say it anyway. I find I enjoy hearing the truth spoken in this room. It happens so rarely."

So Corvin said it. He gave the report kneeling, eyes on the seventh flagstone, and he made himself honest because lying here felt like pouring water into a drain. He described Ondrel: the unregistered city, the erased names, the market where a fugitive priestess and a storm-caller had walked out from under his nose with a bronze sigil humming like a struck bell. He described the hour's head start. He did not explain why he'd given it. He knew the emperor would ask, and he knew what answer he had prepared, and he knew how thin it would sound, and he said all of it anyway, because the alternative was a lie, and lies in this room curdled fast.

When he finished, the silence went on so long that Corvin could hear the dome. There was a faint ticking up there, he realized — he had never noticed it in previous visits — some mechanism in the painted heavens expanding and contracting with heat, ticking away the seconds of an immortal's attention.

"You let them go," Savel said at last.

"Yes, Your Radiance."

"One hour. Why?"

Because she looked at me the way she used to look at thunderheads. Because her friend is dead or worse and she has nothing left but a child and a priestess and a piece of bronze. Because ten years ago I stood in a courtroom and watched her be sentenced for a crime I half-believed she didn't commit, and I have spent ten years telling myself the half was the smaller part. Because if I had killed her in that market, I would have finally become the thing my oath says I already am, and some last cowardly corner of me wanted one more day of not knowing.

"Because a public confrontation risked casualties among unregistered citizens," Corvin said, "whose cooperation we will need to locate the fugitives again. And because I judged pursuit more valuable than arrest. They are traveling toward something, Your Radiance. I intend to follow them to it."

He heard rather than saw the emperor shift on the throne — a dry rustle, cloth and skin and something underneath the cloth that moved a half-second late, like an echo.

"Toward what?"

"The Door, possibly. Or toward allies. Or nowhere. But the sigil is real, and it resonates, and resonance can be tracked. Give me leave to follow the signal instead of the people, and I will bring you the sigil and everyone carrying it."

Another silence. When Corvin finally dared raise his eyes to the permitted height — the collar, the jaw, the portrait-accurate face — he found the emperor watching him with an expression he had never seen on any official likeness: hunger. Not ambition. Not wrath. Hunger of the specific, animal kind that lives in the gut of a thing that is starving and knows exactly what will feed it.

"Do you know," Savel said, "what I was, before the Ascension?"

Corvin knew the catechism answer. Everyone did; schoolchildren recited it. *A humble servant of the people, raised up by the last will of a dying age.* He also knew, from nine years inside the Inquisition's archives, roughly forty answers that had been scrubbed from the record, most of which involved a minor noble house, three bankruptcies, and a great deal of borrowed money.

"A servant of the people, Your Radiance."

The emperor laughed. It was a terrible sound, brief and joyless, a cough with ambition. "Yes. That's what it says. Do you know what I actually was? I was afraid. All the time. Of my creditors, of my cousins, of the pox, of drowning, of the dark at the end of the road between here and Grayford. Fear was the whole of me, Ashe. I was a bag of fear wearing a coat. And then the old gods began to die — do you understand that? They were dying, all of them, the whole senile family of them, and their death was leaking into the world like light under a door, and I found I could drink it. Anyone could have. Any beggar on that road. I got there first, that's all. That's the entire miracle. I got there first and I drank."

The dome ticked. Somewhere behind Corvin, at a distance, a guard shifted his weight, and the leather of his harness creaked like a ship.

"And now," the emperor said, and his voice dropped into something quieter and infinitely worse, "now they are answering prayers again."

"They may merely be—"

"I know what they may merely be. I have analysts, Ashe. I have priests of the Ascension who spend their nights reading entrails and grain prices and border reports, and every one of them tells me what you were about to tell me: that dying gods twitch, that echoes return, that a few answered prayers in a backwater valley mean nothing." He rose from the throne — unfolded from it, more precisely, joint by joint, and the motion had that same half-second lag Corvin had noticed before, flesh following intent like a delayed echo of itself — and came down the steps of the dais with slow, grinding patience. "I am telling you what I know. I know what a god sounds like when it turns its attention back toward the world, because I spent forty years learning to imitate the noise. And what I hear out there—" he gestured vaguely, at the walls, at the kingdom, at the drowned cathedral no map admitted to and the valley no ward admitted to, "—is not an echo. An echo repeats. This is answering. Echoes don't answer."

He stopped three paces away. At this range Corvin could see what no portrait preserved: the fine tremor in the emperor's hands, the sweat at the temple despite the chill, the pupils blown wide and dark, drinking the light of the hall the way the man had once drunk the leaking death of gods. Divinity sat on Savel like an ill-fitting coat — Corvin had thought it in the doorway and thought it again now, closer, worse — shoulders cut for someone broader, hem dragging, seams straining, and beneath it the original man visible at wrist and throat, pale and mortal and terrified, the bag of fear still a bag of fear, just better dressed.

"You testified once," the emperor said suddenly. "Nine years ago. The trial of the storm-caller. Eastwick."

Corvin's stomach dropped with the smooth, sickening drop of a floor removed. "Your Radiance remembers."

"I remember everything. That's another thing they don't put in the catechism — the drinking doesn't fade, it accumulates. Forty years of perfect memory, Ashe, stacked like corpses." Savel tilted his head. "Elodie Eastwick. Nineteen years old. Storm that killed forty souls in Caelmere town. Your testimony was instrumental. Why?"

Why indeed. Because he had seen her that night, on the ridge above Caelmere, arms raised against a sky that shouldn't have held that much lightning, and he had believed — he had *needed* to believe — that the girl he loved had done it, because the alternative was that the world could simply do that, without malice, without reason, without anyone to blame. Belief had been a mercy. He had offered it as testimony.

"She confessed to the working," Corvin said carefully. "And the evidence of channeling was consistent with a caller of her strength."

"Mm." The emperor studied him the way a man studies a lock he intends to break eventually, but not today. "Her sentence was exile rather than execution. Unusual. Warden Voss argued for it forcefully. Some said suspiciously forcefully." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Do you think the exile was mercy, Ashe? Or do you think it was storage?"

The word landed in Corvin's chest and stayed there, cold. *Storage.* As though Maren had known — as though someone had known — that a storm-caller might someday be needed, might someday be useful, might be kept alive the way one keeps a blade oiled in a scabbard against a war one expects. He thought of Maren Voss's face at the trial, gray and certain, arguing down three magistrates who wanted ash. He had admired her certainty. He had never once asked what it cost her, or what it was *for*.

"I don't know, Your Radiance."

"Honest. Good. You're one of perhaps six honest men in this building, and I keep the other five where I can watch them." The emperor turned away, climbing back toward the throne with that lagging, echoing gait, and Corvin understood he had been dismissed in every way except the formal one. Then, halfway up the steps, Savel paused. "You will have your leave to track the resonance. Take whatever the Inquisition can give you. And Ashe —"

"Your Radiance?"

"If the old gods are truly waking — if even one of them puts its shoulder against the wall between the dead land and this one — then hear me, and hear me correctly, because this is the only order I will ever give you that comes from the bottom of whatever I am instead of the top." The emperor did not turn around. His voice came back flat and stripped of all its performance, and for one moment it was just a frightened man on a staircase, forty years deep in a lie he could no longer take off. "Burn the sigil. Burn the priestess. Burn the valley, the city that unhappened, the roads that aren't on maps, and every soul who has prayed to anything older than me. I did not climb onto this throne over the graves of their religion to have them climb back out of it. Whatever returns through that door does not get to want anything ever again. Do you understand me?"

"I understand, Your Radiance."

"Then go. And Corvin —" the head turned at last, just enough to show one profile, one wide dark eye, "— if you find the storm-caller, remember that I remember your testimony. Remember that I know what you were to each other. And remember that I am not a merciful god, only a recent one."

* * *

The corridor outside the Hall of Ascension was long, white, and lit by lamps that burned without flickering, because the air in the palace was not permitted to move. Corvin walked it slowly, giving his legs time to remember how, and found that his hands were shaking — not from fear of the audience, though there had been plenty, but from the shape of what he'd heard.

He had gone in expecting a tyrant. Tyrants he understood; the Inquisition ran on them the way mills run on water. He had expected vanity, wrath, the ordinary appetites of extraordinary power. What he had found instead was dread — pure, undiluted, forty years aged dread — and dread in a ruler was a different animal entirely. Wrath destroyed its enemies. Dread destroyed everything within reach, enemies and instruments and innocent bystanders alike, indiscriminately, the way a panicked man swings a lamp in a burning house and sets fire to the curtains and the children and the stairs.

*Burn the valley.* The Hollow Valley. Ten years of wards, ten years of a sentence served in dust and bees, and the emperor would burn it over a rumor of bells. And Wren would be in it, or near it — the girl with the scraped elbows and the fierce, quick eyes, the girl whose prayer had started all of this, if half of what the resonance-readings suggested was true. A child's prayer, answered by something starving. And for that, fire.

Corvin stopped at a window at the corridor's end. Below, the capital of a country that officially existed spread out in obedient geometry: avenues named for Ascension miracles, plazas where the emperor's portrait smiled from every wall, temples of the new faith with their domes gilded so bright they hurt to look at by day. It was a beautiful city. It was a city built over a graveyard and paved before the graves settled, and now, somewhere beneath the paving, things were turning over in their sleep, and the man upstairs was drinking wine with hands that wouldn't stop trembling.

*Storage,* he thought. *Maren. What did you store her for?*

He had sent no word to Maren since Ondrel. Protocol demanded it — a joint operation, a shared fugitive, and High Warden Voss was owed a full accounting of his failure. But every draft he'd composed on the road home had died unsent, because he could not decide what the letter was *for*: warning her, confessing to her, or testing whether the woman who had saved Elodie Eastwick from the pyre would now help the emperor put her back into it. Nine years of admiration, and he had never once considered that her mercy might have been arithmetic.

Now he had a new variable, and its name was the emperor's fear, and it reorganized everything. If Savel feared the old gods more than rebellion — and Corvin had heard it, plain as a confession, the terror under the throne — then the calculus of the hunt inverted entirely. Maren hunted the sigil-bearers to prevent a war. The emperor would hunt them to prevent a resurrection. Those were not the same mission, and they would not stay aligned, and Corvin was now the only man in the empire positioned to see the seam where they split.

Unless. Unless Maren already knew. Unless the gray-faced certainty at the trial, the forceful mercy, the exile instead of the ash — unless all of it had been the first move in a game she'd been playing for nine years against a throne she'd never named aloud.

He took out his dispatch case. Inside were three letters he had drafted on the road and never sent. He read the topmost one — *To Her Excellency the High Warden, regarding events in Ondrel* — and then, standing at the window with the gilded city burning bright below him and the painted heaven ticking overhead, he fed all three, one by one, into the lamp flame beside the glass, and watched nine years of proper procedure curl and go to ash in a dish built for incense.

It was the second order he had ever disobeyed. The first had been an hour of head start in a fish market.

He wrote the new letter quickly, in the cipher reserved for warden-inquisitor correspondence, and it said only this:

*Maren. The emperor knows about the Door. He is afraid. You need to decide, before he finishes being afraid, which side of the wall you are standing on. — C.*

He sealed it, marked it urgent, and gave it to the night courier with instructions to ride through Grayford without stopping. Then he went down to the stables, woke his horse, and pointed himself north, toward saltmarsh and ghost-road and the fading resonance of a bronze sigil — ahead of any order, ahead of any sanction, a man committing treason at a trot and finding, to his considerable horror, that it felt like the first honest breath he'd taken in nine years.

Behind him, high in the white palace, the painted sky ticked on, cloudless and golden and fixed, and no weather was permitted anywhere in it at all.
