## 20. Sera's Cathedral

The rain had followed them into the saltmarsh for three days, and Sera Ivane had not slept in four.

She sat apart from the others, at the edge of the firelight, where the wet peat hissed and steamed beneath her boots. The bronze sigil hung against her chest on its leather cord, warm as a sleeping bird. It was always warm now. In the beginning it had only hummed — that struck-bell note she'd felt through her ribs the night she pulled it from the altar of Saint Verre's, the night the inquisitors burned her order down around her ears. Now it murmured. Now, when she pressed her palm flat against it, she could almost feel words forming underneath, like fish moving under ice.

He is waiting, the murmur said. He has waited so long.

She had told herself a hundred lies on the road from Caelmere's corpse-country to this drowned green edge of the world. That she hadn't known where the sigil must go. That the god's command had been vague, dreamlike, open to interpretation. That bringing Elodie Eastwick along was obedience enough — the storm-caller was needed at the Door, after all; surely that counted; surely the letter of the command mattered less than its spirit.

But tonight, watching Elodie across the fire — watching her show Wren how to read the pressure in the clouds by the ache in old scars, no magic in it at all, just knowledge handed gently from one pair of hands to another — the lies would not hold anymore.

* * *

It had begun, as all her life's turning points did, with water.

Sera had been eight years old the first time the drowned god spoke to her. Not a voice — never a voice, not until these last terrible months — but a pulling, a tide behind her breastbone, drawing her toward the sea-wall of her father's fishing village on a night when the water stood unnaturally still, mirror-flat, holding the moon whole in its grip. She had climbed the wall and stood looking down at her own reflection, and the reflection had looked back a half-second too long, and something vast had moved beneath the harbor like a thought moving beneath a mind.

The priests found her there at dawn, soaked to the waist though she'd never touched the water. They said: marked. They said: chosen. Her mother wept with pride. Her father stopped speaking to her entirely, which she understood only years later was its own kind of devotion — some men cannot look directly at what they love most and believe.

Twenty-two years in the order of the Drowned God. Twenty-two years of cold chapels and colder rites, of learning the liturgies of a faith everyone else had forgotten or forbidden. Caelmere had been struck from the maps when the god-emperor rose; the order had gone underground, priestess by priestess, chapel by chapel, until they were a rumor even to themselves. And through all those hidden decades, one truth passed from mouth to ear like contraband:

When the gods die, they grasp. And what the Drowned God grasped for, at the end, was the Door.

She had learned the Door's location before she learned her own name's meaning. Every high priestess had. It was the last secret the order kept, the one inheritance that outlasted purges and book-burnings and the slow erosion of belief: *beneath the cathedral, below the floodline, where the bells still ring.* Their own mother-church — the Cathedral of the Deep Communion, sunk half-drowned in its flooded district when the emperor's engineers broke the seawalls a century ago, drowning the congregation mid-service rather than let them keep praying. Sera had been ordained over that fact like an anvil. She had sworn, on salt and on silence, that she would carry the sigil home if the day ever came.

The day came. Of course the day came.

And here was the wound of it, the thing she had carried alone through burning villages and ghost-roads and toll-bridges that ate pieces of her soul: the command had never said *bring the storm-caller.* The command had never said *bring anyone.*

*Bring my key to my door,* the god had said, in the first true voice she had ever heard from him, three nights after Saint Verre's burned. *Alone, little tide-pool. Alone, or not at all.*

* * *

"Sera."

She started. Elodie had crossed the camp without a sound — ten years of exile had taught her feet discretion — and now stood at the edge of the light with Wren's abandoned mending basket in her hands, offering it like a peace treaty.

"You've been sitting out here staring at the water since sundown. The water doesn't get better with practice. I checked, for about a decade."

"I'm praying," Sera said, which was true, which was also the smallest possible truth.

"Then I'll pray with you." Elodie sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and folded her hands with the solemn irony of someone who had stopped believing in listeners long ago. For a while neither spoke. The marsh breathed around them. Somewhere far off, a bird made a sound like a question.

"You're going to tell me eventually," Elodie said at last, quietly. "Whatever it is. You've got the face for it. My bees used to make the same face before a swarm decided to leave."

"The same—" Sera huffed despite herself. "You can't give your bees credit for faces."

"They had very expressive faces. Whole sermons in them." Elodie turned, and her eyes in the firelight were the gray-green of weather coming. "Sera. We're three days from wherever we're going, and you know exactly where that is, don't you? You've known since Ondrel. Maybe since the bridge." She didn't say it cruelly. She said it the way she tended her hives — smoke first, then the blade. "You've been carrying something heavier than the sigil, and you've been carrying it alone, and I have watched people carry things alone, and it always ends the same way. With them setting it down somewhere it breaks."

The tide behind Sera's breastbone surged. *Tell her nothing,* it whispered, in a voice like deep water closing over a stone. *She is not yours. She is the means. You are the means. He is the end.*

But sitting here, smelling woodsmoke and wet wool and the faint honey-sweetness that clung to Elodie even after ten years of exile, Sera found that her hands had already decided. They were shaking. She looked down at them, at the sigil's bronze glow seeping between her fingers like light through a door-crack, and she said:

"Beneath the cathedral."

Elodie went very still. "What?"

"The Door of Caelmere. The cartographer told us it was under the flooded cathedral of the drowned god, and you looked at me like it was news, and I let you." The words came out of her in a rush now, dammed too long, cutting their own channel. "I let you believe it was discovery. It wasn't. I've known since I was a child. Every priestess of my order knows — knew — it's the first thing they teach you after the liturgies. Beneath our own church. Below the floodline. Where the bells still ring." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "They built the Door beneath their holiest place because who would ever think to dig there? Who would dig into their own grave?"

Elodie set the mending basket down between them, slowly, like laying down a weapon. "Okay," she said. "Okay. What else."

That was the worst part — that Elodie didn't rage. That she just waited, patient as weather, for the rest of the storm to break.

"There's more." Sera's voice dropped. "The night Saint Verre's burned, he spoke to me. Truly spoke, for the first time in twenty-two years of service. Not a tide. Not a pulling. A voice." She wrapped both hands around the sigil as if it might anchor her. "He said: bring my key to my door. Alone, little tide-pool. Alone, or not at all."

The fire popped. Down the slope, Wren stirred in her blankets and murmured something in sleep, and both women turned instinctively toward the small sound, and something in that shared motion — that reflexive, maternal flinch toward the child — seemed to strike Sera harder than any argument could have.

"And instead," Elodie said carefully, "you came to the Hollow Valley. To me."

"Instead I broke the command." Sera's eyes were burning now, and not from smoke. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have spent my entire life being obedient. Obedience was the one thing I had when everything else was taken — my family, my order, my name on any living record. And when my god finally spoke, after all of it, I disobeyed him. Because I found you in that valley with your bees and your ward-cracked sky, and because I saw a girl drowning in a flood she prayed for, and because—" her voice fractured— "because I was so tired of being alone with him."

Elodie reached out and took her hand. Not the sigil hand. The other one. Held it the way you hold something that might bolt.

"Why are you telling me now?" she asked.

"Because tomorrow we reach the cathedral." Sera swallowed. "Because every mile closer, the voice gets louder, and every mile closer, I can feel him... leaning on me. And because if I wait until we're standing at the Door to confess it, then it isn't confession, it's ambush." She met Elodie's eyes, finally, and let her see everything — the fear, the shame, the terrible arithmetic of faith. "Betrayal confessed beforehand is just a warning. I'm asking you to treat it as one. If I walk into that water alone, he wins whatever game he's playing. But if I bring all of you—"

"He might refuse you entirely." Elodie finished it. "Or take you anyway. Or take all of us." She exhaled, long and slow, the way she must once have exhaled storms. "Gods. You really are a priestess down to the marrow. Even your confessions come with theology."

"I'm serious, Elodie."

"So am I." Elodie's grip tightened. "Listen to me. Ten years ago my best friend testified against me, and I have spent a decade deciding nightly whether it was betrayal or salvation, and do you know what I've learned? That the difference between those two things is usually just whether the person tells you the truth in time." She smiled, small and tired and real. "You're telling me in time."

"The god commanded me alone." Sera's voice shook. "If I defy him openly — if I stand at his own door with three strangers and a dragon's blessing and say *no, they come too* — I don't know what he'll do. He isn't kind, Elodie. I need you to understand that. Everyone thinks drowned gods are gentle because they're sad. Mine is a famine wearing my father's face. He has waited a hundred years under that water, and he is hungry, and I am his last priestess and possibly his last meal."

* * *

Wren appeared sometime past midnight, dragging her blanket, hair full of marsh-grass, and wedged herself between them without asking. Children have an unerring sense for when the adults are building or breaking something, Sera thought, and choose the moment of maximum structural stress to sit down in the middle of it.

"Are we almost there?" Wren asked. "To the bells-under-water place?"

"How did you—" Sera began.

"Wren listens at fires," Elodie said wryly. "It's why we brought her. Free intelligence."

"It's why you love me," Wren corrected, and yawned enormously, and then, with the terrifying directness of children, looked up at Sera and asked: "Is your god going to be angry that you brought us? Is that what you were crying about?"

Sera blinked. She hadn't known she was crying.

"A little," she admitted.

"My mama said angry gods are just big dogs nobody trained." Wren burrowed deeper into her blanket. "You just have to be braver than them and they settle down." And she fell asleep between them, mid-thought, as children do — as if courage were that simple, as if divinity were that tame.

For a long time, neither woman spoke. The fire burned low. The marsh held its breath.

"Tomorrow," Elodie said finally, "we walk into your god's house together. All four of us. Vaskar will meet us at the coast — he's slower than he pretends, and he knows it, and he hates that we know it." A pause. "And Sera? When your famine speaks to you tomorrow, when he leans on you and says *alone, little tide-pool* — you tell me. The moment he says it. Promise me that much. Not obedience. Just honesty. Can you give me that instead?"

It was, Sera realized, the first prayer anyone had ever asked of her.

"Yes," she said. "That I can do."

She slept, after that — truly slept, for the first time in four nights, the sigil warm against her heart and a child's weight against her side and a storm-caller keeping watch beside her. And in the last hour before dawn, in the borderland between sleep and waking, the tide behind her breastbone rose again, vast and patient and cold, and the famine wearing her father's face leaned close and whispered:

*Very well, little tide-pool. Bring them all. The table is large, and I am so very hungry.*

And far out in the black water, unseen by any waking eye, the drowned bells of the cathedral began — softly, sweetly, horribly — to ring.
