## 21. Night of the Long Rain

The rain had been falling for two days without conviction — a thin, gray drizzle that seemed less like weather than like the sky forgetting what it was doing. It came down through the saltmarsh reeds in threads, beading on the canvas of their lean-to, hissing when it drifted too close to the fire they'd built in the lee of a drowned cart.

The cart had been there a long time. Its wheels were black with rot, its bed half-swallowed by peat, and something pale and jointless lived in the water beneath it that none of them ever mentioned. The marsh was full of such things: the residue of Caelmere, the country that had unhappened itself. A fence post here still bore a brand from a farm no one could name. A milestone leaned in the shallows with letters worn to ghosts. The whole land was an attic of a house whose family had died mid-sentence.

They had stopped running for one night because Wren's feet had begun to bleed through her wrappings, and because Sera's burns needed tending properly instead of hastily, and because even fugitives must sometimes sleep or die of not sleeping. So they had made camp badly, in the open, under the long rain, and built a fire out of peat and driftwood that burned low and orange and smelled of old sea.

Elodie sat closest to it, as she always did now. Not for warmth. For the light. Ten years in the Hollow Valley had taught her that fire was honest in a way weather never was — it consumed exactly what it touched, asked nothing, answered nothing. She fed it a knot of peat and watched the flames climb.

Across the fire, Vaskar lay with his chin on his forearms, wings folded tight against a body that had once blotted out moons and now barely blotted out the lean-to. Even diminished, he was enormous — a hill of bronze and ash-gray scale, his hide cracked in places where the ember-light beneath showed through like coals through a dying grate. He did not sleep. Dragons past their burning did not sleep so much as pause between thoughts, and his thoughts, lately, were shorter each time.

Sera sat wrapped in a blanket with her burned arm held against her chest, salved and bandaged by Wren's careful, clumsy hands. Her face in the firelight looked younger than it had at the gate of the valley, or perhaps only more tired in a way that read as young. She was turning her prayer-beads over and over, though her god had been silent since the saltmarsh began, and everyone knew it, and no one said it.

Wren sat between them all, cross-legged, mending a bootlace by firelight with the fierce concentration of a child who has learned that small useful things are how you make yourself indispensable enough to keep.

And Corvin Ashe sat apart, as he always sat apart, one knee up, his inquisitor's coat dark with rain, his sword across his thighs in a posture that was either restlessness or readiness. Three days since Grayford Bridge. Three days since he had turned his blade on the men he'd marched beside for eleven years. The wound in his side where an inquisitor's knife had found him was bound and ugly, and he refused help changing the dressing, and Wren had left a bowl of broth near him twice, and both times he had eaten it when he thought no one saw.

The rain went on. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the black water, something called once — a bird, or a bell, or a memory — and fell silent.

It was Wren who broke the quiet, because it was always Wren.

"If you could wish," she said, not looking up from her lace, "for anything. And the gods would actually do it." She tied off a knot, bit the thread. "What would you wish?"

The question landed in the fire like a stone in a well. Elodie felt the ripples go out and touch every one of them.

"Wren," Corvin said, in the voice he used for warnings.

"It's just a game."

"It isn't," Vaskar said.

The dragon's voice was a dry landslide, deep enough that the marsh water shivered in rings around the cart. He opened one eye — molten gold gone cloudy at the rim, like an egg yolk beginning to fail — and regarded the child with an expression that on a man would have been wry.

"Nothing is *just* anything, little thief. Not anymore. Not with that thing sleeping in your pack." His snout tipped toward the oilcloth bundle strapped among their supplies, wherein the bronze sigil hummed its struck-bell hum, faint but ceaseless, like a heart that would not quiet. "The gods are listening again. That is the entire problem. Ask your question properly or don't ask it at all."

Wren's chin came up. She was nine years old and had buried both parents in the valley flood and had decided, somewhere in the weeks since, that fear was a garment she simply would not put on. "Fine. I'll ask it properly." She looked around the fire, from face to face, counting them the way she counted everything — supplies, exits, people who might leave. "If the gods answered you. Truly answered. What would you wish?"

The rain filled the silence for a while.

Then Sera laughed — a small, broken sound, surprised out of her. "Oh, that's a cruel game."

"I know," Wren said, satisfied. "Whose turn is it?"

"Yours," Elodie said. "You asked. You go first."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. She looked into the fire. When she spoke again, the performance had drained out of her voice, and what remained was smaller and much heavier.

"My parents back." She said it plainly, the way children say the largest things. "Not alive again, because that would be weird, they'd be all — " she waved a hand vaguely, " — resurrected and confused. Just. Back. Like before. Like the flood never happened and I never had to learn which plants are safe to eat." She shrugged, fiercely casual, and Elodie saw her knuckles whiten around the bootlace. "That's mine. That's the whole wish. It's boring."

"It's not boring," Sera said softly.

"It's the most common wish in the history of the world," Vaskar said. "Which does not make it small. It makes it heavy. Every god who ever starved to death starved on wishes like yours." He shifted, and the ground trembled faintly, and the water in the wheel-ruts shivered. "Next."

"Sera," Wren commanded.

Sera's beads clicked. Clicked. Stopped. She stared into the fire for a long moment, and Elodie watched her decide — watched the priestess weigh truth against the version of truth that was safe to carry in company — and then watched her choose, visibly, wearily, truth.

"I would wish to hear him again," she said. "My god. The Drowned One." Her thumb moved over the beads. "I have served him since I was six years old. I gave him my ordination — my memories of my mother's face, my first home, the taste of bread from my village oven — I gave them gladly, because he spoke to me, because his voice was like being held underwater by someone who loved me." A breath. "He doesn't speak anymore. Or he speaks and I can't hear him, I don't know which is worse. If the gods would answer me — truly answer — I wouldn't ask for anything he could give. I'd just ask him to say my name. Once. The way he used to." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing at the fire. "That's mine. It's worse than boring. It's greedy."

"No," Vaskar said. "It's faithful. Those are different diseases." His eye moved on. "Inquisitor."

"I'm not an inquisitor anymore," Corvin said. "I'm a dead man walking beside fugitives. The order will have entered it in the rolls by now. *Corvin Ashe, traitor, burned in effigy at Grayford.*" He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Ask the dead man his wish, then."

"You're not dead," Wren said. "Dead men don't eat broth."

Corvin looked at her. Something moved behind his face — the memory, maybe, of another fire, another lifetime, a girl who called storms — and he mastered it the way he mastered everything, by force. But the game had teeth in him already; Elodie could see it. The question had gotten past his armor the way questions did, the way no blade ever had.

He was quiet long enough that the rain got loud again.

"Forgiveness," he said finally, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "There was a woman. Years ago. Before the order took everything soft out of me. I testified at a trial — not hers, mine was later — but I stood in a hall and swore things that were true and ruined someone anyway, because true things ruin people all the time, and no one tells you that when you're young." He turned his sword slowly across his knees, watching the firelight run along the fuller. "She's dead now. Has been for years. You can't ask forgiveness of the dead. So if the gods answered me — I'd ask them to tell her I'm sorry. Just to carry the message. That's all. A courier's errand." He almost smiled. "The god-emperor grants miracles and I'd spend mine on postage."

"That's not boring either," Wren said solemnly. "Vaskar. Your turn."

The dragon was silent for so long that Elodie thought he would refuse. His breathing was slow and vast and slightly wrong — she had noticed it for days now, a hitch in the rhythm, a catch like a bellows with a torn seam — and she kept her eyes on the fire because looking at him directly made the noticing worse.

"What would I wish," Vaskar said at last, and the sardonic drawl was gone from his voice, and what remained was older than kingdoms. "Little thief. I am the last. Do you understand what that word costs? When I was hatched there were nine hundred dragons between the ice and the southern sea. I knew their names. I knew their fires — every flame burns differently; my sister's was white and smelled of snowmelt; the old emperor's was green and killed the grass for a mile downwind. I have outlived every creature who remembers what my sister sounded like when she laughed, which dragons do, in their way, if you count thunder as laughter." The coal-light in his cracks guttered. "So if the gods answered me, I would not wish for my fire back. Fire is only breath. I would wish to be *followed*. One egg. One hatchling with my ember in it. To walk into the dark knowing the dark doesn't get everything." His eye closed. "But gods don't grant that. Gods take. Asking them for life is asking the flood for a drink."

Nobody spoke after that. The rain came down. The peat fire settled and flared.

"Elodie," Wren said.

And Elodie understood, in the moment before she answered, that this was the trap of the game — that Wren had built it sweetly and innocently and it was a trap nonetheless, because the sigil was humming ten feet away and the gods were starving and listening, and every wish spoken aloud tonight was a door left unlatched. She thought of Sera's drowned father who no longer spoke. She thought of Vaskar's torn bellows-breath, which he believed he was hiding. She thought of the storm, ten years ago, forty bodies, Maren's face across the trial hall.

She thought of what she wanted, honestly, in the secret room of herself where she kept it.

*I want it back,* said the secret. *I want the storm in my blood again. I want to feel the sky answer me. I want to be what I was before they cut it out.*

And underneath that, quieter, in a voice she trusted more: *I want this. The fire. The rain. These broken people. I want it to last.*

Both wishes were true. Only one of them was safe to say.

"Nothing," Elodie said.

Wren frowned. "You have to wish something. Everyone wished something."

"I've thought about it," Elodie said, and heard how smoothly the lie came, and hated how smoothly it came, and let it come anyway. "Ten years in the valley gives you time to think about it. I wished every night for the first year. Then every month. Then I stopped." She fed the fire a stick, watched it catch. "The gods answering prayers is how we got into this. Every answered prayer out there is a drowning village or a flooding valley or some poor soul who asked for rain and got an ocean. Wishing is how they get their hooks in you. I wish for nothing. Nothing is the safest wish there is. Nothing can't be twisted."

It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. Sera nodded slowly, approving — the priestess in her rising to meet the theology of it. Corvin grunted, something like respect crossing his scarred face. Vaskar's eye opened and fixed on her, gold and clouded and ancient, and stayed there a moment too long, and Elodie had the sudden vertiginous certainty that the dragon could smell the lie, that ten thousand years of listening to smaller creatures justify themselves had given him a nose for it.

But he said nothing. He only closed the eye again, and the look he'd given her settled into her chest like a splinter.

"Nothing," Wren repeated, disgusted. "That's cheating."

"It's strategy."

"It's *boring*."

"You said your parents coming back wasn't boring. Mine's the same kind of wish. It's just the negative space of yours." Elodie reached over and tugged the end of Wren's bootlace, undoing her neat knot, and Wren squawked and swatted at her, and the moment broke the way moments do around children — completely, mercifully, into bickering.

They talked a while longer after that, but the sharp part of the night was over. Sera showed Wren how to braid three strands of rush into a cord that would hold a wet knot. Corvin, drawn in despite himself by the sheer gravitational pull of Wren's attention, ended up demonstrating a wardens' rope-trick with his bootlaces until the girl demanded he teach her and he pretended to refuse and taught her anyway. Vaskar rumbled something that was probably a laugh, which set the marsh water ringing. The rain eased, just slightly, as though the sky had leaned in to listen and forgotten itself.

Later — much later, when Wren slept against Sera's good side and Corvin kept first watch with his sword across his knees and the fire burned down to a red eye of its own — Elodie lay awake under the lean-to and stared at the wet stars through the canvas gap.

*Nothing,* she had said. *I wish for nothing.*

The sigil hummed in the dark, patient as tide.

She reached out, very quietly, and rested two fingers against the oilcloth. The bronze was warm. Under the warmth, faint as a voice through a wall, she felt it — not words, nothing so clean. A pressure. An attention. The sensation of being *known* by something vast and hungry and terribly polite.

*We heard you,* the pressure said, without saying. *No one wishes for nothing.*

Elodie pulled her hand back and pressed it flat against her own sternum, over the place where her storm used to live, and told herself the hammering there was only her heart.

Out in the black water, far off toward the high road, a lantern swung — one light, then two, then a line of them, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who are searching rather than fleeing. Wardens' lights. Grayford was two days behind them; these were ahead.

Elodie sat up. Across the dead fire, Corvin was already on his feet, staring at the same lights, his hand closing around his sword.

"They found the road," he said quietly. "They're sweeping the marsh at first light."

And downstream, small in the reeds where she'd gone to fetch water before dawn, Wren was nowhere to be seen.
