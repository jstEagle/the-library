## 22. Maren Offers Terms

The girl was easier to take than Maren had expected, and that ease sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Wren had wandered from the marsh camp at dawn — children did that, even clever ones, even ones who had learned the hard way what the world did to unwatched children. She'd gone down to the tideline to gather shells, or so she would say later, defiant and unrepentant, chin up, arms crossed, as if she were the one conducting an interrogation. Maren's wardens had taken her without a sound. No hood, no bindings. Just four grown soldiers closing around a small figure on a grey beach, and one of them kneeling to say, gently, *you're not hurt, you're not in trouble, come with us now* — which was a lie, and everyone standing on that sand knew it was a lie, and they said it anyway because lies were kinder than the truth when the truth was *we are using you as bait*.

Maren watched from the dune line with her hands clasped behind her back and her face arranged into the flat patience that twenty years of service had carved into it.

She told herself: this is war. She told herself: the sigil will kill thousands if it reaches the Door. She told herself: one frightened child traded for a kingdom's safety is arithmetic any warden worth her oath would do.

The words went through her like water through a net.

Because she remembered being nine years old herself, standing in a doorway while soldiers argued over her father's fate in hushed voices meant to be overheard by no one, which of course meant they were overheard by everyone. She remembered the particular texture of being a child in the path of adults' terrible decisions — how the decisions never touched you directly, how you were only ever the *reason* for them, the fulcrum, the weight on one side of the scale. How nobody ever asked you.

So she walked down to where Wren stood between two wardens, sand crusted on her bare ankles, and she crouched to the girl's level, which cost her something in front of her own soldiers and she paid it anyway.

"You're Wren," she said.

"I know who I am." The girl's eyes were sharp and brown and utterly without fear, and that frightened Maren more than fear would have. Children who had already lost everything had nothing left to threaten them with. "You're the one who exiled Elodie."

The name landed like a slap Maren had been expecting for ten years and still hadn't braced for.

"Yes," Maren said.

"Are you sorry?"

Behind her, someone shifted. A warden cleared his throat. Maren held the girl's gaze and found, to her horror, that she could not produce the answer duty required — *I did what the realm demanded* — nor the honest one — *every day, in the hour before dawn* — so she gave neither.

"I'm here to make you an offer," she said instead, and hated the smoothness of her own voice. "Not you, exactly. Through you. You're going to carry a message to Elodie Eastwick, and then we're going to trade you back for something she has. Do you understand?"

"I'm not stupid."

"No," Maren said quietly. "You're not. That's what makes this ugly."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then, with the terrible practicality of children who have survived things no child should survive: "Elodie won't give you the sigil for me. She should. It's worth more than me. But she won't."

"She will," Maren said, and heard the certainty in her own voice, and understood suddenly that it wasn't certainty about Elodie at all. It was certainty about what Elodie had become out there in the Hollow Valley — someone who kept bees, who pulled children out of floods, who had spent ten years learning that power was a debt collected later with interest. "That's why I chose you."

"That's cruel," Wren said. Not accusing. Observing. Like noting weather.

"Yes," said Maren Voss, High Warden of Caelmere, and stood up, and her knees cracked, and she thought about her family's graves under a cairn in the Ashfell passes, and she thought about the whispering thing inside the bronze sigil locked in the iron box at her hip, and she thought: *the gods are dead or dying and their dying is getting into everything, into all of us, into the spaces where our certainties used to be.*

The sigil whispered to her at night now. She hadn't told anyone that.

It knew her family's names.

* * *

They sent Wren back at dusk, walking alone across the causeway at Grayford Bridge with a sealed letter in her fist and a warden escort that stopped precisely at the midpoint and withdrew, as agreed, as choreographed, every step of it designed to communicate one thing to the watchers on the far side: *we keep our word; test us and see.*

Maren waited at the bridge's center, alone, unarmed, her cloak beaded with sea-fog. This too was theater. She knew Elodie would read it as theater. But theater was its own language, and sometimes the truest things could only be said in it.

She saw them come through the marsh grass at the far end — three figures and, above the reeds, impossibly, the long dark shape of wings folded, a silhouette she had hoped never to see again outside of nightmares and old tapestries. The dragon. The last dragon, alive after all these years, moving like a ship under reduced sail. Even at this distance she could feel the heat coming off him, faint as memory.

And Elodie Eastwick walking in front of him.

Ten years had changed her. The trial had shown Maren a girl — nineteen, soaked to the bone, hair full of lightning-static, screaming her innocence in a voice that cracked on every word. The woman crossing the causeway now moved slowly, deliberately, hands visible, head high but not proud. There was grey coming in at her temples. There was a scar along her jaw Maren didn't remember giving her occasion to earn. And there was something else, something in the way she carried her shoulders, that Maren recognized with a lurch: it was the way Maren herself carried her shoulders. The posture of a person holding a door shut from the inside.

They stopped six paces apart. Protocol said twelve. Six was a choice, and both of them knew it was a choice.

"Maren."

"Elodie."

The fog dripped off the bridge rails. Somewhere below, the tide worried at the pilings. Behind Elodie, Sera Ivane stood with her hand resting on Wren's shoulder — the priestess looked better than reports had suggested, the burn-scarring faded to silver, though her eyes had the fixed quality of someone listening to a voice no one else could hear. Corvin Ashe was not among them. Maren had noted that absence with professional interest and personal dread. Corvin missing from his post meant Corvin had made a decision, and Corvin's decisions had always been the kind that rearranged the board.

"You got my letter," Maren said.

"Wren read it to us," Elodie said. "Twice. She wanted to make sure we understood every part of it." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "She's very thorough."

"She is."

"You used a child as a courier."

"I used a child as a reason," Maren said. "There's a difference, and we both know I'm lying, and we both know you'll forgive the lie because you need this meeting as much as I do."

Something flickered across Elodie's face — the old anger, banked but not dead, the coals of it glowing up through ten years of ash. Good, Maren thought. Anger was honest. Anger she could work with. It was forgiveness that terrified her, and Elodie's face was dangerously close to forgiveness, and Maren did not know what she would do if this woman forgave her. She had built her whole architecture of survival on the assumption that Elodie Eastwick hated her. Remove that pillar and everything above it came down.

"The terms are in the letter," Maren said aloud. "But terms on paper are cheap. So I'll say them here, where you can watch my face and decide for yourself whether I mean them."

"Say them."

"The sigil, delivered to my hand, tonight, freely given — not surrendered, not seized. Given. In exchange: the girl walks free, unharmed, permanently beyond my authority or the emperor's. And you—" Maren drew the second document from inside her coat, and held it up so the fog-light caught the wax seal, the imperial sunburst pressed deep in gold. "A full pardon. Signed by Aurelian Third-of-That-Name himself. Your exile revoked. Your title restored. Court Storm-Caller of Caelmere, reinstated with all honors, all privileges, all protections. Every crime struck from the record. Every year returned to you on paper, if not in flesh."

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, and Maren watched Elodie's hair move and thought, absurdly, of the storm. Of the night the sky came apart over Callowmere Bay. Of forty bodies laid out in rows on the quay because the emperor's new god demanded examples and the storm had provided them. Of sitting in the trial gallery knowing — *knowing*, in the place where knowledge lives before you let yourself look at it — that the lightning had not come from the girl on the stand.

Knowing, and testifying anyway.

Because the alternative was telling the truth, and the truth was that the gods themselves had convulsed, and the emperor's purges ate truth-tellers, and Maren had chosen to feed Elodie to the machine rather than feed her whole family to it. Exile to the Hollow Valley, where magic could not follow, where the emperor's inquisitors could not reach, where no storm-caller's blood could be harvested by a throne hungry for divinity. A cage. But a cage the fire couldn't enter.

She had never known if Elodie understood that. She had never dared find out.

"And the trap?" Elodie said now, softly. "Where's the trap, Maren? Because there's always a trap with you. There was a trap in your testimony. There was a trap in your mercy. You've never once done anything without a second door hidden in it."

Maren held her gaze.

"The trap," she said, "is that the pardon is real, and the freedom is real, and if you take them you will spend the rest of your life at court, wearing silk, calling storms for a man who wears divinity like a stolen coat — and you will never again be the woman who keeps bees. That's the trap. That's the only trap. I'm offering to give you back everything you lost, and everything you lost is a prison, and you're the only person in Caelmere who can see that clearly enough to refuse it properly."

Silence. The tide. A curlew crying somewhere out over the flats.

"You've changed," Elodie said at last, wonderingly, as if discovering it. "Or I have. Or we both—"

"Don't," Maren said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because hiding it had cost her ten years and bought her nothing. "Don't do the thing where you find the good in me. I can't bear it tonight. I took a child hostage today. Whatever I was protecting, whatever I was preventing, I put a nine-year-old on a cold beach and lied to her with gentleness. Weigh that against everything else you think you know about me, and then weigh the sigil, and then decide."

"Why do you want it?" Elodie asked. "Truly. Not the official answer. The one you'd say at three in the morning."

And there it was. The question Maren had been dreading, asked with the directness of someone who had spent a decade with nothing to do but think.

She could lie. She was good at lying; she'd had practice. But the sigil was warm against her hip through the iron box, warm the way a fever is warm, and it was whispering again — *Maren, Maren, we remember your mother's hands, we remember the song she sang in the pass, we can give them back, open the door, open the door* — and she was so tired, so profoundly tired, of carrying secrets shaped like graves.

"There's a door beneath the old capital," she said. "Under the drowned cathedral. My people found the maps. The gods are dying, Elodie — truly dying, starving, grasping — and they are answering prayers because answering is the last thing they know how to do. And the door is how they'll come back. All of them. The drowned god, the ember gods, the ones under the mountains. Everything the empire buried will stand up again, and the last time the old gods walked, my family died in a mountain pass defending a shrine nobody remembers, and I was nine, and I hid under my mother's body until the soldiers left."

The fog beaded on her eyelashes. She did not wipe it away.

"I want the sigil so I can bury the door so deep that nothing ever climbs out of it. That's the truth. That's the three-in-the-morning truth. Whether I'd still want that if the sigil hadn't started whispering my mother's name to me at night — I genuinely don't know anymore. That's also the truth. Take both or take neither."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. Then she turned and looked back — not at Maren, but at her people. At Sera, whose hand had tightened on Wren's shoulder. At Wren herself, who was staring at Maren with that same terrible unafraid directness. At the dark bulk of the dragon on the ridge behind them, his ember-glow dimming and brightening like slow breathing.

Maren watched Elodie's face do the arithmetic. Watched her arrive at the sum. Watched her understand — visibly, the understanding moving through her like weather — that the pardon was real, the trap was real, and that accepting the trade meant walking into the emperor's court with a target painted on her spine and a leash of gold around her throat, forever.

Watched her understand all of it.

Watched her choose anyway.

"All right," Elodie said.

Maren blinked. "All right?"

"The sigil for Wren. Freely given, as you said. Tonight, here, at the bridge's center." Elodie's voice was steady, and that steadiness frightened Maren more than screaming would have. "But I want it witnessed. By your soldiers and mine. And I want your oath, Maren — not the emperor's seal, your oath, sworn on whatever you still believe in — that the girl goes free and stays free, and that no warden, inquisitor, or imperial agent touches her or hers for as long as you draw breath."

"On my life," Maren said, and meant it, and felt the meaning settle into her bones like a splinter. "On my family's graves. On the pass at Ashfell. She is free the moment the sigil touches my hand."

"And the pardon?"

"Yours regardless. Take it or burn it. It's real either way. I don't offer paper I haven't verified."

Elodie nodded slowly. Then, and Maren would remember this for the rest of her life, she smiled — small, tired, and absolutely clear-eyed, the smile of a woman stepping off a cliff with her eyes wide open, having examined the drop and decided the fall was worth it.

"You should know," Elodie said, "that I see the whole shape of it. The court. The leash. The man on the throne who needs a storm-caller the way a fire needs air. I see that once I'm inside those walls, I'm yours — that the pardon is just a longer chain than exile was. I see that you might be lying about all of it. I see that you're probably not, which is somehow worse, because it means you've spent ten years becoming someone who does this kind of arithmetic too."

"I know," Maren said.

"And you're doing it anyway. Offering it anyway."

"Yes."

"Then we're the same person, Maren. That's the joke neither of us is laughing at. We've been the same person since the trial, and one of us has been paying for it ever since."

Elodie reached into her coat.

The bronze sigil came out into the fog-light, and the world *leaned*. Maren felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of her teeth, in the old break in her left wrist. Behind her, several wardens staggered. Out on the water, the tide flinched. The thing inside the metal woke and stretched and began, softly, joyfully, horribly, to sing — and it sang in her mother's voice, and in her father's, and in the voices of everyone Maren had ever failed to save, and she gripped the rail of Grayford Bridge and did not move and did not look away, because looking away was how it got in.

Elodie held it out across the six paces of wet stone.

"Take it," she said. "And give me the girl."

Maren stepped forward. One step. Two. Her boots rang on the bridge. She took the sigil — it was hot, fever-hot, singing-hot — and she pressed the pardon and the writ of manumission into Elodie's hands, and for one suspended moment they stood closer than they had stood in ten years, close enough that Maren could see the lightning-scar branching faint and white down the side of Elodie's neck, close enough to smell woodsmoke and honey and the sea.

"I'm sorry," Maren said. Barely a sound. "For the trial. For the testimony. For—"

"I know what you did," Elodie said quietly. "I've known for years. I just didn't know *why*." Her fingers closed around the papers. "Ask me again someday, when we're both older and stupider, and maybe I'll tell you if it worked."

Then Wren was running across the stone, and Elodie caught her up and swung her around once, and the sound the girl made — laughter, actual laughter, on this bridge, in this fog, in the middle of this ruin of a night — was the single most obscene noise Maren had ever heard, and she treasured it, and despised herself for treasuring it.

They withdrew into the marsh dark, all four of them, the dragon's glow sinking behind the reeds like a coal dropped in water.

Maren stood alone on Grayford Bridge with the sigil singing against her ribs, and opened the iron box, and put it in, and the singing did not stop, it never stopped anymore, and her hand shook, and she let it shake where no one could see.

*We remember,* it whispered. *We remember your mother's hands. Open the door.*

She had the sigil. She had won.

So why, walking back to her tent through the fog, did it feel exactly like losing?
