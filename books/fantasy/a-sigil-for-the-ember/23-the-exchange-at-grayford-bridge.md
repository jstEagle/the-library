## 23. The Exchange at Grayford Bridge

The fog came off the Grayford water like something breathing.

Elodie stood at the near end of the bridge with Wren's small hand pressed in hers, cold and damp and alive, and tried to remember the last time she had held anything so carefully. Ten years, maybe. Ten years since she had touched another person with the full attention of her whole self, the way you hold something you have already imagined losing. The river beneath them ran brown and slow with the season's rain, thick as broth, and the pilings wore their moss like old green coats, and the fog lay on everything so that the far bank was only a rumor — a gray suggestion of gray, where Maren Voss would appear, or she would not.

"You're squeezing too hard," Wren said.

Elodie loosened her grip. "Sorry."

"You're scared."

"I'm always scared. It's a hobby."

Wren snorted, but she didn't pull her hand away, and Elodie was grateful for that past the point of sense. Behind them, Sera stood at the foot of the bridge with the sigil wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and behind Sera, Vaskar's absence hung in the air like a held breath — the dragon had refused to come, had refused to be seen, had said only, *A bridge is too small a stage for me, little keeper. Go and be lied to with dignity.* His humor had been thin as frost. They had all pretended not to notice.

"You don't have to give it to her," Sera said for the fourth time. "We can run. There are roads in the marsh the wardens don't—"

"She has Wren's face in her memory," Elodie said. "And Wren has my face in hers. And Maren has never once, in all the years I've known her, made a threat she didn't keep. That's the whole of it. That's the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic is wrong."

"The arithmetic is *mine*." Elodie said it gently, and watched Sera's jaw set, and knew the priestess would obey — not because she agreed, but because she had made her own bargain with her god about what obedience cost, and she was not yet finished paying it. "When she gives Wren back, give her the sigil. Don't speak to her. Don't look at the pardon. Just hand it over and walk. Promise me."

Sera's eyes, pale as shallow water, held hers a moment too long. "I promise."

Elodie turned back to the fog. Somewhere in it, on the far bank, wardens were standing in their gray lacquered armor, and Maren was standing among them, and Corvin Ashe was standing somewhere too — she had felt him since morning, a pressure behind her ribs like weather about to turn, the old resonance of a man she had loved before the storm took that word and broke it into pieces. He had let them go in Ondrel. One hour. He had hated himself for it; she had seen the hatred in the set of his shoulders as clearly as she'd once seen the love. And now he was here, on Maren's orders, and Maren did not grant second mercies.

*One hour's head start,* she thought. *And now we spend it all on a bridge.*

The fog moved.

It didn't part — it *thinned*, the way a curtain thins when someone stands behind it, and then the shapes came through: six wardens in a line, then Maren alone in front of them, her gray cloak beaded with mist, her face the same face Elodie had loved across a childhood and hated across a decade and could not, even now, stop reading like a book she'd memorized. Maren's jaw was set. Maren's hands were empty. Maren's eyes went first to Wren, and softened — Elodie saw it, the flicker, the crack — and then to the oilcloth bundle in Sera's arms, and hardened into stone.

"You came," Maren said.

"You said you'd bring her back breathing," Elodie said. "I came to hold you to it."

"I keep my word."

"You kept it once already," Elodie said, and heard her own voice go quiet and terrible. "At my trial. You kept it so well I've spent ten years in a hole in the world."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — a flinch, quickly killed. "The pardon is signed. The emperor's own seal. You walk free, Eastwick. All of you. This is the only mercy you will ever be offered, and it is more than you deserve."

"Then let's not stand on deserving." Elodie lifted her chin toward the wardens. "Send her across. One of them, unarmed, walking Wren to the middle. Then the sigil. Then we're done."

Maren gestured, and one of the wardens — a broad woman with a scarred chin — stepped forward and held out a hand to Wren. Wren didn't move. Her fingers had gone rigid in Elodie's grip, and when Elodie looked down, the girl's face was the face she'd worn the night the wardens took her: not crying, not screaming, just *watching*, filing everything away the way children do when they've learned that the world takes things and you'd best remember what they looked like.

"Hey," Elodie said softly, crouching. "Look at me. You walk to that woman, you walk back to me, and then we go home and I make the terrible honey bread you hate. That's the whole errand. That's everything."

"You said the bridge might be a lie."

"It might be. But I'll be standing right here the whole time, and I have never yet let a lie take you." She kissed the girl's forehead, tasting fog and fear. "Walk."

Wren walked. Small boots on wet planks, one step, two, the scarred-chinned warden matching her pace with a strange, careful gentleness, and Elodie stood at the near end with her heart in her throat and her hands open and empty at her sides, and did not look at the fog, and did not look at the water, and did not let herself think about the storm sleeping in her blood, the one she had promised Vaskar she would not wake, the one she was saving, the one that was *owed* to her. Behind her, Sera unwrapped the sigil. The bronze caught no light at all in the fog, but Elodie felt it — a hum in her teeth, a struck-bell note that lived in the bones of the world — and saw Maren feel it too, saw the High Warden's breath catch, because Maren had been carrying the thing for days now, and the gods had been whispering to her, and the whispering had done its work. Maren's certainty was rotting from the inside. Elodie could smell it like rain coming.

Wren reached the middle of the bridge. The warden stopped. Wren stopped.

"Now the sigil," Maren called.

Sera stepped onto the planks.

And Elodie felt it happen — felt it the way she felt weather before it broke, the pressure drop, the wrongness in the air. Corvin. Corvin moving on the far bank, Corvin's hand going to his sword, and the resonance between them screaming a warning she couldn't parse fast enough, and then Maren's voice, low and clipped, giving an order that the fog carried across the water in fragments: "*...witnesses. No one crosses back. The emperor's word—*"

"No," Elodie said.

She said it quietly, and the river heard it, and the fog heard it, and every drop of moisture in the air between the two banks turned its attention toward her like a congregation turning toward a bell.

The scarred-chinned warden drew her blade. It was a small motion, almost gentle, the blade coming free of its sheath with a whisper, and it was pointed at Wren's back, and Elodie's whole body went white and weightless with a fury so total it felt like silence.

Then Corvin Ashe stepped out of the fog between them.

He had moved faster than a man in inquisitor's blacks should move, and he stood on the bridge now with his back to Wren and his sword drawn and pointed — not at the girl, not at Sera, not at Elodie — at the scarred-chinned warden. The fog curled around his shoulders. His face was gray as the morning.

"The emperor's word," he said, and his voice carried flat and clear across the water, "was to recover the sigil and burn what answers. It did not say to cut down a child on a bridge in front of witnesses."

"The emperor's word," the warden said, "is whatever High Warden Voss says it is."

"Yes," Corvin said. "I know. That's the problem."

What happened next happened in the space of a heartbeat, and Elodie would spend the rest of her life reassembling it from shards.

The warden lunged — not at Corvin, past him, at Wren — and Corvin turned his blade and caught hers with a sound like a struck anvil, and the bridge became a battlefield between two breaths. The other wardens came forward, five of them, blades out, boots hammering the wet planks, and Maren's voice cracked across it all — "*Ashe! Stand down! Ashe, that is a direct—*" — and Corvin did not stand down. Corvin fought.

She had seen him fight before, years ago, in the training yards of the capital, when they were both young and the world still had soft edges. He had been beautiful then, precise and quick. He was not beautiful now. He was *desperate*, all economy gone, spending himself like a man burning his own house for warmth, and he was good — gods, he was still good — but he was one against five, and Wren was screaming and running toward the near bank, and Sera had frozen on the planks with the sigil held out like an offering, and Elodie stood at the bridge's end with the storm in her blood straining against its leash and her own promise in her ears: *not yet, not here, save it, save it—*

A warden broke past Corvin. A woman, quick, blade low, going for Sera and the bronze. Elodie moved without deciding to move — three steps onto the bridge, her hand out, and the fog *answered*, a fist of cold wet air that caught the woman across the chest and threw her into the railing hard enough to crack it. Elodie felt the cost of it immediately, a cold hook behind her sternum, the valley's old lesson: *magic does not follow here, and where it half-follows, it takes twice.* But the warden was down, dazed, and Sera was running now, running with the sigil, and Corvin was bleeding — Elodie saw it, a dark line down his left arm where a blade had found him — and Maren was shouting orders that no one was following, and the bridge was chaos, and Wren reached the near bank and crashed into Elodie's legs and Elodie wrapped one arm around her and kept the other hand raised toward the fight because she did not trust the world not to need it.

"Drop it!" Maren roared at Corvin. "Drop the sword, Ashe, and I will swear to you on my family's graves that you live through this!"

Corvin laughed. It was the worst sound Elodie had ever heard from him — cracked and wild and grief-struck. "On your family's graves," he said. "That's where we all live now, Voss. That's where you've put us." He caught another blade on his, turned it, put his shoulder into the man and sent him off the bridge into the brown water. "I loved her," he said, and Elodie's blood went to ice, because she knew — she *knew* — he didn't mean her. He meant the man he'd been before the inquisition, the one who'd had a name no one used anymore. "I loved her and I burned a village for it, and I have been dead ever since, and today I am done being dead on your orders."

He took a blade across the back for that. Elodie saw it land — saw the warden behind him, saw the steel go in below the shoulder blade — and she heard Sera scream, and she heard Wren scream, and the storm in her blood rose up so fast and so hungry that the fog itself flinched, and she *held it*, gods help her, she held it, teeth bared, fists shaking, because Vaskar's voice was in her skull like a brand: *spend it at the door, little keeper, or not at all — waste it on a bridge and there will be no door.*

Corvin went down on one knee. Two wardens closed on him. Maren stood at the far end of the bridge with the fog boiling around her and her sword half-drawn and her face — Elodie would remember her face — her face was not triumph. It was horror, arriving late, at a thing she had ordered and not truly believed in.

"The sigil!" Maren's voice, ragged. "Someone get the sigil!"

Sera was three steps from the near bank. A warden lunged for her, and Elodie's fog-hand caught him too, threw him sprawling, and then Sera was past, stumbling onto the mud, and Elodie pulled them both back, back, off the bridge, Wren clutched to her side, Sera's arm in her grip, the sigil screaming its bronze note into the bones of the morning.

"Go," Elodie said. "Go, go, *go*—"

They ran. Marsh grass, cold mud, the drowned path south, Wren's hand in hers and Sera's breath tearing beside her, and at the last bend of the path Elodie broke her own rule and looked back.

The bridge was gray with figures. Corvin was on his feet somehow, swaying, his sword gone, his left arm hanging, blood black on the wet planks beneath him. The wardens ringed him but did not close — they were waiting, Elodie realized, for Maren's word, and Maren stood at the bridge's end with her hand still on her half-drawn hilt, and across all that fog and water her eyes found Elodie's and held them, and what was in them was not hatred. It was the look of a woman standing in the wreckage of her own certainty, watching it burn.

"Take him alive," Maren said at last, and her voice carried broken across the water. "He's a traitor. Traitors stand trial."

Corvin laughed again, that terrible cracked laugh, and spat blood into the river. "I'll save you the trouble, Voss."

He stepped backward off the bridge.

The river took him the way the river takes everything — without malice, without ceremony, a brown closing of brown water — and Elodie stood in the marsh mud with Wren's face pressed against her side and the sigil humming against Sera's chest and the storm still straining in her blood, unfed, unsaved, and she thought: *he let us go in Ondrel, and he let Wren live on this bridge, and now he's let the river have him, and I never once said thank you.*

The fog swallowed the bridge. Somewhere behind them, muffled by water and distance, Maren Voss was shouting orders — *drag it, search the banks, find the body* — and somewhere beneath the brown water, if the river gave him back at all, Corvin Ashe was sinking toward the silt with his defection paid in full.

Sera's hand found Elodie's shoulder. "Elodie. We have to move. They'll search the banks."

"I know."

"Elodie. *We have to move.*"

"I know." She turned. Wren was looking up at her, fog-beaded, white-lipped, alive, and Elodie crouched and took the girl's face in both hands and pressed her forehead to hers and said, "Terrible honey bread. I keep my promises too."

Wren made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, and Elodie stood, and they ran south into the marsh, and she did not look back again.

But that night, in the reeds, with Wren asleep against her side and Sera keeping watch, Elodie lay awake and felt the sigil's hum through twenty feet of marsh air, and felt the storm coiled in her blood like a debt come due, and thought about the look on Maren's face — not triumph, never triumph — and thought: *she has the bronze now. She has the whispering. And the gods are still dying, and still grasping, and still hungry for anyone who will listen.*

Whatever Maren Voss had been when she rode to Grayford Bridge, she was not that woman anymore. And the thing she was becoming was riding north with the sigil against her chest, toward the emperor, toward the Door — and Elodie had given it to her with her own eyes open.

She had traded the sigil for a child, and she would make the trade again a thousand times. But the storm was owed to her, and Vaskar was waiting, and the door at the bottom of the flooded cathedral would not stay shut forever.

"We go to the saltmarsh," she whispered to the dark, to Sera, to the sleeping girl against her ribs. "We go and we take it back."
