## 24. Prisoner of the Wardens

The sigil did not sleep.

Maren Voss had learned this on the first night, when she locked it in an iron strongbox beneath her quarters in Grayford garrison and lay awake listening to it hum through three feet of forged steel — a sound like a bell struck underwater, like a voice remembering how to speak. By the second night she had moved the box to the far end of the storeroom. By the fourth she had brought it back.

She told herself it was procedure. Evidence of a heretical artifact belonged under a High Warden's personal seal, not in some common vault where any curious sergeant might pry at the lid. She told herself she studied it because understanding a weapon was the first duty of the one who carried it. She told herself many things, in the flat command-voice she'd used for twenty years, and most of them were even true.

It was the whispering that made the lying necessary.

* * *

The sigil was bronze, no larger than her palm, cast in the shape of a door seen edge-on — a thin vertical seam flanked by two worn figures whose faces had been rubbed featureless by centuries of devout thumbs. It should have been inert. Bronze held no resonance; the imperial assay offices had proven that a hundred years ago when they stripped every relic of the old faith from every shrine in Caelmere and found nothing inside any of them but metal and memory. Magic answered to blood and storm and the emperor's borrowed divinity. Bronze answered to nothing.

This bronze answered to her.

She sat with it on the table before her, lamplight pooling around them both, and turned it slowly the way one turns a key in a lock that one has not yet decided to open. The seam down its center caught the light and gave something back that wasn't light — a warmth, a faint amber breathing, like coals under ash.

"You are evidence," she said aloud. Her voice sounded foolish in the small stone room. "You are contraband and you will be destroyed."

The sigil hummed.

Not threateningly. That was the horror of it. It hummed the way a mother hums over a cradle, patient, unhurried, certain that eventually the child will stop fighting sleep.

Maren closed her hand around it before she knew she had reached for it. The metal was warm as a living thing. And beneath her palm, faint as breath on a winter window, came the whisper.

*Little warden. Little mourner.*

She dropped it. It rang against the table and spun, and the seam glowed, and the glow shaped itself — briefly, unbearably — into a doorway. Not the abstract engraved seam but a real door, standing open in the middle of her quarters, and through it she saw a kitchen. A specific kitchen. Smoke-blackened beams. A pot of barley soup gone cold on the hob because someone had been called away and never come back. A wooden spoon with a chewed handle, because Tam had always gnawed his spoons, her little brother, eight years old and forever ruining kitchenware—

The vision folded shut like a fan. The sigil lay still on the table, warm, innocent, humming its lullaby.

Maren stood with her back against the wall and her hand pressed over her mouth and did not move for a long time.

* * *

Her family had died in the Godswar, the last year of it, when the old gods' armies swept through the river country burning everything that would not kneel. She had been fourteen. She remembered it the way soldiers remember — in fragments too sharp to be whole: the smell of the church burning, which was the same as any other fire except for the bells melting; her father's hands pushing her into the root cellar; Tam's voice going up the ladder after hers and then not following, because he had turned around, because he had forgotten something, because he was eight and eight-year-olds forget things —

She had stopped the memory there for thirty years. She kept it stopped now, the way one keeps a finger pressed on a wound.

And the sigil had opened the door to exactly that room. The kitchen before the fire. The kitchen with the soup and the spoons and the ordinary evening that had not yet ended.

That was the cruelty of it, she thought later, pacing the garrison walls under a sky bruised purple with coming rain. Not a threat. Never a threat. An offer. The old gods were dying — Sera Ivane had said so, the fugitive priestess, and the reports confirmed it: prayers answering themselves across the countryside, wells filling, crops blooming out of season, sick children waking healed while their mothers wept in terror. Dying things grasp. Dying gods grasped harder than anything, because they had farther to fall.

They were not offering her power. They were offering her the past. They were holding out a kitchen, warm and unburned, and saying *come back, come back, we can make it be morning again.*

"Tam is dead," she said to the empty rampart. "Twenty-nine years dead. I buried what they let me bury."

The wind off the moors said nothing. But somewhere below, in the strongbox, the bronze went on humming, and she could feel it through the soles of her boots, faint as a heartbeat through a floor.

* * *

In the morning she wrote her report to the god-emperor's chancellery. She was precise, as always. Artifact recovered at Grayford Bridge. Bearer escaped eastward with two accomplices and a child. One inquisitor, Corvin Ashe, turned traitor during the exchange; wounded, whereabouts unknown; recommend revocation of rank and sanction of pursuit. Recommend the artifact be conveyed to the capital under heavy guard for assay and destruction.

She read the recommendation back twice. Destruction. Yes. That was doctrine. That had been doctrine since the Ascension: what answers must be silenced, what remembers must be burned, what offers must be refused. She had enforced it across half a kingdom. She had burned shrines with congregations still kneeling inside them, and she had slept afterward, because the alternative — the world those prayers would drag back up out of the grave — was worse. She had seen the Godswar. She was one of perhaps four hundred people alive who had seen it as a child and understood what they were looking at. Certainty was not her comfort. Certainty was her duty.

She sealed the report and set it aside for the courier, and then she sat down again at the table, and looked at the strongbox, and did not send for the courier.

One more day of study, she told herself. One more day, to catalogue its properties for the assay office. They would want a full account. It would be negligent to send it forward undocumented.

Her handwriting, when she recorded the sigil's behaviors that afternoon, was perfectly steady. She noted the resonance frequency, the heat differential, the way it glowed in proximity to grief. She did not note that she had held it for eleven minutes that morning with her eyes closed. She did not note what it had shown her.

* * *

It showed her Tam again on the third night. This time he was older.

That was the thing that broke something loose in her chest and let the rot in. Because the first vision had been memory — the kitchen as it was, the boy as he was, eight years old and chewing his spoon. Memory she could dismiss. Memory was just the wound talking. But this Tam was eleven, gap-toothed, wearing a coat she had never seen because he had never lived to grow into it, and he was sitting at the table in her quarters — actually sitting, lamp shadow falling across him, feet not quite reaching the floor — and he was doing sums on slate, tongue between his teeth, and he looked up at her and said:

"You always were bad at sitting still, Ren."

She had not been called Ren since she was fourteen. Her father had called her that. No living person knew it.

"I'm dead," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You're dead. This isn't happening."

"Probably," Tam agreed comfortably, and went back to his sums. He was quiet a while, scratching numbers. Then: "It's nice here, though. Wherever here is. It's not cold." A pause. "It's a little boring."

Maren laughed. It came out of her cracked and awful, half sob, and the boy grinned at her with his gap teeth, and the lamp guttered, and he was gone, and the sigil lay on the table glowing softly, satisfied, patient as tide.

She did not put it in the strongbox that night. She left it on the table, within reach, the way one leaves a door ajar for a cat.

* * *

By the sixth day she had stopped writing in the log entirely.

She knew the signs. She had seen this sickness in others — inquisitors who lingered too long at suppressed shrines, wardens who pocketed relics "for evidence" and were found months later in rented rooms surrounded by candles, murmuring to things that murmured back. She had signed the orders for three of them herself. She had written, in her own neat hand, *corrupted by residual devotion; sentence carried out per doctrine,* and had felt nothing but the clean cold satisfaction of a wall holding against flood.

Now she was the floodwater, and she could feel the wall from the other side, and it frightened her how little she wanted to be on either side of it anymore.

Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid, the sum that sat on her chest at night heavier than the sigil ever had: the gods were dying. Truly dying, not retreating, not sleeping — guttering out like lamps in a draft, all across Caelmere, all across whatever waited behind the Door. And they were spending their last strength answering prayers. Filling wells. Healing children. Offering kitchens to grieving women in garrison rooms.

What kind of monster burned the last mercy of a dying thing?

What kind of monster refused?

She stood at her window on the seventh night and watched lightning walk the horizon toward the saltmarsh, silent and slow, storm-light without storm-sound, and she thought about Elodie Eastwick.

Ten years ago Maren had stood in a marble courtroom and testified, in a clear voice, that her best friend had called the storm that killed forty people at the harvest festival. Every word true. Every word a knife she had sharpened herself. And Elodie had looked at her from the dock — nineteen, soaked to the bone from the storm's aftermath, hair full of festival ash — and had not denied it, had not cursed her, had only looked at her with an expression Maren had spent ten years refusing to name, because naming it meant examining it, and examining it meant asking whether the person Elodie had looked at that day was a traitor or a savior.

The emperor's purges had come six weeks later. Every storm-caller in Caelmere above suspicion was taken to the capital for "instruction." None returned. If Elodie had not been exiled — publicly, irreversibly, cast beyond the wards where the emperor's reach and the purges both ended — she would have been on those wagons. Maren had known it. Maren had used the trial to save her life and had let her friend believe herself betrayed rather than tell her the truth, because the truth required trusting Elodie with a secret that could kill them both, and Maren had been nineteen too, and terrified, and certain that exile was survivable and the capital was not.

She had told herself for ten years that it was the right choice. She had built a career on the foundation of that certainty, stone by stone, promotion by promotion, execution by execution.

And now a piece of bronze the size of her palm was whispering through the stones.

*We know what you did,* it hummed, gentle as rain. *We know why. We knew then, little mourner. We were there, in the storm. We have been waiting a long time to say: you did well. You saved her. Come home. Bring your brother's name. We keep all the names.*

Maren slid down the wall to the floor and sat with her knees drawn up like a child, in her own quarters, in her own garrison, commander of four hundred men, and wept for the first time since she was fourteen years old.

* * *

On the ninth day, the courier arrived for the report she had never sent, and she met him at the gate with a new one — accurate in every particular except the final recommendation, which now read: *Artifact exhibits unprecedented resonance properties. Destruction may prove impossible. Recommend conveyance to the capital for study under the emperor's personal direction.* Which was technically true, if one understood that the study Maren intended was her own, conducted nightly, alone, with the door barred.

Which was also a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she signed it anyway, and felt the wall shift another inch.

That night the sigil showed her the kitchen again, and this time her father was in it, and this time she stayed forty minutes, and when she finally set the bronze down her fingers had to be pried open one at a time, stiff as an old woman's, unwilling.

She washed her face. She straightened her uniform. She took out a fresh sheet of paper and began, methodically, to plan.

Eastwick had the girl — the prayer-child, Wren, the one whose innocent wish for rain had flooded a valley. Eastwick would go to ground, lick wounds, resupply. And there was only one place in all of drowned Caelmere that a sigil-bearer and a priestess of the drowned god would ultimately turn toward, only one destination that explained everything: the Door. Beneath the flooded cathedral. Maren had read the old survey reports; she knew what lay under the saltmarsh waters, and she knew now — with the whispering certainty that was part knowledge and part infection — that the priestess had known all along.

Let them run to it. Let them open the way. Maren would follow with the imperial host, and she would take the threshold, and she would stand before the dying gods with the sigil in her hand and bargain as one ruler bargains with another.

She wanted so little, compared to emperors. One kitchen. One boy, grown or ungrown, it hardly mattered, alive at any age in any world. One morning that didn't end.

The gods, she was sure, would find that a fair price for what she offered in return: the whole armed weight of Caelmere, turned from their destruction to their defense. A warden's word. Her word, which had never once been broken.

Outside, the distant thunder rolled closer, and in the strongbox — no, on the table, she had stopped pretending — the sigil hummed its patient lullaby, and Maren Voss, incorruptible, unbroken, author of a hundred burn orders, fell asleep with her hand resting on warm bronze, and dreamed of soup.

Two days' ride south, she knew, a storm-caller was gathering her people for the marshes.

Maren saddled her horse in the dark and rode to meet her, carrying the door in her pocket.
