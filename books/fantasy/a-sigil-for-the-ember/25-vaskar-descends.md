## 25. Vaskar Descends

The sea had been telling him for three days that he was dying.

Vaskar heard it in the way the water spoke to his scales — not with reverence, as it once had, when dragons were young and the ocean still remembered being sky — but with a kind of impatient tenderness, the way one speaks to an old dog that has wandered too far from home. *Come in,* the water said. *Lie down. It is warm here at the bottom, and no one will ask anything of you again.*

He was floating on his back off the saltmarsh coast, wings half-spread to catch the wind that would carry him nowhere, watching the clouds do their slow, stupid work overhead. His fire sat low in his chest like a coal buried in ash. Once it had been a furnace. Once he could have boiled this whole bay to steam with a sneeze. Now he measured his strength in heartbeats, and he did not have many left to spend.

*Guttering early,* he had told himself, back in the valley, when the sigil's call first began to pull at him like a hook behind the sternum. He had concealed it from Elodie because that was what mentors did: they lied beautifully, and they died on schedule, and they made sure the lie held until the student no longer needed it.

Except she did need it. That was the problem. That was why he was here, forty miles from the valley he had guarded out of duty and boredom in equal measure, staring up at gulls who looked at him the way tax collectors look at a dying man's estate.

The warden stronghold crouched on the headland to the north — Grayford Keep, though the wardens had renamed it something imperial and joyless after the ascension. Vaskar could smell it from here: cold iron, horse sweat, ink, fear. And beneath all of it, threaded through the stone like a vein of ore, the sigil. Bronze and old prayer, humming its struck-bell note into the bones of the world.

Maren Voss had it. The friend. The betrayer. The protector, if Elodie ever learned the truth of it — which she would, eventually, because doors had a way of opening whether or not anyone stood ready in their thresholds.

Vaskar had considered, in the long drifting hours, simply letting events unfold. He was dying anyway. The girl was resourceful, the priestess was fanatical in the useful way, the child was feral in the more useful way, and even the inquisitor had shown cracks wide enough to drive a funeral procession through. Why should a dragon burn his last fuel on other people's war?

Because, he admitted to the clouds, of the lesson. The one he had been teaching her since she was nineteen and furious and exiled to his crater with nothing but bees and grief. *Spend the fire. Don't hoard it.* A hoarded flame dies in its own smoke. A spent one — well. A spent one at least leaves light behind, and warmth in someone else's hands, and a story worth the telling.

He had hoarded for four hundred years. He had watched the others go — proud Ithriax, who saved her fire for a war that never came, and died empty; patient Onduvael, who gave his away drop by drop to warm villages and was forgotten by them within a generation. Vaskar had judged them both, from his crater, from his careful conservation. And now, guttering early with the door's hunger drinking him through a straw of bronze and prayer, he understood that both of them had died better than he was about to.

Unless.

His nostrils flared. Far off, the stronghold's bell began to ring — not alarm, but change-of-watch, the lazy clanging of men who believed the sky belonged to the emperor. Vaskar smiled the way only old dragons smile, which is to say with too many teeth and genuine delight.

They believed the last dragon was asleep. They believed the last dragon was *in the valley*, half a kingdom away, dreaming out his final centuries. No one had told the wardens that the dragon followed the sigil's song the way a drowning man follows light.

*Well,* he thought, rolling over in the air with a groan of joints that had not fully extended in a decade. *No one has told the wardens a great many things. It is practically the imperial motto.*

---

He came down on them out of the sun, because there were traditions, and a dying dragon honors what traditions he can.

Grayford Keep had been built to withstand siege engines, sappers, and the petty ambitions of neighboring lords. It had not been built to withstand four hundred tons of grieving reptile arriving at terminal velocity with his jaws open and his opinion of imperial architecture already formed. The eastern tower went first — he took it in his shoulder, felt the stone burst against scales that had shrugged off ballistae when emperors were still arguing about whether gods were real — and then he was inside the courtyard, and the courtyard was screaming.

Fire, but carefully. Carefully. He was not here to slaughter; he was here to make noise, make ruin, make every eye and blade and bell in the fortress turn toward the sky. He melted the armory gate and breathed along the barracks roof, driving soldiers out into the open rather than onto them. He crushed the signal tower so no word could fly ahead of him. He put his forefoot through the warden-captain's command tent, mostly on principle.

*Buy them time.* That was the whole of the strategy, and it shamed him how little strategy it was. Somewhere south, Elodie Eastwick was doing whatever reckless, self-sacrificing, maddeningly Elodie thing she had decided to do, and every hour the wardens spent fighting a dragon was an hour they did not spend marching on her. He was a diversion. He was, he realized with a huff of smoke that might have been laughter, a *decoy*. Four centuries of majesty, spent as a burning scarecrow.

It was, all things considered, exactly the death he would have chosen.

"DRAGON!" someone bellowed, with the courage of the profoundly unobservant. "THE DRAGON IS AWAKE!"

*Awake,* Vaskar thought, pinning a ballista under one claw and folding its frame closed like a child's toy. *I am never asleep. I am merely selective about what I acknowledge.*

The wardens rallied faster than he'd hoped — Maren trained them well, whoever Maren had become; the girl he remembered had organized childhood games with the rigor of a military campaign. Grapple-lines sang up from the walls. Ballistae turned on greased pivots. And somewhere below, in the keep's heart, the sigil's hum changed pitch — rising, urgent, almost frightened, and beneath it another sound that made Vaskar's blood run colder than the sea ever had.

Whispering. The gods, whispering to whoever held the bronze. Offering things. He knew those voices. He had heard them through the Door, ages ago, before he sealed himself in the valley to be rid of them. They offered everything and paid in everything, and they never, never stopped asking.

*Hold on, Warden,* he thought toward the keep's depths, toward the woman clutching a god's door-knocker and hearing her dead family in it. *Whatever they're promising you, the price is worse. It is always worse. Take it from a creature who read the contract.*

A grapple-line caught his wing joint. Then another. Then six. The wardens swarmed the walls like industrious ants, hauling, and Vaskar felt the old arrogance rise — *you cannot hold a dragon with rope* — and met, head-on, the new reality of his body. His fire came slower now. Each breath cost him a season of life he no longer possessed. When he tore free of the lines, the motion pulled something deep in his chest loose, and he tasted copper and ember-smoke together, and knew.

*Ah,* he thought. *There it is.*

He had perhaps minutes. Perhaps fewer. The sensible move — the hoarder's move — was to climb, to crawl back to the sea, to die privately among the fish who had been so politely inviting him.

Instead, Vaskar did the thing he had spent ten years teaching a storm-caller to do.

He spent it.

The fire that came out of him then was not the controlled breath of a dragon defending territory. It was the whole banked furnace opened at once, four hundred years of careful rationing poured out in a single roaring minute that turned the eastern wall of Grayford Keep into falling glass and lit the noon sky orange from horizon to horizon. Wardens scattered. Horses screamed and broke their lines. The sea itself seemed to lean back from the shore.

And in the ringing silence after, Vaskar stood in the wreckage of the wall, swaying, and looked down at the tiny figure standing firm amid the smoke — a woman in warden-black with bronze in her fist, looking up at him with eyes that were not yet wholly her own.

"Maren Voss," he rumbled, and his voice cracked stones. "You are holding a conversation with dead people. I can hear them from here. They are lying."

She stared at him. For one moment — one naked, human moment — the whispering receded and she was just a tired woman who had lost everyone twice. "You're dying," she said. Not a question. Dragons cannot hide that particular truth, not at the end.

"I am *spending*," Vaskar corrected. "There is a difference. Learn it. You are younger than I am, and you have already begun to hoard your grief. Grief is fire too, little warden. Spend it before it spends you." He lowered his great head until his breath moved her hair. "And tell my student — when you see her, and you will see her — that the valley wards were never her cage. They were her chrysalis. Tell her the storm was never hers, and neither is the ending. But the choosing—" his voice ground low, almost gentle, "—the choosing is hers. It always was."

Then the ballistae fired, all of them, because soldiers are soldiers even in miracles, and Vaskar felt the bolts punch through the thin places where scale had worn to membrane, and he knew the arithmetic was finished.

He climbed. Of course he climbed. Dying dragons climb; it is the last dignity the world allows us. Up past the burning wall, up into the smoke and sunlight, wings beating in strokes that each cost him a memory — Ithriax laughing, Onduvael's patience, the first time a small furious storm-caller had stood at the rim of his crater and refused to be afraid of him —

*Good,* he thought, spending that one gladly. *Take them all. What are memories for, at the end, but kindling?*

The sea rose up to meet him with the tenderness it had promised. The water said, *come in, lie down, it is warm here at the bottom,* and Vaskar, falling, bleeding light instead of fire, finally answered it.

"Not yet," he said aloud, to the ocean, to the sky, to the small stubborn spark he could feel guttering in his chest and refusing — *refusing*, with a stubbornness that would have made him proud if pride were not such expensive fuel — to go out.

Because the ember was not done. The ember had somewhere to be. There was a girl in the world who had been taught by a dying dragon to spend everything, and she was going to need a flame to spend it *into*, and he would be damned — damned by gods, by emperors, by the cold patient sea itself — if he let that light go out before she came for it.

The water closed over his wings.

The last thing Vaskar saw, sinking through the green and fading gold, was the surface far above, rippled with firelight, and beyond it — faint, impossibly far, carried on some thread older than maps — the taste of a coming storm.

*There she is,* he thought, and let the sea take him down, still burning.
