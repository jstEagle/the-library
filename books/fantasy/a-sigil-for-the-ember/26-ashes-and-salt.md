## 26. Ashes and Salt

The sea gave back nothing.

Elodie had walked the tideline since before dawn, barefoot in the cold wash, watching the water for any sign — a scale, a glimmer of ember-light beneath the swell, the long dark shape of him breaching one last time to breathe. The sea gave back kelp and driftwood and the small indifferent bodies of fish, and nothing else.

Vaskar was gone. Not dead — she refused to let that word settle, not yet, not while her hands still shook — but gone down into the deep with wounds that would have killed anything lesser, holding the warden fleet's attention so that three fugitives and a stolen boat could slip out of Grayford Harbor under a sky full of smoke.

*Buy them time,* he'd said, in that voice like a landslide learning to speak gently. *I have been dying for four hundred years, little keeper. Let me do it usefully.*

She had screamed his name from the stern until Sera wrapped both arms around her and held on, and Wren had cried into Elodie's coat, and the boat had run south with the current while behind them the horizon burned orange where a dragon's fire met imperial war-galleys, and then the horizon stopped burning, and the silence after was worse than any sound she had ever heard.

Now it was evening of the second day, and the tide was going out, and Elodie Eastwick stood ankle-deep in salt water on a nameless shore and let herself finally, fully break.

It came quietly, which surprised her. She had expected screaming, the kind of grief that tore things — she was a storm-caller, after all; even exiled, even hollowed, grief in her had always wanted weather. But this was just a slow folding, knees first, then hands in the wet sand, then her forehead against the cold skin of the sea, and the sobs came out of her in long shuddering pulls like waves themselves, each one taking something and leaving something else behind: less hope, more certainty. Less girl. More stone.

Wren found her there. Of course Wren found her there; the child had a genius for finding people exactly when they least wished to be found and most needed it. She didn't say anything. She sat down in the sand beside Elodie, pulled her knees up, and put one small hand flat between Elodie's shoulder blades, the way you'd steady something that might tip over.

They stayed like that until the stars came out.

* * *

The cave Sera had found was an old smuggler's cache cut into the cliff above the tideline, dry-floored, half-choked with collapsed shelving and the ghosts of contraband long since carried off. It held them the way Vaskar's tunnels once had — badly, but without complaint. A driftwood fire burned low near the entrance, screened by rocks. Sera sat cross-legged before it, the bronze sigil across her knees, turning it over and over as though the answer to everything might be engraved on its underside if she only looked long enough. Corvin Ashe stood at the cave mouth with his arms folded, doing what he always did now: watching the dark for pursuit, hating himself in perfect silence.

Elodie came up from the beach with sand still clinging to her hem and salt drying white on her cheeks, and everyone looked at her, and no one spoke, because there was nothing left to say about Vaskar that hadn't been said in the boat, or on the road, or in the terrible quiet of the first night when Wren had asked *is he coming back* and no one had answered.

Except that Elodie was done with unanswered questions.

"Sit down," she said. "All of you. I'm done running."

Corvin turned from the cave mouth. Sera's hands went still around the sigil. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"We've spent two days grieving," Elodie said, "and we'll grieve more — I'm not pretending otherwise. He deserves years of it. Centuries. But every hour we sit in this hole licking our wounds, Maren is carrying that sigil toward the Door, and the emperor's host is marching behind her, and somewhere under the flooded cathedral a god is getting hungrier." She looked at Sera. "And your god knows where we are. Doesn't it? It's known since the saltmarsh."

Sera didn't flinch, which was its own confession. "It calls," she said quietly. "Every night louder. It wants me at the cathedral. It wants the sigil." Her thumb moved over the bronze. "It wants me."

"Then we stop letting it call the tune." Elodie crouched by the fire, close enough to feel its heat on her face, and made herself say the thing she had been building in her chest all day, wave by wave, on the empty beach. "Vaskar told me something, in the valley, when he was teaching me to hold flame that wasn't mine. He said: *spend the fire, don't hoard it.* A dragon who hoards dies on his pile with a cold heart. A storm-caller who hoards—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—ends up in a valley keeping bees for ten years, and look how well that protected anyone."

"Elodie." Corvin's voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on ice over deep water. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the Door needs a caller. A storm-blooded caller standing in the threshold, holding it open. That's why they dragged me out of exile — not for the sigil, not really. Sera could have given that to anyone. They needed *me*, because the gods' dying convulsion has to be channeled through living weather, and there are exactly two storm-callers left in Caelmere, and one of them is Maren Voss, and Maren doesn't know what she is yet." She saw Corvin go pale at that — he knew Maren, knew her history, maybe better than Elodie did — and pressed on before pity could soften the words. "So. If the door needs a storm-caller anyway — let it be me. On my terms. Not the drowned god's terms, not the emperor's, not Maren's. Mine."

"And what are your terms?" Sera asked. There was no challenge in it. There was something almost like awe, and something almost like fear, and Elodie understood that too: she was looking at a priestess watching a woman decide to become the thing her god feared.

"First," Elodie said, counting on her fingers like a market woman, because the ordinariness of it steadied her, "we don't open the Door for the gods. Whatever's behind it has been starving for centuries, and starving things that get fed don't say thank you. We open it — if we open it — to end this. To give the old gods their passage and take back the ember. Vaskar's ember. The Door burns with the same light; I saw it in the sigil's visions. If a dragon can be relit, then everything he was doesn't die with him in the sea." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "That's term one. His death buys something."

"Term two?"

"No one stands in that threshold alone. Not me, not Sera, not anyone. The god wants Sera alone at the cathedral because alone is how it eats people. Alone is how it ate the congregation — did you see them, in the vision? Kneeling in the flooded nave for two hundred years, still kneeling. That's what devotion looks like when there's no one beside you to pull you up." She looked around the circle of firelight: the burned priestess, the defected inquisitor, the orphan girl with storm-grey eyes far too old for her face. "We go in together or not at all. That's not negotiable. I lost one teacher to the water already. I'm not losing any of you to it."

Wren, curled against the cave wall, raised her hand like she was in a village schoolroom, and despite everything, despite the ash and the salt and the ache, Elodie felt her mouth twitch.

"Yes, Wren."

"What if the door needs someone to hold it open from *inside*?" the girl asked. "Like — what if that's the trick? What if it takes whoever opens it?"

The cave went very quiet. Fire popped. Somewhere outside, the tide sighed against the cliffs, patient as everything the sea ever did.

"Then," Elodie said slowly, "it will be very disappointed." And she meant it, and they all heard her mean it, and Wren nodded once, satisfied, and put her head back down against her pack like a child who has checked that the adults have thought of the monster under the bed and found it wanting.

* * *

Later, when Wren slept and Corvin took first watch at the cave mouth, Elodie sat beside Sera at the fire.

The priestess had finally stopped turning the sigil. It lay in her lap, catching the firelight along its grooves, humming — it never quite stopped humming, a note below hearing that Elodie felt in her teeth — and Sera stared into the flames with the expression of a woman reading a letter she already knows is bad news.

"Tell me what it's saying tonight," Elodie said.

Sera was silent a long moment. "It says the door is ready. It says the water remembers the way. It says—" her jaw tightened, "—it says my father is waiting behind it. My god wears my father's voice now. It learned that trick from watching me pray."

"That's not a god. That's a hook."

"I know." She said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "I have known since the bridge, maybe. Since before. Faith is strange, Elodie. You can watch the thing you worship rot from the inside and still love it, because what you loved was never the rot — it was what the rot grew in. The drowned god was real once. It answered prayers. It held back floods for fishing villages and taught us which storms to sail into and which to wait out. What's left is... hunger wearing its robes." She finally looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "You should know that when we reach the cathedral, it will try to take me. It will offer me everything and threaten me with everything, and I don't know — I genuinely do not know — which way I'll fall. I want to be honest with you about that, because you said no one goes in alone, and I am the likeliest person in this company to become the reason that promise breaks."

Elodie reached over and closed Sera's fingers around the sigil. "Then here's my term three," she said. "When it offers, you look at us. Not at it. At the fire, at Wren, at me. Whatever it shows you — your father, your ordination, absolution, drowning, all of it — you look away and find our faces. Can you do that?"

Sera's breath caught. For a moment she looked nineteen instead of forty, a novice on her first night in a chapel by the sea. "No one's ever asked me to fight it that way," she whispered. "We're taught to submit. Surrender is the whole liturgy. *The sea takes what it will.*"

"The sea," Elodie said, thinking of a dragon falling into it, of a valley that rain couldn't touch, of ten years of bees and honey and a life smaller than her heart, "has taken enough of mine. Come stand with me tomorrow, priestess, and we'll teach your god what it will."

* * *

She didn't sleep. Instead she climbed to the top of the cliff path and stood where the wind came off the open water, unbroken by any ward, unfiltered by any valley, and for the first time in ten years she deliberately opened herself to the weather.

Not calling. Just listening.

The wind was ordinary — a freshening sou'westerly, smelling of salt and distant rain, the kind of wind that meant nothing and blew everywhere. But underneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall, she felt the other thing. The pressure in the world's bones. The storm that had been gathering over Caelmere since the prayers started answering themselves, the accumulated weight of a hundred thousand answered wishes, the gods' dying convulsion still rolling through the land like the last echoes trapped in a struck bell. It was vast. It was everywhere. And it knew her — it had always known her, it had poured itself through her once already and torn a valley of lives apart doing it, and it waited now with the patience of weather for her to reach out again.

*I'm not afraid of you,* she told it.

It stirred, interested, hungry, and she felt her old power stir in answer, that terrifying gorgeous lift in her chest like the first breath after nearly drowning, and she understood — finally, completely — what Vaskar had spent his last months teaching her. The power wasn't hers and never had been. It was borrowed light, passed lightning, the gods' own storm looking for hands to live in. Hoarded, it destroyed. Spent — truly spent, all of it, held back by nothing —

— it could relight a dying ember. It could seal a door. It could buy a world one honest dawn.

"Tomorrow," she said aloud to the wind, to the sea, to the great dark pressure of the dying divine. "Tomorrow you come home through me. And I decide what you become."

The wind gusted once, hard, like something enormous exhaling, and Elodie Eastwick stood on the cliff with salt spray in her hair and ash in her heart and made her plans: the flooded cathedral, the drowned nave, the seam of ember-light beneath the altar. Three days' travel south along the ghost-roads. Maren ahead of them with the sigil — no, wait. No. The sigil hummed in Sera's lap below her; Maren had escaped Grayford with nothing but her life and her certainty. The sigil was here. Which meant the race was not for the Door but for the threshold — and the drowned god's voice had been calling Sera louder every night, and tonight, Elodie realized with a cold slide of dread, it had stopped calling.

It had stopped calling because it had stopped needing to.

Far to the south, past the dark line of the coast, a light was rising over the water where no light should be — green-white and deep as the sea floor, pulsing slow, like a bell tolling visible. Like a cathedral's windows lit from within by something that had decided, at last, to stop waiting.

Below her, in the cave, the sigil began to ring — really ring, high and clear and unmistakable, a struck bell with no hand upon it — and Sera's voice rose with it, not words, not prayer, something older, and Wren woke crying, and Corvin shouted for them all to move.

The god had opened its own doors. And it was inviting them home.
