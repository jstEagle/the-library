## 27. The Drowned God Speaks

The cathedral did not look drowned from the outside. That was the first cruelty of it.

Sera had imagined — all these years, all these miles of fugitive road — a ruin rising from black water like a drowning man's hand: broken spires, salt-scabbed stone, gulls nesting in the eye sockets of saints. What she found instead was a hill of green grass above the marshline, gentle as a sleeping animal, with a single bell-tower standing at its crown as if the flood had never come. The water was *inside*. It lived beneath the turf the way a fever lives beneath cool skin, and when Sera knelt and pressed her palm flat against the grass, she felt it: the whole nave below, flooded to the rafters, its bells hanging silent in green-dark water, its congregation still kneeling where death had caught them mid-prayer.

Her god's house had not been abandoned. It had been swallowed whole and kept.

"Is this it?" Wren asked behind her. The girl stood at the edge of the hill with the sigil wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, holding it the way children hold things they have been told are precious and dangerous in equal measure. "It doesn't look like anything."

"It looks like nothing," Sera said. "That's how he liked his churches built. My order believed a god should be felt, not seen. We buried our holiest places under ordinary ground so that pilgrims would have to kneel blind." She almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth remembered the shape of it but could not complete it anymore; there had been too much kneeling since Caelmere burned. "We were very proud of that. I used to think it was humility."

"And now?"

"Now I think we were hiding him from himself."

Elodie came up the hill last, the way she always did — watching the horizon first, then the flanks, then finally the place they were actually going. Ten years of exile had built that habit into her bones. Even here, at the edge of everything, she walked like a woman who expected the world to owe her an ambush.

She stopped beside Sera and looked down at the grassy crown of the drowned cathedral for a long moment without speaking. Wind moved through the saltmarsh below them, combing the reeds flat in long silver waves, and somewhere out in that gray expanse a curlew cried twice and fell silent, as if it too had been told to hush.

"I can feel it," Elodie said quietly. "Under the ground. Like standing on the chest of something breathing very slowly." Her eyes flicked sideways. "Sera. Before we go down. Tell me again what he said to you. Exactly."

*He says come alone,* Sera thought. *He has said it every night since the bridge. He says bring my sigil. He says bring my priestess. He says the rest of you are weather.*

"He says the Door is beneath the altar," she said instead, and heard the smoothness of the lie and hated herself for how easily it slid out now, how worn smooth the channel of it had become from ten years of small obediences. "He says the sigil will open it. And he says—" she made herself breathe, made her voice steady, "—he says the door swings both ways. That what comes through will need someone on this side to hold it open. A priestess. An anchor of faith."

Elodie looked at her for a long time. Storm-gray eyes, older than thirty years should have made them. Sera had watched this woman save a drowning child with hands that remembered how to call hurricanes, had watched her choose bees over thunder day after day for weeks on the road, and somewhere in all that watching Sera had made a decision she had not let herself say aloud, because saying it would make it real, and making it real would give her time to flinch.

"You've known him longer than any of us," Elodie said at last. "You trust him?"

Sera looked out over the marsh. The light was going long and gold, the way light went before storms, though no storm was coming — she checked, the way she always checked now, the little weather-sense Elodie had been teaching them all around the campfire like a grandmother passing on knitting patterns. Clear skies. Clean air. Nothing in the world wrong except everything underneath it.

"I gave him my life when I was sixteen," Sera said. "Ordination isn't a metaphor, whatever the rural temples teach. You lie in the baptismal pool and you let the water take a piece of you, and the piece it takes is yours forever — it doesn't come back if you leave the order. It doesn't come back even if the order burns." She touched the faded brand at her collarbone, hidden all these months under wool and dirt. "I trusted him then because trusting was the whole of what I had to give. I trust him now because—"

Because he is dying. Because he is hungry. Because he has been whispering to me in the dark for three hundred leagues and every word has sounded less like my father-god and more like something wearing my father-god's voice from the inside.

"—because someone should," she finished. "Faith that only exists when it's safe isn't faith. It's arithmetic."

Elodie held her gaze one heartbeat longer. Then she nodded, slow, and turned back toward the others, and Sera stood alone on the crown of the drowned church with her heart hammering like a fist against a locked door, knowing exactly which of them was lying, and to whom.

* * *

They made camp at the base of the hill, because Elodie insisted — *we go down rested or not at all* — and because Vaskar needed the night.

The dragon had come south ahead of them, alone, and met them at the marsh two days past, and seeing him had been like seeing a hearth-fire in a house where someone has died: warmth, and grief, and the knowledge that both were temporary. He was smaller than he had been in the valley. Sera hadn't wanted to say it. Everyone had seen it anyway. His scales had gone dull along the neck, the ember-light beneath them flickering at intervals like a lamp running out of oil, and when he spoke there were pauses now, small gaps where thoughts used to flow, as if parts of him were being drawn away somewhere faster than the rest could follow.

*The Door pulls,* he had said, by way of explanation, settling into the hollow between two dunes with the care of an old dog arranging itself. *Everything that opens it must be paid from something. The gods have been paying from me.*

Now he lay half-buried in the dune-shadow while the camp went about its small rituals around him — Corvin checking blade-edges with the grim thoroughness of a man doing penance in whetstone, Maren sitting apart with her wrists still bound from Grayford Bridge, staring into the fire as though it were a text she was translating. Wren fed Vaskar dried fish one piece at a time, solemn as sacrament, and the great head dipped and took each morsel with a gentleness that would have made the emperor's propagandists weep at the waste of a good monster.

Sera walked the perimeter. It was what she did when she could not pray — walking prayers, her novicemistress had called them, disapprovingly, *God hears you just as well kneeling, child* — and tonight the walking prayer kept circling back to the same petition, worn smooth as a river stone:

*Let me be enough. Let me be the whole price. Let none of them be spent.*

She stopped at the tideline, where the marsh gave way to the gray sea, and the sea looked back at her with the patience of something very old and very empty, and she understood, finally, standing there in the last light, that she had been praying to the wrong thing. Or rather: that she had been praying *to* the thing she ought to have been praying *for protection from*, and the distinction had rotted away so gradually that she could no longer find the seam.

The water at her feet went still.

Not calm. Still. Every wavelet within twenty paces simply ceased, as if the sea had been paused mid-sentence, and the silence that followed had texture to it — a pressure, a leaning-in, the acoustic hush of a cathedral full of people turning their heads at once.

"Oh," Sera said softly. "You're already here."

*Here,* said the drowned god, and the word did not arrive through her ears. It rose up through the wet sand into the soles of her feet, through the bones of her legs, resonating in her chest cavity like a note struck on a bell she was standing inside of. *I have always been here. This is my house. You are standing on my doorstep, little priestess, and you have made me wait three hundred leagues and one burned bridge and one stolen girl, and I am* —

The pause stretched. In it, Sera felt something vast turn its attention fully upon her, and it was like being seen by the ocean during a shipwreck: total, indifferent, and intimately concerned with the precise manner of her sinking.

*hungry.*

"You're not him," Sera whispered. "You're not — the god I ordained to. He was — there were hymns. There was mercy in the hymns. *The sea takes back what it loves.*"

*The hymns were written by men who survived me,* the god said, and there was something almost amused in it, the way a famine might be amused by a full granary. *I am the taking-back, child. The hymns were the part after. You have been praying your whole life to the pause between waves.*

And Sera stood very still on the dead-still waterline and felt the truth of it settle into her like silt, and the terrible thing — the thing she would carry for the rest of however-long-she-had — was that it did not feel like betrayal. It felt like recognition. All those years of ordination, of vigil, of the pool closing over her face while elders chanted, she had never once been able to picture her god's face. Other novices saw lights, shapes, a bearded figure in foam. Sera had seen only depth. Only the feeling of looking down off a boat's edge into water too dark to end.

She had thought it was humility. She had thought it was mystery.

It had simply been accuracy.

"Why answer the prayers?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "If you're — what you are. Why answer them at all? The rain for the farmer's daughter. The bread in Wren's village. All of it. Gods don't feed people out of hunger."

*No,* the god agreed, and now the water at her ankles began to move again, but wrong — circling, drawing inward, a slow spiral centered on her. *But a dying thing grasps. Your Elodie's storm, ten years ago — do you know what that was? Not wrath. Not punishment. A convulsion. We are a body failing, priestess, and a failing body thrashes, and everything the thrashing touches, it touches hard. The prayers across Caelmere answered themselves because we are falling apart, and the pieces fall* toward *things. Toward need. Toward belief. Toward the warm bright places where mortals are still foolish enough to want us.*

The spiral tightened. Cold climbed her shins.

"And the sigil," Sera said. "What is it, really? What did my order make it for?"

Silence, of the textured kind. Then, gently — and the gentleness was the worst thing yet, the tenderness of a trap already sprung, already forgotten by the trapper:

*Come inside, Sera Ivane. Come down into my house. Some truths are too heavy to say on a doorstep.*

* * *

She told them at the fire that night, because she had promised herself she would — because whatever else she had become, whatever channels obedience had worn through her, she would not walk down into that water carrying one more lie among people who had bled for her.

She told them most of it. The god was not her god, or was her god the way a wolf is the ancestor of a lapdog — same blood, everything kind bred out of memory. The Door was real. The altar was real. And the god wanted the sigil, and the god wanted her, and those two wants were the same want, and it was not a want that ended in restoration or return or the old world reborn. It was a want that ended in a meal.

"The visions," she said, staring into the fire because she could not look at any of their faces. "In chapter four of the Ordinal — the visions the sigil gives. Burning Caelmere. Starving gods. I thought they were warnings. *This is what happens if the door stays shut.* That's what I was taught. That opening it was mercy." Her laugh came out cracked. "I don't think they were warnings. I think they were *advertisements*. A starving thing showing you the feast it wants you to believe in."

Corvin shifted, leather creaking. "Then we bury it. Drop it in the deepest trench in the sea and put a dragon on top of it."

"You can't bury it." Vaskar's voice rolled out of the dark beyond the firelight, low and grinding, and everyone went still, because the dragon had spoken maybe six times since the marsh. "I told you. Everything about that door gets paid from somewhere. Burying it doesn't cancel the debt. It just defers the collector." A pause, long, guttering. Sera saw the ember-flicker beneath his jaw stutter and resume. "And the collector has been deferring payment to *me*. Which means the debt is coming due soon regardless. One way or another, that door opens, or the pull tears the last of the old gods loose on its own — unkeyed, unheld, unspent *well*."

"So we spend it well," Elodie said.

Everyone turned to her. She sat with her arms around her knees, firelight moving on her face, and Sera watched her and understood that Elodie had already walked further down this road than any of them, had perhaps been walking it since the valley, and that the woman who chose bees over thunder ten years ago was not the woman sitting here now. Grief had done what power never could.

"Not open," Elodie said. "Not the way it wants. But that door needs a caller, and a caller needs a storm, and there is exactly one storm-caller left in the world who owes the old gods everything and trusts them for nothing." She looked at Sera. "Tomorrow. At dawn. We go down together."

"*Together*," Sera repeated, and the word broke against the truth she was holding behind her teeth like a stone behind a dam.

"He'll refuse you," she said. "He told me — alone. Priestess and sigil. No one else crosses the threshold."

"Then he can tell me himself," Elodie said, "in his own house."

And Sera sat by the fire with her family — because that was what they were, whatever happened tomorrow, they had become that without anyone signing anything — and smiled, and agreed, and planned the descent with them detail by detail, and all the while behind her ribs the drowned god murmured like tide in a cave, patient, certain, and she understood that she was going to break this promise the way she had broken every promise lately: not out of treachery, but out of love, which was so much harder to forgive.

*Alone,* the god whispered, in the space between the fire-crackle and the surf. *You know the shape of the price, little priestess. You have known since the bridge. Say yes.*

And Sera Ivane, last priestess of the drowned god, lying to the people she loved best in the world by lamplight, closed her eyes and said yes.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless over the saltmarsh, the sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the party stood at the crown of the hill while Elodie marked the entrance — a hairline seam in the turf that only showed when the light hit it sideways, running in a perfect ring around the bell-tower's base, a door laid flat beneath the world.

Wren hugged the sigil to her chest and refused, flatly, absolutely, to give it to anyone but Sera. "You're the one it hums for," the girl said, chin up, eyes fierce and red-rimmed. "And you come back up. Both of you. Swear it."

"Sworn," Sera said, and kissed the top of her head, and memorized the weight of her, and lied.

The seam opened at Elodie's touch with a sound like a held breath released after ten years. Stairs spiraled down into green-black water — and the water did not rush in, did not drown them; it parted from the steps as though remembering an old courtesy, leaving a corridor of air that breathed cold and briny and ancient. Far below, impossibly, faintly, bells began to ring. Underwater bells, deep and slow, tolling through stone and flood alike.

The dead congregation was still kneeling in the nave below. Sera could feel them the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

She went first, as a priestess should. Elodie beside her, storm-gray eyes fixed downward. Behind them, Wren's small hand gripping Corvin's sleeve, Corvin's blade drawn despite everything, Maren bound and silent and watching with eyes that had begun, horribly, to listen.

Halfway down, where the light from above failed and the light from below began — a vast, sourceless, underwater luminescence rising to meet them like dawn in reverse — Sera stopped on the stairs.

"There's something I have to tell you," she said, and her voice echoed strangely, doubled, as though the water itself were repeating it. "About the price."

And far below, in the flooded dark, the bells stopped ringing all at once — and in the silence that followed, something immense turned over beneath the altar, and began to rise.

*Say yes,* the drowned god whispered, through her own mouth, through her own voice, and Sera felt it leave her lips like water leaving a broken vessel. *Yes.*
