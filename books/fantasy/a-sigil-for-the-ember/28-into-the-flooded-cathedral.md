## 28. Into the Flooded Cathedral

The sea had taken the cathedral the way grief takes a house: slowly at first, then all at once, and then it would not give it back.

Elodie stood at the waterline where the saltmarsh gave way to black shallows and looked down through forty feet of green-grey water at the roof of a church that should not have existed. The drowned god's seat. Sera's whole life, sunk beneath the tide like a coin dropped from a drowning man's hand. From above, it was only a shadow with a spine — the ridge of the nave, the broken crown of the bell tower leaning like a drunk against the dark. But as the sun slid behind the marsh grasses and the light went long and low across the water, Elodie saw what she had been refusing to see since the cartographer's daughter first unrolled her father's map on the table of a safehouse that smelled of lamp oil and old fear.

There was light down there.

Not the pale scatter of reflected sky. Not the phosphorescent wink of marsh-fish. A warm, steady gold, seeping up through seams in the sunken stone like heat through a banked stove, and it moved — breathed, almost — slow as a sleeper's chest.

"The Door," Sera said beside her. Her voice was flat in the way voices go flat when feeling would otherwise drown them. "It's already open a crack."

"Then we close it," Wren said.

Elodie looked down at the girl. Wren stood hip-deep in the shallows with her boots tied around her neck by their laces, her small arms crossed, her chin set at the angle she used when she had decided something was hers to do. The sigil hung against her chest on its cord of braided hair and river-reed, and even now, even here, it hummed. Elodie could feel it through the soles of her bare feet where they pressed into the cold silt — a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing, traveling up through the bones of the world into the bones of her.

*We close it,* she thought. *Yes. That is the story I will tell myself while I walk down into the mouth of it.*

Corvin crouched at the water's edge upstream of them, where a freshwater spring bled out of the marsh and made a lens of clear water over the salt. He was testing the current with two fingers, frowning the way he frowned at everything, as though the ocean were a suspect who might yet confess to something useful. His left arm was still bound from Grayford Bridge, from the moment he had turned his blade on his own brothers-in-ash rather than let them cut down witnesses, and he had not spoken of it since, and none of them had asked. Some debts you let a man carry quietly or he puts them down and runs.

"There's air," he said. "Pockets of it. Under the vaulting, along the clerestory. Cathedrals are built upside-down boats, more or less. If the stone held, there'll be rooms the flood never filled."

"And if it didn't hold," Elodie said, "we'll find out quickly."

"That's the spirit." He did not smile when he said it. Corvin Ashe had stopped smiling somewhere between the market in Ondrel and the bridge at Grayford, and Elodie suspected the man who came out the other side of that distance was still being assembled, piece by piece, from whatever he had left.

Sera waded out until the water took her at the waist. She turned back, and for a moment the last light caught her face — half-burned still, the old scarring from her crossing of the valley ward laid over new exhaustion, and beneath both of them something Elodie had learned to read in ten years of watching bees read weather: a stillness that was not peace.

"When we're under," Sera said, "you may hear things. Singing, mostly. Bells. My order rang the hours for six hundred years; the water remembers the schedule better than I do." She paused. "And you may see people. Kneelers. Do not speak to them. Do not answer them if they speak to you. They are not ghosts exactly. Ghosts want something. These have already been given everything they asked for."

"What does that mean?" Wren asked.

"It means," Sera said, "that my god answers prayers too."

She said it lightly, the way you'd say *mind the third step*, and then she filled her lungs, folded her hands around the bronze cross of her drowned office, and slipped beneath the surface without another splash, going down into the gold-lit dark like a needle finding its old scar.

* * *

They went down in the bell tower, because the tower's throat was open to the sky and because Sera knew the way — of course she knew the way; she had been born in this building's shadow, ordained in its choir, and had known its location all along, a betrayal confessed before it happened and forgiven, mostly, in the nights after. Elodie went second, because Wren refused to go anywhere but directly behind her, and Corvin came last, because someone had to be last, and he had decided — silently, in the manner of men doing penance with their bodies — that the position was his.

The water closed over Elodie's head and the world changed its mind about everything.

Cold first, cold like a hand clapped over the whole body, and then the strangeness: sound arriving wrong, huge and soft, her own heartbeat suddenly a drum in a cathedral — which, she supposed with a bubble of near-hysterical laughter, it literally was. Light came down the tower shaft in wavering columns, and the gold from below came up to meet it, and where they mingled the water turned the color of honey held to a window.

Wren's hand found hers in the murk. Small fingers, cold, gripping hard enough to hurt, and Elodie gripped back and did not let the hurt show, because the hurt was the proof, and the proof was the thing keeping them both sane.

They breathed from the prayer.

That was the part Elodie could not stop turning over in her mind, all the way down, past the first gallery, past the rotted scaffolding where swallows had once nested and now something pale and blind drifted instead. On the marsh road, three days ago, Wren had prayed — the small automatic prayer of a child raised on the old gods' names, the kind of prayer no one expects anything from anymore, *please let us breathe where there is no breath* — and the dying god below, grasping at every voice that called it, had answered. Had wrapped each of them, gently, horribly, in a skin of held air, a membrane of pressure that rode their bodies like a second self and fed them, breath by borrowed breath, from some reserve Elodie did not want to imagine the source of.

She could feel it now, if she paid attention: a faint coolness at her lips and nose, an invisible bell-jar. When she exhaled, silver threads of air escaped and did not rise. They hung around her shoulders like a veil, then thinned, then were gone — spent, given to the water, which accepted the gift the way the sea accepts everything.

*It is answering us,* she thought. *Every breath is a prayer being answered.* And beneath the thought, the older hunger stirred and rolled over in its sleep, and Elodie put her hand flat against the tower wall and held on to the cold stone until it passed.

They descended through the belfry.

The bells were still there. Of course they were — bronze does not rot — but they hung in their great oak headstock gone black with ten years of brine, and they were ringing. Not swinging. There was no wind down here, no hand, no rope. But as Elodie passed through the chamber, the nearest bell shivered, and a single note came out of it, round and enormous, moving through the water and through her body and through the stone itself, and she felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her teeth, in the marrow behind them.

One note. Then silence, thick as wool.

Then, far below, a second bell answered it.

"They're counting the hours," Sera's voice said, arriving impossibly clear through the water, though her lips barely moved — the god carrying her words the way it carried their air, spending itself freely, lavishly, terribly. "Vespers soon. It always rings vespers. Six hundred years, and the tide has come in, and it still rings vespers."

*For whom?* Elodie wanted to ask. She kept the question behind her teeth, because she already knew, and knowing was bad enough.

* * *

The nave opened beneath them like the inside of a lung.

They came out of the tower door into the upper dark of the great space, and Elodie stopped swimming — stopped moving entirely — because the sight took the motion out of her the way a blow takes the breath.

The cathedral lay flooded to the height of its clerestory windows, and the last daylight came through those high windows in long tilted blades, and in those blades the water was full of drifting motes — silt, scale, the fine ash of ten drowned years — turning slowly, all together, like snow falling upward in a glass globe. Below, the floor of the nave spread out vast and pale, and the pillars rose from it in ranks, and the arches sprang from pillar to pillar in stone geometry too large and too patient for human grief, and at the far end —

At the far end, the altar glowed.

The Door. Even from here, even across the whole length of the drowned nave, Elodie could see it: a seam of ember-light in the stone behind and beneath the altar, thin as a knife-cut, burning the color of a dragon's heart. It threw no shadows. It made shadows — everything in the nave leaned away from it, pillar and pew and kneeling figure alike, cast outward in long radiating lines like the spokes of a wheel whose hub was fire.

And the figures. The kneelers.

They filled the nave. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, arranged in the ordered rows of a congregation at the holiest hour, and they knelt on the flooded stone with their heads bowed and their hands folded, and they did not move. Their clothes had gone the grey-green of deep water. Their hair moved gently in a current Elodie could not feel, all together, all in the same slow rhythm, like a field of kelp, like one creature wearing ten thousand shapes. Some had been kneeling so long that the silt had built up around them in soft drifts, burying their feet, their knees, the folds of their robes, so that they seemed not placed upon the floor but grown out of it — stone flowers, coral statues, a garden of the faithful.

"Breathe," Corvin murmured at her shoulder, and she realized she had stopped.

"I'm breathing."

"You've taken four breaths in a minute. I counted. Counting is most of what I'm good for now." His voice was steady but his eyes weren't; they tracked the rows of kneelers the way a soldier tracks a treeline, cataloguing exits, counting enemies, and Elodie understood that to Corvin this was a battlefield full of hostiles who merely happened to be praying. Perhaps that was the sanest available reading.

"They're dead," Wren whispered. Her borrowed air carried the words to all of them. "Aren't they? They're all dead."

"Drowned at vespers," Sera said. She had gone ahead a little, drifting down the central aisle with her priest's ease, at home in a way that made Elodie's chest ache. "When the god-emperor broke the seawalls and erased the city, my order stayed at the offices. All of them. The abbot said—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "The abbot said a shepherd doesn't leave the flock because the wolf is coming. The wolf was the emperor. The flock was nine hundred souls. The shepherd was a fool, and holy, and I loved him, and they all drowned on their knees mid-prayer, and my god caught their prayers in its hands as they died, and it has been answering them ever since."

"Answering them how?" Elodie asked. "What did they pray for?"

Sera turned in the water. The ember-light from the far end lit half her face, and the other half was deep-water dark.

"To stay," she said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. *Let us stay.* Every one of them, in the last moment, prayed to stay — in the church, in the light, in the presence. And my god, who was already starving, who was already beginning to grasp—" Her hands opened, helpless. "It kept them. It holds them. Nine hundred prayers, answered perfectly, forever. This is what an answered prayer looks like, Elodie. I need you to understand that before we reach the altar. Whatever it offers you down there — however beautiful the shape of it — look at the pews. Look at what yes costs."

The bells rang again — nearer now, all of them, a peal rolling through the flooded stone, and the sound was so vast and so mournful that Elodie felt tears slide hot and immediate into the cold water around her eyes, indistinguishable from the sea.

Vespers. The hour of evening prayer. The lights going out one by one, and the faithful gathering anyway.

* * *

They went down the aisle.

There was no other way; the Door was at the altar, and the altar was at the end of the nave, and the nave belonged to the kneeling dead. Swimming low, close above their bowed heads, Elodie saw them truly for the first time and wished she hadn't. Their faces were peaceful. That was the horror of it — not decay, not terror, but peace, the specific unbearable peace of people who got exactly what they wanted. An old man with his lips still moving around a word that had ended ten years ago. A young woman with a baby wrapped against her chest, both of them sleeping the same sleep. Two boys, brothers by the set of their chins, kneeling side by side, and between their folded hands, preserved somehow, a single dried flower that had never been allowed to finish crumbling.

Wren's grip on Elodie's hand had gone from painful to crushing. Elodie did not pull away. She fixed her eyes on the ember-seam at the far end and swam, and the hunger in her blood rose with every stroke, because the Door was calling her — had been calling her since the valley, since the rain fell upward, since before that, maybe, since the storm she swore she never summoned — and it called in a voice made of everything she had lost. Power. Home. The right to be what she was. Ten years of exile, and here at the bottom of the sea was a keyhole shaped exactly like her.

*Look at the pews,* she told herself. *Look at what yes costs.*

Halfway down the nave, the dead began to sing.

It was not sound, quite. It rose out of the water itself, out of the stones, a chord without beginning, and it was the plainchant of Sera's order — Elodie recognized it from hearing Sera hum it over cookfires, thinking herself unheard — sung by nine hundred throats that had not drawn breath in ten years. The melody moved through the flooded cathedral like light through the clerestory blades, touching everything, changing nothing, and the kneelers' lips did not move, and the song went on.

Wren made a small sound. Elodie pulled her close, and the girl buried her face against Elodie's shoulder, and they hung there a moment in the middle of the drowned congregation, in the middle of the song, two living things clinging to each other among the perfectly answered.

"It's not frightening," Wren said finally, muffled, wonder-struck, horrified. "That's what's frightening. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard and it's a graveyard singing."

"Yes," Elodie said. "Hold on to that. Hold on to me. We're nearly there."

Ahead, Sera had stopped.

She hung in the water before the altar steps, and Elodie saw why she had stopped, and her own heart stumbled and went on. The ember-seam in the stone had widened. Only a finger's width, only a crack — but light poured from it now in visible currents, rising through the water like heat-shimmer made solid, and in the light, coiling and uncoiling, vast beyond the architecture to contain it, something moved. Something that had once been a god of harbors and mercy and had starved so long it had become a famine wearing its own name.

It was looking at Sera.

It had always been looking at Sera.

"My child," the water said, in a voice like the whole sea exhaling, gentle as a wave withdrawing over sand, and every kneeler in the nave lifted its head at once — nine hundred faces turning, serene and sightless, toward the priestess who had come home at last. "You brought it. You brought my key. Come down to the altar, beloved, and be received."

Behind Elodie, Corvin's sword cleared its sheath with a scrape that sounded obscene in the singing water. Ahead, Sera Ivane, last priestess of the drowned god, folded her hands in the old gesture of ordination and began, alone, unhurried, to descend the altar steps.

"Sera," Elodie said — warned, pleaded, it was impossible to say which.

Sera did not turn around.

"I know," she said. "I know what yes costs. I've known since the valley." A pause, and her voice cracked open just enough to show the person inside the priestess. "Watch Wren for me."

And the god of the flooded cathedral reached down through forty feet of answered prayer, and gathered its last faithful servant into its hands.
