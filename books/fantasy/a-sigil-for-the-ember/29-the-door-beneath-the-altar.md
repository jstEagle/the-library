## 29. The Door Beneath the Altar

The water did not feel like water.

That was the first thing Elodie understood, wading down into the flooded nave with borrowed breath swelling in her lungs — Wren's small answered prayer, a bubble of grace no bigger than a held secret, wrapped around the three of them like a second skin. The water pressed against her, but it did not drown. It *remembered*. That was the trouble with everything in this drowned place: the water remembered being ocean, remembered being rain, remembered being the tears of a god who had wept so long his own cathedral filled. It pressed against her skin with the weight of all that remembering, and it whispered.

She could not make out the words. She was almost grateful.

"Stay close," Sera said, her voice strange and rounded in the water, arriving a half-beat after her lips moved. She walked ahead with her lantern held high — a real flame, stubborn and mortal, guttering in its glass. The drowned god's own priestess, lighting her way through his drowned house with fire. There was a joke in that. Elodie did not think Sera would laugh at it.

The nave stretched before them, vast and vertical, and the bells rang overhead.

They had been ringing since the party descended — slow, deep, mournful strokes that came not as sound but as pressure, rolling through the water and through Elodie's chest, through the soles of her boots, through her teeth. Each toll was the size of a heartbeat. Each toll said *someone is still here, someone is still here, someone is still here*.

And someone was. The dead congregation still knelt.

They lined the aisles on either side — dozens of them, scores, figures in the robes of an order Elodie had only seen in Sera's stories, kneeling at their pews with their bowed heads furred in silt and pale weed. Their hands were folded. Their spines were patient. Fish moved through the open arches of their ribs, and still they knelt, and still the bells rang for them, and Elodie understood that they were not corpses. Corpses were finished with their praying. These had simply never been told the service was over.

Wren's hand found Elodie's and gripped it. The girl did not scream. Elodie had learned, over the long gray miles of the ghost-road, that Wren's silences were louder than most people's screams.

"They can't see us," Sera said, and her voice wavered on the lie. "They're — they're held in the last moment of the service. They don't turn around. They don't turn around." She said it twice, the way people repeat things they are trying to make true.

One of the kneeling figures did turn, at the far end of the aisle. Slowly. Its face was long dissolved, but Elodie felt its attention settle on her like a hand on the back of her neck, and she felt the water's whisper sharpen, and for one syllable she heard it clearly:

*Storm-blood.*

Then the figure bowed its head again, and Elodie's heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her throat.

* * *

They had come down through the sacristy, following the cartographer's map and Sera's terrible knowledge, and the deeper they went the more Elodie felt the old shape of herself stirring under her skin like a scar aching before rain. Ten years in the Hollow Valley had taught her body to be quiet. It had not taught it to be empty. The storm was still in her — the storm had *always* been in her, the gods' dying convulsion, borrowed and blamed and buried — and this place knew it. The water leaned toward her. The dead congregation's bowed heads seemed to tilt, all together, like a field of grain bending before wind.

She was the wind here. That was the horror of it.

"Elodie." Sera had stopped at the foot of the chancel steps. "Look."

The altar had drowned last and best. It stood on the platform above them, a great slab of black stone carved with the drowned god's sign — a circle broken by a falling line, rain falling into a ring of water — and the flood had not dimmed it. It had *fed* it. Light moved under the altar's surface the way light moves under ice, slow and green-gold and alive, and Elodie's breath caught in the borrowed air, because she knew that color.

She knew it from a crater in a valley where magic could not follow.

She knew it from the banked heart of the last dragon in the world.

"Ember-light," she said. Her voice came out small. "Sera. That's ember-light. That's *his* light. How is his light under the altar of your god?"

Sera did not answer at once. She climbed the steps, and her lantern-light and the altar's under-light met and made a third color between them, and Elodie climbed after her with Wren's hand locked in her own, and when they reached the top Elodie saw it.

A seam.

It ran across the face of the altar like a crack in a kiln-fired pot — no wider than a knife's edge, running floor to crown of the stone and vanishing into the flood above — and through the seam burned ember-light. Not green-gold now. *Gold*, true gold, the gold of Vaskar's banked chest-fire on the coldest valley nights, the gold Elodie had watched from her cottage window for ten years and told herself it was only a star fallen low. The seam breathed. She could see it — the light swelled and dimmed, swelled and dimmed, slow as a sleeping thing's breath.

"It's not a crack," Elodie said. "It's a *door*."

"The Door of Caelmere." Sera set her lantern on the altar's edge. Her hands were shaking. Elodie noticed, and noticed that she noticed, and noticed that she no longer trusted the priestess's shaking hands to mean what they had always meant. "The place where the living land and the dead one touch. Where prayers go. Where answers come *from*."

"And my dragon's fire is leaking through it."

"Your dragon's fire." Sera turned to look at her, and in the ember-light her face was stripped of everything but the question underneath all her questions. "You said *your* dragon. Ten years of exile, Elodie, and you never once went down into his crater, and you call him *yours*."

"I fed the valley that kept him warm." Elodie heard the defensiveness in her own voice and hated it. "That's not the same thing. I know it's not the same thing." She stepped closer to the seam, and the light breathed against her face, warm as a hearth, warm as the one thing the Hollow Valley had never given her. "But he's dying, Sera. He's been dying for centuries, politely, in the dark, and this—" she gestured at the seam, at the whole drowned cathedral, at the kneeling dead and the ringing bells and the water full of remembered ocean, "—this is what's been *drinking* him."

The sigil answered before Sera could.

It hung at Sera's throat on its leather cord, and it had been silent since they entered the water — but now it began to hum. Elodie felt it in her molars, in the plates of her skull, a struck-bell note that the water carried everywhere at once. The bronze disc swung gently on its cord, though there was no current, and its engraved face — the same broken circle, the same falling rain — began to glow with a light that was not bronze-light at all but the same ember-gold as the seam.

The seam *brightened*.

And the bells overhead rang — not their slow mournful toll but a quick, glad peal, three strokes, four, five, like a church on a wedding morning — and every kneeling figure in the congregation turned its head.

All at once. All toward the altar. All toward the sigil, and the girl who carried it, and the storm-blood standing beside her.

"Down," Sera breathed. "Everyone down, now—"

But the dead did not rise. That was worse. They only *watched*, their dissolved faces aimed at the altar like a hundred compass needles, and the water's whisper rose around Elodie into something she could no longer pretend not to understand, a chorus in a hundred drowned voices:

*The door. The door. She has come to the door. The caller has come to the door.*

"I'm not a caller," Elodie said aloud, and her voice cracked the water-silence like a stone through a window. "I'm not anything. I'm a beekeeper."

Wren made a sound — a small, furious, terrified sound — and pressed closer against Elodie's side.

* * *

They worked it out on the chancel platform, in the breathing gold light, while the dead watched and the bells waited and the water whispered its one word over and over like a beggar who has learned only a single prayer.

The sigil was the key. That much Sera had always known — had crossed a kingdom half-burned to bring the key to the lock. But a key in a lock was nothing, Sera said, nothing at all, without a hand to turn it. And the Door of Caelmere did not open the way doors opened. It did not swing. It did not slide.

It *thinned*. The seam of ember-light was the Door at its most closed; at its most open, the stone itself would become the passage, solid and burning and passable at once. And to make it thin — to hold it in that state, to keep the living land and the dead one from snapping shut like a jaw — someone had to stand in the threshold.

"Stand *in* it," Elodie repeated. "In the stone."

"In the seam." Sera's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reciting scripture she wished she had never memorized. "A caller. Someone with storm in their blood. The old rites are specific. The Door is the boundary between breath and water, life and death, the living land and the dead one — and a storm is the same boundary. Rain is the sky touching the earth. Lightning is the sky touching the earth *hard*. Only a storm-caller can stand at the seam and be both sides at once, and hold it open from inside."

"For how long?"

Sera was quiet.

"Sera. For how long?"

"As long as the door must stay open." The priestess's hands had stopped shaking, which was somehow worse. "The rites say — the rites say the caller holds the door, and the door holds the caller, and the two of them hold *each other*, and what passes between the worlds passes *through* them. Every prayer. Every answer. Every god." She looked up, and her eyes in the ember-light were wet, and Elodie could not tell, anymore, which of the wet things in this place were water. "The rites also say that no caller who stood the threshold ever walked out of it again. That's why the order kept it sealed. That's why the drowned god drowned his own cathedral, if you believe the heresies. Not in rage. In *mercy*. He flooded the lock so no one would ever bring the key."

Elodie looked at the seam.

It breathed its slow gold breath, and she thought of Vaskar in his crater, guttering, polite, ancient, hiding the true speed of his dying from her because he thought she would do something exactly like this. *Spend the fire, don't hoard it,* he had taught her, and she had thought it was a lesson about storms. She was beginning to understand it was a lesson about everything.

"And the gods," Elodie said slowly. "The starving gods, the ones answering prayers, the ones grasping. If the door opens—"

"They come through." Sera's voice did not waver. That was the most frightening thing about her in this moment: how steady she was. "All of them. Whatever is left of them. They pour through the caller into the living world, and the caller—" she stopped.

"Say it."

"The caller is the door, Elodie. The caller is what they pour through." Sera closed her eyes. "I have read every rite, every commentary, every heresy my order ever wrote, and there is no version of this where the caller survives. There is no version where the caller survives *as a person*. At best — at *best* — she survives as a place. A wound in the world that the gods walk through forever."

The water was very quiet. Even the bells had stopped. Even the dead had bowed their heads again, as if in mourning for something that had not yet happened.

Wren tugged Elodie's sleeve. The girl's face was pale in the gold light, her borrowed breath fluttering fast, and when she spoke her voice was very small and very hard, the way it always went when she was most frightened.

"Then we don't open it," Wren said. "We take the sigil and we throw it in the deepest trench in the ocean and we go home and keep bees."

Elodie knelt and pulled the girl against her, and over Wren's head she looked at the seam, and she did not answer, because they both knew the answer. The gods were already answering prayers. The door was already leaking — leaking ember-light, leaking whispers, leaking whatever was draining the last dragon in the world toward his death a century too soon. Sealing it was not a choice they could decline. It was a bill that had already come due; the only question was who would stand in the threshold to pay it.

And the water had told her. The dead had told her. The seam itself, breathing its gold breath against her face, had told her.

*Storm-blood.*

There was exactly one storm-caller left in Caelmere, and she was kneeling on the chancel of a drowned cathedral holding an orphan girl who was afraid she would leave.

"I need to think," Elodie said. "Not about whether. About *how*." She stood, and her legs were steadier than she had any right to expect. "Vaskar taught me something. About fire that isn't yours. If I'm going to stand in that seam, I'm not going to stand there as a door that gets walked through. I'm going to stand there as a *storm*." She looked at the sigil, swinging its slow pendulum swing at Sera's throat, glowing its patient gold. "Storms aren't doors. Storms are weather. Things pass *through* weather and come out the other side changed."

"You don't know that's possible," Sera said.

"No," Elodie agreed. "But I know a dragon who does. And before anyone stands anywhere, we're getting out of this water and we're finding him." She turned for the sacristy stair — and stopped.

Because the seam had changed.

The gold light had gone still. Not breathing now. *Listening.* And along the knife-edge of the Door, faint as a hairline crack in ice, a new sound had begun — a sound Elodie felt in the water, in the stone, in the old scar of her own stolen storm. A knocking. Slow. Patient. From the *other side*.

Three knocks. A pause.

Then a voice, thin and vast at once, coming through a seam no wider than a knife, speaking with the borrowed mouths of every kneeling dead thing in the congregation at once:

*We know you are there, storm-caller. We have been praying to* you.

The bells began to ring again — fast, frantic, joyful — and far above, muffled by fathoms of remembered ocean, came a sound Elodie knew in her bones before her mind caught up to it.

Boots. On stone. Many, many boots.

And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the cold silver chime of warden-forged steel.

"They're here," Wren whispered. "Elodie. *They're here.*"
