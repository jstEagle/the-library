## 30. Maren at the Threshold

The water came up to Maren Voss's knees before she understood that she was no longer walking into a cathedral but wading through its grave.

The imperial host moved behind her in silence — three hundred wardens and inquisitors, torchlight guttering on the flooded nave's surface, their reflections wavering below like a second army marching upside down. Nobody spoke. The whispering had taught her that words spent aloud were words the sigil could not use, and lately she hoarded her silences the way a miser hoards coin.

*They are close,* the bronze said against her palm. It did not speak in words, exactly. It spoke in the way grief speaks: as pressure, as certainty, as the memory of voices she had buried so deep she'd needed ten years and an artifact of dead gods to dig them back out.

*Tomas is close. Lira is close.*

Her children. Her dead children, drowned in the river at Caelmere's fall, when the old gods went to war with each other and used the living country as the board. She had been twenty-six. She had held Tomas under the water for one breath too long trying to keep him from floating away, and the last thing he had said — the thing the whispering returned to her every night now, polished smooth as river glass — was *Mama, it's cold.*

The sigil had promised to make it warm again.

She climbed the steps of the altar dais, water sheeting off her greaves, and looked down into the place where the Door slept.

It was not what she expected. She had imagined something vast — a gate of black iron, a mouth, a wound. Instead it was a seam. A hairline fracture in the altar stone, thin as a knife cut in silk, and from within it came a light like the heart of a banked fire: ember-orange, patient, breathing. The light moved. That was the worst of it. It rose and fell in a slow rhythm, and Maren realized, standing hip-deep in the drowned god's nave, that she was looking at something asleep — or something waiting to be born — and that both possibilities made her want to kneel.

Behind her, boots splashed. Corvin Ashe came up the steps slowly, his sword still drawn, his face gray in the ember-light. He had been gray since Grayford Bridge, since he had turned his blade on his own inquisitors and let a storm-caller and a child walk away. The emperor's writ on him was death; Maren had countermanded it twice with forged orders, and if he wondered why, he had not asked. She could not have explained it anyway. Some debts you pay without naming them.

"The stair is held," he said. "For now. The dead in the pews have stopped moving." A pause. "Mostly."

"Good."

"Maren." His voice dropped low enough that only she could hear it over the drip of water from the vaulted dark. "You've been holding that thing since Grayford. You haven't slept in four days. I can see it — the way you listen when no one's talking. Let me carry it the rest of the way."

The bronze hummed against her palm, warm as a living thing, and Maren felt the old reflex rise in her throat — the warden's reflex, the commander's reflex, *I do not hand my weapon to anyone* — and beneath it, newer and far more frightening, the other reflex: *no one else may touch them. No one else gets to bring them back.*

"They answer me," she said. "Not you. Not anyone else. Me."

"That's what worries me."

She turned to look at him fully then, and saw what she had been refusing to see since the bridge: Corvin Ashe was afraid of her. Not of the host, not of the emperor's justice waiting behind them, not of the dead congregation kneeling in their submerged rows with their salt-white faces tipped toward the altar. Of her. Of the woman who had once loved a storm-caller and testified against her anyway, who had built a life on the principle that power must be caged, and who now stood waist-deep in a dead god's house cradling the cage door in her bare hand.

"You think I've lost myself," she said.

"I think," Corvin said carefully, "that the sigil gives people exactly what they most want, and that wanting is the trap. I watched it happen to Sera Ivane's whole order. I read the reports from Ondrel. And I've known you twenty years, Maren, and I have never once seen you look at anything the way you look at that piece of bronze."

Maren looked down at the sigil. The drowned god's face was worn nearly smooth on its obverse — centuries of thumbs, centuries of desperate prayer — but the reverse showed the Door itself, rendered in lines fine as spider silk, and around the Door a ring of small figures: callers, keepers, priests. People whose whole vocation had been to stand between the living land and the dead one and say *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

That had been her whole life too. She had simply done it with walls and warrants instead of bronze.

"I'm going to open it," she said quietly. "I want you to understand that before we go further, because I won't lie to you, Corvin. Not here. This place is too honest."

His jaw tightened. "Open it, and everything the emperor feared comes true. The gods come back. The war comes back. Everything we burned Caelmere's name off the maps to end—"

"The gods aren't coming back," Maren said. "I've listened to them for days. They're not an army, Corvin. They're starving men clawing at a pantry door." The ember-light pulsed, and the water around her ankles warmed by a degree, and she knew — she *knew*, the way you know your own heartbeat — that they had heard her, that they were pleased, and she hated how much she wanted their pleasure. "They don't want thrones. They want to be remembered. Fed. Answered. And in exchange—" her voice caught, and she let it, because lying here would be sacrilege "—in exchange they will give me back what the war took."

Corvin went very still. "Your children are dead, Maren. Eleven years dead. Nothing behind that door un-drowns anyone."

"You haven't heard them."

"No. And neither have you. You've heard what a dying artifact wants you to hear." He stepped closer, water dragging at his legs, and for a moment he was not an inquisitor but the young officer who had stood beside her in the ash-fall after the trial, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say. "I loved someone once who was condemned for a storm she didn't call. I helped condemn her because the alternative was believing the world was less lawful than I could survive. Don't make me watch you do the same thing in reverse."

The words landed like a slap, and Maren almost — *almost* — set the sigil down.

Then the whispering rose.

It came the way weather comes: a change in pressure, a hush across the water, every torch flame in the nave leaning toward the altar at once. Three hundred soldiers felt it and did not know what it was. Maren felt it and knew it by name.

*Tomas.* The voice was small and wet and eleven years old forever. *Mama, it's cold. Why did you let go?*

"I didn't," she whispered. "Tomas, I didn't let go, I held on until my arms—"

*Maren.* Corvin's hand on her shoulder, real, warm, human. "There's nothing there. There's no voice. It's the sigil, it's wearing your grief like a coat—"

"She's here." Lira's voice now, older than she had ever gotten to be, aged forward by the sigil's cruel arithmetic into the woman she might have become. *Mother. We waited. We waited where you told us to wait, and the water came, and you weren't fast enough, and we waited anyway.*

"I know," Maren breathed. Tears ran hot down her cold face. "I know, my loves, I know. I'm here now. I came back for you."

And the ember-light in the seam brightened — just slightly, just enough — and the warmth spread up through the floodwater and into her bones, and it felt, God forgive her, it felt exactly like being forgiven.

* * *

She gave the order to clear the nave at dusk, or what she judged was dusk; down here beneath the collapsed roof, time was a rumor. The host withdrew to the upper galleries and the broken cloister above the waterline, leaving the flooded nave to its congregation of kneeling dead and its two living trespassers. Maren remained at the altar. Corvin remained two steps behind her, which was as close to desertion as his conscience could manage and as close to obedience as hers would allow.

She studied the seam.

The cartographer's intelligence had been thorough — the old man in the saltmarsh had given up the cathedral's location before Maren's wardens had even finished being polite to him — but no report described this. Up close, the Door was not stone at all along its hairline length. It was light compressed until it behaved like matter, and threaded through it ran darker filaments, veins, and when Maren tilted her head she could see them moving: slow currents of ember-color traveling the seam's whole length, gathering near the center, pooling, dispersing. Like breath. Like circulation. Like a heart the size of a kingdom, beating once a minute, beneath the floor of a church full of drowned faithful.

*It opens for two things,* the sigil murmured, and for the first time its voice sounded tired, which frightened her more than hunger would have. *Bronze and blood. The key and the keeper. Bring me the storm-blood, little warden, and I will show you the shape of forever.*

Storm-blood. Elodie Eastwick.

Maren closed her eyes. Ten years ago she had stood in a marble courtroom in Vhalen's Reach and watched a girl of nineteen shake apart under questioning, and Maren had risen, and sworn the oath, and spoken the testimony that ended a life — exile, erasure, the valley where magic cannot follow. Everyone believed she had betrayed her friend. She had let them believe it. Because the truth — that the storm had come out of the sky with no caller at all, that the dying gods had convulsed and used Elodie Eastwick as a lightning rod, that the god-emperor's purges would have burned the girl alive rather than risk what lived in her blood — that truth required a faith in mercy that Maren Voss had lost in a river with her children. Exile had been the only cage strong enough. Exile had been love, wearing its cruelest face.

But the valley had cracked. The prayers were answering themselves. And the girl was here, somewhere in this drowned city, walking toward the same seam of light.

*She will spend it,* the sigil whispered slyly. *The storm. The fire. She will spend what you saved her for, and it will all have been for nothing, and Tomas and Lira will stay cold.*

"Stop," Maren said aloud.

Corvin shifted behind her. "Warden?"

"Not you." Her knuckles were white around the bronze. "Be quiet. Both of you. All of you."

The nave obeyed — the water stilled, the torches steadied, the dead kept their salt silence — and in that ringing quiet Maren heard, very faintly, from far above, through the broken roof and the ruined streets of a city that had unhappened itself: bells. Underwater bells, ringing from some deeper chamber of the cathedral, though the water should have swallowed any sound. Ringing in a pattern she almost recognized. Ringing like a summons.

Or like a warning.

"They're coming," Corvin said. He had drawn his sword again without her noticing. "Eastwick. The priestess. The dragon's shadow passed over the marsh an hour ago — the sentries saw it. If Vaskar is flying, this is the hour."

Maren turned the sigil over in her hands. In the ember-light, the ring of little carved figures around the Door seemed almost to move, processional, circling. Callers and keepers and priests, generation after generation, standing between.

Once, she had thought she was one of them.

"Then we do this now," she said. "Before they reach the nave. Before Eastwick touches it." She waded to the edge of the dais and looked out across the black water, at the hundreds of drowned heads bowed toward her, at the seam of sleeping fire beneath her feet. "Set the perimeter. No one enters the nave — no one, Corvin, not the emperor himself. What happens at this altar happens between me and the dead."

"And what happens at this altar?" he asked quietly. "What exactly are you going to do?"

Maren looked at the sigil. It looked back, the way it always did now, patient and warm and full of her children's voices.

"I'm going to knock," she said, "and see who answers."

She pressed the bronze against the seam.

The light flared — a single pulse, enormous, silent, turning the whole flooded nave the color of a forge — and every drowned face in the congregation lifted at once, and every torch in the galleries above went out, and in the sudden dark Maren heard, rising from beneath the altar, from beneath the city, from beneath the world:

A heartbeat. Faster now. Awake.

And beneath it, threading through the water, faint and far off but unmistakably approaching — the sound of wings.
