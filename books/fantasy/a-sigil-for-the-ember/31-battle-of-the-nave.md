## 31. Battle of the Nave

The water came for them all at once.

It had been rising since they entered the cathedral — slow, patient, the way grief rises in a house where no one will say the dead one's name — but when the imperial host breached the western doors, the drowned god exhaled, and the nave flooded to the waist in a single rolling surge. Candles that had burned underwater for a century guttered and flared green. Somewhere above the waterline, bells began to ring, though there was no wind and no hand upon them, and every soul in the flooded dark understood at once that the ringing was not for them.

It was an alarm. It was a welcome. It was both, because the old gods had never learned the difference.

Corvin Ashe stood on the third stair of the great descent, sword drawn, and watched his own order come down into the water after him.

There were forty of them. He had trained eleven personally. He knew the way Brother Halloway shifted his weight before a lunge, the way Sister Vey said a prayer under her breath not out of piety but to time her breathing. He had eaten beside them. He had buried their dead. And now he stood between them and a girl of perhaps twelve years who was clutching a bronze sigil to her chest like something she'd been told never to let go of, which was exactly what she'd been told, and Corvin found that he could not make his feet move down the stair.

"Inquisitor." Halloway's voice carried flat across the water. "Step aside. The emperor's mercy extends even to traitors who kneel."

"The emperor's mercy," Corvin said, "is a word you use for things you burn."

He heard it leave his mouth and understood, distantly, that some door inside him had just opened as well — a smaller one, a meaner one, but a door nonetheless. Ten years of orders. Ten years of *burn what answers, question nothing*. Ten years of waking at night with salt on his lips and a name he would not say, and here at the bottom of everything was the reason, standing hip-deep in holy water with a storm-caller's blood in her veins and her people gathered around her like a hearth he had never once been invited to sit beside.

"Last chance, Ashe."

"No," Corvin said, and raised his blade. "I'm done with last chances. They're how I got here."

They came at him three abreast, because the stair was narrow and the water slowed them, and Corvin Ashe discovered in the first thirty seconds of the fight that he had spent ten years being very good at a job he had never once believed in, and that this — this narrow stone stair, this green-lit flood, these faces he knew — was the first honest work of his life.

* * *

Wren ran.

She did not think about running. Thinking was for afterward; Elodie had told her that on the ghost-road once, in the flat voice she used for true things: *when it's bad, your legs know the way home before you do.* So Wren's legs knew, and Wren ran, the sigil pressed against her sternum under her soaked shirt, its hum traveling through her ribs like a second heartbeat that didn't match her own.

The arches were coming down.

She felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the floor, a rain of grit from ceilings that had held for four hundred years — and then the first arch went with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and masonry the size of a hay cart plunged into the flood twenty feet behind her. The wave it threw caught her and spun her, and she came up coughing, one hand still fisted in her shirt over the sigil, and kept running.

Above her, along the clerestory gallery, the dead congregation had begun to move.

Wren had seen them kneeling when they first descended — hundreds of them, robed in rotted silk, bowed toward an altar that was now only a suggestion beneath black water. She had thought they were statues. Statues did not turn their heads. Statues did not rise, joint by joint, with the patience of things that had waited a very long time and were in no hurry now.

But they weren't coming for her. That was the strange part, the part she would try to explain later and never quite manage. The dead flowed past her like fish around a stone — she felt the cold of them, smelled the deep-water smell of them, saw the green candlelight shine through cheeks that were more memory than face — and they went down the stair, past Corvin's sword, past the shouting inquisitors, and fell upon the warden line at the western doors with the gentle, terrible industry of a tide going out.

They were not angry. Wren understood that with the certainty children have about animals and weather. They were *answering*. Someone had prayed in this room once, thousands of someones, and their prayers had gone unanswered for so long that the unanswered-ness had pooled in them like rain in a cellar, and now the door was cracking open and all of it was spilling out at once.

"Left!" Elodie's voice, raw, from somewhere near the altar. "Wren — left aisle, don't stop, whatever you see don't—"

Wren turned left. An arch collapsed in front of her.

She stopped so hard she sat down in the water. Stone dust billowed; the passage she needed was a wall of rubble and churning foam. Behind her, another arch groaned. Ahead, through the dust, she could see the far colonnade — ten steps away, maybe twelve — and between her and it, nothing but falling stone and the flood boiling up through cracks that hadn't existed a minute ago.

Her legs wanted to run. There was nowhere for them to run to.

So Wren did the other thing Elodie had taught her, the small thing, the thing they practiced on the road with dandelion heads and candle flames: she cupped her hands around the sigil through her wet shirt, closed her eyes, and prayed. Not to any god. She was done with gods; gods took tolls. She prayed the way you talk to a friend across a dark room.

*Please. I'm little and I'm scared and I'm carrying something that isn't mine and I just need the roof to stay up twelve more steps.*

The water went still.

Not calm — still, the way a held breath is still. All across the nave, in a circle maybe fifty feet wide with Wren at its center, the flood simply stopped moving. Falling dust hung in the air. A chip of stone paused an inch above the water's surface and stayed there, turning slowly, catching green light.

And in that terrible held silence, Wren ran the twelve steps, and the world let her.

When the breath released, the roar came back all at once, and the arches came down behind her like applause.

* * *

Maren Voss fought her way through the drowned nave with the sigil in one hand and a warden's saber in the other, and she had stopped hearing the battle as a battle. It was arithmetic now. Forty inquisitors, half committed against Corvin at the stair. Two hundred wardens at the doors, bogged in waist-deep water against congregants who could not be killed because they were already dead. Herself, and the door, and the whispering bronze in her fist that had not stopped speaking to her since Grayford Bridge.

*We can bring them back,* it murmured, in a voice like her mother's, like her brother's, like all three of her dead at once. *The door opens both ways. You know it does. You've always known.*

"I know it's lying," Maren said aloud, and slashed through a dead man's shoulder without slowing, and felt the blade pass through him like a knife through river fog, and hated how much comfort the voice gave anyway. That was the rot in her, she understood now. Not weakness. Comfort. Ten years of certainty had been its own kind of armor, and the sigil had found the seam.

Ahead, at the threshold beneath the altar, a seam of ember-light glowed through the flooded dark — thin as a knife cut, impossibly warm. And standing in it, silhouetted, her hair lifting in the impossible updraft, was Elodie Eastwick.

Maren's whole body turned toward her like a compass needle. Ten years of turning toward her. The trial. The testimony. The night Maren had stood before the tribunal and lied by telling the truth in the wrong order, and watched them take everything from a girl who had called no storm, because the alternative was watching the god-emperor's purges take her head — and Maren had told herself, every day for ten years, that exile was mercy, that the Hollow Valley was a kindness, that she had saved her friend the only way the world allowed.

The sigil pulsed against her palm, warm as a sleeping bird.

*She'll forgive you,* it whispered. *Or she won't. Either way, you'll have them back.*

Maren waded toward the light.

* * *

Sera Ivane stood in the flooded transept with her god rising out of the water in front of her, and she was screaming, and the scream made no sound, because the drowned god had taken her voice the way it took everything — gently, thoroughly, as its due.

It was vast. That was the only word her mind would hold. Not large — vast, the way the ocean is vast, a bigness that does not end so much as stop being measurable. It wore the shape of a man the way a flood wears the shape of a valley: briefly, condescendingly, out of politeness to the drowning. Its eyes were two fathoms of cold water. When it moved, the nave moved with it.

*MY PRIESTESS,* it said, in the register that cracked stone. *THE DOOR. THE SIGIL. YOURSELF. ALL THREE, OR NOTHING.*

"I brought the sigil," Sera managed. Her ordination mark — the brand on her wrist, the last anchor of forty years of faith — burned like a coal. "I brought it alone, as you commanded. But the others—"

*WERE KINDLING,* her god said. *YOU KNEW THAT WHEN YOU CARRIED ME TO THEM.*

And the worst of it, the part she would carry out of this place or die carrying, was that she had. Some priestess-part of her, deep below the part that loved Elodie's stubbornness and Wren's fierce little prayers and even the sardonic ruin of the dragon, had known from the first night at the valley door that she was leading lambs somewhere, and had called it faith, and had kept walking.

Across the chaos, through the falling stone, she saw Wren — alive, running, *alive* — and something in Sera's chest turned over and refused to turn back.

"No," she said.

The vastness paused. In four hundred years, its priestesses had said many things. *Yes*, mostly. *How long*, sometimes. Never, in the whole history of the drowned order, *no*.

Sera put her branded wrist into the floodwater and began, with numb fingers, to unpick the knots of her ordination — the memory she had never paid at the bridge, the one she had saved the way a miser saves a coin, the memory of the day she first heard her god's voice and wept with joy. It was the coin of her entire life. She had told herself she kept it for this moment. She had lied. She had kept it because spending it meant he was never real.

*DAUGHTER—*

"I was never your daughter," Sera said, and paid the toll.

* * *

At the stair, Corvin killed his ninth inquisitor and stopped counting, because counting was a thing you did when you expected to answer for it later, and he had finally accepted that he would not survive to be answered for anything.

Halloway was down. Vey had lowered her sword at the sight of the dead pouring past them both and not raised it again — she stood in the water with her arms at her sides, weeping, saying her timing-prayer out loud now, actually praying, and Corvin understood that whatever the emperor had built out of fear and ash was coming apart faster than any of them could hold it. Above, the cathedral groaned. The bells rang on. The water rose — knee, waist, chest — and through it all, at the center of everything, the ember-seam beneath the altar burned brighter, and brighter, and brighter.

Corvin looked at it and felt, absurdly, the way he had felt at nineteen, standing in a tribunal gallery, watching a girl with storm-grey eyes refuse to cry.

*Hold the line,* someone had told him then, too.

He set his feet on the wet stone, raised his sword at the next rank climbing out of the flood, and held it.

* * *

And at the threshold, hip-deep in light, Elodie Eastwick stood before the Door of Caelmere and felt it look back at her.

It was not a door the way doors are doors. It was a seam, a wound, a place where the living land and the dead one pressed together like two hands in prayer — and it was keyed to the bronze, yes, but more than the bronze. It was keyed to *her*. To storm-blood. To the convulsion that had torn through a nineteen-year-old girl ten years ago and killed forty people who had never done anything wrong, and as Elodie stood in its light with the battle roaring behind her and Maren Voss wading toward her through the flood with murder and grief written in equal parts across her face, the Door opened — not outward, not yet, but inward, into Elodie, and showed her the truth.

She saw the sky over Caelmere ten years ago. She saw the gods — starving, grasping, dying by inches in a country that had erased them from every map — convulse all at once, a death-throes of divinity with nowhere to go. She saw it pour down, seeking any vessel, finding one: a girl on a hillside with old blood in her and no training worth the name. She saw the storm break through her like water through a cracked dam, and she saw —

— she saw Maren, on that hillside, *after*, kneeling in the wreckage, understanding in one white-hot instant what had happened and what the emperor's inquisitors would do to a storm-caller who had "called" a storm that killed forty souls. She saw Maren stand. She saw Maren walk into the tribunal and arrange the truth like flowers in a vase — beautiful, deliberate, and cut short of blooming — and she saw Maren carry the weight of it alone for ten years, believing to the last hour that exile was the only shelter left to offer.

The storm was never hers.

The betrayal was never a betrayal.

Elodie made a sound that was not a word, and the Door drank it, and behind her, close enough now to touch, Maren Voss raised the sigil high, and the ember-light flared to fill the whole drowned nave —

— and the last thing Elodie saw before the light took everything was the seam beginning, at long last, to open.
