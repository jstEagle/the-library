## 32. The Truth of the Storm

The water was warm.

That was the first wrongness Elodie noticed as she waded toward the threshold — not cold, though they were deep beneath the flooded cathedral of a drowned god, though the sea pressed down on them like a held breath. The water around the seam of ember-light in the altar stone was warm as bathwater, warm as blood, and it moved strangely, sliding against her skin in slow spirals, as if it remembered being something other than water once and hadn't entirely given up the habit.

Behind her, Wren's small answered prayer still hung in the air around their heads — a bubble of borrowed breath that tasted of rain on hot stone, of the first summer storm after drought. Sera had gone back up to hold the stair with Corvin; Maren's host was coming, boots and steel and torchlight swallowed strange by the waterline. There was no time. There had been no time for ten years, if she was honest, and honesty had become a habit she could no longer break.

The sigil hummed in her hand. Bronze, older than bronze should be, shaped like a door seen from the side — a flat plane, a hinge, a keyhole that wasn't a keyhole but an eye. It had hummed differently for every person who'd held it. For Sera it sang like a struck bell. For Vaskar it had growled low, guttering. For Elodie, since the moment she'd taken it from Maren's bleeding hands at Grayford Bridge, it had been quiet.

Not silent. Quiet the way the air goes quiet before lightning.

She reached the threshold. The seam of light ran floor to vaulted ceiling through the altar stone, thin as a knife's edge, bright as the moment before dawn, and where it met the floodwater the water did not extinguish it — it *drank* it, glowing faintly gold for yards in every direction, so that the drowned nave around her became a cathedral of amber, the kneeling dead congregation lit like figures in honey.

The dead congregation still knelt. She tried not to look at them. She failed, the way she always failed. Two hundred souls caught mid-prayer when the sea came in, their robes drifting like jellyfish bells, their faces serene or terrified depending on how honest they'd been about dying. One of them, a woman near the front, had turned her head slightly since yesterday. Elodie was almost sure. Almost sure enough never to come back here, which was, she supposed, the point of looking away.

"Okay," she said aloud, and her voice came out flattened and close inside the breathing-bubble. "I'm here. I'm standing in your stupid doorway. Show me."

The sigil grew warm.

Then it grew *bright*, and the brightness didn't stay in the bronze — it climbed her wrist like ivy, poured over her shoulder, and the world folded.

* * *

She was nineteen again, and it was raining.

No. Not raining. That was the word everyone used afterward, the word the trial used, the word carved into her sentence: *storm-caller Elodie Eastwick did call, did summon, did unleash.* But standing here now — wherever *here* was, this memory-space that smelled of ozone and beeswax and the particular green smell of Caelmere before a killing heat — she could see what she had never been permitted to see.

She saw the sky over the capital, and it was not a sky that any caller had made.

It was *full*. That was the only word. Full past fullness, swollen like a blister, like a lung drowning, like a cup poured from by hands that would not stop pouring. And threaded through it — she could see them now, the way you can see the pattern in wallpaper once someone points it out — were shapes. Vast ones. Slow ones. Shapes like the drowned god she'd glimpsed at the cathedral's edge, like famine given wings, like grief the size of weather. The old gods of Caelmere, the ones the maps had erased along with the country's name, and they were *dying*, and their dying was coming out of them the way a scream comes out of a body — uncontrollably, all at once, with no regard for what stood beneath.

The convulsion gathered itself above the capital. Forty people stood in the market square below — she knew the number, had heard it read aloud in a voice like dry paper while she knelt in chains — and the sky needed somewhere to put what it was holding, and there was one thing in the city that could hold it. One vessel. One creature whose blood was keyed to storms the way locks are keyed to keys.

A girl of nineteen, walking home from the apiary with honey on her sleeves.

*It went through me,* Elodie thought, watching her own younger self look up at that swollen, terrible sky. *It didn't come from me. It came* through *me.*

The difference between those two things was a life. Her whole life. Ten years of exile, of wards that kept magic out like a wall keeps out the sea, of learning to be a woman who kept bees instead of a woman who broke skies — all of it built on a word. *Did call.* A lie so large it had become the ground everyone stood on.

She watched the storm pass through her younger self. It was horrible and it was beautiful and it was nothing like choosing. The girl threw her arms up — anyone would have; it was like catching a falling child, reflex, terror, the body doing the only thing bodies can do under an avalanche — and the convulsion broke apart around her, channeled, divided, spent across the rooftops in ways that killed forty people and would have killed forty thousand if it had landed whole. Elodie watched herself collapse in the ruined square, watched the rain fall upward off her skin because even then, even at nineteen, the magic left her the way it entered: violently, without permission.

And then the memory shifted, the way dreams shift, and she was in the trial chamber, and she was not the one in chains.

Maren was.

No — Maren stood at the witness stand, twenty years old, in her brand-new warden's gray that still had the creases from the folding, and her hands were shaking so badly she had locked them behind her back, and her face was the face of a person performing calm the way a drowning man performs floating. Elodie knew that performance. She had invented half its techniques herself, the two of them, giggling in the academy dormitories, practicing *warden-faces* in the blackened window glass.

But it was Maren's *eyes* that stopped Elodie's heart, here in the amber dark where no one could see her watch. Because Maren wasn't looking at the judges. She wasn't looking at the accused.

She was looking at the emperor's inquisitors along the side wall. Three of them. Taking notes.

And Elodie understood, with the slow sick clarity of a bone setting wrong, that Maren had counted them before she began.

*"Did you see the accused summon the storm?"* the chief judge asked.

And Maren — who had been there, who had run through that square, who had pulled Elodie's unconscious body out of the floodwater with her own two hands, who knew better than any living soul except Elodie what had happened under that sky — Maren said:

*"Yes."*

The word fell into the chamber like a stone into a well. Elodie watched her friend say it. Watched her jaw tighten around it. Watched her do the warden-face, the good one, the one that said *I am certain, I am loyal, I am not afraid of you* — and underneath it, visible now only because ten years and a door of ember-light had burned everything else away, Maren's eyes flicked once more to the inquisitors' table, and there was something in them that Elodie had spent a decade calling betrayal and had never once, not in her worst nights, called by its true name.

Calculation. Terrible, desperate, loving calculation.

Because Elodie could see the rest now, the part that had happened in rooms she'd never been shown. The inquisitors' report that went up the chain. The god-emperor's ascension only three years past, his divinity new and ill-fitting, his purges already beginning — every storm-caller in the empire summoned for *examination*, every examination ending in ash. She had known that. Everyone knew that. What she had never known — what Maren had known, standing in that courtroom with her shaking hands behind her back — was what the examiners looked for.

They looked for vessels. Things the gods had *used*. Doors the dying powers had passed through, marked, keyed. A storm-caller who had merely *called* a storm was a criminal. A storm-caller through whom the old gods themselves had convulsed — that was an asset. That was a weapon to be seized, studied, bled, and when the bleeding stopped, discarded. The emperor was gathering doors. He wanted to be the only one left standing when the old powers finished dying, and he intended to stand on the threshold himself.

If Maren had told the truth — *the storm used her, she's marked, she's a door* — Elodie would not have faced exile.

She would have faced a table, and straps, and men with instruments, and then a fire.

Exile to the Hollow Valley — the one place in the world where magic could not follow, where whatever the gods had left inside her would go dormant, starve, sleep. Exile was the cruelest sentence the court could give. It was also the only sentence that hid her. Maren had stood at that witness stand and lied — had condemned her best friend to ten years of loneliness and beekeeping and the long gray silence of a valley without magic — because the lie was the only shield the law allowed her to raise.

*You let them think I was guilty to keep them from thinking I was precious.*

Elodie came out of the vision gasping, and the breathing-bubble around her head shuddered with it, and the warm water caught her as her knees gave, buoyant, almost gentle, as if the sea itself were trying to soften the landing.

* * *

She knelt at the threshold for a long time. The ember-seam pulsed beside her, patient, ancient, and she realized distantly that it had shown her this on purpose. Doors remember what passes through them. This one had felt the gods' convulsion tear through a nineteen-year-old girl, and it had kept the shape of that tearing the way wax keeps a seal, and now, with her blood on the threshold and the sigil in her fist, it had simply... played it back. Truth as toll. The bridge on the ghost-road had wanted a memory; the Door wanted a certainty.

It had wanted her to stop hating the wrong person for the right reasons.

"I don't—" Her voice cracked. She pressed her palm flat against the wet stone. "I don't know how to carry this."

The sigil answered by humming, low, and the hum wasn't sound exactly — it slid straight past her ears into the place where her storm lived, the sealed room behind the wall the valley had built, and for one heartbeat the room opened, and she felt it all: every storm she'd ever been owed, banked and waiting, the gods' convulsion still coiled in her blood like a seed that had never been allowed to sprout. Ten years of dormancy. Ten years of the ward pressing it down. And beneath the hunger of it — and it *was* hunger, she could admit that now, she'd admitted it the night the storm nearly took Wren — beneath the hunger was something else.

Purpose. The seed knew what it was for.

The Door was keyed to storm-blood. To *her* blood. Not because she was guilty. Because she was the last vessel the old gods had touched, the last lock their dying had turned, and when the time came — and the time was now, the water trembling, the bells ringing their drowned peal overhead, Maren's army descending the stair — the Door would open for no one else.

That was why Maren had taken the sigil at Grayford Bridge. Not to open the Door herself. She couldn't. Elodie had seen it in the vision's final frame, the truth layered under the truth: the whispering sigil had promised Maren her dead family back, and Maren — incorruptible Maren, certain Maren, who had sacrificed everything once already on the altar of protecting someone — had begun to believe that if she brought the sigil to the Door, if she controlled the opening, she could bargain. Control the terms. Keep everyone safe, the way she had kept Elodie safe, the way she had kept everyone safe her whole life, whether they wanted it or not, whether it destroyed them or not.

*Oh, Mare,* Elodie thought, and the name came out of her soaked in something that wasn't forgiveness yet but was no longer purely rage, and that frightened her more than the inquisitors did. Rage was simple. Rage had kept her upright for ten years. Whatever this was — this understanding, this devastating, unbearable clarity — it required her to grieve all over again for a betrayal that had never quite been one, and grieving Maren Voss was a labor she had sworn, on her mother's grave, never to begin.

Above her, muffled by fathoms and stone, the bells changed their rhythm. Not the tide-peal anymore. Something faster. Something like alarm.

Corvin was holding the stair. Corvin, who had turned his blade on his own inquisitors at Grayford Bridge and paid for it in blood he could not spare. How long could a wounded man hold a stair against an imperial host? Not long. Not long enough.

And Sera — Sera was somewhere below, deeper than the nave, in the dark where her god waited, having said *yes* to a famine wearing her father's voice. Elodie had let her go. Had believed, still believed, that love sometimes meant letting people walk into the water. But the Door's truth sat in her chest now like a swallowed coal, and one of its edges was this: the drowned god had commanded Sera to bring the sigil *alone*. Everything Sera had done — the lies, the refusals, the sanctuary refused on their behalf — had bent toward that command. And if the god got what it wanted, the Door would not seal. It would open *wide*, and the starving dead powers of Caelmere would pour through into a world that had barely survived their dying.

Unless a storm-caller stood in the threshold and chose otherwise.

Unless she spent it. All of it. Every storm she was ever owed.

Vaskar's voice rose in her memory, sardonic and guttering and kind: *Spend the fire, little keeper. Hoarded flame is just smoke waiting for an excuse.*

The bells crashed again, closer now, and through the amber water she saw them — torchlight at the head of the stair, dozens of points of flame bending strangely in the flood-glow, and a figure in warden's gray descending ahead of them, sword drawn, moving with the grim economy Elodie would have known anywhere, in any light, at the bottom of any sea.

Maren had come to the threshold. And Elodie rose out of the warm water to meet her, the sigil burning quiet in her fist, the truth of the storm sitting between them like a third person neither of them had ever been allowed to see.

"Maren," she said, and the water carried her voice strangely, tenderly, all the way up the stair. "We need to talk about what really happened."
