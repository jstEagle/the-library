## 33. Confrontation in the Water

The water was at Elodie's waist now, and rising.

She could feel it in the way she had learned to feel weather over ten years of exile — not with magic, which was still a locked door inside her, but with her skin, her bones, the old animal knowledge that lived beneath all of it. The flood in the nave was not rain-fed. It was *remembering*. Every drop that slid down the cathedral's drowned stairways carried the taste of the sea that had once covered this land, before Caelmere built its cathedrals and then un-built them from every map and memory it could reach. The water wanted to be ocean again. It was climbing toward that want, inch by patient inch.

Above her, through the shattered oculus where the roof had folded in on itself a century ago, moonlight fell in a single silver column and turned the flooded nave into a corridor of ghosts. The dead congregation still knelt in their rows, preserved in the silt and the strange cold, their upturned faces soft with a devotion that had outlasted their god's attention. Bells rang somewhere far above the waterline — slow, arrhythmic, the sound arriving warped through fathoms of flood. Wren's small answered prayer had given them air to breathe down here, a shimmer of borrowed breath that sat around Elodie's head like a second skin, and even now she could hear the girl's voice in it, thin and determined, carrying the sigil through the collapsing arches somewhere behind her.

But Elodie had stopped at the threshold. She had stopped because Maren Voss was standing on the altar steps.

High Warden of Caelmere. Her best friend. The woman whose testimony had put her on the exile road at nineteen with nothing but a name that meant *storm-murderer* in every town she'd passed through.

Maren stood with the water to her knees and the sigil cradled in both hands like a sleeping infant, and she was not looking at the Door. She was looking at Elodie.

* * *

The Door itself was exactly what Sera had promised and nothing Elodie had imagined: a seam of ember-light in the altar's foundation stone, thin as a knife-cut, warm as a hearth in winter. It did not blaze. It *glowed*, the way a banked fire glows, patient and enormous, and Elodie understood in her marrow that what slept inside it was not a door at all. It was an ember. The last unspent coal of the old gods' long dying, and everything in the flooded cathedral — the water, the dead, the whispering sigil, the god that Sera had gone to feed herself to — was drawn to it like moths to a flame they could not survive.

"You came," Maren said.

Her voice was wrong. Elodie had rehearsed this confrontation for ten years — in the valley, tending bees, staring at the crater where Vaskar slept. In every version, Maren's voice was cold. In every version, Elodie got to say the things she'd composed in the dark: *You knew. You knew and you let them take me. Forty people, Maren. Forty.*

In this version, Maren's voice was raw as a scraped knee.

"You sound surprised," Elodie said. Her own voice came out steadier than she felt. The borrowed air shimmered around them both; sound carried strangely underwater, intimate and close.

"I hoped you wouldn't." Maren's thumb moved over the sigil's bronze rim, a small unconscious motion, the way she'd once thumbed the spine of her book when they were girls reading by candlelight in the Warden barracks. "I hoped you'd stay in your valley. Grow your honey. Let the world burn politely around you."

"You came to my valley with fire."

"I came to your valley with *orders*." Maren's jaw tightened. "There's a difference. I've been telling myself there's a difference for ten years."

* * *

They had been nineteen and inseparable, and Elodie had believed — right up until the trial — that friendship was the one thing in Caelmere that could not be confiscated.

Now she waded forward, one step, two, and the dead congregation watched her pass with their silt-softened eyes, and the ember-light from the Door painted her shadow long and trembling against the flooded pews.

"Say it," Elodie said. "Whatever you came here to say. Say it, because I have spent ten years not knowing, Maren, and the not-knowing has been worse than the exile. Did you know the storm wasn't mine? Did you know and testify anyway?"

The water between them shivered. Somewhere above, the bells rang again, and a section of the clerestory groaned and shed itself into the flood with a sound like a whale dying.

"Yes," Maren said.

One word. It went through Elodie like a spear through water — no resistance, just depth.

"I knew." Maren's composure was coming apart now, and she seemed to know it, and she seemed past caring. "I knew the moment I felt it. You have to understand — I was raised on the old stories, Elodie. My grandmother remembered the Godswar. She remembered what the gods' dying did the last time they convulsed — whole coastlines, gone. Cities drowned on purpose, by their own gods, so the enemy couldn't have them. When the sky over the harvest festival went green and *wrong*, I knew. I knew it wasn't you. You were standing next to me and your hands were at your sides and you were as frightened as I was, and the storm came *through* you anyway, like you were a window someone had left open."

"Then why—" Elodie's voice cracked, and she hated it. "Why did you stand up in that court and tell them I called it?"

"Because the emperor's inquisitors were already in the square." Maren laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "You don't remember. You were half-drowned, they'd already put the iron cuffs on you, and Corvin Ashe — he was nobody then, a junior inquisitor who'd pulled you out of the wreckage — he was begging his superiors for a proper investigation. And his superiors were asking a much simpler question: *who do we execute?* The emperor had just taken the throne. He'd just erased Caelmere from the maps. He needed a storm-caller, Elodie. He needed a witch, a monster, a story with an ending. And if they'd dug into the truth — if they'd found out the old gods were still alive enough to kill forty people through an innocent girl — do you know what would have happened?"

Elodie's mouth was dry, which was absurd, standing in waist-deep water.

"Purges," Maren whispered. "Not of you. Of everyone who'd ever prayed to the old gods. Every hedge-witch. Every village priestess. My grandmother. Sera Ivane's entire order, ten years before they were ever born into danger. The emperor would have burned the faith out of the country root and branch to make sure no god ever found another window. Forty dead would have become four hundred. Four thousand." Her hands tightened on the sigil until Elodie heard the bronze creak. "So I stood up, and I swore the storm was yours, and I made them believe it was contained. One guilty storm-caller. One exile. A story with an ending. And I have hated myself every single day since, and I have told myself every single day since that it was the only mercy the world would let me buy for you."

* * *

The water had reached Elodie's ribs. She barely noticed.

"You could have told me," she said, and her voice was very small. "You could have come to the valley. Any time in ten years. You could have written—"

"And said what?" Maren stepped forward, and now they were close enough that Elodie could see the gray in her hair, the lines around her eyes that hadn't been there at nineteen, the way her hands shook around the sigil she'd killed her soul to hold. "'Hello, Elodie, I destroyed your life to save it, please forgive me'? I tried to write you a hundred letters. I burned every one. Because the truth would have freed you and doomed everyone else, and the lie protected everyone else and *drowned* you, and I chose, and I have been drowning in the choosing ever since." Her voice broke on the last word, and she did not try to repair it. "You want to know the worst part? The whispering got me too. The sigil. On the road here. It offered me my family back — my grandmother, my brothers, everyone the Godswar took. And do you know what I felt, when it offered?"

Elodie said nothing.

"*Relief*," Maren said. "Because if the gods were real, if they could give back the dead, then maybe I hadn't traded my best friend for nothing. Maybe the arithmetic finally balanced." She looked down at the sigil, and her expression was one Elodie recognized from her own mirror, from her own worst nights in the valley: the look of someone who has just understood that the thing they've been leaning on was hollow all along. "It doesn't balance. It never balanced. I just made you carry the remainder."

* * *

Above them, the battle for the nave was reaching the water. Elodie could hear it now — steel ringing on steel, Corvin's voice bellowing orders that were no longer the emperor's orders, the splash and scream of inquisitors meeting wardens on the flooded stair. Wren's borrowed breath flickered at the edges. They were running out of cathedral.

"Maren." Elodie held out her hand. "The sigil. Give it to me."

"You don't know what you're asking." Maren's eyes were wet, and the water was rising, and she did not wipe them. "The Door needs a caller. Someone to stand in the threshold and *hold* it while it opens. Sera's god told her that much before it told her the rest. The one who holds the Door holds it with everything they are, Elodie. Not their body. *Them*. You'd be pouring yourself into that seam like water into a wound."

"I know." And she did know — Vaskar had taught her, in his sardonic, guttering way, the lesson he was now bleeding out in the sea to prove: *spend the fire, don't hoard it*. "I'm not asking you to understand it. I'm asking you to trust me with it. One more time. The last time."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Ten years passed between them in that moment — the trial, the road, the valley, the letters never written, the arithmetic that never balanced.

"You're not the girl I condemned," Maren said quietly.

"No," Elodie agreed. "I'm the woman you saved. Whether you meant to or not."

And Maren Voss — High Warden of a country that did not exist, keeper of a lie that had cost her everything except her life, which she had never figured out how to spend — began to cry. Not gracefully. Not the way queens and wardens cry in stories, with a single tear and a steady chin. She cried the way Elodie had cried at nineteen on the exile road, ugly and gasping, ten years of it coming up out of her all at once, and Elodie waded the last few feet and took the sigil from her hands and held her, because some debts cannot be repaid, only carried together.

"I can't go with you," Maren said into her shoulder. "Someone has to hold the line. Corvin can't hold it alone, and Wren— Wren can't see what you're about to do. Let her remember you walking toward the light, not—" She couldn't finish.

"Then hold the line," Elodie said. She pulled back and looked at her — really looked, for the first time since the trial. "And Maren. When this is over, if there is an over — come to the valley. There's honey. The bees won't know what you did. They only care whether your hands are steady."

Maren laughed through the ruin of her tears. "They always were," she said. "That was always the problem."

She stepped back, squared her shoulders, and turned toward the stair where the fighting was thickest, and Elodie watched the High Warden of Caelmere draw herself up out of her grief the way a soldier draws a sword — not because it stops hurting, but because it's the only shape the hurt can take that still does some good.

* * *

Elodie turned to the Door.

The ember-seam pulsed against the flooded stone, patient, waiting, and the water around it was warm now, warm as blood, warm as the inside of a name being called. In her hands, the sigil had stopped whispering. It was singing — low and vast, a chord made of every prayer ever answered too well, every god too hungry, every dying thing reaching for one more dawn.

Behind her, steel rang. Maren's voice rose over the water, clear and hard and steady, calling the warden line into formation around the stair. Ahead of her, the seam of light breathed.

Elodie Eastwick, storm-caller, exile, beekeeper, took one step toward the threshold — and then another — and the water rose to meet her like a hand.

And deep beneath the flooded cathedral, in the dark where the drowned god waited, something vast turned its attention toward the light she was carrying, and began to rise.
