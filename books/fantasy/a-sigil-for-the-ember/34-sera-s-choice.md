## 34. Sera's Choice

The water in the nave had gone still.

That was the first wrongness Sera Ivane noticed — not the god itself, not yet, but the water. For three days it had moved with them, curling around their ankles as they waded the drowned cathedral's aisles, tugging at their hems like a child demanding attention. Living water. Her god's breath made visible. Now it lay flat and silent as a mirror, and every kneeling corpse in the congregation was reflected perfectly in its surface, row upon row of the drowned faithful, their stone-still devotion duplicated downward into darkness until there seemed to be two churches: one above, one below, and no way to tell which held the living.

Sera stood at the edge of the altar platform with her boots in six inches of floodwater and felt her god rising beneath her like a whale surfacing under a rowboat.

*He comes,* whispered the sigil against her sternum, where it hung on its cord of braided hair and bronze. It had spoken to her since Grayford Bridge, since Maren's hands had let it go, since Wren had run the collapsing arches with it clutched to her chest like a stolen egg. Not words, exactly. More like pressure. Like the memory of a voice heard through a wall. *He comes, little priestess. He is hungry, and you have brought him home.*

"Elodie," Sera said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "It's time."

Elodie Eastwick turned from the Door.

They had found it an hour past midnight, though *found* was too gentle a word for what had actually happened — Elodie had pressed her palm flat against the altar stone and the stone had *opened*, peeling back like skin over a wound to reveal a seam of ember-light running floor to ceiling, thin as a knife blade, warm as a hearth in winter. The Door of Caelmere. The seam between the living land and the dead one. It threw orange light across the flooded nave now, across the drowned pews and the kneeling dead and the faces of the only family Sera had left, and it was beautiful, and it was terrible, and it hummed against her teeth like a struck bell that would never finish ringing.

Elodie crossed the water toward her, storm-grey eyes catching the ember-glow. Behind her, Corvin Ashe held the stairhead alone, his inquisitor's blade drawn and wet to the hilt, watching the dark archway where the imperial host would come. Wren sat on a half-submerged pew, small and soaked and stubborn, refusing to be sent anywhere safer because there was nowhere safer. And beyond them all, out in the black water beyond the cathedral walls, something vast was moving — they could all feel it, a displacement in the deep, the sense of a weight turning over.

"What do you feel?" Elodie asked. Always practical questions with her. Sera had learned to love her for it.

"Him." Sera's hand went to the sigil without her permission. She was always touching it now. She suspected, distantly, that this was what addiction looked like from the inside. "He's been quiet since we entered. I thought — I hoped he was sleeping. Gods don't sleep, Elodie. They wait." She swallowed. "And he knows where we are. He's always known. I was never leading us here. I was being led."

"I know."

"You can't know—"

"Maren knew," Elodie said quietly, and the name still landed between them like a dropped blade, even after everything, even after the bridge and the blood and Maren Voss holding the stairwell below with a borrowed sword and thirty years of guilt finally spoken aloud. "Maren knew the truth of my storm for ten years and said nothing. You've known your god wanted this door since before you ever met me. I did the arithmetic on the road, Sera. I'm slow, not blind." A ghost of her old dry smile. "The question isn't whether you lied. The question is what you'll do now that you don't have to."

The water shivered.

Not moved — *shivered*, the way a dog shivers off rain, all at once and everywhere, and the reflection of the kneeling dead rippled into ribbons. Cold poured up through Sera's boots, cold like the inside of a bell, cold like the moment before ordination when the elders had lowered her backward into the sea and she had seen, for one endless heartbeat, the face of the god who claimed her: vast and green-dark and patient as famine, with eyes like the places maps give up.

"He's coming up," she said. "Through the foundations. Through the old channels — the cathedral was built over a trench, did you know that? The drowned god's order didn't build here by accident. We built our house on our father's mouth." Her laugh came out cracked. "We thought we were making a sanctuary. We were making a *larder*."

Wren scrambled off the pew and came to stand at Sera's side, which was foolish, and brave, and exactly the sort of thing Sera had come to expect from her. "What does he look like?" the girl whispered.

"Like hunger," Sera said honestly, because she had promised herself she would never lie to these people again, not about anything that mattered. "Like the thing hunger looks like when it stops pretending to be anything else."

The center of the nave began to rise.

It was not dramatic, at first. Just a swelling of the water, a dome lifting, as if something enormous were pushing up gently from below, careful even now, even starving, to keep from spilling. Water sheeted off the dome's crown and rained down in silver threads through the ember-light. And then the dome crested and broke, and the drowned god came through.

Sera had spent twenty years trying not to remember his face and failed completely, because his face was not a face. He was a rising column of green-black water shaped loosely like a man the way a mountain range is shaped loosely like a sleeping king — suggestion, not form. Currents moved through him where veins should be, and in those currents swam things: fish pale as fingernails, drowned birds, a boat no bigger than a coffin, and — Sera's heart seized — small shapes that might once have been people, drifting in him like flies in amber, preserved, unburied, *kept*. His eyes, when they formed, formed out of the dark water itself, two abysses opening, and they found Sera first, because of course they did. She was his. She had always been his.

*LITTLE PRIESTESS,* the god said, and his voice was not sound but pressure, the deep-sea crush of a hundred fathoms arriving all at once, and the candles that weren't drowned guttered along every wall. *YOU HAVE BROUGHT MY DOOR HOME TO ME.*

Behind her, Sera heard Elodie step forward, felt the air change the way it changed before weather — pressure dropping, hair lifting, the taste of ozone and iron. The last storm-caller of Caelmere, standing hip-deep in a drowned church with lightning waiting in her blood.

"The Door isn't yours," Elodie said. "It was never yours. It's a door, father-of-the-deep. Doors open both ways, and everything on the other side is dying. You don't want passage. You want to *keep* them. All of them. Every prayer they ever sent you, every soul you ever answered — hoarded in the dark, forever, so you never have to be the last one alone." Her voice shook, and she kept speaking anyway. Sera loved her for that too. "That's not godhood. That's just grief with power."

*AND WHAT WOULD YOU KNOW OF GRIEF, STORM-CALLER?*

"Everything," Elodie said simply. "I paid ten years for mine. What did you pay for yours?"

The god turned from her. Slowly, terribly, the way a glacier turns. His attention settled back on Sera, and it settled like a hand, like a yoke, like the collar she had worn gladly at nineteen and had never once, in twenty years, managed to take off.

*I AM THE LAST,* he said to her, and for one moment the hunger in him cracked along some older fault line, and underneath it she heard the thing she had always sensed and never named: loneliness, oceanic and total. *THE STORM GOD IS ASH. THE HARVEST MOTHER IS DUST. THE LAUGHING ONE STOPPED LAUGHING BEFORE YOUR GRANDMOTHER WAS BORN. I ALONE REMAIN OF THE OLD ONES, AND I AM SO HUNGRY, LITTLE PRIESTESS. I KEPT THEM ALL. EVERY PRAYER ANSWERED, EVERY DROWNING BLESSED, EVERY SOUL GIVEN TO THE DEEP — I KEPT THEM SO I WOULD NOT BE EMPTY. OPEN MY DOOR. GIVE ME THE THRESHOLD, AND I WILL POUR MYSELF THROUGH INTO THE LIVING LAND, AND I WILL NEVER BE HUNGRY AGAIN.*

"And everyone else will be," Sera said.

Her voice surprised her. It didn't shake at all.

*WHAT?*

"You heard me." She waded forward, one step, two, water dragging at her knees, the sigil burning against her chest like a coal. "You'll pour through, and the living land will learn what it's like to be prayed to by something that doesn't know how to stop taking. I've served you twenty years, my lord. I know you. I've known you since I was a girl kneeling on this floor, and I loved you, I loved you *so much*, and love was the trap, wasn't it? Love was how you kept me." She stopped three paces from the towering dark of him, close enough to see the drowned things swimming in his currents, close enough to see that none of them were struggling. "You're not asking for the door. You're asking for me to hold it open. Because that's what a priestess is, isn't it? A hinge. A servant-shaped gap in the world for you to move through."

*IT IS WHAT YOU WERE MADE FOR.*

"No," Sera said. "It's what I *agreed* to be. When I was nineteen years old and drowning in faith. And agreements—" her hand rose, closed around the bronze sigil, and lifted it from her neck, cord snapping, "—can be unmade."

The god went very still. Stillness, from something that size, was worse than rage.

*THAT CANNOT BE DONE.*

"It can," said Sera. "Because you told me how. Do you remember? On my ordination day, when you marked me? You said: *what is given to the god belongs to the god, and what belongs to the god may be recalled by the god.* You thought you were giving me a comfort. You were giving me instructions." She held the sigil up between them, and the ember-light of the Door ran along its bronze channels and caught fire, a thin white-gold flame, and the whole cathedral seemed to lean in. "My ordination lives in me, my lord. You've carried it all these years — my vow, my name-in-service, my anchor. It's the hook you use to find me anywhere in the world. It's why your voice reaches me and no one else. And it is a *memory*, and memories are tolls, and tolls—" she smiled, and it hurt, and it was the truest expression of her life, "—tolls can be paid. Or refused. Or *spent*."

*SERAIANE.* Her full name in the pressure-voice, the name the elders had sunk her under the waves to find. *IF YOU SPEND IT, YOU ARE NO PRIESTESS. YOU WILL BE NOTHING TO ME. I WILL NOT KNOW YOU. I WILL NOT HEAR YOU. YOU WILL PRAY INTO A SILENCE UNTIL YOU DIE.*

"Yes," Sera said softly. "I know. That's the price."

"Sera." Elodie's hand closed on her shoulder, and Sera could feel the storm trembling in the woman behind her, barely leashed, ready to spend itself against a god if she asked. "There has to be another way. There's always—"

"There isn't, and you know it, and I need you to let go of my shoulder now, because if you don't I'm going to lose my nerve, and I have been losing my nerve for twenty years and I am *done* with it." She reached back and squeezed Elodie's hand, once, hard, a promise compressed into five fingers. "Listen to me. All of you. When I do this, he loses his anchor. He can't hold himself in this shape without it — I'm the reason he fits inside the world at all. He'll unravel. Not die — gods don't die while the Door stands, that's the whole horror of them — but unravel, thin out, become current instead of creature. Long enough. Long enough for Elodie to do what she came to do." She looked at each of them. Corvin at the stairs, bleeding and steady. Wren, chin up, eyes huge and dry through sheer furious will. Elodie, storm-hearted, beloved. "This isn't sacrifice. Don't you dare make it beautiful. This is just the last duty of the last priestess of the drowned god, and I am performing it *badly on purpose*."

*SERA.* The god's voice had changed. The hunger was still there, but beneath it, faintly, horribly, was pleading. *I KEPT YOU TOO. ALL THESE YEARS. EVERY PRAYER YOU EVER SENT ME — DID YOU THINK THEY VANISHED? I HAVE THEM ALL. YOUR FIRST PRAYER, WHEN YOU WERE SIX AND YOUR MOTHER WAS DYING AND YOU ASKED ME TO MAKE THE SEA GIVE HER BACK. I KEPT IT. IT IS WARM IN ME STILL. IF YOU UNMAKE OUR BOND, YOU LOSE IT FOREVER. YOU WILL NOT REMEMBER WHAT IT FELT LIKE TO BE LOVED BY SOMETHING VAST.*

And there it was. The knife turned in the only soft place he'd ever found in her.

Sera closed her eyes. Her mother's face floated up, young again, the year before the fever — salt on her lips from laughing at the shore. *Make the sea give her back.* She had prayed it into the tide pool behind their house, six years old, certain that somewhere the water was listening. And something *had* listened. Something vast and cold had gathered up that small desperate prayer and kept it, the way misers keep coins, the way dragons keep gold, the way lonely things keep anything at all.

"That prayer was never yours," Sera said, opening her eyes. "None of them were. We gave them freely, and you *hoarded* them, and that was never love, my lord. That was just the loneliest kind of theft." She raised the sigil high, white flame streaming from it, and spoke the words she had sworn at nineteen and was about to unspeak at thirty-nine, word by word by word, backwards down the long litany of her life:

"*What is given to the god belongs to the god—*" 

The water began to scream. Every surface of it, the whole drowned nave shrieking in a register below hearing, and the god lunged—

"—and what belongs to the god—"

Corvin roared something. Wren was crying without knowing it. The ember-light of the Door flared blinding, and Elodie's storm broke loose overhead, thunder cracking through the cathedral's drowned ribs like the sky itself tearing—

"—*may be recalled*—"

Sera drove the burning sigil against her own heart.

"—*by the god*," she finished, "and I recall it. I take it back. All of it. My name, my vow, my twenty years, my mother's prayer — *mine*. Given back. Kept. *Mine.*"

She pulled.

It did not feel like pain. It felt like drowning in reverse — like every fathom she'd ever sunk rising through her at once, twenty years of service tearing free of her chest in a single silver thread, and with it came the memories, pouring out of her into the flame: her ordination, the elders' hands lowering her backward into the sea; the first time she'd heard his voice and wept with joy; the thousand prayers she'd offered at a thousand altars; her mother's face; her mother's dying; the six-year-old girl at the tide pool and the warmth of being answered —

She let all of it go into the light. She did not try to keep a single piece. That was the trick of it, the terrible grace of it: a toll only counts if you pay the whole thing. Half-memories bought nothing on any bridge in any world.

The god screamed.

It was not the scream of rage. It was the scream of a house whose foundation stone has been pulled out — structural, vast, hollow. He unraveled from the top down, that mountainous suggestion of a man dissolving into current, currents into eddies, eddies into a thousand separate streams that went writhing away through the flooded cathedral and down through the cracks in the world, thinning, spreading, becoming less a creature and more a weather, more a rumor, more a sadness with nowhere to live. The drowned things in his veins drifted free and sank gently, gently, at last allowed to rest.

Before the last of him thinned past recognition, Sera felt — or imagined she felt — something brush against her mind. Not a voice. Not a word. Just an impression, brief as foam: something that might have been gratitude, or might have been grief, or might have been nothing at all, because gods lie even in their deaths, and she was done listening either way.

Then the nave was only a nave. Flooded, ruined, ember-lit, and empty of everything except the people she loved and the silence where her god had been.

Sera knelt down in the water. She hadn't decided to; her legs had simply resigned. The sigil lay spent in her lap, its bronze channels dark, its hum stilled — an ordinary relic now, or nearly. She was crying, she realized, but her face felt strangely light, unlined, unmarked, like a page scraped clean for writing.

"Sera?" Elodie crouched beside her, storm-light still crawling in her hair. "Are you — what did it cost? Tell me what it cost."

Sera searched herself, carefully, the way you search your pockets after losing your purse. Her ordination: gone. Her god: gone. Her mother's face —

Her mother's face was there.

Faded, softened, the edges blurred like something seen through water — but *there*, hers, kept, paid-for. And beside it a strange new warmth she couldn't source, like sunlight remembered rather than felt, like the knowledge of having once been loved by something vast without any of the details surviving.

She laughed. It came out wrecked and wondering.

"I don't know what I kept," she said. "I paid the whole toll. Everything. And something's still warm in me anyway." She looked up at Elodie, at Wren sliding off the pew to wrap small soaked arms around her neck, at Corvin limping down from the stairs to stand guard over them all out of habit, and the warmth spread, and she understood, finally, what it was. "Oh. Oh, that's what it is. It's *them*. It was never him. Twenty years I thought the vastness was the gift, and the whole time the gift was —"

The ember-light behind them surged.

Every head turned. The seam of fire in the altar wall had begun to sing — the struck-bell hum climbing toward a pitch that vibrated in the marrow — and through the seam, for the first time, they could see movement: shapes pressing against the membrane of the world from the other side, vast and dim and starving, the old dead gods gathering at their locked door, drawn by the unraveling of their brother, moaning in voices like continents shifting.

And above it all, threading through the singing, Sera heard — or the ember-light dreamed she heard — a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Wings. Far out in the black water beyond the cathedral walls. Enormous, laboring, wounded, rising.

"Elodie," Sera breathed, gripping her arm, turning her toward the light, toward the sea, toward the impossible drumbeat approaching through the deep. "The dragon's coming. Whatever you're going to spend — *spend it now.*"
