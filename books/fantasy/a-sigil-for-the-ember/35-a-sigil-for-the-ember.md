## 35. A Sigil for the Ember

The water in the nave had gone still.

That was the first thing Elodie noticed, standing at the threshold of the Door with the drowned god's hunger pressing against the cathedral walls like a tide held back by glass. All through the battle the flood had churned — black water full of silt and bells and the slow-turning shapes of the kneeling dead — but now it lay flat as a mirror, and in it she could see everything: the seam of ember-light beneath the altar, burning gold through fathoms of dark; Wren clinging to a broken pillar with the sigil hugged to her chest; Corvin on the stair above, blade red to the hilt, holding back the imperial host alone; and far below, past the drowned streets, past the sunken harbor, out in the open sea where the water turned from black to bruise to nothing at all — Vaskar.

He was dying. She could feel it now, the way she had learned to feel weather before it broke. The old dragon had fallen into the sea for them, had torn a hole in the warden fleet with the last of his fire, and what remained of him was guttering like a candle in a doorway. His ember — the true one, the one the gods had lit when the world was young and no one had thought to ask how long it would burn — was going out.

And here, humming under her palms, was the Door.

* * *

It did not look like a door. It looked like a mistake in the stone, a hairline crack filled with light the color of a hearth at midnight, warm and patient and utterly indifferent to her. The sigil fit into a hollow at its center the way a key fits a lock it was made for, and Elodie had already felt it want to turn. All morning it had wanted to turn. The gods on the other side were not pounding; they were *pulling*, gently, the way the sea pulls at a swimmer who has stopped kicking, and Sera's god — vast, drowning, famine-shaped — was pulling hardest of all.

"Elodie." Maren's voice came down the nave, ragged, from where she stood braced across the warden line with a shield wall of her own soldiers pushing against her. Her armor was scored, her warden's white gone gray with ash and spray. "Whatever you're going to do — do it now."

"You knew," Elodie said. Not loudly. The water carried it anyway. "Ten years ago. You knew the storm wasn't mine."

Maren didn't flinch. That was the worst of it — she had never once flinched, not at the trial, not here. "I knew," she said. "And I'd testify to it again, if testifying again would keep you alive long enough to finish this. Now *move*."

Elodie looked at her — at the girl who had shared her bread and her secrets and her bedroll through six years of apprenticeship, who had stood up in a marble hall and called her a storm-caller and a killer with tears running down her face, who had done it because the emperor's purges took storm-callers whole and exile only took ten years — and found that there was nothing left in her that needed saying. Some debts could not be paid in words. They could only be paid forward.

She turned back to the Door.

Wren reached her first, wading through water that came to the child's ribs, holding the sigil up with both hands. Her face was streaked with soot and salt and something older than either. "You're going to spend it," Wren said. It wasn't a question. Children heard everything, Elodie was learning. Children heard the things adults arranged their voices to hide.

"I'm going to spend all of it," Elodie said.

"All of it all of it?"

"All of it all of it."

Wren considered this with the terrible gravity of an eight-year-old being asked to accept the unacceptable. Then she thrust the sigil at her. "Then you take my prayer too. I've got one saved up. For rain, when we get home. You can put it in."

Elodie's throat closed. She took the sigil. Its bronze was warm as a living thing, and it hummed against her palm like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. "I'll put it in," she promised. "First thing."

Behind Wren, Sera knelt in the shallows where her god had left her, emptied. Whatever the drowned god had taken when she unmade her ordination, it had taken cleanly — Elodie could see the hole in her the way you can see where a tooth used to be, a absence shaped exactly like faith. But Sera lifted her head, and her eyes, though hollowed, were clear.

"The Door will offer you everything," Sera said. "It offered me my god. It will offer you your power back — all of it, more than they took, more than you ever had. That is how doors work. They show you the room you most want and let you believe the threshold is free."

"And if I don't want the room?"

Sera smiled — small, cracked, real. "Then you are the first person in three hundred years to stand at this door honestly. Spend it well, Dragonkeeper."

* * *

Elodie stepped into the threshold.

The ember-light took her the way water takes a diver — not violently, but completely. And there, in the space between the living land and the dead one, the Door kept its promise. It showed her the room.

She saw herself at nineteen, arms raised over Caelmere, the sky answering her like a lover. She saw the Court Storm-Caller's tower with its windows open to every wind. She saw power pouring into her without limit or cost, storms queuing up to be born at her word, lightning lying down for her like dogs. She saw the emperor's crown, offered. She saw the valley's wards dissolved by decree rather than choice, her name restored, her record burned, forty deaths officially reassigned to the weather itself. She saw everything she had lost and everything she had been told she'd never have again, arranged around her like a garden in bloom.

*Come through,* said the Door, in a voice made of every answered prayer since the beginning. *Come through, and be owed nothing ever again.*

"No," Elodie said.

The light went very still.

"I don't want to come through." She planted her feet on the threshold, one foot in each world, and felt the seam of it bite into her like a blade laid flat between bone. "I want to make a withdrawal."

*There is nothing here you may take.*

"There's one thing." She raised the sigil. "You've been grasping. Starving. Answering prayers you have no strength to answer, killing villages with kindness, flooding valleys with oceans that forgot how to be seas. You're dying, and you're so afraid of dying that you'll drag the whole living world through this door with you just so you won't go alone." The words came out of her steady and cold, and she recognized their temperature — it was the temperature of the eye of a storm, the calm she had been exiled for, the calm that had never actually been hers. "So here is my offer. One last answer. The biggest prayer anyone has ever prayed."

*Speak.*

"Not for me." She closed her eyes and reached — down, out, through the flooded city, through the harbor bar, into the deep green dark where something ancient and sardonic and kind lay bleeding out among the ruins of a fleet he had broken for people he had known for barely a season. "For him."

* * *

Calling a storm, she had learned at seventeen, was not shouting. Everyone thought it was shouting — arms up, voice raised, the sky bullied into obedience. But Maren's grandmother had taught her better, in the years before any of it, in the kitchen of a house that no longer existed: *a storm isn't summoned, love. It's agreed with. You find the part of the sky that already wants to fall, and you tell it yes.*

Elodie had spent ten years telling the sky no.

Now, standing in the threshold with the sigil burning and the Door straining against her like a horse against a halter, she opened every gate she had ever built inside herself, all of them at once, and told the sky *yes*.

They came the way debts come due.

Every storm she had ever been owed. Every squall that had gathered over the Hollow Valley and broken apart against the ward because magic could not follow her there. Every thunderhead that had built over Caelmere in ten years of her silence and had nowhere to put its anger. Every rain she had watched fall on other people's fields through other people's windows. The storm that killed forty people and had never been hers — that came too, huge and grieving, the gods' own death-convulsion finally coming home to the body it had borrowed. And underneath all of them, oldest and quietest, a girl's saved-up prayer for rain, folded small as a seed.

They poured into her and she did not hoard a drop.

This was the lesson Vaskar had taught her in the crater's shadow, in the last conversation they'd had before he flew south: *We hoard because we fear the dark after the spending. But fire is not coin, little keeper. Fire is breath. You do not die of breathing out. You only die if you refuse to breathe in again — and that refusal, not the spending, is the true death.* He had coughed smoke when he laughed at his own wisdom, and the laugh had sounded like a landslide deciding to be gentle.

*Spend the fire,* he had said. *Don't hoard it.*

So she spent it.

The storm came down through the broken roof of the drowned cathedral in a column wide as the nave, and the water screamed, and the bells rang all at once, and the dead congregation rose from their knees — not in wrath, but in witness, turning their drowned faces upward toward the light like flowers toward a sun they had waited centuries to see. Above, she dimly knew, Maren was roaring orders, Corvin was bleeding on the stair, the imperial host was breaking, the world was ending or beginning — but all of that happened at a great distance, the way weather happens to a mountain. Elodie stood in the eye of everything she had ever been denied and gave it all away.

Because she was not opening the Door.

The Door strained. The gods pulled. The seam of ember-light widened a finger's width, then another, hungry to be a gateway, and Elodie leaned her whole weight against it — against the opening, against the hunger, against three hundred years of prayers answered wrong — and held it shut. Held it the way you hold a wound closed. Held it while the storm tore through her and out of her, and the sigil drank the lightning and the lightning drank the storm and the storm drank ten years of exile, and what gathered in the bronze at the center of it all was not power.

It was light. Ember-light. The Door's own fire, drawn up out of the seam like iron from a forge, condensed and concentrated and *carried* — every spark the old gods had ever fed it, every calorie of divinity that had leaked through the crack in the world, gathered now into a single coal no bigger than a heart.

A sigil for the ember.

*What are you doing,* said the Door, and for the first time its voice was afraid.

"What you should have done centuries ago," Elodie gasped. "Giving it back."

She turned, within the threshold, toward the sea.

* * *

The deep water beyond the harbor was black and enormous, and somewhere in it Vaskar was sinking, and she could feel him the way you feel a hand gone numb — by its absence, by the terrible effort of remembering it was there. His ember was a spark in a wind tunnel now. Minutes, maybe. Less.

*You cannot,* said the Door. *The distance—*

"I'm a storm-caller," Elodie said, and for the first time in ten years, maybe for the first time in her life, she said it without flinching. "Distance is a rumor."

She threw the ember.

Not with her arm. With the storm. She wrapped the coal in the last spiral of the last storm — the girl's saved-up prayer for rain, because Wren had been right, because children always are, because the purest thing in her whole arsenal was an eight-year-old's wish for her garden — and she hurled it out through the shattered roof, over the drowned streets, over the harbor bar, down through fathom after fathom of cold and crushing dark, a single point of gold falling like a star that had changed its mind about the sky.

And Elodie Eastwick stood in the threshold of the Door of Caelmere, holding it shut with everything she had left, which was almost nothing, and watched her last fire sink into the sea.

The storm emptied. The lightning finished. The great column of wind and water thinned to a breeze, the breeze to a stillness, and the stillness spread outward through the nave like a held breath. In the sudden quiet Elodie could hear everything: Maren shouting for the wardens to fall back, Corvin laughing on the stair — actually laughing, cracked and disbelieving — Sera singing something low and wordless that might have been a funeral rite or a lullaby, Wren screaming her name, small and furious and alive.

The sigil went dark in her hands. Spent. A plain bronze disc, cool as riverstone, worth nothing to any god or emperor ever again.

Her knees buckled. The threshold caught her — or she fell out of it, she couldn't tell — and she came to rest in the shallow water before the altar with the Door's seam narrowing above her, closing, the pull of the starving gods fading from a hurricane to a sigh. Through the shrinking gap of light she glimpsed them: vast, dim, innumerable, the old gods of Caelmere turning at last away from the living world, not dragged but *released*, carrying whatever scraps of answered prayer they'd managed to gather like beggars carrying coins to a country no map had ever named.

The seam closed. The ember-light went out. The Door of Caelmere became, in a single silent instant, only stone.

Elodie lay in the flooded nave and felt the shape of everything she had given away, and found — to her astonishment — that the shape was not empty. It was simply quiet. Quiet the way the valley was quiet. Quiet the way a house is quiet when everyone you love is home.

Then the sea lit up.

Far out past the harbor, down in the deep where she had thrown it, a glow bloomed — small at first, a lantern, a candle, a coal — and then it *caught*. Light climbed through the water in branching veins like lightning learning to be gentle, and the whole bay turned the color of dawn, and the pressure wave came rolling in over the drowned city and rocked the cathedral on its foundations, and out of the sea, streaming water like a river giving birth, rose—

* * *

Rose something on fire.

Elodie got to her knees in the shallows. Around her the others were rising too — Wren splashing toward her, Sera standing with her ruined face tilted up, Maren frozen mid-order with her sword half-drawn, Corvin leaning in the broken doorway with blood on his teeth and wonder on his face like a man watching a miracle he intended to deny to his grave.

The shape climbed out of the bay on wings that blotted out the rain-clouds she herself had called, and the clouds caught fire from beneath, and the whole sky over the drowned capital turned to hammered copper. Water sheeted off scales the size of barn doors. And the roar that came out of him was not the sardonic rasp she knew, not the guttering cough of a dying thing — it was *young*. It was the sound a volcano makes the first time, the sound the world made before anything had names.

Vaskar turned in the burning air, and his eyes — old, sardonic, impossibly fond — found hers across the ruined nave, across the water, across ten years of exile and one impossible night.

"Well," the last dragon said, and his voice rolled through the stones like a bell being forgiven. "*That* was a withdrawal worth making, little keeper."

Behind her, in the sealed and silent stone of the altar, something ticked once — softly, like a cooling hearth — and went out forever.

Above the cathedral, wings wide against the copper sky, Vaskar opened his mouth and set the rain itself alight.
