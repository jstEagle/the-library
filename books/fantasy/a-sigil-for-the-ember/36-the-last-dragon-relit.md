## 36. The Last Dragon Relit

Dying, Vaskar had decided long ago, was mostly a matter of bookkeeping.

He lay in the cold green dark at the bottom of the sea with his ledger nearly closed. He knew the exact weight of what remained to him — a little less each hour, like sand through a cracked claw. The ember in his chest had guttered down to a coin of heat no larger than a sparrow's heart, and he had learned to lie very still around it, the way an old man learns to lie still around his last good memory. Above him the water pressed down in its patient tons. Around him the wreck of a warden stronghold was still settling, stone sighing against stone, and somewhere in that ruin were the bodies of men he had killed on purpose and one great wound in his own flank that he had earned honestly.

He was not afraid. That surprised him a little. He had expected fear — he had counseled others about it often enough, in the long centuries when pilgrims still climbed to his crater and left offerings of honey and iron. But fear required a future worth dreading the loss of, and Vaskar's future had shrunk to the width of this dark water and the slow arithmetic of his failing fire.

Then the ember-light came down.

It came the way dawn comes to deep valleys — not arriving so much as being remembered by the world. A seam of gold split the black above him, thin as a hair, then wider, then wider, and Vaskar felt it before he understood it: warmth. Not the borrowed, secondhand warmth of sun filtered through fathoms of sea, but *his* warmth, his own old fire coming home, and riding inside it a signature he knew better than his own name.

The storm-caller. Little Elodie Eastwick, who tended bees and avoided his crater and flinched whenever lightning showed on the horizon of her face. She was standing somewhere far above him in a threshold between worlds, holding open a door that should have killed her, and she was spending — all of it, everything she had ever been owed, every storm the dying gods had ever poured through her young shoulders — she was spending it downward. Toward him.

*Foolish girl,* he thought, and the thought came out of him as grief, because foolishness like hers had another name, and he had spent four hundred years pretending he didn't know what it was.

* * *

The light reached him in threads.

They came through the water like the roots of some impossible tree, golden filaments swaying in the current, and where they touched his hide they did not burn — they *recognized*. Vaskar felt them find the old channels, the fire-ways that ran through him the way rivers run through countries, dry for so long he'd half forgotten their shape. One thread slid into the channel along his neck and he remembered, all at once and completely, the smell of the first morning of the world: hot stone, wet clay, the ozone-sweet air before the first storm ever broke.

Another thread found the channel beneath his wing and he remembered flight — real flight, the kind dragons had done before the sky grew small and sad, climbing until the air itself thinned to song.

Another found the wound in his flank and closed it, not healing so much as deciding the wound had never been permitted, and the pain that had been his constant companion since the stronghold fell folded itself away like a letter.

*Hold it,* he heard her say. No — he didn't hear it. The words arrived inside the light itself, woven into its grain, because she was past speaking now, past anything so small as a voice. She was a storm wearing the shape of a nineteen-year-old exile who had once, ten years ago, stood at the rim of his crater and shouted down at him that she hadn't done it, she hadn't called that storm, and she would die before she claimed it.

She had been telling the truth the whole time. He had known it even then. Dragons are poor liars and worse listeners, but they are excellent knowers, and he had looked into her furious tear-streaked face and known the storm had not been hers. He had said nothing. It had not been his secret to tell, and besides — he had wanted, in his old sardonic way, to see whether the world would break her or make her. It had tried very hard to do the first. It had accidentally done the second.

*Hold it,* the light said again, and Vaskar, who had held nothing but his own dying for three hundred years, held.

The ember came last.

It detached from the great golden seam above — a single spark, falling up through the water against every law the sea owned, drifting down to him with the unhurried grace of something that has crossed the distance between life and death and is unimpressed by either. It settled against his chest, over the coin of old heat, and paused there a moment, and Vaskar would have sworn — he would have sworn on his wings, on his name, on the grave of every dragon who had ever called him brother — that it hesitated. That it considered him. That it asked.

*Yes,* he answered, in the wordless tongue of fire answering fire. *Yes. I am here. I am still here.*

The ember went in.

* * *

What happened then cannot be told in the order it happened, because it all happened at once, the way a struck bell makes every part of its sound simultaneously.

His heart, which had beaten perhaps once an hour for a century — a slow funeral drum — caught the ember and lurched into rhythm. Fire followed it, pouring down the old channels the threads had reopened, filling him the way spring flood fills a riverbed that everyone had forgotten was a riverbed. His veins lit gold. His scales, dull as river stones for three hundred years, flushed color from within — deep red first, then copper, then the fierce bright orange of a forge at full anger. His wings, folded and salt-stiff against the seabed, unfurled with a crack of displaced water, and every membrane glowed faintly along its bones like stained glass behind candlelight.

And his eyes — his eyes, which had seen dimly for so long, which had watched the world fade to gray at the edges like an etching worn by thumbs — his eyes *blazed*, and the whole drowned ruin of the stronghold leapt into view, every stone, every drifting silt-mote, every silver thread of the storm still pouring down from the seam above.

Vaskar roared.

The sea took the sound and made it into a thing of pressure and wonder, a shockwave that rolled outward across the ocean floor, and fish that had never known his name would tell the story of that vibration in their blood for six generations. He rose. He rose the way the sun rises, inexorably, gathering speed, his claws closing on nothing and finding purchase on the water itself because a dragon fully alight does not swim, he *ascends*, and the ocean parted around his burning body and boiled to steam in a widening ring.

He breached the surface like the end of the world and the beginning of one, and the night sky received him, and he climbed.

Oh, he *climbed.* Wings full of fire, lungs full of storm-air, the ember seated in his chest like a king returned to a throne left empty too long — he climbed until the sea below was a black mirror and the clouds were a country he could walk through, and there he leveled off and turned, and looked back down at what Elodie Eastwick was doing with her one wild life.

* * *

The Door stood open in the flooded cathedral, and from this height, through the shattered roof, it looked exactly as it should: a seam of ember-light in the floor of the world, widened now into a standing doorway of gold. And the old gods were going through it.

Vaskar watched them pass, and something in him — something older than his fire, older than his grief — finally unclenched.

They did not go the way he had always feared the dead go, dragged and dissolving. They went the way guests go home after a long, long evening: slowly, gratefully, with the tired dignity of the very old. He saw the Rain-Mother fold her gray vastness through the golden seam like a woman wringing out her hair one final time. He saw the Hearth-Father pause at the threshold and look back — not at the world, Vaskar thought, but at some hearth in particular, some specific kitchen in some specific village, some small human warmth he had warmed himself beside for ten thousand years without ever being asked in — and then step through, and the seam accepted him gently, and he was gone. He saw smaller shapes, godlings and spirits and the half-remembered guardians of wells and thresholds and birthing-hours, streaming through like sparks rising from a bonfire, each one winking out into the gold and the gold growing no smaller for receiving them.

*Answered at last,* Vaskar thought. That was the truth of it, the terrible simple truth Elodie had carried alone for ten years without knowing it. The gods had not come back to conquer. They had come back because prayers are food, and they were starving, and dying things grasp. Every answered prayer across Caelmere had been a hand reaching up from a drowning creature. And now someone had finally reached *down*, and taken hold, and was walking them home.

Released at last. There was mercy in it, and the mercy was the hardest part to bear. He had spent three centuries assuming the age of gods would end in violence — theirs or the world's. Instead it ended in a girl's arms, holding a door, saying with her whole spent self: *you can rest now. You did enough. Go.*

The last of them through was the drowned god.

Vaskar felt the sea shudder as it passed — the vast famine-shaped presence that had eaten its own priestess's faith and demanded more, the god Sera Ivane had unmade her ordination to starve. Even diminished, even anchorless, it filled the flooded nave like ink fills glass. It fought, briefly, at the threshold. Vaskar saw the golden seam bend inward, darken at the edges, and he opened his mouth and added his fire to the wind above the marsh — one long deliberate breath of dragonflame across the water, not as a weapon but as a witness, the oldest blessing his kind knew: *I see you ending, and I do not look away.*

Whether it helped, he could not say. But the darkness thinned. The seam straightened. And the drowned god went through last, the way the host of a feast goes through last, having devoured everything including its own table.

Then the door began to close.

* * *

It closed the way a wound closes — from the edges inward, golden light drawing down to a seam, the seam to a line, the line to a scar. And Vaskar, wheeling above the marsh with his new-old fire roaring in his chest, saw the figure standing in the narrowing threshold and understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with flight, that she was still holding it.

That was how it worked. He had told her so himself, in his crater, five chapters of her life ago: *opening the door will kill whatever opens it.* He had meant it as warning. He had not imagined she would hear it as instructions.

*Let go, little storm,* he thought fiercely, diving. *Let it close. Let go.*

But the door was not crushing her. That was the miracle buried in the miracle, the thing he would only understand later, when she told it haltingly, embarrassed, as if reporting a debt: the storms she spent did not open the door for the gods. They carried the door's ember-*light* out of it — out of the dying world, down through the sea, into him. She had turned the doorway itself into a wick, and poured it, and the door had nothing left to be a door *with*. She had not held it open against its will. She had emptied it. And an empty door does not kill its keeper. It simply goes out, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is spent, and the keeper stands in the dark afterward, alive, hollowed, weeping, ordinary.

The seam closed to a line. The line closed to a thread. And in the last instant before it sealed, Vaskar — blazing, ascending, impossibly alive — saw her silhouette raise one hand. Not to hold. To wave.

Goodbye, the gesture said. Goodbye, you old hungry beautiful fading things. Thank you for the rain. Thank you for the harvests. I'm sorry we stopped believing in you politely.

The thread of gold winked out.

The sea settled over the drowned cathedral like a sheet drawn over a sleeping face, and the waters did not churn, and the bells did not ring, and for the first time in ten thousand years the old gods of Caelmere were nowhere in the world — not hiding, not starving, not grasping. Just gone. Gently. Answered at last, released at last.

Vaskar roared again, and this roar was different. This one was farewell.

* * *

He flew until dawn found him.

Not toward anything — away from the sealing, out over the coast, up the spine of the country that no longer existed on any map. His shadow ran along the hills below him, huge and winged and whole, and he watched it with the greedy delight of a beggar given back his own hands. Fire sat in him the way it had sat in his youth: not a coin now but a treasury, banked and breathing, warming every channel, every joint, every ridge of his spine. He tested it cautiously — a breath of flame at altitude, a short ribbon of gold in the dark air — and it answered instantly, eagerly, and he laughed, and the laugh came out as smoke, and the smoke smelled of cedar and thunderstorms and home.

He was not young. He would never be young; three hundred years of guttering had left their architecture in him, and his joints would speak of cold mornings for whatever centuries remained. But he was *alight*, and aliveness, he discovered, is not a quantity but a quality. A candle and a bonfire contain the same element. Tonight he was the bonfire.

Below him, at the edge of the saltmarsh, he saw lights — a campfire, small and stubborn, four figures around it. He could have passed over. He almost did; dragons are proud creatures, and there was a part of him that wished his first return to company might come later, when he had preened and circled and composed himself into dignity.

Instead he descended.

He came down through the last of the night softly, for a dragon, folding his wings a hundred yards out and walking the final stretch with his head low, so as not to frighten. It did not work, of course. Wren shot upright and pointed and made a sound that was half scream and half cheer. Sera pressed both hands over her mouth. The big inquisitor — Corvin, the one who smelled perpetually of guilt and bad gardening — stumbled backward over his own sword.

And Elodie Eastwick, storm-caller, exiled at nineteen, spent to her last lightning and somehow sitting upright by the fire anyway, looked up at him with eyes red-rimmed and absolutely dry, and said, in a voice scraped down to gravel:

"You're supposed to be dead."

"Madam," Vaskar said, settling onto the marsh grass with the care of an ancient creature lowering himself into a chair he intends never to leave, "I am four thousand years old. I have been supposed to be dead since before your grandmother's grandmother invented disappointment. I have merely, at last, gotten around to contradicting the supposition."

Wren laughed first. Then Sera, wetly. Then, helplessly, Elodie herself, and the sound of it did something to the ember in his chest that fire had not done — settled it deeper, warmer, as though laughter were also fuel, as though belonging burned cleaner than glory ever had.

He folded his wings at last and let the dawn come up on his scales, and looked at the four of them — his keeper's descendant, his keeper's priestess, his keeper's orphan, his keeper's reluctant soldier — and understood that this, too, was part of the ledger. Not the dying of things, but the keeping of them.

It was Elodie who finally broke the quiet, leaning forward, firelight and dragonlight mingling on her exhausted face.

"Vaskar," she said slowly, "when I was in the threshold — the door showed me things. About the storm. About Maren." Her jaw tightened. "There's something I have to understand before I can face her. And I think—" she met his ancient eyes steadily, "—I think you've known it all along."

The ember pulsed once in his chest, and Vaskar, who had kept other people's secrets for four thousand years, sighed out a long curl of smoke and prepared, at last, to spend one of his own.
