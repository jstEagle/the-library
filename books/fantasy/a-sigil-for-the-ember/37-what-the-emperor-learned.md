## 37. What the Emperor Learned

Corvin Ashe had knelt in the Amber Court a hundred times before, and he had never once looked up. That was the discipline of it — the whole architecture of the place was built to make looking up feel like theft. The ceiling alone was worth a man's life: forty feet of gilded coffering painted with the Ascension, the god-emperor Aurelian rising on wings of hammered light while the old gods fell around him like cut marionettes. Every supplicant who entered the Court was meant to feel that painting pressing down on them, the weight of a divinity already achieved, already finished, already beyond question.

Corvin had spent eleven years not looking up.

Today he looked.

It was not defiance. It was simply that something in him had broken loose on the stairs of the drowned cathedral, some final bolt sheared away when he turned his blade against his own brothers-in-white, and what remained of Corvin Ashe no longer possessed the machinery for reverence. He stood among the petitioners and provincial lords and hooded inquisitors with his hands still bandaged where the warden steel had bitten them, and he raised his eyes to the dais, and he saw.

The god-emperor's light was guttering.

He could not have said how he knew. There was no dramatic dimming, no smoke, no crack running through the marble. Aurelian sat enthroned as he always sat, robed in cloth-of-gold so heavy it took two attendants to arrange the train, his face composed into the mild, distant benevolence that had been drilled into him by whatever college had manufactured his divinity. The crown of nine suns rested on his brow. The Court breathed around him in its usual rhythm — the rustle of silk, the murmur of translators, the soft footfalls of the Silent Guard.

But the light was wrong.

Corvin had spent years tracking resonance. He knew what living power looked like from the outside: the way it bent air, the way candles leaned toward it without flame, the way birds went quiet or loud depending on its temper. He had felt the sigil hum across three hundred miles of ghost-road like a struck bell that would not finish dying. And now, watching the emperor hold court, he recognized the absence of all of it. The candles did not lean. The air did not bend. Aurelian sat in his gold like a man sitting in gold, and the divinity that had draped him for twenty-two years had become exactly what it had always been beneath the drapery:

A coat. An ill-fitting one, worn too long, and coming apart at the seams.

* * *

The audience itself was brief, and Corvin understood immediately that it was designed to be witnessed rather than conducted.

"Lord Inquisitor." The chamberlain's voice carried the particular hollowness of a man reciting words he had spoken ten thousand times. "You are summoned to account for the matter of the Door of Caelmere."

Corvin came forward across the amber floor, each step ringing faintly, and knelt — because his knees still remembered how even if nothing else did.

"Report."

He gave it plainly. The cathedral flooded and lost. The sigil spent — he chose those words carefully, *spent*, as though it were coin dropped down a well rather than fire carried up out of hell by a woman he had once loved. The old gods gone quiet. The door sealed. Vaskar risen and blazing over the northern sea, visible now on clear nights from the coastal provinces, a red star with wings, and the pilgrimages already beginning despite every edict against them.

He did not mention Maren Voss. He did not mention the hour's head start he had given in Ondrel, or the inquisitors bleeding out on the cathedral stair, or the fact that his own orders — *burn what answers, question nothing* — had died somewhere between Grayford Bridge and the waterline, killed quietly, without ceremony, by a man who had finally allowed himself to ask a question.

The silence after his report lasted long enough for Corvin to count his own heartbeats. Nine of them. Then the emperor spoke.

"So the gods are gone."

Not a question. A statement delivered to the Court at large, pitched for the galleries, for the record-keepers, for history. *So the gods are gone,* said in the tone of a king announcing a harvest.

And Corvin, kneeling, looking up past protocol for the first time in eleven years, watched the emperor's face as he said it — and saw the fear come through the paint of it like water through lime plaster.

* * *

Afterward they kept him waiting in the antechamber, which was itself a message; emperors did not wait on inquisitors, but gods did not wait on anyone at all, and the distinction apparently still mattered to someone. Corvin sat on a bench of black oak beneath a tapestry of the Purges and let his bandaged hands rest open on his knees.

The antechamber was not empty. A boy waited there too — six, perhaps seven, dressed in the plain grey of a temple foundling, clutching a wooden bird with chipped wings. Some nobleman's charity case, summoned to be blessed, to be shown off, to be returned to the cloister with a story about touching the hem of divinity. The boy stared at Corvin's white coat with the frank terror of childhood and edged, very slowly, along the wall to put maximum distance between them.

Corvin almost smiled. Eleven years ago children had run *toward* inquisitors. There had been a time when the white coat meant safety, when the Order of Ash had been sold to the provinces as shepherds instead of wolves. He wondered which decade the boy's grandparents had been born into, and whether they'd told him stories about the burning years, and whether any of those stories had been true.

"You can sit," Corvin said. "I don't bite."

The boy did not sit. But he stopped retreating, which Corvin supposed was the most any of them could hope for now — not trust, merely the cessation of flight.

They waited together, the wolf and the sparrow, for the better part of an hour. And it was there, in the antechamber, that Corvin began to understand what he would later call the lesson — though *lesson* was too clean a word for it, too pedagogical, too much like something learned on purpose. What happened in that room was less a lesson than a collapse, the slow structural failure of a building everyone had agreed to pretend was sound.

Because the doors opened early. Because the chamberlain came out flushed and whispering to the under-clerks, and the under-clerks went pale, and someone ran — actually *ran*, in the Amber Palace, where running was a flogging offense — down the corridor toward the College of Radiance. And through the doors, left ajar in the confusion, Corvin heard the sound that would stay with him longer than anything else from that day.

The emperor was praying.

Loudly. In the old tongue, the dead tongue, the tongue of Caelmere-before-Caelmere, the language of the gods his ascension had supposedly supplanted. Corvin knew enough of it from confiscated liturgies to catch the shape of the words without their meaning, and the shape was unmistakable. It was begging. It was the begging of a man who has just learned that the well is dry and is shouting down it anyway, *please, please, please*, the vowels cracking, the consonants dissolving, dignity abandoned somewhere back on the far side of the throne room doors.

The boy with the wooden bird looked at Corvin with wide eyes.

"Is the emperor hurt?" he whispered.

Corvin considered the question with more care than it deserved.

"No," he said finally. "I think he's just learned something."

* * *

They admitted him again at dusk.

The Court had been cleared — cleared of petitioners, cleared of lords, cleared even of most of the Silent Guard, which told Corvin everything about how bad it was, because the Silent Guard were the emperor's eyes and ears and you did not dismiss your own eyes unless you feared what they'd see. Only a skeleton remained: the chamberlain, two under-clerks clutching their ledgers like life vests, the Grand Radiant of the College, and a handful of senior inquisitors standing in their ranks with the rigid stillness of men pretending not to smell smoke.

Aurelian stood at the window.

That was wrong too. Emperors sat; gods presided; neither stood at windows like a merchant watching for a ship. But there he was, his cloth-of-gold dragging unheeded across the floor, his crown slightly askew — *slightly askew*, and no attendant daring to straighten it — looking north through the glass toward a horizon Corvin knew held nothing but darkening sea and, far out at the edge of sight, on nights when the air was clean, a glow like a banked forge.

"He's awake," the emperor said, without turning. "The dragon. You saw it."

"I saw it, Radiance."

"And the door is shut. And the gods—" His voice caught, a small terrible hitch, and recovered. "The gods are quiet."

"Quiet," Corvin agreed. It was kinder than *gone*. It was also, he suspected, truer. He had stood in the drowned nave. He had felt the moment the old gods passed through the sealing — not destroyed, not banished, but *answered*, released, the way a fever breaks or a held breath lets go. Whatever remained of them now was quiet the way the dead are quiet: completely, and without suffering.

"They were answering prayers," Aurelian said. Still to the window. "For months. Did you know that? Little prayers. Rain for crops. Fever-breaks. Safe roads. My censors burned four hundred letters about it and I approved every burning personally, and I told myself it was heresy, and it wasn't heresy, it was—" He stopped. His reflection in the darkening glass looked older than the man casting it, older than the empire, older than the lie. "It was hunger. They were starving, and reaching, and I spent twenty-two years telling the world there was nothing left to reach for."

The Grand Radiant stepped forward, robes whispering. "Radiance, the College counsels restraint. The manifestation of the dragon is a localized phenomenon. The people's faith in the Ascension remains—"

"The people saw a god relit over the northern sea," Aurelian said, "and they will see my face tomorrow at the Rite of First Light, and they will compare." He turned from the window at last, and Corvin saw it fully then, the thing he had been circling all day, the truth underneath the guttering: the emperor was not afraid of rebellion, or of Maren's wardens, or of the dragon, or even of the gods.

He was afraid of being *ordinary*.

Twenty-two years ago, a clever man — Corvin had read the suppressed histories; there had been a man, there was always a man — had taken a soldier-prince with a talent for theater and a kingdom exhausted by the old gods' wars, and had dressed him in light. Not real light. Borrowed light. Reflected light, the way the moon wears the sun. And it had worked, because the world wanted it to work, because terror of the old gods had made the counterfeit look like salvation. But borrowed light has a term. And tonight, with the genuine article blazing over the sea and the sigil spent and the last prayer answered, the loan had come due.

"Lord Ashe." The emperor's eyes found him, and Corvin saw the calculation begin behind them, quick and desperate, the old machinery grinding toward its habitual solution: find the threat, name the threat, burn the threat. "Your report mentions the storm-caller. Eastwick. She stood at the threshold. She held the door."

"She did."

"She could have opened it. For herself. For the gods. Power beyond anything the Ascension ever pretended to." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Why do you suppose she didn't?"

And there it was. The question. *Question nothing* — the first law of the Order, inked into his ordination vows at nineteen, the law he had obeyed through Ondrel and Grayford Bridge and the drowning nave until obedience had cost him everything except himself. The emperor was asking it now, and asking it wrongly, and Corvin understood with a clarity that felt almost like grief that this was the whole lesson, the entire curriculum of the collapsing court: Aurelian had ruled for twenty-two years without once asking why anyone served him, because the answer had never mattered while the light held.

Now the light was failing, and he needed the answer, and there was no one left to give it to him honestly.

Except, perhaps, a traitor with bandaged hands.

"Because she had something better," Corvin said.

The silence rang. One of the under-clerks made a small sound, like a mouse deciding whether to run.

"Better," the emperor repeated. "Than godhood."

"Than *power*, Radiance. They're not the same thing. She had people. A girl who wasn't afraid of her. Friends who stayed when staying was expensive." Corvin heard his own voice go quiet. "She was offered everything you're wearing, and she chose bees."

Someone should have struck him for that. In the old days someone would have. Corvin watched the emperor's face for the blow to fall — the signal, the guard, the white-hot flash of borrowed wrath — and instead saw something worse.

He saw understanding arrive.

It crossed Aurelian's face like weather, like the shadow of a cloud over a field: the sudden, naked comprehension of a man who has built an empire of fear and just been told, in one sentence, by his own disgraced inquisitor, that fear was never the only currency. That there had been another coin all along, common as dirt, and he had never minted a single piece of it, and now the banks of heaven were closed and his vaults were full of nothing.

"Get out," the emperor said softly.

*Radiance—* began the Grand Radiant.

"All of you. Out."

* * *

What happened next, Corvin watched from the doorway, because he was slow to leave, or fast, depending on how you judged it — slow in body, having turned at the threshold, and fast in spirit, having already gone somewhere the palace could not follow.

The Court emptied. The clerks fled with their ledgers. The Grand Radiant withdrew in a rustle of offended silk. The last of the Silent Guard filed out, and their boots on the amber floor sounded, Corvin thought, like rain ending.

And the god-emperor of Caelmere, alone in his hollow court beneath a painting of his own invented glory, knelt down on the floor.

Not in prayer. Corvin knew prayer when he saw it; he had heard enough of it that morning through the doors, the old tongue cracking with need. This was different. This was smaller. Aurelian knelt the way a man kneels when his legs simply stop agreeing to hold him — hands flat on the amber, crown sliding forward, cloth-of-gold pooling around him like molten wax gone cold. The light that had never truly lived in him flickered once more across the coffered ceiling, a last reflection of a last reflection, and went out so gently that only someone watching for it would have known.

Then the emperor began, very quietly, to laugh. Or to weep. From the doorway, with the dusk deepening in the high windows, Corvin could not tell the difference, and suspected that by now neither could Aurelian.

Corvin stood with his hand on the door.

Here was the moment. Here was the hinge on which the whole rotten day balanced, and he felt it turn under his palm: because the guards were gone, and the Radiant was gone, and the man on the floor was only a man, and a man could be saved. A cup of water. A hand under the arm. A word — *Radiance, let me help you to your chambers* — the small mercies that keep a fallen thing alive to fall another day. Eleven years of service said *kneel beside him*. The Order of Ash said *protect the vessel of the Ascension at any cost*.

Corvin thought of a market in Ondrel, and an hour's head start, and how little good either had done him until he stopped counting the cost.

He thought of Elodie Eastwick, spending everything, keeping nothing, choosing the hard ordinary thing.

He took his hand off the door.

"Water's in the ewer by the couch," Corvin said. "If you want it. It won't answer prayers. Nothing will, now. That's not a punishment, Radiance. It's the first honest morning this country's had in twenty-two years."

The laughing-weeping stopped. The emperor lifted his head, and his eyes — ordinary brown eyes, Corvin saw, just a man's eyes, they had probably always been just a man's eyes — fixed on him with something that was not yet hatred and might, given time and honesty, become gratitude, or might simply become the beginning of learning.

"You'll hang for this night," Aurelian said hoarsely.

"Probably," Corvin agreed. "There's a queue forming."

He left the god-emperor alone with his empty court, his cold gold, and his lesson — the one no college teaches, the one that costs a throne: that power taken is never power kept, that worship bought with fear is a debt that compounds in the dark, and that the only light that doesn't gutter is the kind you give away.

In the corridor, the foundling boy was still waiting with his wooden bird, forgotten by whoever had summoned him. Corvin crouched, joints aching, and held out the bird when the boy shyly offered it up.

"Take me to the west postern," Corvin said. "And then take me anywhere but here."

Behind them, through the doors, the Amber Court lay silent — no leaning candles, no bending air, no voice in the old tongue. Just a man on the floor of his own legend, learning at last what the storm-caller had known all along.

By dawn, Corvin knew, the whole palace would know the light was out. By noon, the provinces would. And somewhere north, over a sleeping valley where wards had dissolved by choice rather than decree, a dragon's ember would be warming the sky — and the world, for the first time in a generation, would have to decide what to believe in that hadn't been manufactured for it.

Corvin intended to be gone before it decided.
