## 38. Pardons and Beekeeping

The pardon arrived on vellum so thick it could have stopped a sword, sealed in wax the color of old blood, and it was delivered by a courier who looked terrified of the bees.

"They won't sting you," Elodie told him, watching him flinch as a worker investigated his collar. "They're more afraid of you than you are of them."

"Everything is more afraid of me than I am of it, lately," the courier said, which was either a joke or a confession, and pressed the scroll into her hands as though it might bite him.

Elodie read it twice, sitting on her doorstep with the morning sun warm on her shoulders and the hive humming its low gold note behind her. *By order of the Provisional Council of Caelmere, all charges against Elodie Eastwick, formerly Court Storm-Caller, are hereby struck from the record. The exile of nineteen years past is declared void. The crown — such as it is — offers its apologies, its restitution, and its gratitude.*

The apologies were in a smaller hand, added later, cramped against the margin: *We are sorry. There is no sum of money that fixes what was done to you. We have tried to find one anyway. — M.V.*

Elodie laughed, and the laugh came out strange, half a sob, and the bees hummed on, unbothered by the weather inside her.

*Formerly Court Storm-Caller.* She turned the phrase over like a stone found in a garden bed. Ten years ago those three words had been a sentence of death in slow motion. Now they were just — a description. Of someone who had once been her. Someone who had stood in a flooded cathedral and spent every storm she was ever owed and walked out the other side with nothing in her hands but soot and salt and Wren's small, sticky grip.

The courier cleared his throat. "There's, ah. There's a second part. The Council asks — respectfully, they were very clear about the respectful part — that you come to Grayford for the formal proceedings. The emperor's abdication is being ratified next month. Everyone who was anyone in the old regime is being either pardoned, tried, or retired, and they want—" he winced, "—they want the famous ones there. For the optics."

"The optics," Elodie repeated.

"Their word, my lady. Not mine."

"I'm not a lady. And I'm not anyone's optics." She folded the pardon along its crease, carefully, the way you handle something that might still be alive. "But I'll come. Someone should watch Maren resign in person, or she'll find a way to make it a military operation."

* * *

Grayford had been a garrison town when Elodie was nineteen, all grey stone and greyer faces. Now it was a city in the middle of learning how to exhale. Banners of the god-emperor had been cut down and not yet replaced, leaving pale rectangles on the walls like scars after a healing wound. Market stalls sold both the old gods' charms and the new Council's broadsheets, and nobody had rioted about it, which Corvin Ashe later told her was the most miraculous thing he'd witnessed in twenty years of service.

The formal proceedings were held in the garrison hall, which had been scrubbed within an inch of its life. Elodie sat between Sera and Wren — Wren fidgeting in a dress Sera had bullied her into, Sera in her plain grey traveling clothes because, she said, she'd burned her vestments along with her ordination and wasn't going to pretend she had a rank anymore — and watched the machinery of a country trying to remember how to be one.

It was, mostly, tedious. There were speeches. There were so many speeches. A clerk read names for two hours, and for each name someone stood and either wept, protested, or, in one memorable case, tried to sell the assembly land he did not own.

Then: "Maren Voss, High Warden of the Realm."

Maren walked to the front of the hall in her dress uniform, every button fastened, her spine doing the work of a load-bearing wall. Elodie had seen her break in the water under the cathedral — had held her while she shook apart, grief pouring out of her like bilge from a holed ship — and she was the only person in the hall who knew what it cost Maren Voss to stand this straight.

"I hereby resign my commission," Maren said, and her voice carried to the rafters without her raising it, because of course it did. "Effective immediately."

A rustle went through the assembly. The presiding magistrate leaned forward. "High Warden — the Council had hoped you would oversee the reformation of the Wardens. Your record—"

"My record," Maren said, "is forty dead villagers and ten years of a friend in exile, and I signed both. The Council may have the reformation. I would only ruin it." She set her sword on the table in front of the magistrate — laid it flat, unhurried, the way you put down something heavy you have carried too far — and walked back down the aisle.

She sat down next to Elodie. For a moment neither of them said anything. Then Maren, eyes fixed forward on the next poor soul being called up, said quietly, "I practiced that. The sword part. In front of a mirror."

"It showed."

"Shut up, Elodie."

But her shoulder was warm against Elodie's, and it stayed there for the rest of the interminable proceedings, and when the magistrate finally declared the session closed, Maren stood, stretched like a woman setting down a yoke, and said, "Right. I have absolutely no idea what to do with myself. This is horrifying."

"I have bees," Elodie offered.

"You have — what?"

"Bees. Come see the bees. It's very restorative. You shout at nothing and the nothing doesn't shout back."

Maren stared at her. Then, slowly, like a drawbridge deciding to lower, she smiled. "I would like to shout at nothing very much."

* * *

Corvin Ashe had taken a cottage.

This was, in itself, a sentence that would have made Elodie laugh a year ago. Corvin, who had once tracked her across three provinces by the resonance of a bronze sigil and the memory of a girl who called storms; Corvin, who had turned his blade on his own inquisitors at Grayford Bridge and bled for it; Corvin, who now walked with a permanent hitch in his left knee and a permanent expression of baffled grievance — that Corvin had rented a cottage with a garden, on the strength of a rumor that gardening was peaceful.

Elodie and Maren found him on their second day back in Grayford, standing in a plot of churned mud behind the cottage, holding a cabbage plant at arm's length as though it had insulted his mother.

"You're holding it wrong," Elodie said.

"I am holding it *exactly* as the manual instructs." He didn't turn around. He'd heard her walk, of course; he always heard her. "Root ball down. Leaves up. The manual has diagrams."

"The manual," Maren said, "was written by someone who has clearly never met a cabbage."

"The manual was written by the Council's Agricultural Commission, which is why there are four appendices on soil pH." Corvin finally turned, and Elodie saw that he had mud to the elbows and a smear of it across one cheekbone like war paint applied by a child, and that he was, unmistakably, happier than she had ever seen him. "This plant has been in my hand for a quarter hour. It is winning."

"Dig the hole first," Elodie said, crouching at the edge of the plot. "Wide, not deep. Loosen the roots a little — they're cramped in that pot, they've never known anything else. You have to convince them there's more world than they thought."

Corvin looked at her for a moment too long. "That," he said, "is either gardening advice or a metaphor, and I genuinely cannot tell."

"Both. Most true things are both, if you're tired enough." She took the cabbage from him, worked it loose, and set it into the earth he'd finally, grudgingly, dug. "There. Now water it and apologize to it."

"Apologize."

"For the quarter hour of hostile captivity."

Maren laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the first Elodie had heard from her since before the trial — and Corvin scowled at them both with the expression of a man who had once been the most feared inquisitor in Caelmere and was now being teased by two women in a mud patch, and who had, against every expectation, decided he didn't mind.

"I killed a man at Grayford Bridge," he said, quieter, after a while, kneeling to pat the soil down around the cabbage with surprising gentleness. "Two, actually. And I'd do it again. Some nights that sits easy and some nights it doesn't." He wiped his hands on his thighs, leaving darker streaks. "The manual says gardening teaches patience. So far it's teaching me that everything I plant dies and everything I don't plant thrives, which feels like a personal message from the universe."

"Give it a season," Elodie said.

"I gave the empire fifteen years. Look how that went." But he was smiling as he said it, crooked and unwilling, and when they left he was still crouched over the cabbage, talking to it in a low voice, and if the words were apologies, neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

Sera's chapel went up in the autumn, on the hill above the saltmarsh, and it was the smallest holy building Elodie had ever seen: one room, whitewashed, with a door that didn't quite close and a window that let the rain in sideways when the wind came from the sea.

"It's perfect," Sera said, the day they finished the roof, standing back with plaster on her hands and in her hair and, somehow, one eyebrow. "It leaks. A chapel should leak. If a chapel doesn't leak, it's a fortress, and I've had enough of fortresses."

"It's four walls and a floor," Wren said critically, from atop the woodpile where she'd appointed herself supervisor. "The cathedral had a thousand pillars."

"The cathedral had a god in it," Sera said, "and look how that went." She said it lightly, but Elodie caught the flinch underneath, the old bruise showing through. Sera had unmade her own ordination in the flooded dark — had torn the memory of her god out of her own chest to starve him — and there were nights, Elodie knew, when Sera woke reaching for something that wasn't there anymore and couldn't remember what it had been. A gap where faith used to live, shaped exactly like faith.

So Sera built a chapel with no god in it. She rang a bell at dawn — an actual bell, salvaged from a wreck, tuned to a note that made dogs howl — not for anyone, she said, but because the day deserved to be noticed. She kept a lamp lit in the window for travelers, and she listened to anyone who came up the hill with a grief or a gratitude, and she gave neither blessings nor absolutions, only attention, which turned out to be the rarer gift.

People came. That was the strange part. Fishermen whose boats had come home empty. Mothers from Ondrel who still flinched at the sight of any official seal. A warden, once, still in uniform, who stood in the doorway a long time and finally said, "I don't know how to be a person when the orders stop," and Sera sat him down and made him tea and listened until dark.

"You could have had a real congregation," Elodie told her one evening, watching the lamp glow through the crooked window. "The Council would have given you a cathedral. A title. They're handing out redemption like bread."

"I had a god," Sera said, "and he was a famine wearing my father's voice. I'd rather have a leaky roof and a bell nobody has to answer to." She smiled, tired and true. "Besides. Someone has to be honest in this country. It may as well be the woman with plaster in her hair."

* * *

The offer came in the spring, and it came with trumpets, because the Council had learned nothing.

Elodie was in Grayford for the ratification — she'd promised Wren they'd see it, and Wren had wanted to see the god-emperor's empty throne carried out of the hall on a cart, and it had been, Wren declared, "the best funeral for a chair ever" — when a herald in absurd livery found her in the market and announced, at volume, that the Provisional Council of Caelmere wished to offer Elodie Eastwick reinstatement to her former office, with full honors, estates, and authority over all weather-matters of the realm.

The market went quiet. Forty faces turned toward her.

"They want to make me a *what?*" Elodie said.

"Court Storm-Caller," the herald repeated, and then, sensing perhaps that the wind had shifted, added hastily, "or — the Council is flexible on the title. Weather Minister. Mistress of Skies. They have a list."

Elodie looked at him. She looked at the parchment in his hands, with its fat seal and its list of honors, and she thought about the girl she had been at nineteen, who would have wept to hear those words. Who had spent ten years in exile dreaming of exactly this — the door opening, the name cleared, the power handed back with an apology attached.

And she thought about the storm she'd called at the saltmarsh, and how good it had felt, and how it had nearly killed Wren, and how the wanting had never once stopped wanting. Power was a door too, she'd learned. It didn't open onto the world. It opened onto you, and what came out was whatever was hungriest.

"No," she said.

The herald blinked. "I — beg pardon?"

"No, thank you," Elodie said, more kindly. "Tell the Council I'm a beekeeper. Tell them the bees are very demanding and I simply couldn't take on outside work." She paused. "Tell them the skies can look after themselves. They always did. It was the people underneath who kept forgetting."

She turned back to the honey stall she'd been haggling at, and after a moment the herald, with the relief of a man escaping a collapsing building, withdrew, trumpets and all, and the market exhaled and resumed its noise, and the honey-seller, an old woman with forearms like dock rope, said, "You just turned down the sky, girl."

"I had it," Elodie said. "It didn't fit."

* * *

She told the others that night, around Sera's table in the cottage Sera kept below the chapel, with Maren's boots on the table and Corvin nursing a bruised thumb from a fencing match with a rosebush and Wren asleep against Elodie's side, her breathing the slow tide of a child deeply, safely asleep.

"They offered it back," Elodie said. "Everything. I said no."

Maren nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd already written in a report. "I told them you would. They didn't believe me. There's a betting pool."

"There's *what?*"

"Corvin's on your side. He put money on 'declines and makes a joke about it.' He's very smug."

"I am," Corvin agreed, "and the joke was better than I predicted. I had 'the weather can manage without me.' The bees were an improvement."

Elodie laughed, and the sound of it surprised her — easy, unarmored, a sound she was still learning to recognize as hers. Ten years ago she had wanted the world to give her back her name. It had. And it turned out the name had never been the thing she'd lost. The thing she'd lost was smaller and harder to replace: a valley, a hive, a dragon's glow on the northern sky, a table with too many elbows on it.

"I want to go home," she said, before she'd quite decided to say it.

The table went still, the way tables do when something true has just been set down among the dishes.

"The valley," Sera said. Not a question.

"The wards are gone — we dissolved them, all of us, when we sealed the Door. There's nothing keeping anyone in or out anymore." Elodie turned her cup slowly on the wood. "It's just a valley now. With a crater. And a very large patient in it who is, as far as any of us know, still asleep at the bottom of the sea, glowing." Her throat tightened. "I keep bees here. I could keep bees there. The clover in the Hollow Valley is the best in the world; I've had ten years to establish this."

"You want to move back to the place you were exiled to," Maren said, "now that you're allowed to leave."

"Yes."

Maren considered this with the gravity she'd once given troop deployments. "That's the most Elodie thing I've ever heard."

"It's not exile if you choose it," Wren mumbled from Elodie's side, half asleep, and everyone at the table went quiet, because the child was right, and because she said things like that the way other children said *pass the bread.*

Corvin leaned back, chair creaking. "The garden here is a disaster anyway. Everything I plant dies."

"Your cabbages are thriving," Maren said.

"Two of them. The rest is a graveyard. I could garden somewhere with worse soil and lower expectations." He glanced at Elodie, and beneath the dry humor there was something older and more careful. "You're sure? The valley took ten years from you. Most people burn a house like that down. You want to move back in and keep bees."

"That's exactly why," Elodie said. "You don't let the worst room in your life stay dark forever. You open the shutters. You let the light in until it stops being the worst room." She looked around the table — Maren with her boots down now, leaning forward; Sera with her plaster-scarred hands folded; Corvin with his bruised thumb; Wren breathing against her ribs like a small warm anchor. "Besides. I'm not going alone. Am I?"

Nobody answered out loud. But Maren was already, visibly, drafting supply lists behind her eyes, and Sera had that expression she got when she was deciding where the bell should hang, and Corvin said, "I'm told the Hollow Valley has *terrible* soil," in the tone of a man who had just won a bet with himself.

And Wren, without opening her eyes, smiled.

They would leave in a week, Elodie decided. There were hives to prepare, and a road that wasn't on any map to walk one more time — but this time walking it toward, not away, and this time when the valley rose up around them, green and quiet and humming, it would not be a prison with the door shut.

It would be a room with the shutters open.

She only hoped, watching the fire settle and listening to her strange, improbable family argue cheerfully about cabbage logistics, that the valley had kept everything the way she'd left it — and that whatever slept in the deep water off the northern shore still knew the sound of her footsteps on the crater's rim.

She'd find out soon enough.
