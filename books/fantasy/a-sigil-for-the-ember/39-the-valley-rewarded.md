## 39. The Valley, Rewarded

Wren counted the mountains before she counted her breaths, because the mountains were easier.

Seven of them, the same seven she'd memorized from the ridge above Grayford Bridge when everything had been screaming and Corvin's sword had been red to the hilt and Elodie's hands had been shaking so badly she'd had to hold them against her own ribs like they belonged to someone else. Seven peaks, and the last one leaning east the way a tired old man leans on a fence. If those seven were still there, then the world hadn't ended while she slept. It was a system. It worked. She'd used it every morning for two months now, lying in whatever bed or bedroll or hollow of warm ash they'd found, opening one eye, counting, breathing again.

This morning there were seven peaks, and below them, at the end of a road that was more suggestion than road, the Hollow Valley lay green and gold in the late-summer light, and Wren forgot to count her breaths entirely because they all went out of her at once.

"There it is," she said. Her voice came out small. She cleared her throat and tried again, bigger, the way you'd announce something important. "There it is!"

"I see it," Elodie said beside her.

"You're not looking."

"I'm looking." But Elodie was looking at Wren, actually, with that crooked half-smile that meant she'd caught something and wasn't going to say it out loud yet, which was annoying, which was also — Wren admitted it privately, grudgingly, the way you admit you like boiled sweets even though they're for children — one of her favorite things about her.

Behind them, the mule snorted. The mule was named Turnip, and Wren had named him herself, and he carried their packs and Sera when Sera's legs got tired, which was more often than Sera liked to admit, because what the drowned god had taken out of her when she unmade her ordination had apparently included some of the marrow in her bones. She walked anyway. Most days she walked.

"Ten years," Elodie said softly, to the valley, to the seven mountains, maybe to nobody. "It took me ten years to walk away from this place, and four months to want to come back."

"That's not slow," Wren said. "That's just how long things take when they matter. Maren says—" She stopped. Saying *Maren* still made the air go strange, even now, even with the pardon signed and folded in Elodie's pack like an ordinary piece of paper and not a thing that had once cost a girl at a bridge almost everything. "Sorry."

"Don't be sorry." Elodie scratched the mule's nose. "She said something true, did she?"

"She says things are only heavy if you try to carry them fast." Wren kicked a stone down the path and watched it bounce ahead of them, downhill, toward home. "She also said I should stop eavesdropping on wardens, but that one I'm ignoring forever."

* * *

The valley had changed, and it hadn't.

That was the thing about places, Wren was learning. You could leave them and come back and expect them to be frozen, like a fly in amber, like the moment of your leaving preserved forever — but places didn't hold still for anybody. The bees were still there; she could hear them from half a mile off, a low golden hum that Elodie had once described as *the sound of everything being fine*. The hive boxes stood in their crooked row by the stream, weathered silver-gray, some of them listing like old drunkards. But weeds had gotten into the garden. A bird had nested in the chimney of the little stone house and filled half of it with twigs, and from the look of the droppings on the doorstep, the bird had opinions about ownership. And the crater — the great black bowl at the valley's northern end where Vaskar had slept his centuries — was empty. Just empty. A scar of glassed rock holding rainwater that reflected the sky so perfectly it looked like a hole into somewhere better.

Wren looked at it exactly once and then made herself stop looking.

"He's not gone," Elodie said. She hadn't followed Wren's gaze, but she always knew. That was another thing about her. "You know that, right? He's in the deep water past the saltmarsh. Corvin saw him breach last week. Says the whole horizon caught fire."

"Corvin exaggerates."

"Corvin is constitutionally incapable of exaggeration. It's why he's so bad at gardening. You can't grow anything without telling yourself hopeful lies."

Wren laughed, and the laugh felt good in her chest, and she let it carry her up the path to the house.

The wards should have stopped them.

That was the strangest part, the part she kept turning over like a river stone in her pocket. Ten years ago the wards had been a wall around the whole valley, high and humming and absolute — magic could not follow Elodie Eastwick here, and neither could anyone who wanted to use magic on her, and neither could Elodie herself once the door shut behind her. Wren had lived inside that wall for most of a year. She knew its texture the way you know the texture of a blanket you slept under: the faint pressure behind the eyes near the rim, the way sounds flattened, the way the sky over the valley always seemed slightly closer than sky should be.

Now they walked through where the wall had been, and nothing happened at all.

"It doesn't push," Wren said, stopping dead on the old ward-line — she could feel where it had been, a seam in the world like a healed break in a bone. "Why doesn't it push?"

Elodie crouched and pressed her palm flat against the grass. For a moment her eyes went distant, storm-gray, the color they turned when she was listening to something underground. "Because it isn't fighting us," she said. "A wall fights. This is..." She frowned, hunting for the word. "This is an open hand."

"Whose?"

"Mine, I think. Or the valley's. Or both." She stood, brushing off her knees. "When the emperor's clerks dissolved my exile — officially, with wax seals and everything, Maren made me sit through the entire ceremony, it took three hours — the decree said the wards would fall. They didn't fall. They just... waited. Like they wanted to know what I'd choose first." Her mouth twisted. "Everything in this valley has always been stubbornly alive. Why would the wards be any different?"

"So it let you back in."

"It let *us* back in." Elodie looked at Wren, then back down the path where Sera came walking slow with her hand on Turnip's flank, and something moved across her face that Wren couldn't name — grief and gratitude tangled together, the way roots tangle under a road. "That's the whole point, I think. It was never a cage. It was a held breath. And now—"

"Now you can breathe," Wren finished.

Elodie blinked at her. "When did you get wise?"

"Maren's been teaching me. Don't tell her. She'll only say something true about it and make it weird."

* * *

They spent the first day doing nothing that mattered and everything that counted.

Wren learned where the bees had requeened themselves — twice, apparently, without supervision, because bees were better at government than most kingdoms — and helped Elodie patch the chimney and chase the nesting bird out, which took an hour and ended in a truce involving a wooden box nailed to the gable at a diplomatic distance. Sera sat in the doorway with her feet in the sun and watched them, and every so often she smiled at something small — the way Elodie swore at the bird, the way Wren fell in the stream — and the smiles sat differently on her face now than they had on the ghost-road. Lighter. Like someone had set down luggage she'd forgotten she was carrying.

It was on the second evening that Wren worked up the nerve.

She'd been carrying it since Ondrel. Longer than Ondrel — since the bridge itself, the gray old bridge over the black water that had stopped them and asked, in a voice like wet rope, for a memory as its toll. She remembered standing between Elodie and Sera with her heart going like a rabbit's, watching Sera step forward first, watching her press her fingers to the toll-stone and close her eyes and give something away. Watching her come back from it a half-inch lighter and lie about it immediately, smoothly, saying *nothing important, an old lesson, barely worth keeping* — and watching Elodie notice, because Elodie noticed everything, and say nothing, because Elodie was learning, slowly and painfully, that noticing and accusing were different muscles.

But Wren was eleven years old and had no reputation to protect, and so she had noticed too, and she had done something about it, and she had been waiting months for the right moment, and the right moment, she decided, was now: firelight, full bellies, Sera mending a sock with the patient frown of someone whose hands needed occupation or they started trembling.

"Sera," Wren said. "Hold out your hands."

Sera looked up. "What?"

"Hold out your hands and close your eyes. Please. It's a gift and gifts don't work unless you do it properly."

"That is not how gifts work," Sera said, but she set down the sock, and she held out her hands, and she closed her eyes, because underneath all the priestess training and all the grief and all the new lightness, she was still a woman who had crossed a burning ward for strangers, and women like that did not refuse children asking politely.

Wren had found the memory on the bridge. That was the secret nobody knew — not Elodie, not Corvin, not even Sera. When the toll-taker had taken Sera's memory, it had dropped it, or spilled it, or simply lost interest, the way a magpie loses interest in a button. Wren had seen it lying on the stones of the bridge like a little pool of silver light, shaped like a teardrop, humming. And she had done the single most reckless thing of her entire life, which was saying something, because the bridge had already tasted her party's fear and she'd thought — childishly, furiously — *you can't have hers, you've had enough of ours.* So she'd picked it up. It had burned cold, like snow held too long, and she'd stuffed it inside her shirt and walked across the bridge on legs made of jelly, and the bridge had let her, because bridges are old and strange and perhaps even merciful, and she had carried the memory through Ondrel and the saltmarsh and the drowned cathedral and the whole terrible bright ending of the world, and she had never told anyone, because telling people meant it could be taken away again.

She knelt in front of Sera now and opened her cupped hands over Sera's open ones, and let it go.

The silver light sank into Sera's palms like water into dry ground.

Sera gasped — a real gasp, involuntary, the sound of a woman surfacing — and her eyes flew open, and they were streaming, and for a long moment she didn't move at all. Then her hands closed slowly around nothing, around the shape of something returned, and she said, in a voice Wren had never heard her use, young and unguarded:

"The chapel. The novices' chapel, the morning I was ordained. Sister Betha was late. She was always late. She came running in with her veil on backwards and everyone laughed and the god was—" Sera stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I gave this away to cross a bridge. I told you all it was nothing. I have been *without* this for—"

"Since Grayford," Wren said. "I know. I saw it drop. I picked it up." She scuffed her foot. "Are you angry? Everyone says stealing is bad but it wasn't really stealing, it was more like rescuing—"

Sera pulled her into a hug so sudden and so fierce that Wren's ribs creaked.

"You impossible, brave, ridiculous child," Sera said into her hair. "You carried it. Through everything. All this time." She pulled back and held Wren at arm's length, and her face was wrecked and radiant at once. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you'd have felt bad. You felt bad enough already. About the bridge, about lying, about—" Wren waved a hand vaguely at the whole ruined-and-mended shape of recent history. "And because it wasn't time yet. Things take as long as they take when they matter."

Sera stared at her. Then, over Wren's head, at Elodie, who had gone very still by the fire: "Where does she *get* this?"

"I've stopped asking," Elodie said. Her voice was rough. "I just write it down and hope some of it sticks."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to orange bones and Sera slept with her hands curled loosely, palms up, like a woman no longer afraid of what might be placed in them, Wren sat beside Elodie on the doorstep and looked north, where the sky above the far mountains glowed the faintest ember-red, so faint you'd think you imagined it if you weren't the kind of person who checked.

"Does it hurt him?" she asked. "Being lit again. After being asleep so long."

"I don't know," Elodie said. "I think... I think it hurts like walking does, after you've been ill a long time. Hurts and heals in the same motion." She turned her hands over in her lap. They were bare. No sigil, no storm-light, no calluses from staffs or swords — just beekeeper's hands, stung and capable. "He sent me a dream last week. First one since the sea. He said—" she smiled at the dark — "*Tell the small fierce one she counts mountains wrong. There are eight.*"

Wren's head snapped up. She counted. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, and the eighth — the eighth peak, low and eastern, the tired old man — had been hidden behind cloud every single morning until today.

"That's cheating," she said. "He can't just add mountains."

"He's a dragon. Cheating is most of what dragons are." Elodie bumped her shoulder gently against Wren's. "Get some sleep, small fierce one. Tomorrow we start the hives proper, and after that—" she nodded south, toward the road, toward the world — "the valley children arrive next week. Maren's sending them. Weather-sense lessons. No magic. Just listening."

"To the sky?"

"To each other, mostly. The sky's easy." Elodie stood, and stretched, and looked out over her valley — her chosen valley now, not her sentenced one — and the ember-glow touched the edge of her face like a blessing from an old friend who never learned to whisper. "Goodnight, Wren."

"Goodnight, Elodie."

And Wren went to her bed beneath the patched roof, and did not count the mountains, because for the first time since Grayford Bridge, she already knew they'd be there.
