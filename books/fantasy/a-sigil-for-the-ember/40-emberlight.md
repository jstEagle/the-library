## 40. Emberlight

The bees woke before Elodie did, the way they always did in late spring, and by the time she came down the hill with her veil pushed back off her face they had already found the white clover along the eastern fence and were working it with the steady industry of small creatures who had never once questioned their purpose in the world.

She envied them that, a little. Not bitterly — she was long past bitterness, or she told herself she was — but with the fond wistfulness of a woman who remembered what it was to be twenty-two and certain that her purpose would arrive like weather, all at once, and rearrange everything.

Her purpose, it turned out, had arrived like beekeeping instead: gradually, seasonally, one small obligation at a time.

"Mind the left hive," she called down to the boy waiting at the gate. Tam, eight years old, gap-toothed, wearing his father's old coat with the sleeves rolled four times. "She's moody this week. Smoked twice, not once."

"She's *moody*?" Tam repeated, delighted. Bees could have moods. Bees could have *weeks*. In the valley where magic could not follow — where magic no longer bothered to try, since there was nothing left on either side of the world worth knocking for — this was still the most miraculous thing Elodie knew how to teach.

"Everything alive has moods," Elodie said, lifting the smoker. The coals caught with a soft orange breath, and she held it a moment longer than she needed to, watching the light move inside the tin. Some habits of attention never left a storm-caller, even a retired one. Even one whose storms had been spent down to the last drop, poured out into a dying dragon's chest on a night the whole northern sky had turned the color of forge-iron.

That had been nine years ago. Some mornings it felt like ninety. Most mornings it felt like last night.

* * *

The children came up the hill on the fourth morning of every week, when the weather was likeliest to be doing something instructive. There were eleven of them now, from six families scattered along the valley floor, and none of them had been born when the valley burned, and only the oldest two had been born when Vaskar rose from the sea trailing fire like a comet coming home.

They called the lesson weather-sense, because Wren had named it that when she was fifteen and helping teach, and the name had stuck the way true things stick.

"Now," Elodie said, standing in the tall grass with the wind moving through it in long silver strokes, "what's the sky telling us?"

Eleven faces tipped upward. Eleven frowns of concentration. Somewhere behind her, Sera sat on the porch of the little house with tea going cold beside her and a book of scripture she no longer believed in but still read the way one reads letters from a difficult parent — out of habit, out of love, out of the stubborn refusal to pretend the past hadn't happened.

"Cirrus," said Tam's older sister, squinting. "High and wispy."

"And?"

"Weather's coming. Two days? Maybe three?"

"From which direction?"

"West." A pause. The girl chewed her lip. "But the wind's out of the south, so — it'll bend? It'll come around before it comes over?"

Elodie smiled. Ten years ago — twelve, thirteen — she would have simply *known*, the way you know your own heartbeat, the whole sky laid open to her like a page written in her first language. That knowing was gone now, spent, sealed, given away freely and forever, and she had expected to mourn it the way one mourns a severed limb.

What she had not expected was how much she loved learning it again the slow way. The human way. Reading clouds like anyone else, getting it wrong half the time, being *delighted* when she got it right.

"That's exactly it," she said. "Weather isn't a decree. It's a conversation. You just have to be willing to listen longer than it's willing to talk."

Wren, twenty-four now and taller than Elodie and wearing her hair in the same practical braid she'd worn at nine, snorted from the back of the group. "You used to just tell it what to do."

"I used to *think* I did." Elodie let the wind lift her words the way she'd once lifted rain. "Turns out the sky was mostly talking to itself. I just happened to be standing in the way."

The children laughed. They always laughed at that part; it was in the lesson plan, more or less. But underneath the laughter Elodie felt the old truth settle again, gently, like ash after a fire: the storm had never been hers. She had carried it, been blamed for it, been broken by it, and in the end she had *spent* it — every ounce, every year of it, every unshed thunderhead of her girlhood — and what remained afterward was not emptiness.

What remained was this. A hillside. A hive. Eleven children learning to read the wind without asking it for anything.

Peace earned, not granted. Maren had said that once, at Grayford Bridge, with blood on both their hands, and Elodie had not understood it then. She understood it now the way she understood the bees: completely, and only through years of tending.

* * *

Some nights the northern sky glowed.

Not every night. Never in summer, when the dark came late and shallow. But in deep autumn and all through winter, when the cold settled into the valley like a guest determined to stay until spring, the horizon above the far mountains would take on a low amber warmth — faint, patient, unmistakable — as though somewhere beyond the ridgeline an enormous hearth had been banked rather than extinguished.

The children called it the Emberlight. So did everyone else. There had been a brief official attempt, in the first years, to call it *Vaskar's Vigil*, promoted by the same cartographers' guild that had finally put Caelmere back onto maps — redrawn, honestly surveyed, its erased towns restored in careful ink — but names like that need a herald's budget, and the Emberlight needed nothing. It spread on its own, the way true things do.

Elodie walked out to see it on the nights it burned strongest. This was not duty. Nobody asked her to. It was simply that on those nights the air felt different — warmer than it should have been, faintly resinous, smelling of cedar smoke and hot stone — and she wanted to be outside under it, the way you want to sit near a sleeping animal you love.

He was out there. Past the mountains, past the drowned cathedral with its doors sealed shut forever, past the trench in the gray sea where he had gone down wounded and come up blazing. He ranged widely now, the way the last of anything does, patrolling a world that no longer feared him and did not entirely know what he was for. Fishermen swore by him. Sailors left him offerings they knew he wouldn't take. Once, three winters ago, he had flown directly over the valley — high, high up, a black shape against the stars with his chest glowing like a coal wrapped in silk — and the whole population had stood in the streets and fields watching, and no one had screamed, and Elodie had raised one hand as he passed, and whether he saw her she never asked, because some answers are better carried than opened.

But on the strong nights she talked to him anyway, quietly, from the hilltop, the way you talk to someone across a great distance who can nonetheless always hear you.

"Hive two swarmed while I was at market," she said, one such night, her breath fogging in the emberlit air. "Caught them myself. No ladder. You'd have laughed."

The glow did not answer. It never did, in words. But it pulsed — very slightly, very slowly, the rhythm of something enormous breathing in its sleep — and Elodie chose, as she chose every time, to believe that counted as laughter.

"You'd better not die yet," she added. "I've gotten attached. That's your fault. You taught me to spend everything and then you went and made yourself worth spending on."

The ember-pulse came again, a fraction brighter, and she took it for what it was: agreement. Or affection. Or a dragon's version of *leave an old man alone*, which amounted to the same thing.

She stayed until the cold drove her in, and the glow kept burning behind her the whole walk home, warming the back of her neck like a hand.

* * *

Sera's chapel stood at the crossroads by the mill, and it was honest in every particular: whitewashed, one room, a bell small enough for one woman to ring, and a congregation that averaged nine people and included two goats who attended for the warmth. Sera preached the drowned god the way a widow speaks of a husband who turned out, too late, to have been someone else entirely — with grief, and anger, and a tenderness that refused to be ashamed of itself.

"He wasn't famine," she told Elodie once, sweeping the chapel floor in the gold light of evening. "Or — he was, but so is the sea, and I loved the sea before I ever knew what it ate. I don't think faith has to be about what the god was. I think it's about what you do with the loving after."

"And what do you do with it?"

Sera leaned on the broom. Her ordination-memory was gone — she had unmade it herself, the highest act of love, the anchor torn free — and what filled the space was not a hole but a garden, planted deliberately, tended daily. "This," she said simply, gesturing at the floor, the pews, the bell, the goats. "Small things. Honest ones. Turns out that's a whole religion if you do it every day."

Corvin's garden was less successful. He had taken the cottage at the valley's southern mouth — the one with good light and terrible soil — and approached gardening the way he had once approached inquisition: rigorously, methodically, with detailed notes and a mounting sense of personal betrayal. His carrots were legendarily bad. His cabbages were a scandal. He brought Elodie his failures every market day with the expression of a man presenting evidence of sabotage.

"The soil is conspiring," he said.

"The soil is fine. You're drowning them."

"I am *watering* them."

"At inquisitor rates." Elodie handed him a jar of honey, as she did every week, as penance neither of them named. "Less. Trust them to find what they need."

He looked at the jar, then at her — the same look he'd worn in Ondrel's market nine years ago, love curdling forward into something cleaner, something that had survived guilt and blood and the sight of a god-emperor guttering out hollow before his own court — and he said, "Trust them to find what they need," in the flat voice of a man writing it down inside himself, next to the other lessons that had cost him everything to learn.

His turncoat scar ran from wrist to elbow now, pale and straight. He never covered it. When children asked, he told them the truth: that he had pointed his blade the right direction one hour too late, and that lateness was a debt you paid for the rest of your life, gladly, in vegetables.

* * *

Maren came twice a year.

She arrived on foot, having resigned the wardenship in her third year — "a warden who hears whispers in bronze should not keep soldiers," she'd told the council, and the council, remembering Grayford, had not argued — and she always brought the same things: salt fish, ink, news, and a stiffness in her shoulders that loosened only after the second cup of tea.

They did not speak easily. They never had. But they spoke truly, which was harder-won and worth more.

"I dreamed about the trial again," Maren said, on her last visit, staring into her cup. "Forty people. And you at nineteen, so frightened you couldn't even defend yourself, and me standing up there knowing—" Her jaw tightened. Knowing what the Door had shown them both, in the flooded dark: that the storm had been the gods' death-rattle channeled through a girl who happened to be standing in the way; that Maren's testimony had not condemned an innocent so much as smuggled one out of the emperor's purges inside the only prison the emperor didn't watch.

"And?" Elodie prompted, gently, because this part of the ritual mattered.

"And I still don't know if I did the right thing."

"I know." Elodie reached across the table and took her friend's scarred hand. "Neither do I. About anything. That's what makes it mine, you know. A choice I'm sure of would've been somebody else's."

Maren huffed — almost a laugh. Outside, the bees worked the clover in the long evening light, and somewhere north, faint as a banked coal, the sky held its warmth. "You sound like the dragon."

"I sound like a beekeeper. Same thing, eventually."

* * *

On the last night of autumn, Wren climbed the hill to find her sitting on the porch steps with her veil in her lap and the hives humming their winter hum below.

"They're sealed up for the season?" Wren asked, sitting down beside her. Twenty-four years old, weather-sense teacher now herself, keeper of the small answered prayer that had once breathed borrowed air into a drowned cathedral full of kneeling dead.

"Tucked in. Fed. Moody." Elodie bumped her shoulder. "All accounted for."

They sat in silence a while. The Emberlight burned low and steady on the northern rim of the world, and beneath it the valley lay dark and ordinary and utterly itself: lamplit windows, woodsmoke, the mill wheel turning in its sleep, Sera's chapel bell silent until morning.

"Do you ever miss it?" Wren asked at last. Not looking at her. Asking the way she'd once asked, at nine, whether Elodie would leave them to be powerful again — the question that had landed deeper than any blade, and been answered by a decade instead of a sentence. "The power. Being able to just— reach up and take the sky."

Elodie considered it honestly, because Wren deserved honesty and always had.

"Sometimes," she said. "On the first real storm of spring, when the pressure drops and my hands ache with old memory — yes. For about a heartbeat. Then I remember what it cost, and what it was for, and whose it actually was. And then I come out here and check the bees, and the wanting goes back to sleep." She paused. "It doesn't die. Wanting doesn't. You just stop feeding it the important parts of yourself."

Wren nodded slowly. Then she leaned her head against Elodie's shoulder, the way she had at nine and fourteen and nineteen, and said, "Good. Because we'd have followed you either way. But we liked it better when you stayed."

"I know," Elodie said, and her throat was tight, and she let it be tight, because peace earned includes the earning. "I know, little wren."

Below them the hives murmured. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful, asking nothing of anyone. And northward, patient as a promise, the Emberlight warmed the dark — proof that even the smallest remaining flame, tended faithfully, is enough to hold back a whole sky's worth of night.

She had wanted power once. She had wanted it so badly it had broken her against the world.

What she had gotten instead was harder and better: a hillside, a family gathered rather than granted, a dragon glowing on the edge of the map, eleven children who could read the wind, and mornings that began — every single one — with the sound of bees waking, unafraid, in a valley where nothing needed protecting anymore except the living of ordinary days.

Elodie Eastwick sat on her porch steps between her oldest friend and the warm dark, listened to her bees dream, and was home.
