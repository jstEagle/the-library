## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The rain came down the way it always did in the foothills of Thornfell — sideways, mean, and without any intention of stopping. Lena Villalobos crouched beneath the canvas awning of the lead wagon and watched the water run off the brim in a steady silver thread, pooling in the mud around her boots.

*Veyra, who does not speak. Veyra, who sees in the dark. Keep my hands steady and my mouth shut.*

She said it the way she'd said it every morning for twenty years, the way her mother had taught her before her mother had anything left to teach. A habit, worn smooth as a river stone. A prayer with no expectation behind it, the way a soldier checks a strap she knows is already buckled.

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered for a long time.

That was the way of it with Veyra. The silent god had never been a god of thunder and burning bushes. The old priests said she answered in the space between heartbeats, in the stillness of a blade before the cut, in the moment a liar's eyes flicked away. You didn't hear Veyra. You noticed her absence, the way you noticed a missing tooth — by what the silence used to hold.

But lately the silence had changed texture. Lena couldn't have explained it to anyone who'd ask, and no one would ask, because mercenaries didn't talk about gods unless they were drunk, and Lena didn't drink on contract. The silence used to feel like a held breath. Now it felt like an empty room. Like standing in a house where someone had lived and left, and the dust hadn't even settled right.

*She stopped answering,* Lena thought, not for the first time. *And I keep praying anyway. What does that make me?*

Loyal. That was the word, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Lena." Doss's head appeared around the wagon's edge, rain dripping off his nose. "Master Corren wants eyes on the ridge before we hit the switchbacks. Says the last caravan through here lost two mules."

"The last caravan through here wasn't guarded by us."

"No," Doss agreed. "It wasn't." He grinned, gap-toothed, twenty years her junior and still delighted by the world's capacity for violence. Then he was gone, splashing off through the mud toward the rear of the column.

Lena rolled her shoulders, checked the knife at her hip out of the same habit that drove the prayers, and went up the ridge.

* * *

The switchbacks above the Vale of Corren were old smuggler roads, cut into the mountainside so long ago that no one remembered who'd cut them. They were good roads — better than the king's highways, if you asked anyone who actually used them — and they had always been neutral ground. Smugglers, merchants, pilgrims, soldiers on leave. The foothill roads belonged to everyone, which meant they belonged to no one, which was the only law the mountains had ever respected.

Lena had walked these roads on a dozen contracts. She knew every shrine niche cut into the rock, every one of them holding a weathered carving of one of the old gods. Veyra's mark appeared seven times between the valley floor and the pass: a closed eye inside a circle, the lid drawn as a single unbroken line. Pilgrims had pressed coins into the cracks around the carvings for centuries. The coins were still there. Nobody stole from the silent god. Even the bandits had their superstitions.

She climbed to the overlook with rain hammering her hood and crouched in the wet heather, scanning the road below.

The caravan stretched out beneath her — six wagons, forty mules, Master Corren's dyed wool and Corren salt, worth more than most villages would see in a decade. Twelve hired blades, of whom Lena was senior. A routine escort. Boring, even. Boring was what you paid for, in her trade.

She was about to signal all-clear when she saw the shrine.

It was the fourth one down, tucked into a fold of rock where the road bent back on itself. Lena had passed it a hundred times. The carving of Veyra's closed eye had been there since before her grandmother was born, soft-edged with age, the coins around it green with verdigris.

Someone had chiseled it out.

Not defaced — *removed*, cut clean from the stone with masonry tools, leaving a raw rectangular scar in the rock face. And in its place, freshly cut, still pale as a wound, was a new mark.

Lena had never seen it before. That was the thing that made her stomach tighten. She knew every sigil in the foothills — the old gods' marks, the free cities' trade glyphs, the smuggler signs scratched under overhangs to mark safe water and bad company. This was none of them. It looked like a key viewed from the wrong side, or a knot that had been tied and then untied in a hurry. A circle, broken, with a line driven through it like a nail.

She stared at it for a long moment, rain running down her face, and felt the first cold thread of unease wind through the weariness that had been her constant companion for months.

Someone was carving new gods onto the old roads.

* * *

"Chiseled it out?" Master Corren was a fat man with a merchant's soft hands and a moneylender's hard eyes, and he looked at Lena over his tented fingers as if she'd suggested the rain was a personal insult. "The whole carving? Stone and all?"

"Stone and all. Took them time. Took them tools. Took them not caring who saw."

"Vandals."

"Vandals don't haul masonry chisels up a switchback in the rain." Lena stood in the doorway of Corren's wagon, dripping, refusing the stool he offered because sitting down in front of employers was a habit that got you treated like furniture. "There were marks like it on the third shrine too. I checked the second on the way back. Same thing. Old gods cut out, new mark cut in. Fresh."

Corren's fingers drummed. "What does it mean?"

"I don't know yet."

"You're the one who's always telling me you know the roads, Villalobos."

"I know the roads," Lena said. "The roads are changing."

That shut him up for a moment. Outside, the rain kept up its drumming on the canvas, and somewhere down the column a mule complained in the long-suffering tone of its kind. Doss caught Lena's eye through the wagon's gap and raised his eyebrows. She gave him a small shake of the head. *Not yet. Nothing to report yet.*

"Double the watches tonight," Corren said at last. "And if you see anyone cutting at my mountains—"

"They're not your mountains."

"—*anyone*," Corren continued, with the patience of a man accustomed to being rich, "you'll tell me immediately."

Lena left without answering. It wasn't insubordination, exactly. It was that she'd already decided she wasn't going to tell him immediately, and lying about it seemed redundant.

* * *

The ambush came at the third switchback, an hour past dawn, when the rain had thinned to a grey mist and the column had let its spacing slacken.

Later, Lena would go over it a hundred times — the way the ambushers had chosen the exact bend where the road narrowed against the cliff, the way they'd waited for the wagons to string out, the way they'd hit the lead guard first with a crossbow bolt that should have been hers to catch. It was professional work. That was the part that kept her up in the weeks after: not the blood, but the *competence*. Whoever they were, they'd scouted the caravan, timed the route, and picked their ground the way she would have picked it.

The first Lena knew of it was the bolt.

It took Hesk through the throat, ten feet ahead of her, and he was dead before his knees hit the road. Then the rocks on the uphill side came alive — a dozen figures in grey oilcloth, faces wrapped, crossbows and short spears, and the disciplined silence of people who'd done this before and intended to do it again.

"Ambush! Left side, rocks!" Lena was already moving, already shouting, her body running the arithmetic of survival while her mind was still catching up. "Doss, wagons — get the mules turned, get Corren behind the salt! Shields up, *shields*!"

The hired blades were good men and women, mostly, and they did what hired blades do: they made a wall of themselves between the money and the knives. Lena put her back against the cliff face and went up the rocks at the flankers, because that was the only move that mattered — the high ground or the grave, and she'd never cared much for graves.

She killed the first man before he saw her. The second turned at the last moment and she took his spear-hand off at the wrist and put him off the path with a boot to the chest. The third was faster.

They traded three strokes on the wet stone, rain-slick and breathless, and then the man's hood tore back on a parry and Lena saw his face — young, ordinary, frightened — and on his cheekbone, in ink so fresh it still shone wet in the mist, the broken circle with the nail through it.

The same mark from the shrines.

The man saw her see it, and something changed in his face. Not fear. *Recognition*, or the hope of it. He opened his mouth — to say something, to ask something, she'd never know — because Doss came up the rocks behind him with a maul and the conversation ended the way most conversations ended on the old roads.

The ambush broke ten minutes after it started. The grey-cloaks withdrew in good order, taking their wounded, leaving six dead on the road and Hesk among them. They hadn't touched the wool. They hadn't touched the salt. They hadn't touched Corren.

They'd gone for the *guards* first.

Lena stood in the aftermath, chest heaving, her knife wet to the hilt, and looked down at the dead man at her feet. She crouched and pulled his oilcloth open. Under it he wore no soldier's kit, no bandit's motley. He wore a uniform — plain, grey, well-made — and beneath the collar of it, on a cord around his neck, a wooden talisman carved with the broken circle.

She'd cut men down for ten years. She'd never once had one wear a *holy symbol* into a fight against her.

"Captain?" Doss, pale, holding his maul like he wasn't sure what it was for. "They didn't take anything. Why would they not take anything?"

Lena didn't answer. She was looking at the shrine niche ten feet up the road, where the new sigil had been cut into the stone. Rainwater was pooling in the fresh grooves, and in the grey light, with the blood washing off the road in pink threads toward the valley, the broken circle seemed to hold the water the way a cup holds wine.

*The old roads are changing hands,* she thought. And the thought that followed it was worse, quiet, the kind of thought you don't say out loud because saying it makes it a plan:

*And nobody asked the old god who used to hold them.*

* * *

They buried Hesk at the roadside, under a cairn, because the ground was too wet to dig deep and no one wanted to carry a body down the switchbacks. The hired blades stood in the rain with their helmets off, and Master Corren watched from his wagon with the expression of a man recalculating his margins.

Lena said the words over the cairn. Not the free cities' words, not the company's words. The old words, the ones her mother taught her, the ones for sending someone into the dark with their eyes open.

*Veyra, who does not speak. Veyra, who sees in the dark. He walks into your country now. Keep his hands steady. Keep his mouth shut. Tell him —*

She stopped.

*Tell him what?* There was nothing to tell him. There had been nothing to tell anyone for years. The prayer was a habit, and the habit was a lie she told herself every morning, and standing over Hesk's cairn in the rain, with a dead man's broken-circle talisman in her pocket, Lena found she couldn't say the lie one more time.

She finished the rite in silence. The others probably thought that was fitting — Veyra's blade, sending her dead off wordlessly. They didn't know.

That night, in her bedroll under the wagon, Lena took out the talisman and turned it over in her fingers. Cheap wood, careful work. A soldier's god, whoever he was. A god with a mark fresh enough to still smell of the knife that cut it.

*Veyra, who does not speak,* she thought, one last time, the way you close a door on a room you're leaving.

And for the first time in twenty years, she didn't say the rest. She just lay in the dark and listened to the rain, and the silence where her god used to be, and tried to decide which frightened her more: that Veyra had abandoned the roads, or that something new had come to take them.

Three days later, at the waystation below the pass, a dying smuggler would press a folded scrap of hide into her hands and whisper about the gods singing under the stone. But that was three days away, and Lena didn't know it yet.

All she knew was that the mountains had a new mark on them, and her god had gone quiet as the grave, and somewhere in the wet dark above the road, the broken circle was watching her back.
