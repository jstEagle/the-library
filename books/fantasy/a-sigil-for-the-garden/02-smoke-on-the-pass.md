## 2. Smoke on the Pass

The waystation at Grey Hollow smelled of wet wool and old smoke, the way all mountain places did in autumn, and Lena Villalobos sat with her back to the wall because a mercenary who trusted walls was already half dead.

Rain had followed her up from the foothills for two days. It came down now in long grey sheets that turned the pass road to soup and made the pines stand like mourners along the switchbacks. Inside, the station was one low room with a hearth that smoked more than it burned, six travelers pretending not to look at each other, and a smuggler bleeding out on the bench nearest the door.

He'd come in an hour past, walking upright through the rain like a man determined to die indoors rather than in the mud. He was maybe fifty, grey-bearded, dressed as a tinker with a pack too heavy for tinkering. Three arrows stood out of him — no, two now; he'd snapped off the third himself when he thought no one was watching, and Lena had watched, because watching was what she was paid for.

The innkeep's wife had tried to stop the bleeding. The smuggler had let her try for ten minutes, then put his hand over hers and said, "Save the linen," which told Lena everything about his odds.

She should have looked away. She didn't.

Veyra's silence had been sitting on her chest since the foothills, since the ambush, since the sigil she'd seen burned into a dead soldier's pauldron — a mark she knew from her mother's stories and had never once seen in the flesh. Praying felt foolish now. She prayed anyway, the way you scratch an itch that isn't there. *Hear me, silent one. Or don't.* The god gave nothing back. Veyra hadn't given anything back in ten years, and before that, never loudly.

The smuggler's eyes found her across the room. Not by accident. He'd been tracking her since he came through the door — she'd noticed that too, catalogued it, filed it under *trouble*.

Now he beckoned.

Lena stayed where she was. In her experience, men who beckoned wanted something, and what they wanted was usually expensive.

"Mercenary," he said. His voice carried further than it should have, given the state of him. "Grey cloak. Veyra's knot at your throat." His finger touched the cord at his own neck — the plain looped knot every servant of the silent god wore, or used to wear, back when anyone bothered. "I need a woman who keeps her word."

"You need a priest," Lena said.

"Priests run." He coughed, wet and deep. "You don't strike me as a runner."

The innkeep's wife retreated to the hearth with her basket of ruined linen. The other travelers developed sudden fascinations with their cups. Rain hammered the shutters.

"What's in the pack?" Lena asked.

"Roads." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "Roads that aren't supposed to exist anymore."

* * *

His name was Doss. He wouldn't say more than that, and when Lena pressed — name, employer, who shot him — he laughed until the laughing cost him something and stopped.

"Soldiers," he managed instead. "New livery. New mark." He drew it in the spilled ale on the table with one shaking finger: a circle broken by three descending lines, like a cage seen edge-on. "You've seen it."

"I've seen it," Lena admitted. "On the men who hit my caravan."

"And you're still breathing, so they let you go." He studied her with the flat appraisal of a man pricing a horse. "Or they wanted you to carry something home. Fear travels fastest."

"They didn't leave much to carry." Six of her employer's twelve guards dead, the cargo scattered, Lena herself left alive in the mud with rain running into her eyes and a new sigil staring up at her from a corpse's shoulder. She'd taken the pauldron. It was wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, and she hadn't decided yet whether that was evidence or trophy or warning.

Doss fumbled at the buckles of his pack. His hands weren't working right anymore; she could see it, the fine tremor, the blood loss finally collecting its debt. She crossed the room without deciding to and undid the straps herself.

Inside: oilskin bundles, wax-sealed, and beneath them a leather tube such as mapmakers used. And beneath *that*, wrapped in a scrap of altar-cloth that made the innkeep's wife cross herself from across the room, a strip of cured hide covered in marks.

Not letters. Sigils. Lines and knots and interlocking circles, drawn in ink that shifted color when the firelight caught it — brown, then deep red, then a green like moss on north stone. Lena had seen script like it exactly once, carved into the lintel of a shrine her mother had taken her to as a child, back before the shrines went dark. Her mother had called it *the gods' own shorthand*. Her mother had also believed the world was fair, and both beliefs had died around the same winter.

"That's not a map," Lena said.

"It's a map," Doss said. "For the right reader. For everyone else it's kindling." He pulled the hide toward himself, cradling it a moment like a child, then held it out. "Take it east. Over the pass, down the Salt Road, to Corvale. There's a woman — Sera Vane — she'll know what to do."

"I'm not a courier."

"No." He grinned again, bloody. "You're worse. You're faithful. I watched you pray when you thought nobody saw. Nobody prays to Veyra anymore unless they mean it. She doesn't answer. That's the whole point of her."

Something cold walked down Lena's spine. "What do you know about Veyra?"

"I know she stopped answering everywhere at once, ten years ago, same season Malachar started winning battles he had no business winning." Doss's voice dropped, and even dying, even soaked in his own blood in a leaking waystation, he glanced at the shutters before he said it. "I know what's under Thornfell. I've run the old roads my whole life — the ones the kings closed, the ones the priests pretended were never dug. And I'm telling you, mercenary, the roads are singing."

"You're feverish."

"I'm dying. Different thing." He gripped her wrist. His hand was ice. "The gods are singing under the stone. All of them. Every god that ever went quiet — Veyra, the Twins, old Marrow-and-Bloom, the river mothers, all of them — they're under Thornfell, and they're *singing*, and Malachar is listening. Do you understand? He built a cage and he's drinking them dry, one hymn at a time, and every soldier in his army fights like a man possessed because some of them literally are—"

The door banged open.

* * *

Four soldiers came in with the rain behind them, and these wore the broken-circle sigil on black-lacquered breastplates, and they moved like men who had done this before: one to each side of the room, one to the door, one to the hearth, cutting the space into quarters before anyone finished standing.

"Innkeep," the leader said. He was young, clean-shaven, bored in the way that frightened Lena more than cruelty would have. Bored men didn't negotiate. "Anyone come through in the last day?"

The innkeep's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Lena had already moved. It wasn't a decision; it was ten years of contract work folding itself around her like a cloak. She'd drifted toward the shadowed end of the bench while the soldiers' eyes were still adjusting from the rain, and now she stood behind the chimney stack with her hand on her knife and her weight balanced, watching the geometry of the room.

They were looking for Doss. They would find Doss. The question was whether they swept the rest of the room first.

The bored one's gaze traveled the travelers — a wool merchant, two pilgrims whose piety had clearly been a costume choice, a drover, the innkeep's wife wringing her apron. Then it reached the empty bench where Doss had been.

Because Doss wasn't on it.

He was beside Lena, having crossed the room on legs that shouldn't have worked, moving with the particular silence of men who'd spent their lives on roads that punished noise. He pressed the map tube into her hands — hard, insistent, final — and then he stepped out from behind the chimney into full view.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "Looking for me?"

The soldiers moved. Lena didn't watch all of it. She watched enough: the bored one drawing steel without hurry, Doss meeting him with a tinker's hammer he must have kept up his sleeve, the first exchange ringing loud, the second ending it. Doss went down against the bench and the bored one put a boot on his chest and looked around the room with those flat, patient eyes.

"The pack," the soldier said. "Where's the pack?"

Nobody answered. The rain filled the silence.

"He came in with a pack," the soldier said, still bored, still scanning. "Leather. Big. Someone took it off him, or someone's wearing it, or someone's holding it, and I want to know which."

Lena had the tube inside her coat, against her ribs, and the sigil-hide wrapped around it, and her heart was going like a war drum, and she understood with perfect clarity that she was going to die in this room if she didn't do something, and equally clear that the something available to her was nothing.

Then Doss laughed.

It was a terrible sound, blood and air and mockery all together, and it dragged every eye in the room to him. "Pack's in the stable," he said. "Under the floorboards. Third stall. Go on. Fetch it."

The bored one considered him. "Why tell me?"

"Because I'm dead either way," Doss said, "and I'd rather die knowing you wasted time digging in horse shit."

The soldier's expression didn't change. He nodded to one of his men, who went out into the rain. Then he crouched, took Doss's chin in one gauntleted hand, and studied his face like a scholar studying text.

"Doss of the Low Roads," he read aloud. "Courier, smuggler, thief of things their owners forgot they owned. His Excellency has been looking for you for a month." He straightened. "Burn the station. Leave the locals. Kill the mercenary."

The last sentence arrived without emphasis, the way weather arrives, and Lena was already moving — through the gap between pilgrim and wall, knife out, not toward the door but toward the window at the room's far end, the small one over the washbasin that every waystation kept for throwing out dishwater and, occasionally, mercenaries.

Glass broke. Cold air and rain swallowed her. Behind her, inside, Doss shouted something — words lost under thunder — and she would spend years wondering if it had been a curse or a blessing or just her name.

She hit the mud, rolled, came up running.

* * *

The stable was thirty feet away and might as well have been thirty miles. She could hear the soldier in the third stall already, boards tearing up, cursing. Which meant the pack was real, which meant Doss had sent them chasing a decoy, which meant whatever was in the actual pack—

Was in her hands. The tube. The hide. She'd forgotten, in the chaos, that she was carrying it, and now its weight against her ribs felt less like luggage and more like a brand.

Behind her, light bloomed orange against the rain. They hadn't waited to search properly. Oil, torch, done — the waystations of the high passes burned fast, pitch-soaked timber going up like a signal pyre, and Lena understood that was exactly what it was. A signal. *Here. This road. This night.*

She ran the other way, up-slope, off the road entirely, into the pines where the rain came down in needles and the dark was total. Behind her the waystation roared. She heard a scream that might have been the innkeep's wife and might have been the wind, and she hated herself for not turning around, and she turned around anyway, and there was nothing to be done, and she hated herself for that too.

At the treeline she stopped, chest heaving, and looked back.

The station burned like a fallen star. Shapes moved before it — soldiers, unhurried, thorough. One of them dragged something out onto the road and left it there for the rain to deal with. She didn't need to see the face. She knew the beard, the tinker's clothes, the pack that had been too heavy for tinkering.

Doss had walked into that room knowing they were coming. Had known, probably, for miles — had seen the smoke of their cookfires, or heard horses, or simply understood that a man carrying what he carried didn't get to die warm. So he'd chosen a room with a stranger in it. A stranger who prayed to the silent god. A stranger who kept her word.

*I need a woman who keeps her word.*

"I never promised you anything," Lena said out loud, to the rain, to the burning building, to nobody. Her voice cracked on the second word and she despised that too.

The rain kept falling. The fire kept burning. Somewhere under the thunder, she thought — and told herself she was imagining it, told herself it was exhaustion, told herself every reasonable thing a reasonable person tells herself in the dark — she thought she heard singing.

Low. Many-voiced. Rising out of the stone beneath her boots like heat out of summer rock, harmonies stacked on harmonies, a sound with no single source, the way a choir has no single throat. It lasted three heartbeats. Maybe four.

Then it was gone, and the pass was only a pass, and the rain was only rain, and Lena Villalobos stood alone in the pines with a dead man's map against her ribs and a choice she had not agreed to make.

East was Corvale. East was the Salt Road, and this Sera Vane, and whatever waited at the end of a smuggler's dying errand. West was down the mountain, back to the company, back to contracts and wages and a captain who asked no questions, back to a world where gods stayed silent and dead men stayed dead and mercenary meant *nothing is mine to carry*.

She stood there long enough for the rain to find the seam of her collar and start working down her spine.

Then she unslung her pack, took out the oilcloth bundle, and unwrapped the pauldron she'd taken from the ambushed soldier. The broken circle gleamed dully in the firelight from below. Cage-work. She could see it now, with Doss's voice in her ears: three descending lines, bars, a lid. She'd been carrying a piece of the cage on her back for two days without knowing its shape.

She threw it into the ravine. It fell without sound, swallowed by dark and water, and somehow that felt like the first honest act of her life.

Lena tightened her cloak, checked her knives out of habit, and turned east.

She told herself it was because the western road was watched. She told herself it was because Corvale paid well and her purse was thin. She told herself a dozen mercenary reasons, practical and unsentimental, and every one of them rang false as a copper-plated coin.

Under the stone, the gods were singing. And one of them — the one who had never once answered her, in ten years of prayers offered into silence — had let a dying stranger put a map in her hands.

That wasn't coincidence. Lena didn't believe in coincidence. She believed in payment, in debts, in ledgers that always balanced. Somebody had spent a smuggler's life tonight to put that map against her ribs, and somewhere, someday, the ledger would come due.

She just wished, walking east through the rain with the fire fading behind her and the song still ghosting at the edge of her hearing, that she knew whose account she was spending from — and what, in the end, it would cost her to pay.
