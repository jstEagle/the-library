## 3. What the Free Cities Remember

Corvale smelled of incense and wet stone when they rode through the south gate, and both smells were wrong.

Lena had known the city for eleven years — long enough to know it as a place that kept its gods the way merchants kept ledgers: closed, balanced, and rarely opened in public. The temples of Corvale had been shuttered since before she'd been old enough to hold a blade. The old faith was a thing people whispered about at funerals and nowhere else. Veyra's shrine behind the tanneries had been a storage room for salted hides for as long as anyone could remember.

Now there were candles in every window of the temple district, and the smell of myrrh rolled down the hill like fog off a river.

"Tell me this is a festival," Brann said beside her, reining his mare close to hers. He was chewing on something — he was always chewing on something — and his voice carried the particular flatness he used when he didn't want the captain to hear him thinking out loud.

"It's not a festival," Lena said.

"How do you know?"

"Because festivals end."

They rode on. The company strung out behind them in road order, forty-two riders and two wagons, mud to the axles from three days of foothill rain. They'd come down out of the high country with an escort contract half-finished and a dead employer, and Captain Ondrey wanted them inside walls before anyone started asking questions about the ambush on the pass. Corvale was neutral ground, or had been. Neutral cities made good markets for mercenary companies, and better ones for rumors.

The rumors here had teeth.

At the fountain square where the Salt Road met the processional way, a crowd had gathered around the steps of the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn — a building Lena had only ever seen boarded up, its stained glass cracked and gray with pigeon filth. Now the boards were gone. The glass blazed. And the doors stood open, and people were walking out of them weeping.

Not grieving. That was the thing that stopped her hand on her reins and made her sit there in the churn of traffic while carts honked around her. These people weren't grieving. A woman came down the steps holding a small clay lamp lit from the altar flame, and her face was wrecked in the specific way of someone who has received something they'd stopped believing they'd ever get. Behind her, a man knelt on the cold stones with his forehead pressed to the ground, saying something over and over that Lena couldn't hear over the noise of the square, and didn't need to. She knew the shape of it. Everyone who'd ever prayed knew the shape of it.

*You answered. You answered me.*

Brann spat. "That's theater," he said. "Some priest got ambitious. Paid a few criers."

"The priests are dead, Brann. Most of them. The ones that aren't are eighty years old and live in attics." She nudged her mare forward. "Who paid for the glass? Look at it. That glass hasn't been whole in forty years. Someone replaced it overnight."

"People do strange things when the wind changes."

"The wind didn't change." She heard her own voice come out low and hard and didn't soften it. "Something woke up."

* * *

The company billeted at the Sign of the Broken Wheel, a caravanserai near the stockyards that Ondrey had been using for years because the owner asked no questions and watered the wine just enough to be profitable without being insulting. Lena took a room facing the yard, cleaned her kit, and tried not to look at the map.

It was folded into oilcloth inside her breastplate, where it had lived since the pass. Three days now. She could feel its weight the way you feel a splinter — not painful, exactly, but present, insisting on its own reality. Every time she thought about taking it out and looking at it again, some animal part of her brain said *no*, the way a dog refuses a doorway it doesn't like.

She took it out anyway, alone, lamplight low. Unrolled it across the bed.

Sigils crawled across the hide in lines that seemed to shift if she looked away and back — she'd told herself that was firelight, exhaustion, the afterimage of the burning waystation still printed on her eyes. The dying smuggler's blood had dried brown along one edge. Under the sigils, faint as watermark, ran roads: the smuggler roads under Thornfell, the mountain that guarded the eastern passes into Malachar's territory. Roads that weren't on any map she'd ever bought, and she'd bought maps from men who sold maps to other mapmakers.

And threaded through the sigils, one mark repeated — a vertical line crossed by three short strokes, like a reed cut and notched. She knew that mark. Every child raised anywhere near a Veyran shrine knew it, even if the shrines had all been converted to granaries a generation ago. It was the sign of the silent god. The listening god. The one who never answered.

Except that now, apparently, everything answered.

A knock at the door. She had the map folded and back inside her breastplate before the second knock fell, which told her something about how fast her hands had gotten lately.

"Lena." Ondrey's voice. Not asking permission. "Common room. All of us."

* * *

Forty-two mercenaries filled the common room of the Broken Wheel, and for once nobody was drinking much. Ondrey stood at the head of the long table with her arms crossed — a broad, gray-templed woman built like a door, who'd fought in four border wars and buried two husbands and ran the company the way a millrace runs a wheel: relentlessly, and in one direction only.

"You've all seen what's happening in this city," she said. "Temples opening. Crowds in the streets. Yesterday the garrison commander publicly burned his commission from the Regency and declared Corvale a free city again. This morning, three more towns in the valley did the same. I'm told there's praying in Harrowgate. *Harrowgate.*"

Someone laughed. It died quickly.

"Now. Some of you think this is wonderful. The old gods coming back, miracles in the streets, the usurper's writ tearing like wet paper. Some of you think it's a trick. I've heard both arguments at this table for two days and I'm tired of them." She let her gaze travel the room. "Here's what matters to us. There are two sides forming, and both of them will pay, and we are going to sell to one of them. That's the business. Everything else is weather."

"Which side?" That was Vess, up near the front, a lean Kettering woman with a scarred jaw. "Because if the free cities win, they'll remember who helped Malachar. And if Malachar wins—"

"Malachar wins nothing," said an old voice from the back. Doss, their farrier, sixty years of campfires in his lungs. "You don't know what's marching under his banners. My cousin saw the army at Vell's Crossing. Said the soldiers don't sleep. Said they sing in their sleep, all together, same song, and no officer ordered it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"They're well paid," Ondrey said flatly. "That's what they are. And well-paid soldiers beat pious ones nine times in ten. But we're not deciding tonight. We have a contract outstanding and a reputation to keep, and while we decide, we work." Her eyes found Lena across the crowded room, and Lena felt the moment settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. "Villalobos. A word. Upstairs."

* * *

Ondrey's room was spare: cot, chest, map table, a window overlooking the stockyards. She poured two cups of the bad wine without asking and pushed one across.

"You were on the pass," she said. "When Halvatt's caravan went down."

"I was."

"You brought out six of his people and left eleven in the dirt, and you brought back a story about soldiers wearing a sigil nobody recognized." Ondrey drank. "Describe it again."

Lena described it again. The black sunburst, but wrong — the rays bent inward, curling toward the center like fingers closing around something. She'd cut it off a dead man's pauldron herself. It was in her saddlebag now, wrapped in cloth, because she hadn't been able to make herself throw it away and hadn't been able to explain why either.

Ondrey listened without expression. Then she said, "Show me what else you brought off that pass."

The room went very quiet, though it had already been quiet. Lena kept her face still, which was the first skill the company had ever taught her, before swordwork, before contracts. *Your face is your gate,* Sergeant Imre used to say. *Keep it barred.*

"What else would I have brought?"

"Don't." Ondrey set down her cup. "I've run this company twenty years. I know what a scavenger looks like coming off a burned site, and I know what you look like right now, which is a woman carrying something she hasn't reported. I'm not angry. I'd have done the same at your age. But we're past that now." She leaned forward, palms on the map table. "There were agents at the gate this morning. Malachar's people, traveling quiet, buying information. They're paying gold for anything out of the eastern passes. Accounts of the ambush. Descriptions of the new sigil. Names." A pause. "Especially names."

"Names of whom?"

"Anyone who came off that road carrying something unusual." Ondrey held her eyes. "Lena. What did the smuggler give you?"

Every instinct she had said *lie*. Lie well; she was good at it; she'd lied to death-debt brokers and border wardens and once, memorably, to a bishop. But Ondrey had pulled her out of a gutter in Corvale at fifteen, fed her, armed her, taught her that a contract was the only holy thing left in the world. Loyalty wasn't love. Loyalty was a debt you kept paying because otherwise you were nothing but an animal with a knife.

"He gave me a map," Lena said. "Of the roads under Thornfell."

Ondrey didn't move for a long moment. When she did, it was slow, the way you move around a snare.

"Under Thornfell," she repeated. "The smuggler roads."

"So the man claimed. I haven't verified it."

"And the sigils on it?"

"Some kind of cipher. Old script. I can't read it."

That much was true, and it was also, Lena realized as she said it, no longer entirely true. Because last night, half-asleep, staring at the ceiling, she had looked at the memory of those marks — the notched reed above all — and felt something in her recognize it. Not understand it. *Recognize* it, the way you recognize a face across a market crowd, someone you knew before you knew yourself. She had decided not to think about that. She was getting good at deciding not to think about things.

Ondrey straightened. "Then here's how it goes. Tomorrow morning you take that map to the agents at the Gilded Scale inn. You negotiate. You get us a retainer against whatever contract comes out of it — Malachar pays in advance or we walk. That's an order, Villalobos, and it's a fair one. The company eats off what we find. You know the rule."

"I know the rule."

"Good." Ondrey softened by a degree, which for her was nearly tenderness. "You did well on the pass. Six lives out of an ambush that should have killed forty. Whatever's in that map, it's worth more to forty-two people than to one, and that's the whole arithmetic of what we are. Get some sleep."

Lena took her cup, saluted with it, and went out.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat on her cot with the map unrolled on her knees and the lamp turned down to a coal, and she thought about arithmetic.

Forty-two people. She could count them: Brann arguing dice in the yard below, Vess oiling her crossbow, young Pell who still wrote letters home to a mother who thought he was a carter, Doss with his cousin's story about singing soldiers. Forty-two mouths that ate because the company held together, and the company held together because contracts were kept, and this was a contract, or would be, the moment she walked into the Gilded Scale and put the map on a table.

And on the other side of the scale: a burned waystation. A dying man's blood drying on goatskin. *They're singing under the stone.* The words wouldn't leave her. She'd heard a lot of dying men say a lot of things, and most of it was noise — mothers' names, debts, curses. But this man had pressed the map into her hands like he was passing a brand, and he'd looked at her — at *her*, specifically, out of everyone in that yard — and said, "You can read it. I can see it on you. You can read it," and then the soldiers' arrows had finished the sentence.

Below in the yard, someone laughed. Life going on, the way it does.

Lena thought about the woman with the clay lamp on the temple steps, and the man with his forehead on the stones. People who had prayed into a silence for so long they'd built their whole lives around the silence, and then one morning the silence spoke, and they wept in the street because they didn't know what else to do with a universe that had changed its mind.

She had prayed too. Out of habit, on the pass, kneeling in the mud with dead men around her — *Veyra, if you're there.* Nothing. Ten years of nothing. She'd assumed the gods were gone, worn out like old coin, and she'd built her life around that silence the same as everyone else.

But the smuggler had said the gods weren't gone. He'd said they were *singing under the stone*, and that the roads under Thornfell moved for the faithful, and he'd given the map to her.

And now her captain — her captain, who owned the debt of every meal Lena had eaten since she was fifteen — wanted her to carry that map to the man who was, if any of it was true, doing *whatever was being done to them* under the mountain.

Lena looked down at the sigils. In the low light, the notched-reed mark seemed darker than the rest, a hole cut in the skin of the map. She put her thumb over it.

*Keep the gate barred,* Imre used to say. But Imre was ten years dead, and the gate wasn't the problem anymore. The problem was that something on the other side of the gate knew her name.

She rolled the map up. She did not put it back inside her breastplate. She wrapped it in oilcloth, tied it with cord, and set it on the table where she could see it from the cot — visible, separate, a thing she was choosing what to do with rather than a thing she simply carried.

Tomorrow morning, she told herself, she would go to the Gilded Scale. She would sit across from Malachar's agents. She would negotiate.

She told herself that twice.

Outside, far off toward the temple district, a bell began to ring — not the garrison bell, not a market bell, but one of the old temple bells that hadn't sounded in a generation, whose rope Lena would have sworn had rotted away years ago. It rang slow and deep, and under its note, so faint she might have imagined it, the city seemed to hold its breath.

Lena lay back on the cot and did not sleep, and the bell rang until dawn, and in the morning the agents at the Gilded Scale would be waiting — and so, though she didn't know it yet, would a defrocked priest with mud on his hem and a story about prisoners.
