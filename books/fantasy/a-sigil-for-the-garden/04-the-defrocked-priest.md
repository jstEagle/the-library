## 4. The Defrocked Priest

Anselm had been sweeping the steps of a shrine that no longer had a god when the woman with the mercenary's walk came through Corvale's temple quarter, and he knew before she reached the third landing that she was carrying something holy.

He knew it the way he'd once known rain coming over the hills — not through evidence but through the body, some older instrument than reason. The shrine of Veyra had been empty for eleven years, its altar cold, its offering bowls full of nothing but rainwater and pigeon filth, and Anselm swept it anyway because a man needed a reason to climb stairs in the morning and because defrocked priests, like retired soldiers, do not stop performing their duties merely because the pay has stopped. He was fifty-three years old. His hands ached in damp weather. And when Lena Villalobos passed below him on the street with her hood up and her stride eating ground, every ache in his body turned toward her like a compass needle finding north.

There was a weight on her. Not the weight of coin or steel — Anselm had spent twenty years among mercenaries' chaplains and knew what armed wealth looked like moving — but the weight of *meaning*. Something that bent the air around it. Something that made the old stones of the shrine hum faintly against his broom handle, a vibration he felt in his teeth.

He dropped the broom.

He took the steps two at a time, which his knees punished him for immediately and without mercy, and caught up with her at the corner where the Street of Bells met the processional way.

"Forgive me," he said, breathless. "Sister. Traveler. Forgive me, but you're carrying something that doesn't belong to you."

She turned. She was younger than he'd expected — thirty at most, dark-haired, with the flat gray eyes of someone who had stopped expecting good news and organized her life accordingly. Her hand had already found the knife at her belt without any visible decision to draw it. That, too, told him things about her.

"I don't know you," she said.

"No one does. That's rather the point of my current condition." He gestured at himself: the plain wool coat, the collarless shirt, the absence of any mark of office. "Anselm Vairre. Formerly priest of the Third Circle at the Cathedral of the Nine Voices, formerly lecturer in sacred sigilry, currently a man who sweeps an empty shrine because no one will give him anything better to do."

"You're mad," she said, without heat. It was an assessment, not an insult. Mercenaries sorted the world quickly: threat, non-threat, madman. He could see himself being filed under the third heading, filed and dismissed.

"Almost certainly. But indulge a madman for one question." He leaned closer, and lowered his voice, though the street was nearly empty in the gray drizzle. "The thing under your coat, wrapped in oilcloth, carried high on your left side so it won't knock against your sword arm — is it marked? Does it bear sigils?"

He watched her stillness change quality. A soldier's stillness was already a kind of coiled readiness; hers went from ready to *set*, like a trap armed. For three full seconds neither of them moved, and the drizzle came down between them, and somewhere down the Street of Bells a bell rang the hour badly, off-key, the way all bells rang now that the gods had stopped listening.

"Who sent you?" she said quietly.

"No one. That is precisely what I've been trying to make people understand for eleven years." He spread his hands. "No one sends me anywhere anymore. I send myself. It's a habit I can't break."

"Then here's my answer." She stepped past him.

"*The gods didn't leave,*" he called after her.

She stopped. She didn't turn around. But she stopped, in the middle of the wet street, with the drizzle beading on her hood, and Anselm felt the words land the way a thrown stone lands — heard the impact even if he couldn't see the wound.

"They didn't die, they didn't depart, they weren't never-there at all, whatever the fashionable theologians are preaching this season." He came closer, careful, the way one approaches a horse that might kick. "They were taken. Caged. There is a difference between silence and absence, sister, and I have spent eleven years trying to teach that difference to men who burned my credentials and told me grief makes scholars of no one."

Now she turned. Slowly. The gray eyes had come alive in a way that wasn't comfort — there was fury in them, and worse, hope, and hope was clearly the thing she hated more.

"Say that again," she said.

"The gods are prisoners."

"Where."

"Under Thornfell. Under the mountain, in the deep ways, where the old smuggler roads run and where the broker-houses keep their ledgers." He watched her face as he said it and saw — this was the strange part, the part that made his heart hammer against his ribs like a fist on a door — saw that she already knew. Not suspected. *Knew.* Knew the way you know a scar is yours. "You've seen something."

"I've heard something," she said. "A dying man sang it. Gods singing under the stone." Her jaw worked. "And I've been ordered to deliver this—" she touched the oilcloth bundle without drawing it out— "to Malachar's agents. By my captain. Tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow you'll hand the cage-keys to the jailer."

"I know." The fury flashed openly now. "You think I don't know that? You think I haven't spent two days knowing exactly what I'm carrying and to whom?" She laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it at all. "I prayed to Veyra for ten years and got silence. Ten years. I built my whole life inside that silence. And now a stranger in a wet street tells me the silence was a *cage*, and expects me to—what? What exactly do you want from me, priest?"

"To come east with me," Anselm said. "To Thornfell."

The look she gave him would have stopped a charging boar.

"No," she said.

"Sister—"

"Don't call me sister. My name is Villalobos. Lena Villalobos. And the answer is no. I'm a sellsword, not a pilgrim. I take contracts, not causes. Causes get you killed and then they get other people killed explaining why." She was walking again, fast, and he followed, because following was the only tool left in his kit and he had learned long ago that stubbornness could substitute for grace when grace ran dry.

"The map is written in sigils only the faithful can read," he said, keeping pace. "That's not poetry, that's mechanics. The old roads around Thornfell move. They shift like riverbeds. A sigil-map isn't paper showing a place; it's a contract showing a relationship. Without a reader, it's just ink."

"Then I'll find another reader."

"There are four living readers east of the Salt Road. I trained two of them. One is dead, one is Malachar's, and one lost her faith so thoroughly she now sells indulgences to smugglers and would sell you to them too if the price was right." He let that sit. "The fourth is me."

She stopped at the mouth of an alley, turned, and looked at him for a long moment with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she resented.

"Why were you cast out?" she asked. "Really. Not the version you tell drunk strangers."

"The real version." He sighed, and the drizzle chose that moment to thicken into honest rain, and he pulled his coat tighter and told her the truth standing in an alley because apparently that was the shape his life had settled into. "Eleven years ago I stood in the Cathedral of the Nine Voices during the autumn convocation and told the assembled hierarchy that the gods had not withdrawn from the world. That their silence had a *texture*. That prayers weren't going unanswered — they were being *intercepted*. That somewhere beneath the earth something was drinking the voices of heaven, and that the church's new doctrine of the Quiet Departure was not theology but collaboration." He shrugged. "The Patriarch asked me to retract. I asked him to open an investigation. We were both disappointed in each other."

"And they cast you out for it."

"They cast me out for embarrassing them. The heresy was incidental. One does not tell powerful men that their house is built on a theft; one especially does not tell them while wearing their vestments." He smiled, and knew the smile was crooked. "So. Now you know the worst of me. I am a man who cannot stop telling the truth in rooms where truth is unwelcome. It has cost me everything except my convictions, which cling like burrs. Come east with me, Villalobos. Or go east and let me follow, since I suspect you'll choose that, and I'd rather we both admit it now and share the fire."

"I said no."

"You did. Clearly and repeatedly. I respect clarity." He nodded toward the alley's far end, where the rain was silvering the rooftops above the merchant district. "I'll be at the shrine of Veyra until dawn. Sweeping. In case you change your mind. And if you don't—" he bowed, absurdly formal, water running off his nose— "then I'll see you on the eastern road. I walk faster than I look."

* * *

He did not sleep. He swept the shrine instead, and thought about the nature of stubbornness, which he had come to regard as the last sacrament available to broken men.

It was near midnight when he admitted to himself what he had known since the moment he'd seen her on the street: that the humming in the old stones hadn't come from her bundle alone. There had been something *in* her, some resonance underneath, the way a bell keeps ringing after the strike. He had felt it again when she stopped in the alley — a pressure behind his eyes, a taste like iron at the back of his throat. Twenty years of sigilry had taught him what resonance meant. Resonance meant relationship. Relationship meant history.

This woman and whatever she carried went back further than a dying smuggler's gift. Much further. He had no doctrine for it yet. He had only the certainty, which sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

Near dawn, footsteps on the shrine steps. He turned, broom in hand, expecting her.

It was not her. It was two men in traveling leathers with the particular clean-cut look of soldiers pretending to be civilians, and the taller one wore a signet ring that Anselm recognized from a lifetime ago: the seal of the Convocation's Office of Inquiry. The men who had come for him once before, in another city, in another life.

"Anselm Vairre," the tall one said. Not a question.

"You have the advantage of me."

"We have reports of sigil materials moving through Corvale. Restricted materials. We're making inquiries at the temples." The man's eyes went past Anselm, cataloguing the shrine, the empty altar, the swept stone. "Odd place for a defrocked man to keep his devotions."

"It's an odd age for devotions generally."

The shorter one drifted, casually, toward the door — cutting off the exit, though Anselm noted he did it with the smoothness of a man who believed he was being subtle. "We understand you correspond with certain parties. Smugglers. Road-wardens. People who use the old marks."

"I correspond with everyone. Loneliness is very democratic."

The tall one stepped closer. Rain dripped from the lintel between them. "Where is the map, priest?"

And there it was. Anselm felt the world tilt slightly, the way it does when a private fear becomes public fact. They knew. Which meant Malachar's people knew, which meant the hunt was already on, which meant the woman with the mercenary's walk and the coal in her chest was carrying the most dangerous object in the world through a city crawling with the Office of Inquiry.

"I don't have any map," Anselm said. "I have a broom. Would you like to inspect the broom?"

The tall one sighed, the sigh of a bureaucrat denied paperwork, and nodded to his companion, and Anselm had time to think, with remarkable calm: *this is going to hurt* — when a voice came from the doorway behind the shorter man, flat and gray and utterly unhurried:

"Gentlemen. You're blocking my shrine."

Lena Villalobos stood in the entrance with the dawn behind her and her hood down, and Anselm saw the two soldiers take in the sword, the stance, the total absence of fear, and begin — visibly, professionally — to recalculate.

"This is church business," the tall one said.

"Not in this building it isn't. This building belongs to a god who hasn't answered anyone in eleven years, which means nobody's minding it, which means it's mine." She hadn't moved. Her hand rested on her belt, easy as conversation. "You want to arrest a sweeper, do it outside. On the street. Where people can watch the Office of Inquiry drag away a man whose crime is holding a broom."

The soldiers looked at each other. Whatever their orders were worth, a scene in a temple quarter at dawn was worth less, and they knew it. The tall one produced a card from his coat and laid it on the altar with two fingers, delicately, as if the stone might bite.

"When you reconsider, priest. The Convocation rewards cooperation."

"When I reconsider," Anselm said, "you'll be the first to know."

They left. Lena watched them go, then turned to Anselm, and her expression was the most interesting thing he'd seen in years: fury and resignation fighting to a draw on a tired face.

"You followed me," she said.

"You didn't answer my question in the alley, so I assumed the matter remained open."

"I told you no."

"You told me no twice. The third time is traditionally decisive, and you haven't spent it yet." He set the broom against the wall. "Also, you saved my life just now, which suggests either conscience or investment, and I'd bet my remaining books on investment."

"I saved my own morning. I need this shrine quiet for an hour and corpses attract attention." She pulled the oilcloth bundle from inside her coat at last and set it on the altar, and even before she unwrapped it Anselm's teeth began to ache and the old stones began to sing.

The map came into the gray light. Sigils crawled across the hide in lines that shifted subtly when he wasn't looking directly at them — alive, responsive, *hungry* — and threaded through them all, repeated like a heartbeat, was the closed-eye mark of Veyra.

"Oh," Anselm breathed. "Oh, you poor beautiful thing. Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me. Soldiers burned the waystation down around us." Her voice was level, but her hand, resting on the altar beside the map, was not quite steady. "He said the gods are singing under the stone. Then he died."

"Yes." Anselm traced the air an inch above the sigils, not touching, feeling the warmth rise off them like heat off summer stone. "Yes, that's the Thornfell script. Pre-conquest. Older than the Quiet Departure, older than the Convocation, older than anything the church admits to remembering." He looked up at her. "And there's a prayer-key woven through the route markers. Do you see these triplets? Each one is half a verse. The reader supplies the rest. Only the faithful can complete them — literally. If you don't believe, the road won't hold your weight."

"So take me east," Lena said. "Read the map. That's the contract. Guide fee negotiable, food and blade provided, and when it's done we part ways and you can sweep whatever you like."

"That's your offer."

"That's the offer."

"And if I say the price is that you listen — really listen — to what the mountain tells us when we get there? That you pray at least once, even if nothing answers? That you consider the possibility that the silence you've built your life inside has a *reason*?" He held up a hand before she could speak. "No. Forget it. Forget conditions. I've been stubborn for eleven years and it's accomplished nothing but blisters. Read the map, walk the road, save the gods or don't. I'll be there regardless. Consider me weather."

She stared at him. Outside, Corvale was waking — carts, gulls, the off-key bell striking a wrong hour — and inside the empty shrine the map lay glowing faintly between them like a coal refusing to die.

"Why?" she said finally. "Why follow a woman who's told you no? Why burn what's left of your life on this?"

Anselm picked up his broom. Turned it over in his hands. Looked at the worn wood where his grip had polished it across eleven years of pointless, faithful mornings.

"Because I told the truth once," he said, "and no one listened, and I have wondered every day since whether the truth was ever said at all. This time I intend to be present for the answer."

* * *

They left Corvale at first light by the pilgrims' gate, separately, thirty minutes apart, because Lena planned movements the way she planned fights — angles first, exits always. Anselm approved of this completely and said so, and was ignored, which he was beginning to understand was simply the climate of their association.

The eastern road climbed through terraced orchards gone wild, through villages where shrines stood freshly swept — swept, Anselm noted, by hands other than his, ordinary hands, hopeful hands, the whole countryside quietly reopening its doors on the chance that someone was home. At midday they passed a woman kneeling at a roadside altar with her children, teaching them an old prayer, stumbling over the verses, and Anselm heard Lena slow her stride by half a step and then force herself forward again.

By late afternoon the rain had cleared and the mountains stood up ahead of them, blue and enormous, with Thornfell somewhere in that massif wearing its crown of cloud like a lid on a pot.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen boundary stone — an old godstone, Anselm realized, its carved sigils worn nearly smooth, one of thousands that had lined the roads before the Departure. He built a small fire. Lena cleaned her blades. Neither spoke for a long time, and the silence was, for once, companionable.

"The dying man," Anselm said eventually. "The one who gave you the map. Did he finish the verse?"

"What verse?"

"The prayer-key. The triplet on the map's western edge. Every faithful reader completes it differently — it's keyed to belief. Mine ends *and the stone shall remember what the tongue forgets*." He poked the fire. "What did yours become?"

Lena was quiet for so long he thought she wouldn't answer.

"When I was a girl," she said at last, staring into the flames, "my sister and I used to pray together. She did the words. I did the listening. She always said the gods liked my listening better than her praying." Her jaw tightened. "The verse finished itself as *and someone should have listened sooner*. Happy now?"

Anselm looked at her across the fire — this hard, guarded, grieving woman who had prayed for ten years into a silence she now knew was a cage, who carried a holy map she'd been ordered to betray, who had saved a stranger because her body wouldn't let her do otherwise — and he understood, suddenly and completely, why the old stones hummed around her.

Not the map. *Her.*

He said none of this. Some truths, he had learned, you carry until the road is ready for them.

Instead he lay back against the godstone, folded his hands on his chest like a man already practicing for a tomb, and said, "Get some sleep, Villalobos. Tomorrow we cross the Salt Road, and I have a feeling the mountain already knows we're coming."

Above them, faint as breath, the worn sigils on the boundary stone began — very softly — to glow.
