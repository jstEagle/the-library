## 5. A Bargain Already Paid

The broker's coin was cold. That was the thing Lena remembered most, ten years later — not the smell of the tunnel, not the sound of her own heartbeat, but the weight of that single black coin in her palm, colder than the mountain around it, as if it had never once been warm.

She had thought about it every day since. She had told herself she'd stopped thinking about it. Both things were true, and neither had ever made the other a lie.

Now she sat in the loft of a waystation three days east of Corvale, the smuggler's map spread across her knees, and the memory came back the way blood comes back to a limb you've slept on — painful, insistent, hers.

* * *

Ten years ago, in the winter the fever took half of Harrow's Bend, Lena Villalobos had owed a death-debt.

Not to a man. To a ledger.

Her father had borrowed against his life from the undermountain brokers — that was how the poor of the foothills said it, *borrowed against your life*, though what they meant was sold a piece of it, years off the end, strength out of the middle, whatever the ledger wanted that season. Her father had sold deep. Deeper than he knew. And when the collectors came down from Thornfell in their gray lacquered masks, they didn't take him. They took the debt itself, folded it into paper, and pressed it into Lena's seventeen-year-old hands, because her father was already three weeks dead and debts, unlike grief, survived the debtor.

Two hundred crowns. Or one soul of comparable weight.

They had explained it politely. That was the worst part, looking back — the courtesy. The collector had removed his mask inside their kitchen so she could see he had an ordinary face, tired, even kind, and he had set the paper on the table between them and turned his teacup handle toward himself like any traveling clerk.

"The ledger is flexible," he'd said. "It prefers coin. It accepts alternatives."

Ilsa had been asleep in the next room. Fourteen years old, fever-skinny, breathing in that whistling way the sickness left behind. Lena could hear her through the wall, could have heard her through stone.

"There's another option," the collector said, following her eyes to the wall, and Lena understood, in the slow cold way you understand a thing you've always known was coming, that he had come for Ilsa all along. That the debt was the shape of a door and her sister was the shape of the key.

"No," Lena said.

"Then we'll return in thirty days."

He left the teacup. He left the paper. He left the door open behind him, which was its own kind of message: *run if you like. The mountain is patient.*

Lena ran. Of course she ran. She took Ilsa over the pass in the dead of winter with a stolen mule and a bag of barley flour, and they made it forty miles before the fever caught Ilsa properly, in a charcoal-burner's hut with snow to the eaves, and the girl burned so hot the snow on the roof above her bed melted in a perfect circle, and Lena sat by her sister's side and watched the circle widen and understood that running was just dying with extra steps.

That was when the second collector found them. Not the polite one. A woman, this time, unmasked, with a voice like water moving under ice.

"You misunderstand the ledger," she said, crouching at the foot of Ilsa's bed. "You think it takes. It trades. Nothing is lost, child. Everything is kept."

"She'll die," Lena said.

"She'll die here." The woman touched Ilsa's burning forehead, and Ilsa sighed and settled, the fever easing by a degree, no more than a degree, a taste. "Or she'll live below. Kept. Whole. The court beneath the mountain has need of souls, and it pays for them, and it does not spend them carelessly. Ask me what she'd be."

"What would she be?"

"Kept," the woman said again, and smiled, and the smile was the first truly frightening thing Lena had seen in her life, because there was nothing cruel in it. There was nothing in it at all. "Ask me what you'd be."

"I don't understand."

"You owe two hundred crowns or a soul. Hers buys your freedom. Yours buys nothing — you're already spent, girl; the debt owns your name. But there is a third entry." The woman drew something from her sleeve and set it on the blanket beside Ilsa's feet. A coin. Black, smooth, no stamp, no face, colder than the room. "A bargain already paid. You walk away free, clear, your name struck from every page. And she goes below, alive, kept, and in exchange—" the woman tilted her head, listening to something Lena couldn't hear, "—the ledger forgives the interest. All of it. Every year your father sold. Gone. Your line starts clean."

"And I never see her again."

"You never see her again," the woman agreed, gently, as if confirming a delivery date. "But she lives. Fevered girls don't, up here. You know that. You've buried half your village."

Lena looked at her sister. Ilsa, fourteen, who had once walked four miles through a storm to bring Lena home from the high pasture with a broken ankle, carrying her on her back, fourteen years old and ninety pounds, laughing the whole way, saying *you're lighter than the mule, Lena, you're barely anything at all.*

"She'd be kept," Lena said. "Alive."

"Asleep, at first. Then awake. Serving. The court is beautiful below. She won't suffer."

Everyone who says *she won't suffer*, Lena would learn, means *I won't watch.*

She took the coin. That was the sin, and she had committed it with her own hand, in a charcoal-burner's hut, while snow fell past the doorway like the world quietly erasing itself. She took the coin because it was warm — no. Because it was cold. Because it was the only thing in the room colder than her choice, and holding it felt like holding the choice outside her body where it could be someone else's.

The woman wrote in a small book with a pen that scratched like a claw. "Struck clean," she said. "Villalobos, Lena. Free. Go north. Forget the road you came by."

And Ilsa, waking briefly in the gray hour before they took her, had looked at Lena and said, "Did you pay it? Did you pay it all?" and Lena had said yes, yes, everything, go back to sleep, and Ilsa had smiled and gone back to sleep, and that lie was the second coin, and Lena carried both of them for ten years.

* * *

In the present, in the loft of the waystation, Anselm looked up from the map and said, "Your hands are shaking."

"They're not."

"They are. You've been staring at the same sigil for a quarter hour and your hands are shaking." He closed the book of old prayers he'd been reading by lamplight. He had a way of closing books that ended conversations he wanted to have. "We should talk about where this map leads."

"It leads east. Under the falls. You've said."

"It leads to the court beneath Thornfell." He kept his voice low. The waystation keeper dozed below; the fire ticked. "Which means it leads among the brokers. Which means, Lena, that you know something about brokers that I can see sitting on your face like weather."

She rolled the map. Her hands were shaking. She let him be right about it and hated him mildly for noticing.

"My father sold years of his life to them," she said. "Debt collectors came after he died. I paid it."

"How?"

"Coin."

Anselm watched her the way he watched everything — too long, too directly, with the particular patience of a man who had been thrown out of his church and had decided patience was the only sacrament left to him. "Veyra's silence started eleven years ago," he said. "The god went quiet. The temples went dark. Everyone says she abandoned us." He tapped the rolled map. "The faithful say otherwise. The faithful say she was *taken*. And taken gods get used, Lena. Their voices get spent. Do you know what else gets spent, down there, according to the oldest texts? Souls. Sold souls. They're currency. They're *light*. They're—"

"Stop."

"—ornament," he finished softly.

The word went into her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Ornament. She saw it immediately, horribly: Ilsa, alive, kept, hung somewhere in a beautiful dark place like a lamp.

"You knew," Anselm said. Not accusing. Worse — understanding. "You've known where souls go. Someone you—"

"I said stop."

He stopped. He was good at that, at least. He gathered his book and moved to the far end of the loft with the tact of a man giving a wounded animal room, and Lena sat with the map across her knees and the lamplight guttering, and did not sleep.

* * *

She dreamed of the kitchen in Harrow's Bend. The collector turning his teacup. The paper on the table. In the dream the paper was the smuggler's map, sigils crawling across it like insects, and the collector's ordinary kind face was Malachar's face was the broker-woman's face was her own, and Ilsa's voice came through the wall, whistling breath, saying *did you pay it, did you pay it all,* and Lena woke with her knife half-drawn and the dream still on her like a hand.

Below, the waystation keeper's dog barked once at nothing and went quiet.

Lena dressed, took the map, and climbed to the roof ridge where the night was hard and starless and the wind came off the pass smelling of snow and, faintly, of smoke — the soldiers had burned the eastern waystation two days back; the smell traveled farther than fire.

She spoke aloud, to the dark, out of habit older than the debt.

"Veyra."

Nothing. Ten years of nothing. She had prayed on contract jobs, on ambush nights, on the morning she'd buried her captain's son, on every anniversary of the hut in the snow, and the silence had been total, seamless, a wall. She had told herself it was the nature of her god — Veyra the Silent, the Unanswered, patron of those who work unseen. Silence was doctrine. Silence was comfort. Silence meant she was listening the way the dead listen.

But Anselm's words had gotten under the doctrine. *Taken. Used. Spent.* And now, tonight, for the first time, the silence didn't feel like absence.

It felt like held breath.

"You knew," Lena whispered to the dark. "Whatever happened down there — you knew what they were doing with us. With the ones we sold." Her hands were steady now. Steadiness was the mercenary's gift; it arrived precisely when feeling would kill you. "Was my sister worth something to them? Is that why the ledger wanted her? Was there a reason, or was she just — inventory?"

The wind moved. Somewhere down the valley, a wolf sang and was answered by another, and the answering call was wrong somehow, too resonant, too much like a voice and not enough like a wolf, and Lena stood very still on the ridge with her hand on her knife until it faded.

The old roads are changing hands, she'd thought on the rain-slick job in the foothills. Now she thought: the old *debts* are changing hands. Everything her family had ever sold, everything anyone had ever sold beneath that mountain, was down there in the dark, kept, whole, ornamented — and the man who owned the mountain was building armies out of voices, and the map on her back led straight into his pocket.

She should burn it. Give it to Malachar's agents, as Captain Dree had ordered, collect the fee, forget the mountain forever. That was the sane arithmetic. Two hundred crowns of sense.

Instead she heard herself say, into the wind, in the flat tone she used for reporting casualties: "I'm coming, Ilsa. I don't know what you are now. I don't know if there's enough of you left to hate me. But I'm coming, and I'm going to pay it — all of it — the way I should have the first time."

The silence received it. Held it. Like breath.

* * *

Three days later, on the Salt Road, with Anselm limping beside her and the mountains standing up blue ahead, Lena saw the rider come down the switchbacks at a pace that meant either pursuit or desperation, and put her hand on her sword.

The rider reined in twenty feet away. A woman, court-clothes ruined to road-clothes, dust to the thighs, eyes doing rapid arithmetic — counting weapons, exits, odds. She looked like money that had learned to run.

"Lena Villalobos," the woman said. "Captain Dree sells your location cheaply, by the way. You should renegotiate." She swung down from the horse without waiting for permission, which told Lena everything about her breeding and nothing useful about her intentions. "My name is Sera Vane. I served at Malachar's court until four days ago. I have proof of what he keeps beneath Thornfell — proof that will end him, if it reaches the right hands. And you—" she pointed at the map case at Lena's hip, and her voice dropped, and underneath the court polish Lena heard something raw, "—you have the only key in the world that opens the door to it. So. Let's discuss terms."

Lena's hand hadn't left her sword. "Why me?"

Sera Vane smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had seen the broker's ledger and read the entries.

"Because," she said, "I've seen the galleries where they keep the bought souls. And I've seen your face before — in the court, in a portrait, hanging in the broker's hall. They keep a picture of you down there, mercenary. Someone below paid a great deal to make sure of it."
