## 6. The Turncoat's Offer

The Salt Road ran white and straight as a scar through the brown hills, and Sera Vane had been walking it for three days in a dead man's boots.

They were good boots, at least. She'd taken them off a courier whose throat she'd opened with a hairpin, and the courier had been a man of taste — oiled leather, double-stitched, sized only slightly too large. She'd stuffed the toes with wool torn from her own petticoat, because a courtier of Malachar's subterranean court understood, above nearly all other truths, that presentation was survival. You could be bleeding out in a ditch, but if your boots were fine and your spine was straight, people hesitated before they finished you.

Hesitation was currency. Sera had spent her whole life learning to mint it.

She paused on the crest of the hill and looked back the way she'd come. No dust. No riders. The road behind her lay empty under a sky the color of old pewter, and for the hundredth time since she'd slipped out of Thornfell through the ash-chutes, she allowed herself to believe — just for a breath — that no one was following.

Then she looked forward again and stopped believing it, because belief was a luxury and she had never once been able to afford it.

Ahead, maybe half a mile down, three figures moved along the road. Two walked close together, heads bent. The third trailed behind like a dog deciding whether it belonged to the party or merely to its habits. Even at this distance Sera could read them the way she read everything: quickly, coldly, and with an eye for what they were worth.

The tall one in the lead moved like a soldier who'd stopped pretending otherwise — weight forward, eyes working the treeline without seeming to. That would be the mercenary. Villalobos. Sera had never met her, but she knew her file the way she knew every file that crossed the court's desks: Lena Villalobos, sellsword of the silent god's order, sister of a sold soul, carrier of something the usurper wanted badly enough to burn waystations over.

The priest was obvious too. Defrocked men always carried their defrocking like a smell — Anselm something-or-other, the heretic who'd preached that the gods weren't departed but imprisoned. He'd been a joke at court for years. Jokes, Sera had learned, were frequently the most dangerous things in any room.

And the third figure — the one trailing, the one with the crossbow slung casual and the walk of someone who'd been paid to follow rather than to arrive — that one interested her far more than either of them.

Because Sera Vane had not come all this way carrying the heaviest secret in the world to hand it to strangers on an open road. But she had run out of road, out of food, and very nearly out of time, and the arithmetic of desperation was simple: three armed travelers were better than none, and the mercenary with the sigil map was the only person alive who might actually believe what Sera had seen beneath the mountain.

She did not hurry. Hurrying invited arrows. She walked down the hillside at the pace of a woman out for an afternoon stroll, hands visible, hips swaying just enough to say *I am no threat*, and when she was thirty yards off she called out in a voice trained to carry across banquet halls:

"Travelers! I bring news worth more than your contracts, and I'm about to be shot by whoever's following me, so I'd appreciate a decision made quickly."

* * *

Lena Villalobos watched the woman come down the hill and thought: *trap*.

Everything about her said trap. The fine boots gone wrong at the heels. The court accent polished smooth as river stone even after days in the open. The way she kept her hands loose and empty while her eyes catalogued every weapon on the road — counting, weighing, pricing. Lena had spent ten years among killers and had learned that the dangerous ones never looked dangerous. They looked *expensive*.

"Company business," Renn said quietly behind her, meaning: *this is above our pay grade, let's keep walking.* Renn had been shadowing the company since Corvale, ostensibly as liaison, actually as leash, and Lena still hadn't decided whether to kill him. He shifted his crossbow up. "I can put one in her now and save the conversation."

"You'll do nothing," Lena said, "until I know what she is."

"I know what she is." Renn's voice went flat. "That's Sera Vane. Courtier. Malachar's own household. There's a likeness posted in every fort from here to the coast — *fleeing, do not approach, kill on confirmation.*"

Lena felt the map shift against her ribs where it was sewn into her coat lining, warm as a second heartbeat. A turncoat from the usurper's own court, running east with soldiers behind her, intercepting the one mercenary in the free hills who carried a smuggler's map written in god-sigils.

Either the best luck she'd ever had, or the worst.

Anselm had already stepped forward, which was exactly like him. "Lady Vane," he called, and there was something hungry in his voice. "You've come from below. From the mountain."

"From beneath it," the woman corrected, stopping at ten paces — close enough to talk, far enough to dodge. Her gaze found Lena and stayed there, deliberate as a hand placed flat on a table. "You're Villalobos."

"And you're a dead woman walking," Lena said. "Renn says there's a price on you."

"There's a price on everyone now. That's rather the point." Sera Vane reached slowly into her coat — Lena's hand went to her knife; Renn's bolt tracked her throat — and drew out not a weapon but a scrap of black silk, embroidered in thread that caught the grey light and held it wrong, the way deep water holds light. "Do you know what this is?"

Anselm made a sound like a man being unmade. "That's a *court seal*. That's— that's woven from captive voice. That shouldn't exist outside the mountain."

"It exists everywhere inside the mountain. It's how they pay for things." Sera turned the silk so they could see the sigil worked into its center — and Lena's breath stopped, because she knew that mark. It was on her map. It was the mark that glowed brightest, the one Anselm said sat closest to the heart of Thornfell. "I stole this off the broker's ledger-desk the night I ran. And I can tell you exactly where it came from, and what it buys, and why your usurper king has been harvesting gods like wheat." Her eyes never left Lena's. "But not on an open road. Not with his riders two valleys back and closing. So. Decision time, mercenary. Do we talk, or do we die separately?"

* * *

They talked in the lee of a fallen watchtower a mile off the road, in a hollow where the wind couldn't carry voices and the sightlines were long enough to satisfy even Renn, who took the high ground with his crossbow and radiated the specific displeasure of a man watching his leverage evaporate.

Sera Vane told it plainly, which surprised Lena. She'd expected performance. What she got instead was inventory.

"The court beneath Thornfell runs on souls. Sold ones. They're currency, ornament, labor — the broker keeps a ledger, and every soul in it is a debt that never gets forgiven. Your sister is in that ledger, Villalobos. Ilsa Villalobos, sold ten years ago, entry four-forty-one. I read it while I was stealing the seal." She said it without cruelty, which was worse than cruelty. "Malachar doesn't just wear a crown down there. He wears a *voice*. A dead god's voice, caged with the others, and whatever he speaks in it comes true at the edges of things — borders bend, roads move, armies that should starve don't. The cage isn't a prison. It's a *loom*. He's rewriting the world's boundaries one thread at a time, and the only thing that can cut those threads is the final sigil, and the final sigil—" she paused, and for the first time something human flickered through the court polish, something almost like fear, "—the final sigil is a person. Or so the whispers in the galleries say. And every whisper in that place agrees on one more thing: the usurper knows the map exists, and he knows who carries it. You're not hunting him, mercenary. He's been waiting for you."

Wind moved through the dead grass. Somewhere above, Renn reloaded with exaggerated care.

Lena looked at the silk seal glowing faintly against Sera's palm, then at the woman herself — exhausted, hunted, beautiful in the way a drawn blade is beautiful — and asked the only question that mattered.

"What do you want?"

"Passage. Protection. To not die in a ditch wearing stolen boots." Sera folded the silk away. "In exchange: I know the mountain's inner ways. The smuggler roads end at gates, and the gates are sealed, and every gate answers to court-signs that would take you years to learn. I can open them. I can navigate the court itself — its etiquette, its ledgers, its lies. Without me, you reach the mountain and bounce off it. With me, you get inside." She spread her hands. "I'm offering you the mountain, Villalobos. All I ask is that I live long enough to see it broken."

"Why?" Anselm asked softly. "Why turn? What did they do to you?"

Something closed behind Sera's eyes, a door shutting on a lit room. "They did nothing to me. That's what they do. Nothing, forever, to everyone, beautifully." She stood, brushing grass from the dead man's boots. "Think on it. But think fast. The riders are a day behind me, maybe less, and I promise you they aren't riding to negotiate."

* * *

That night, in a ruined shepherd's hut, Lena sat sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen and listened to the argument happen around her.

"We take her," Anselm said. "Whatever she is, she knows the way in."

"She's a court creature. Everything she says is priced." Renn, from the doorway, arms crossed. "And she named your sister, Villalobos. How does she know that name? How does she know *you*? Either she's been sent, or she's bait, and both ways you're walking into a mouth."

"Or," Anselm said, "she's exactly what she looks like: a woman who saw the machine from inside and ran."

"People like that don't run," Renn said. "People like that defect upward."

Lena said nothing. She was watching Sera through the hut's broken window, sitting apart in the dark, awake, back to a wall, hands empty and visible even with no one watching. A woman who performed vigilance for an audience of zero. Lena had known soldiers who did that. She had never once known a liar who bothered.

She went out and stood over her. "If you're bait," Lena said, "say so now. I'll make it quick."

"If I were bait," Sera said, looking up, unflinching, "I'd have let them catch me closer to the mountain, and you'd already be in the trap. Instead I've spent three days in stolen boots finding you on the worst road in the world." A pause. "I'm not bait. I'm a woman with one asset left — what I know — and I'm spending all of it on you. That's not loyalty. Don't mistake it for loyalty. It's spite. I want to watch his world come down, and you're the only crowbar I've found."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "Spite I can work with. Loyalty worries me."

"Good. Then we understand each other." Sera rose. In the dark her face lost its polish entirely, and what was underneath was younger and harder and tired all the way down. "One condition, mercenary. When we're below — if we get below — if it comes to saving me or saving the mission, save the mission. Promise it. Because if I think you'll hesitate for me, I'll start hesitating for you, and neither of us will survive that."

"That's the strangest offer of trust I've ever heard."

"It's not trust. It's terms." Sera extended her hand. "Well, mercenary? The riders are coming. Do we have terms?"

Lena looked at the offered hand — court-soft, uncalloused, steady as a rope bridge — and took it.

"Terms," she said.

Behind them, from the darkness of the southern hills, came a sound that ended the conversation forever: horns. Three notes, repeated, patient as dripping water. Renn appeared in the doorway, face grey in the starlight.

"They're not even hurrying," he said. "Why aren't they hurrying?"

Sera answered without turning, her hand still locked in Lena's, her voice stripped of every ounce of court polish down to bare iron.

"Because they already know where you're going," she said. "The question isn't whether they'll catch us. It's whether we find the gate first."

Far to the south, on the black line of the hills, a fire bloomed — small, precise, deliberate. A signal. Answering fires woke along the ridgelines east and west, one after another, ringing the road ahead of them like a noose being drawn shut.

"The inner ways," Sera said quietly. "We go tonight."
