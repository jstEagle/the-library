## 7. Roads That Move

The road was wrong.

Lena had walked smuggler roads since she was sixteen, and she knew their grammar the way she knew the weight of a knife: switchbacks that lied about distance, fords that appeared only in dry seasons, waystations marked by a notch cut into a tree at shoulder height. Roads were stubborn things. Men built them once and then spent generations arguing with them.

This one argued back.

They had entered the mountain's skirt through a ravine Sera called the Needle's Eye, and by midmorning of the second day, Lena realized the path behind them no longer matched the path they'd climbed. She stopped, turned, and looked at a slope of scree that had not been there when they passed it. The ravine mouth they'd come through was gone — not hidden, not fogged over. *Gone*, as if the mountain had swallowed its own throat.

"You're staring at it," Sera said without turning around. "Everyone stares at it the first day."

"The road moved."

"The roads *move*. That's rather the point." Sera picked her way down a shelf of wet granite with the unhurried grace of a woman descending a staircase in her own home. "Malachar's surveyors have mapped these ways three times in ten years. Three maps, three different mountains. The smugglers' guilds used to charge a fortune for guides who could read the old sigils. Now nobody sells passage east at any price, because the sigils are waking up."

Lena caught up to her, boots finding the same holds Sera's had. Behind them, Anselm came last, breathing hard, his defrocked priest's gray coat dark with sweat. He carried nothing but a satchel of books he refused to abandon and a walking staff he'd taken from a shrine two days back without asking anyone's permission, on the grounds that shrines belonged to gods who were, in his professional opinion, still present.

"Explain the waking part again," Lena said. "Slowly. As if I'm stupid."

"You're not stupid," Anselm said from behind. "You're unlettered. There's a difference, and it's correctable."

"I've killed men for less than that sentence."

"And yet here I am, alive, which proves you're curious." He drew level with her, cheeks flushed, eyes bright with the particular fever Lena had learned to distrust in scholars and fanatics alike. In Anselm it was somehow both. "The sigils aren't markings, Lena. They never were. A sigil is a word spoken so deeply into stone that the stone remembers it. The smugglers didn't invent those marks — they *copied* them, off walls older than any kingdom, older than the free cities, older than writing. And now that Malachar is pulling the gods' voices up through the rock like sap through a tree—"

"The words are remembering themselves," Sera finished. She said it flatly, without wonder, the way one reports weather. "The deep marks glow. The closer you get to Thornfell, the brighter. It's how we'll know we're not lost. It's also how anything hunting us will know where we are."

"Wonderful," Lena said. "A road that glows. For trackers."

"A road that glows," Sera corrected, "for *readers*."

* * *

Anselm began teaching her the prayers that evening, in the lee of an overhang while Sera built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand.

He did it the way he seemed to do everything — as if he'd been waiting his whole life for permission and had finally decided to stop asking for it. He wrote the first prayer in the dirt with a stick: a vertical stroke crossed by three descending arcs, like a hand letting something fall.

"This is the oldest one. The Opening. Every other prayer is built on it." He tapped the arcs. "Three falls. Breath falling into the body. Light falling into the eye. The soul falling toward whatever waits beneath the world. You don't say this prayer *to* a god. You say it to the space a god would stand in, if one were listening."

"That's the most depressing theology I've ever heard."

"It's the most *honest* theology you've ever heard. Most of it is decorated lies." He glanced up at her, and for a moment the fever in him banked down to something quieter. "I said the Opening every morning for twenty-two years, Lena. In a temple, in vestments, before a congregation that believed the gods had simply — left. Gone over the edge of the world like migrating birds. I told them the silence was holy. Then I found a wall in the Catacombs of Vell with this mark on it, glowing, and a voice behind the wall singing a note no throat can make, and I understood that the silence wasn't holy. It was *engineered*. Someone had shut the doors from the outside and told everyone the house was empty." He wiped the mark away with his palm. "The synod defrocked me for saying so. They were right to be afraid. Fear is the only honest response to what's under Thornfell."

Lena copied the mark in the dirt. It sat there, inert, just scratches.

"Say it," Anselm said.

"Say what? It's a drawing."

"It's a drawing the way a keyhole is a drawing of a door. Say the words under it. *Veyra veth, veyra veth, il marun.* The opening. The opening. Let what fell keep falling."

Lena looked at the fire. At Sera, who was watching with an expression carefully arranged into indifference and failing at the edges. At the dark beyond the overhang, where the mountain went down and down toward things she had sold a soul to reach.

She said it. Quietly, badly, tripping on the second syllable.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. She almost laughed — and then the mark in the dirt shivered, the way water shivers when a hoof strikes ground far away. Just once. Just enough.

Sera's cupped fire guttered and leaned sideways, though there was no wind.

Anselm made a sound like a man being handed something he'd paid for decades ago.

"Do it again," he whispered.

"No." Lena scrubbed the mark out with her boot heel. Her heart was going hard against her ribs, and she hated it, hated the heat crawling up her neck, hated how much she wanted to do it again. "We're not lighting beacons in the dark. We move at dawn."

"Lena—"

"*Dawn.*"

But she lay awake most of the night with the shape of the mark behind her eyes, and more than once her lips moved silently around the syllables, testing them, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

* * *

The third day, the sigils began to glow.

It started as a trick of the light — or so Lena told herself — a greenish shimmer in a notch of stone as they passed through a defile barely wide enough for one. By noon there was no explaining it away. Marks she would have sworn were plain chisel-work caught the light and held it, a pale gold like lamplight through honey, and the deeper they went, the more of them there were, until the walls of the pass were stitched with them, hundreds, thousands, a script running up the canyon walls and vanishing over the rim as if the whole mountain were a page written edge to edge.

Sera walked faster. Anselm walked slower, because he kept stopping to weep, quietly, into his sleeve, and then hurrying to catch up as if ashamed.

"They're not all smuggler marks," he said, catching his breath beside Lena. "Look — that spiral, there. That's a binding formula. And that lattice above it, that's a *name*. A god's name, written the way names were written before people were afraid of them." His voice cracked. "These walls are a prison ledger, Lena. Every mark is a line item. This isn't decoration. It's an inventory."

"Of what?"

"Of everything they took." He looked up the pass, toward the white shoulder of Thornfell rising ahead, and his face did something complicated — grief and fury and a scholar's terrible joy all fighting for the same ground. "And we're reading it. Do you understand? Ten years I was called a madman, and the proof has been overhead the entire time, glowing, waiting for someone to walk this road and *look up*."

"Keep your voice down."

"Why? Who's going to hear? The mountain already knows we're—"

The mountain answered him.

It was small, at first. A click of stone somewhere upslope, like a knuckle cracking. Then another, lower. Then a hum — not sound exactly, more a pressure in the teeth, in the fillings, in the fluid of the eyes — and every sigil within sight flared at once, gold flooding the defile, and somewhere far below them, down through miles of living rock, something *sang*.

One note. Held. Enormous. Not heard so much as remembered, the way you remember a voice from childhood — a note that made the scree shift on the slopes and sent a rill of pebbles hissing down the canyon walls and set Sera's hair lifting from her shoulders as if the air itself had stood up.

Then it stopped. The gold dimmed to embers. The silence rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Pebbles were still ticking down the slopes.

"What," Lena said finally, very evenly, "was that."

Anselm had both hands pressed flat against the canyon wall, against a glowing lattice-mark, and tears were running freely down his face and he didn't seem to know it. "It answered," he said. "The Opening — I've been saying it wrong my whole life, or maybe right, it doesn't matter — it *answered*. Something down there heard us. Heard *me*." He laughed, wet and broken. "Ten years of silence and it heard me."

"Or it heard the map," Sera said. Her voice was steady but her hand wasn't; it had gone to the knife at her belt and stayed there. "Or it heard *her*." She nodded at Lena. "You said the first prayer. Not him. Whatever woke up down there, it woke to *your* voice."

Lena said nothing. Because she had felt it too — in the half-second before the note, a sensation she had no clean name for, a sensation like being *recognized*. Like a vast attention turning over in its sleep beneath miles of stone, and finding her, and knowing her.

Knowing her the way you know a scar by touch in the dark.

"We camp outside the defile," she said. "Cold camp. No prayers, no marks, no fires. And tomorrow—" She looked up the pass, at Thornfell's white shoulder catching the last light, at the sigils glowing along the road toward it like a lit fuse burning toward a keg. "Tomorrow we stop talking on the march. We read the map, we walk, and we don't give this mountain one more reason to notice us."

Anselm opened his mouth.

"Don't," Lena said. "Whatever you're about to say — save it. Pray it into your books. I don't want to hear it."

But that night, wrapped in her cloak with her back to cold stone, she dreamed of a garden. Black soil, warm rain, rows of something growing that she couldn't name — and standing in the middle of it, a woman with her back turned, humming a note no throat could make.

When Lena woke, the sigil of the Opening was scratched into the dirt beside her bedroll.

She hadn't drawn it.

Below it, in the same impossible hand, someone had added a single word in the old script — and though she had never been taught to read it, she found she could, the way you find you can breathe:

*Come.*

Farther up the pass, faint but unmistakable in the pre-dawn dark, torches were kindling along the ridgeline. Someone else was on the road to Thornfell.

Someone who moved like soldiers.
