## 8. Malachar's Voice

The fort at Greywatch had been built to keep things out. Now it kept things in.

Sera felt it the moment they passed under the gate — a wrongness in the air, like a room where someone had died and no one had opened a window. The garrison soldiers wore Malachar's black and gold, but they moved strangely, in perfect unison, boots striking stone at the same instant as if the whole column shared one pair of lungs.

"Smile," she murmured to Lena and Anselm. "We're merchants. We're bored. We are the least interesting people in this fort."

"I'm a defrocked priest carrying a heretic's map," Anselm said. "I am extremely interesting."

"Then be boring about it."

They had come to Greywatch because there was no way around it. The Salt Road narrowed here to a single pass between the fort's walls and a cliff face that went up and up until the rock forgot itself into cloud. Three days of cold rations and colder silence had brought them this far; Sera had spent all three rehearsing what she was about to say, because she had known since Corvale that she would have to say it eventually, and she had been hoping — cowardly, she admitted it — for a reason not to.

She found the reason anyway, standing in the fort's courtyard.

A herald was reading proclamations from a raised dais, and the crowd of travelers and garrison men had gone still in a way crowds did not go still. Not bored-still. Caught-still. Sera had seen it once before, in the throne room at Thornfell, the first time she'd heard *the voice*.

"—and let it be known," the herald read, "that the roads of the east are closed to the faithless, that the old shrines are to be reported, and that any soul bearing marks of the elder script is to be taken alive—"

"Taken alive," Lena repeated softly beside her.

Sera didn't answer. She was watching the herald's mouth. Because the words were his, but the voice wasn't. It came out pitched too low, too resonant, each syllable arriving with a weight that made the stones hum faintly, like a finger run along a wet glass rim. She'd told herself on the road that she'd imagined it at court. That grief and fear had bent her hearing. But here it was again, in a border fort, coming out of a minor functionary's throat, and every hair on her arms stood up beneath her sleeves.

*He's everywhere,* she thought. *Or it is.*

Lena had gone rigid. "Sera." Her voice was barely breath. "That voice."

"I know."

"You *know*? You've heard it before?"

"Come with me." Sera took her elbow and steered her toward the fort's tavern, a low-beamed hall called the Drowned Ram, where travelers drank standing up and asked nothing of anyone. She paid for a corner table with coin that had no history, ordered wine she didn't want, and waited until Anselm had planted himself between them and the door.

Then she said: "Malachar doesn't have a voice of his own anymore."

Lena stared at her. "What does that mean?"

"It means exactly what it sounds like." Sera turned her cup slowly on the wood. "When I served at court — before I ran — I attended him. Privately. Do you understand what an honor that was supposed to be? The inner circle, the sealed chamber, the Usurper himself deigning to speak with a junior minister of accounts." Her laugh came out dry and ugly. "I was good with numbers. That's how I found the ledger discrepancies. Souls in, souls out. Columns that shouldn't exist."

"The cage," Anselm said quietly.

"The cage." She nodded. "But that's not what matters right now. What matters is this: the man who sat on that throne when I was presented to him was not entirely a man. Oh, he looked it. He ate, he slept, he grew tired, he had opinions about the vintage of his wine. But when he spoke — really spoke, when he gave orders or made decrees or pronounced judgment — the voice that came out of him belonged to something else. Something vast and dead and *furious* about being used." She looked up. "You've heard the stories. Malachar the Godspeaker. Malachar whose word can level a wall. They think it's sorcery. It isn't. It's theft."

"Whose voice?" Lena asked.

"That's the question, isn't it." Sera drank, finally, because her mouth had gone dry. "No one at court knew. There were names — the Shepherd of Ashes, the King Below, the Drowned Choir — half of them invented by ministers trying to sound informed. But I'll tell you what I know that they don't. I saw the tribute carts go down. I audited them, gods help me, I counted what went into the mountain. And one night, drunk on stolen wine and braver than I had any right to be, I asked him. To his face. Whose voice do you wear?"

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Even the fire in the hearth burned quieter.

"And?" Lena said.

"He laughed. And then he answered me, which was worse. He said—" Sera closed her eyes, put herself back in that amber-lit chamber, the smell of hot metal and incense, the thing on the throne leaning forward with its borrowed mouth. "*'I wear the Voice of the First Word, little clerk. The god who sang the first boundary and named the dark so the light could have something to push against. He died screaming when I caged him, and I caught his last note before it faded. Every decree I speak, I speak with the sound that unmade the first darkness.'*" She opened her eyes. "That's what he said. Verbatim. I've had ten years to forget it and I haven't forgotten a word."

Anselm had gone pale. "The First Word. If that's true — if he truly holds the primordial voice — then no army can stand against him. A voice that defined reality at its edges—"

"Can rewrite them. Yes." Sera set down the cup. "That's why I ran, priest. Not the ledgers. Not the cruelty, though there was plenty. I ran because I understood what the cage was *for*. Everyone thinks Malachar harvests the gods to power his armies — and he does, the soldiers out there march on harvested voice, you've seen how they move. But that's the small ambition. The large one is this: the old gods don't just live in the world. They *sing* it. Their voices are the boundaries. Where the sea ends. Where a man ends. What a soul is permitted to be. He means to take their song and sing new boundaries. A world with him at the seam of it."

Silence. Somewhere behind them a drunk laughed too loudly and was shushed by his companions, embarrassed, as if joy itself might attract attention.

Lena hadn't moved. She sat with her scarred hands flat on the table, staring at nothing, and Sera watched her work through it the way she'd watched mercenaries work through battle plans a hundred times — fast, ruthless, unsentimental.

"So we don't fight him," Lena said finally. "We can't. Not while he wears that voice."

"Not while he wears it. And he will never take it off willingly, because without it he's just a clever, cruel man with a very good army."

"Then we break the cage."

"Now you're catching up."

"But the map leads to the cage, and the map—" Lena stopped. Sera saw it land, the thing she'd been waiting three days for Lena to see. The mercenary's eyes came up, sharp as drawn wire. "The herald said *taken alive*. Bearers of the elder script. How does a border fort know to look for sigil-marks, Sera? That order didn't come from a provincial commander. That came from the top. From *him*."

"Yes," Sera said simply.

"He knows about the map."

"He knows more than that." Sera leaned forward, lowered her voice to almost nothing. "There was a name on the extraction orders I stole along with my proof. I told you I fled with documents. One of them was a standing directive, sealed with the First Word's mark. It concerned a person, not a thing. A description. A woman, mid-twenties, mercenary trade, southern features, a scar across the left palm. Traveling with a defrocked priest and a former courtier of the Thornfell court." She held Lena's gaze. "It described our company exactly, Lena. It was written *before* we ever met. The date on it was eleven years old."

The color left Lena's face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Eleven years," she whispered.

"Before Ilsa was sold. Before your bargain. Before any of it." Sera spread her hands. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Malachar has known about you since before you knew there was anything to know. The broker under the mountain, the soul-trade, the whole rotten economy of it — you weren't a customer, Lena. You were a *delivery*. Something was placed in motion the day you walked into that broker's den, and it has been walking toward this pass for a decade, and now it's here, and the man who started it speaks with the voice that named the dark."

Lena stood up so fast her chair went over.

For a moment Sera thought she'd bolt — out the door, into the pass, alone, the way people like Lena solved problems, with distance and a blade. The mercenary stood with her back to the fire, one hand on her knife, breathing like a woman surfacing from deep water.

"The sigil," she said at last. "The one Veyra — the one I pray to and get nothing. Anselm. When you taught me the prayers on the road. The one that made the mountain answer. Why did it feel like it came from *inside* me?"

Anselm opened his mouth. Closed it. Sera watched the priest wrestle with something and lose.

"I don't know," he said. "But I've begun to suspect the map isn't leading you to the cage, Lena. I suspect it's leading the cage to *you*."

Outside, a horn sounded — two notes, then three, the signal Sera had heard at court a thousand times and hoped never to hear in the wild. *A mark sighted. Hold all exits.*

Boots began to move in the courtyard. In unison. All of them.

"They've found us," Sera said, rising, already calculating doors and distances. "Or they've found someone wearing our description."

Lena picked up her chair, set it down gently, and drew her knife.

"No," she said. "They've found *me*. I'm done running from a hunt that started before I was born. Let's go introduce ourselves."

* * *

The courtyard was worse than the tavern had promised.

Soldiers stood in ranks around its perimeter, and they were not looking at the gate, or the walls, or the road. They were looking at the center of the yard, where a palanquin of black iron had been set down by bearers who now knelt with their foreheads to the stone. Curtains of grey silk moved in a wind that Sera could not feel on her own skin.

"It's him," she breathed. "He doesn't leave Thornfell. He *never* leaves Thornfell."

"Then either the world is ending," Anselm said, "or he wanted to meet her personally."

The curtains parted.

The man who stepped out was ordinary in the way that well-made things are ordinary — middle-aged, silvering at the temples, dressed plainly in black, with the mild face of a provincial magistrate. Sera had sat across ledgers from him. She knew the trick of him: the ordinariness was the costume. Everything terrible about Malachar lived underneath, in the seams.

He looked directly at Lena. Past Sera, past Anselm, past forty armed men, as if the courtyard contained only the two of them.

"Lena Villalobos," he said.

And the voice — the Voice, the First Word, the sound that had named the dark — rolled out of that mild mouth and pressed against her like deep water. Sera saw Lena sway and hold. Saw her plant her feet the way soldiers did when they decided the ground they stood on was theirs.

"You know my name," Lena said.

"I chose it," Malachar said pleasantly. "Eleven years ago, when I needed a vessel for the final sigil, I chose a family with faithful blood and desperate poverty. Your sister's soul was the price of admission. Your survival was the incubation. And now, after a decade of patient tending, my little garden has grown." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man admiring a harvest. "You've come all this way carrying my map. Shall we finish the journey together?"

Behind her, Sera heard Anselm whisper a prayer — and heard, for the first time in her life, the mountain itself lean down to listen.
