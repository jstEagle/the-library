## 9. The Company Breaks

They caught her at the ford.

Lena had known they would — you didn't leave the Iron Vetch without a knife in your back eventually, and she'd left them twice now, once in Corvale and once more thoroughly on the Salt Road — but knowing did nothing to soften the sight of their cookfires strung along the far bank like a necklace of orange beads against the dusk.

"Six tents," Sera said, crouched beside her in the alder scrub. The courtier had a way of stating the obvious as though it were intelligence of the highest order. "Perhaps thirty blades."

"Twenty-two," Lena said. "The Vetch never overmanned. Maren counts every mouth twice." She could see the captain's banner even from here, the stitched vetch-flower gone dark in the failing light. Twenty-two people she'd eaten with, bled with, buried. Twenty-two people carrying a kill order with her name on it.

"And you're certain it's a kill order," Anselm murmured. "Not a summons."

"Maren doesn't summon." Lena settled lower into the mud. "Maren delivers."

The priest said nothing to that. He'd been quiet all day, since the border fort, since Sera had told them what Malachar's voice sounded like up close — like a hymn played backward through a dead man's throat. Quiet, but watchful, the way he always was when Lena talked about her old company, as if he were cataloguing something for later use.

The plan was simple, because plans made at fords at dusk had to be simple: cross upstream where the water ran shallow over gravel, cut north through the pine break before the Vetch's pickets closed the gap, and be three miles gone by moonrise. No fighting. Lena had said no fighting out loud, twice, so that it would be true.

They were halfway across when the picket found them.

Not by skill — by a horse. One of the Vetch remounts, grazing loose on the shingle, decided that three strangers wading its river at twilight required commentary. It screamed the way horses do, high and idiotic, and lanterns bloomed along the far bank like fever blisters.

"Run," Lena said.

They ran.

* * *

The pine break saved them and betrayed them in equal measure. The trees were thick enough to swallow lantern light, which meant the Vetch couldn't form a line — but it also meant everyone was blind, and blind soldiers with orders shoot at sounds.

A bolt hissed past Lena's ear and buried itself in bark with a sound like a knuckle cracking. She put a trunk between herself and the flash of the next lantern and kept moving, low, counting strides the way Veyra had taught her to count breaths — except Veyra taught her nothing anymore, Veyra was a hole where a god used to be, and the habit was all that remained, prayer-shaped and empty.

Anselm stumbled ahead of her, robes snagging, muttering — not prayers, she realized, but the sigil-names from the map, under his breath, like a man touching iron. Sera moved better than a courtier had any right to, skirts hiked and knotted, choosing her ground like someone who'd grown up doing this in worse places than palaces.

Then the trees ended, and the meadow beyond was open as an open grave, and there were torches on both flanks, and Maren stood in the middle of everything with her sword drawn and not raised.

"Lena."

Just that. Her name, in Maren's voice, carrying across forty yards of wet grass the way only certain voices can carry — the voice of someone who'd pulled you out of a river once, who'd sewn your scalp, who'd stood over your sickbed in Corvale and lied to the guild about how many men you'd killed.

Lena stopped. Behind her, Anselm and Sera stopped too, because there was nowhere to stop *to*.

"You should have sent someone else," Lena called back.

"I did. Twice." Maren walked forward alone. That was Maren — she'd never ask a soldier to stand in a place she wouldn't stand. Torchlight caught the gray coming into her cropped hair; Lena had been gone from the company barely two seasons and somehow the gray had doubled. "Both times they came back and told me you were polite about refusing. I thought maybe I'd come myself and we'd skip the polite part."

"The map isn't mine to give."

"It's not yours to *keep*, either. You know what Malachar pays for defiance these days? Not gold." Maren stopped ten paces off. Close enough to talk. Close enough to kill. Her sword stayed down, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "He pays in examples. He burned Hallowmere for less than this. There were children in Hallowmere, Lena."

"I know what he burns."

"Do you?" For the first time Maren's voice cracked along a seam. "Because from where I stand, you've got a scrap of leather with drawings on it, a defrocked priest, and a courtier who'd sell her own shadow if the price was right, and you're walking all three of you toward the mountain that eats people. And twenty-two of mine have orders to bring you back or bury you, and half of them love you, and the other half are scared enough to do it anyway, and I am *tired*, Lena. I'm tired of carrying other people's destinies in my company. We're mercenaries. We sell steel. Since when do we sell martyrs?"

"Since the gods started singing under the stone," Anselm said quietly.

Maren's eyes flicked to him and back. "Nobody's heard a god in ten years."

"Somebody's heard them anyway," Lena said. "Somebody's been hearing them scream."

The meadow held its breath. Somewhere in the tree line a bolt-clack sounded, small and mechanical, a man easing his crossbow's tension and losing his nerve.

Maren looked at her for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone private, meant for ten paces and no farther. "I read the order, Lena. Before I took it. Do you know why I took it?"

"Coin."

"Because if I didn't, they'd send Torv's file instead, and Torv doesn't read orders, he just does them." Her jaw worked. "I took it so that when the moment came, there'd be one person on this field who might choose wrong slowly enough to give you a chance. That was my whole strategy. Ten years of command, and my strategy was *be slow*."

Something in Lena's chest turned over. "Maren—"

"Don't." The captain stepped back, and her sword came up after all, and her voice went flat and official, pitched to carry. "Lena Villalobos. In the name of the Provisional Compact and the lawful court at Thornfell, surrender the document in your possession and submit to escort."

"Sera," Lena said softly, without turning her head. "The north treeline. The gap behind the split boulder. On my word."

"I know," Sera breathed. "I counted it coming in."

"Anselm. You run first. Don't argue, don't pray, just run."

"Villalobos—" the priest began.

"*Run.*"

Maren sighed. It was the sigh of a woman watching a door close that she'd propped open with her own body. "Last chance, Lena."

"No," Lena said. "But thank you for offering it slowly."

She threw her knife at the nearest torch.

It wasn't a killing throw — it was a *lighting* throw, blade through oil-soaked rag, and the torch spun away into the wet grass and died, and the darkness that rushed in behind it was total and friendly and hers. She heard Maren shout *hold, hold, watch your lines* — bless her, still commanding, still trying to keep it clean — and Lena was already moving, angling hard right, counting strides, trusting the ground she couldn't see because she'd walked this meadow's edge an hour ago and Veyra's empty silence had never once failed her memory.

The problem was that the Vetch knew her tricks. They'd trained on her tricks. Someone had thought to post a pair at the north gap, and she met the first one hip-deep in the bracken — a young spearman named Col, who'd joined the year she'd taught the company's footwork drills, who cried during the ballad about the drowned ferryman and pretended he hadn't.

Col thrust at the sound of her. It was a good thrust. It would have opened her throat if she'd been a stride slower, and she hated him a little for making it good, and then she was inside it, turning his spear arm, breaking his balance, putting him down into the ferns with the edge of her hand across his windpipe — hard enough to drop him, soft enough that he'd wake angry rather than not at all.

The second one was not Col.

The second one was Kess.

Kess had been with her the night of the death-debt, ten years ago, when Lena had gone down into the broker's dark to buy her life with her sister's soul. Kess had held her hair back while she vomited afterward and never asked why. Kess had a scar on her forearm from catching a blade meant for Lena's ribs outside Vell's Crossing. Kess was the closest thing to family the world had left that Lena hadn't already sold.

Kess saw her in the last of the torch-glow and hesitated — one heartbeat, a full one, a heartbeat with a whole history inside it — and then didn't, because orders were orders and fear is faster than friendship, and her short sword came around in a flat, professional arc aimed at Lena's sword arm, disabling, nonlethal, Maren's training written all over it.

Lena should have taken the cut. Later, lying awake, she would understand that: she should have let Kess open her arm, should have dropped the map, should have let the whole terrible destiny pass her by and gone back to selling steel. Instead her body did what ten years of Veyra's discipline had built it to do, the discipline of a silent god whose only sacrament was survival, and her body *killed the arc* — parried inside it, stepped through, and drove her knife up under Kess's collarbone, into the meat above the lung, deep, deeper than disabling, deeper than anything she'd ever put into a friend.

Kess made a small sound. Not a scream. A sound like a question being asked and answered at once.

"Oh," Lena said. "Oh, no. Kess—"

Kess slid down her front, and Lena caught her, and they went into the bracken together, and somewhere behind them Maren was roaring the company into a line and Sera was screaming Lena's name from the treeline and none of it mattered, none of it existed, there was only the heat of blood soaking through her sleeve and Kess's eyes finding hers in the dark, wide and confused, a child's question in a veteran's face.

"You went east," Kess whispered. Blood at the corner of her mouth. "You went east and you didn't say goodbye."

"I'm saying it now," Lena said, and her voice broke in the middle like cheap rope. "I'm sorry. Kess. I'm sorry."

"Stupid," Kess said, almost fondly, and her eyes rolled, and her weight went slack, and Lena pressed her palm hard over the wound because pressure was the only prayer she had left and the god it was addressed to had stopped answering years ago.

Boots crashed through the bracken. Lantern light swung wild. Lena laid Kess down as gently as she could — gently, gently, the way you lay down something you might never pick up again — and ran.

* * *

They didn't stop until the stars were out and the pines had changed to birch, and even then it was Sera who called the halt, gripping Lena's shoulder and steering her off the trail like a woman handling a spooked animal.

"She'll live," Sera said. "You put it above the lung. Deliberate or lucky?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously to what kind of person you are, which matters enormously to whether I stay on this road with you." But Sera's grip on her shoulder gentled as she said it, which was the most honest thing the courtier had done yet.

Anselm built no fire. He sat Lena down against a birch trunk and crouched in front of her, and for once he didn't offer scripture. He offered silence, which was, Lena supposed, the one sacrament still reliably available to her.

"She's alive," Lena said. It wasn't a question but she needed it said aloud, witnessed, made true by air.

"She's alive. Your captain will get her back to Corvale if anyone can." Anselm paused. "And the company?"

"Gone." Lena looked at her hands. Kess's blood had dried black in the creases of her knuckles, in the lines of her palms, filling every furrow like ink filling a signature. "That's the thing nobody tells you about choosing. Everyone talks about the doors opening. Nobody mentions that every door opens by closing another one, and you're the one standing in the frame."

"You chose the map over the company."

"I chose strangers." Her laugh came out wrong, jagged. "People I've never met. People in cities Malachar hasn't burned yet. I wounded the person who held my hair back when I came home from selling my sister's soul, and I did it for *strangers*, Anselm. Tell me that's holiness. Tell me that's worth it. Because from here it looks exactly like what it cost, which is everything, paid out one friend at a time."

The priest was quiet a long moment. Fireflies drifted through the birches, small green sparks, indifferent and lovely.

"When we reach the shrine cave," he said finally, "there's something I have to tell you. About the broker. About Ilsa." His voice didn't shake, but it took visible effort to hold it level. "You deserve it tonight, and I can't. I'm a coward about one thing only, and it's your face when you hear it. Give me until the cave."

Lena stared at him. Exhaustion made the words swim, made them mean too many things at once. "If it's bad enough to need a cave, it's bad enough to need telling."

"I know." He rose, knees cracking, an old man shaped by old guilt. "Tomorrow, then. Sleep. Tomorrow the mountain starts answering us back, and you'll want your strength."

Sera took first watch without being asked. Lena lay down on the cold ground with the sigil map beneath her shirt, against her heart, where it had lived since the smuggler died pressing it into her hands — and she found that she could not stop seeing Kess's face, the question in it, the *you went east and you didn't say goodbye*.

Somewhere south of them, in a camp she would never eat in again, Kess was breathing shallowly and living, and Maren was keeping her own counsel, and twenty-two people were learning what Lena had learned tonight: that some roads can only be walked alone, and that loneliness is not the punishment for choosing.

It's the payment. And the ledger, she was beginning to understand, balances both ways.

She slept, and dreamed of a garden she had never planted, and woke before dawn to Anselm shaking her shoulder, his face gray in the starlight.

"Get up," he whispered. "The mountain's answering early."
