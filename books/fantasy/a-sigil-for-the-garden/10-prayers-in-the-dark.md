## 10. Prayers in the Dark

The storm caught them an hour past dusk, and the mountain offered them a cave the way a miser offers charity — grudgingly, and with a price attached.

Anselm saw it first: a black seam in the cliffside, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen runoff. He had to shout three times before Lena heard him over the wind, and when she turned, snow crusted in her lashes, her face wore that expression he was coming to know too well. The one that said *I don't trust anything the mountain gives me.*

"It's a shrine," she said when they'd squeezed inside.

He didn't answer. He was already looking at the walls.

The cave was small — ten paces deep at most — but someone had loved it once, long ago. Niches had been cut into the stone, seven of them, arranged in the old pattern Anselm knew from seminary texts nobody read anymore. Most were empty. Two still held offerings: a clay lamp gone to dust inside, and a child's carved bird, its wings worn smooth by small hands. Above the niches, faded almost to nothing, ran a line of sigils. Not smuggler's marks. Older. Prayer-marks.

"Veyra," Lena said flatly. She'd found the seventh niche, the one set apart from the others, empty of everything except a circle scratched into the floor. "Your god kept roadside shrines?"

"The faithful did." Anselm set down his pack and worked feeling back into his fingers. "Veyra was the god of silences. She didn't ask for temples. She asked for quiet places, and people gave them because —" He stopped himself. Ten years of talking to people who no longer believed had taught him to stop mid-sentence.

"Because?" Sera prompted from the doorway, shaking snow from her fine cloak with the distaste of a woman who had not been cold in years until recently. She'd been quieter all day, since the border fort, since Malachar's voice had followed them out of that place like smoke following a fire.

"Because everyone needs somewhere the world can't follow," Anselm finished. "That's what she guarded. The unspoken thing. The prayer you're ashamed of."

Lena snorted. It was not a kind sound. "My silent god never answered a single prayer I ever gave her."

"No," Anselm agreed quietly. "She didn't."

* * *

They made camp without fire. Sera argued for one — her teeth chattered through the argument, which undermined it considerably — but Lena vetoed it with a look at the sigils above the niches, as if light might wake something that was better asleep.

Anselm understood. He also knew something Lena didn't yet understand about shrine caves: they held sound strangely. The stone here was old and dense, and it remembered voices. In seminary they'd called such places *listening rooms*. He had spent half his life in them, and half his life since avoiding them.

He sat against the wall and watched Lena clean her blades by touch alone, refusing even the moonlight through the entrance. Sera had curled up in her cloak near the door, feigning sleep with the imperfect stillness of a courtier who had spent years sleeping among enemies. And between the two women, in the dark, Anselm felt the confession rise in him like water rising in a flooded mine. Slow. Certain. Unstoppable.

He had been carrying it since Corvale. Since the Salt Road. Longer, if he was honest — since the night ten years ago when he'd stood in a candlelit gallery beneath Thornfell and watched a girl of nineteen sign her name to a ledger with hands that shook so badly the ink blotted twice.

He should tell her now. Here. Because if he told her on the road, in daylight, she would kill him or leave him, and either way the map would keep moving toward Thornfell without him. But here, in a listening room, under Veyra's own faded marks — here the truth might land differently. Or it might not land at all, and simply destroy everything, which was also a kind of honesty.

"Lena," he said.

She didn't look up from her blade. "No."

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You've got your confessor voice on. I've heard it for three hundred miles. It means you want something, or you're sorry about something, and I don't want either."

Sera opened one eye. Closed it again, with the theatrical discretion of someone planning to hear every word.

Anselm leaned his head back against the stone. Behind him, faintly, the sigils seemed to warm. Or perhaps that was only his guilt, finding its temperature at last.

"I served the broker," he said. "Under Thornfell. For six years."

The whetstone stopped.

* * *

For a long moment there was no sound but the wind outside, worrying at the frozen falls like a dog at a bone. Then Lena set the blade down — carefully, deliberately, the way she did everything that required her not to kill someone — and turned to face him fully.

"Say that again."

"I was a novice of the Order of the Quiet Hand. Veyra's order. You won't have heard of us; we were small, and we were mostly archivists." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Twenty years ago the order began dying. Prayers unanswered, novices leaving, funds failing. I was sent east to find out why our god had gone silent. I went down into the mountain following rumors of a man who could buy and sell souls, and I found him, and instead of reporting what I found —"

"What did you find?"

"A ledger," Anselm said. "A ledger of souls. And a market. And men who paid in memories and walked out lighter. And I stayed." He laughed, and it was an awful sound in the small stone room. "Do you know why I stayed? Because they let me read. Six years in the deepest archive in the world, every text the old faiths ever banned, and all I had to do was keep my mouth shut while girls like your sister signed themselves away."

Lena had gone very still. It was the stillness he'd seen her use on ambushes, on contract negotiations, on men who reached for weapons. The stillness of a woman measuring distance.

"How do you know about my sister?"

"Because I processed the entry." He made himself hold her gaze. "Ten years ago. A broker named Havel Osten brought me a new acquisition for the archive — a soul-deed, freshly signed. The seller was listed as the purchaser of record, buying her own freedom from a death-debt owed to the Hollow Court. The commodity was her sister. Nineteen years old. Name of Ilsa Villalobos. I entered her into the ledger myself, Lena. My handwriting is on her soul."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on the cave, on the niches, on the little carved bird in its alcove, and Anselm thought distantly that Veyra's shrine had heard a thousand confessions worse than his, and survived them all, and perhaps that was why the stone felt warm — not holiness, just endurance.

"You've known," Lena said at last, very quietly. "Since Corvale. You looked at me in Corvale and you knew whose sister I was, and you said nothing. You walked with me for three hundred miles and said nothing."

"Yes."

"Why now? Why this cave? Why not the road, where I couldn't—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Why not somewhere I couldn't reach you."

"Because I'm a coward," Anselm said simply. "And because I needed you to hear it in a place where killing me would be a desecration."

"That," Lena said, "is the single worst reason anyone has ever given me for anything."

She stood. Sera sat up smoothly, both eyes open now, one hand drifting toward the knife in her boot — though whether to defend Anselm or Lena, or simply to be ready for whichever way the night broke, Anselm couldn't guess.

"You searched for her," Lena said. "You told me on the Salt Road you left the order because you claimed the gods were prisoners. You told me you'd been searching for *proof*. Was that proof, Anselm? Was my sister your proof?"

"She became my purpose after I lost the taste for proofs." He rose too, slowly, keeping his hands visible, an old habit from dealing with dangerous people in small rooms. "I stayed in the broker's service for six years, and then one day I was sent to fetch a soul from the galleries — the living archives, we called them, where the spent ones serve — and I looked at her face and I realized I had filed a human being. That she had a sister somewhere in the foothills who probably prayed to a silent god for her safety every night, and the silent god hadn't answered because the silent god was in a cage downstairs, and the sister's prayer had been routed, like everything else in that mountain, through a ledger."

"And then?"

"Then I preached it. In the market square at Thornfell town, in front of the pilgrims. I said the gods weren't departed, they were imprisoned, and the mountain was their prison, and every soul bought and sold below fed the jailer. They stripped my orders, burned my writings, and put a price on my head that I'm told remains competitive." He spread his hands. "That's my whole confession. I helped bury your sister. Then I spent ten years trying to dig her up. Neither act makes the other clean."

Lena crossed the cave in four strides.

Sera moved — Anselm saw it, the fluid shift of weight, the knife clearing her boot — but Lena wasn't reaching for her weapons. Her hand closed on Anselm's collar, and she pulled him close, close enough that he could see the storm-light reflected in her eyes, and her voice came out low and shaking.

"Six years. You stood in that archive for six years, and you want absolution from *me*?"

"No," Anselm said. "I want nothing from you. That's what I came to tell you. Somewhere on the Salt Road I stopped wanting absolution and started wanting the cage broken, and those are different hungers, and only one of them can be satisfied by your forgiveness."

Her grip tightened. The fabric creaked. Behind her, Sera said softly, "Mercenary. The sigil-map. Think."

"I am thinking," Lena said, without turning. "I'm thinking about a nineteen-year-old girl who signed herself away so her sister could live, and about the man whose handwriting is on her soul, and about how that man has been eating my food and praying beside my fire for three hundred miles." Her breath came ragged. "Give me one reason. One. And it had better be good, priest, because I have killed men for less, and I have *liked* it."

"Because I'm the only person alive who knows the galleries' layout," Anselm said, "and the only person alive who has read the ledger, and the only person alive who knows that your sister's deed was purchased three years ago by the court itself — which means she isn't currency anymore, Lena. She's *property of the throne*. And property of the throne lives in the inner court, close to the cage. Close enough to reach."

The words landed. He watched them land — watched the fury in her face collide with the hope trying to get in behind it, two forces meeting at a gate neither could hold alone.

"Inner court," Lena repeated.

"The Queen's Gallery. Where the court keeps what it treasures." He swallowed. "It's also where they'll take the map, if Malachar gets it. Everything precious goes to the same place. Your sister and your sigil are stored in the same vault, Lena. If that isn't a reason to keep me alive until we get there, then kill me now and may Veyra judge us both."

* * *

Lena let go of his collar.

She stepped back, and the step cost her more than the grabbing had — he could see it, the effort of unclenching, of folding the rage away somewhere it wouldn't leak. She picked up her whetstone. Sat down. Picked up the blade.

"If you lie to me again," she said, "about anything, however small, I will leave you on the mountain. Alive. I'll leave you somewhere the weather can consider you." She drew the stone along the edge, once, the sound very loud in the listening room. "We walk at first light. We go to the inner court. You get me to my sister and to the cage, and if you die down there, priest, I will not pray for you."

"That's fair," Anselm said.

"It's more than fair. It's generous." The whetstone whispered along the steel. "Now shut up. Some of us are trying to think."

He sat back against the wall, beneath the faded sigils, and closed his eyes, and found — to his surprise — that the cave had grown warmer, or that he had stopped noticing the cold. Above him the old prayer-marks held their faint warmth like banked coals, and Anselm, who had spent twenty years listening to a silent god and hearing nothing, heard — or dreamed he heard, and knew he would never be sure — a single syllable in the dark.

Not a word. Just a tone. The sound a stone makes settling deeper into a mountainside.

Sera stirred by the entrance. "There's something you should both see," she said, and her voice had none of its usual silk. "The sigils on the map. Look at them."

Anselm opened his eyes. Lena was already unfolding the smuggler's map across her knees, and in the darkness of the shrine cave, unlit by any flame, the sigil-marks along the route to Thornfell had begun to glow — softly, steadily, like embers answering a bellows — and the nearest mark, the one marking the pass ahead, was pulsing.

Faster now. Like footsteps. Like something under the stone, running toward them through the dark.
