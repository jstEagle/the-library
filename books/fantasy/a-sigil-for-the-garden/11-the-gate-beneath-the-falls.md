## 11. The Gate Beneath the Falls

The map changed again on the third morning above the tree line.

Lena had learned to stop trusting her eyes with it. The sigils crawled like insects when she wasn't looking directly at them, rearranging themselves against the vellum, and twice now the route it showed had contradicted the terrain in front of her — and the terrain had been wrong. She'd followed the map over a ridge that no sane scout would have attempted and found a smuggler's stair cut into the living rock on the far side, hidden from the valley, older than any of the free cities.

Now the ink had gathered itself into a single dense knot near the map's eastern edge, a clot of marks so thick they overlapped and blurred, and beneath it, in a script that predated the trade-tongue, a word she couldn't read.

"It's finished with us," Sera said. She stood at the lip of the overlook with her courtier's cloak pulled tight against the wind, looking down into the gorge where the river ran white over black stone. "Whatever it's been leading us to."

"It's not finished." Anselm crouched beside Lena, his ruined priest's hands hovering over the vellum without touching it, the way a man hovers over a wound he's afraid of. His breath came out in gray plumes. "It's concentrated. Everything the map knows is pouring into one place." He looked up at her, and in the flat morning light his eyes were the color of old ice. "We're close, Lena. Closer than I've ever been. Closer than anyone's been in ten years."

"Close to what?" Sera's voice had the edge she put on it when she was frightened and wanted it to sound like contempt. "You've both been dancing around it since the shrine cave. Say it. A gate. A door. A hole in the world. What exactly are we walking into?"

Lena rolled the map closed and stood. Her knees ached. Everything ached — she'd stopped noticing it around the second day of the climb, the way you stop noticing a tooth. "We're walking into whatever's worth hiding behind a mountain," she said. "Same as we have been."

She started down the goat track toward the gorge, and after a moment she heard them follow.

* * *

The river had cut the gorge deep enough that the walls rose on either side like the inside of a throat, and the cold down there was different from the cold above — older, patient, a cold that had never once been interrupted by sunlight. Ice sheathed the rock in sheets and fangs. The river itself ran fast and black between shelves of ice, loud enough that they had to shout to be heard, and Lena hated it. Noise like that covered the sound of an ambush. She'd killed men in canyons louder than this one, and she'd buried friends in them too.

Anselm stopped so suddenly she nearly walked into his back.

He was staring at the far wall. Lena followed his gaze and saw nothing at first — just ice, a frozen cascade where some tributary had poured over the gorge wall and hung there like a curtain, forty feet of it, blue-white and seamless in the shadow.

Then she saw the sigil.

It was small. That was what struck her first — after the shouting marks on the map, after the burning brands the soldiers wore, after all the loud ugly magic of the last month, this was small. A single mark cut into the rock behind the ice, no bigger than her palm, and it glowed with a light so faint and so steady that she might have taken it for a trick of the light off the river.

She knew it. That was the thing that stopped her heart. She knew it the way you know your own name — not learned, but *carried*. The silent mark. The closed mouth. The mark of Veyra that her mother had inked onto her wrist when she was six years old, that every mercenary of the silent god wore somewhere on their body, that Lena had touched every night for ten years of prayers that fell into nothing.

"That's not possible," she said. She heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "She doesn't answer. She doesn't *do* anything. That's the whole — that's what she *is*."

Anselm was already wading toward the falls, boots splashing through the shallows, and for once in his stubborn life he wasn't talking. He reached the ice curtain and pressed both palms flat against it, and Lena watched the light of the sigil strengthen — watched it answer him, the way a lamp answers a struck flint.

"Ten years," he said, and his voice cracked on it. He was a defrocked priest, a man who'd been cast out of his own temple for saying the gods were prisoners, and he stood there with his hands on the ice like a man touching the face of someone he'd mourned. "Ten years I said she was still there. They laughed me out of the seminary for it. *Still there.*"

Sera hung back, arms crossed, but Lena saw her looking at the sigil too, and saw the calculation in her face stutter and fail. Even a courtier of Malachar's court — *especially* a courtier of Malachar's court — knew what the silent mark meant, and what it meant that it was burning here, in the middle of nowhere, on the road to Thornfell.

Lena crossed the river.

The water soaked her to the knee and she didn't feel it. She stood beside Anselm and put her hand on the ice, and the cold bit through her glove, and the sigil brightened under her palm — brighter than it had for him, brighter with every heartbeat, until the light was coming up through the ice like a drowned star and the whole frozen curtain was lit from within, blue-white gold, and she could see through it now, see the rock face behind, and the rock face was not rock.

It was a door.

Seamless, doorless, without hinge or handle — a slab of stone set into the gorge wall, and carved across its whole surface, covering it the way scales cover a serpent, were sigils. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The same crawling marks from the map, the same script that predated the trade-tongue, arranged not in sentences but in something older than sentences. And at the center, vast, the size of a shield, burned the silent mark of Veyra.

Lena took her hand off the ice.

The light dimmed. Not all the way — it stayed, a low ember-glow, patient. But it dimmed, and the gorge seemed darker for it, and she stood there in the shallows with river water freezing in her boots and her heart going like a war drum.

"It's waiting for you," Anselm said softly.

"It's a door, Anselm. Doors don't wait."

"This one does. Look at the arrangement. The outer rings are the old wards — I've seen them in fragments, in texts the temple burned. But the center mark, the silent mark, that's the key. And it's not keyed to a word or a bloodline or a payment." He turned to her, and there was something in his face she'd never seen there before, something that frightened her more than the soldiers or the kill order or the mountain itself. Reverence. "It's keyed to *faith*. To hers. To someone she marked as her own."

"I wear the mark because my mother inked it on me. Half the company wears it. It's a mercenary's brand, it means *we take contracts no one else will*, it doesn't mean—"

"When did you last pray to her?"

The question landed like a blade between the ribs. Lena opened her mouth to answer — *last night, this morning, an hour ago, out of habit, always, every day for ten years into a silence that never once broke* — and found she couldn't.

Because she had prayed. That was the humiliating truth of it, the thing she'd have killed a man to keep from Sera's ears. Ten years, and the god had never answered, never stirred, never given her so much as a dream — and Lena had kept praying anyway, every night, a soldier's flat mechanical prayer, *keep my blade true, keep my friends breathing, keep my soul out of the dark*, knowing it fell into nothing, knowing it, and doing it anyway.

Faith without evidence. Faith without answer. Faith that had no reason left to exist except that she couldn't put it down, the way you can't put down a debt.

"Oh," she said.

The sigil flared.

Not the small one behind the ice — the great one, the shield-sized mark at the center of the door, blazing up through forty feet of frozen waterfall like the sun through a window, and the ice *sang*, a high clear note that cut straight through the roar of the river, and Lena felt it in her teeth, in her wrist where the brand was, in the place behind her sternum where she kept the things she never said out loud.

The ice began to melt. Not to crack — to *melt*, all at once, from the inside out, forty feet of frozen cascade sloughing off the wall in a single thunderous sheet that would have crushed them if Anselm hadn't hauled her back by the collar, and then the water was everywhere, a freezing deluge, and through it Lena saw the door standing bare and blazing in the gorge wall, and the great silent mark burning like an open eye.

Then the light settled. The mark dimmed to its patient ember. The river roared on as if nothing had happened, and the three of them stood soaked and half-drowned in the shallows, staring.

Sera laughed. It was a thin, unsteady sound, nothing like her court laugh. "Ten years of nothing," she said. "Ten years of a god who wouldn't so much as flicker for anyone, and she melts a glacier for *you*."

"She didn't do it for me." Lena's voice came out wrong. She cleared her throat. "She did it because—"

Because Veyra was silent. Because the silence was the point. Because a god who hid something precious didn't light lamps over it, didn't leave signs, didn't answer — and yet here was a sign, here was an answer, here at the threshold of the one place in the world the silent god had every reason to keep dark.

Unless she wanted someone to find it.

Unless she'd been waiting ten years for the one person too stubborn to stop knocking.

Lena walked to the door. The stone was warm under her hand — warm, in a gorge where the cold could crack teeth — and the sigils under her palm shifted as she touched them, crawling, rearranging, the whole carved surface moving like the map had moved, like ink that couldn't hold still. The great mark at the center pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat.

Like a held breath about to be released.

Hope hit her then, and it hit her like vertigo — that lurch you feel at the top of a climb when you look down and understand how high you are. All the way down the mountain, through the burning waystation and the kill order and the shrine cave and Anselm's confession, she had kept one thing folded small and hard in her chest, the way you carry a coal: *the gods are real, the cage is real, and if the cage is real then what was sold can be found.* She had not let herself believe it. Believing it was a cliff edge.

She was standing on the cliff edge now. The door was warm. The mark was burning. And somewhere behind all that stone, in the dark under the mountain, a god she had prayed to for ten years was holding that breath for her.

"Lena." Anselm's voice, gentle. "We should rest before—"

"No."

She put her shoulder to the door, which was a stupid thing to do, and it didn't matter, because the door was already moving. The sigils spun. The great mark flared white, and the stone parted along a seam no blade could ever have found, sliding inward with a sound like a held note finally released, and warm air breathed out of the dark — air that smelled of stone and deep water and something else, something old and sweet, like a temple that had been closed for a very long time.

Stairs went down. Cut smooth, wider than a road, descending into the mountain's black interior, and along their edges, at intervals, small sigil-lamps kindled one by one as Lena watched, lighting her way down into the earth.

They lit for her. Only her. When Sera stepped toward the threshold, the nearest lamp guttered and went dark, and kindled again when she stepped back.

"Right," Sera said faintly. "Only you, then."

Lena stood at the top of the stairs. Behind her: the gorge, the cold, the road back to everything she understood — contracts, blades, the flat mercenary arithmetic she'd lived by since she was fourteen. Ahead: the dark, and the warm air rising out of it, and somewhere down there a ledger with her sister's name in it, and a cage with her god in it, and whatever else ten years of silence had been hiding.

She touched the brand on her wrist. For the first time in ten years, it was warm.

"Stay close," she said, and stepped down into the mountain, and the door began to close behind her like a mouth.
