## 12. Descent

The gate behind the waterfall opened the way a throat opens — reluctantly, and with the sense that something on the other side had been waiting to swallow.

Sera Vane went first because she was the only one of the three who knew how to be swallowed politely.

"Rules," she said over her shoulder, voice pitched low so it wouldn't carry into the dark beyond the gate. "There are rules under the mountain, and every one of them matters more than your life does. Listen and repeat them back."

"I'm not a child," Lena said.

"You're worse. You're a mercenary." Sera smoothed her ruined court dress as best she could — the Salt Road had taken its silk hem, its dignity, most of its color — and turned to face them both in the blue-white spill of godlight leaking through the gate. "Under Thornfell, everything is a transaction. Every word is priced before you say it. Every courtesy is a contract. If someone offers you wine, refusing insults them; accepting indebts you. The trick is knowing which debts are cheap."

"And if I don't want to play?"

Sera held her gaze. Behind Lena, Anselm had gone very still, the way he always did when theology got close.

"Then you'll die beautifully," Sera said, "and they'll sell what's left of you by the ounce. I've seen it done. I've *bought* it done." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "So we play. All three of us. Until we're past the galleries, past the ledger hall, down to where the cage sings. Agreed?"

Lena's jaw worked once. Then: "Agreed."

Anselm said nothing at all, which Sera had learned to read as agreement from him, or possibly as prayer. With Anselm the two were difficult to tell apart.

She stepped through the gate.

* * *

The mountain's interior was not what travelers' tales promised. There was no labyrinth, no crawling tunnels, no dripping dark. Instead the smuggler road opened, three hundred paces in, like a fist unclenching — into an inverted city hanging beneath the roots of Thornfell itself, built downward into a hollow vast enough to have weather.

Sera had spent four years of her life here and had never once stopped feeling vertigo.

Balconies jutted from walls that were really ceilings that were really floors; staircases climbed toward the center of the earth. And everywhere, light — not torchlight, never torchlight, fire was vulgar down here — but a pale radiance that seeped out of the stone itself, out of veins of crystal running through the architecture like luminous blood. Captive godlight. She could feel it against her skin even now, faint and wrong, like standing too near a caged bird whose wings beat the air without sound.

"What is that light?" Anselm whispered. His hand had found the faded prayer-knots at his belt, worrying them.

"Ask me later," Sera said, "when we can afford the answer." Which meant: when there weren't ears in the walls. There were always ears in the walls.

They descended the Grand Inversion on a staircase wide enough for ten abreast, joining a thin traffic of figures moving with the unhurried purpose of people who belonged. Courtiers in lacquered half-masks. Servants carrying trays of things Sera chose not to identify. Brokers' runners with ledgers chained to their wrists. And others — walking carefully, smiling carefully, eyes fixed slightly past everything — whom the court called *the invested.*

Souls, Sera thought, watching one drift past with a candle it never lit. That's what a soul looks like after it's spent. Like a person wearing a person.

Beside her, Lena had gone rigid. Sera watched her watch one of the invested — a young man in servant gray, beautiful, vacant, pausing at a balustrade to look down at nothing for a long, long moment before remembering how legs worked.

"Lena." Sera kept her voice at court-pitch, pleasant, meaningless. "Eyes forward. Curiosity is visible here, and visible things get appraised."

"What did he do?" Lena said. Not looking away. Of course not looking away.

"He didn't do anything. He was sold. Or his sister was sold, and the debt came due sideways. It doesn't take much. A death-debt, a broken contract, a bargain made in desperation by someone who loved you more than they loved themselves." Sera heard her own voice flatten and made herself lift it again, re-apply the varnish. "Smile, Captain Villalobos. We're nearly at the tollhouse, and the tollkeeper counts frowns as interest."

Ahead, the staircase fed into an archway hung with bells — small ones, silver, silent until a breath of air from below set them shivering without ringing. The tollhouse beyond was a low chamber of black marble where a woman sat behind a desk of glass, doing nothing, magnificently.

Sera approached, stopped precisely four paces out, and inclined her head to the exact degree owed to a functionary of the second rank. Beside her, she felt Lena begin to copy the motion and caught her elbow — too deep a bow, you idiot, you've just offered deference you'd have to buy back — and converted it into a shrug, as though adjusting a strap.

"Tollkeeper." Sera smiled with everything above the teeth. "Three travelers bound inward. Business: audience."

"Audience with whom?" The tollkeeper didn't look up.

"The Ledger."

Now she looked up. Her face was smooth as the desk. "The Ledger receives no audiences. The Ledger conducts transactions. What do you offer?"

"We offer our presence at a transaction already scheduled." Sera reached into her bodice and drew out the token she had stolen off a dead courier six days ago and had been praying to no one in particular about ever since: a sliver of bone, stamped with the broker's mark, good for entry to the ledger hall during the season's reconciliations. "Invitation, third seal. My associates are my witnesses. Witnesses enter free."

"They enter *bound*," the tollkeeper corrected. "Witnesses may not speak, may not touch, may not leave before the reconciliation concludes. Do your witnesses understand?"

"Say yes," Sera murmured, barely moving her lips.

"Yes," Lena said.

"Yes," Anselm echoed, and his voice cracked on it, and Sera closed her eyes briefly and asked whatever gods remained uncaged to give her strength, because Anselm had just sworn a binding contract in a place where contracts were enforced by things that ate people, and he'd sworn it in the voice of a man reciting scripture.

The tollkeeper extended her hand. Sera laid the bone sliver on the glass desk. The tollkeeper touched it once, and the mark on it glowed a sullen red, and somewhere far above — or far below; directions were negotiable down here — a bell rang once.

"Witnesses Sera Vane, returned; the mercenary; the priest," the tollkeeper intoned, and Sera felt the words land on her like tags clipped to her ear. Named, catalogued, entered into the record. Everything spoken aloud in this room went into the Ledger. Everything in the Ledger was owned by someone.

"Welcome home, Lady Vane," the tollkeeper added, and smiled for the first time, and Sera wished to every silent god that she hadn't.

* * *

Beyond the tollhouse lay the galleries, and Sera had warned them about the galleries, and it hadn't helped.

The galleries were where the court displayed what it owned. Alcoves lined the descending corridors, each framed in silver filigree, each lit by its own knot of captive godlight, and each holding — displayed, arranged, *curated* — a soul.

Some were people. Some had been people. One alcove held nothing but a wedding ring turning slowly in midair, polished by devotion, worn by a love so large it had been worth selling everything else. Another held a soldier's last battle, replaying eternally behind glass: a boy of maybe seventeen charging uphill forever, brave forever, dying nowhere, because his courage had been extracted and mounted and the dying left out as inelegant.

"That's not possible," Anselm whispered. He'd stopped in front of an alcove containing an old woman rocking an empty cradle, humming. "A memory isn't — you can't take the *humming*, the humming isn't a thing, it's—"

"It's a thing here." Sera took his sleeve. "Walk."

Lena wasn't walking. Lena had planted herself in front of an alcove near the gallery's turn, and Sera saw her face and knew, with the cold certainty of a courtier reading a rival's expression across a ballroom, that this one was personal.

Inside the frame sat a girl of perhaps fifteen, knees drawn up, staring at her own hands. She looked up as Lena stopped — and there was nothing behind her eyes. Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness would have been kinder. There was a *waitfulness*, the look of a house whose family has gone out and left the lamps burning.

"Do I know you?" the girl asked. Politely. The way you ask a stranger.

"No," Sera said quickly, stepping between them. "No, she doesn't. Come along, witness, you're falling behind the reconciliation party—"

But Lena had already answered, quietly, to the girl: "You look like someone I used to know."

"Oh." The girl considered this with the mild interest of someone considering weather. "I don't know anyone anymore. They took all of it. But they said parts of me still work." She held up her hands and turned them over, examining them like tools belonging to someone else. "See? They still fold."

They folded. Small, precise, terrible.

Sera got her hand around Lena's arm and pulled, and Lena let herself be pulled, which frightened Sera more than resistance would have. Resistance she could manage. This obedience was the obedience of a woman in shock.

At the gallery's end, before the doors to the ledger hall, Lena finally spoke, and her voice came out sanded flat.

"How much for her? To buy her back. Ballpark. Give me a number so I know what shape the world's cruelty comes in."

"There is no buying back," Sera said. "That's the entire design. What's sold here stays sold. The Ledger balances both ways, always, and it never balances toward mercy." She straightened Lena's collar with two brisk tugs, an intimacy that was pure theater for the benefit of the door-guards. "Whatever you're planning, plan it smaller. We are witnesses. We stand, we watch, we leave. Then we go down to the cage and we burn the whole rotten arithmetic out of the world. Not before."

"You think I'm planning something."

"I think," Sera said, "that you looked at that girl the way people look at altars. Yes. I think you're planning something."

The great doors swung inward on the ledger hall, and the sound that rolled out of it was singing.

* * *

The ledger hall was a bowl of white stone sunk deep in the mountain's heart, ringed with balconies where the court's true power reclined behind gauze screens, and at its center stood the Ledger itself: not a book, though books attended it in their hundreds, chained and fluttering, but a column of light and script rising from floor to unseen ceiling, writing and unwriting itself perpetually, each line a soul, each soul a line.

And threaded through it, woven into the light, was the song.

Sera had heard it every day for four years and had trained herself not to hear it, the way you train yourself not to hear your own heartbeat. Now, watching Lena and Anselm hear it for the first time, she saw what it did. Anselm pressed both hands over his mouth. Lena swayed like a struck bell.

It was voices. Dozens. Hundreds. Layered and harmonized and *harvested* — old voices, vast voices, squeezed thin and braided together into something that sounded almost like music if you didn't listen closely, and like screaming if you did.

"The gods," Anselm whispered into his hands. "Oh, merciful — it's *them*. It's actually them."

"Not so loud," Sera breathed.

Down on the hall's white floor, the brokers were gathering for the evening reconciliation, robed in colors that hurt to look at directly, and among the petitioners waiting to sell stood a figure Sera recognized with a lurch that dropped through her stomach like a stone through ice water.

Tall. Gray-cloaked. Face shadowed under a hood that the hall's light refused to touch properly.

She knew that silhouette. Everyone at Malachar's court knew it. The Voice's own herald, come in person, which happened only for transactions of state — only for acquisitions the usurper wanted witnessed personally.

The herald turned its hooded head, slowly, scanning the balconies. Searching.

And stopped on their balcony.

Stopped on *her*.

Every rule Sera had learned in four years of surviving this place collapsed at once into a single animal instinct, and she grabbed Lena's arm and Anselm's shoulder and pulled them both down behind the balcony rail as the herald began, unhurried, to climb the stairs toward them.

"Sera," Lena whispered, hand going to her knife. "Who is that?"

"Malachar's herald," Sera said. "And it just looked at us like it knows exactly why we came." She risked one glance over the rail. The hood was still climbing. "Which means somewhere between the falls and here, somebody told it we were coming."

Above them, threaded through the harvested song, one voice — low, female, familiar in a way that stopped Sera's breath — sang a single note louder than the rest.

Almost, Sera thought, like a warning.
