## 13. The Broker's Ledger

The broker kept his office behind a door made of coin.

Not gold — that would have been honest. The door was struck through with a hundred metals, copper and tin and dull grey iron, each disc stamped with a face Lena did not recognize and could not stop studying as Sera negotiated their passage into the counting-house beneath the galleries. Some of the faces were smiling. None of them had eyes.

"They're the spent ones," Sera murmured, following her gaze. "Souls that have been traded so many times there's nothing left to trade. They mint them."

"Into doors?"

"Into everything." Sera's voice was flat, court-trained, but Lena caught the hairline crack in it now — the sound of a woman who had walked these halls for years and only recently let herself see what they were built from. "The chandeliers in the west gallery. The inlay on Malachar's throne. He calls it the economy of devotion. Souls are currency because currency is what people will give anything to keep, and everything to spend."

Anselm stood apart from them both, hands clasped like a man at a graveside. Which, Lena thought, was not far wrong.

The door opened without being touched. Cold air breathed out, smelling of ink and tallow and something older underneath — stone, deep stone, the smell of a place that had never seen weather.

"Three," said a voice from within. "But one is owed already. Come in, mercenary. Your account has been open for ten years. I do so hate an unbalanced book."

* * *

The broker was small. That was the first obscenity of him. Lena had imagined — needed to imagine — something vast, some spider-thing at the center of a web of sold souls, a monster she could hate cleanly. Instead he was a narrow old man in a grey robe, bald as an egg, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on a nose sharp enough to open letters. He sat at a desk that filled half the room, and the desk itself was the other obscenity: a slab of black glass, and beneath its surface, if you looked too long, faces moved. Thousands of them. Drifting slow as fish under ice.

He wrote without looking up.

"Sit," he said. Chairs appeared. Lena did not trust them and stayed standing, which earned her the first flicker of his attention — pale eyes above the spectacles, quick and dry as a clerk tallying a discrepancy.

"You don't sit when you grieve," he observed. "Interesting. Most of them sit. Most of them want very much to believe this is a dream they can wake from." He set down his pen and laced his ink-stained fingers. "Lena Villalobos. Sold: one soul, female, age nineteen at time of transaction, name of Ilsa Villalobos. Purchased by me on behalf of the court below. Payment rendered: remission of your death-debt to the Kestrel Syndicate, plus passage out of the Salt Cities, plus—" he consulted nothing, remembering perfectly, "—plus the erasure of the debt from every ledger that mattered, including the ones written in blood. A generous rate. You haggled surprisingly well for a girl of sixteen who'd been stabbed twice that winter."

"Stop talking."

"I haven't started." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried, a man who owned time the way other men owned cattle. "You've come to buy her back. Everyone comes back eventually. It takes most of them longer than ten years — pride, usually, or cowardice wearing pride's coat. You took exactly as long as I predicted. I have you down for eleven years and four months, actually, but I'll round in your favor. Sentiment."

"I'm not here to buy her."

His hands went still. Beneath the desk-glass, the drifting faces seemed to pause with him.

"No?" he said softly. "Then why descend through my mountain, cross my inner ways, flatter my etiquette with my turncoat here"—Sera didn't flinch, but her jaw tightened— "and stand in my office smelling of rain and stubbornness? People come to me for three reasons. To sell. To buy. Or to burn the ledger. And you can't do the third, mercenary, because the ledger isn't paper. It's her."

Lena's hand had gone to her knife somewhere in the last few sentences. She made herself take it away. Made herself breathe. Veyra gave no answer — Veyra had given no answer in ten years — but she prayed anyway, the way a man taps a dead man's shoulder out of habit, out of love, out of refusal to accept the arithmetic of loss.

"The map," she said instead. "The sigils on it. Anselm read them. One of them is a name. Hers. Written in the old script, the way the gods write things when they want them kept."

Something moved behind the broker's eyes. Not surprise. Recalculation. "The priest," he said. "Yes. I wondered when someone faithful would finally carry one of those maps far enough. They were drawn by believers, you know, before the cages — smuggler roads marked in prayer-script so the faithful could find their way home even if the world changed around them. Malachar thinks they're trade routes. He collects them. He cannot read a single line." He smiled, thin as a ledger-line. "You can't either. But the script can read *you*. That's how you found the gate behind the falls. That's how you found my door. The sigils are still doing their original work, Miss Villalobos. They are leading the lost home."

"She isn't lost. She's *here*."

"She is an asset," the broker agreed pleasantly, "in gallery nine, serving wine to the minor nobility of a usurper's court, and she is very good at it. No memories worth mentioning. Excellent posture. The buyers prefer them blank — a soul with memories argues with its new shape, and arguing souls depreciate." He turned the black glass of the desk toward her. "Would you like to check the balance?"

Lena looked.

She had braced herself to see Ilsa's face and failed anyway. There she was, small beneath the glass surface among the thousands — nineteen forever, dark-haired, standing in a gallery lit by godlight, pouring wine with perfect posture and empty eyes. As Lena watched, a noblewoman snapped her fingers and Ilsa refilled a cup without looking up, and the motion was so familiar — Ilsa had poured water for their mother that way, hip-cocked, impatient, humming — that Lena's knees nearly went.

The humming was gone. Everything was gone. The glass showed her soul the way a butcher's window shows meat: clean, cold, portioned.

"How much," Lena said. Her voice came out level. She would remember that later, with something like awe — that her voice held. "To buy her back."

"That," said the broker, "is the wrong question, and I'm delighted you asked it."

* * *

He rose, at last, and crossed to the wall, where shelves of ledgers rose floor to ceiling into the dark. But he didn't take one down. He pressed his palm flat against the shelf and the wood — the whole wall, the whole room, Lena realized, the whole *mountain* — hummed like a struck bell.

"Everyone misunderstands the trade," he said. "They think we deal in souls. We don't. Souls are merely the vessel. We deal in *weight*." He turned. "Every memory, every love, every grief a person carries has mass. When you sold your sister, you didn't sell her body — the syndicate got your body, briefly, until the debt cleared. You sold her *weight in you*. Every year of her. Every scar. The sum total of what Ilsa Villalobos meant to Lena Villalobos, measured and extracted and transferred to my ledger, where it became currency." His ink-stained finger tapped the black glass. "That is what's floating in there. Not her. What she weighed in you. And the court spends it — burns it as fuel, trades it as ornament, feeds it to soldiers who need courage they weren't born with. Malachar's armies march on memories, mercenary. Mostly other people's."

"So I buy her weight back."

"With what coin?" He spread his hands. "Gold? The mountain mints its own. Favor? Whose favor outweighs a sister? No. There is only one currency accepted at this counter for a withdrawal of this size, and you are carrying it." His pale eyes settled on her head, her heart, her hands — weighing her, she understood suddenly, the way she weighed a mark before a contract. "Memories. Yours. Of equal weight to the extraction. Nothing more, nothing less. The ledger balances both ways, always. It is the one law even Malachar cannot rewrite, though he has tried."

"And if I pay?"

"She walks out of gallery nine with her soul restored. Her memories of her life return — all of them except, of course, the ones you paid with, since those will no longer exist anywhere. She will remember her mother. Her childhood. The Salt Cities. The winters." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "She will not remember you. You will be a stranger who appears at her side in a burning courtroom, and she will have no idea why your face makes her want to cry."

The words landed and Lena felt each one separately, the way you feel individual blows through armor.

"There's another way," Anselm said quietly, from his corner. "There must be. Broker — in the old days, before the cages, the temples taught that no debt of love could be—"

"The old days ended," the broker said, without heat. "Your temples are ruins and your god is silent and the girl's sister is floating in my desk. Pray if it helps you, priest. But pray at someone else's counter."

"He's right about one thing, though," Lena heard herself say. "There's another way. I take her out by force. Gallery nine, the inner ways, Sera knows the roads—"

"And deliver her where?" The broker wasn't mocking her. That was the worst part. He sounded genuinely, professionally curious. "Her soul stays in my ledger whether her body stands in Thornfell or Corvale. You'd be carrying home an empty coat. Unless—" the pale eyes glittered, "—you intend to shatter the cage. Ah. Ah, yes. The map. The final sigil. You don't know yet, do you? What you're carrying." He laughed once, a dry sound like a page turning. "No. Don't tell me. I only balance books; I don't spoil endings. But understand this, mercenary: if the cage breaks, every soul in it goes free, hers included, and my ledger burns with it, and I lose the finest collection ever assembled. So I have every reason to refuse you. And yet."

He came back around the desk and stood close, close enough that she could see the ink stains went all the way to the wrist, as if he'd been writing for centuries.

"And yet I am going to offer you terms," he said, "because the ledger balances both ways, and Malachar owes this mountain debts he will never pay, and I have been waiting eleven years and four months for someone stubborn enough to walk in that door and mean it. Sit down, Lena Villalobos. Let's weigh your sister."

* * *

The scale was her own skull.

That was how it worked — he explained it while she sat rigid in the chair she'd refused before, his pen moving across a sheet of vellum that drank the ink rather than holding it. Memory-weight wasn't counted in years. It was counted in *load-bearing*: how much of you rested on a given recollection. A stranger's face might weigh nothing. A single afternoon might hold up a whole life.

"So," the broker said. "Ilsa. Tell me what you remember. All of it. Spare nothing — I'll know if you do, and shortchanging the ledger is the one crime I punish personally."

And because there was no point lying to a man who weighed souls, Lena told him.

She told him about Ilsa learning to whistle before she learned to talk, and driving their mother half-mad whistling funeral hymns at market stalls. About the fever winter, when Ilsa was seven and Lena was nine and they'd slept wrapped together in one blanket and shared dreams without meaning to. About teaching her knife-work in the reed-beds, Ilsa laughing, Ilsa fast, Ilsa better than her within two years and insufferable about it for a decade after. About the last night — the death-debt come due, the syndicate's men at the door, and Lena at sixteen kneeling in a cellar beneath a different mountain making a bargain with a different clerk while Ilsa slept upstairs believing her sister would fix everything, because Lena had told her she would, because Lena had always fixed everything—

Her voice broke on that one. The broker waited, pen poised, patient as erosion.

"Enough," he said at last, gently, and she hated the gentleness. He consulted the vellum, where lines had begun to move on their own, arranging themselves into columns. "A heavy account. She carried a great deal of you. Which means restoring her costs a great deal." He looked up over the spectacles. "You cannot pay it all. Not and remain functional — the ledger doesn't accept suicides, only trades. So we select. Give me a memory of equal weight to the extraction, and I release her. Choose carefully. Whatever you surrender is gone — not hidden, not sleeping. Gone. You will not mourn it, because mourning requires the memory of what was lost. You will simply have a hole shaped like it, and everyone else will remember what you no longer can, and that, Miss Villalobos, is the particular hell of my profession, and I have occupied it long enough to know."

Lena sat in the chair and understood, finally, the true architecture of the trap she'd walked into ten years ago and again today. Not cruelty. Arithmetic.

"What do you recommend?" she asked, and despised herself for asking.

"Nothing. I don't recommend. I itemize." He turned the vellum toward her. Lines of script glowed faintly — the old prayer-script, she realized, the same hand that had written the smuggler's map. Even here. Even in the ledger. "But I will note that the heaviest single item in your inventory, heavier than any year of Ilsa, heavier perhaps than all of them together, is this: *your mother's face.*"

The room went quiet. Under the desk-glass, the drowned faces drifted.

"You were twelve when she died," the broker said. "You've carried her face for twenty-two years. It is the foundation-stone of everything you built on top of it — every mercy you've shown, every contract you've refused, every night you've spent praying to a silent god because your mother prayed to her first. Remove that stone and the structure stands — you'd be surprised what stands — but it will never stand the same way. And here is the itemizer's observation, freely given:" he leaned forward, "Ilsa remembers your mother. Every day of her. When her soul returns, that memory returns *with* her. Trade me your mother's face, and you lose it alone — but your sister keeps it. Keeps all of it. Somewhere in the world, your mother's face survives, worn by the person you did all this to save." He straightened. "The ledger balances both ways, mercenary. Sometimes it balances kindly. Rarely. But sometimes."

Lena closed her eyes.

Her mother's face. Brown hands kneading bread. The gap in her teeth when she laughed. The way she'd squint at the sky and say rain was coming two days before it came, and be right, and smug about it. The last thing she'd said — Lena reached for it and found it, and held it, and understood that in an hour she would reach for it and find nothing, a smooth wall where a door used to be.

Veyra, she prayed. Silent god. If you're anywhere — if you ever were anywhere — witness this.

The silence answered nothing. But it didn't feel empty tonight. It felt like a held breath.

"Do it," Lena said.

* * *

The extraction didn't hurt. She'd expected pain; the broker had warned her against expecting anything, and he'd been right. It was like a tide going out. Like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she wore. Warmth left her — not the warmth of her body, a deeper warmth, banked-coal warmth — and she felt it go with a kind of horror that had nowhere to attach itself, because the reason for the horror was leaving with it.

The broker caught it in a small glass vial. Inside swirled something brown and golden and laughing, something with a gap-toothed smile, and Lena looked at it and felt —

Nothing. A hole, shaped like everything.

"Balance achieved," the broker said softly, and for just a moment his clerk's voice sounded almost sorry. He uncorked the vial and poured it into the black glass of the desk, and beneath the surface the thousand drifting faces parted like water to let it through.

In gallery nine, a woman pouring wine stopped mid-pour.

Lena couldn't see it. But she knew it the way you know a change in wind — a pressure shifting somewhere far above, a bell rung in a tower you couldn't see. The broker watched his desk, spectacles flashing, and nodded once, twice, like a man confirming a delivery.

"She's coming back to herself," he said. "It takes a moment. Ten years is a long time to be spent." He glanced up. "You should know, before you go — the court has noticed the disturbance. Malachar keeps a finger on the ledger's pulse, and yours just skipped. He will know a withdrawal was made from gallery nine. He may even guess whose." He began cleaning his pen, unhurried, already turning to the next column of his endless accounts. "You have perhaps an hour. Possibly less. Go collect your sister, mercenary. And when you find whatever it is you're clearly going to find in the depths of this cage—" the pale eyes flicked up, glittering, "—remember that I balanced your book fairly. It may matter, afterward. It matters to very few, but to me it will matter."

Lena was already at the door — the door made of coins, the blind faces smiling their blind smiles — when his voice caught her one last time.

"Miss Villalobos. Your mother's face. Describe it to yourself now, quickly, before the hour turns. It's the last chance anyone will ever have to check."

She stood in the doorway and reached inward, desperately, into the hole —

—and found nothing, smooth as riverstone, and understood that she had already forgotten, that she'd forgotten the moment the tide went out, and that the woman she'd done all of this for was upstairs remembering their mother's laugh while Lena stood in a cellar unable to picture the face that had loved them both first.

She climbed the stairs to gallery nine with her heart hammering and her past full of holes, and turned the corner into godlight, and there — alive, upright, pouring wine with her hip cocked the way it had always been cocked — stood Ilsa.

Who looked up at the sound of her boots.

And frowned, politely, at a stranger.
