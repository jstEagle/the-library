## 14. Ilsa

The galleries of the inverted court were built to be walked slowly.

Lena understood that within the first hundred steps. Everything down here was engineered for lingering — the vaulted ceilings painted with constellations that didn't exist in any sky she knew, the floors of black stone polished so fine they threw back your reflection like standing water. Servants drifted between the alcoves carrying trays of candied fruit and dark wine, and the courtiers who lounged on the low divans barely looked up as the three of them passed. Sera had dressed them for it: Lena in a grey courier's coat with no insignia, Anselm in the plain robes of a household chaplain, herself in silk the color of wet slate. *Walk like you belong,* she'd said, *and the court will assume someone important sent you.* So far it was working. Down here, belonging was a costume anyone could rent.

But it wasn't the wealth or the strangeness that made Lena slow her steps. It was the light.

The godlight came from everywhere — from veins in the walls, from the chandeliers, from the wine itself when you held it up. It was gold shot through with threads of blue, warm as a hearthfire, and it made everything beautiful in the way that a lie is beautiful. She'd felt it pressing against her skin since they came through the gate behind the falls, like walking into water too deep to stand in. Anselm kept one hand pressed flat against his chest as he walked, over his heart, where the old prayers lived.

"It's louder here," he murmured, for the third time since the descent began.

"You said that."

"Because it keeps getting louder." His eyes moved across the ceiling, tracking something she couldn't see. "They're not singing freely anymore, Lena. It's — metered. Rationed. Like a well being drawn from by force." He swallowed. "This is what I told them, ten years ago. This is exactly what I described, and they stripped my orders and burned my books for it." There was no bitterness left in his voice. Only a tired kind of vindication that looked worse than grief.

"Keep your voice down," Sera said without turning. She moved through the galleries like she owned them, chin level, pace unhurried, acknowledging no one. Every few paces her eyes flicked sideways — cataloguing exits, counting guards, noting which courtiers wore Malachar's new sigil at their throats and which still dared to go unmarked. "The steward's gallery is two levels below us. The ledger hall is beyond that. If we're going to find your sister's name in the broker's records, we do it before the third bell, when the night households change over and the corridors fill."

"I know the plan, Vane."

"Then stop looking at every servant like—"

Lena stopped looking at servants.

She stopped because one of them had stopped.

* * *

She was three alcoves away, arranging sweetmeats on a lacquered tray, and she moved wrong. Not clumsily — the opposite of clumsily. She moved the way Lena moved, the way soldiers and mercenaries moved, weight balanced, eyes doing quiet work. But there was nothing behind the efficiency. No wariness. A body trained to watch, running empty.

Her hair was different. Shorter than Lena remembered, pinned back severe and practical, and she'd filled out in the shoulders the way people did when their labor turned physical. The face was older. Ten years should have done that anyway. But the face was the same face, and Lena's whole body knew it before her mind caught up — her heart slamming once, hard, like a fist against a door.

*Ilsa.*

She didn't say it. Some instinct older than thought clamped her jaw shut, and thank all the silent gods it did, because Sera's hand closed on her elbow with surprising strength and steered her two steps onward before she could break.

"Not here," Sera breathed.

"That's her."

"I know. I can see your face. Not here."

"She's right there, Sera. She is standing *right there*—"

"And forty people can see you standing there shaking apart in a borrowed coat." Sera's grip didn't loosen. Her voice stayed smooth and pleasant, pitched for the room, a courtier's murmur. "Look at me. Look at me, mercenary. You are a courier waiting on a delivery. Your contact is late. You're mildly annoyed. That is all you are for the next hour, until I get us into a side gallery where a scene won't cost us our heads. Do you understand?"

Lena understood. Understanding didn't help. Her hands had gone numb and far away, and the godlight seemed suddenly very loud, humming against her teeth.

Anselm stepped in close on her other side, blocking her view of the alcove with his broad back, an accident of positioning that was surely no accident at all. "Breathe," he said quietly. "In through the nose. You taught me that, on the pass, when the mountain answered. Now I'm teaching it back."

"I don't need—"

"In through the nose."

She breathed. Once. Twice. The gallery swam back into focus: the trays, the murmur of conversation, the impossible stars overhead. Somewhere below, a bell rang soft as a struck glass.

"The third bell," Sera said. "Then the changeover. When the servants rotate out of the public galleries, your sister will walk past the service corridor on the east stair. Alone, if the rotation runs as it used to. We'll be in the shadow of the archway. You'll have thirty seconds, maybe less, and then we move toward the ledger hall and you keep moving with us." She finally released Lena's arm. Her expression, for just a moment, wasn't the courtier's mask. Something almost human moved underneath it. "Thirty seconds. Make them count, and don't make them loud."

* * *

They waited among the wine casks of a service landing while the court above them changed shifts. It was strange, hearing a palace breathe. Doors opening and closing in sequence, footsteps in waves, the low tide-murmur of a thousand small duties changing hands. Anselm prayed under his breath — not aloud, never aloud, but his lips moved through shapes Lena had begun to recognize. *Veyra who walks unwitnessed. Veyra who keeps the gate. Keep her gate-ward now; keep her steady.* He'd taught her that one four nights ago in a smuggler's tunnel, by sigil-light, and she'd laughed at how simple it was. Prayers, he'd said, weren't requests. They were handrails. Something to hold while the world tilted.

The world was tilting now.

Sera counted the footsteps overhead. Nodded once. And then Ilsa was coming down the east stair with a folded cloth over one arm and an empty tray balanced on her palm, and the thirty seconds began.

Up close, the hollowing was worse.

Lena had imagined — in ten years of imagining, in ten years of not letting herself imagine — every version of this meeting. Ilsa furious. Ilsa weeping. Ilsa spitting in her face, which would have been fair, which would have been earned. She had rehearsed apologies in the dark of a hundred camps, whole speeches delivered to the ceiling of her tent, words shaped and discarded and shaped again. None of those rehearsals had prepared her for a sister who looked straight through her. For eyes that were brown and clear and entirely vacant, like windows in a house where the fire had gone out long ago but the furniture was still neat.

Ilsa reached the landing. Saw them. And inclined her head — a servant's nod, courteous, automatic.

"More wine for the west gallery?" she asked. Her voice was even. Pleasant. Trained.

"No," Lena managed.

"Then forgive me. I'm needed on the floor." She moved to step past.

Lena's hand moved faster than her fear. She caught Ilsa's wrist — lightly, barely touching, the way you'd touch a wild bird — and Ilsa stopped, and looked down at the touch with mild, faintly puzzled interest, the way you'd look at weather.

"Your name," Lena said. It came out wrecked. She tried again. "What's your name?"

A small crease formed between Ilsa's brows. Genuine confusion, no offense in it at all. "Ilsa," she said. Then, after a pause, as if checking the word against some inner inventory and finding it correct: "Ilsa. Yes."

"Yes," Lena whispered. "Yes, that's right. That's your name. Ilsa."

"You've had too much of the gold wine." Ilsa smiled — and gods, the smile was the same too, the crooked one, the one that had gotten them both out of trouble and into worse, and it was aimed at her like sunlight aimed at a stranger. "Everyone smiles strangely down here after the gold wine. Don't worry. It passes."

"Ilsa. My name is—" Her throat closed. Ten years of speeches, and this was the one that mattered, and she couldn't push a single syllable through. She started again. "My name is Lena. Lena Villalobos. We grew up—" In Saltmere, before the debt, before the brokers, before I signed the paper with your name on it because mine was the one they wanted and I chose mine. "We knew each other. Before. Do you remember anything before?"

Ilsa considered the question with the patience of someone asked to recall a dream. "Before the court," she said slowly, "there was a road. And cold. I think there was snow." Her brow furrowed deeper, working. "There was a sound like singing, very far off. Everyone was afraid of the singing." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and empty and kind. "That's all. Is that what you wanted?"

Somewhere behind Lena's ribs, something that had been holding for ten years went very quietly, completely, irreparably wrong.

"No," she heard herself say. "No, that's not—"

"Lena." Anselm's voice, low, urgent. The bell. The changeover ending. Footsteps beginning again above them.

"One moment. One more moment—"

"We have to move."

Ilsa had already stepped back, the courtesy reassembling itself over her face like a visor. "If there's nothing else, I'm needed on the floor. The stewards mark late deliveries." A beat, and then — small, reflexive, human — "You seem sad. I hope whatever you lost comes back to you."

And she walked past, tray balanced, spine straight, and took the stairs down into the gold-lit dark, and did not look back, because there was nothing in her that knew to look back.

Lena stood in the shadow of the archway with her hand still half-raised, holding the shape of a wrist that was no longer there.

* * *

Sera got them into a side gallery — some minor courtier's audience room, abandoned between appointments, all gilt chairs and cold hearth — and put her back to the door and watched the corridor while Lena came apart as quietly as a person can come apart, which is to say not quietly at all, but with her fist jammed against her own mouth so it was mostly soundless. Anselm knelt nearby and didn't touch her. He just stayed, solid, breathing audibly, an anchor of ordinary noise.

"She asked my name twice," Lena said finally, when her voice worked again. "Did you hear that? She said her own name and then — checked it. Like she wasn't sure it fit."

"They do that," Sera said, not turning from the door. "Spent souls hold their names loose. Names were the first thing the brokers learned to draw off. Everything else pools behind them — skill, habit, training — but the name sits shallow, like water over ice." A pause. "It's why the court uses them for service. They're obedient without being broken. Broken things are worth less on the market."

"She remembered snow. And singing." Lena laughed, and it came out awful. "Ten years. I traded ten years of her life, and what's left is *snow and singing.*"

"You didn't trade her life," Anselm said quietly. "You traded her memory. And the ledger says memories aren't destroyed, Lena. They're held. Held isn't gone."

"Held by *him.* By the thing under the mountain wearing a dead god's voice." She pushed herself up the wall to standing, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and found that the grief had done something strange to her — burned off the tremor and left something colder and harder underneath. She recognized the feeling. It was the feeling she got before a fight she couldn't win but couldn't walk away from either. "Then we go get it back. The ledger. Tonight, before the third bell, like Vane said. Find her entry, find what it costs to buy a soul back out of that book."

Sera turned from the door at last, and her face was careful. Too careful. "Lena."

"What."

"The ledger balances both ways. You told me the broker's warning yourself, and you believed it, and you were right to believe it. Nothing comes out of that book free. Not a soul, not a name, not a memory." She hesitated — actually hesitated, this woman who negotiated like breathing — and Lena felt the floor tilt a degree further. "And there's something else you need to hear before we take another step toward that hall. Something about the entries themselves. About whose name is written next to yours in the margin of Ilsa's line."

The godlight hummed in the walls. Above them, faint through the ceiling, the rationed song of the caged gods rose and fell like breathing.

Anselm had gone very still. "How do you know what's written in the margins?"

"Because I read the ledger," Sera said, "the night I ran. And because, mercenary — your sister's line has been marked *reserved* for eleven years. Since before you ever sold her." She met Lena's eyes. "Someone bought her soul long before you did. And the buyer's sigil is the one you were born carrying."

Far above them, muffled by miles of stone, a horn sounded — three notes, clean and cold, rolling down through the mountain's bones.

Sera's face drained white. "That's a summons," she whispered. "He knows we're here. Malachar is calling us to court."
