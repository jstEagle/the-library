## 15. The Usurper's Court

The summons arrived on silver paper.

Sera had seen a great deal of Malachar's court in her eleven years of service, and she knew its language the way she knew the taste of poison — by the shape it left on the tongue. Silver paper meant courtesy. Courtesy meant he already knew everything and wanted to watch them pretend otherwise. The invitation bore no seal, because no seal in the world outranked him, and it named three names: *the mercenary Villalobos, the priest Anselm, and the Lady Sera Vane, who is missed.*

*Who is missed.* Only Malachar could make a kindness sound like a threat laid on silk.

They read it in the guest quarters they'd been given beneath the galleries — rooms too fine for prisoners and too guarded for guests. Lena read it twice and handed it back without expression. Anselm held it at arm's length like something that might bite, which, Sera reflected, was not entirely wrong.

"He knows we're here," Lena said.

"He's known since the Salt Road," Sera said. "Possibly since Corvale. He lets things walk toward him when walking toward him is useful." She smoothed the silver paper flat against her knee. "We don't refuse. Refusal is a message, and messages cost more than visits."

"And if it's a trap?"

"It's always a trap," Sera said. "The trick is that it isn't only a trap."

Anselm looked up from the sigil map spread between them, its inked marks still faintly warm to the touch, glowing now with a light that needed no candle. "What does he want?"

Sera almost laughed. Eleven years of court etiquette, of reading a man's desire from the angle of his goblet, and the defrocked priest asked the question as though it were difficult. "He wants what he has always wanted. But tonight he'll want us to believe he wants something else."

* * *

They dressed for the court the way soldiers dress for a battle they expect to lose slowly. Sera wore grey — mourning colors, deliberately; let him read into that what he liked. Lena wore her mercenary leathers under a borrowed coat cut too fine for her, and carried no blade, because the summons had specified none, and because Sera had told her that Malachar enjoyed counting other people's weapons. Anselm wore his old cassock, patched at the hem, the vestments of a church that no longer existed, and dared anyone in the mountain to comment.

The way up to the throne hall was a lesson in what sold souls bought.

Sera had walked these corridors before her defection and had learned, over years, to stop seeing them. Now she saw. The lanterns along the stair did not burn oil; they burned voice — she could feel it, a thin harmonic whine behind the light, like a struck glass that never finished ringing. The tapestries moved when no draft moved them, woven from thread spun through captured prayer. A servant girl curtsied to them at the second landing, and Sera caught the flicker behind the girl's eyes: not emptiness exactly, but distance, the look of someone watching their own life from across a river.

Lena stopped once, mid-stair, staring at nothing.

"What is it?" Anselm murmured.

"Someone's praying," Lena said quietly. "In there." She touched her own sternum. "I can hear it. It sounds like—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Sera had heard it too, for years, and had told herself it was the acoustics.

"Keep walking," Sera said, and hated how steady her voice came out.

The doors to the throne hall opened before they reached them, which was also his style. No guards announced them. No herald called their names. The hall simply received them, the way deep water receives a stone.

Malachar was not on his throne.

He stood at the far end of the hall beside a table laid with wine and winter fruit, and he greeted them the way an old friend greets travelers — arms slightly open, head tilted, warmth arranged precisely on his face like flowers arranged on a grave. Up close, Sera thought — and she had thought this many times, in the years she'd smiled at this man across council tables — up close there was something wrong with his voice before he even spoke. It resonated too low. It filled the room the way a god fills a temple: without effort, and without asking permission.

"Lady Vane." His eyes found her first, as she'd known they would. "You've been gone eleven months. The garden you kept on the east terrace has gone wild without you."

"I'm sure it prefers me absent," Sera said. "Most living things do, after a while."

His smile widened a fraction. "And Father Anselm. Or should I say — Anselm? I understand the Synod revoked your orders some years ago. Heresy, wasn't it?" He poured wine himself, four cups, unhurried. "You claimed the gods hadn't departed Thornfell. You claimed they'd been taken." He handed the cup to Anselm last, holding it a moment longer than courtesy required. "The Synod called it madness. I called it premature."

"You knew," Anselm said. Not a question. His knuckles were white around the stem of the cup.

"Anselm. Everyone who matters has always known." Malachar turned then to Lena, and Sera watched the quality of his attention change — sharpen, focus, become the thing it actually was underneath all the velvet. This was the gaze he spent on armies and on maps. "Lena Villalobos. Mercenary of the silent god. Do you know, you're the most expensive thing ever to walk into my hall uninvited?"

"We were invited," Lena said.

"You were permitted." He gestured, and chairs came forward — actual chairs, sliding across marble as though the floor itself were obedient — and they sat, because refusing a seat at Malachar's table was its own kind of statement, and statements were expensive. "I want to talk about value," he said. "About what things are worth. You've been carrying a map across half a mountain, Villalobos, drawn by dead smugglers in a script only the faithful can read. Do you know what it's worth to me?"

"Nothing," Lena said. "You already know where your own cage is."

"Ah." He settled back, delighted, a teacher whose student has finally raised her hand. "But that's just it. It isn't my cage. That's the misunderstanding everyone arrives with, and I do so enjoy correcting it." He rose and crossed to the great window behind the dais — a window that should have shown rock, and instead showed a slow churn of colored dark, godlight bleeding upward from the depths. "Everyone believes I found the old gods sleeping and locked them away. That I stole their voices out of greed or fear. Even my enemies flatter me that way — they imagine me a thief." He glanced back at them, and for one unguarded instant Sera saw the thing beneath the courtesy: hunger, vast and patient, worn smooth by decades of waiting. "But a thief takes what exists. I am doing something far more interesting than theft."

"Rewriting," Sera said quietly.

His head turned to her, and she felt the full weight of that borrowed attention settle on her shoulders. "You always were quick, Lady Vane. Yes. Rewriting." He turned back to the window. "Let me tell you what the gods actually are, since your church never managed it. They are not protectors. They are not judges. They are masons. Every boundary in this world — every border, every shore, every wall between one country and another, between one truth and another — was sung into place by those voices you hear humming under the mountain. The mountains stand where they stand because a god sang them there. The rivers run where they run because something agreed, long ago, that they would." He spread his hands, and the godlight in the window bent toward him, subtly, like plants toward sun. "And agreements, Lady Vane, can be renegotiated."

"You want new borders," Anselm said hoarsely.

"I want *better* ones." For the first time, heat entered his voice, and Sera understood she was hearing the true sermon — the one he'd been polishing alone for thirty years. "Borders are wounds, Father. Every line ever drawn on this world was drawn by frightened men and enforced by hungry ones. Wars breed in the seams. Famine pools along them like water against a dam. And all of it — all of it — rests on songs sung by beings who never once asked the people living inside those boundaries whether they wanted them." He smiled again, gentle as frost. "I am not ending the world. I am editing it. Carefully. Lovingly. And when I am done, there will be no Thornfell marcher lords bleeding the valleys dry. No free cities starving behind tariff walls. No borders at all, perhaps, in time — one world, seamless, whole. Peace, gentlemen. Real peace. The kind that requires no one's consent, because consent is just another boundary, and I am in the business of removing those."

Silence. Somewhere below them, faint through the stone, Sera heard the caged voices shift — a chord changing, uneasy, like sleepers turning over.

"That's why the map matters," Lena said slowly. "Not where the cage is. How it works. The sigils aren't locks. They're—" 

"Staves," Malachar said. "Musical notation. Your smugglers' script and my engineers' script come from the same source, Villalobos — the first drafts of the world. The map you carry shows the tuning of the cage. Which voices sing which walls. Where the harmonies bind tightest." He turned from the window at last and faced her fully, and Sera watched the performance assemble itself around him piece by piece: the warmth, the respect, the terrible reasonableness. "So here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and I will make it honestly, because honesty disarms people far better than lies do.

"Surrender the map. Sing nothing, break nothing, leave my mountain with your sister's soul restored to her body — whole, healed, remembering everything except, if you wish, the parts that pain her. I can do that. The ledger bends for me. In exchange: command of my armies. Not a captaincy. Command. Third voice in my court after myself and the choir. You have spent your life selling violence to men who wasted it. I am offering you the chance to spend it once, well, on the last war there will ever be. After that — gardens, Villalobos. Land. A name that outlives you. Your sister safe behind walls you helped design. Every prayer you ever whispered to a silent god, answered in stone and steel."

Sera watched Lena's face and saw the trap working exactly as designed. Not the offer itself — Lena would refuse the offer. But the frame. *Your sister, safe.* Malachar had done his research; he knew about Ilsa, knew about the ledger, knew precisely which vein of grief to lay his chisel against. Temptation dressed as ambition, with love sewn into the lining.

"And if I refuse?" Lena said.

"Then in ten years you'll be dead somewhere in the foothills, having sold your sword for less each year, and the world will be remade without your consent — which, I note, is how it was made the first time. You weren't consulted about the mountains either." He lifted his cup to her. "Refusal costs you nothing but pride. Acceptance costs you nothing but the illusion that the world as it stands deserved preserving. I genuinely do not see the loss."

"The loss," Anselm said, rising, "is that you'd be singing the world with stolen throats. Those voices down there aren't instruments. They're *people*, Malachar. Gods with names, and the souls you've bought and spent — every servant in these halls, every lantern—"

"Every lantern burns brighter than any village ever did," Malachar said, without heat, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Father. Sit down. I haven't dismissed you yet, and I dislike being preached at by a man whose own church threw him into the street." He waited until Anselm sat — Sera saw the priest's jaw working, fury and shame in equal measure — and then turned back to Lena, and his voice dropped into something almost intimate. Almost kind. "One more thing, and then we'll dine. You've heard them by now. On the stairs. Under the floors. The prayers leaking through the walls. Tell me — did it sound holy?"

Lena said nothing.

"It doesn't," Malachar said. "That's the secret no priest survives learning. It sounds like crying in the next room. It sounds like begging. Ten thousand years of worship, Villalobos, and what did the gods ever give back? Silence. Silence, and borders, and wars fought over lines they drew drunk on their own music. I have given their voices work. Useful work. For the first time since the world began, someone is *listening* to what they sing — and correcting it." He drained his cup. "History will call me a tyrant for a century and a founder forever after. I've made my peace with the exchange rate."

Then his eyes moved to Sera, and she felt the floor tilt.

"But you, Lady Vane. You I haven't finished with." He came around the table, unhurried, and stopped close enough that she could smell the cold mineral scent that clung to him — stone-water, deep places, the smell of the mountain's heart. "Eleven months ago you walked out of my court with documents in your luggage and treason in your step, and I let you go. Have you wondered why?"

"Every day," Sera said. Her voice held. She was proud of that.

"Because I wanted to see where you'd run. And you ran true — straight to the one person in the world who carries what I need most." He smiled, and this time the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Did you really think the final sigil was written on paper? Paper burns. Ink fades. The last word of the world's first song was placed somewhere fire couldn't touch it." His gaze slid past her shoulder, deliberate as a knife laid down between them, and rested on Lena. "It was placed in blood. Before she could walk. Before her mother even knew what her daughter was."

The room went very quiet. Sera heard Anselm's breath stop. She heard, beneath everything, far down in the stone, the caged chorus falter — one clear note rising out of the mass, thin and urgent, like a hand reaching through bars.

Veyra's note. Sera knew it now. She'd been hearing it for eleven months in her dreams.

"You see," Malachar said softly, settling back against the table as though the evening were going pleasantly, "I don't need the map, Villalobos. I need *you*. And the beautiful part—" he raised his refilled cup to her, courteous and terrible and utterly certain, "—is that you've just walked yourself, voluntarily, directly above my cage. So. Shall we dine first, or would you prefer we begin?"

Far below them, the mountain began, very gently, to sing.
