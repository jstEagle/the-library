## 16. What Veyra Hid

The stair went down farther than the mountain should have allowed.

Lena counted the steps because counting was something to do with her hands and her fear. Two hundred. Four hundred. The sigils on the map had faded behind them at the gallery of sold souls, but here, in the deep throat of Thornfell, new marks appeared — not painted, not carved, but *grown*, like veins of light through black stone, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse.

Anselm walked ahead of her, staff in both hands like a man crossing ice. Sera was gone. That thought kept arriving and kept being unbearable, so Lena set it down each time and picked up the next step instead.

"She's alive," Anselm said, without turning. "Malachar doesn't waste things. Not souls, not courtiers, not leverage."

"I know."

"You're not thinking about her."

"No," Lena said. "I'm thinking about Ilsa's face when she asked my name twice."

Anselm said nothing to that. There was nothing honest to say.

The stair ended without warning. One moment it descended; the next it opened into a space so vast that the godlight above them — that pale captive glow that lit the whole inverted court — became a distant ceiling, a false sky hung over a true dark.

The floor was stone, but the stone was *wrong*. It moved. Not shifting, exactly — breathing. Great slow swells passed through it like wind through wheat, and where the swells crested, sigils surfaced: thousands upon thousands of them, interlocked, layered, older than any script Anselm had ever taught her. They were the source of the veins of light. They were the mountain's heartbeat made visible.

And in the center, sunk to its shoulders in that breathing floor, stood the cage.

She had imagined iron. She had imagined chains, bars, some honest metal she could break. But the cage was a lattice of silence — a shape you could only see by what it erased. Where it stood, the sigils did not glow. Sound did not live there. Even her own footsteps died an arm's length from its edge, so that approaching it felt like walking into deeper and deeper water, pressure building against her ears, against her ribs, against whatever part of a person holds their name.

Within the lattice, small and dim as a coal wrapped in ash, sat Veyra.

* * *

Lena had spent her whole life praying to a god she'd never seen, and so she had built one out of habit and longing: tall, veiled, a blade across the knees, patient as winter. The thing in the cage looked nothing like that and everything like it.

Veyra was diminished. That was the first truth and it struck harder than any blow Lena had taken in fifteen years of contracts — the silent god, the unanswering god, was *small*. Kneeling, she would have come to Lena's shoulder. Her form flickered like a candle seen through smoked glass, sometimes a woman, sometimes a shape of gathered shadow, sometimes only an outline the dark refused to fill. Her hands rested palm-up on her thighs. Open hands. A god who had stopped fighting.

But her eyes were conscious. That was the second truth, and it was worse.

They lifted as Lena came within reach of the lattice. Gray eyes. The gray of a road before dawn, of a blade before it's drawn, of every hour Lena had ever waited for an answer that didn't come.

"You're smaller than I imagined," Lena said. Her voice flattened against the silence and came back thin.

"And you are exactly what I imagined." The voice didn't travel so much as arrive, already inside her ear, inside the bone behind it. It sounded tired the way old soldiers are tired — not weak, just finished with pretending otherwise. "You have her jaw. The stubbornness too. I watched you refuse four people on your way down here."

Lena's hand found the hilt of her knife without asking permission. "You've been watching me?"

"Since before you could walk."

"That's a lie." The words came out fast, ugly. "That's a *lie*, because if you were watching — if you heard anything, ever — then all those years I prayed, all those years I begged you after—" She couldn't say Ilsa's name. Not down here. Not yet. "You heard me and said nothing. Ten years. Do you know what that does to a person? Do you know what I did because I thought no one was listening?"

"Yes." Veyra's open hands turned slightly, as if weighing something invisible. "I know what you did. I know what it cost. I have known every hour of it, and I have carried every prayer you sent into the dark, and I could answer none of them. Not would not. *Could not.* And I am going to tell you why, and when I am done, you will wish I had simply stayed silent forever. So sit down, Lena Villalobos. What I have to say is heavier than you are standing."

Lena didn't sit. But she stopped moving toward the cage, and that was surrender enough for now.

Behind her, Anselm lowered himself to the breathing stone with the care of an old man, staff across his knees, and said nothing at all. He had waited ten years to be this close to a god. He was giving her the room.

"The final sigil," Veyra said. "You've heard the term. Malachar wants it. The broker fears it. The map led you here because the map is mine, and I wrote it in a language only the faithful can read, and I made sure it would fall into the hands of one person in all the world." The gray eyes held her. "The gods sing the world's boundaries into being. You know this now. Malachar told you, or Sera did, or the mountain itself whispered it in your sleep. What none of you know is what the final sigil *is*. It is not a word. It is not a mark on paper or stone. Those are copies. Echoes. The original has to live somewhere Malachar could never search, somewhere his brokers could never buy, somewhere his soldiers could never burn."

Lena felt the floor move under her feet. Or felt something move. The sigil-light beneath her had begun to pulse faster, matching — she realized with a lurch — her own heart.

"The original lives in blood," Veyra said. "It lives in a soul, woven into the making of one, placed there at the moment a life begins. It cannot be cut out. It cannot be traded. It cannot be sold, which is why the broker's ledger has never balanced for you, girl, though you've carried the debt of it ten years." Something moved across the diminished god's face that might have been grief, if grief could look that ancient. "The final sigil is you, Lena. It has been you since the night you were born, in a farmhouse above the Salt Road, in the worst storm in a generation. Your mother prayed to me that night. She prayed that the child would survive until morning. And I answered her — the last prayer I was free to answer — and my answering cost me everything, and bought the world one chance."

The silence of the cage pressed in. Lena heard her own blood. That was the horror of it — she *heard* it now, a low tone beneath her skin, a note held for twenty-seven years, and the sigils in the floor around her were glowing in time with it, ring after ring after ring, out into the dark.

"No," she said.

"It is not a question—"

"*No.*" The word cracked off the walls of the vastness. "I'm a mercenary. I'm a hired knife from the foothills. I sold my sister's soul because I was nineteen and terrified, and you're telling me — you're telling me the reason I survived that debt, the reason any of this happened, is that I'm some kind of *vessel*? Some sigil you *hid in me* without asking? My whole life. Every choice I thought was mine. Was any of it mine?" Her voice dropped, and somehow that was louder. "Was I even born, or was I *made*?"

"You were born screaming, like everyone," Veyra said. "Your mother wept. Your father held her. None of that was me. I put one thing in you and one thing only, because it was the only place left to hide it." The god leaned forward within her lattice, close enough that the silence between them felt like glass about to give. "Everything else — the years, the scars, the sister you love, the sister you lost, the woman you chose to become with no god's help at all — that is yours. I hid a seed in a garden. I did not grow the garden. You grew the garden. In the dark, unwatered, unwitnessed, you grew it. Do not take that from yourself. Everyone else will try to. Don't help them."

Lena's legs gave out. She sat down hard on the breathing stone, and it rose warm beneath her, and the sigils pulsed under her palms like a second heartbeat answering the first.

Anselm spoke at last, very quietly. "Why silence? Why ten years of it? If you were hiding the sigil, hiding *her*, why let the world believe the gods had gone? Why let Malachar build all this?"

"Because a hidden thing is safest when no one looks for it," Veyra said. "If the gods still answered prayers, Malachar would have hunted us openly from the beginning, and he would have searched the faithful one by one, and he would have found her before she could stand. So I went quiet. All of us agreed. We let our temples crumble. We let the priests be mocked. We let the faithful feel abandoned, because abandonment was the only shelter left." The gray eyes closed. Opened. "Do you understand what I am telling you, priest? Every unanswered prayer for ten years was a wall around this woman. Every soul that lost faith was a brick. We built her fortress out of our absence, and it worked, and it worked, and it worked — until a smuggler with a map and a defrocked fool and a turncoat brought her down here anyway."

"That wasn't us," Anselm said. "That was you. The map was yours."

"The map was mine," Veyra agreed. "Because the hiding is over. Because Malachar found another way — he stopped looking for the sigil and started building a machine to *listen* for it. The cages aren't only prisons. They are ears. He has been tuning them for years, listening for the one voice he's never been able to steal. And three days ago, on the Salt Road, in the rain, his instruments heard a note they had never heard before." She looked at Lena. "He knows the sigil exists now. He knows it walks. He does not yet know whose blood carries it — but he will, the moment you sing, and he will know it soon regardless, because his armies are already descending, and because the woman he captured was followed here by more than soldiers. Sera Vane is brave and clever and I believe she loves something, underneath it all. But she was seen. Everything that comes to Thornfell is seen."

The floor shuddered. Far above — impossibly far, through galleries and gates and a frozen waterfall — came a sound Lena felt rather than heard: the mountain's doors being opened from the outside.

"So here is the truth entire, and then I will ask nothing of you, because I have no right to ask," Veyra said. "The final sigil can free the gods. Sung aloud, by the blood that carries it, it unmakes any cage, including this one, including the ones holding my brothers and sisters beneath the other peaks. Freed, we would break Malachar's armies in an afternoon, because half his power is stolen from us, and what is stolen returns to its owner when the thief's grip breaks." The god's voice roughened. "Or the sigil can enslave us. Sung in the wrong mouth, bound to the wrong will, it does not open the cages — it becomes one. One cage large enough to hold every god there has ever been, and the world would belong to whoever held it. Malachar cannot sing it himself. But he does not need to. He needs only the blood. There are rites. Old ones. He has had ten years and a broker's ledger, and there are ways to make a soul sing whether it consents or not."

Lena stared at her own hands. Ordinary hands. Knife-callused, scarred at the knuckle, one nail grown back crooked from a bad winter in Corvale. Hands that had signed contracts and buried friends and once, once, pushed a paper across a broker's table that cost her everything.

"You knew," she said. "When Ilsa was taken. When I prayed to you afterward, every night, begging — you knew what I was. You could have told me. You could have told me the ledger balances both ways. You could have—"

"I could have," Veyra said, "and then you would have gone back for her alone, and been caught, and been sung, and everything ends here. Silence was the price of keeping you safe. I paid it gladly and I have hated every day of it, and neither of those facts changes what it cost *you*, and I do not insult you by pretending otherwise." The god straightened within the lattice, and for a moment — one flickering moment — she was tall again, veiled again, the shape from every prayer Lena had ever spoken, and her voice filled the vastness like weather. "So I will not ask you to save us. I will tell you what is true, and leave you the one thing no god should ever have taken from you and no usurper can: the choice.

"Malachar's army is entering the mountain. Your friend is in his hands. Your sister is upstairs in a gallery, hollow-eyed, wearing a borrowed life. The gods of the old world are in cages under seven peaks, and the world's boundaries are fraying wherever their voices fail. And the song that decides all of it is in your blood, and only yours, and it must be sung soon, sung *here*, or never sung at all."

The doors above boomed again. Closer.

Veyra's form settled back to its dim kneeling size, and the gray eyes were tired and kind and terrible.

"I hid myself for ten years to hide you," she said. "Now the hiding is done, Lena Villalobos. Rise, and choose what you were always going to choose — and forgive an old god for loving you enough to let you hate her."

The sigils beneath Lena's palms blazed once, white and total, like a held breath finally let go —

— and from far above, faint through leagues of stone, Lena heard singing. A single voice. Thin, ragged, unmistakable.

*Ilsa.*

Singing the way the broker's slaves sang. Singing because she was made to.

Lena stood up.
