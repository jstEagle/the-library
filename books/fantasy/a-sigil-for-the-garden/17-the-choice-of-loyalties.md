## 17. The Choice of Loyalties

Anselm had spent thirty years learning to read the faces of the dying, and so he knew, watching Sera Vane turn back toward the sound of boots in the dark, that he was looking at a woman arranging her own death.

"Go," she said. She didn't look at them. Her eyes were on the gallery's far arch, where torchlight was beginning to pool like spilled honey — too much of it, too steady, an army's worth. "The service stair behind the reliquary alcove. It comes out above the falls. Take Ilsa and don't stop until—"

"Sera." Lena's voice cracked on the name.

"Don't." Sera finally turned, and Anselm saw her do the thing he had seen soldiers do on a hundred battlefields: she put on a face, quick and seamless as a mask, and behind it something was quietly bleeding out. "You have perhaps two hundred heartbeats before they seal this gallery. Spend them on sentiment and we all die in here, and Malachar rewrites the world with three fewer problems."

"They'll know what you did," Lena said. "They'll know you led us here."

"Oh, they already know." Sera smiled, and it was the courtier's smile, flawless and terrible. "That was always the shape of this. I ran with proof in my satchel and a target on my back, and every road since has been me choosing which direction to run when they caught up. They've caught up." She pressed something into Lena's hands — a slim ring of keys, court-stamped iron. "The lower galleries. Where they keep the ones who still have voices worth stealing. If you're going to do what I think you're going to do, you'll want them."

Anselm watched Lena take the keys. Watched her open her mouth.

"Don't thank me," Sera said. "Thanking implies it was a gift. It was a debt. I sat at that man's table for nine years and laughed at his jokes while he fed gods into a cage. This is merely arithmetic." She stepped backward, toward the light, drawing her knife — a slender, ornamental thing that had never been meant for war but would have to be. "Anselm. Priest."

"I'm not—" he began.

"You wear the collar under your shirt. You've worn it since Corvale. A defrocked man who keeps his collar is a man who hasn't finished believing something." Her smile flickered, just once, into something real. "Pray for me. Loudly. It will annoy him."

Then she turned and walked into the torchlight, spine straight, chin lifted, every inch the courtier presenting herself to power — and the darkness took her voice mid-sentence, mid-word, *mid-name*, because she had been saying something else, something soft, and Anselm would spend the rest of his life wondering what it was.

* * *

The service stair was narrow and cold and smelled of wet stone and old incense, and Anselm climbed it behind two women who were not speaking, and thought: *this is what my whole life has been climbing toward, and it feels like a tomb.*

He had imagined this moment differently. In the years after his expulsion — after he'd stood in the synod hall with his evidence spread across the table, the sigil transcriptions, the resonance measurements, the testimony of three brokers' clerks, and been told gently by men he'd loved that grief had unmade his reason — he had imagined the moment of vindication as a kind of sunrise. Trumpets, maybe. Or just quiet: the old gods' voices returning to the world like water finding its level, and himself kneeling somewhere in the rain, weeping, forgiven by no one, needing no forgiveness.

Instead there was this: a stair behind a reliquary alcove, a hollow-eyed woman ahead of him who had forgotten her own sister's face, and the weight of everything pressing down on a single question that he could feel Lena asking herself with every step.

*Ilsa, or the world.*

He heard her ask it even though she never said it aloud. He heard it in the way her hand kept drifting to the keys in her pocket. He heard it in the way she looked at Ilsa — the small, hungry glances, the way you look at water in a drought — and then away, guiltily, toward the depths below where a diminished god waited in a cage with Lena's name written into her silence.

The stair let out onto a ledge above the frozen falls, and the mountain shook.

Not the deep convulsion of the cage straining — Anselm had felt that for days now, a subsonic groan in the stone like a whale mourning. This was different. This was boots. Hundreds of them, thousands, the disciplined thunder of Malachar's regulars moving through galleries built for whispers and trade. The broker's court had been designed so that no one inside it could ever raise their voice; Malachar's soldiers were about to discover why that architecture cut both ways, and they were coming up through it anyway, floor by floor, sealing exits, and somewhere below, Sera was buying them time with whatever currency she had left.

"We can't go up," Ilsa said. She spoke rarely, and when she did it was with the flat calm of someone narrating weather. "The falls gate is watched. They sealed it an hour ago; I heard the orders." She glanced at Anselm, then at Lena, with no recognition at all — only assessment, the cool arithmetic of a soul that had been spent and repurposed. "There is a way down. Past the ledger-hall. The broker keeps a door for himself. Everyone powerful keeps a door."

"How do you know that?" Lena asked, and her voice was careful, careful, the voice of a woman handling glass.

"I carried messages for six years." Ilsa shrugged. "I remember doors. I don't remember why I remember doors." Something moved behind her eyes then, brief as a fish under ice — not memory, but the *shape* of one, the ghost of a groove worn by feeling. She frowned at Lena. "Why do you look at me like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like you're drowning and I'm air." Ilsa said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "People have looked at me like that before. I never understood it. I don't understand it now."

Lena turned away. Anselm saw her hands close around the keys until the knuckles went white, and he understood — with the awful clarity available only to bystanders — that she was holding both answers at once. The door down led to Veyra, to the final sigil, to the choice that would remake the world. The stair up, if any way up remained, led to a life: Ilsa restored someday, maybe, if the world survived to allow it. Two roads. One sister. One world.

And only one pair of feet to walk either.

* * *

They went down, because the way up was closed, and because Ilsa remembered doors.

The ledger-hall was vast and dim and full of the sound of breathing — that was the horror of it, the thing Anselm had never gotten used to in his years of investigation, the way the broker's repositories *breathed*, souls packed in soulstuff humming at the edge of hearing, a sound like a congregation murmuring through a wall. Shelves rose into darkness. On them, in vessels of glass and lead and carved bone, the traded dead waited out eternity at market rates.

Ilsa moved through it like a clerk through a familiar office, touching shelf-edges, counting turns. "Left here. The broker's door is behind the master ledger — there's a mechanism, a sigil-lock, he trusted no one but himself, but—"

"But you've seen him open it," Anselm said.

"Four hundred times." She paused at a shelf, head tilting. "There's a vessel here that's singing. Do you hear it? Most of them hum. This one sings."

Anselm listened. And felt the bottom drop out of his thirty years.

Because he knew the song. He had transcribed it, note by note, from a dying woman in a border shrine fifteen years ago — a hymn to Ondrelis, the Tide-Mother, drowned-goddess of the western coasts, whose cult had gone silent within a single season and whose absence the synod had explained away with comfortable lies about migration and mystery. He had believed, all these years, that Ondrelis was caged with the others beneath Thornfell.

But this was not Thornfell. This was the broker's deep repository, miles distant, and on a shelf in the dark a goddess's captured voice sang a hymn Anselm alone in the world could name.

"She's not all there," he whispered. "Ondrelis. Malachar keeps pieces of them *outside* the cage. Distributed. Hidden. That's how he holds the boundaries — not from one place, but from everywhere, so no single breaking—" He stopped. Looked at Lena. "The cage isn't a prison. It's a *vault*. And vaults have branches."

Lena wasn't listening. Lena was staring at the singing vessel with an expression Anselm recognized from mirrors: the look of a person doing arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

"If pieces of the gods are outside the cage," she said slowly, "then breaking the cage doesn't free them. Not all the way. He keeps anchors." Her jaw tightened. "Then we take the anchors."

"That's not the mission," Anselm said. "The mission is the cage. The sigil. You—"

"The mission changed when I found out my sister's soul was *currency*." Lena's voice came out low and level and more frightening than shouting. "Everything in this room is a piece of someone. Every vessel. And he's using them as ballast, as anchors, as *leverage* against the gods themselves — break the cage and he still holds enough of their voices to choke the song. So we don't break the cage yet. We break the *ledger* first."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the fighting reached the upper galleries. Anselm heard the particular crash of a barricade failing, and beneath it, faint and clear, a woman's voice raised not in fear but in *negotiation* — Sera, buying minutes with words, spending herself at courtier's rates.

Ilsa touched the singing vessel, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "If we carry them," she said, "the soldiers will follow the sound. Souls sing louder when frightened."

"Then we sing louder," said Lena.

* * *

It was in the dark of the broker's private passage, with four stolen god-shards wrapped in Anselm's cloak and humming against his chest like a heart that wasn't his, that Anselm finally said the thing he had been carrying since the ledge above the falls.

"Lena. Stop."

She stopped. Ahead, Ilsa's lantern bobbed in the black. Behind, the sounds of the court's fall grew closer. Between, in the narrow dark, Anselm caught Lena's arm and made himself say it.

"Flee. Take Ilsa and go."

She turned. Even in the dark he could see her go still, the stillness of a drawn blade.

"There's a way out through the broker's door — Ilsa knows it, she remembers doors. You take the shards, you take your sister, you go east to the coast, and you let the world fend for itself." His voice was steady. Thirty years of practice at sounding steady. "Listen to me. You are one woman. The sigil in your blood may be nothing — a story Veyra told a dying priestess, a hope, a *maybe*. But Ilsa is certain. Ilsa is here, Ilsa is warm, Ilsa is your sister, and every hour you spend chasing a maybe is an hour the maybe costs her. That's the bargain in front of you. A certainty against a possibility. Love against duty. And I am telling you, as a man who chose duty over love thirty years ago and has regretted it every single day since—"

"You're telling me to choose love."

"I'm telling you to choose *her*. Yes." He exhaled. "Because I have watched you look at her, and I have watched you look at the depths, and I know which one you'd die for without hesitation and which one you're only willing to die for because a god hid a secret in your blood without asking. That's not a calling, Lena. That's an inheritance you never consented to. You can refuse an inheritance."

For a long moment there was only the humming of the shards and, far above, the fading cadence of Sera's voice — still talking, still stalling, still spending.

"And what happens to the world?" Lena asked quietly. "If I take my certainty and run?"

"The world has survived worse."

"No." She said it simply, without heat. "It hasn't. That's the point. Everything the world *is* — the borders, the tides, the seasons, the shape of every coastline — the gods sing those into being. Malachar doesn't want to destroy that. He wants to *edit* it. And I've seen what he edits." She touched the cloak-wrapped shards. "Every stranger whose prayer gets rewritten. Every village that ends up on the wrong side of a new border he draws for his own convenience. Every soul in rooms like this one. You're asking me to save one person from the fire and walk past the burning city, and you know what's in the city, Anselm. You've spent thirty years telling anyone who'd listen what's in the city. They defrocked you for it."

"Yes," Anselm said. "They did."

"So don't ask me to make your old mistake in reverse." Her hand found his shoulder in the dark — brief, hard, almost gentle. "You chose duty and lost love. I'm not choosing between them. I'm telling you they were never separate. Ilsa's soul is in his *ledger*, Anselm. Her freedom is in the same act as theirs. There is no version of this where I save her and abandon everyone else, because everyone else is *shelved in the same room.*"

Behind them, up the passage, a door burst. Torchlight. Boots. And a voice like cold oil pouring over the stones:

"There you are."

Malachar had come down to collect his anchors personally.

* * *

What happened next, Anselm would remember forever in fragments, because some moments are too large for sequence.

Lena shoving the shard-bundle into his arms. Ilsa stepping in front of her sister — *sister*, though she didn't know the word's weight, acting it anyway, some groove deeper than memory deciding for her — knife out, stance perfect, a body trained by six years of the court's quiet wars. Malachar walking forward unhurried, his borrowed voice filling the passage like floodwater, offering — always offering — command, wealth, Ilsa whole and restored, everything, everything, if Lena would only kneel and surrender the map and the blood and the maybe.

And Anselm, backing toward the broker's door with an armful of singing godlight, understanding suddenly what he had to do.

He had spent thirty years believing the gods needed a priest. Standing in the dark with four captive voices humming against his ribs, he finally understood they needed something else. A witness. A mouth. Someone to stand in a doorway and *pray loudly* — as a courtier once asked, to annoy a tyrant — and mean every word.

So as Lena drew steel, as Ilsa set her feet, as Malachar smiled his patient, terrible smile, Anselm Thornbury — defrocked, disgraced, unforgiven — planted himself in the broker's doorway, unwrapped the shards, and began the old litany of the Tide-Mother at the top of his voice.

The passage filled with water that had no source.

The singing vessel answered him. Then the others. Then, from far below, from the depths of the cage itself, something vast turned over in its sleep and *listened* — and Anselm felt, rising through the stone, through his boots, through the collar he had never taken off, a silence that was not empty.

A silence with a name in it.

Veyra, waking, reaching for the final sigil in Lena Villalobos's blood — and Lena, meeting Malachar's eyes across the rising water, beginning to breathe in the rhythm of a song older than mountains.

"Hold the door, Anselm," she said.

He held the door.
