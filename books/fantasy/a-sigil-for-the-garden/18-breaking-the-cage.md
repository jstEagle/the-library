## 18. Breaking the Cage

The mountain shook first.

Not the deep groan of settling stone that smugglers learn to ignore, but a convulsion — a full-body shudder that sent dust cascading from the inverted court's ceiling and made the godlight gutter like candles in a storm. Somewhere far above, iron boots were finding the stairs. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Lena felt them through her soles the way she'd once felt hoofbeats across open ground: as arithmetic. Distance divided by time equals not enough.

"Tell me you have it," Sera said. She had a knife out, though against what was coming a knife was a prayer with an edge. Her courtier's calm had cracked hours ago, when Malachar's heralds had begun their march, and now she stood in the wreckage of the broker's gallery with soot on her cheek and her voice pitched low and fast. "Lena. Tell me you have the sigil."

"I have it." Lena's hand pressed flat against her own sternum, where Veyra had shown her the mark lived. Not inked on skin. Not carved in stone. *In* her — woven through her blood the way a song is woven through a throat. "I've always had it."

Anselm stood at the edge of the cage-dais, his defrocked vestments hanging from him like the shed skin of another man. He was weeping without seeming to notice, tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face, his lips moving through the old prayers he'd taught her on the road — the ones for dawn, for safe passage, for the naming of children. Prayers no one had been able to say aloud in ten years because there had been no one left to hear them.

"Lena." His voice broke on her name. He cleared his throat and tried again. "When you sing it — the gods will come back into themselves. Everything they are. Everything they've been holding back." He met her eyes. "And everything Malachar has done with their stolen voices will come due."

"I know."

"The mountain—"

"I know, Anselm."

He nodded slowly. Then, because he was who he was, he knelt. Not to her — she saw that clearly, and was grateful for it. He knelt facing the cage, facing the dim vast shapes coiled within it, and he began the oldest prayer of all, the one he'd only ever whispered: *We did not forget you. We could not reach you. We are here.*

Above them, the doors of the great stair burst open.

* * *

They came down into the court like water finding a breach — Malachar's soldiers in their grey-lacquered armor, filling gallery after gallery, their faces lit from below by the captive godlight. And Lena heard it now, what Sera had tried to describe on the Salt Road, what Anselm had only theorized: the soldiers didn't shout as they advanced. They *hummed*. A single sustained tone rolling off thousands of throats in perfect unison, and underneath it, threaded through it, a voice that was not human at all.

A dead god's voice, worn like a mantle. Worn by every one of them at once.

The sound crawled up Lena's spine and settled between her shoulder blades. She knew that timbre. Every child raised near the old shrines knew it, even if they couldn't name it — the warmth of it, the way it seemed to promise shelter. It was the voice of a harvest god whose name had been struck from the calendars, sung now from the mouths of men who burned temples.

"They're here," Sera said unnecessarily.

The first ranks reached the foot of the dais stairs and stopped, as if the humming itself were enough — as if they'd come not to fight but to witness. Behind them, borne on a palanquin of black iron, something moved that might once have been a man. Malachar did not descend from it. He simply stood, and the dead god's voice poured out of his mouth alone now, amplified until the galleries rang with it.

"*Daughter of Veyra.*"

Lena stepped forward. The sigil in her chest answered the sound of its own name — heat blooming under her ribs, spreading outward along veins that suddenly felt like aqueducts.

"You have two choices," Malachar said, courteous and terrible, exactly as he had been in his court. "Give me what you carry, and rule beside me. The old gods rewrote nothing; they only maintained. I mean to *improve*. No more droughts. No more plagues. No more prayers going unanswered while farmers bury their children." The voice swelled, and she understood it was meant to be seductive, and hated that part of her — the part that remembered unanswered prayers, that remembered rain-slicked foothills and silence where comfort should have been — leaned toward it anyway. "Or keep it, and die here in the dark, and I will take it from your corpse."

"You missed a choice," Lena called back.

The humming faltered, almost imperceptibly.

"I can give it away," she said. "To them."

* * *

Veyra met her at the cage's inner wall.

The silent god was diminished past bearing — a shape that should have filled valleys folded down into something barely larger than a woman, her edges translucent, her presence less a light than the memory of one. Ten years of silence. Ten years of being harvested note by note, and still Veyra had spent what remained of herself on a single act: pressing her awareness through stone and soil to find the daughter she'd marked at birth.

*You came,* Veyra did not say. She had no voice left to say it with. But the meaning pressed against Lena like hands on either side of her face, and it was so much like her mother's touch that Lena's breath caught.

"I need you to do something for me," Lena said. Her voice held. She was proud of that, later. "When I break this — when they all come flooding back — hold the line. Hold *me*. Don't let them take more than I'm giving. Whatever they demand." She swallowed. "Whatever the price turns out to be."

The silent god's regard moved over her, slow as weather. And then — the closest thing to speech she had left — Veyra inclined her head. A bargain older than language, sealed the way bargains were sealed before brokers and ledgers existed: eye to eye, will to will.

Behind them, the first soldiers started up the stairs.

Anselm's prayer rose behind her, cracking but unbroken. Sera put her back to Lena's, knife out, and said something wry that Lena would never afterward be able to recall. The dead god's voice thundered through Malachar's throat, commanding her to stop, commanding the world to stop, and the mountain convulsed again, harder, dust raining gold through the godlight—

Lena opened her mouth and sang the final sigil.

* * *

It was not music. It was the thing music is made *from*.

The sound left her chest before her mind caught up — a syllable that had never been written down because paper burns, a name that predated names, and the moment it entered the air every piece of the court recognized it. The godlight flared white. The galleries rang like struck bells. The sigil-map in her pack ignited into ash, its work finished, and the mark beneath her ribs blazed so brightly she could see its light through her own skin, through her armor, through her closed eyelids.

She sang it again, louder, and the cage heard.

The first crack sounded like the world clearing its throat. The second like grief. The third was not a sound at all but a *removal* — a silence tearing open inside the silence Veyra had kept for ten years, and through that torn place, the old gods poured back into themselves.

Lena felt it happen the way a river must feel ice breaking. Power that had been siphoned drop by drop into armies and wards and the usurper's borrowed voice came home all at once, and the cage — built to hold whispers — shattered trying to hold the whole song.

The mountain screamed.

Stone split overhead in veins of light. The inverted court tilted, then righted itself, then tilted again as the bedrock beneath Thornfell remembered what it meant to be alive. Galleries collapsed inward. The black-iron palanquin toppled, and Malachar fell with it, and the dead god's voice ripped out of his throat mid-command — torn away like a bandage, like a theft reversed — leaving behind, for one naked instant, just a man's scream. Human-sized. Almost pitiable. Then the crowd of his own soldiers swallowed him, and Lena stopped looking, because the gods were rising and looking anywhere else felt like blasphemy.

They came up out of the broken cage in shapes too large for the space, and the space made room. The harvest god whose stolen voice had marched armies unfurled like wheat in wind. River-shapes and root-shapes, the grey lady of crossroads, things with antlers and things with wings, presences that had been caged so long they'd nearly forgotten their own outlines — all of it pouring upward through splitting stone, seeking sky.

And the voices went with them.

That was the part that undid her. Every freed god sang as it rose — its true voice, restored, ringing off the mountain's bones — and the song passed up through the rock, out through the peaks, into the world above. Lena knew without seeing that somewhere on the surface, in temple ruins and roadside shrines and the hearts of everyone who had ever prayed into silence, people were hearing answers for the first time in ten years. Knew that Malachar's armies, above ground and below, were stumbling mid-march as the borrowed voice drained out of them like marrow from bone.

Veyra rose last.

She paused beside Lena — a pause that contained a decade of silence and a birth-mark and a debt neither of them would ever finish counting — and then she too went up, unfolding toward the fractured ceiling, growing as she climbed, until her passing opened the mountain's crown like a bloom and starlight poured in where godlight had been.

The court was coming down around them. Sera hauled at Lena's arm, shouting something about the smuggler roads. Anselm stood in the ruin of the dais with his arms spread wide, laughing or weeping or both, refusing to run, and Sera swore and grabbed him too, and the three of them fled through collapsing galleries as the world's oldest prisoners went home singing.

Behind them, the last of the god-cage crumbled into glittering dust.

Ahead, the stairways up were gone — sealed by the convulsions, drowned in rubble. There was no way out. There was only the deep dark of the mountain's heart, and the trembling ground, and Sera's grip on her arm, and Anselm's voice saying, gently, urgently:

"Lena. They're not done with you yet."

She turned. In the settling dust, the freed gods' attention had gathered — not hostile, not kind, simply *present*, the way gravity is present. The final sigil still glowed beneath her ribs. Still hers.

Still owed.

The largest of the shapes bent toward her, and a voice like the turning of seasons filled the ruined court:

"*Balance, bearer. The song must be paid from its singer.*"

Lena stood in the falling dark, hand pressed to the light in her chest, and understood what was coming for her before the words finished forming — and found, to her horror, that she already knew which memory the ledger would reach for first.
