## 19. The Price of the Song

The mountain was still screaming.

Not the stone — the stone had gone quiet the moment the cage shattered, settling into its new shape like a man exhaling after holding his breath for a hundred years. It was the gods who screamed, all of them at once, their voices pouring back into bodies that had been starved of speech for so long that sound itself seemed to hurt them. The inverted court blazed with it. Godlight ran along the walls like water finding its old channels, and every corridor Lena had walked in terror now rang like a struck bell.

She stood in the ruins of the cage with Ilsa's hand in hers and did not know how much longer she would be allowed to keep it there.

The sigil was still burning.

It lived under her skin — had always lived under her skin, Veyra said, placed there at her birth like a coin sewn into a beggar's coat — and now that she had sung it aloud, now that it had done its work, it was not content to go quiet again. It pulsed. It *wanted*. She could feel it pressing against the inside of her ribs, looking for another boundary to redraw, another law of the world to unmake, and the freed gods were circling her like moths around a lamp they both loved and feared.

*Balance,* they said. All of them, in one voice, though each voice kept its own timbre. The river-god's voice was cold and many-throated. The hearth-mother's was smoke and banked coals. And beneath them, faintest of all, the silence that was Veyra — not silent anymore, never silent again, but speaking the way snow speaks, in pressure and weight rather than words.

*What was given must be paid from what remains.*

"I don't understand," Lena said. Her throat was raw from singing. Blood had run from her nose during the breaking; she could taste it still, copper and old iron, the taste of the mountain's deep roads. "I gave you the song. It's done. The cage is broken."

*The song was the key,* said the hearth-mother gently, and gentleness from something that vast was somehow worse than anger. *But keys are not free. Your usurper paid in stolen voices. You must pay in kind — from yourself.*

Lena looked at Ilsa.

Her sister stood beside her in the wreckage of the gallery where she had served as ornament and currency for ten years. The hollowness was gone from her eyes. That was the miracle of it — the broker's ledger burned when the cage broke, every debt written in it cancelled or called due, and Ilsa's soul had come home to her like a bird released from a snare. Color sat in her face again. Memory sat behind her eyes again. Lena could see it there: their mother's kitchen, the smell of rain on the salt flats, the game they used to play counting gulls off the breakwater. Ten lost years, returned whole.

Ilsa was looking at her with an expression Lena had not seen since they were children — that fierce, unguarded love that sisters carry before the world teaches them to armor it.

"Whatever they're asking," Ilsa said, "the answer is no."

"You don't even know what—"

"I know you." Ilsa's grip tightened on her hand. "You've had that look since you were six years old. The look you get right before you decide to bleed so nobody else has to."

Lena laughed. It came out broken. "You remember that?"

"I remember everything." Ilsa turned to the gods, and though they filled the ruined court like weather fills a valley, she did not flinch. "Take it from me instead. Whatever the price is. I've already been spent once — I know how it's done."

*No,* said the river-god, and its voice moved through the flood channels of Lena's bones. *The bearer pays. The sigil lives in her blood. There is no other ledger.*

"What price?" Lena asked. "Tell me plainly. I'm a mercenary. I can haggle with anything except silence."

The voices conferred — she felt it rather than heard it, a pressure like a change in the wind. Then Veyra spoke alone, and for the first time in ten years, in Lena's whole life, her god used words.

*A memory of equal weight. The sigil's power is great enough to unmake boundaries. Its price must be something equally boundless.*

"Name it," Lena said. "My sword arm. My sight. My—"

*Not flesh.* A pause, and in the pause Lena understood, and began, quietly, to shake. *Memories are the only currency that weighs the same on both sides. You have already traded one this day. Your mother's face bought your sister's location. The remainder of the debt is larger.*

"How much larger?"

Veyra did not answer with words. She answered by showing her.

And Lena saw it laid out before her like a contract in the broker's ledger: *every memory of Ilsa.* Not one. Not the worst ones, or the easiest to spare. Every one. The gulls off the breakwater. The fever when Ilsa was nine and Lena had sat up three nights feeding her broth. The bargain itself — the broker's smoky den, the terrible arithmetic of her sister's soul weighed against her own death-debt. The guilt. The grief. The decade of carrying it. All of it, every hour, every wound, every tenderness, lifted out of her like a splinter drawn whole from the flesh.

She would not remember she had a sister.

She would look at Ilsa's face and see a stranger.

"Oh," Lena said. Very small, very steady. The way she sounded when she took a wound she knew was mortal.

"No," Ilsa said, louder. "No. Find another way. Anselm — *Anselm!*"

He came limping through the shattered gate, Sera half-carrying him, both of them grey with mountain dust and the aftermath of Malachar's storming vanguard — the crash of battle still echoed somewhere above, distant now, the freed gods' armies of weather and stone turning the usurper's legions back toward the light. Anselm took one look at Lena's face and stopped as if he'd hit glass.

"The price," he said. Not a question. He had served the broker once; he knew how ledgers read.

"There's no other way?" His voice cracked on it. "Great gods, there must be—"

*There is always another way,* said the hearth-mother, *and it is always worse. Refuse, and the sigil stays live in her blood until some hungrier thing finds it. Take it by force, as Malachar tried, and we unmake the bearer entirely. This is mercy, priest, though it will not feel like it tonight.*

Sera said nothing. She stood with her arms folded, watching Lena with those courtier's eyes that missed nothing, and when Lena glanced at her, Sera inclined her head — the smallest bow. *Your choice,* the gesture said. *Yours alone.* It was the most honest kindness anyone had offered her in days.

Lena looked at Ilsa.

That was the mistake. That was the gift. She let herself look — really look, greedily, hoarding — at the chipped front tooth Ilsa had gotten falling off the seawall. At the scar on her knuckle from the knife fight behind the fish market that neither of them had ever told their mother about. At the way she stood with her weight on her left foot, always, even now, even after ten years in a cage, because habits of the body outlast habits of the soul.

Ten years of searching compressed into thirty seconds of looking.

"I was going to find you," Lena said. "However long it took. I want you to know that. Wherever I end up after this — whatever's left of me — some part of me spent ten years walking toward you. So if I look at you like a stranger tomorrow, it's a lie. Remember that. You're the one who gets to keep the truth."

"Don't do this." Ilsa was crying now, ugly and furious, the way she'd cried at nine with the fever. "There are seven gods standing in front of us. Seven. And not one of them can find a cheaper price than—"

"It's not cheap," Lena said. "It's the most expensive thing in the world. That's why it works." She turned to the gathered dark and light of them. "I'll pay it."

*Lena.* Veyra's voice, snow-soft, and underneath it something that might have been apology — the first sound her god had ever made that sounded like grief. *Know what you are owed. When the balance is struck, a debt remains on our side of the ledger. We do not forget our creditors.*

"Put it in writing," Lena whispered, and almost smiled.

* * *

They did it in the heart of the broken cage, because that was where the sigil lived and where the accounting had to be done.

The freed gods formed a circle around her — or the idea of a circle; their bodies were weather and current and ember, and the shapes they wore were courtesies for human eyes. Ilsa refused to leave. She stood at the circle's edge with her fists clenched, daring any god to make her go, and none of them tried. Anselm knelt and prayed, actually prayed, for the first time since his defrocking, and his prayer had no words in it at all, just her name, over and over, like a bell.

"Will it hurt?" Lena asked.

*Everything true does,* said Veyra.

"Good. Then it'll feel honest." She rolled her shoulders the way she did before a fight, an old habit, muscle memory that would survive even when everything else went. "How do we—"

Ilsa kissed her.

Quick, fierce, on the cheek, the way she'd done every morning of their childhood, *for luck*, she'd always said, *so nothing bad finds you before breakfast.* Her lips were warm. Her breath caught. And then she pulled back and held Lena's face in both hands and said, clearly, so that it would be the last thing carved deepest:

"My name is Ilsa Villalobos. You're my sister. When you forget, I'll tell you again. As many times as it takes. That's not a threat, it's a promise."

Then she stepped back, and the gods closed in, and the sigil opened its ledger.

It didn't feel like being robbed. That was the terrible part. It felt like being *unburdened* — each memory lifting away with a softness that was almost kind, and Lena felt herself reach for them and understood, dimly, that reaching was exactly what made them real enough to take. The gulls went first, wheeling off the breakwater, white wings against a grey sea. Then the fever, the broth, the three sleepless nights. Then the fish-market knife, the seawall, their mother's kitchen — no, wait, the mother's face was already gone, sold hours ago, she'd forgotten she'd forgotten—

The broker's den. Smoke and arithmetic. The weight of her own death-debt in one pan and Ilsa's soul in the other, and her hand pushing the pan down, down, down.

Grief came up like floodwater and was taken too.

She fought it at the end. She couldn't help it. Some animal part of her sank hooks into the last memories and held — Ilsa's laugh, Ilsa's hand, Ilsa saying *you're my sister* thirty seconds ago — and the gods waited, patient as stone, letting her hold on until holding on hurt more than letting go.

*I'm sorry,* Veyra said, and meant it, and took them anyway.

* * *

The silence afterward was enormous.

Lena stood in the middle of the broken cage, swaying, empty-handed, feeling the strange lightness of a debt paid — and knowing, in some cellar of herself below the reach of memory, that something enormous had been carried out of the house. She knew the *shape* of the absence. She just couldn't see what had made it.

Blood dripped from her nose onto the floor. She wiped it with the back of her hand and looked up.

A woman was running toward her through the godlight, and Lena's body reacted before her mind could — weight shifting, hand moving toward a blade that wasn't there, the mercenary's arithmetic of distance and threat. Then the woman stopped, close enough to touch, and looked at her with an expression of such naked devastation that the arithmetic failed entirely.

"Hi," the woman said. Her voice shook. "I'm Ilsa."

Lena blinked. "I— hello." She searched herself for recognition and found a smooth blank wall where something should have been, and the blankness frightened her more than the cage ever had. "Do I... know you?"

Something moved across the woman's face — grief, huge grief, swallowed whole. But underneath it, rising through it like a green shoot through rubble, was something else. Stubbornness. Lena didn't know why she recognized it. She just did.

"You knew me," Ilsa said. "A minute ago, you knew me better than anyone alive. And you gave that up so the world wouldn't be rewritten by a man who wears a dead god's voice." She laughed wetly, dashed her eyes with her sleeve. "So. No, you don't know me. But you're going to."

"I am?"

"That's the promise." Ilsa held out her hand — the same gesture, Lena understood without understanding, that she'd been making since they were children. "We start here. My name is Ilsa Villalobos. What's yours?"

Behind her, the freed gods were withdrawing into the walls of the world, their voices spreading outward through stone and sky and river, going home to every temple and wellspring and thunderhead they'd been barred from. Somewhere far above, Malachar's borrowed voice was failing mid-command. Somewhere beyond that, the free cities were beginning to rise.

Lena looked at the offered hand. Looked at the woman attached to it, who was crying and smiling at the same time and refusing, absolutely refusing, to let either one win.

"Lena," she heard herself say. "Lena Villalobos."

The woman's breath caught — hearing her own name in her sister's mouth, the first time, the first time of however many times it would take.

"Well," Ilsa said, gripping her hand like a drowning woman grips a rope, like a rescuer grips one, both at once. "I guess we'd better find somewhere to sit. Because I have about ten years of stories to tell you, sister, and I'm not skipping a single one."

Above them, through the shattered roof of the mountain, dawn was coming down.
