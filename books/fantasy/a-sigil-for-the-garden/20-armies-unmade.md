## 20. Armies Unmade

Sera had spent eleven years learning to read a court by its silences — which minister's laughter stopped a half-beat too soon, which general's toast carried a threat folded inside it. She knew silence as a weapon. What she saw from the ridge above the Salt Road that morning was silence as a wound.

The column of Malachar's army stretched along the valley floor for two miles. Ten thousand men in black-lacquered plate, marching with the terrible precision she remembered from parades beneath Thornfell: every bootfall the same length, every spearhead catching the sun at the same angle. They did not talk as they marched. They never talked. The Voice spoke through their officers, and the men below the officers simply moved.

She had watched that column swallow three towns on its way west toward Corvale. She had stood at a window in the usurper's court and listened to Malachar rehearse his borrowed god-voice until it rang true, and she had thought: *nothing made of this can ever be broken.* That was the whole architecture of his power. Not the soldiers — the voice above them, singing boundaries into flesh.

So when the column stopped mid-stride, all ten thousand of them, in the same instant, like a puppeteer dropping his strings —

Sera's hands went to the rope at her wrists and began working it before her mind caught up to what her eyes were seeing.

* * *

She was bound because capture had been part of the arithmetic. When Anselm urged flight and Lena refused it, when the shouting started down in the court of the inverted mountain, Sera had done the only useful thing left to her: she'd stepped backward into the path of the first guard and let herself be taken, buying them thirty seconds with the one currency no one suspects a turncoat of carrying — her own body, freely spent.

It should have been a longer game than this. She'd planned to be dragged up out of the mountain, marched with the army, and escape somewhere south where the roads forked. Instead the quake hit while they were still hauling her up the smuggler stairs, and the world outside the gate had come apart like wet paper.

Now she knelt among the baggage train at the rear of the column, wrists raw, flanked by two guards who hadn't spoken since the mountain screamed. The whole army felt it — she could see it in their backs. Something had gone wrong under Thornfell, and every man jack of them was waiting for the Voice to tell them what.

The morning was cold and clear. Frost still silvered the gorse on the ridgelines. Somewhere behind the eastern hills, Corvale waited with its militia and its reopened temples and its prayers that suddenly worked again, and Sera thought: *they don't know yet. Nobody down there knows what Lena just did.*

Then the officers began to fall.

Not shot. Not cut. The nearest one — Captain Dreven, who'd personally bound Sera's wrists and joked about what the usurper paid for turncoats' tongues — simply stopped walking, turned in place with the smooth mechanical grace she'd seen a hundred times in the throne room, opened his mouth, and produced nothing.

He tried again. His jaw worked. His throat strained. What came out was a sound like wind through a broken flute, thin and wrong, and then nothing at all, and then Dreven's face changed — changed the way a man's face changes when he wakes from sleepwalking and finds himself standing over a cliff. Horror. Confusion. The sudden, animal knowledge of how far he'd walked without knowing.

Down the column, officer after officer stopped and opened their mouths and found them empty.

Sera started laughing. It came out of her cracked and ugly, half sob, but she couldn't stop it. "There it is," she whispered. "There it is, you beautiful stubborn mercenary."

The Voice was gone. Malachar's stolen god had been pulled out of his throat like a splinter, and everything built on top of that theft — the obedience, the precision, the unbreakable certainty — was collapsing from the top down.

For perhaps a minute, the valley held its breath.

Then the free cities' horns sounded from the western ridge, and the world remembered how to make noise.

* * *

They came down off the hills in loose crescents, the way militia always does — badly, bravely, in clumps — but Sera, watching from her knees in the baggage train, saw immediately that someone competent was shaping them. The crescents weren't random. They were converging on the column's joints: the baggage, the flankers, the gap between the third and fourth regiments where discipline would fail first.

And leading the center wedge, riding a stolen cavalry horse with a militia banner tied to her lance like an insult, was a broad-shouldered woman in a scarred mail shirt, bellowing orders in a voice that carried the length of the valley.

Sera knew that voice. Everyone in Malachar's court had learned to know that voice — Captain Ysolde Brann of the Grey Company, the mercenary outfit whose contract the usurper had bought two years ago, whose captain had spent those two years being paraded at feasts as proof that even free-company steel could be purchased.

Ysolde Brann had just turned her coat in front of ten thousand witnesses.

"She'll hang for this," said one of Sera's guards, but he said it the way men say things they don't believe. His spear was pointing at the ground. Beside him, the other guard hadn't even picked his up off the wagon bed.

"They'll write songs instead," Sera said. "Bad ones. She'd hate that more."

The wedge hit the column's flank and the column — the invincible, clockwork column — broke.

It didn't break everywhere at once. That was the terrible, magnificent thing about watching a machine die. Some regiments fought on out of habit, wheeling and forming with a precision that now looked obscene, puppets still twitching after the hand withdrew. Others simply dropped their spears and stood there, blinking, ten thousand men waking up mid-march to discover they'd been asleep for years. One entire regiment near the rear sat down in the frost all together, in rows, like children told to wait.

And the officers — the hollowed-out officers, the men whose wills had been poured out to make room for the Voice — some of them wept. Sera watched a colonel tear his own gorget off and hurl it into the river, screaming soundlessly, and she felt something complicated move in her chest that she decided not to examine until later.

Later. Everything later. Right now the ropes needed to come off.

* * *

The guard on her left was the problem; the guard on her right was already gone, drifted six steps toward the fighting to watch Ysolde's wedge carve into the baggage. Sera got her bound hands under the left guard's elbow as he turned back, hooked it, and put him over her hip into the frozen mud with all eleven years of court survival and exactly none of soldier training behind it. He landed badly, winded. She took his knife, sawed through her own wrist bindings, and considered him.

"Stay down," she advised. "Today of all days, stay down."

He stayed down.

The baggage train was coming apart around her — teamsters cutting horses loose, militiamen swarming wagons, a cook-fire cart overturned and burning bright against the frost. Sera ran low between the wagons, away from the melee, toward the tree line on the eastern slope, because eleven years of survival had taught her one law above all others: *when the structure collapses, do not be found standing inside it.*

She was forty yards from the trees when she heard her name.

"Sera Vane!"

She turned. Ysolde Brann had fought her way most of the way through the baggage train and sat her horse at its edge, bloodied, grinning like the end of the world suited her. Two Grey Company veterans flanked her — Sera recognized the Vell twins, or half-recognized; everyone looked different out of court livery.

"You're supposed to be dead," Ysolde called down. "Or captured. Or something expensive."

"I'm all three, depending on the ledger." Sera walked toward her slowly, hands visible, empty. "You turned your coat."

"Someone finally gave me a reason worth the price." Ysolde swung down from the horse. Up close she looked like a woman who hadn't slept in a week, and Sera understood why without asking. The kill order. The hunt. Lena Villalobos, carrying a map the whole world wanted, with her old captain sent to bring her back dead or worse. "Your friend. The quiet one with the knives. Where is she?"

"Under Thornfell," Sera said. "Doing what nobody else could." She let that land, watched it land, watched Ysolde's grin fade into something rawer. "The army you're currently dismantling was powered by a stolen god. Your former employer wore its voice. As of roughly an hour ago, he doesn't anymore."

"And the girl?"

"Still under the mountain. With her sister." Sera hesitated, then spent the truth like coin, because today of all days it was the only currency that mattered. "What it cost her, I don't know yet. But I intend to find out."

Ysolde studied her for a long moment. Behind her, the battle was becoming a rout, then becoming something less than a rout — becoming a dispersal, ten thousand men walking away from a war in every direction at once. A regiment's worth of soldiers sat in the river shallows washing black lacquer off their armor, quietly, like penitents.

"The free cities are going to want to debrief you," Ysolde said at last. "Corvale especially. You were inside the court. You've seen the cage, if it exists."

"It exists."

"Then you're either the most valuable prisoner in the valley or the most valuable ally." The captain's mouth twisted. "Knowing you, probably both."

"That," said Sera, "is the first accurate thing anyone's said to me all day."

* * *

By late afternoon the Salt Road belonged to no one and everyone. Militia columns moved along it in both directions — some advancing to secure the passes, some escorting long lines of disarmed imperial soldiers south under white rags, and here and there small knots of freed officers wandering like ghosts, touching walls, touching each other's shoulders, checking with their hands that the world was still solid.

Sera stood at the crest of the ridge with Ysolde's spare cloak around her shoulders and watched the smoke rise off the burned baggage train, and permitted herself, for exactly sixty seconds, to feel it.

Triumph. But secondhand, and she knew it. She hadn't broken anything. She had navigated, negotiated, traded truths at the right moments and kept her skin intact — that was her craft, and she was not ashamed of it, but the actual breaking had been done underground by a mercenary who prayed to a silent god and a defrocked priest who wouldn't stop talking and a sister who didn't remember her own name. Sera had witnessed the fall of Malachar the way one witnesses weather. From a safe distance, admiring the lightning.

*We won,* she thought, testing the shape of it. *The old gods are loose, the usurper is mute, the free cities are rising, and I am alive.*

It should have tasted sweeter. Instead it tasted like a door left open somewhere behind her.

Because she had been in the throne room. She had heard Malachar speak with the borrowed voice, and she knew something the celebrating militias did not: the voice was gone from his throat, yes. But Malachar himself had been in the court when the mountain convulsed. And men like Malachar did not die in earthquakes. Men like Malachar crawled out of them.

Below the ridge, a rider came up the Salt Road at a gallop, militia colors streaming, and reined in hard beside Ysolde. Even at fifty paces Sera could read bad news in the set of the rider's shoulders — news delivered at a gallop is almost always bad news.

She drifted closer. Eleven years of court survival; eavesdropping was a sacrament.

"—scouts from the eastern passes," the rider was saying, breathless. "The mountain's gate is sealed — fused shut, stone melted like glass. But there's people coming down the pilgrim road. Three of them. Slow. One's being carried."

Ysolde went very still. "Carried how? Wounded?"

"No, captain. That's the thing." The rider swallowed. "The one walking in front — dark hair, swords — she keeps stopping. Stares at the other two like she doesn't know them. And the tall one, the priest, says—" the rider faltered, clearly repeating words he didn't understand, "—he says she paid the toll. Says she bought the gods' freedom fair and square, and the ledger took its fee, and she doesn't remember what she paid with."

The wind moved across the ridge. Somewhere down the valley, a freed soldier was singing — tunelessly, alone, the first song any of them had sung in years.

Sera closed her eyes. She saw it instantly, completely, the way she saw all ledgers: the balance struck both ways, the memory of Ilsa pouring out of Lena like light through a cracked jar, spent to the last drop so the cage would break. She had watched Lena fight for that memory across half a mountain. She had watched her find her sister and lose her twice in one night.

"Well," Sera said, opening her eyes and turning toward the pilgrim road, toward the three slow figures descending out of the smoking east. "Somebody has to introduce themselves."

She started down the hill, and Ysolde, after a moment, followed.
