## 21. Two Strangers on a Road

Lena woke to the smell of bread and the sound of someone humming.

She lay still for a long moment, cataloguing. Whitewashed ceiling, water-stained in one corner. A window with shutters half-open, letting in gray morning light and the smell of rain on stone. Her sword — she found it by feel before she opened her eyes fully — leaning against the wall within reach of the bed, which meant whoever had put her here either respected her or feared her, and both were fine.

Then the humming stopped, and a woman's voice said, quietly, "You're awake."

Lena turned her head. A woman sat in a chair by the window, mending a shirt that Lena recognized, after a confused beat, as her own. Brown hair going gray at the temples. Hands that had been soft once and weren't anymore. She looked at Lena the way people look at something they've been watching for hours, afraid it would stop breathing.

"I know you," Lena said. The words came out slow, careful. "I don't know how I know you."

The woman's hands stilled over the mending. "Ilsa," she said. "My name is Ilsa."

*Ilsa.* The name landed somewhere under her ribs and ached like an old break in cold weather. Lena pressed her palm flat against the blanket and made herself breathe.

"Say something else," she said.

"You snore," Ilsa said, and her mouth trembled into something that was almost a smile. "You've always snored. You used to deny it. You'd fall asleep sitting up by the fire and swear to every god in creation — well." She looked down at the shirt in her lap. "Every god but one, I suppose."

The memories were there. Lena could feel them the way you feel a wall in a dark room — solid, present, and full of holes. She knew Veyra. She knew the mountain, the cage, the singing, the terrible bright moment when she'd opened her mouth and given everything away. She knew Anselm's voice cracking on a prayer. She knew Sera Vane's laugh, sharp as a knife being drawn.

And where her sister should have been there was a shape. An outline. The idea of *someone*, worn smooth as a river stone from all the years of carrying it, but empty. She reached for a face and found nothing. Reached for a childhood and found the word *childhood* and nothing behind it.

"How much did I lose?" she asked. It wasn't really a question. She already knew. She just needed to hear the size of it said out loud.

Ilsa set down the mending. "Anselm says the gods took what the sigil cost. He says they took it clean — no rot, no fragments left bleeding into everything else. Just gone." Her voice held steady the way a rope holds steady when someone is hanging from it. "You remember the mountain. You don't remember me."

"No," Lena said. "I don't remember you."

"And yet," Ilsa said, "when I walked into that room in Corvale three days ago and told them I was your sister, your hand went to your sword hilt. Not to draw. To check it was there. Anselm says that's how you are with family — you guard first, ask second." She folded the shirt, laid it aside. "So some part of you knows. Some part of you kept me anyway."

Lena looked at her hands. Callused, scarred, one finger slightly crooked from a break she couldn't remember getting. Ten years of a life with the middle carved out.

"What do I call you?" she asked. It felt like the most important question she'd asked in her whole career, and she'd once had to name a price for a man's life while he listened.

Ilsa considered this seriously, which Lena found herself grateful for. This woman — this stranger — understood that questions like this deserved weight.

"Ilsa," she said at last. "Just Ilsa. We'll start there. Names first. The rest comes after."

* * *

Corvale had changed.

Lenna walked its streets that afternoon because lying in bed felt like drowning, and Ilsa walked beside her because Ilsa apparently intended to walk beside her for the rest of both their lives, and had decided not to make a fuss about it.

The city was loud in ways it hadn't been. Every temple that had stood shuttered for a decade stood open now, doors flung wide, incense smoke rising like questions finally answered. In the old market square a priestess of the Twin Rivers was baptizing infants in a fountain the constables had given up trying to protect, and the crowd around her sang without any leader, any book, any plan — just hundreds of voices finding the same melody because it had been waiting in them all along.

Free cities. That was what people were calling them now. The phrase had spread faster than any army could have carried it.

"The fighting's mostly done," Ilsa said, following her gaze. "Malachar's soldiers woke up one morning and the voice in their heads was gone. Half of them threw down their arms and wept. The other half just... stopped knowing why they were marching. Your old captain—"

"Maren," Lena said. The name surfaced intact, along with a scar on her forearm and the taste of bad wine on a rooftop. Some things had survived. "Maren turned coat."

"Maren turned coat," Ilsa agreed. "They say she led the charge at the Salt Road. They say she carries your sigil painted on her shield." A pause. "They say a lot of things about you."

"They say a lot of things about everyone who survives something."

A boy ran past chasing a dog, and both of them stepped aside in the same rhythm, the same direction, shoulder turning the same way. Ilsa caught Lena's eye and didn't comment. Lena filed it away: they moved alike. Whatever else had been cut out of her, her body remembered its sister.

At the corner of Tanner's Row, an old shrine stood — small, humble, a niche in the wall with a bowl for offerings and a carving so worn it might have been anyone. Someone had left fresh flowers and a candle. Lena stopped in front of it without deciding to.

Veyra.

She felt the silence where her god used to live — no, not silence, not anymore. Something quieter than sound and kinder than speech. When she thought about the mountain, about the cage, about the choice she'd made in the dark, the presence inclined toward her like a listener.

*Thank you,* it didn't say. *I'm sorry,* it didn't say. But it was there, and it was gentle, and it was hers.

"She answers you most softly of anyone," Ilsa said quietly. "The priests say that. The god who hid you. They say when you pray, it's like watching someone speak to their mother."

Lena stared at the worn carving. "Do you pray?" she asked. "To her? To any of them?"

"I'm learning to," Ilsa said. "Ten years I thought my soul belonged to a broker in a hole in the ground. Turns out it belonged to my sister, who bought it back with everything she had." She shrugged, and her eyes were wet and her voice was not. "Seems rude not to say thank you to somebody."

* * *

That evening, over bread and cheese and the last of a bottle of Corvale red, Ilsa began to tell her their childhood.

Not all at once. Not like a lesson. She did it the way you feed a fire — one piece at a time, waiting to see what caught.

"When you were six," she said, "you decided you were going to be a wolf. Not dress up as one. Be one. You ate nothing but meat for four days until Mother caught you trying to bury eggs in the yard for winter and made you sit at the table until you ate them."

Lena laughed before she could stop herself, and then the laugh caught strangely in her chest, because it was a real laugh at a real memory — but it was Ilsa's memory, borrowed, secondhand. A story about a girl she'd never been.

"It's not the same," she said. "Knowing it isn't the same as remembering it."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It isn't." She poured more wine, unhurried. "But here's what I've decided. I spent ten years with no past at all — sold, hollowed, walking around a beautiful court serving wine to monsters. And do you know what filled the space? Other people's stories. Servants whispering about home. Guards bragging about their villages. I rebuilt myself out of scraps other people gave me, and it held. It was enough to keep me human until I got free." She met Lena's eyes. "So I figure stories must be worth something. Maybe not as much as memory. But enough to build on."

"That's not fair to you," Lena said. "You shouldn't have to be the keeper of both our pasts."

"Fair," Ilsa repeated, tasting the word like wine gone off. "You gave up every memory of me to unmake an army of the dead and free eleven gods. I think the ledger between us can stand a little imbalance for a while." She leaned forward. "Besides. Here's the secret nobody tells you about sisters: you don't get to choose each other. You're just stuck. Stuck is a kind of love, if you work at it."

"I don't remember loving you."

"I know." No flinch. No wound showing. "And tomorrow morning I'll tell you about the time you broke your arm climbing the mill roof to prove you weren't afraid, and how I climbed up after you and fell too, and how Mother grounded us for a month and we dug a tunnel under the fence like prisoners. And the day after that I'll tell you another one. And another. Until you've heard enough of them that you start dreaming them, because that's what happened to me in the court — the stories sank in deeper than the hollow ever was." She smiled, tired and fierce. "I'm stubborn, little sister. Ask anyone. Ask especially you — you'll find out you always said so."

*Little sister.* Two words, freely given, from a stranger who knew her better than she knew herself. Lena looked down at her cup and found she couldn't drink past the knot in her throat.

"Why?" she managed. "Why bother? You got your soul back. You could go anywhere. Start over. Find people whose faces aren't a debt you're paying."

"Because," Ilsa said, "when they brought you off that mountain, half-dead, and they wouldn't let me in to see you — I stood outside that door and I thought: she doesn't remember me, and she never will, and I am going to love her anyway or die trying. And then I thought: actually, that's been my job my whole life. Loving you when it costs something." She reached across the table and took Lena's hand — the crooked-fingered one, the scarred one — and held it. "You paid for the world, Lena. Let me pay for you."

Outside, rain started again, soft against the shutters. Somewhere across the city a temple bell rang vespers, and beneath the bell, beneath the stone, beneath the whole wounded singing world, Lena felt the old gods stirring like sleepers turning toward dawn.

* * *

She dreamed that night, for the first time since the mountain.

In the dream she was small, and there was a garden behind a house she almost recognized, and a girl with brown hair was daring her to climb something too high. There were eggs buried in the dirt for winter. There was a mill roof against a summer sky. None of it was hers and all of it was true, and when she woke before dawn with tears on her face she couldn't explain, she lay listening to Ilsa breathing in the next room and thought: *maybe that's how it starts. Maybe that's the seed.*

Maybe stories planted deep enough become roots.

On the table by the door, she found Ilsa had left something: a scrap of paper, and on it, in a careful hand, a single line.

*Tonight: the tunnel under the fence, and what we found at the end of it. Bring your appetite. — I.*

Lena read it twice. Then she folded it small and put it in her pocket, next to her heart, where she kept things that mattered.

Outside, Corvale was waking. Bells, birdsong, the smell of bread again. And far to the east, past the passes, travelers were already telling each other about a garden gate in the foothills where prayers got answered quicker than anywhere else in the world — and about the two women who tended it, who were said to be strangers to each other, and were said to be inseparable, and were, both of those things, exactly right.
