## 22. A Sigil for the Garden

The frost came late that year, and when it came it came gentle, like a hand laid over a sleeping child's brow.

Lena felt it first on her knuckles as she knelt in the turned earth above the sealed gate. The morning was gray-gold, the kind of light that made even Thornfell's black shoulders look almost kind, and her breath hung in the air in small white ghosts that rose and rose and did not come back down. She had learned to think of them that way — things given up, things let go — rising instead of falling. It was a new way of thinking. She was still practicing.

The soil here was good. That was the joke of it, the joke only she and Anselm and a handful of others could appreciate: all those years of smuggler roads and shifting stones and prayers that went unanswered into the dark, and underneath it all the mountain had been sitting on the richest loam in the free cities. As if the old gods, caged and singing, had been feeding the ground above their prison out of sheer stubborn generosity. Or maybe just habit. Gods were like that, Anselm said. They gave even with their hands tied.

Lena pressed the first seedling in — a rowan, because Ilsa said rowans kept off ill luck, and because Ilsa had been right about everything for eleven months now and Lena saw no reason to start doubting her on botany — and firmed the soil around its roots with her palms.

Then she drew the sigil.

It took longer than it used to. Not because she'd forgotten it — she would never forget it; it lived behind her eyes now the way her own name used to — but because she'd learned to draw it slowly, deliberately, the way you'd set a table for someone you loved rather than the way you'd throw a knife. Veyra's mark. The silent god's sign. Once it had meant hiding. Once it had meant a cage door and a price written in memory and a sister who looked at her like a stranger.

Now it meant *grow*.

She traced it in the air above the seedling with two fingers, and the sigil bloomed faint gold where she drew it, hanging in the cold air like pollen, then sinking into the soil the way rain sinks. Somewhere beneath her knees — forty feet down, fifty, through stone and old dark and a gate fused shut by her own voice — something vast and quiet stirred, the way a great dog stirs in its sleep when someone says its name kindly.

Lena smiled at the dirt.

"You're showing off," she told the mountain.

The mountain did not answer. It never answered in words. But warmth spread up through her palms, slow as honey, and the seedling straightened in its little mound of earth as if it had already decided to live, and that was answer enough.

* * *

Ilsa found her an hour later, carrying two cups of barley tea and wearing the expression she wore when she'd been rehearsing something on the walk over.

"You're doing it again," Ilsa said, handing her a cup.

"Doing what."

"Sitting in the dirt talking to rocks." Ilsa sat down beside her without ceremony, skirts and all, because ten months of living in Corvale had not yet taught her court manners and Lena prayed every night that nothing ever would. "Anselm says it's a religious practice now. He's calling it *tending the threshold*. He wants to write a pamphlet."

"Anselm wants to write a pamphlet about everything. Last month it was the proper way to address a river god. Before that, bread."

"Bread is important," Ilsa said solemnly. "You should hear what Ondra of the Deep Roots has to say about sourdough."

And there it was again — the small vertigo, the trapdoor feeling, gone almost before it opened. *Ondra of the Deep Roots.* A god whose name Lena had known once, probably. A god whose name Ilsa knew now, intimately, the way you know a neighbor. Her sister spoke the names of gods over breakfast the way other people discussed weather, and every time she did it Lena felt that brief lurch of a life she'd paid away — not with regret, exactly. Regret needed a shape, and the shape had been taken from her. It was more like pressing your tongue to the socket of a missing tooth.

She'd traded every memory of Ilsa to break the cage. That was the ledger's balance. And so her sister was a stranger who insisted on loving her anyway, and the love was real, and the memories were gone, and this was the arithmetic she woke up inside of every morning.

"Ilsa," Lena said. "Tell me another one."

Ilsa's face did the thing it always did — brightened and shadowed in the same breath, joy and grief holding hands. She'd been offering stories since the day Lena woke in the temple hospice in Corvale with gaps in her head and a woman crying over her who she didn't recognize. One story at a time. An offering, she called it. Rebuilding a house room by room from the outside, since the owner couldn't remember the floor plan.

"Which one?"

"The pond one. You've told me three times but I keep hoping it gets better with retelling."

"It does not get better with retelling," Ilsa said. "You pushed me in. On purpose. You were nine and I was six and you wanted to see if frogs would land on me." She sipped her tea, eyes on the mountainside. "Mother fished us both out with the laundry pole. You got the switching. And you stood there dripping and getting switched and you would not say sorry, and that night you left a dead frog on my windowsill as — and I quote — *compensation*."

Lena laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh cracked something open in her chest, the way it always did. Because she couldn't feel the memory. She could only hold it the way you hold someone else's photograph — knowing it was hers, knowing the girl in it was her, feeling nothing but the shape of the absence where the feeling should be.

But she was learning that this was its own strange gift. Every story Ilsa told her arrived twice: once as information, once as a small resurrection. She heard about the frog and she laughed, and somewhere below thought, in whatever cellar of her the gods kept things they'd borrowed, maybe nine-year-old Lena laughed too.

"A dead frog," Lena said. "As compensation."

"You were a menace. You're still a menace. You threatened a herald of the Thorn Court last week."

"He was rude to Sera."

"He was *confused*. Sera speaks seven dialects of insult and he only understood one of them."

"Sera counts that as rudeness," Lena said, and Ilsa snorted tea, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. The rowan stood in its fresh-turned earth. The frost burned off. Below their feet, patient as a heartbeat, the freed gods sang the boundaries of the world — softly now, gently, the way a mother sings to a child who has finally stopped crying.

* * *

Sera arrived at midday, which was early for Sera, who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of arriving precisely whenever she intended to and no sooner.

She came up the hill road in riding leathers that cost more than Lena's old mercenary kit had earned in a year, leading a second horse, and she dismounted at the garden gate — there was a gate now; Anselm had built it in autumn and carved Veyra's sigil into the lintel, and pilgrims touched it for luck the way they'd once touched the old temple doors — with the air of a woman delivering news she'd already decided how to spin.

"Lena Villalobos," Sera said. "Terror of the Salt Road. Scourge of Malachar's heralds. Kneeling in mud."

"It's good mud."

"Mm." Sera handed the second horse's reins to Ilsa with a familiarity that still made Lena raise an eyebrow, and surveyed the rows of seedlings, the half-built trellises, the low stone wall going up along the south side. "You know there are people in Corvale who think this whole thing is a shrine. There are rumors. Miraculous harvests. Flowers that bloom out of season. A silent god who answers in warm soil."

"The flowers do bloom out of season."

"Yes, well, don't encourage them." Sera's humor held for a moment, then settled, the way it did when she was about to be serious and hated it. "There's word from the east. From what's left of the Thorn Court."

Lena set down her cup. "Malachar?"

"No one's seen him since the mountain shook. Some say the gods unmade him when they took back their voices. Some say he crawled into a crack in the world and is waiting." Sera shrugged, elegant and unbothered, though Lena had spent enough months beside her to read the tension under it. "What matters is his generals are surrendering or running, the free cities are arguing about borders instead of survival — progress — and the Council wants to formalize the sanctuary around this mountain. Make it law. Which means they want to formalize *you*, since you're the one who broke it."

"They can formalize the garden. I'll water it."

"They want to give you a title."

"I'll water the title too."

Sera laughed despite herself, and then stopped, and looked at Lena the way she sometimes did lately — measuring, careful, fond in a way she'd deny under oath. "They also want Anselm to rebuild the order of Veyra. Properly, this time. Priests who tend thresholds instead of hoarding secrets. He asked me to ask you—" she paused, and Lena watched her choose the honest version over the diplomatic one, a habit she was acquiring against her will, "—he asked whether you would object. To the order existing again. Since it failed you. Since it failed everyone."

Lena was quiet for a while. Down the slope, past the wall, the terraced fields of the valley lay gold-brown and stubbled, and beyond them Corvale smoked gently, ten thousand chimneys, ordinary lives. A year ago she'd stood on a rain-slicked road in the foothills and prayed out of habit to a god who wasn't answering, and the silence had felt like abandonment. Now she knew the silence had been a shield held over her head for thirty years, and the god who held it had grown thin and dim doing it, and had never once complained.

"Tell him yes," Lena said. "But tell him the first rule has to change. No more hiding things from people to protect them. People get to choose what they carry."

"That's not very mysterious," Sera said. "Gods are supposed to be mysterious."

"This one spent thirty years being mysterious. She's earned a rest."

* * *

Evening came down the mountain in long blue stripes, and Lena walked the garden rows alone, as she did most evenings, checking the winter cloth over the tender beds, talking — quietly, idly, the way you talk to a friend who doesn't need answers — to whatever listened beneath the stone.

This was the part no one warned her about: that peace was not a place you arrived. She'd thought, in the dark of the cage, singing the final sigil while Malachar's soldiers poured into the court, that breaking the cage would be the end of it. Catharsis. Ruin, then rest. Instead the ruin healed over, the way ruins do, and every morning after that was a choice made again — to plant instead of sharpen, to listen instead of brace, to sit across from a beloved stranger at breakfast and say *tell me another one* instead of grieving the teller she couldn't remember.

Peace as a daily practice. Like prayer. Like weeding.

She reached the center of the garden, where the ground was bare by design — no beds, no trellis, just clean raked earth above the sealed gate — and knelt there, and put her palm flat against the cold soil.

"Good evening," she said.

Warmth rose through her hand, unhurried, immense, gentle as the tide.

Once, the gods' voices had come back into the world like a dam bursting — she remembered that, remembered the mountain convulsing and the sky full of returned sound, every prayer everywhere answered at once, armies falling silent mid-march as their stolen voices were reclaimed. It had been too much. People had wept in the streets. Some had gone mad from it. For the first weeks, the gods' answers crashed through the world like guests who hadn't learned the house was small.

But they'd learned. That was the miracle nobody sang about. Ondra of the Deep Roots answered farmers in riddles about drainage ditches. The river god of the Cor had started leaving smooth stones on the banks where lost children liked to walk, each one marked with a sigil that meant *this way home*. And Veyra — diminished, patient, silent no more but speaking only in warmth and growing things — answered Lena most softly of all, as if mindful of the debt between them, as if aware that the last time she'd shaped this woman's life she'd done it without asking.

Lena stayed kneeling until the stars came out. Then she stood, brushed off her knees, and went down to the house where lamplight waited and her sister was telling Anselm the frog story wrong on purpose, and Sera was correcting her with the relish of a professional witness, and there was soup.

* * *

Winter passed the way winters pass in the free cities now — with arguments in council halls instead of sieges at gates, with temples reopening in towns that had forgotten they'd ever had them, with children learning the old names as songs. Lena planted the overwinter beds. Ilsa filled two more notebooks with stories, in her terrible handwriting, labeled in her own private system: *For Lena, when she's ready. For Lena, when she asks. For Lena, someday, maybe never.*

On the first true morning of spring, Lena was in the garden before dawn, turning the earth for the new rowan saplings — twelve of them, one for each god freed from the cage, though Anselm argued the count and Sera argued worse — when she noticed the frost on the north wall.

It had melted everywhere else. Only there, in a patch the size of a shield, the frost remained, thick and white and patterned.

Not patterned. Written.

Sigils. Old ones, older than Veyra's mark, older than anything Anselm's books contained — and threaded among them, unmistakable, a line of script in a language Lena had seen exactly once before: on a dying smuggler's map, in a burning waystation, in the ledger of a broker beneath the world. The language of the roads themselves. The moving roads, the ones that had shifted and hidden during Malachar's reign and had, everyone assumed, gone dormant since.

Except this message hadn't been there yesterday. And the roads, as far as anyone knew, did not write letters.

Lena stood in the pink light of dawn with her hands full of dirt and read the frost slowly, twice, her heart keeping mercenary time the way it hadn't in a year. Then she straightened, wiped her palms on her trousers, and turned toward the house where her sister was just lighting the morning lamp.

"Ilsa," she called, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Get your coat. Bring the notebooks."

The mountain had a question. And for the first time in a year, it wasn't asking her to stay.

It was asking her to walk.
