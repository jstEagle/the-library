## 1. The Weight of Forgotten Things

The widow's grief weighed four ounces.

Silas knew this because the brass scale on Odile Verrec's kitchen table told them so—a small glass vial on one pan, a stack of calibrated weights on the other, the needle trembling to rest at the mark stamped *IV*. Four ounces of grief, drawn from a woman's chest through a needle no thicker than a hair and stored in glass the color of drowned sunlight. On any market day in the Hollow, that much raw sorrow would fetch enough coin to eat for a month. Grief was dense. Grief kept.

"Hurts less already," Odile said. She sat with her hands folded in her lap like a schoolgirl awaiting praise, though her knuckles were white as candle wax. "Is that normal?"

"That's how it works." Silas stoppered the vial with wax and a sigil pressed into it while soft—not magic, nothing like magic, just a maker's mark, a signature, the way a cooper branded his barrels. "It doesn't leave a hole. It leaves a room where the hole used to be. You'll walk past it every day and feel nothing but a draft."

"And I won't remember him?"

"You'll remember him. You'll remember everything except the weight of it." Silas slid the vial into a padded case, then into an inner pocket. "You'll remember his face. His voice. The way he hummed off-key when he shaved. What you won't be able to do is lie awake at three in the morning wondering why you were the one who lived."

Odile's eyes went bright and wet, and Silas watched her decide not to cry, the way people in the Hollow learned to decide things. "That's worth more than money."

"Then you're overpaying me."

The laugh that came out of her surprised them both—short, cracked, real. It was the first laugh she'd managed since the fever took Bram Verrec in the winter, and Silas had heard it happen before, dozens of times, always in the same minute after the extraction: the body's relief arriving before the mind understood what had been taken from it. Some clients wept anyway. Some thanked Silas like a priest. One old ferryman had kissed both their hands and called them a saint of small mercies, which had made Silas want to wash.

They were not a saint. They were a thief, and everyone at this table knew it, and everyone pretended otherwise because pretending was the Hollow's native language.

"Rules," Silas said, rising, tucking their chair under the table out of habit—the habit of leaving no sign. "One: don't go digging for what's gone. Two: if anyone asks who did your extraction, you never heard my name. Three—" they paused at the door, "—in six months, if you want it back, come find me. Some do. Most don't."

"Do you keep them? All of them?"

"I keep everything."

It wasn't quite a boast. It was closer to a confession, but Odile Verrec didn't have the ears for it yet.

Outside, Corben Street was doing its evening business in the amber light of the valley lamps. The Hollow had no magicians—that was the whole point of the place—but it had lamplighters and fishmongers and a woman on the corner selling roasted chestnuts whose cart wheel squeaked on the same note as a dying violin. Smoke rose straight up from a hundred chimneys because the wind died at the ridgeline, turned back by the wards the way rain turns back off slate. Above the smoke, above the rooftops, above everything, the sky was doing what the sky always did in the Hollow: holding still.

No stars here. That was the other thing about the place, the thing the brochures didn't say because there were no brochures. The valley's founders had paid for their safety with the night sky itself; the wards that kept magic out also kept the deep heavens dimmed to a brown smear. Children in the Hollow grew up thinking *stars* was a word for something that happened elsewhere, like kings or whales. Silas had been thirty years old before they climbed high enough on the eastern ridge, in a hard frost, close to where the wards thinned—and saw them. Thousands. A spill of cold fire across black silk. They'd stood there shaking until dawn, unable to say whether it was wonder or homesickness, since they had no home it could be sickness *for*.

Then they'd climbed down, gone home, and gotten back to work.

Work waited tonight in the form of Marrow's ledger, which Silas carried everywhere and consulted like scripture. Three extractions booked tomorrow: a debtor's shame (light work, two ounces), a soldier's fear of water (heavy, handle with care), and a rich man's memory of his own wedding, which he wanted cut out whole because the bride had left him and he couldn't bear the sight of happiness in retrospect. There was a time Silas had found the work grim. Now they found it clean. Other thieves stole silver, land, reputations. Silas stole pain, and pain, unlike silver, was better off stolen. The trade kept the valley sane. Somebody had to carry what the valley set down, and Silas Thackeray carried more of it than anyone alive, sealed in a cellar under their lodgings in rows of labeled glass, a library of everything the Hollow had chosen to forget.

They ate chestnuts on the walk home and did not think about the widow's face when the grief came out of her, the way her shoulders dropped like a puppet with its strings half-cut. They did not think about whether her husband would have wanted to live on only as a fact instead of a wound. In the Hollow, wanting was a luxury item. Nobody stocked it.

Their lodgings were above a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, three rooms and a roof access they paid extra for, because a thief who can't see the street below is a thief in a box. Silas climbed the iron ladder at dusk, as they did most evenings, and settled onto the warm shingles with the ledger open across their knees, checking figures against memory the way other men checked prayers against saints.

That was when the first star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*—snuffed between one blink and the next, a point of light in the northeast that simply stopped being there, leaving a socket of perfect dark.

Silas looked up from the ledger and frowned.

Clouds, probably. A wisp of high vapor crossing the gap where the wards ran thin. But the air was dry and dead still, chimney-smoke rising in threads like plumb lines, and there was no cloud, and the star was gone, and the star beside it was beginning to gutter—flickering the way a lamp gutters when someone is pinching the wick.

"Hello," Silas said softly, to no one, to the sky, the way you speak to a dog behaving strangely.

The second star went out. Then a third, farther east. Then a fourth and fifth in the same breath, and now Silas was standing, ledger forgotten, sliding on the shingles, because the extinguishing was moving—it was traveling along the sky in a slow deliberate line, like a finger drawn across a page, erasing.

The Hollow had no words for this. That was the trouble with a country built on forgetting: when the impossible finally came, it arrived unnamed. Silas's mind reached for explanations and found only trade-craft. *Something is drinking the light. Something is coming through the place where the light gets in.* And underneath, colder, older, in a voice that didn't sound like theirs:

*This has happened before.*

The thought arrived whole, without history, like a coin found in the street. Silas turned it over and found no edge, no date, no story attached—just the flat certainty of it, the way you know you've been somewhere in a dream. They shook it off. Unexplained thoughts were a hazard of the trade; they spent all day handling other people's minds, and sometimes the cargo leaked.

Below, on Corben Street, the chestnut seller had stopped mid-pour. Down the lane, others were stopping too, faces tilting upward, the whole town going quiet the way a forest goes quiet. Somewhere a child began to cry, and its mother hushed it without looking away from the sky, and the hushing sounded like prayer.

By full dark, a third of the visible heaven was blank. The remaining stars burned harder, frantic, as if competing for a light source being shut off behind them. And in the great brown smear where the Milky Way should have poured across the zenith—the river of stars the wards had dimmed for two hundred years—there was a new darkness, vast and slow, moving like a lid being lifted off something that had been kept under it.

Kept. The word again. Kept, sealed, locked—

Silas's hands were shaking. They noticed this with distant professional interest, the way they'd notice a tremor in a client's pulse during extraction. Their own pulse. Interesting. They catalogued the symptom and moved on, because that was the discipline, that was the whole craft: name the feeling, weigh it, bottle it, sell it to someone who needed it worse.

But you couldn't extract your own fear. Everyone in the trade knew that. The needle wouldn't take from the hand that held it. Whatever was pooling cold and heavy in Silas's chest tonight, they were going to have to carry it themselves.

The last star in the northeast went out, and in its place—for one heartbeat, no longer—Silas saw something that was not a star and not a darkness either. A shape. A door-shaped absence, tall and narrow, hanging where light used to be, outlined in a faint grey shimmer like breath on cold glass.

Then it was gone, and the sky was only a wound, and Silas Thackeray stood alone on the rooftop of Tallow Row with a ledger full of other people's sorrows and one thought of their own, arriving at last with total clarity:

*Tomorrow,* they thought, *I need to talk to Marrow.*

Below them, faint on the night wind, from the direction of the border ridges—miles away, impossibly far to carry on still air—they heard a sound like teeth breaking.

* * *

Dawn came up wrong.

Silas knew dawn. Dawn in the Hollow was a grey seep, light arriving without drama, the valley filling like a bowl. This dawn came in gold—actual gold, shafted and burning, pouring over the eastern ridge in bars that lit the dust in Silas's rooms and threw their shadow long across the floor. Light like that belonged to the outside world, to the country of stars and mages and kings. It had no business here.

Neither, when Silas reached the roof, did the crowd.

They lined the ridgeline to the east, tiny at this distance, a procession of dark figures walking down out of the hills where no road ran—walking straight through the place where the wards had stood for two centuries, unburnt, unhindered, the way water moves through a broken fence. Even at this range Silas could see they weren't refugees in the ragged sense. They walked in order. They walked like an argument arriving.

And above the ridgeline, where the sky had been amputated all night, the stars were still gone—but now, in the clear gold daylight, Silas could see what had replaced them. Thin lines of pale fire, hanging in the air over the valley like cracks in porcelain, spreading slowly outward from the broken east.

Marrow was going to pay for this information. Or Silas was going to pay him for it. Either way, the ledger was already obsolete.

Silas picked up the case of tools, checked the vials—four ounces of a widow's grief riding safe against their ribs—and went down into a valley that had just stopped being safe, carrying everything they knew how to steal, including, though they did not yet know it, the key to the door at the end of the world.
