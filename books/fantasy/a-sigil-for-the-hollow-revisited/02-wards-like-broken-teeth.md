## 2. Wards Like Broken Teeth

The sound woke Silas before the light did—a crack that ran through the valley floor like something enormous stepping on it, followed by a silence so complete the ears rang against it. Then dawn arrived all wrong: not the slow gray seep of a normal morning but a bruised purple flush along the eastern ridge, as though the sky itself had been struck and was bleeding color.

Silas sat up in the narrow bed above the shop and listened.

No second crack. No shouting. Just the town of Lowmark stirring below—shutters opening, dogs complaining, someone hauling water with the same tired rhythm as any other day. The Hollow's great talent was forgetting, and its people were already half done with the sound.

But Silas had spent eleven years learning what people wanted to forget, and the first lesson of the trade was this: nobody forgets the things that frighten them unless they're made to. The town would remember that crack. Silas remembered it too, down in the teeth, in the marrow of the bones, in a way that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with instinct.

*Something broke,* said the part of Silas that had survived this long by believing the worst early.

The wards had stood at the valley's rim for longer than anyone living could account for—not walls, exactly, nothing you could see or touch, but a pressure at the edge of awareness. Every child raised in the Hollow learned it the way they learned to swallow: there was an outside, and it could not follow you in. Magic stopped at the ridgeline like weather against glass. That was the whole covenant. You gave up wonders; the wonders gave up on you.

Silas dressed quickly, took the stairs two at a time, and stepped out into a street that looked ordinary and felt like the held breath before a scream.

The lamplighter was still making his rounds, which meant the light itself was strange—the oil flames burning violet at their tips, guttering sideways as if pushed by wind that wasn't there. Old Tam, who swept the steps of the assay office, had stopped mid-sweep and was staring at his own broom. When Silas passed, he didn't look up. His lips were moving. Counting, maybe. Or praying. In the Hollow, the two had always been close cousins.

At the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where the street opened toward the eastern fields, Silas saw the first of them.

A woman, walking. Not running—walking, with the deliberate pace of someone who has traveled very far and refuses to look exhausted. Her clothes were road-ruined, patched with cloth from three different countries, and around her throat she wore a collar of small iron keys, dozens of them, none matching any lock Silas had ever seen. As she passed, she turned her head, and her eyes found Silas with the terrible accuracy of a compass needle.

She smiled.

Not a threat. Worse—an *acknowledgment*. The look you give something you've been told to expect.

Then she was past, heading uphill toward the ridge, and behind her came more: a pair of young men carrying a rolled banner between them, an old man whose shadow moved a half-second out of step with his body, a family of five whose children were singing in a language that made Silas's back teeth ache to hear it. They came up the eastern road in ones and twos and then in groups, and they did not scatter into the valley the way refugees do, frightened and grateful. They walked like people coming home to collect a debt.

Someone in the crowd near the well said the word, and it moved through Lowmark faster than fire through dry grass:

*Reckoning.*

Silas knew the name from the old stories, the ones told quietly in the Hollow, the ones parents hushed when children repeated them. Exiled mages. Cast out over the border generations ago—or so the story went—when the valley sealed itself against every working and wonder. They were supposed to have died out, or given up, or forgotten. But forgetting was the Hollow's specialty, and Silas should have known better than anyone that the Hollow only ever forgot one side of a story.

By midmorning there were hundreds of them camped in the eastern fields, and by noon, thousands, and the town square had filled with townsfolk staring at the tents and cookfires and banners with the expression of people watching a tide come in over ground they'd been assured was high land.

Marrow kept his shop in the tannery district, behind a door with no handle, because Marrow believed that anything worth having shouldn't be easy to enter. Silas knocked the pattern—two, pause, three—and waited through the long minute Marrow insisted on even now, even today.

Inside smelled of tallow and old paper. Marrow sat behind his desk, a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, his hands folded on a ledger he closed the moment Silas entered. That was the first bad sign. The second was that he didn't offer tea.

"You've heard," Marrow said.

"I've seen. The wards are down."

"The wards are *down*," Marrow agreed, "and every soul who ever got thrown out of here is walking up the eastern road asking directions." He rubbed his jaw. "Sit down, Thackeray."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He opened the ledger again, slowly, and turned it around so Silas could read the entries. Three of them, marked in Marrow's careful hand, each with a price that made Silas's eyebrows rise. "Business first. Because business is the only language I trust before breakfast."

"Whose orders?"

"New clients." Marrow tapped the page. "Came in with the dawn, before the gates were even properly open, like they'd been waiting years for the chance. Mages, or dressed like it. And do you know what they asked for? Not grief. Not guilt. Not the sweet little sins I usually move for you." He leaned forward. "They asked for memories of *the sealing*. The night the door was locked. The night the valley cut itself off."

The room tilted, gently, the way a floor tilts just before you realize you've been standing on a trapdoor.

"I don't have those," Silas said. "Nobody does. The sealing isn't something anyone remembers selling."

"That's what I told them." Marrow's eyes stayed level, and there was something in them Silas had never seen before—pity, maybe, or its cheaper cousin. "And do you know what they said to me? They said, *someone must remember, because someone did it.* Doors don't lock themselves, Thackeray. Sigils don't draw themselves. Somebody in this valley stood at that door with power in their hands and chose to use it. And whoever that somebody was—" he turned the ledger back around, "—they either sold the memory or they buried it. And my new friends intend to find out which."

Silas kept their voice steady. Eleven years of practice. "What did you tell them?"

"What could I tell them?" Marrow spread his huge hands. "I'm a fence, not a fortress. I told them I'd make inquiries."

"Marrow."

"I told them I'd make inquiries," he repeated, "and then I sat here all morning wondering how many inquiries it would take before somebody said your name."

The silence stretched. Outside, faintly, came the sound of the Reckoning camp—a low murmur of voices, a bell ringing some unfamiliar hour, the children still singing.

"Why me?" Silas asked finally. "There are a hundred memory-workers in the Hollow. Two hundred."

"Because you're the best, and because you work alone, and because—" Marrow hesitated, which he never did, "—because about six years back, there was a job you took that you never told me about. Big extraction. Bigger than anything you've touched since. You came to me afterward wanting untraceable storage, top tier, no questions. Paid triple. And whatever's in that storage, Thackeray, you never once asked to see it. Not once in six years." He shook his head slowly. "People check their valuables. People visit their valuables. You treated yours like a grave."

Silas's mouth had gone dry. There was a shape in their memory where that recollection should be—a job, a client, a night—but pressing on it was like pressing on a numb tooth: the absence had a texture, and the texture was deliberate.

"I need that storage," Silas heard themself say.

"No," Marrow said. "You need to leave town. My new friends paid me a third fee this morning. Retainer. For introductions." He couldn't meet Silas's eyes. "I haven't decided whether to introduce them yet. That's the honest truth, and it's worth exactly what honesty is worth in this valley, which is whatever someone will pay for it. Go home. Pack light. By tonight, somebody will say your name—I can't stop that anymore than I can stop the tide—and when they do, you want to be a rumor instead of an address."

Silas stood there a moment longer, looking at the man who had fenced their stolen years for a decade, trying to feel betrayed and finding only the dull familiarity of arithmetic. Everything in the Hollow was a transaction. It had simply never occurred to Silas that they might someday be the goods.

"Which gate?" Silas asked.

"West, if you're smart. The Reckoning came east." Marrow finally poured the tea, cold and bitter, and drank it like a man toasting a funeral. "And Thackeray—one more thing. The woman with the keys. She asked for you by description, not by name. Knew the coat you wear. Knew the scar on your hand." He nodded at Silas's left palm, at the thin white line Silas had never been able to explain and had long ago stopped trying to. "They didn't come here for a door, friend. They came here for the person who locked it."

Silas walked home through streets filling with strangers, wearing invisibility like a borrowed coat that had suddenly grown too thin. Smallness had been the whole trick—stay minor, stay gray, stay forgettable in the valley built on forgetting. Watching the Reckoning's fires kindle across the eastern fields, watching the violet light bleed off the broken wards like a wound refusing to clot, Silas understood that the trick was spent. Somewhere out there was a version of themself they had chosen not to know, and it was walking up the road to collect.

That night, Silas packed one bag and did not sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn, someone scratched softly at the shop door below.

Three knocks. A pause. One more.

A pattern Silas had never taught anyone—because Silas hadn't known it, until this moment, until their hand rose without instruction and answered it, knuckles rapping the reply from some memory they didn't own.

From the other side of the door, a woman's voice said: "Thackeray. We need to talk about what you forgot."
