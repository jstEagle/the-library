## 3. What the Widow Kept

The extraction should have been routine.

Odile Marchand had come to my cellar office three days after the stars went out, which told me everything about her state of mind. Grief makes people reckless, and the Hollow runs on recklessness. She sat in the cracked leather chair across from me with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral that hadn't started yet, and she said, "I want it gone. All of it."

"All of it" was her husband. Forty-one years of him, by the look of the sediment in her—Annecy's death had settled through her memory like silt in a riverbed, coloring everything downstream. The wedding. The farm on the south slope. The way he hummed when he mended fences, off-key, always off-key.

"You understand what you're asking," I said. "It doesn't just take the hurt. It takes the man."

"I know what I'm asking." Her voice didn't waver. That was rare. Most people wavered. "I've been asking since Tuesday. I can't eat. I can't sleep in our bed. I stood in the kitchen yesterday and I couldn't remember why I'd walked in there, and then I remembered, and I wished the stars-out night had taken me instead of leaving me to do this." She looked at my hands. People always looked at my hands, as if they could see the work in them. "Take it. Take all of it. I'll pay what you ask."

In the Hollow, magic cannot follow you over the border. That's the whole point of the place—a valley where the exiled and the gifted come to be ordinary, where wards older than anyone living press down flat on anything that might flicker or burn or whisper. But the wards don't stop everything. They stop what comes *in* from outside. What grows here, native and small and patient—that stays. My trade is one of those things. A craft, not a sorcery. Callused fingers and a steady heart and the knowledge of where memory lives in a person: not in the head, whatever the outsiders think, but behind the sternum, folded like letters in a drawer.

I took out my kit. Vials of grey glass, a tuning fork worn smooth from thirty years of other people's griefs, a sliver of bone for the cutting—not flesh, never flesh, but the membrane between remembering and forgetting, which is thinner than anyone wants to know.

"Lie back," I said. "Think of him. Think of the first time you saw him, and let the rest follow."

She closed her eyes. And I went in.

Extraction is listening before it is taking. You put your awareness against the fold of someone's memory and you read it like a man reading a letter through an envelope, deciding what's worth the postage. Odile's grief was enormous, well-organized, forty-one years deep. Annecy young, Annecy gray, Annecy's hands, Annecy's terrible humming. I worked outward from the wound, gathering the threads.

That's when I found it.

A splinter. Buried deep, deeper than Annecy, deeper than her girlhood, tucked into the oldest fold of her—the kind of place memories go when someone has hidden them there deliberately, the way you'd hide a letter inside the lining of a coat. It wasn't hers. I knew that immediately, the way you know a stranger's cough in your own house. It had a different weight. A different temperature. Cold, somehow, though memory has no temperature, except this did.

And it was mine.

Not mine the way a stolen thing is mine. Mine the way a scar is mine. I knew the shape of it from the inside before I knew anything else about it—the particular pressure of my own attention, the signature every extractor leaves on their work. Someone had used my hands. Someone had used my craft, my exact technique, down to the little double-fold I do at the edges so nothing bleeds. And they'd buried this splinter in Odile Marchand, who until three weeks ago had never spoken to me in her life.

I should have stopped. Every rule of the trade says stop when you find something that isn't yours. But rules are for men whose pasts stay put, and I was already reading it through the envelope.

* * *

Hands. Not my hands—I could see them from outside, the way you see yourself in dreams, doubled and wrong. Broad hands, scarred across both palms in old burns. They held a stylus of black glass and they were drawing on stone.

Stone door. Enormous, taller than two men, set into a hillside or a mountainside—it was hard to tell, because the fragment had no sky. No ground either, really. Just the door, and the dark pressing around it like water around a wreck. The sigil went on line by line, and I knew the lines. Gods help me, I knew them. The opening curve was the same curve I use to seal an extraction, scaled up a thousandfold, drawn with a precision no human hand should hold for the hours it must have taken.

There were voices. Muffled, arguing, pleading—one voice in particular, a woman's voice saying *you don't have to do it this way, Silas, you don't have to be the one—*

Silas.

My name, in a fragment I had never lived, buried in a widow I had never met.

The hands finished the last line. The sigil flared—and the fragment cut off, sheared clean, the way I shear extractions when a client asks me to leave them the ending. Whoever hid this had wanted the viewer to know the beginning and never the end.

I came back to the cellar with Odile's grief still gathered in my grip and my own heart going like a trapped bird.

* * *

"Silas?" Odile was sitting up, watching me. "You've gone white as milk. Is something wrong?"

I looked at her—at this woman I'd extracted for twice before, small jobs, a fear of wells, a humiliating memory of a public fall she'd wanted gone before her daughter's wedding. Ordinary client. Ordinary woman. And now a dead-letter drop for a message written in my own hand.

"When did you last visit Marrow?" I asked.

Her brow creased. "Three years ago, maybe. He took something for me—" She stopped. Something moved behind her eyes, some instinct of privacy even now. "Why does that matter?"

Because Marrow is the only fence in the Hollow big enough to move a splinter like that. Because if someone had planted this in Odile, they'd have done it through a trade, and every trade in this valley passes through Marrow's ledger. Because Marrow had been looking at me strangely for months, and I'd told myself it was nothing.

"Did he ever mention my name? In connection with something called the sealing?"

"No." Then, slower: "But he asked me once if I dreamed. He said—" She laughed, short and unhappy. "He said some clients keep things they don't know they've bought. I thought he was being poetic. Marrow's always being poetic when he's cheating you."

I finished Odile's extraction. I want that recorded, whatever else becomes of me: I finished the job. I took Annecy entire—the wedding, the farm, the humming—all of it folded into grey glass vials, and I sealed the edges with my double-fold, and I watched her walk out into a morning without stars carrying forty years less than she'd arrived with. She paid me double. She smiled at me on the way out, light as a girl, and asked me if we'd met somewhere before.

Then I sat alone in my cellar and turned the vials in my hands and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that the splinter had been meant for exactly this moment. Planted years ago. Waiting. Not for Odile to find—but for me. For whoever came to take her grief. For the thief who would recognize his own signature and be unable, *unable*, to look away.

Someone had robbed me, and hidden the loot inside a job, and mailed it to my future self.

The woman's voice said *you don't have to be the one.* The hands drew the sigil. And beneath the dread, beneath the vertigo of a man meeting his own stranger, one question sharpened to a point:

If I had sealed a door—if I had done it with these hands, at some cost large enough that I'd chosen to forget—then what exactly was I willing to forget to become the man who could sleep at night?

I blew out the lamp. Above the cellar stairs, the starless sky was going grey toward dawn, and dawn was when the wards would break, though I didn't know it yet. All I knew was that Marrow opened his shop at first light, and that I intended to be standing in front of his counter when he did.

Marrow had questions to answer. Starting with the simplest one:

What had I sold him—and what had he sold of me?
