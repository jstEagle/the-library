## 4. The Reckoning Arrives

Silas heard the drums before they saw the banners.

Three beats, a rest, three beats again—the rhythm of an army that wanted to be counted. From the window of Odile's spare room, where Silas had spent the night pretending to sleep with the widow's memory-splinter burning a hole in the lining of their coat, they watched the Reckoning come down the Hollow road in ranks of grey.

Not robes. Uniforms. That was the thing Silas noticed first, and it frightened them more than the staves or the sigil-lanterns swinging from pole-arms like captured fireflies. Exiled mages were supposed to arrive ragged, desperate, wild-eyed at the edge of a magicless country. These came in formation, boots striking cobbles in time, grey coats buttoned to the throat, and above them a banner of white cloth bearing no symbol at all—just a clean rectangle of nothing, as if even their flag had been erased.

"They're orderly," Silas said.

Odile stood at the other window, her hands folded the way they always were now, since the extraction, since the hole where her grief used to live. "Orderly is worse," she said. "Ragged people want something. Orderly people have already decided."

The column halted in the market square below, and the town gathered the way towns do—slowly at first, then all at once, drawn out of doorways and off stoops by the gravity of spectacle. Silas knew they should stay hidden. They had spent thirty years in the Hollow learning how to be small: small debts, small rooms, small footprints on rooftops. Smallness had kept them alive through famines, feuds, and the particular resentments of a valley full of people who had sold pieces of themselves and regretted it.

But Vess Adair's voice carried up through the morning air like a blade slid between floorboards, and Silas went down the stairs before they had decided to move.

* * *

Vess Adair stood on the scaffold that the town carpenter had built overnight—she hadn't needed to threaten anyone; he'd simply woken knowing it was wanted—and surveyed the crowd of the Hollow's largest town with the patience of someone who had waited years for this exact view.

She had crossed the border at dawn with four hundred soldiers and a list of names, and the names mattered more than the soldiers. The Hollow thought of itself as a place outside history. Vess had spent eleven years in exile dreaming of nothing else. Every night beyond the wards, she had lain awake in countries that hummed with magic she couldn't fully touch—magic that flinched from her, the way a dog flinches from a beating—and she had recited the names to herself like a rosary. The ones who had sealed the door. The collaborators who had helped. The thieves who had made forgetting into a business while the old country bled.

Her mother's name was not on the list. Her mother was under the ash at Kellin's Ford, and no name would bring her back. But justice didn't require resurrection. Justice required only that the accounting be done in public, in daylight, with everyone watching.

"Citizens of the Hollow," she said, and her voice carried without amplification because she had paid for that trick with two years of her exile and a scar across her palm. "I am Vess Adair, commander of the Reckoning. We are not conquerors. We are neighbors you forgot."

A murmur ran through the square. She let it.

"Your valley has no magic. You know this. What most of you do not know—or have been paid not to know—is why." She unfolded a sheet of paper she did not need; every word was carved into her. "There was once a door beneath this valley. A door to the old country, to everything your grandparents fled or were made to forget. Forty years ago it was sealed. Not locked—sealed, with a working so complete that even the sealers could not remember doing it. And the key to that seal—" she paused, savoring it, letting the silence do its work, "—was not destroyed. It was stolen. Cut out of a mind and sold, the way everything is sold in this valley of yours."

Her eyes moved across the faces below. Farmers, shopkeepers, a knot of Marrow's people near the well trying very hard to look uninvolved. Somewhere in that crowd, she was certain, stood a man or woman who had bought and sold more memories than anyone alive. The intelligence had been specific. A thief who dealt in grief. Who took payment in coin and left payment in absence.

"I offer clemency," Vess said. "Anyone who comes forward with knowledge of the door, the sealing, or the thief called Thackeray will be treated as a witness, not an enemy. You will keep your homes, your trade, your lives." She let that settle, then turned her head slightly, deliberately, toward the gallows at the corner of the scaffold. "And for those who prefer the old arrangements—"

Two soldiers brought him up the steps. Tobbe Grange, the wardens' paymaster. Vess had taken him at his farmhouse an hour after crossing the border; he had been one of the hands who had mixed mortar for the sealing, back when he still remembered it. He wept quietly the whole way up, and she did not look away from him, because looking away would have been mercy, and mercy was what had gotten everyone into this.

"He helped bury your history," she told the crowd. "He took coin for it. I am not here to be cruel. I am here to make sure that when this is over, no one in this valley can say they didn't know what forgetting cost."

They hanged him at midmorning. The crowd made no sound at all, which Vess understood to mean they had heard her perfectly.

Then she raised her voice one final time, and gave the name to the wind, to the rooftops, to every ear in the valley.

"Silas Thackeray. You stole the key. I know you're listening somewhere. Come find me—or I will burn this valley down to the door, and you can watch me dig."

* * *

Silas did not go to the square. They went up instead, to the rooftops, because rooftops were where fear turned into thinking.

From the ridge of the cooper's shop they watched the hanging from a distance that felt like a moral choice rather than cowardice, though Silas was honest enough, alone up there, to admit it might just be cowardice wearing better clothes. Tobbe Grange. They'd known him. Sold him a summer once—a good one, lake water and someone's laughter—and he'd wept with gratitude afterward and paid double. He had helped seal the door. He had been one of the hands.

*I know you're listening somewhere.*

Silas pressed flat against warm shingles as a patrol passed below, grey coats and blank white banners, and felt something unfamiliar moving in their chest where fear should have been simple.

It wasn't just fear anymore. Fear they knew; fear was a professional tool, honed over decades of jobs gone sideways. This was something with edges. Tobbe Grange had died for mortar and coin, and the woman on the scaffold had said *justice*, and the terrible thing—the thing Silas kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it—was that part of them agreed with her.

Because Silas had seen the fragment. Hands drawing a sigil on stone. Their own hands, though they hadn't known it until the splinter showed them. Someone had sealed that door at a cost so terrible they had chosen to cut the memory out of their own head rather than carry it. And then—this was the part that made Silas's jaw tighten—they had sold it anyway. Or lost it. Or buried it in a client's extraction like a body in a shallow grave.

Either way, Vess Adair was right about one thing: the key existed, and it was loose, and Silas Thackeray had put it there.

Below, the patrol stopped. One soldier looked up—not searching, just idly, the way men do—and for a moment Silas looked down at the blank white rectangle of the banner and felt it looking back.

*Small,* Silas reminded themselves. *Be small.*

But smallness had died this morning on a rope in the market square, along with Tobbe Grange and the comfortable lie that the Hollow's walls would hold forever. There was no being small around a woman who burned valleys down to find doors.

Silas slipped along the roofline, dropped into the alley behind the cooper's, and walked—not ran, running drew eyes—to the back entrance of Marrow's counting house, where the fence kept his ledger and his lies in equally careful order.

Marrow owed them passage. Or Silas would make him owe it. Either way, the arithmetic had changed: yesterday the plan had been to run, to hide, to wait out the Reckoning the way one waited out weather.

Now the plan was to get to the door first.

Inside, Marrow looked up from his ledger, and Silas saw—in the half-second before his face arranged itself—that he already knew everything.

"You've heard," Silas said.

"Everyone's heard." Marrow closed the ledger slowly. "Sit down, Thackeray. We need to talk about what things cost now."

* * *

Vess Adair sat in the seized customs house that evening with the day's reports spread before her, and allowed herself, briefly, the luxury of doubt.

The hanging had worked and hadn't. Informants were already queuing at the door of her provost's tent—a cobbler who swore he'd seen a thin figure on the cooper's roof; a girl who remembered a stranger paying in old-country coin—but the leads tangled, contradicted, dissolved. The Hollow protected its thief the way it protected everything: not with loyalty but with fog, with a hundred small transactions no one wanted examined.

She touched the scar on her palm. At Kellin's Ford they had made her watch, too—made the whole village watch—and afterward the survivors had begged the sealers not for justice but for sleep. Give us forgetfulness, they'd said. Take it away. And the sealers had obliged, generously, and called it mercy, and Vess had woken in exile carrying every hour of it while the murderers' neighbors slept sound as children.

No. No doubt. Doubt was what the merciful felt, and mercy without consent was just another kind of theft.

"Commander." Her lieutenant stood in the doorway, holding a message slip. "The fence. Marrow. He's asking to meet tonight. Says he knows where Thackeray sleeps."

Vess smiled, and did not like the shape of it on her own face.

"Tell him," she said, "that justice keeps business hours. And tell him his price just went down—he'll take clemency, or he'll hang next to whoever he sells."

Outside, over the Hollow, the sky was doing something wrong. The stars were gone—had been gone since the wards broke—and in their absence the dark seemed thicker, closer, as if the whole valley were holding its breath behind a held door.

Soon, Vess thought. Soon she would stand in front of that door. And whatever was sealed behind it, whatever mercy had cost, she would drag it into the light and make the whole world remember.

In the dark of the customs house, the message slip lay on her desk, and beneath Marrow's careful hand, one line waited:

*He doesn't sleep anywhere. He sleeps everywhere. But ask him about the widow.*
