## 5. Marrow's Price

Marrow kept his shop behind a tannery, because he liked the smell of the world to be honest with him. You came down an alley that stank of lime and rendered fat, and you knocked on a door that had no handle on the outside, and if Marrow wanted you, it opened.

It opened now. It always opened for Silas. That was the thing about Marrow — for eleven years he had been the closest thing to a friend this valley permitted, and Silas had never once let themselves forget that friendship in the Hollow was just a transaction with the receipts torn up.

"Thackeray." He stood aside. "You look like a man being followed."

"I'm not being followed."

"That's what a man being followed would say." He closed the door and shot three bolts, which was two more than usual. The shop was low-ceilinged and warm, shelves of glass jars catching lamplight like a wall of trapped fireflies. Memories didn't keep well in the Hollow — magic couldn't survive here, but memory wasn't quite magic, or wasn't only magic, and so the trade lived where everything else exiled lived: here, in the one place nothing could follow you home.

Except something had followed everyone home last night. Something had come through the border like water through a rotted dam.

"I need passage," Silas said. "East. Past the salt flats, up into the unmapped country."

Marrow didn't ask why. Asking why cost money, and Marrow never spent when he could earn. "The unmapped." He turned the word over like a coin of doubtful metal. "Where the wards are leaking. Where a man's own name might come back to him wearing someone else's face."

"You've got guides who run it."

"I've got guides who *ran* it. This morning two of them came back without their clients, and one of them came back without himself, if you follow me. He keeps introducing himself to his own hands." Marrow lowered himself into the chair behind his counter, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. He was old the way river stones are old — smooth everywhere the world had rubbed him. "Passage east is expensive today, Thackeray. Passage east is expensive because every mage in the Reckoning wants the same road, and my guides have developed a sudden attachment to staying alive."

"I can pay."

"With what? Coin's going soft. Half the town is already spending it on walls and candles. By week's end it'll be worth its weight in itself."

Silas set the satchel on the counter. Inside were the extractions from yesterday's work — the widow Odile's grief, still humming faintly against the glass, and three smaller jars besides, odds and ends harvested off clients too poor to matter. Marrow looked at them the way a miser looks at a beggar's cup.

"Grief sells," Silas said. "Right now it sells beautifully. Half the town watched the stars go out and decided they'd rather not have seen it. I've got a waiting list."

"You've got a waiting list," Marrow repeated softly. "And I've got a buyer list, and do you know whose name is at the top of it?" He smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only the shape of warmth, worn smooth as the rest of him. "Vess Adair. Commander of the Reckoning. She sent a man this morning with a purse that made my knees go weak, asking after memories of the sealing. And asking, by name, after the thief who took the door's key-memory out of the world."

* * *

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, in the walls, a rat moved with the unhurried confidence of a creature that had never once been afraid.

Silas kept very still. Stillness was the first thing they'd ever learned in the trade — before extraction, before sigil-work, before any of it. A client's memory sits shallow, just under the skin of the mind, and it startles easy. But there was no client here. There was only Marrow, looking at them across eleven years of bought and sold, and Silas understanding, slowly, the way ice understands the spring, that the ground beneath this room had already given way.

"When did she send him?" Silas asked.

Marrow's smile didn't move. "This morning."

"This morning," Silas said. "*Hours* ago."

"Hours ago."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because I like you," Marrow said simply. "Which is a weakness, and I've spent sixty years making sure I don't have any, and here we are anyway." He tapped one knuckle on the counter, twice, like a doctor sounding a chest. "I sold your location, boy. Street, door, the hour you'd likely come collecting payment. She has people watching this shop right now, probably. I told them you always come eventually. Thieves come back to their fences the way rivers come back to their beds."

The jars on their shelf seemed suddenly loud, all that glass, all that held light. Silas thought about the window at the back, and the tannery yard beyond it, and how many steps to the alley, and whether the bolts Marrow had shot were for keeping people out or keeping Silas in.

"Why tell me at all, then?"

"Because I want to make one more sale first." Marrow rose, slow, and went to the back of the shop, to the long cabinet he kept locked and never opened while clients were present. His keys made a sound like teeth chattering. "Vess Adair doesn't want your head, Thackeray. Heads are cheap. She wants what's inside it. The key-memory. Whatever you took off that door all those years ago — and don't look at me like that, I've known since the night you staggered in here twenty-two years old with a hole in your skull where a life used to be, asking me to hold something you wouldn't name. You think I became the richest fence in the Hollow by not knowing my stock?"

He laid something on the counter between them. Not a jar. A vial, dark glass, sealed with black wax, and inside it a darkness that moved the way deep water moves.

"What is it?"

"A map," Marrow said. "Of a kind. A memory of the road east, from a guide who walked it thirty years ago and died rich and stupid. It'll get you past the flats and into the leak-country alive — mostly. It's yours for a price."

"The grief I brought you isn't enough."

"The grief is lovely and I'll take it regardless." He pushed the vial an inch closer. "But passage east costs more than goods tonight. It costs *you*. One extraction. Deep. The oldest thing you've got."

Silas knew what was in the deepest room of their own house. Everyone in the trade did — you learn early which doors in yourself you keep locked, and Silas had kept one locked so long they'd nearly forgotten it was a door. Their mother. What remained of her. Three memories, maybe four, salvaged from before, carried through the sealing and the forgetting and eleven years of theft-for-hire like coals carried in bare hands.

"No," Silas said.

"Yes," Marrow said gently, horribly. "Because in four hours Vess Adair takes it off your corpse, or off your living self with instruments, and gets it free, and I get nothing, and you get nothing, and the road east stays closed. Whereas if we do it now, civilized, professional—" he spread his hands, "—everyone gets what they came for. That's all the Hollow ever promised anybody. That's all it ever delivered."

* * *

They did it standing up, because Silas refused the chair.

Marrow worked the extraction rig with the tenderness of a priest and the speed of a pickpocket — the copper circlet, cold against the brow, the sigil-ink already drying on Silas's temple in lines that itched like ants walking. The Hollow's great mercy was that none of it hurt. That was also its great lie.

"Tell me what you're taking," Silas said, mouth dry. "Exactly. All of it."

"Her hands," Marrow said quietly. "The way she hummed when she worked. The smell of her shawl. Her voice saying your name." He paused, ink-stylus hovering. "That's the deep vein. That's what she asked for, and what I agreed to sell her."

"*Sell* her—"

"She'll want it whole. Grief-sellers always do." He met Silas's eyes. "Last chance to walk out. Though we both know the watchers in the alley have orders to bring you back if you try."

Silas thought of their mother's hands. Flour on the knuckles. The way she'd held a needle. They had built a career on taking exactly these things from other people — had stood in parlors across the valley, circlet gleaming, watching widowers and deserters and frightened children sigh as the worst hours of their lives slid out of them into glass, and had called it mercy, and had taken their fee.

Now the trade had come home, and it wanted the best of what Silas owned.

"Do it," Silas said.

The extraction didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. There was a moment of pressure, like a wave passing over you in deep water, and then Marrow was capping a jar and the jar held a light the color of late afternoon through a kitchen window, and Silas reached for their mother and found —

A shape where she had been. An outline. Hands they knew they should remember and could not picture. A name that meant warmth and produced only the word *warmth*, secondhand, like a definition copied from a dictionary.

"Oh," Silas said. It came out very small. Eleven years in the trade and they had never once heard a client make that sound without understanding it. Now they understood it completely. It was the sound of a house with the furniture gone — rooms still room-shaped, echoing, clean.

"I'm sorry," Marrow said, and meant it, which somehow made it worse. He slid the vial across the counter. "Road east. Thirty-year-old feet, but they knew their business. Stay off the ridgelines after dark; the leaks come down to drink. And Thackeray—" he hesitated, and for one naked moment he looked every one of his sixty years, "—when you find that door. If what's behind it is what I think is behind it. Some prices shouldn't be paid twice."

Silas took the vial. Their mother sat in a jar on Marrow's shelf now, between a soldier's cowardice and a bride's first kiss, labeled in Marrow's neat hand: *THACKERAY, M. — LOT 4471.*

Every relationship in the Hollow was a transaction. Silas had always believed that. Had *relied* on it. And now, walking toward the back window with the vial in their fist and a hole in their chest shaped like humming, they finally understood what the belief had cost: that they had never once been robbed in this valley until tonight, because robbery requires wanting what was taken.

Behind them, Marrow shot the bolts again, one by one, sealing himself in with his inventory.

Ahead, through the fogged glass, the tannery yard lay grey under a starless sky — and at the edge of the lamplight, three figures in the uniform of the Reckoning stepped out from behind the rendering sheds, unhurried, patient, certain.

They hadn't come for the shop.

They'd waited for Silas to come out carrying something worth taking.
