## 6. Road Through the Unmapped

The road east of Corvane wasn't on any map, which was precisely why we took it.

Maps were promises, in the Hollow. This road leads here; this river runs there. For two hundred years the valley had kept those promises by refusing to be anything but what it was—flat, gray, honest, magicless. But the wards had broken three days ago, and already the land was forgetting its own promise.

We saw the first sign an hour past the last farm: a field of barley growing in spirals. Not blown into spirals by wind—*grown*, each stalk curved like a question mark, all of them leaning toward a single point at the field's center where the air shimmered faintly, like heat off a forge that had no fire.

Odile stopped at the fence line and stared. "That's not right."

"No," I said. "It's not."

"It's *beautiful*," she said, and her voice caught on the word as if it surprised her coming out. Three days ago she'd been a woman so hollowed by grief that selling it to me had seemed like the only mercy left. Now there was something else moving behind her eyes—the splinter of my own memory lodged in her extraction, and whatever it was waking up in her.

"We don't touch anything," I said. "We don't drink from anything we can't see the bottom of. And if you start remembering things that never happened to you, you tell me."

"What kind of things?"

"Anything with light in it." I kept walking. "Magic remembers itself, out here. It'll try to lend you some."

She hurried to catch up. "And you'd know? What it lends people? You've walked this?"

"I've never walked this," I said. "Nobody has. That's the point."

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a chapel that had been erased twice over—once when the Hollow swallowed the old country, once again when the founders scrubbed even the memory of scrubbing. The stones remembered anyway, now. Faint letters moved across them like fish under ice, surfacing and diving, never quite legible.

Odile sat close to the cold hearth, working a splinter of wood between her fingers. "Tell me what's in my head," she said. "This thing of yours. The one Marrow said got mixed into my grief."

I fed twigs into the hearth rather than look at her. "A door. Hands drawing a sigil on stone. Maybe thirty seconds of it."

"Whose hands?"

"That's the problem. Mine."

"You don't remember doing it."

"I remember everything I've ever taken from anyone. Every grief, every first kiss, every dying breath I've pulled out of a skull and bottled. That's the trade—you hold what others can't. But I don't remember this. Which means either I sold it and forgot the sale—" I shook my head. "You don't forget a sale. You can't. The taking is the only part that stays."

"So someone took it *from* you."

"Or I took it from myself." The twig snapped in my fingers. "Which is worse."

Odile was quiet a while. Then: "When you pulled my grief out of me—I felt it go. Like a tooth coming loose. Did you feel mine settle into you?"

"For a while. Grief is heavy freight. It passes."

"And now it's passing through me too. The splinter." She looked up, and the firelight did something unkind to her face, sharpening it. "I dreamed about the door last night, Silas. I dreamed I was standing in front of it and I was *afraid*, and the fear wasn't mine, it was older than me, it came up through the floor like water. Whose fear is that?"

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was: probably mine, before I cut it out of myself, before I became the man who could walk away from anything because he'd already paid himself for the leaving.

"Get some sleep," I said instead. "We move before dawn."

* * *

Dawn found us in country that had stopped pretending.

By midmorning the barley spirals had given way to trees that grew their rings on the *outside* of the bark, pale whorls exposed like nerve. A stream ran uphill for fifty yards before thinking better of it. Odile counted her own fingers twice and got nine both times, and neither of us wanted to discuss which finger had gone or where.

And the memories—God, the memories. They hung in the air out here like weather. I'd spent my whole life in the Hollow where remembering was a commodity, bought and sold and weighed to the dram. Out here, remembering was ambient. It pressed against the skin. Twice I caught myself standing still with tears on my face and no idea whose they were—a soldier's, maybe, or a bride's, some dead person's joy leaking up through the ground because the land had finally unclenched enough to feel it.

"Does it hurt?" Odile asked, watching me wipe my eyes. "Holding other people's feelings."

"It's not holding. It's more like—being a riverbed. Things flow through. You shape the channel, that's all. That's the whole craft."

"And who shaped your channel?"

I laughed despite myself. "You've been talking to Marrow."

"Marrow talks *at* people. There's a difference." She scrambled up beside me onto the ridge, breathing hard, and together we looked down at the valley below—and stopped.

It was the old country. Not a ruin of it. The whole thing, laid out under a thin morning haze: rooftops, a market square, a bell tower, smoke rising from chimneys in fat contented columns. Except no smoke smelled like that, sweet as bread, and no bell tower rang without a bell, and the sound drifting up to us was a ringing made of pure insistence, a bell that existed only because the land remembered bells.

"It's not real," Odile whispered.

"It's memory. Leaking back in where the wards broke worst." I should have turned us around then. I knew the terrain was poisoned, knew that a town made of remembering would want to be remembered *by* someone, would reach out and pull. But Vess Adair's riders were somewhere behind us, and ahead was the only route I knew to the door, and so I did what I had always done with dangerous knowledge: I used it instead of heeding it.

"We go around," I said. "Wide. We don't enter. Whatever calls to us from down there—"

Something called to us from down there.

It was my mother's voice. That was the filthy genius of it—it didn't call in any voice I knew, it called in *hers*, saying the one sentence I'd paid Marrow for the privilege of forgetting. I heard it clear across the ridge, warm and specific, and my feet were moving toward it before my mind caught up, and Odile grabbed my arm and dug her nails in and shouted my name, my actual name, the one almost nobody in the Hollow had ever bothered to learn—

—and that was what saved me. Being named. Being somebody's particular concern.

I came back to myself crouched ten feet downslope with no memory of walking there. My cheeks were wet. Odile held my arm in both hands like she was keeping me tethered in a gale.

"There you are," she said. "There you are."

"The ambush," I said stupidly. "There could have been an ambush."

"There still could be." She didn't let go.

* * *

It came at dusk, and it wasn't Vess.

Three of them dropped from the rocks above the trail where the land went narrow—Reckoning scouts, young, half-trained, wearing the gray sash and hungry look of people who had been promised a homeland and told a thief was keeping it locked. No Vess. No discipline. Just need, weaponized.

"Thackeray!" the leader shouted. He couldn't have been twenty. "Vess Adair offers clemency for the key-memory. Give it up and walk away!"

"It doesn't work like that," I called back. "You can't just hand over a memory like—"

He threw something. Not a spell—they barely had those, exiles' children raised magicless in foreign countries, their craft secondhand and furious. He threw a *jar*. Sealed glass, glowing faintly, and inside it a stolen memory, bought or looted from some fence along the refugee road, and when it shattered at my feet the contents poured out and *into* me the way water finds a crack.

It was drowning. Someone's drowning, decades old, black water and burning lungs and the enormous indifference of the deep. I went down under it. I heard Odile scream my name and then I heard nothing, because I was twenty years old and underwater and no one had ever taught me how to die.

Through the roaring I registered the fight in flashes: Odile swinging my pack like a club, screaming words that weren't curses but prices—*you want to know what forgetting costs? I'll show you what it costs*—and then a flash of steel, quick and graceless, and the sound a body makes when it takes a blade.

The drowning-memory receded just enough for me to surface into the world. One scout was running. One lay groaning, disarmed, Odile standing over him with a knife she clearly didn't know how to hold. And Odile herself had a hand pressed to her side, and blood was coming through her fingers, dark and steady.

"He meant it for you," she said. Her voice was very calm, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "The knife. He was aiming past me the whole time."

I crawled to her through the last black rags of someone else's drowning and pressed my hands over hers and felt her life pushing warm between my fingers, and something happened in my chest that I recognized from the trade: the sensation of a memory arriving that isn't yours. Except it wasn't a memory. It was the present tense. It was *this*, her blood, her steadiness, the terrible arithmetic of a stranger choosing my death over hers.

"Why?" I said. "You don't know me. You owe me nothing. I *took* your grief, Odile. I made money off the worst day of your life."

"Yes," she said. She smiled, gray-lipped. "And then you gave it back mixed with something true. Nobody's ever handed me anything true before." Her knees buckled; I caught her. "Besides. Someone has to keep you named, Silas Thackeray. Otherwise you'll wander off after voices forever."

I tore open her coat and packed the wound with the clean linen I carried for bottling, and bound it, and cursed my hands for shaking, hands that had never shaken over anyone. The wounded scout watched from the dirt with wide eyes, and I knelt over him long enough to take something—not his memory, just his message: *the rest of the patrol is a day behind you, and Vess knows the road.*

Then I lifted Odile, all five sharp stubborn feet of her, and carried her up the trail toward the bell tower that wasn't real, because it was the only shelter in a country made of memory, and because the land that remembered everything might remember, just this once, how to heal.

Halfway there, she stirred against my shoulder and said, dreamily: "Silas. When we get to the door. Ask it whose hands drew the sigil."

"I will."

"Ask it *out loud*." Her eyes closed. "Because I think it's going to say something you cut out of yourself so hard you left a scar shaped like a country."

Ahead of us, in the haze, the bell tower began to ring.
