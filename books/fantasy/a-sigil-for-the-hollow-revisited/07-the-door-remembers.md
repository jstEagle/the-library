## 7. The Door Remembers

The door was exactly where the fragment said it would be, and that frightened Silas more than anything else had.

Not because doors were hard to find. Because the fragment—the splinter of their own stolen self, recovered from a widow's grief like a coin fished out of a drain—had been buried under six years of deliberate forgetting, and it had still led them true. Down through the collapsed quarry road. Past the leaning boundary stones where the wards' ruins hummed like struck bells. Into a hollow of black poplars where the ground sloped wrong, where birds circled without landing, where the air tasted faintly of copper and old rain.

Odile walked beside them with her arm bound in strips of Silas's shirt, her face gray but steady. She hadn't spoken for an hour. When she finally did, her voice came out flat as slate.

"I know this place." She stopped. "I've never been here. But I know this place."

"That's the door," Silas said. "It remembers people. Even the ones who were made to forget."

The poplars thinned. And there it was.

A stone slab set into the hillside, taller than two men, its surface carved with a sigil Silas knew the way you know your own handwriting—except they didn't. They'd never seen it before. Their hands had drawn it, the fragment showed that much: hands younger than theirs, steady, certain, tracing each line while the world behind them burned quietly. But the memory stopped at the wrist. Whoever had sealed this door had cut off their own recollection above it, like a butcher trimming fat.

Silas raised their palm to the sigil's center.

The valley held its breath.

Then the memory came home.

* * *

It is harvest season, six years ago, and Silas Thackeray is twenty-three and already the best thief in the Hollow, which means the best thief anywhere, because there is nowhere else.

They are standing in a wheat field outside a town called Vell, and the field is full of the dead.

Two hundred and eleven bodies. Silas counts them because counting is what thieves do; numbers are the only thing you can steal honestly. The dead are farmers and millers and one entire choir that had been practicing for the autumn festival. The men who killed them wore no colors, carried no banners—just soldiers doing what soldiers do when a country wants something a village is standing on. The water beneath Vell. Something about a spring that fed magic into half the nation's wards.

And here is the thing Silas understands, standing in the wheat with blood on their boots: the country will not punish anyone. Because the country will not remember. The government will call it a flood, a fever, an act of God. The families in the next valley will hear vague stories and let them fade. Grief is heavy, and people set down heavy things.

Unless someone carries it for them.

Silas has never done a working this large. It isn't even a working, really—it's a theft on a scale that shouldn't be possible, and that's exactly why it calls to them. Every memory of the massacre, from every mind that holds it: the survivors' screams, the soldiers' orders, the widows' testimonies, the mass graves' locations, the names. All of it. Lifted clean.

They will tell themselves afterward that it was mercy. That the survivors begged for forgetting—that some of them did, that a mother knelt in the wheat and said *take it, take all of it, I can't*. That is true. It is also true that Silas did not ask the others. Did not ask the soldier who wept into his hands by the well, who might have testified. Did not ask the girl hiding in the root cellar who saw everything and might have grown up to accuse. Silas took their testimony the way they took everything: because they could, because no one could stop them, because mercy was such a beautiful word to hide behind.

They seal it all inside the door—a door they build from a hillside and their own genius, a vault for a crime so large it needed a country-sized lock—and then, because even Silas Thackeray cannot carry the knowledge of what they've done, they cut the memory out of themselves too. Above the wrists. A butcher trimming fat.

The last thing the young thief thinks, before the forgetting takes them, is: *no one should have this power. Not even me.*

The first thing the older thief thinks, coming back to themselves against the stone with Odile shouting their name, is: *I lied.*

* * *

Silas slid down the door's face until they were sitting in the dirt. Their hands wouldn't stop shaking.

"Two hundred and eleven," they said. "There were two hundred and eleven people, and I—" They laughed, or something near it. "I stole their funeral, Odile. I stole the one thing the dead had left. Their claim."

Odile crouched in front of them. Her wound had soaked through the bandage, but her eyes were clear. "You gave the living peace."

"I gave the living *nothing*." The words came out of Silas like something torn. "Peace they didn't choose. I decided for two hundred families that their grief was too heavy for them. I decided the soldiers got to sleep at night. I decided—" Their voice cracked. "Do you know what your splinter was? Inside your grief? You kept a piece of me because part of me couldn't bear to forget what I'd done. I robbed myself and even my guilt escaped."

"And now?"

"Now I know why the Reckoning wants the door." Silas looked up at the sigil, at the lines their own hands had drawn. "Vess doesn't want to destroy it. She wants to open it. Two hundred and eleven deaths' worth of memory—it's a weapon. Show a nation its massacre, aim its rage wherever you want. Or worse." They swallowed. "Or she genuinely wants justice. And I'm the criminal standing between her and it."

"You're going to give yourself up."

"I'm going to give *it* back. There's a difference." Silas stood, legs unsteady, and pressed their palm flat to the sigil again—not to open it, but to feel it, the way you'd touch a scar. "Mercy without consent. That's what I built here. That's what the whole Hollow is, if you think about it. One big locked door where a country put everything it couldn't carry."

The poplars shivered. Not from wind.

From footsteps. Many.

* * *

Vess Adair had marched three days through leaking magic to reach this hillside, and she arrived knowing she would find either a door or a grave—possibly both.

What she found was a thief sitting against the stone like a man waiting for his own trial, and a wounded widow standing over him with a farm knife and the posture of someone who had already decided how to use it.

"Silas Thackeray," Vess said. Her voice carried the flat authority of a woman who had hanged a collaborator that week and slept fine after. "You're smaller than the stories."

"Stories always add weight," Silas said. "It's the one thing I can't steal."

She studied him. In the reports he was a monster—the thief who had taken a nation's grief and locked it away. Standing here, hollow-eyed, hands trembling, he looked more like a man reading his own confession. It changed nothing. Justice did not care how sorry the guilty looked.

"You sealed it," she said. "The massacre. You took every memory of Vell and buried them behind your sigil, and two hundred and eleven people became a rumor, and the men who killed them died old and praised." She stepped closer. "My mother was from the valley next door. I grew up on a story about a flood. A *flood*, Thackeray. I am here to open that door."

"I know." Silas rose slowly, palms open. "And I'll help you. On one condition."

Vess almost smiled. "Thieves bargain even at the gallows."

"This isn't a bargain. It's a warning." Silas nodded at the sigil. "I've seen what's inside. All of it. The screaming, the soldiers, the names—I took them from minds that were begging to keep them and minds that were begging to lose them, and I didn't ask either kind. If you open this door and pour that memory back into the world raw, it won't be justice. It'll be a fire. You'll have two hundred and eleven martyrs and a nation ready to burn whoever you point them at. You'll have turned grief into an army." He met her eyes. "I've seen what people do with other people's pain, Commander. It's the only trade I've ever known."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Vess's face that was not command. Doubt, maybe. Or recognition.

"The Reckoning doesn't weaponize the dead," she said.

"No," Silas agreed quietly. "But armies do. And you brought one."

Behind her, at the tree line, the Reckoning soldiers were setting up camp with the practiced efficiency of people preparing for a long occupation. Someone was driving stakes. Someone was building a pyre, just in case.

Odile shifted beside Silas, knife low.

Vess drew a breath, and when she spoke again, her voice was iron again, decision already hardening around whatever doubt had flickered there. "Open the door, Thackeray. Tonight. Before your conscience writes another chapter." She glanced at the sigil—at the lines his own hands had drawn—and something in her expression sharpened. "Because if you don't, my people will carve it open. And they won't be gentle with what comes out."

Silas looked at the door. At the hill that remembered him. Somewhere behind that stone lay two hundred and eleven voices that had waited six years in the dark for someone to listen.

"All right," he said. "But understand what you're asking for. Once it's out, it's in everyone. Including yours."

He raised his hand to the sigil—and felt, beneath the stone, something answer. Not the door. Something behind the door, pressing against it, patient and enormous, like a tide that had been told to wait.

It had learned, in six years of dark, exactly whose fault it was.

And it knew his name.
