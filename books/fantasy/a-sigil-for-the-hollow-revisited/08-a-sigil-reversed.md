## 8. A Sigil Reversed

The wards came down in ribbons.

Silas felt them go the way you feel a tooth loosen—wrong, intimate, a piece of your own architecture giving way. The ruins of the border wards stretched across the pass like the spine of some slaughtered god: black stones taller than houses, each one carved with sigils that had once hummed with refusal. Now they were screaming. Magic poured back into the valley through every crack, and it pooled around Silas's ankles like cold water finding its old channels.

Odile knelt at the base of the first stone, one hand pressed to the bandage on her side, the other holding chalk. Her grief-splinter—the fragment of Silas's own memory lodged inside her for months—glowed faintly under her skin, a lantern behind her wrist. She had drawn the beginning of the reversed sigil there in the dirt, and where her chalk touched stone, the old wards remembered what they'd been for.

"They're coming," she said. No fear in it. Just weather-reporting.

"I know."

Silas could hear them coming too—boots on scree, the wet click of Vess Adair's cane of ironwood striking rock in that patient rhythm she used when she wanted you to know she was in no hurry. Behind Silas, down the slope, the door waited under its skin of turf and stone, and above it hung the whole sealed weight of a country's worst night. Vess didn't want the door destroyed. She'd said as much in the square, with a hanged man swinging behind her. She wanted it *opened*.

And Silas Thackeray was the only keyhole left standing between her and it.

"Whatever happens," Silas said, "you hold the line. Chalk doesn't break line-of-sight. You don't chase me, you don't run to me. You draw."

Odile looked up. Her face was gray from blood loss and stubborn as a mule's. "You're going to fight an army of exiled mages alone."

"No." Silas opened the coat, and the vials caught the lightless sky—dozens of them, glass stoppered with wax, each one holding something someone had paid good money to lose. "I'm going to make them fight themselves."

* * *

The Reckoning came over the ridge in a wedge, thirty strong, and Vess Adair walked at the point like a woman strolling to market. Up close she was smaller than the square had made her—compact, gray-templed, eyes the color of a winter river. The ironwood cane was not a affectation. It was a focus, and it was older than the Hollow.

"Thackeray," she called, when they were fifty paces apart. "Last chance at clemency. Give me the sealing memory and walk away whole. I keep my bargains. Ask anyone I haven't hanged."

"You stole a country," Silas said.

"The country was stolen first." Vess's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about her; nothing about her rose. "By people who thought mercy was theirs to give without asking. You among them, thief. You erased my dead. Now stand aside, or I'll take the memory out of your skull and let the door have what's left."

Silas pulled the first vial.

The trick of extraction—the trade that had fed them for eleven years in a valley where magic couldn't follow—was precision. A memory lifted clean keeps its shape. But Silas wasn't lifting now. They were *giving*, and giving is violence.

They hurled the vial into the wedge's front rank, and it shattered against a shield-charm, and the memory inside came out like smoke with teeth in it.

It belonged to a baker from Marrow's ledger—a man who had paid to forget the year his daughter drowned. And the front-rank mage, a young Reckoning soldier named for a war he'd never seen, caught it full in the face. He stopped mid-stride. His shield-charm guttered. Because suddenly he *remembered* a daughter. Remembered her small cold hand, the pond, the years of waking up reaching for a child who had never been his—and the grief hit him like a wall falling sideways. He sat down in the scree and began to weep for a girl who had never existed anywhere but in a stranger's surrendered pain.

The second rank faltered around him.

That was the thing no one understood about stolen memories until Silas threw them like knives: conviction is just memory wearing armor. Take a person's reasons away—or drown them in someone else's—and the armor comes off by itself.

A fire-caller raised her hands and Silas gave her a widow's memory of burning her husband's letters, page by page, choosing each flame, and the fire-caller's hands dropped because suddenly she knew exactly what it cost to burn something you loved and she could not do it, could not light so much as a candle. An officer bellowed orders and Silas cracked a vial against a stone and gave him twenty years of a coward's life—every small surrender, every time he'd looked away—and the officer's voice failed him mid-word, because the memories fit him like a glove.

Vess watched all of this without moving. Watching, cataloguing. Cane tapping once, twice.

"Clever," she said. "Cruel, too. They'll carry strangers' griefs for the rest of their lives. You've done to my soldiers what you did to the country."

"I'm doing it to people who can put it down," Silas said, pulling another vial. "The dead in that door never got the choice."

Something crossed Vess's face then—not doubt. Calculation arriving somewhere colder than doubt. She raised the ironwood cane, and the air above the wedge went dark with gathered power, and Silas understood that the conversation part was over.

* * *

The first spell broke over the ward-stones like surf.

Silas ran—not toward Vess, but along the line of ruins, low, using the standing stones as cover while the slope behind them turned to glass and smoke. Every few strides, another vial. Not aimed anymore; thrown wide, scattered, a sower sowing grief in a field of soldiers. The wedge lost its shape. Mages sat down mid-charge, weeping, laughing, staring at their own hands as though they belonged to someone else—which, for a few minutes, they did.

But Vess was walking through it untouched.

She had warded herself with something old—older than exile, older than the Hollow. Strangers' memories slid off her like rain off oiled cloth. She pointed the cane and spoke one word, and the ward-stone beside Silas exploded, and Silas went down hard behind the next one with blood in their mouth and their ears ringing like struck bells.

Through the ringing: Odile's voice, high and steady, chanting the reversal. The chalk-line was lit now, running from stone to stone, and where it burned, the leaking magic bent back on itself, feeding the wards instead of flooding past them. The valley's old refusal was being rebuilt by a grieving woman with half a lungful of blood and a stick of chalk.

Vess turned toward her.

Everything in Silas went quiet and clear.

Not fury—fury had come earlier, in the square, watching a collaborator hang. This was past fury, into the flat bright place beyond it, where you stop feeling the weight of the thing in your hand and simply know what it's for.

Silas had one vial left unbroken. They had saved it through the whole fight without knowing why, and now they knew.

It was their own.

The oldest extraction. Eleven years ago, three days after the sealing, Silas had sat in this same pass and cut out the memory of *why*—and had kept it, unspent, unable to destroy it, unable to look at it. The key to the key. Marrow had offered fortunes. Silas had refused, because some part of them had always understood that the moment they sold it, the last honest witness to what they'd done would be gone.

Now they pulled the stopper with their teeth.

"VESS." Their voice carried across the ruined slope. "You want the sealing? Come take it."

She turned. And for the first time, Vess Adair hurried.

She came fast, cane blazing, spells tearing the ground open at Silas's heels, and Silas ran *toward* her—ran into the reach of her power, into the space where a lesser thief would die, and at the last possible instant drove the open vial forward like a dagger and let their own memory go.

It left them the way blood leaves a wound.

And it entered Vess the way truth enters a room where no one has spoken honestly in years.

She stopped. Mid-stride. Mid-spell. The cane's glow died.

Because now she remembered it too: the massacre, yes—but also the survivors, begging a stranger with a thief's hands to make it stop hurting. Also the choice, made alone, without consent, in the belief that mercy was a gift you could give people whether they wanted it or not. Also the price. Also the door closing. Also the sound the victims' claim to justice made when it was locked away—like a country swallowing its own scream.

She remembered that Silas Thackeray had done the most merciful and most unforgivable thing imaginable, and that she, Vess Adair, had spent ten years planning to open that door not to free the truth but to *use* it—to aim the dead like a weapon at everyone who had ever profited from forgetting.

Her soldiers were watching. Weeping their borrowed griefs, they watched their commander stand motionless in the smoking ruin, holding a stranger's sin in her head alongside her own.

"Odile!" Silas shouted. "How long?"

"As long as I last!" Blood on her lips now, chalk still moving. "Finish it!"

* * *

Vess lowered the cane.

For a heartbeat Silas thought it was over—that the memory had done what no army could, that conviction unmade was conviction defeated. Then Vess looked up, and her eyes were wet and absolutely terrifying, and she said:

"You should have let me be righteous."

She swung the cane, and Silas, empty-handed for the first time since childhood, did the only thing left to do.

They grabbed the ironwood with both hands.

Magic discharged through the contact—hers, and the wards', and the leaking centuries of everything the Hollow had swallowed—and Silas held on. Held on the way they'd once held a dying friend's hand, the way Odile held chalk, the way you hold anything that matters more than your own skin. Vess was stronger. Vess had always been stronger. But strength needs somewhere to go, and Silas gave it somewhere: down through their grip, into the chalk-line, into Odile's burning sigil, into the ward-stones, which began—one by one, along the whole ruined spine of the pass—to sing.

Behind Vess, her soldiers were rising. Changed. Carrying griefs that weren't theirs and convictions that no longer fit, and looking at their commander with the particular horror of people who have seen the inside of a thing they served.

"Adair," said the young soldier who had wept for the drowning-girl, and his voice was his own again, and it shook. "What's in the door?"

Vess did not answer. Her grip on the cane was failing. Her eyes were on Silas, and in them was a question Silas couldn't yet answer either: *what are you, if you open it?*

The singing of the stones climbed to a pitch that hurt the teeth. Under the turf below the pass, something vast shifted its weight—the door, feeling its lock turn, remembering it had an inside.

"Odile," Silas called, without letting go of the cane or of Vess's gaze. "I think it's ready."

"It's been ready for eleven years," Odile gasped. "You're the one who isn't."

She was right. The memory was out of Silas's head and loose in the world, and the door would open for anyone now—but opening it meant the massacre-memory went everywhere, into everyone, and there would be no taking it back, no extraction, no sale, no forgetting ever again.

Vess's hand loosened on the ironwood. One finger at a time.

"Do it, then, thief," she whispered. "And live with what the world becomes."

The last ward-stone sang out, and the ground beneath the turf split open like a healed scar finally admitting what was underneath it.
