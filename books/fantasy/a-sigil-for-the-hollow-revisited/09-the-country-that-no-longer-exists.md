## 9. The Country That No Longer Exists

The door was smaller than the memory of it.

That was the first thing Silas understood, standing before it in the dark beneath the hill, with Odile's blood drying on their sleeve and Vess Adair's soldiers fanning out through the cavern like water finding every low place. The door was a slab of grey stone set into the living rock, no taller than a man, no wider than a coffin lid. On its face, the sigil they had drawn years ago—drawn with their own hands, in their own blood, though the blood was long gone and only the groove remained—glowed faintly, like an ember refusing to die.

They had imagined something grand. A gate fit for a country. What they'd made instead was a grave marker.

"You already know how to open it," Vess said behind them. Not a question. Her voice carried in the stone chamber the way voices do underground—flat, close, stripped of echo. "You sealed it. Unseal it."

Silas didn't turn around. They were holding the splinter—the widow's fragment, the piece of themselves they had stolen back across half a valley—and it was warm against their palm like a held bird. Around its edges, the rest of the memory pressed. It had been pressing all night, since the moment they'd stood in this chamber and let the fragment slot home into the hollow where it belonged. The full memory of the sealing wasn't like other memories. Other memories were things you had. This one was a thing that had you.

*Hands drawing a sigil on stone.* Their hands. Younger. Steadier than they'd ever been since.

Behind them, steel whispered against leather. Thirty soldiers, maybe forty. Odile leaned against the cavern wall with her hand pressed to her bandaged side, white-faced, upright anyway. She caught Silas's eye and shook her head once: *Don't do anything stupid.*

Silas smiled at her, which she correctly read as a promise to do exactly one stupid thing.

"Commander," Silas said, still facing the door, "do you know what's behind it?"

"I know what my people say is behind it." Vess came forward until her boots crunched on grit just behind Silas's shoulder. Close enough to drive a knife between their ribs. She didn't. "The old country's memory. Everything the Hollow ate when it was built. Our dead. Our names. Our claim."

"And if I told you that's true," Silas said, "and that opening it will be the worst thing that ever happens to you?"

"I'd say I've been exiled twice and hanged in effigy three times." A dry note entered her voice—not quite humor. "Worst is a competitive category."

Silas turned then. Looked at her properly for the first time since the square, since the hanging, since her voice had said *Silas Thackeray* over a crowd like a curse. Up close, without the uniform's architecture holding her together, she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. Tired the way people get tired of carrying a country on their backs.

"The massacre at Vael Hollow," Silas said. The name came out of their own mouth and tasted like someone else's. "Two hundred and eleven people. Farmers, mostly. A miller's family. A schoolteacher who'd come out from the capital because she believed in teaching children whose accents were wrong." They watched Vess's face as the words landed. "Your Reckoning has been telling that story for twenty years. Martyrs. A crime the world forgot."

"Yes."

"That's not what's behind the door." Silas's throat worked. "What's behind the door is the memory of doing it."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the soldiers stopped shifting.

"The survivors couldn't live with it," Silas went on, because now that the seal was cracking in them, the whole shape of it poured out like floodwater through a broken weir. "Not the victims' families. The ones who survived the massacre itself—the ones who ran, who hid in cellars while their neighbors burned, who lived because they were lucky or fast or because someone else died slower. They came here, into the valley that magic couldn't follow, and they asked me—" their voice cracked, and they hated it, hated the young thief they'd been who'd said yes, "—they paid me everything they had, and I took it. All of it. Every memory of what happened. Not so they could heal. So they could stop remembering that they'd watched. And then I sealed the whole of it—the deed and the watching and the names—behind that sigil, so no one could ever put the country back together and demand justice. I didn't steal your history, Commander. I stole your guilt. And guilt is the only thing that ever makes anyone answer for anything."

Vess hadn't moved. But something in her face had changed—the way ice changes, invisibly, right before it goes.

"You're lying," one of the soldiers said, far back in the dark. "The Reckoning remembers the massacre. We've kept the lists. The names. We—"

"From records," Silas said. "From what your grandparents wrote down before the forgetting reached them. Paper remembers what people can't afford to." They gestured at the door. "Every one of you has a grandmother or a grandfather who stood in that mill yard. You've spent twenty years avenging martyrs. Open this door, and you'll spend the rest of your life being the granddaughter of a woman who threw stones."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere in the rock overhead, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"So open it," Vess said finally.

Silas blinked. "You heard what I—"

"I heard." She stepped closer. In the sigil's faint light her eyes were very bright and very hard. "You think you're offering me a reason not to? You think I want my Reckoning to be a story about innocent victims forever?" Her jaw tightened. "I have spent twenty years watching good people become monsters in the name of the dead. Informants hanged. Villages burned because someone sold bread to the wrong family. I told myself it was justice. Justice for the two hundred and eleven." She looked at the door. "If what's behind there says otherwise—if it says we were never clean—then it's the first honest thing anyone has given me since I was a child. Open it."

And that was the trap of her, Silas thought. That was why she'd hunted them across a valley and hanged a man in a square and burned the safety out of Silas's small life. Not cruelty. Hunger. A hunger for truth so total it would eat her own cause rather than live another day on a lie.

It was also, possibly, the only reason Silas could bear to do this.

"Odile," Silas said quietly.

"I'm here." She pushed off the wall, crossed the chamber, and stood beside them at the door. Her hand found the edge of the sigil-groove, following it the way she must have followed it in the fight above, holding the line while Silas fought. Her fingers were shaking. She did it anyway. "Together," she said. "Whatever comes out."

"It won't stay behind the door," Silas warned her. "A memory released doesn't ask permission. It goes into everyone. Everyone in the valley. Everyone in the world, maybe—I don't know the reach of it. Every survivor's child. Every comfortable person in every town who sleeps well because the Hollow promised forgetting." They swallowed. "Me included. Whatever absolution I built my life on—it goes too."

"Good," Odile said simply.

Silas put both hands on the sigil.

The last time they had done this, they had been someone else—young, certain, paid in coin and gratitude, believing mercy was a gift you could give without asking. Now their hands knew better. Now the grooves knew better. The stone was cold, then warm, then *open*, the way a fist opens, the way a mouth opens to speak after long silence.

Silas reversed the sigil.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The dark held its breath.

Then the door remembered.

It came out of the stone like light comes out of a struck match—but it wasn't light, it was *everything*: smoke and screaming and the specific smell of a burning mill, a child's shoe in the mud of a yard, the sound a crowd makes when it stops being a crowd, two hundred and eleven names spoken in the order they died, and underneath all of it, woven through every thread of it, the watchers—the cellar-hiders, the runners, the ones who lived—their hearts hammering, their doors shut, their eyes at the cracks. It poured outward through the chamber, through the rock, up through the hill, and Silas felt it pass through their own chest like a blade of pure knowing and leave them standing in the wreckage of themselves.

They remembered everything. They remembered choosing to forget.

Silas dropped to their knees on the stone. Around them, soldiers were falling too—not wounded, but *struck*, hands over mouths, some of them saying names, mothers' names, grandmothers' names, saying them the way you say a word you've just learned is rotten. One man began to scream and could not stop. Another sat down where he stood and stared at his own hands as if they belonged to the dead.

Vess Adair did not fall. She stood in the flood of it, spine straight, tears cutting lines down her dusty face, and she *watched*—because that was the whole of her, Silas realized. Watching was all she had ever been able to do, and now she would do it forever, awake.

When she could speak, her voice was ash.

"My grandmother," she said. "Her name was Marta. She was sixteen." A breath. "She watched from the treeline. She lived eighty more years and she never told anyone, and I built an army on her grave-martyr, and she was—" The word wouldn't come. She made herself say it. "She was one of them. One of the ones who watched."

"Yes," Silas said. There was no kindness left in them to soften it with. The truth had taken even that—or perhaps had finally made kindness unnecessary.

Vess looked down at Silas kneeling among her soldiers. Slowly, deliberately, she unclasped the sword at her hip and laid it on the stone. Then she raised her voice, and it carried through the chamber and up the stair and out into the night above, to the campfires and the tents and the thousands who had come seeking a stolen country:

"The Reckoning stands down. By my order. The door is open. Go home—and when you get there, remember what you remember."

* * *

Dawn found Silas sitting on the hillside above the buried door, watching the sky do something it had not done in years.

The stars were coming back.

One by one, then in handfuls, then in rivers—they surfaced through the black like fish rising, and the valley below lay silent under them, every town and farmhouse suddenly lit from above by a truth no one had asked for. Somewhere down there, people were waking weeping. Somewhere down there, a fence named Marrow was learning what he'd been fencing all these years. Somewhere down there, two hundred and eleven graves that had been anonymous mounds would be visited by morning.

Odile lowered herself onto the grass beside Silas, wincing, and said nothing for a long time.

"They'll come for you," she said at last. "Not with armies. With questions. With grief. With *accounts*."

"I know." Silas watched the stars thicken. "Let them come. I'm done deciding what people get to keep." A pause. "But Odile—the trade isn't over. It can't be. People will still need to give memories, hold them, carry each other's. Someone has to teach the difference between taking and receiving."

Odile studied them sidelong. "You're volunteering?"

"I'm *accountable*," Silas said. "There's a difference. It's going to take me the rest of my life to learn it."

Far below, at the mouth of the cave, the first of the survivors emerged into the starlight—an old, old woman, leaning on a soldier's arm, her face wet, her lips moving around a name. She looked up the hill. Found Silas sitting there in the open, hiding from nothing.

And began to climb.
