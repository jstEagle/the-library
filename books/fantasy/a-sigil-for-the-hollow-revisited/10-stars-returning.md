## 10. Stars, Returning

The stars came back on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing about the Hollow had ever been dramatic when it could be ordinary.

Silas was on the rooftop again—the same one where they'd watched the sky go out, because it seemed only honest to watch it come back from the same place. Below them, the town had gone quiet in the way towns do when everyone is pretending not to look up. Doors open. Lamplight doused. A hundred people standing in their yards with their faces tilted like flowers after a long winter.

The first star returned over the eastern ridge, small and white and unremarkable, and Silas heard someone in the street start to cry.

They didn't. They'd done their crying in the weeks since—crying for the massacre-memory they'd carried alone for six years, for the victims whose justice they'd smothered out of mercy no one had asked for, for Odile's wound and their mother's memories sold to a fence and gone wherever sold memories go. There wasn't much left in the tank. What remained felt cleaner.

More stars. Dozens. Then the spill of the Milky Way, faint as spilled milk, then brighter, until the whole sky was the crowded, indifferent thing it had always been before the Hollow taught everyone to look away.

Silas sat down on the cold shingles and let it be beautiful. That was the whole ceremony. No sigil. No door. Just a thief on a roof, watching the sky remember.

* * *

The shop had been Marrow's, once. After everything, the Reckoning's provisional council had no idea what to do with a fence's inventory—four hundred vials of stolen lives, catalogued in Marrow's cramped hand—and so they'd done what councils do: they'd given it to the person who understood it best and washed their hands.

Silas kept the sign. MARROW'S, in flaking gold paint, because painting over it felt like another kind of theft. But inside, everything had changed. The back room, where Marrow had once weighed memory like butcher's meat, was now a parlor with two chairs and a kettle. A hand-lettered card in the window read:

*MEMORIES RETURNED, EXCHANGED, OR GIVEN. NOTHING TAKEN.*

The *nothing taken* had been Odile's addition. She'd argued for it over tea, her left arm still in a sling, her voice carrying the particular authority of a woman who had once paid a stranger to cut her grief out and had learned exactly what that cost.

"People read *returned* and think it's a trick," she'd said. "They read *nothing taken* and know where you stand."

Silas had written it on the card that same night.

The work was slow. That was the point. Under the old economy, Silas could strip a memory in twenty minutes and be out the door before the tea went cold. Now the same work took an afternoon, sometimes a week, because the new method wasn't extraction at all. It was more like translation. You sat with the person. You found the memory together. And then—and this was the part that had taken Silas months to learn, the part they were still learning—you asked.

*Do you want to keep this and lose its teeth? Do you want to give it away entirely, and to whom? Do you understand that once it leaves you, it is no longer yours, and it never comes back the same?*

Consent was a slow, clumsy instrument. Silas had spent a career with the fast one, the one that didn't ask. Their hands still remembered the old speed, and some nights they missed it the way an addict misses the needle, and they told Odile this, because accountability that stayed secret was just vanity wearing a hair shirt.

"Good," Odile would say. "Missing it means you know what you're giving up. That's the only honest kind of giving."

* * *

The queue outside the shop on any given morning was a cross-section of a valley learning to live with itself.

There was the tanner's son, sixteen, who wanted to give back the memory of his father's funeral because he'd sold it during the lean years and had recently learned that the buyer was dead and the memory unfindable. Silas couldn't fix that. Nobody could. But they sat with him for two hours while he raged and then wept and then, finally, asked the question Silas had learned to wait for: *what can I do instead?* The answer, as always, was to make a new memory with the same weight. He left with an apprenticeship at the tannery and no magic involved at all.

There was the woman who had bought a stranger's wedding and wanted to return it to the bride, who turned out to live three valleys over and had no idea what she was missing. Silas traveled with it personally. The reunion took four hours and Silas stood outside in the rain the whole time, because some transactions you don't witness.

And there were the hard ones. The ones who came to give back terrible things. A former Reckoning soldier who'd stood in the square the day Vess hanged the collaborator and wanted to unburden himself of the memory of cheering. Silas refused to take it—not out of cruelty, but because taking it would be the old trade wearing new clothes. Instead they taught him the giving rite: how to carry a memory to the family of the hanged man, how to kneel, how to offer it without demanding forgiveness in exchange. The soldier came back a week later, gray-faced, and said the widow had taken his hands and said nothing, and that it was the worst and best thing he had ever done.

Silas wrote that one down. They were keeping a book, now. Not a ledger—Marrow's ledger had been a list of what people owed. This was a record of what people had chosen. It was thinner than Marrow's ledger and it grew slower, and Silas thought it was the only book in the Hollow worth the paper.

* * *

Vess Adair came to the shop on an afternoon in late autumn, alone, out of uniform, looking smaller than she had in the square.

Silas poured the tea before she said anything. It seemed important to establish that.

"I'm not here for clemency," Vess said. "The council's finished with both of us. You're not a criminal anymore. I'm not a commander. We're just—" she gestured vaguely at the parlor, the vials, the whole strange economy of it, "—whatever this is."

"Accountable," Silas said. "That's the word Odile uses. I've stopped fighting it."

Vess turned a vial in her fingers—one of the few the Reckoning had never claimed, its label in Marrow's hand: *V.A., purchased 4 years prior. Contents: the argument.* She'd bought it back when she was still climbing, still hungry, and had never had the nerve to take it.

"I came to ask if the offer still stands," she said. "The giving rite. For the things I did that can't be undone."

"It stands," Silas said. "But it's not a shortcut. You carry it to them. You kneel. And then you live in the valley where they know, forever, that you know. That's the price. There's no version where you hand it over and walk away clean."

Vess was quiet for a long time. Outside, Odile could be heard arguing cheerfully with a delivery boy about the correct price of lamp oil, her voice fully healed in every way her arm was not.

"I watched you open that door," Vess said finally. "I came to weaponize what was inside and you handed it to everyone instead, and I stood down because I realized I was about to do exactly what you did six years ago—decide for a whole country what it should be allowed to remember." She set the vial down on the table between them, not taking it back. "I hated you for that. For being right in a way I couldn't be."

"I wasn't right," Silas said. "I was late. There's a difference. The right thing would have been asking, six years ago. Everything since has been interest on that debt."

Vess almost smiled. "Then I'll start paying early." She stood, and at the door she paused. "The council wants to formalize what you do here. Licenses, oversight, a charter. Make it a *trade* instead of a black art."

"And?"

"And I told them you'd say no to the license and yes to the oversight. That you'd want the valley watching you forever." She shrugged. "Was I wrong?"

"No," Silas said. "Tell them yes to both, actually. A license I can hang on the wall. It'll remind me how far I've fallen and how far I've climbed, and that those might be the same direction."

* * *

Winter came early that year, and with it the first snow, and with it Odile's first solo giving.

She'd been apprenticed to the work for two months—learning the sitting, the asking, the terrible patience of it—and the widow Maren had requested her specifically, because Maren had been Odile's neighbor for thirty years and trusted her the way you trust weather. The memory was an old one: Maren's sister, drowned in the millrace when they were girls, and the guilt Maren had carried like a stone in her shoe for six decades.

Silas sat in the back room and listened through the wall, which they had promised themselves they wouldn't do, and did anyway.

He heard Odile's voice, low and steady. He heard the long silences she'd learned to leave, the ones where the real work happened. He heard Maren laugh once, wetly, at something. And near the end he heard the words that had taken Silas a year to find and Odile apparently two months:

*"You don't have to give it up, Maren. You could keep it and just... put it down sometimes. Set it on a shelf. Guilt you carry on purpose is different from guilt you carry because you don't know you're carrying it. The first kind can become love, if you let it."*

Silas closed their eyes. Somewhere in the valley, under the snow, the old door sat sealed and silent, and the sigil on it was reversed now, and no one would ever again decide alone what a whole country got to forget. Somewhere in the shop, a woman was learning that the worst day of her life could be held differently. And somewhere in the back room, a thief with no more thefts left in them sat listening to the future of the trade, and it sounded nothing like them, and it sounded better.

* * *

On the last night of the year, Silas climbed the rooftop again. Odile came up behind them, carrying two cups of something warm and alcoholic, and sat down close enough that their shoulders touched.

The stars were all there. Every one. The valley glowed below with ordinary lamplight, and people were setting off the cheap fireworks the tanner's son had started making, and somewhere Vess Adair was kneeling in a stranger's doorway offering up her worst self, and somewhere the widow Maren was sleeping better than she had in sixty years.

"Any regrets?" Odile asked. "About staying? You could have gone over the ridge. Started clean somewhere the name Thackeray means nothing."

Silas considered it honestly, the way they now tried to consider everything.

"Every day, a little," they said. "And every day, less. Forgiveness isn't a door, Odile. I kept thinking it was—a thing you open once and walk through. It's not. It's a trade, like everything else. You pay a little every day. You answer to people. Forever." They lifted their cup toward the sky. "Turns out that's cheaper than forgetting."

Odile clinked her cup against theirs. "To the trade, then."

"To the trade."

Below them, the fireworks bloomed and faded, and above them the stars burned on, patient and countless, keeping watch over a valley that had finally stopped looking away—and Silas Thackeray, who had once stolen the sky itself out of other people's heads, sat beneath all of it, owing everything, paying daily, and for the first time in their life, home.
