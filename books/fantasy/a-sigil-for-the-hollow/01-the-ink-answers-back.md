## 1. The Ink Answers Back

The petition had been waiting on Freya's desk since before dawn, folded twice and weighted with a river pebble the way all petitions to Hollowmere's temple were weighted—so that the gods, should they stoop to read them, would know the prayers were heavy enough to stay put.

He had copied four hundred and six of these in his seven years at the scriptorium. He knew their shapes the way a ferryman knows ripples: the widow asking after her husband's soul, the farmer asking for rain in measured quantities, the young mother asking that her child's fever break before the border stones could decide the fever belonged to something else. None of them would be answered. Everyone in the valley knew this. The gods of Hollowmere had gone quiet three generations ago—not dead, the elders insisted, only *distant*, the way a father is distant who has gone to another country and stopped writing. The petitions were copied anyway, in careful ink on careful vellum, and filed in the archive beneath the temple where the damp got into everything and softened the letters year by year until even the prayers forgot what they had asked.

Freya did not mind the work. That was the truth of him, the small unglamorous truth: he liked the copying. He liked the hour before the other scribes arrived, when the scriptorium smelled of cold tallow and cut reeds and the light through the east windows came down grey as milk. He liked being no one in particular in a room full of no one in particular. Invisibility was not a curse to Freya Okonkwo; it was a coat he wore well, one the temple had given him along with his debt—the debt that had brought him here at fourteen, orphaned and owing, and kept him here ever after, accruing interest the elders recalculated each spring with the serene arithmetic of men who owned the ledger and the pen.

He dipped his quill. He began, as he had begun four hundred times:

*To the Quiet Ones, keepers of the valley, hearers-who-do-not-hear: I, Marta Ellswicke of the lower terraces, ask—*

And then his hand stopped being his hand.

It happened without drama, which was the terrible part. No shudder went through the room. The candle did not gutter. His fingers simply continued moving while something else moved *through* them, the way water continues through a pipe after someone upstream has turned the tap. Freya watched his own wrist flex at an angle it had never learned, watched the letters come out taller than his, older, with strokes that dragged like feet through ash.

*I hear you, Marta Ellswicke.*

The nib scratched. The sound of it filled the whole scriptorium.

*Your husband crossed the stones in the ninth winter carrying what he should not have carried. Ask the ferrywoman. She remembers him. She remembers everything, that one. Tell her Veyren says the river was colder than he let on, and worth it.*

Freya tried to pull his hand back. It did not resist him exactly—it simply declined, politely, the way a door declines to open when the house has decided otherwise. Ink pooled and fed out into words. The handwriting shifted again, and this second hand was different from the first: narrower, quicker, with an impatience in its downstrokes.

*And to the one holding the pen,* it wrote. *Little warden. You read petitions to us for seven years and we heard every one. Did they tell you we were deaf? We were bound. There is a difference, and you above all people are going to need to know it.*

The quill lifted. Hovered. Dropped a final line in yet a third hand—heavy, deliberate, each letter set down like a stone laid on a cairn:

*The seal is thinning. Come to the border stones before the next dark moon. Bring nothing. Trust the blind.*

Then his hand was his own again, and the silence that rushed back into the room was so complete he could hear the pebble-weight trembling against the desk where his elbow had knocked it.

Freya sat very still.

Wonder came first—that was honest, that was what he would remember afterward. Wonder like heat in the chest, like the first swallow of plum brandy. *We heard every one.* Seven years of copying grief into a damp archive, and somewhere under the weight of it all, something had been listening. He looked at the page—at *his* page, at the petition now swollen with three alien hands—and felt, briefly, chosen. Seen. After seven years of being no one in particular, he had been addressed by name. *Little warden.* Whatever that meant. Whatever that—

The wonder curdled almost audibly, milk left out in thunder weather. Because *little warden* meant they knew him. Knew him specifically, knew something about him that Freya himself did not know, and things that knew you were things that could find you, and things that could find you were things the temple would want to question, and the temple questioned no one gently. He thought of Brother Aldous, who had laughed during silent prayer twenty years ago and been sent to the terraces to dig graves until his hands forgot how to hold anything else. He thought of the debt ledger, and his name in it, and how a man whose name lived in someone else's book was never entirely free.

Footsteps in the corridor. The pre-dawn quiet breaking.

He had perhaps ten heartbeats. Long enough to do something foolish. He considered tearing the page out, wetting it, feeding it to the brazier—but the words had been written in god's ink through god's hand, and some animal part of him understood that destroying them would be like tearing up a letter from the dead. Instead he blotted the page hastily, folded it inside the finished copy of Marta Ellswick's petition, and set the pebble back on top, and told himself—with the practiced fluency of a man who had been lying to authority since he was fourteen—that no one would look twice at a scribe's work before filing.

Elder Cassian looked twice at everything.

*

The summons came at noon, which meant they had known since morning, which meant someone watched the scriptorium more closely than Freya had ever allowed himself to believe. He walked the long colonnade to the chapter house with his hands loose at his sides and his pulse knocking in his throat, past novices who did not meet his eye—which itself was answer enough. Invisible men were invisible because everyone agreed not to see them. Sometime between dawn and noon, the agreement had lapsed.

Cassian sat behind the reading table with the confiscated page flat before him, squared to the table's edge with a precision that frightened more than anger would have. Two other elders flanked him. The high priest's chair stood empty at the head of the room, which was worse than if it hadn't.

"Transcribe it again," Cassian said quietly. "From memory. Everything your hand wrote that was not yours."

"I don't—" Freya began.

"Everything."

So Freya gave it back, word by word, watching the old man's face for horror and finding instead something far more unsettling: recognition. Cassian listened to *little warden* and did not blink. He listened to *come to the border stones* and his jaw tightened around something swallowed. When it was done he sat in silence for the length of three candles' flicker.

"The page is confiscated," he said at last. "You will speak of this to no one, including the ferrywoman, should her name occur to you." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "Your debt has been reassessed. The temple finds that your service has been undervalued these seven years. The figure is doubled."

"Doubled." Freya's voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Elder, I've served faithfully. What does the ink have to do with my—"

"Everything," Cassian said, and rose, gathering the page with both hands as though it were a wound he was bandaging. "The ink has to do with everything. You are dismissed."

At the door, the old man spoke once more, not unkindly, which was somehow crueler.

"Freya. If a blind woman speaks to you—if she so much as turns her face toward you in the street—you will report it within the hour. Do you understand?"

He said he understood. He lied the way he had always lied, smoothly, invisibly.

But walking back across the courtyard in the flat afternoon light, past the border hills standing pale and patient beyond the temple roofs, one thought kept time with his footsteps, over and over, in a dead god's dragging hand:

*They doubled the debt because they are afraid of what I am. And Sefa—the blind ferrywoman—they named her before I did.*

He had never mentioned her name aloud in his life.

* * *

That night he did not sleep. At the grey edge of morning he took the river path downstream, past the last lamplit houses, to the ferry landing where the mist came off the water thick as wool. The boat was there. So was Sefa, sitting straight-backed at the tiller, her milky eyes already fixed—not sightlessly, but *precisely*—on the spot where he would step out of the reeds.

"You're late, little warden," she said. "Get in. We have until dark moon, and the river is long."
