## 2. What the Border Stones Remember

Freya did not sleep.

The confiscated page had been taken from him at dusk, carried out of the scriptorium between Elder Maren's thumb and forefinger as though it were a dead thing that might still bite. *Burn it,* the elders had murmured to one another. *Burn the ink. Burn the pen.* But they had not burned Freya's hand, though he had offered it to them, palm up, in the cold light of the reliquary lamps. They had only looked at it for a long time—looked at *him*—and then Elder Maren had said, very quietly, "Your debt has been recalculated," and that was all.

Recalculated. As if a soul were an arithmetic problem. As if the sum could be made larger by decree.

Now he lay on his cot in the copyists' dormitory and listened to the valley breathe. Rain came off the fells in long grey sheets, and beneath it, beneath everything, he could feel something new: a pulse at the edge of his awareness, patient and vast, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. It had been there since the moment his pen had moved without him. The god—the dead god, the drowned god, whatever name the temple had struck from its rolls three hundred years ago—was not finished with him.

*The stones remember,* the whisper had said, in the voice that was also his own handwriting. *Come where the valley ends. Come where your grandmothers stood.*

Freya rose before the bell. He dressed in the dark, took nothing but his satchel and the stub of red wax the copyists used for sealing errors, and slipped past the dormitory door where Brother Callow slept with his mouth open, dreaming temple dreams. In the corridor outside, the frescoes watched him go—saints and wardens, the painted history of Hollowmere's founding—and for the first time Freya noticed that every warden in the mural had dark skin and close-cropped hair. Every one. His own face, multiplied across four centuries of paint, holding staffs and keys and standing at the feet of gods who no longer answered.

He had copied prayers beside these walls for eleven years and never once seen himself in them.

The rain thinned as he climbed. By the time the temple bells rang prime behind him, muffled by distance and mist, Freya stood among the border stones.

There were nine of them, great leaning monoliths ringed with moss, arranged in a broken arc along the ridge where the valley met the high moor. Pilgrims came here once a year to touch them and be reminded that magic ended at this line—that beyond the stones lay the ordinary world, flat and safe and godless. That was what the catechism said. *The stones keep us humble. The stones keep us whole.*

Freya laid his palm against the nearest stone and felt the truth like a slap.

Heat. Not warmth—*heat*, the deep heat of a forge banked under ash, humming up through the moss and into his bones. And threaded through the heat, writing itself into his skin letter by letter, came words in no alphabet the temple taught:

*You are the ninth generation. Your blood opened this seal. Your blood was meant to keep it.*

"I don't understand," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on the wet air. "Understand *what*?"

The stone answered in images instead of words, pressing them into him the way a signet presses into wax:

A door. A door of pale wood set into the living rock of the valley's deepest hollow, carved with a sigil that hurt to look at—a sigil of unwriting, of erasure, drawn so that the eye slid off it like rain off glass. And around the door, a country. Green roads, towers of woven reeds, rivers that ran silver with unrecorded names. A country so full of magic that the bound gods had fled into it when the other gods came to cage them, and the caging gods had answered not with war but with a map-maker's cruelty: they erased the country. Unwrote it from every chart, every chronicle, every memory that touched the world outside. And then, because a hidden thing must still be *hidden*, they built the valley over it like a lid. Hollowmere. The border stones. The temple. A prison shaped like a parish.

And the wardens of the lid—Freya saw their faces now, generations of them, his jaw, his mother's brow, his grandmother's hands—had been given one duty: *copy the prayers*. Copy them, and copy them, and copy them, ten thousand pages of petition and praise, because every prayer written inside the seal fed the lock. The temple had not stopped believing in answers. The temple *was* the answer—an answer written so large that no one inside it could read it anymore.

"No." Freya stepped back from the stone, and his heel caught a root, and he sat down hard in the wet grass and laughed, or wept, or both. "No, you're wrong. They're good people. Elder Maren gave me boots my first winter. Sister Ivetta taught me my letters when—" His voice failed. "I owe them."

*Yes,* the god said, gentle now, terrible now, speaking through the stone and through the rain and through the small voice in his own chest. *You owe them. That is the leash. Debt is the oldest chain, little scribe. They let you buy your freedom page by page, and every page you wrote made the door heavier. Eleven years. Do you know how many prayers that is?*

"Four thousand two hundred and six," Freya whispered. He knew exactly. He had counted them the way prisoners count days.

*And every one a rivet in the lid.*

He knelt there a long time, letting the rain find the back of his neck, while somewhere below the temple bell rang terce and the valley went about its morning as if it were real, as if it had ever been anything but a beautiful, deliberate cage. He thought of Brother Callow snoring. Of Sister Ivetta's arthritic hands. Of the children who ran messages through the cloister and had never once, in all their lives, seen anything happen that the temple did not first permit.

They didn't know. Most of them couldn't know. Which meant the debt was not only his—it was theirs too, inherited, unpayable, a chain passed down like a christening gown.

And the elders *did* know. Elder Maren had looked at his open hand and doubled the sum without blinking.

When Freya finally stood, his legs were stiff and his mind was made of two pieces grinding against each other, loyalty and truth, and neither would win. But the god had left him one more thing, pressed into the moss beneath his knee like a coin left in a loaf: a direction. West, past the ninth stone, down to the river where the mist never lifted.

Where someone was waiting who could take him across.

---

Sefa had been blind since before she could remember seeing, which was her way of saying she had never bothered much with eyes at all. She kept her ferry at the western shallows, poled travelers across the Greywater for copper coins and gossip, and everyone in Hollowmere agreed she was odd but harmless, which was how she liked it. Harmless people were told things.

She had known this morning would come for thirty years. She had felt it the night Freya Okonkwo was born, when the river had run backward for the length of a heartbeat and every fish in the Greywater had turned to face upstream. Blind, she had simply smiled and gone on mending her nets.

Now she heard footsteps on the shingle—young, bare-headed, walking like a man whose floor had just become water—and she set down her pole before he even spoke.

"You'll want the crossing," Sefa said. "And you won't have copper."

Freya stopped. The mist between them moved like something alive. "How did you—"

"The river told me. Rivers talk, boy; it's people who've forgotten how to listen." She tilted her head, and her white eyes found him with unsettling accuracy. "I ferried your grandmother once. Same look on her face. Half of her wanting to run back to the temple and half of her already gone. Which half is winning?"

Freya's throat worked. "I don't know yet."

"Honest. Good. Liars drown on my crossing." She pushed the boat into the shallows and held it steady against the current, which pulled westward, always westward, toward the place where the maps went blank. "One thing before you step in. Once we cross the line the stones mark, you'll be outside the seal. Things will see you. Old things. Hungry things. Some of them loved your family. Some of them hated your family enough to wait three hundred years for a descendant." She paused. "And the high priest knows you're missing by now. Veyran doesn't send wardens. He sends offers. Whatever he promises you—freedom, forgiveness, your name struck clean from the debt books—remember that a man who builds a prison always keeps the prettiest cell for bargaining."

"How do you know all this?"

Sefa smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Because I was the last person your grandmother spoke to before she walked into that country and never came out. Now get in, scribe. The mist is thinning, and thinning mist means company."

Far upstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of horses.

Freya stepped into the boat.
