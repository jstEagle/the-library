## 3. A Country With No Name

The river did not want him.

Freya understood this the moment Sefa's flat-bottomed ferry slid away from the reed bank and out into the current, because the water seemed to lean against the hull, patient and enormous, like a hand pressing down on a lid. Mist closed behind them. The temple's bells had stopped ringing an hour ago—no, not stopped; they had changed. What he'd once heard as calls to prayer now rang with a different cadence entirely, a rhythm he recognized from the confiscated page: *come home, little scribe.* The wardens were already on the water somewhere behind them, and every bell in Hollowmere was singing them toward his position like a choir of hounds.

"Row," Sefa said. She sat at the stern with her hands folded in her lap, her milky eyes fixed on nothing, or on everything. "And don't look back at the stones when we pass them. They remember faces."

"The stones are a mile inland."

"Their memory isn't." She tilted her head, listening to something beneath the river's voice. "They'll be watching through me if I let them. So I won't."

He rowed. His arms burned. The mist thickened until the far bank was a rumor and the near bank was gone, and Freya found himself thinking, absurdly, about ink—about how the temple taught that a thing unwritten was a thing unmade, and how he had spent nine years copying prayers into ledgers so that the gods would have something to read in their long silence. Nine years of debt for his father's funeral rites. Nine years of being invisible: a scribe no elder could name, a boy in gray robes whom even the wardens' eyes slid past. Invisibility had been his shelter. Now it was ending, and whatever came next would see him clearly.

"There," Sefa said.

Ahead, where there should have been only more mist and more river, stood a line of things that hurt to look at. Not border stones—those had been left behind, humming faintly with the seal's old authority. These were different: standing stones worn smooth as river pebbles, each one carved with script that kept rearranging itself when he tried to read it, letters flowing like water finding its level.

"What do they say?" he whispered.

"Nothing. That's the point." Sefa rose, steadying herself against the pole she'd never used. "They're the *unwritten* marks, child. Every name that was struck from the record, every road pulled off the maps, every god the valley bound—they all went somewhere. It went *here*. A country with no name can't be found, but it also can't be erased twice."

She pushed the ferry forward with one hand, an old woman's gesture that somehow moved the whole boat, and the stones parted around the hull like a curtain of teeth.

Crossing felt less like travel and more like being *edited*.

One moment Freya was soaked, cold, aching—the river still dripping from his sleeves—and the next the air was dry and warm and smelled of cut hay and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something like libraries and loam. He stood on a road. Not a forgotten road in the way Hollowmere understood forgetting—not overgrown, not ruined—but a road that had never been written down, and so had never been obliged to end. It ran ahead through country that made his breath catch: fields of wheat the color of late honey, orchards heavy with fruit no temple ledger had ever taxed, hills folded green and blue into a distance that seemed, impossibly, to go on further than Hollowmere's whole valley wide.

And the dead were everywhere, walking calmly among the living.

An old man passed him carrying a scythe, nodding pleasantly. Beside him walked a young woman in a style of dress three centuries out of fashion, her hand resting on the old man's shoulder as though they'd been keeping company for years. Neither cast a shadow. Neither needed to.

"Don't stare," Sefa said quietly. She had come through with him—or something had come through wearing her shape; her eyes were still blind, but now they reflected light like coins at the bottom of a well. "The unrecorded dead aren't ghosts. Ghosts are what happens when a death is written down wrong. These were never written at all. They just... stayed."

"They stayed here? All this time?"

"Where else? Your temple wrote the world so tightly there was no room left for anything loose." She smiled without warmth. "This is the loose part."

They walked. The road took them through villages that appeared and dissolved like thoughts—a market square where vendors sold memories by the spoonful; a chapel dedicated to a god whose name, carved above the door, was simply a blank space everyone bowed to respectfully; a field of standing graves with no names on them, tended lovingly by children who might or might not be alive. Everything here was vivid precisely because no record held it still. Colors were brighter. Grief was sharper. Joy arrived without warning, like weather.

Freya felt his own name loosening in his mouth. When a child asked who he was, he opened his lips and found the word *Freya* sitting strangely on his tongue, like a borrowed coat.

"It's starting," Sefa observed. "You crossed carrying a debt. Debts are heavy with writing. This land is reading you."

"Then I should hurry."

"Hurry, yes." But she caught his sleeve, and her coin-bright eyes found his with terrible precision for a blind woman. "Listen to me before you go up that hill. There's a door at the top of it—the Door of the Unwritten, the hinge your valley was built around. And there will be someone waiting for you when you reach it. Someone who loves you, in his way. Someone who would rather see you sealed than free."

"High Priest Aldous."

"He's already crossing. The bells carried him." Sefa released his arm. "When he offers you the bargain—and he will—remember that survival and truth are both real prices, child. The question is only which one you can bear to pay."

* * *

The hill was taller than it had looked, or perhaps distance meant nothing here. Freya climbed through orchards where forgotten apples fell upward, past a shepherd whose flock were clouds grazing on grass, and with every step he felt the erased country taking his measure. By the time he reached the crest, winded and sweating, he could no longer remember his mother's face—only that he had loved her, which somehow remained when everything specific drained away. He clung to that. Love without detail. It was enough to climb by.

The Door stood alone at the summit in a circle of white stone.

It was not grand. That was the first shock—that after all the whispers, all the god's handwriting moving beneath his pen, the Door of the Unwritten was a plain wooden door set in a plain frame, standing upright on the hilltop with nothing behind it but sky. Its wood was scarred and mended a hundred times. Its hinges were iron, rusted gentle with age. Only the surface of it moved: grain shifting like slow water, knots opening and closing like eyes, and across it, in handwriting Freya knew intimately—because it was his own, and it was not—

*OPEN ME AND I WILL OPEN YOU.*

Behind him, boots on grass. Measured, unhurried, familiar.

"Freya."

High Priest Aldous walked the last stretch of hill alone, his gray robes immaculate despite the crossing, his face lined with something that was not quite anger and not quite grief. He stopped ten paces away, and Freya saw that the old man's hands trembled—saw, too, the small leather case he carried, the one used for sealing oaths.

"You've come a long way to die on an unnamed hill," Aldous said.

"To open a door."

"That door destroyed a civilization." The high priest's voice stayed level, but his knuckles whitened around the case. "You think we built the seal out of cruelty? Before the binding, magic poured through this valley like blood from a wound. People burned from the inside. Children woke up as weather. We sealed the Door because our ancestors begged us to, and we have kept it sealed for four hundred years while the rest of the world forgot why. You would undo all of that—for a dead god's handwriting and an old woman's riddles?"

"I would undo it for the truth," Freya said. "You doubled my debt the day the ink answered. You didn't ask what the god said. You didn't ask *why it answered me*. You just reached for the leash."

Aldous flinched. Then, softly, terribly gently, he made the offer Freya had known was coming since the river:

"Walk away. Resign the seal—press your palm to the Door and speak the closing words, and it stays shut for another century. Do that, and I burn every ledger myself. Your father's debt, your debt, nine years of gray robes—all of it gone. No wardens. No valley. You walk out of Hollowmere a free man with a clean name, and you live wherever you like, however you like, for as long as you like." He stepped closer. "That is not a small thing I'm offering, boy. That is *everything you have ever wanted*. Freedom. A life. A self."

And it was. That was the horror of it—the offer wasn't poison wrapped in sugar. It was real. Freya felt it move through him like warmth: the image of himself unbound, walking roads with a name that belonged to him, sleeping without debt, waking without ledgers. He wanted it so badly his chest ached with it. Survival. Selfhood. The ordinary miracle of being someone.

All he had to do was keep the largest prison in the world locked, and lie about it forever.

His hand drifted toward the Door—not to open it, but because the wood looked warm, and he was cold, and some treacherous part of him wanted to touch anything honest before he chose.

Beneath his fingertips, the grain rippled. And across the Door's surface, in his own impossible handwriting, new words surfaced like drowned things rising:

*IT KNOWS YOUR NAME IS ALREADY LOOSE. IT ASKS ONLY WHAT YOU WILL BE WHEN NOTHING HOLDS YOU.*

Aldous saw the words too. Whatever he read in Freya's face made him reach into the leather case and draw out the sealing stylus—iron and bone, ancient, humming with the same authority as the border stones.

"Last chance, scribe."

The Door, very softly, began to breathe.
