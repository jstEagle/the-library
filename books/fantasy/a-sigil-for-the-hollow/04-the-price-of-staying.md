## 4. The Price of Staying

The Door of the Unwritten was smaller than Freya had imagined.

He had carried the image of it across the whole of the erased country—a vast gate, a mountain split open, something worthy of the silence that surrounded it. Instead it stood in a hollow between two hills that no map had ever named: a door of grey wood, iron-banded, set into nothing at all. No wall behind it. No frame holding it. Just a door standing upright in the grass, patient as a gravestone, with the wind moving freely through the space where a wall should have been.

Sefa stopped ten paces from it and would come no closer. Her white eyes turned toward him, and for once she did not seem to be looking at anything at all.

"It's listening," she said.

Freya's hand went to his satchel, where the god's pages lay folded—the handwriting that had answered him in the dark of the scriptorium, the ink that had moved without him, the voice that had whispered *warden* and *daughter of wardens* and *come home*. Behind him, somewhere down the long slope of unrecorded hills, the temple wardens were still searching, their lanterns like fallen stars. And behind them, further back than lanterns could reach, High Priest Aldous Vane rode with an offer folded in his breast like a knife: *reseal the door, boy, and your debt dies with it. Walk away free.*

He had not answered. He had run instead, which was its own kind of answer.

Now he stood before the door, and the door stood before him, and neither of them moved.

"I'm here," he said quietly. "I don't— I don't know what I'm supposed to do."

His own hand answered. Not the pen this time—he had no pen, no ink, nothing but skin—but the old scar across his palm from the first night in the scriptorium began to warm, then to ache, then to shine faintly gold, as if something beneath his skin had been waiting all his life to be asked.

The door opened inward on darkness, and the darkness spoke.

*Look first, warden. Always look first. That is what your bloodline forgot, in the end—they sealed us out and never once looked at what they were sealing in.*

"What do you mean?"

*Open me, and see.*

Freya stepped forward. The threshold was cold as well-water. And when he crossed it—not into the dark but into a kind of seeing—the valley unfolded before his mind's eye, all of Hollowmere at once, every hearth and field and border stone, and threaded through it all he saw the seal: a great knot of binding laid over the land like a net over a sleeping animal, holding back not only the gods but everything they were. Magic itself. The raw current of the world.

And he saw what would happen if he simply opened the Door of the Unwritten wide.

The current would flood in. All of it, at once, after three hundred years of drought.

He saw the miller's wife in her kitchen, whose grandmother had taught her that bread rose because of yeast and luck and nothing else—and the magic pouring through her like water through a cracked jug. He saw the children in the temple school reciting prayers they did not believe, their small bodies suddenly full of a power that had never learned gentleness. He saw Sefa's village, the ferry crossing, the orchards, the graves. Three hundred years of forgetting, and forgetting had made them fragile. They had been living in a house with the windows painted shut, and he was about to shatter every window at once, and the light would not comfort them. It would burn them hollow.

"They'll die," Freya whispered. His voice sounded very small inside the seeing. "If I open it, they'll die."

*Most of them,* the darkness said, without cruelty. *Truth, when it arrives too fast, is indistinguishable from violence. Your ancestors knew this. It is why they built slow walls around a sudden thing.* A pause, and then, softer: *But there is another way, warden. There has always been another way. A seal must have a keystone. Yours was blood. It can also be will. Break the binding from within—unpick it thread by thread, let us return the way rain returns, slowly, season by season—and the valley will learn again before the river rises.*

"Then why ask me to open the door at all? Why bring me here?"

*Because a lock cannot be picked from outside. Because we are not permitted to touch our own chains. And because the unpicking requires a hand that belongs to the valley and does not—one willing to stand at the seam while it opens, and give the seam something to hold onto while it learns to close gently behind us.*

Freya understood then. He understood it the way one understands falling—not gradually, but all at once, with the ground already rising.

"What will it cost?"

The darkness was quiet for a long moment. When it answered, it was almost gentle.

*A name. One name, freely given, to bind the seam. The valley will keep everything else—their memories, their dead, their gods returning like spring. But you, Freya Okonkwo: the records will forget you. The temple ledger will show blank pages where your debts were written. Your mother's face will turn toward the space where you stood and feel only a warmth she cannot explain. You will walk out of every memory you ever lived in, and no one will grieve, because grief requires a shape, and yours will be gone.*

Behind him, distantly, came the jingle of harness and the mutter of men. The wardens had found the hills. He had perhaps a quarter of an hour before High Priest Vane stood on this same grass and made his offer again with soldiers at his back.

Freya thought of the scriptorium. Of twenty years of copying prayers into a silence that never answered, of being the boy nobody noticed, the debt nobody discussed, the ink-stained ghost of the temple. He had spent his whole life invisible, and he had hated it, and now he was being offered invisibility eternal, and the terrible joke of it nearly made him laugh aloud.

Except this time it would be different. This time the invisibility would not be a cage. It would be a door held open for everyone else.

He thought of his mother's hands kneading bread. He thought of Sefa, who alone might remember, because blind eyes saw differently—and even she would lose him, probably, or keep only a warmth.

"Do it," he said. "Unpick it. I'll hold the seam."

*You are certain?*

"No." He laughed, wet-eyed. "But certainty is just fear wearing good clothes. Let me through."

* *

It did not hurt, exactly. It hurt the way birth hurts, perhaps—which is to say someone else would have to describe it, because the one being born is too busy becoming.

The gods came back the way dawn comes back: not in an explosion but in a gradual turning up of light. Freya stood at the seam with his shining scar pressed against the door's cold wood, and he felt the great net of the seal loosen strand by strand beneath his fingers, and through each gap the world poured in like water finding an old channel. The forgotten gods returned to Hollowmere one by one—first the small ones, the spirits of hearth and hedgerow, slipping home like cats through a window left ajar; then the older ones, the river-mothers and the hill-fathers, waking slow under fields that had slept on top of them for centuries. The valley would learn. The miller's wife would find her bread rising strangely light and call it a blessing and not be wrong. The children would grow up with magic seeping into them the way language seeps in, gently, before anyone thinks to be afraid.

And Freya unraveled.

He felt it begin at the edges. The ledger in the temple scriptorium, three hundred miles of entries, and the lines bearing his name going pale, then grey, then blank. His mother, stirring a pot at dusk, pausing with a spoon in her hand, frowning at nothing, then humming and carrying on. The debt he owed dissolving into a debt no one remembered anyone owing. His own reflection growing unfamiliar—not changing, but *unattended*, like a room no one visits.

He held on anyway. He held the seam until the last strand of the old binding dissolved, until the Door of the Unwritten swung wide and empty and harmless, until the gods were fully home and the valley breathed its first easy breath in three hundred years.

Only then did he let go of his own name.

It went softly, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is spent. He felt the last of it leave—the *Okonkwo*, the *Freya*, the little boy who had once copied prayers in a silent temple—and in the space where it had been, there was not emptiness but something wider, a warmth with no shape, belonging to everyone.

* *

Sefa found the door standing open in the hills and no one beside it.

She stood a long time in the wind, her white eyes full of a morning brighter than any she had ever seen, and magic moved through the grass like weather coming home. Somewhere far off, she knew, a high priest was shouting orders that no longer mattered; somewhere nearer, a woman was setting out bread to cool and humming a song about a river goddess she'd suddenly begun, quite naturally, to believe in.

At her feet, on the doorstep of the unwritten country, lay a single sheet of paper, weighted with a stone against the wind.

In handwriting she did not know and yet somehow recognized the way one recognizes a voice heard once in childhood, it read:

*Tell them the gods came back on their own. Tell them no one opened the door.*
*Tell them whoever did is fine.*
*S.*

She smiled, though tears ran freely down her face, and folded the page into her coat, and turned back toward the valley—where, in the scriptorium, a blank page on a writing desk had just begun, very faintly, to fill with golden ink.
