## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tomorrow

The mountain had a way of making liars out of maps.

Astrid Halloran stood at the head of the caravan with one boot on a switchback stone worn smooth by a century of smugglers' wheels, and consulted a map they had drawn themselves — twice, in ink that didn't run, because the rain on Thornfell's western face came down sideways and ate ordinary paper like a hungry thing. The map said the road bent left. The mountain said otherwise. The mountain, as always, was winning.

"Left," called Brannick from behind, his mule-train snaking down the switchback like beads on a greased string. "You said left."

"I said the map says left." Astrid didn't turn around. "The map is an optimist. We're going right."

"You drew the damn map."

"I did." They let the wind take their coat out behind them, felt it snap at their shoulders like something eager to be gone. "Which is why you should trust me when I tell you it's wrong. I've been lying to myself for years. It's only fair I extend you the same courtesy."

Brannick's laugh rolled down the slope ahead of them, big as the man himself. Somewhere below, one of his drivers cursed at a wheel hub, and somewhere above, a hawk turned a slow circle over the pass, riding the thermal off the black rock of Thornfell's shoulder. The mountain didn't have snow this time of year, but it had light — a hard, high-altitude glare that made everything look recently made, freshly forged, as if the world up here were still cooling from the smith.

Astrid walked on. The sword at their hip rode easy in its plain leather sheath, unremarkable, which was the entire point. A swordspeaker's blade should have sung. Should have hummed against the hip like a struck bell held half-muffled, the way oath-steel did when it carried a vow in its grain. Grandmother Halloran's blade used to whisper all night by the fire, reciting her old promises in her own voice while she slept, and young Astrid had lain awake listening to it, drunk on the romance of it all.

Their own blade said nothing.

It hadn't said anything in four months.

*Fading,* the last hedge-witch in Vaelmoor's gutter-market had told them, squinting at their palm like a horse trader reading teeth. *Not gone. Fading. Whatever you swore, it's being paid slower than it's earning.* Then she'd taken their coin and told them to stop wasting money on fortune-tellers, which was, in its way, the most honest prophecy Astrid had ever received.

"Hey." Brannick drew level with them, breathing harder than a man of his size should on a downhill grade, which meant he wanted to talk without the others hearing. He nodded back along the train. "Marta says there's smoke at the waystation. Thin. Cooking-smoke thin, not trouble-thin. But we weren't expecting anyone at Hollowmere, were we?"

"We're smugglers, Brannick. We're never expecting anyone anywhere. That's the whole profession."

"Astrid."

Astrid sighed and let their eyes flick upward along the trail, past the third switchback, to where the waystation crouched in its fold of the mountain like a barnacle on a whale. Hollowmere Station: a low stone shelter with a slate roof and a chimney, built by salt-runners three generations back, maintained by no one, claimed by everyone, owned by whoever happened to be standing inside it. Smoke curled from its chimney in a thread so straight it looked drawn with a ruler. Windless day. Cooking-smoke thin.

Trouble never announced itself loudly on Thornfell anyway. Trouble up here was quiet as altitude sickness — you didn't notice it until your legs stopped working.

"Could be salt-runners," Astrid said.

"It's not salt season."

"Could be lost pilgrims."

"Pilgrims don't come to Thornfell anymore. Not since the usurper put a price on holy things." Brannick spat, ceremonially, over the edge. Everyone spat over edges on Thornfell; it was practically liturgy. "Could be inquisitors."

"Inquisitors don't cook. They requisition." Astrid started walking again, boots finding the rhythm of the switchback, and found their hand had drifted to rest on the silent sword hilt without asking permission. An old habit. A comfort habit. Which was absurd, because the sword offered no comfort at all anymore; it was just steel now, dead weight and good balance, and touching it was like touching the cheek of someone who'd stopped recognizing you.

But the hand went there anyway. Hands are sentimental creatures.

"Hollowmere has a second door," Astrid said. "South wall, behind the woodpile, drops into the old drainage cut. If it's trouble, you take the mules through the main room loud and stupid, and I go quiet through the south. Agreed?"

"That's the plan every time."

"The plans that keep working deserve to be reused."

They came around the last bend with the sun full on the station's face, and Astrid got their first proper look at the smoke, and then — as they closed — at the figure sitting on the bench beside the door, and everything in their chest performed a small, complicated maneuver that ended in dread.

The figure was thin. Hooded. Reading.

Nobody read on Thornfell. There was nothing to read but ledgers, and nobody read those for pleasure, and this figure was not reading a ledger. It was reading a book so large it sat open across both knees like a sleeping cat, and the figure's hands moved along its margins the way a blind person reads, or the way a priest does — with familiarity, with ownership, with the terrible intimacy of someone revisiting a sentence they know by heart.

Astrid had seen that posture before. Three years ago, in the archive under Harrowgate, before the fire, before the exile, before the name Sef became something people whispered instead of said. They had seen a disgraced archivist hunched exactly like that over a book exactly that size, and they had thought, even then: *that person would burn a city down to finish a footnote.*

"Sef," Astrid said aloud, and the hooded figure looked up, and the years hadn't softened the eyes at all. Grey, sharp, unhurried. Eyes like a man who had already read about whatever was going to happen to you and was deciding whether to spoil the ending.

"Astrid Halloran." Sef's voice was dry paper. "You're late."

"I wasn't aware we had an appointment."

"We do now." Sef closed the book — carefully, reverently, the way you close a coffin — and stood, and Astrid saw that they were thinner than three years ago, hungrier, wrapped in a traveling cloak that had been good once and had since learned humility. "I need to talk to you. Alone. And you're going to want to sit down, and I want to be very clear that I am not a man who offers that advice lightly, because I watched my own father receive similar news standing up and he broke his ankle on the hearthstone and bled into the carpet, which detracted from the gravity of the moment considerably."

Behind Astrid, Brannick rumbled, "Friend of yours?"

"No," said Astrid and Sef simultaneously, and then, after a pause, Sef added, "Worse."

* * *

Inside, Hollowmere Station smelled of old smoke and cold stone and, faintly, of salt — the whole mountain was threaded with salt veins, and the walls here wept white crystals in wet weather like the building itself was crying mineral tears. Marta got a fire going because Marta always got a fire going; it was her answer to every problem including, once, a bear. The other drivers took the mules around to the lee side and pretended very hard not to be curious, which among smugglers is the highest form of courtesy.

Astrid sat on a bench. They refused the tea Marta pushed at them, then accepted it, because refusing Marta's tea was not among the freedoms of this life.

Sef set the book on the table between them.

Up close, the Vellum Codex was worse than Astrid remembered. It was huge — a foot thick, bound in something that might have been vellum and might have been anything else, its cover stained dark in patches like a map of old wounds. Its pages, visible at the edges, were layered so fine the block of them looked like compressed fog. It had no clasp. It had never needed one. Books like this did not stay shut unless they chose to.

"You know what this is," Sef said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know what they say it is. Prophecies. A century of them. Written by some mad archivist who—"

"Who was me." Sef sat down across the table, and for a moment the sharpness in their eyes guttered, and underneath it Astrid saw exhaustion of a kind that doesn't sleep off. "Or — will be me. Or has already been me. The tenses get difficult. That's rather the point." Their long fingers rested on the cover. "I kept this book for eleven years, Astrid. I read every page. Every date, every entry, every smug little margin note. Do you know how many entries it contains?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the horror of it. It has more entries than pages. You count them and lose track and count again and the number comes out different, and eventually you stop counting, and eventually — if you're weak, if you're curious, if you're me — you start reading the ones you're not supposed to read. The ones dated after your death." Sef smiled, and it was not a pleasant sight. "That's the heresy they exiled me for. Not theft. Not burning. *Reading ahead.*"

The fire popped. Outside, Marta was shouting at a mule in terms that would have gotten her arrested in three cities. Astrid became extremely aware of the weight of the teacup in their hands, of the heat of it, of the fact that they could feel the heat of it, and found themselves holding onto that sensation like a rope over a drop.

"So read ahead," Astrid said. "Read me my fortune. I'll warn you, the last witch told me I'm fading, so set your expectations accordingly."

Something crossed Sef's face — a flinch, quickly mastered — and Astrid understood, with a slow cold turning in their gut, that Sef already knew about the fading too, and had known, and had come anyway.

"There is an entry," Sef said quietly, "dated tonight."

"All right."

"Dated tonight, at Hollowmere Station, between sundown and full dark."

"Sef."

"And it has your name in it."

The teacup was warm. The stone floor was cold through their boot soles. Marta's voice outside rose and fell over the mule, which was apparently winning. These facts arranged themselves around Astrid like furniture in a familiar room, solid and dumb and reassuring, and Astrid gripped them and said, in a voice they were pleased to note sounded almost bored:

"Half the free cities have my name in something. Wanted notices mostly."

"Notices are written by men who guess." Sef opened the book.

The sound of the cover coming off the pages was soft, and vast, like a door opening in a very large house. The smell that came up off the pages was old paper and something else — something faintly sweet and faintly wrong, like flowers in a sickroom. Astrid had sworn oaths over relics older than kingdoms. They knew what old power smelled like. This was it, wearing a book's costume.

Sef turned pages. They did not hunt for the entry. Their fingers went to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth, and that frightened Astrid more than anything else so far: Sef knew this page. Had read it before. Possibly many times. Had perhaps been carrying the knowledge of it for three years across half a continent, looking for the person it described.

"Here," Sef said, and turned the book around.

The script was small, precise, and written in an ink that was brown-black, the color of old blood or old oak gall. The date at the head of the entry was rendered in the archive cipher Sef had invented themselves — Astrid recognized it from the burned archive's catalogs, that private shorthand of loops and slashes — and the cipher spelled, unmistakably, tonight.

And beneath the date, in letters that seemed to press harder than the rest, deeper, as though the pen had been shaking:

*Astrid Halloran comes to the mountain with the book in the company of thieves. The oath runs thin because the price was never fully paid. The soul beneath Thornfell stirs and calls its debtor home. What was sold must be returned or broken. The swordspeaker's name was bought; the name beneath it was stolen.*

Astrid read it twice.

Then a third time, because the words refused to cohere into meaning, sliding apart like fish in dark water, and on the fourth reading they stopped sliding and simply sat there, flat and black and patient on the page, waiting for Astrid to catch up.

"The soul beneath Thornfell," Astrid said. Their voice came out steady. Some soldier-part of them had taken the wheel and was speaking on their behalf, and Astrid was grateful, distantly, the way you're grateful to a colleague who volunteers for the worst duty. "Stirs and calls its debtor home."

"Yes."

"I'm not a debtor. I don't owe anyone. My family paid its debts — that's the whole legend, isn't it? The Hallorans keep their word. It's carved over our door in Corrow. *We Keep Our Word.* My grandmother had it engraved on her tombstone and misspelled *word*, and we left it, because correcting a dead woman's spelling felt worse than the mistake."

"Astrid."

"What soul? Whose soul?" They heard their own voice climb and hated it. "Because there's a line in there — you see it, the last line — *the name beneath it was stolen.* Beneath whose name? Mine? There's nothing beneath mine. I'm not a ledger. I'm—" They stopped.

The teacup was warm. The floor was cold. Marta had won the argument with the mule; the silence outside had the specific texture of victory.

"Astrid," Sef said again, gentler now, the gentleness somehow worse than everything before it, "there's more."

"Don't."

"The ink." Sef touched the page, hovering, not quite daring to touch the words themselves. "Look at the ink."

"I'm looking at the ink. It's ink. It's a hundred years old, you said—"

"I said the book is a hundred years old. Look closely." Sef tilted the page toward the firelight, and Astrid leaned in, and leaned closer, and the soldier-part of them that had been driving went very still, because the ink of the entry — unlike the faded brown of every line surrounding it — was dark. Wet-dark. Fresh-dark. It gleamed in the firelight the way ink gleams in the moment after the pen lifts.

"That's impossible," Astrid said. "That's a century-old pigment. You tested it yourself, you told me, in the archive—"

"I tested the book. Every page. For eleven years." Sef withdrew their hand slowly, as if from a hot stove. "This entry was not there when I fled Harrowgate. I would stake my remaining credibility — which is, admittedly, worth approximately nothing — that it was not there six months ago. And yet the pigment matches the rest of the codex exactly. Same oak gall base, same iron salts, same aging. As if it were written a hundred years ago." They looked up, and their grey eyes held no comfort whatsoever, only the naked professional fascination of a scholar watching their life's discipline commit suicide in front of them. "As if the book wrote it a hundred years ago and hid it until tonight. Or as if *tonight* arrived early and soaked backward into the page. I've spent three years deciding which of those frightens me less, and I still can't choose."

Astrid sat back.

The bench was hard beneath them. The fire was warm on one side of their face. The tea had gone lukewarm in their hands, and they realized they had been gripping the cup hard enough to whiten their knuckles, and made themselves loosen their fingers, joint by joint, because a swordspeaker's hands did not shake, and theirs were beginning to want to.

*The oath runs thin because the price was never fully paid.*

Four months of silence from the steel. Four months of reaching for the well of vow-light and hauling up less each time, less and less and less, like drawing water from a source that was drying — or like payments on a debt running out. They had told themselves it was fatigue. Age. The mountain air. Anything.

*What was sold must be returned or broken.*

"Sold," Astrid said. The word came out small. "Sold by whom?"

"The entry doesn't say." Sef hesitated. Astrid watched them hesitate, and understood that hesitation, in a man like Sef, was the loudest confession in the world. "But the Halloran oath didn't come from nowhere, Astrid. Oaths like yours aren't learned. They're *bought*. Everyone who studies the old families knows that much. The question no archive ever answered was what the Hallorans paid. Power like that — speech that binds itself into steel — that's not a talent. It's a contract. And contracts have two sides."

Outside, the light was failing. Astrid could see it through the shuttered window, the hard mountain glare softening toward blue, toward dusk, toward the hour Sef had named — *between sundown and full dark* — and the entry lay open on the table describing this exact room, this exact evening, this exact conversation, and Astrid Halloran sat inside their own prophecy and felt the floor of certainty tilt beneath them like a deck in weather.

Every vow they had ever spoken into steel. Every memory it had taken in payment — faces, names, whole seasons gone soft and missing, holes they'd explained away as age, as drink, as the ordinary erosion of living. They had assumed the price was fair. That was the covenant. That was the whole shape of their life: give a memory, bind a promise, protect the road, protect the cities, protect the people who couldn't protect themselves. Fair trade. Honest weight.

Unless the trade had been rigged before they were born. Unless the scale itself belonged to someone else.

"My grandmother," Astrid said slowly, "swore her last oath to me. On her deathbed. She held my hands and she said — she said, *keep the word, little bird, keep the word, and mind the mountain.*" The memory surfaced whole and vivid, one of the ones that had never eroded, and for the first time Astrid wondered why *that* one had survived when so many others hadn't. Why the dying woman's voice remained perfectly preserved while her own mother's face had gone to fog. "*Mind the mountain.* I always thought she was rambling. She was dosed on poppy for the pain."

"She wasn't rambling." Sef's voice was barely above the fire's crackle. "Astrid — the codex has other entries about the Halloran oath. Older ones. I couldn't read them all before the archive burned, but I remember fragments. One of them mentions a bargain made 'beneath the stone' — a purchase. One of them uses the phrase *the warden's price*. And every single one of them, without exception, ends the same way." Sef turned the great book gently, so the fresh-inked entry faced Astrid again, the wet-black letters gleaming like something alive and patient. "With a warning. The same warning, every time, in the same nine words."

"Say them."

"*What the mountain holds, the mountain will collect.*"

The fire settled. Somewhere out in the dark, Marta laughed at something one of the drivers said, a bright human sound, ordinary and oblivious, and it reached Astrid as if from another world — a world where books stayed shut, where names belonged to their owners, where the worst thing waiting at a waystation was bad tea.

Astrid looked down at the entry one last time. At their own name, pressed deep into a century-old page by a pen that had never existed yet. At the final line, which they now read differently, the way you reread a letter from a dead friend and find in it what you couldn't bear to see the first time:

*The swordspeaker's name was bought; the name beneath it was stolen.*

Beneath their name. Underneath it. Like a second signature hidden under the first. Like something buried.

The vertigo came then, sudden and total — not of the body but of the self, the ground-level certainty of *being Astrid Halloran* tilting and cracking like lake ice — and Astrid gripped the edge of the table and held on, and outside, far up the black flank of Thornfell, faint as a memory, almost certainly imagined, almost certainly nothing:

the mountain hummed.

Low. Patient. Like a throat clearing before it speaks.

Sef heard it too. Astrid watched the color leave their face, watched the archivist's hands flatten on the codex as if to pin it down against what was coming.

"That," Sef said, "is earlier than the entry predicted."

Astrid stood, took up the silent sword, and buckled it on.

"How much earlier?"

"Everything," Sef said, closing the book, "is earlier than it predicts. That's the problem with prophecy. It's always catching up."
