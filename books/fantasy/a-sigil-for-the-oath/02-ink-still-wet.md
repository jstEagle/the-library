## 2. Ink Still Wet

Sef had spent thirty years among books, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing worth teaching: a lie always dries faster than the truth.

The Vellum Codex lay open on the waystation table between a guttering tallow candle and a bowl of cold barley soup, and its newest page was still telling the truth. That was the problem.

*On the eve of the frost-moon, the last swordspeaker comes down from the high passes with salt and lies upon her tongue.* The letters were small, precise, executed in the cramped hand of some long-dead scribe whose name the archive had never bothered to record. *Her name is Astrid Halloran, and her soul is not her own.*

Sef read it again. Then a third time, because the first two readings had produced nothing but a ringing in his ears and a sensation like standing at the edge of a stair that wasn't there.

Across the table, Astrid stood with her back to the wall and her hand nowhere near her sword, which was how she held herself when she was trying very hard not to reach for it. The smugglers — Brannic and his crew of five — had withdrawn to the far end of the common room, ostensibly to argue about axle grease, actually to watch the two of them the way men watch a fuse they can't put out.

"You're going to tell me it's a forgery," Astrid said. Her voice was level. Too level. "Some archivist's joke. Some—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Sef. Tell me it's a forgery."

"I'm going to tell you something worse." Sef turned the book toward the candlelight and tilted the page until the ink caught the flame's angle. "Look at the sheen. Not the words — the sheen."

She leaned in. She had a swordspeaker's eyes, quick and unromantic, and he watched them narrow as she saw what he wanted her to see: the faint oily iridescence that rose off old iron-gall ink when light crossed it just so. Fresh ink was flat. Dead ink glittered. This ink glittered.

"Oxgall and copperas," Sef said, "with oak-gall tannin cut by rainwater — mountain rainwater, you can tell by the lime sediment in the strokes. Bound in lambskin vellum, scraped twice. That pigment recipe went out of use a hundred and ten years ago, when the scriptoriums moved to walnut-shell extract because copperas ate through vellum if the monks got lazy about washing the page. There are perhaps four people alive who could fake this sheen, and I have exiled or buried three of them."

"So it's old."

"It is *old*. It is older than my grandmother, older than the Codex's binding, older than every marginal note and correction in the whole cursed volume. And it names," he said, and here his voice did something humiliating, something that cracked on the final word, "it names an event that happened six hours ago. Your descent of the pass. The salt in your pack. The lie you told Brannic about your cargo being dye-fixative." He looked up at her. "Ink does not do that, Astrid. Ink has never done that. I have read forgeries from nine kingdoms and I am telling you, with the full weight of everything I know, that this page is genuine, ancient, and correct."

The candle guttered. In the silence Sef became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, and of the smell of the waystation — woodsmoke, wet wool, the sour mineral breath of the salt cliffs outside — and of the fact that his hands were shaking, and that he had not eaten since morning, and that none of these ordinary facts were doing anything whatsoever to make the world feel ordinary again.

Astrid sat down slowly, the way a person sits when their knees have filed a formal complaint.

"My grandmother used to say the future is a country you can't map because it moves when you look at it," she said. "She said anyone who claimed otherwise was selling something."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a fine archivist."

"She made a better liar." Astrid reached out — hesitated — and touched the edge of the page without letting her fingers cross onto the writing itself, the way one touches a sleeping animal. "It says my soul isn't mine. Whose is it, then?"

"That," Sef said, "is where the page stops being helpful." He flipped forward. The vellum after tonight's date was blank — not empty-blank but *waiting*-blank, a texture he knew from ledgers that expected entries. He flipped backward instead, through decades of dates, and found what he had been half-hoping not to find: other names, other nights, each entry written in that same antique hand. A ferryman drowned in 812. A choir mistress in 809. A king's bastard in 798, though that entry had been scraped nearly clean by someone with a knife and strong feelings.

"They all come true?" Astrid asked.

"Every entry I've ever been able to verify. Which is why I was exiled for reading them." He closed the book gently, as if it might bruise. "The Council called it incitement against providence. I called it due diligence. We disagreed loudly, and then I disagreed loudly from the road."

Brannic chose that moment to abandon the pretense of axle grease. He came over with his thumbs hooked in his belt and the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. He was a broad, gray-bearded smuggler with a voice like a landslide, and Sef had known him for two years and trusted him approximately as far as a man in exile could afford to trust anyone.

"Right," Brannic said. "I've hauled contraband salt past three customs houses with you two, and I've never asked what the tall one carries under his coat, because asking is bad luck on the switchbacks. But now there's a woman at the table looking green as pond water over a book, and my grandmother always said—"

"—the future moves when you look at it," Astrid murmured.

"—when the book starts naming folk, you burn the book and change roads." He planted both fists on the table. "So. Do we burn it?"

Sef felt the question land somewhere behind his ribs. Thirty years of his life, his title, his name in the archives — all of it bound up in that book, in the principle that knowledge preserved was knowledge defended. Burn the Codex and he would be free of it. Burn the Codex and he would be exactly the kind of man the Council had always accused him of being.

"No," he said.

"Didn't think so." Brannic sighed through his nose. "Then we've a problem, because there's dust coming down the pass road, and it's moving wrong for weather and right for riders."

* * *

They heard the hooves before they saw the banners — no, before they saw the horses, because the riders had struck their colors for the approach, and that alone told Sef everything. Honest soldiers flew their colors. Men hunting a specific person hid them.

There were six of them, coming up the track from the valley side at a canter the footing didn't forgive. Even at distance Sef could pick out the details that mattered: the matched grays, the lacquered half-armor worn under travel cloaks, the long polearms slung across saddles in the manner of men who expected to dismount and use them indoors. And at the center, a rider in black-and-silver whose posture was less a seat than an occupation of the horse.

"Inquisitors," Sef said quietly. "Kestrian's, not provincial. Look at the harness — that's Vaelmoor work, court-forged."

"How many ways out of this station?" Astrid was already moving, low and unhurried, gathering her bedroll with hands that betrayed nothing.

"One road in, one road out, and they're on it." Brannic scratched his beard. "Cellar's a dead end. Loft's a coffin."

"There's a third way." Everyone looked at Astrid. She jerked her chin toward the eastern wall, toward the cliff beyond it. "The salt caves. The whole ridge is honeycombed — miners cut galleries into it two hundred years ago, before the vein played out. Every survey says they're impassable. Every survey is wrong, because every survey was drawn by men who never talked to smugglers." A ghost of a smile. "I have maps."

"Of course you do," Sef said.

"Away, then," Brannic said, and here he surprised Sef, though perhaps he shouldn't have. The big man turned to his crew and started issuing orders in a voice pitched to carry no further than the room: split the cargo, muddle the ledger, if questioned the passengers left before dawn and took the valley fork south. To Astrid he said only, "You never stopped here. I never saw you. And if you live, send word by the usual channels, because I'll want to know how the story ends."

"Why?" Sef couldn't help himself. "Why risk it?"

Brannic looked at him with something that wasn't quite pity. "Because I've seen what Kestrian's collectors do to villages that sheltered strangers. Because somebody once carried my sister out of a burned town on their back and never gave a name." He shrugged, enormous and embarrassed. "Because the arithmetic doesn't always come out in coin. Go. Through the pantry, there's a hatch behind the flour shelf. Mind the drop — it's farther than it looks."

The hatch dropped them into darkness that smelled of centuries.

* * *

Sef had a scholar's relationship with the dark: he respected it, kept his agreements with it, and had never once enjoyed its company. Now he descended a salt-miner's ladder in absolute blackness with a priceless heretical book strapped to his chest under his coat, listening to Astrid's breathing below him count the rungs, and tried not to calculate how many feet of empty air waited past the lantern-light's reach.

The lantern came alive at the bottom — Astrid worked flint like she'd been born to it, another item for the growing list of things that didn't fit a simple caravan guard — and the cave opened around them like the inside of some vast pale throat.

Salt. Walls of it, floor of it, ceiling lost in shadow above, all of it glowing faintly where the lantern touched, translucent amber shot through with veins of white and rust-red. The air tasted clean and ancient, mineral and dry, utterly without rot. Their footsteps crunched and echoed, and each echo came back subtly wrong, flattened, as if the salt swallowed the personality out of sound and returned only its bones.

"This way," Astrid said, and unrolled a map that was, characteristically, not a map any guild would recognize. It was inked on oiled silk, annotated in three hands, covered with symbols Sef half-recognized from smuggling cant and margin notes in a script he definitely recognized: the same cramped antique lettering as the Codex.

He stopped walking.

"Astrid. Where did you get this?"

"Family thing." She didn't slow down. "Grandmother's. Before that, great-grandmother's. Why?"

"May I see it?" He kept his voice mild, scholarly, the tone he used with skittish donors. Inside, something had begun to move very fast. "Just for a moment."

She handed it over without breaking stride, which meant she either trusted him completely or hadn't understood the question. He held the silk up to the lantern and let his eyes do what thirty years had trained them to do — walk the lines, date the hand, match the habits. The oldest annotations predated the Codex's binding. The most recent were in a living hand, a hand he had seen within the hour, because he had watched its owner write her name in Brannic's ledger at the valley tollhouse.

And threaded through the smuggler's markings, sparse and deliberate, ran the same phrase, repeated across two hundred years of margins, in the antique script:

*The stair remembers. The stair remembers. When she descends, the stair remembers.*

"Sef." Astrid had stopped ahead, lantern raised. "You're making that noise again. The one where you're reading and forgetting to breathe."

"The Codex named tonight," he said carefully. "Tonight, and you, and a stolen soul. I assumed it was singular. Books of prophecy usually are — one thread, pulled taut, followed to the knot." He lowered the map. "But this isn't singular. Your family hasn't been guarding a route through these caves. They've been keeping an appointment. Two hundred years of appointments. Astrid, whoever wrote the Codex didn't foresee you stumbling into a prophecy." He heard his own voice go thin. "Someone arranged for you to be standing where the prophecy needed you. Generation after generation. Your whole line. You weren't born into a family. You were born into a delivery."

The lantern light trembled — her hand, he realized; her hand was trembling, though her face had gone perfectly smooth, the smoothness of someone holding a door shut against a gale.

"Say the rest," she said.

"The soul the page mentions. The bargain underneath Thornfell. I think the Halloran oath itself is the bargain. I think your power was purchased, and the price is still being paid, and the Codex is the receipt." He swallowed. "And I think tonight the debt comes due."

For a long moment the only sound was the salt eating their echoes.

Then, from above and behind them, muffled through forty feet of rock, came a sound that made them both freeze: voices. Human voices, distorted to gibberish by the stone, but unmistakably many, and unmistakably organized. The scrape of boots on the waystation floor overhead. The hatch opening.

"They found it fast," Astrid breathed. "Too fast. Brannic wouldn't—"

"Brannic wouldn't. Someone else in that station sold the hour of our leaving, or the inquisitors had the station mapped already." Sef was re-rolling the map with hands that refused steadiness. "Which means they know about the caves. Which means—"

"Which means we stop talking and start running." She took the lead, lantern high, and the pale corridors swallowed them.

* * *

They ran through the mountain's glassy intestines for what felt like hours and was probably one. The galleries narrowed, branched, narrowed again; the ceiling came down until Sef walked bent double, the Codex digging into his sternum with every stride, while behind them the pursuit echoed — or seemed to echo, because in the salt it was impossible to say whether the boots they heard belonged to men twenty yards back or their own fear bounced off the walls. Once, a shout rang clear and human down a side gallery: *"Light ahead! Two sets of prints!"* — and Astrid snuffed the lantern without breaking stride and led them on by touch, her hand cool and certain on Sef's wrist, guiding him past a lip of rock he never saw.

That was the thing he kept returning to, later, in the hollow where they finally dared stop: not the fear, though there had been plenty, but her certainty. She navigated the dark like a memory. Twice she chose forks without consulting the map. Once she stopped, head tilted, and said "brine pool ahead, don't drink," and she was right.

"You've done this before," he whispered.

"Once. Coming north, two winters ago. With my grandmother." A pause. "She knew every turn. I thought I was learning the caves." Another pause, longer. "I think I was learning the route."

In the absolute dark, Sef reached out and found her shoulder — an intrusion he would never have permitted himself in daylight, but daylight had been confiscated along with everything else. "Listen to me. Whatever your family built around you, whatever was decided before your birth — the page names you, but pages don't command. I have spent my life watching powerful people treat texts as licenses. The Codex is a record, Astrid. Records can be argued with. I know, because arguing with records is the only crime I've ever committed, and I'd commit it again."

He felt her breathe. Felt the trembling in her shoulder ease by a degree, not gone, merely acknowledged.

"You're a strange man, Sef."

"I'm an exiled archivist. Strange is the entry requirement."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her — brief, cracked, real. And then, from somewhere deeper in the mountain, further down than any gallery they had crossed, came a sound that ended the laughter in both their throats.

It was a voice. One voice, vast and layered, rising through the salt like heat through a grate — and though the acoustics shredded it almost past recognition, two things survived the journey intact. First, that it was singing, or chanting, or counting something in a cadence older than any kingdom Sef could name.

Second, that it was saying a name. Repeating it, patiently, the way one calls across a great distance to someone who has been walking away for a very long time:

*"Halloran. Halloran. Halloran."*

In the dark, Astrid's hand found her sword hilt — and Sef, who had spent the whole day cataloguing horrors, discovered there was room for one more, because the blade answered. Not with steel-song or anything a bard would recognize, but with a faint, hungry warmth, like an animal waking beneath her palm. Like something answering to its master's name.

Or its owner's.

"Oh," Sef whispered, into the salt-dark, to no one, to the book against his chest, to the two-hundred-year appointment closing around them like a fist. "Oh, that's what the oath *is*."
