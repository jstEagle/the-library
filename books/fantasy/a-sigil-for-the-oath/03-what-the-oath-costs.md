## 3. What the Oath Costs

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Astrid tried to remember their mother's laugh.

They sat with their back against the waystation's far wall, sword across their knees, turning the memory over the way you'd turn a stone to see what lived underneath. There was a shape where the memory should have been. A warmth. The idea of laughter — high, quick, ending in a cough that meant she'd laughed too long and too hard. But the sound itself was gone. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the jaw: you run your tongue across the gap again and again, and every time it surprises you.

"You're doing it again," Sef said from across the hearth. The archivist sat cross-legged with the Vellum Codex wrapped in oilcloth on their lap, not reading it, just resting their hands on it like a man warming himself at a fire he didn't trust. "That thing with your face."

"What thing with my face?"

"Like someone asked you a question and you're afraid of the answer." Sef nodded at the sword. "You keep touching the pommel when you think I'm not looking. You've done it eleven times since we made camp."

Astrid took their hand off the pommel. Then, annoyed at themselves, put it back.

"It's the date," they said. "Tomorrow. Tonight now, I suppose. The page said tonight."

"I know what the page said. I read it to you four times."

"You read it to me because you wanted to watch me hear it each time. That's an archivist's trick. I've smuggled for archivists before. You're all little torturers with your footnotes."

Sef almost smiled. Almost. The Codex lay between them like a corpse neither would bury. *On the night the frost comes early to Thornfell's shoulder, the last swordspeaker stands at the crossroads of her own making, and the debt comes due.* And beneath it, in pigment two centuries old, a name written as if the ink had always known it would be read by its owner: *Astrid Halloran.*

"The debt," Astrid said quietly. "Everyone assumes that means the usurper. Or the prophecy. Some grand accounting." They drew the blade an inch from its sheath. The steel caught the firelight and held it the way it always did — greedily, hungrily, as though light were another vow waiting to be spoken into it. "But I've been thinking about what my oath actually costs. What it has cost. And I can't make the sums come out."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning I'm going to tell you a story, archivist, and you're going to write nothing down."

Sef closed their hand flat over the Codex. "Nothing down," they agreed.

* * *

She died in autumn, Astrid's grandmother, in the same house where she'd raised them — a low stone house below Thornfell's southern shoulder, with a roof of slate that sang in the wind like a choir tuning up. Astrid had been fourteen. They remembered the house perfectly. They remembered the smell of the sickroom, tallow and fever-sweat and the bitter bark tea no one ever finished. They remembered that the frost had come early that year, silvering the window glass while the rowan trees outside were still red.

What they did not remember — what they had never once remembered, in all the years since — was why.

Why them. Why a dying woman would call her granddaughter to the bedside, take her hands, and say the words that started everything. Astrid knew the words themselves; those were carved into them deeper than any memory. *Kneel, child. Take up the steel. Speak after me.* But the reason — grief for a dead daughter? Fear of something coming? Love, or its terrible cousin, duty? — there was only smooth blankness where the reason should have been, and Astrid had spent twenty years walking around that blankness without looking down.

In the flashback of it, in the version that lived behind their eyes, the room was candlelit and small. Their grandmother propped against pillows like a marionette whose strings had been cut one at a time. Her hair had gone white early — Hallorans went white early, Astrid thought, touching their own temple where a streak had begun at nineteen — and her eyes were still dark and quick, the eyes of a woman who had out-argued tax collectors, border lords, and at least one minor demon according to family legend.

"Astrid." Her voice was gravel in a silk bag. "Do you know what our name is worth?"

"Nothing. Da used to say it was worth less than nothing, it came with debts."

"Your da was right about most things he regretted saying." A smile, cracked at the edges. "Our name is worth exactly one thing, child, and it is this." She lifted the sword from beside the bed — the Halloran blade, older than the house, older than the road, its steel dark as deep water. "One oath, spoken true, binds into this steel and stays bound. Do you know what that means?"

"It means stories. It means the songs about Grandfather holding the pass at—"

"It means power." No softness in it now. "And power is never free. Listen to me, because I will say this once and then I will lie, afterward, because all who teach this craft must lie a little or no one would ever take it up." She drew a breath that cost her visibly, like a woman hauling rope. "Every vow you speak into the steel takes payment. Not blood. Not years. Something worse. It takes a memory. One memory, chosen by the oath, not by you. You will not know which until it's gone."

Astrid, fourteen, kneeling on cold flagstones with their grandmother's hands over theirs on the flat of the blade, had felt no fear. That much they remembered clearly — the strange, clean absence of fear, as if some part of them had already decided and the deciding had used up all the room fear needed.

"What kind of memories?" young Astrid had asked.

"Small ones, at first. The oath takes what you will least miss, so that you'll keep speaking. That is its cruelty and its mercy both. The color of a wall. The taste of bread from a baker who dies the next winter anyway. You will not notice the small payments." Her grip tightened. "But swear often enough, and one day the oath reaches deeper. And you will lose something you cannot spare, and you will not even know what it was. You will only know the shape of the hole."

"And you swore anyway," young Astrid had said. "You and Grandfather and his mother before him."

"We swore because the world does not leave the Hallorans alone, child. Because there are things coming down the mountain roads that answer to no crown and no god, and someone must be able to speak a wall of force between them and the sleeping villages. We swore because—" and here the grandmother had stopped, and looked toward the frosted window, and whatever she saw there made her finish differently than she intended: "—because we were asked. Now kneel straighter. Repeat after me, and mean every word, because the steel will know."

And Astrid had repeated it. The old words, in the old cadence, the vowels rolling like stones downhill: *Steel, take my word and hold it past my death. Let my vow be a wall, my word a blade, my truth the only coin you need.* Nine vows in the full oath. Nine sentences. And between the fourth and the fifth, Astrid remembered — this they had always remembered, this single bright splinter — the candle flames all leaning sideways at once, toward the sword, as though drawn by breath. And a sensation like a page being turned somewhere behind their eyes.

They hadn't known yet what they'd lost. They wouldn't for months. That was the design of it, the grandmother had said later, gentler now, dying in earnest: the oath steals quietly, and shows you the empty shelves only when it needs you hungry.

* * *

Present-day Astrid opened their eyes. The waystation fire had sunk to coals. Across the hearth, Sef was watching them with the particular patience of a person who had learned that silence pulls more truth than questions.

"Well?" Astrid said.

"You went somewhere."

"I went home. Fourteen years old. My grandmother dying, teaching me the cost of the family trade before she'd let me practice it." Astrid slid the sword fully out and laid it across their knees, naked steel drinking the emberlight. "Here's the arithmetic that won't come out, Sef. Twenty years of oaths. Do you know how many vows I've spoken into this? Walls at Kestrel Ford. Bindings at the Saltmarch. Every contract I've sealed for smugglers who wanted their deals unbreakable. Hundreds. Call it three hundred paid memories, conservatively. Three hundred holes in me." They tapped their temple. "Now count what I actually feel missing."

Sef's brows drew together. "Go on."

"My mother's laugh. Gone — I noticed that years ago and told myself it was the fever I had as a child. The layout of the house I grew up in — I couldn't draw you the second floor if you put a knife in my ribs, and I *lived* there. My grandfather's face — I have his hands, his voice, his terrible jokes, and no face. And here's the worst one." Astrid heard their own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when the ground was giving way. "I don't remember why I took the oath. She asked me to. I knelt. There should be a *because* in there somewhere, archivist, and there isn't. It's not even a hole shaped like a reason. It's a hole shaped like a hole."

"That could be trauma," Sef offered gently. "Grief. She was dying. Children seal over—"

"Three hundred times?"

Silence. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the switchbacks above Thornfell, carrying the mineral smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet.

"Or," Sef said slowly, sitting forward, "the ledger doesn't balance because the payments weren't going where you were told they were going." They unwrapped the Codex a handspan, just enough to show the edge of that impossible page, and Astrid felt the sword hum faintly against their knees — it did that near the book, had done it since the waystation, a sound felt more than heard, like a plucked string under water. "A soul sold to the court beneath Thornfell. Your soul, if the page means what it says. If part of you was bargained away before you were born, then perhaps the oath wasn't taking memories as payment at all. Perhaps it was taking them as *interest*. Withdrawals against an account your ancestor already emptied."

Astrid looked down at the blade. Twenty years it had been the surest thing about them — surer than their name, which everyone mispronounced, surer than their face, which changed whenever they caught it in unexpected mirrors. And now it lay across their knees humming like something guilty.

"If that's true," Astrid said, "then every vow I ever spoke, I spoke with money that was never mine."

"Then every wall you raised still stood," Sef countered. "Whatever the coin was, the shelter was real. I've spent three years reading marginalia, Astrid. The Undercourt doesn't counterfeit. It only lends."

"Lend," Astrid repeated, tasting the word. "And what does the Undercourt charge for lending, Sef? What does a debt come due as, when the debtor didn't sign anything?"

The archivist didn't answer, which was itself an answer. Astrid stood, sheathed the sword, and walked to the waystation's narrow window. Frost had come early to Thornfell's shoulder — of course it had, the page had promised it, the page that was two hundred years old and still wet with tomorrow. Below, the switchbacks descended into darkness where the mountain's roots went down further than any map admitted. Further, maybe, than any map was allowed to admit.

*Shape of the hole,* her grandmother had said. *You will only know the shape of the hole.*

And Astrid understood suddenly, with a lurch like a stair missing in the dark, that the hole might not be a wound at all. It might be a room. Rooms had walls. Walls had doors. And somewhere inside them, behind three hundred locked and empty shelves, something might be standing at one of those doors, listening, waiting for its name to be called.

"You've gone pale," Sef said behind them. "Paler."

"There's a second person in these memories," Astrid said. "I can't explain it. Some of the gaps aren't missing, they're *occupied*. Like a coat that fits wrong at the shoulders because it was cut for someone else." They pressed their forehead briefly against the cold glass. "My grandmother lied to me at that bedside. Not about the cost. About the count. She made it sound like I'd pay and pay and stay myself, diminished. But what if the oath wasn't diminishing me? What if it was building me — assembling me out of pieces, mine and not-mine, to fill a shape the bargain required?"

Outside, far down the mountain road, a light appeared. Then another. Lanterns, moving in formation — not the loose scatter of smugglers or the drunken wander of miners, but a disciplined line, climbing the switchbacks with purpose, toward the waystation.

Sef was already wrapping the Codex. "Inquisitors ride in threes," they said quietly. "That's nine lanterns."

Astrid's hand found the pommel — twelfth time — and this time the gesture wasn't a habit. It was a question. *Are you mine?* the hand asked the hilt. *Was any of it ever mine?* The steel hummed its underwater note and answered nothing, and down in the dark below Thornfell, in a court lit through with bone-white veins, Astrid could have sworn — could have sworn, though they'd sworn their last easy oath years ago — that something very old and very patient turned a page.
