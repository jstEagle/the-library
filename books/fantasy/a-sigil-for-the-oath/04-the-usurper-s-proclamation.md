## 4. The Usurper's Proclamation

Maren Duskwell had been awake for nineteen hours when the summons came, and she was grateful for it. Sleep, in her experience, was where the day's work went soft at the edges — where the face of a man she'd arrested in Brindle Row started to look like her brother's, or worse, like her own. She preferred the sharp hours. The ones with lamplight and ink and a ledger that behaved exactly as written.

The summons was written on grey card, sealed in black wax with the crowned thorn of House Kestrian, and delivered by a page who couldn't have been more than fourteen. He stood in her office doorway in the Inquisitor-General's wing of Vaelmoor's citadel, holding the card out at arm's length as though it might bite.

"Inquisitor-General. His Radiance requests your presence at the Proclamation. Dawn assembly. The Grand Square."

*His Radiance.* The title still had the smell of fresh paint about it. Three years since Kestrian had taken the capital, and his people had only just settled on what to call him — not king, because kings could be contested by blood, and Kestrian had none worth mentioning; not lord protector, because that had been tried twice and both men had died badly. *Radiance* had been the alchemists' suggestion. Light without warmth, Maren thought. A shine you couldn't put your hand to.

"Tell His Radiance I'll attend," she said, and did not look up from her ledger.

"He said—" The boy hesitated, swallowed. "He said to tell you there will be an order for you. After."

"Then he can give it to me after."

The page fled. Maren dipped her pen, finished the entry she'd been writing — *Duskwell, M., interrogation of suspected oath-cultist, Brindle Row, confession extracted, sentence pending* — and allowed herself one moment, precisely one, to press her thumb against the ache behind her eyes before she rose to dress for court.

She dressed for court the way other women armed themselves. Grey coat, high collar, silver chain of office laid flat and straight across her breast. Her hair pinned so tightly it pulled at her temples — a small pain, held constant, that kept her mind from wandering anywhere it shouldn't. At her hip, in a plain scabbard that drew no eye among the finery, rode her commission blade: unadorned steel, never sworn upon, never spoken to. A blade that owed nothing to anyone's voice.

That was the whole of the Duskwell creed, and her father had beaten it into her before he'd taught her letters: *A sword that talks is a sword that lies. Steel is honest. Keep steel honest.*

Her father had also, in the last year of his life, wept over a smuggler's ballad about the Halloran swordspeakers, and Maren had learned young that creeds were load-bearing walls built around rooms no one wanted to enter.

---

The Grand Square of Vaelmoor had been built by better centuries than this one. Its flagstones had once hosted coronations, harvest festivals, the great open-air trials where accused oath-breakers could demand judgment by swordspeech — a custom abolished now, along with most customs that required anyone to trust a voice.

By dawn, the square held perhaps forty thousand people, packed shoulder to shoulder behind lines of citadel guards in their new black-and-gold. Scaffolding stood at its eastern edge, hung with grey banners bearing the crowned thorn. From the scaffolding's highest platform, the whole city seemed arranged below like something poured.

Maren took her place on the second tier, beside the other officers of state, and watched the crowd the way she watched everything: for exits, for hands moving toward hidden steel, for the particular quality of silence that preceded violence. The crowd was quiet in the ordinary way — the tired, obedient quiet of people who had learned that assemblies announced in advance were safer attended than avoided.

Kestrian appeared at the hour the sun cleared the eastern rooftops, and Maren, who had studied him for three years, made her usual private inventory.

He was beautiful. That was the first thing, and it never stopped being the first thing, and she suspected it was engineered. Tall without stooping, broad through the shoulders, with the kind of face painters charged extra for — strong jaw, dark hair going silver at the temples in a way that suggested gravity rather than age. He moved like water finding its level. And beneath all of it, if you knew where to look, was the faint wrongness she'd begun noticing a year ago: a smoothness, a certain glassy evenness of skin, as though he were lit from within by something trying very hard to look like health.

*Feeding,* the whisper-networks said. *He drinks the oaths.* She'd read the reports herself, cross-checked them, found them thin but persistent. Confiscated oath-blades stored in the vault beneath the citadel, guarded by alchemists rather than soldiers. Officers rotated away from vault duty after six months, pale and forgetful.

She filed it where she filed everything she couldn't yet act on: under *later.*

"My people." Kestrian's voice carried across forty thousand heads without visible effort. It was a good voice — warm, worn, like a boot broken in by someone else's walking. "My patient, long-suffering people."

Below him, the crowd breathed.

"For three years I have told you the plague was ending. For three years I have told you the Grey Fever would pass, that we would bury our dead and count our blessings and rebuild." He paused, let the grief settle over the square like frost. "I lied to you. Not from cruelty. From hope."

A ripple through the crowd. Maren watched hands grip rails, watched an old woman near the barricade close her eyes.

"The Grey Fever does not pass because it was never a fever of nature. My alchemists have proven it. The sickness that has taken your children, your mothers, your fathers — it was brewed. Crafted. Loosed deliberately into the wells of the honest and the loyal." His voice dropped, and the square leaned in to catch it. "Brewed by those whose trade is the corruption of words. By the swordspeakers."

The sound that came off the crowd then was not quite a roar. It was lower and worse — a groan, collective and animal, the sound of grief given somewhere to go. Maren had seen crowds grieve before. This was not grieving. This was grief being *issued*, like uniforms, like orders.

"You know the truth of it in your own houses," Kestrian continued, softer now, almost tender. "You know the fever came first to the towns where oath-stones stood. You know the swordspeakers' guild halls closed their doors in the plague years while honest folk died in the streets. You know—" and here his voice broke, beautifully, on schedule, "—you know what it is to lose a child and be told it was God's will, when it was the will of men with magic in their mouths."

In the front rows, someone began to weep loudly. Others took it up.

Maren stood at attention and did not move. She had been present at the drafting of this speech. Not the words — Kestrian wrote his own words, or something did — but the architecture. She had sat in the war room eleven days ago while his alchemists presented the findings she had requested and received, and which she had read four times looking for the flaw that would let her refuse to stand on this platform today.

There was no flaw. There never was. The samples matched. The fever's spread pattern aligned with old guild territories. The evidence was elegant, comprehensive, and manufactured — she was nearly sure it was manufactured; she had watched the lead alchemist, a soft-spoken woman named Corvale, blink too slowly when asked how long the analysis had taken — and it did not matter whether Maren believed it, because forty thousand people below her wanted it to be true, and a thing forty thousand people want to believe stops being a lie in any way that matters.

*You are complicit,* said a small clear voice in her, the voice she kept locked in the smallest room of herself. *You are standing on the scaffold of a lie.*

*I am standing where I can do the most good,* answered the older voice, the father's voice. *From inside. The alternative is outside, where you help no one and hang alone.*

She had run this argument against herself for three years, and it always ended in the same place: a draw, and another morning of dressing like armor.

"So I declare it," Kestrian said, and the tenderness left his voice all at once, the way a lamp goes out. "By my authority as Protector of the Realm, by right of the peace I have kept and the dead I have avenged: every swordspeaker is hereby declared traitor to the realm. Their guild charters are revoked. Their oath-stones will be broken and ground for road-fill. Their blades are forfeit. And any soul who shelters one, feeds one, or speaks kindly of one shares their treason."

The groan became a roar this time, and the roar had teeth in it. Somewhere in the middle distance of the crowd, Maren saw a man strike another man — saw the blow land, saw guards wade in, saw blood on flagstones that had hosted coronations — and no one, including her, moved to learn what the fight had been about. It didn't matter anymore. Treason was everywhere now. That was the point of treason.

"And know this," Kestrian said, raising one hand, and the roar bent toward silence like wheat under wind. "I take no joy in this. I have loved the swordspeakers as one loves a wayward child. But love does not stay justice's hand." He turned his head, slowly, until his gaze found the second tier of the scaffold, and settled on Maren with the weight of a hand on her shoulder.

"There remains one," he said. "One last of that cursed line. One who carries the old magic in her mouth and the old treason in her blood. My officers report she moves north, through the Thornfell passes, in the company of smugglers." A pause, measured to the breath. "Inquisitor-General Duskwell."

Maren stepped forward to the rail. Forty thousand faces tilted up toward her, and she gave them nothing — no expression, no mercy, no fear. Below, someone spat. It fell short.

"You have hunted the oath-traitors for three years," Kestrian said. "You have taken down the guild masters, the stone-keepers, the cultists of the old speech. You have earned rest, and I regret denying it. But there is one order left, and it must be yours, because only you have never failed me." He smiled — and Maren, watching closely, saw that the smile reached his eyes the way light reaches the bottom of a well. "Bring me the last swordspeaker. Alive. Unharmed. Her voice intact."

*Her.* Maren kept her face still, but something ticked over behind her ribs. The reports said the last swordspeaker's name was Astrid Halloran, age uncertain, sex recorded variously. Kestrian had just said *her* to forty thousand people, with certainty he should not have possessed. Someone knew more than the reports showed. Or something did.

"Alive," Kestrian repeated, quieter, and the whole square hushed to hear it. "Do you understand why alive, Inquisitor-General? Because a dead traitor is a story's end, and I am not finished with this story. Because her magic dies with her, and I want it *breathing*. Because—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do in three years of service; it faltered, half a heartbeat, on a word — "because the mountain remembers, and I intend to ask it what it remembers about the House of Halloran."

Then it was gone, the falter, smoothed over like a hand passing over a wrinkle in cloth. He raised his arm to the crowd.

"Vaelmoor endures! The realm endures! Bring me the traitor, and the fever dies with her line!"

The square detonated. Forty thousand throats, grief transmuted into purpose, roaring the crowned thorn up into the cold morning air. Banners unfurled. Guards' spears came up in salute. Beside Maren, an elderly minister wept openly with relief, as though a weight he'd carried for years had finally been given permission to set itself down.

Maren saluted. Held it. Turned on her heel and descended from the scaffold with her spine a rod and her face a door.

---

The order came to her office within the hour, as promised, written in Kestrian's own hand — a rarity he deployed like currency. She read it standing at her window, overlooking the grey city, while the proclamation criers repeated their rounds in the streets below and somewhere, distantly, glass broke.

*To the Inquisitor-General, Maren Duskwell: Find Astrid Halloran. Take her living. Take her voice intact. Spare no expense. Trust no officer above the rank of captain with the full truth of your commission. Report to me alone. — K.*

She read it twice. Then she set it in the iron box where she kept documents too dangerous to file, and sat down at her desk, and pulled toward her the folder marked *HALLORAN, A. — LAST KNOWN MOVEMENTS.*

Three years of paper. Border reports, tavern sightings, a witness statement describing a tall figure in a rain cloak who had ended a bar-fight in Saltmarsh by *talking* to the combatants' weapons until they grew too heavy to lift. A sketch, done from memory by a harbor clerk: sharp-boned face, watchful eyes, a scar through one eyebrow. Maren had looked at that sketch more times than she could count. She knew the shape of that face better than some faces she loved.

And now, beneath the papers, the thing she had pulled from the folder's bottom that morning and had not yet let herself examine: a single sheet in Corvale's careful alchemist's hand, slipped to her anonymously two nights ago, unsigned.

*The Grey Fever samples were seeded. The guild-territory correlation was constructed backward from the desired result. I participated. Ask me nothing, and burn this.*

She hadn't burned it.

She sat with it now, and with the order in its iron box, and with the sounds of the city below — the criers, the breaking glass, a mob forming somewhere with a purpose she had helped hand them — and she performed the arithmetic she had been performing for three years, and which had never once balanced.

On one side: her father's creed. Order. The honest blade. The belief that someone decent had to stay inside the machine, greasing the parts that could be greased, blocking what could be blocked, saving whom could be saved. On that side too: three years of results she could point to. Interrogations halted mid-torture because she arrived. Sentences commuted. Two of her own inquisitors dismissed for brutality. A prison wing where her name was known and feared in the specific way that meant prisoners slept better when it appeared on their transfer orders.

On the other side: today. Forty thousand people roaring for the blood of a person Maren had reason to believe was innocent of everything except existing. A plague blamed on the blameless. An order signed in a beautiful hand to bring that person breathing to a man who drank something in a vault, and who had said *her* with a certainty that smelled of sorcery, and who had faltered on the word *mountain* as though it cost him something.

And underneath both sides, in the foundation, where she kept the thing she never said aloud: her brother Tam.

Tam, who had died of the Grey Fever at fourteen, coughing blood into a rag in a rented room while Maren, twenty-two and already wearing grey, held his hand and promised him the crown would find a cure. Tam, whose death was the reason she had joined the inquisition at all — who had made her want, with a hunger that had never dulled, to find the *why* of things and drag it into the light.

If Corvale's note was true, then the why of Tam's death had been sitting in a palace vault this entire time, wearing a crown of thorns, drinking youth from stolen vows.

Maren closed the folder. She opened the iron box. She took out the order and read the signature again, and noticed — for the first time, and how strange that she had never noticed it before — that Kestrian's *K* was drawn exactly like the sigil stamped on the confiscated oath-blades in the vault manifests. The same crowned thorn. The same curve.

Coincidence. Everything was coincidence, until it wasn't.

She sent for her captain.

"Ready a column," she said. "Twenty picked riders. Cold rations, no fires, no banners. We ride north within the hour."

"North, General? Toward Thornfell? There's nothing up there but—"

"I know what's up there." She was already pulling maps from the shelf — the military surveys, and beneath them, the older ones, the smuggler's charts sold in river-town back rooms that marked roads no army had ever officially acknowledged. Roads that remembered footsteps, according to drunk miners. She bought such things the way she bought everything: quietly, and against future need. "The last swordspeaker is in those passes. And I want her found before the mob finds her first. Do you understand the distinction?"

The captain, a stolid woman named Breck who had served her six years, studied her face with the frankness of long service. "And when we find her, General?"

"When we find her, we bring her in." Maren met her eyes steadily. "Alive. As ordered."

Breck nodded and went out. Maren remained at the window a moment longer, watching the smoke of burning guild-halls rise over the eastern districts — already, then, the proclamation bearing fruit — and pressed her thumb once more against the ache behind her eyes.

*Alive,* she thought. *Unharmed. Voice intact.*

She had spent three years telling herself that staying inside the machine was a form of mercy. That she was the grease, the brake, the hand on the lever that slowed the worst of it. And she would tell herself that again on the road north, and again in the passes, and perhaps even at the moment she finally put irons on Astrid Halloran — because that was the nature of the machine, that it let you keep telling yourself stories right up until the moment the stories ran out.

But she caught herself, in the last moment before duty closed over her like water, thinking of Tam. Fourteen years old. Coughing blood. Believing her.

*Find the why,* she had promised him. *Drag it into the light.*

She buckled on her commission blade — honest steel, unsworn, silent — and went down to her horse.

The mountain was waiting. So, somewhere in it, was the truth.

* * *

They made the foothills by nightfall of the third day, riding hard through country that grew poorer and stonier the further north they climbed, and it was there, in a waystation inn called the Drowned Bell, that Maren heard the first true rumor of her quarry.

Not from spies. Not from informers. From a smuggler's child, maybe nine years old, sitting on the inn's step in the blue dusk, whittling a stick into the shape of a sword.

"That's a fine blade," Maren said. She had a way with children that surprised people who knew her reputation; she spoke to them like small adults, without cooing, and they trusted it.

"It's not real," the girl said. "It doesn't talk."

"No. They don't talk much these days."

The girl looked up at her with the flat, assessing stare of children raised around danger. "My gran says there's one left. A real one. Says she came through here on a caravan, heading for the high passes." The girl resumed whittling. "Gran says the swordspeakers used to swear vows into steel, and the steel would remember. Says my granddad's plow-blade still remembers him, a little. He died in the Fever."

Maren crouched, unhurried, bringing her eyes level with the child's. "Did your gran see her? The swordspeaker?"

"Saw her buy rope. Said she paid too much and wouldn't haggle. Gran says that's how you know a swordspeaker — they pay what a thing costs, because their word is worth the same everywhere." The girl shrugged, suddenly bored with antiquity. "Gran cried after, though. Said the swordspeaker looked at her plow-blade and said sorry to it. Who says sorry to a knife?"

Maren rose slowly. Her heart was doing something complicated that she declined to examine.

Someone who pays what a thing costs. Someone who apologizes to steel. Someone a child's grandmother wept over, sight unseen, in a doorway, for reasons the grandmother probably couldn't have named — because kindness, real kindness, has a texture that survives even when everything else about a legend rots away.

*Alive. Unharmed. Voice intact.*

She went inside and gave the order to ride at dawn, and lay down on the inn's narrow bed without removing her boots, and stared at the ceiling beams, and did not sleep.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not make balance, the equation that had no solution she could write: she was hunting, with genuine skill and genuine relentlessness, a person who said *sorry* to knives. And every mile north brought her closer to the day she would have to choose between the machine that had given her Tam's death a meaning — or at least a *where* to aim her grief — and whatever the truth was, waiting under Thornfell like a candle behind bone.

Somewhere ahead, a swordspeaker was climbing toward a mountain that remembered everything.

And Maren Duskwell, inquisitor-general, keeper of the honest blade, follower of orders, was climbing after her — carrying, in the iron box against her heart, an order she intended to follow, and a note she refused to burn, and a promise to a dead boy that she was beginning to suspect could not both be kept.

The horses stamped in the yard below. The mountain stood black against the stars.

She closed her eyes, and did not sleep, and in the morning they rode.
