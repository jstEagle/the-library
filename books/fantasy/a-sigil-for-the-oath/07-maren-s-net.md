## 7. Maren's Net

The river came out of the high country the way grief does — quietly at first, then all at once.

Astrid heard it before they saw it, a low grinding under the birdsong, and pulled Sef off the road with two fingers hooked in his collar. "There."

"The ford?" Sef squinted through the alder screen. Below them, the Greywater spread wide and shallow over pale stones, braided into a dozen silver threads that rejoined on the far bank. A waystation hut slumped at the crossing like an old man asleep against a fence post. Smoke from its chimney. A boat turned over beside it. Everything about it said *safe*.

"Everything about it says safe," Astrid murmured.

"That's what I was thinking too."

They had been three days out of the salt caves, moving north along roads that existed on no surveyor's chart, and the strange ease of it had been sitting wrong in Astrid's chest since morning. Smuggler roads were supposed to be hard. This one had fed them — a snare with a hare in it, still warm; a cache of smoked fish wrapped in waxed cloth, dated this season. The road remembered footsteps, yes, but lately it seemed to remember someone else's footsteps better than theirs. Someone walking ahead of them. Laying the table.

"Could be kindness," Sef offered, though he didn't sound convinced. He hadn't sounded convinced of anything since the Codex. He carried it now strapped to his back like a wounded animal he refused to put down.

"Kindness doesn't leave fresh fish," Astrid said. "Kindness leaves old boots and a note saying sorry." They crouched in the alders and studied the ford the way their grandmother had taught them to study anything they intended to survive: not for what it was, but for what wanted to be seen. The waystation. The upturned boat. The ford itself, broad and inviting, the water barely knee-deep across its whole glittering width.

Too easy to cross. That was the thing. Every smuggler knew you didn't build a ford that easy. You made travelers funnel, made them choose, because a traveler who chooses is a traveler who can be predicted.

"There's another crossing upstream," Astrid said slowly. "Two days north. The Stones' Marriage, we called it. Boulders thrown across the narrows like a quarrel between giants."

"Two days." Sef did the arithmetic aloud, the way he always did, lips moving. "Maren Duskwell has been behind us since the waystation fire. If she's riding hard—"

"She isn't riding hard. She's riding *comfortable*." Astrid sat back on their heels. The oath-blade hummed faintly at their hip, the way it did when a vow spoken into it was near something that could unmake vows. It had started humming an hour ago, and Astrid had told themselves it was the river stone, the iron in the hills. They were done telling themselves things. "She wants us to cross somewhere. She'd rather it be here."

"So we go north. Two days. We lose time but keep our skins."

"We go north," Astrid agreed.

And that was when the singing started.

* * *

It rose out of the river valley like heat-shimmer given a throat. At first Astrid took it for water over stone — rivers do sing, in their fashion, a long syllable repeated until the mind stops hearing it. But then the syllable changed, and it was a name. It was *their* name, sung in a voice like a struck glass, pure and sweet and utterly without mercy, and every hair on Astrid's arms stood up.

"Echo-singer," Sef breathed. He had gone the color of tallow. "Under the bank. Under the *water*, maybe. Astrid, get away from the edge—"

"I'm not at the edge." But their feet had moved, sometime in the last ten heartbeats, down the slope, toward the ford, toward the sweet clear voice that was singing their name as if it loved them. Astrid looked down at their own boots planted on the path and felt cold pour down their spine. "Sef. I'm walking."

"I can see that you're walking!"

"It's not pulling me." Astrid dug their heels in, physically stopped, and examined the sensation with the clinical part of their mind, the part Grandmother Halloran had called the sword-room. "It's not pulling. It's — asking. It sings my name and my body thinks it's been *vouched for*. Like a door opening because someone inside knows you." They laughed, and hated the sound of it. "That's clever. That's genuinely clever. Who taught the inquisitors to be clever?"

"Maren Duskwell went to the Collegium at Vaelmoor before Kestrian ever—" Sef caught himself, swallowed. "I read her file. Before. When I still had access." His hands were shaking as he fumbled at the Codex's straps. "Bound echo-singers are in the forbidden index. Third rank of proscription. Their voices don't compel — they *unravel*. Every vow you've ever sworn, they can hear the seams. And they sing along the seams."

The humming of the oath-blade sharpened into something almost like pain. Astrid wrapped their hand around the grip and spoke into the steel, low, the old cadence, words that had come to them whole since childhood: *"Steel remembers what I say. Let no voice but mine wear it away."*

The blade flared warm. The song faltered — one skipped beat, like a singer stumbling on a stair.

Then it resumed, and now it was singing *the counterwords*. Astrid's own vow, played back to them in reverse, in that beautiful glass voice, and where the reversed syllables touched the blade the warmth guttered like a candle in wind.

"Oh," Astrid said softly. "Oh, that's how it works."

"What?"

"It learns your oaths by listening to you fight them. Every word I speak into the steel, it takes home with it." Astrid let go of the hilt as if it had bitten them. Down at the ford, the water glittered on, innocent. "If I speak one more vow anywhere near that thing, it'll have me entire. I could stand at the far side of this valley and it would sing my sword dead in a week."

"Then don't fight it," Sef said. "Run. North. The Stones' Marriage. It can't unravel what you don't say."

"No," Astrid said. Because they had finally seen it — the third thing, the thing that had been bothering them since the fish. Upstream, half-hidden in the willows, a line of picket stakes. Horses. And on the far bank, positioned exactly where a fleeing pair would emerge from the ford, wet and slow and grateful for solid ground: six men in travel-grey, lounging like farmers waiting for rain to end. Not hiding at all anymore. Done hiding.

"They weren't trying to catch us at the ford," Astrid said. "The ford is the *anvil*. We run north, we're tired, we take the only other crossing within four days, and it's waiting there instead. The echo-singer isn't the trap. The echo-singer is the *shepherd dog*." They watched the grey men by the water and felt something settle in them, cold and clear. "She's herding us onto ground she chose. Which means she studied me. My maps, probably. Sef — who else has copies of Halloran route-maps?"

Sef's face did something complicated. "The Archivum. Before my exile, I catalogued — there was a bequest, forty years ago, a full set of—"

"Then she's read the same maps I have, plus everything the Archivum knows about swordspeakers, plus three years of reports on me." Astrid exhaled. "Good. Then she's expecting me to do the sensible thing. Run north, save my voice, spend my legs."

"And are you going to do the sensible thing?"

"I'm going to do the *expensive* thing." Astrid drew the blade. The song swelled instantly, eagerly, and they let it listen while they talked, because talking cost nothing. "Every vow I've ever spoken into this steel is a payment plan, Sef. Did I ever tell you that? Grandmother never lied to me about it. Each vow takes a memory. Small ones, mostly. The taste of my first winter apple. The name of the boy who taught me dice. I've been spending myself for fifteen years and calling it strength."

"Astrid—"

"But there are vows and there are *vows*." They turned the blade so the light ran along it. "And there's one I've kept in reserve since the night I swore it, because it's the cheapest heavy thing I own. I swore to my grandmother's grave that I would never cross running water after dark. Don't ask me why. She was dying, she was frightened of something, and I promised, and the promise took a memory I've never even missed." Their jaw tightened. "It's dusk in an hour. If I break it, I lose whatever she took in trade. If I keep it, Maren owns the night, the ford, and both crossings."

Sef stared at them. "You'd pay a memory just to—"

"I'd pay a memory to choose the *shape* of the loss," Astrid said. "That's the whole trick of it. That's the whole cursed trick of every bargain anyone's ever made under this mountain's shadow." They lifted the blade skyward, and the echo-song below rose to meet them, sweet and hungry, already learning.

* * *

They came down to the ford at last light, openly, the way you approach a thing that has already seen you.

The grey men straightened from their lounging. On the near bank, beneath the waystation's leaning eave, stood a woman in a riding coat the color of wet slate, hands folded, posture of someone attending a recital. Maren Duskwell was smaller than her reputation — Astrid had imagined a tower of a woman, and got instead someone compact and precise, with a face built for listening rather than speaking. Her eyes found Astrid's across the water and did not narrow, did not gloat. That was somehow worse.

"Swordspeaker Halloran," she called, and her voice carried flat and clear over the river without effort. "Inquisitor-General Maren Duskwell. I'm ordered to bring you to Vaelmoor alive. I'd prefer to do it politely."

"You've brought a bound singer," Astrid called back. "The Collegium's third proscription. There are people in the free cities who'd hang you for that alone."

"There are people in the free cities who'd hang me for breathing." No anger in it. Inventory. "You've deduced the shape of the evening. Good. I dislike wasted hours. The ford is open. My men will not touch you or your companion. Cross, and walk to me, and no one bleeds."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you spend the night on that bank with a voice singing your vows apart, and in the morning you're considerably less than you were, and I collect the remainder." She tilted her head, and for one unguarded moment something moved behind her eyes that was not ambition at all — something tired, and old, and sad. "I've read the Archivum's files, Swordspeaker. All of them. Do you know what struck me? Not the power. The *price list*. Fifteen years of payments. I confess I find the arithmetic obscene. Come to Vaelmoor. Refuse the usurper to his face, if you like. But stop paying."

For a heartbeat — one whole heartbeat — Astrid almost believed her.

Then the echo-song crested again, and this time it wasn't singing their name. It was singing Grandmother Halloran's deathbed cadence, the exact rise and fall of *steel remembers what I say*, learned from the single vow Astrid had spoken on the ridge, wearing her memory like a stolen coat, and the tenderness in Maren's voice curdled into strategy, because that was the whole game: make the cage feel like shelter.

Astrid raised the blade to the darkening sky, and spoke.

*"I vowed to the grave that I'd not cross water after dark. I unswear it. I unsay it. I pay."*

The world went white at the edges.

Not pain — pain would have been bearable, pain would have been honest. It was subtraction. Somewhere in Astrid's head a room door opened and something walked out carrying a lamp, and Astrid stood on the riverbank watching it go and could not afterward have said what left, only that there had been a lamp, once, and a figure holding it, and the figure had —

had —

*A face.* The absence arrived a half-second after the loss, the way thunder follows lightning. There had been a woman whose face Astrid knew better than any face in the world. There had been a kitchen, a smell of bread, hands — whose hands? — smoothing a blanket over a feverish child. *Whose child?* The memories were still there, all of them, but every one was a portrait with the face scratched out. Bread rising. A lullaby with no mouth singing it. Love, vast and specific and entirely orphaned, aimed at a blank.

Astrid staggered. Sef caught their elbow. "What did you pay? What did it take?"

"My mother," Astrid said, and heard their own voice come out level, which was its own kind of horror. "Her face. Maybe more than her face — the edges go with it. Her voice. Whether she was tall." A laugh scraped out of them. "You know what's funny? I used to think the worst vow-loss would be something dramatic. A battle. A betrayal. No. It's bread. It's a blanket. It's loving someone so much and not knowing what she looked like when she smiled at me."

Downriver, the echo-singer's song broke apart mid-phrase. The broken vow had torn a seam in it too — a bound voice fed on vows, and now one of its threads snapped and frayed. Astrid heard it clearly: beneath the glass purity, something small and human sobbed, once, and was silenced.

They looked at Maren Duskwell across the water, and she was looking past them, at the willows where the singer hid, and her folded hands had gone white-knuckled, and her face — her careful listening face — had cracked along the same seam.

"Cross now," she said, and her voice wasn't flat anymore. "Please. While it's wounded. Before my men regroup. I won't shoot." A pause, and quieter, almost to herself: "I didn't know it could scream."

Astrid didn't trust her. Astrid understood, with a new and terrible clarity, that trust was exactly the currency this whole war traded in, and that everyone — the usurper, the council, the ancestor beneath the mountain, the prophecy itself — wanted to buy from them at their own price.

But the singer was wounded. The ford was open. The night was theirs for one borrowed hour.

They waded in. The Greywater closed around their knees, cold as honesty, and the oath-blade hung silent and lightless at their hip — poorer now, emptier, and somehow more truly theirs.

Behind them, Maren Duskwell turned to her men and began, in a voice like filed iron, giving orders that would scatter them upstream for two crucial days. Ahead, on the far bank, Sef waited with the Codex strapped to his back, and when Astrid reached him he said nothing about the memory, which was its own kindness.

Only later, wrapping their feet by a fire of wet alder, did Astrid notice the page of the Codex lying open where Sef had checked it — checked it reflexively, the way he did a dozen times a day now — and see that tonight's entry had grown by one line, ink older than either of their grandmothers:

*At the Greywater she pays the mother's face, and the river keeps it.*

The river kept it. Astrid stared at those five words until the fire burned low, and understood that somewhere downstream, in the cold braided channels of the Greywater, something now held a woman's smile that belonged to them — and that sooner or later, everything sold beneath this mountain's shadow ended up in the same market.

Far to the east, beyond the hills, the free cities were lighting their councils. And the Codex's next page, Astrid saw as Sef closed it, was dated tomorrow — and bore a name that was not theirs at all.
