## 8. The Free Cities' Council

The council chamber of Harrowgate had been built to hold arguments, and it held them the way a riverbed holds water — patiently, endlessly, wearing grooves into everything that passed through.

Ivo Tanzer had sat in those grooves for three years now, the youngest delegate ever seated by the Saltmarsh Compact, and he knew every creak of the floor. He knew which delegates leaned forward when they meant to lie. He knew that Delegate Ostrava of Greywater polished her spectacles when she was stalling, and that old Veymar of Dunmark fell asleep with his eyes open, and that Chancellor Rellis counted the votes before anyone spoke, and announced them after, as though the debate were a formality performed for the benefit of history.

Today the chamber was full past its capacity. Word had come up the coast road three days ago: Kestrian's legions had crossed the Weir at last, thirty thousand men under banners of black and gold, and his heralds rode ahead of them through every market town reading the same proclamation from the same scroll.

*All swordspeakers are traitors to the realm.*

*One swordspeaker remains.*

*Deliver her, and be spared.*

Ivo had read a copy of the proclamation himself, brought in by a fish-merchant who'd paid a herald two coppers to let him copy it down. The ink was cheap and the hand was hasty, but the words were precise in the way of things drafted by lawyers rather than soldiers. *Deliver her.* Not *find her*. Not *hunt her*. Deliver. As though Astrid Halloran were a debt owed, a tithe, a thing the free cities had been keeping on their shelves without realizing it.

He'd read it eleven times. Each time, he found something new to hate about it.

"The question before us," Chancellor Rellis said, "is not whether we admire the last swordspeaker. I'm sure some of us do. It is whether we admire her more than we love our own children." He spread his hands, a man presenting an obvious truth to children of his own. "Thirty thousand soldiers are crossing the Weir. Harrowgate's walls have never been stormed, but they've also never been tested by anything like this. We have grain for four months and walls that will hold for six weeks. The arithmetic is not difficult."

"Arithmetic," said Delegate Ostrava, "is exactly what this is. One life against—"

"One life," Ivo said, and then heard his own voice crack across the chamber, too loud, too young. Forty faces turned toward him. He made himself continue, quieter. "Forgive me. But she isn't one life. She's one person, yes, but what she carries — the oath, the swordspeech, whatever it truly is — no one in this room knows what happens if it's taken from her. No one knows what Kestrian does with it."

"We know what he does without it," Veymar said, eyes still open, voice flat. "He burns things. He doesn't need magic to burn things."

"No," Ivo agreed. "But there's a reason he wants her alive. There's a reason the proclamation says *deliver* rather than *destroy*. And there's a reason—" He stopped, steadied himself against the rail of the delegate's box. Say it plainly, he told himself. Say it plainly or sit down forever. "There's a reason the Vellum Codex names her. Some of you know I've been petitioning this council for two years to open the restricted archive. The Codex is real. The prophecies in it have come true — not metaphorically, not approximately, but true, line for line, date for date. And the page that concerns our present moment says that a soul sold beneath Thornfell will be either the free cities' ruin or their salvation, depending on whether it stands free or kneels."

Rellis smiled the smile he used on delegations from farming towns. "Delegate Tanzer. You're asking forty practical people to stake the survival of six cities on a book of fortunes. A book, I might add, that your own archivists declared heretical and sealed behind three locks."

"I'm asking you to consider that the seals were imposed by a chancellor who is now dead, whose successor is now Kestrian's quartermaster-general, and whose seals were designed to keep us ignorant precisely so that days like today would go the way he wanted them to."

Murmurs. Somewhere above, gulls cried against the glass of the clerestory windows. Rellis let the murmurs run, patient as tide.

"Let us suppose," Rellis said at last, "that the Codex is genuine. Suppose the prophecy is real. Then tell me, Delegate — what does your prophecy actually promise? Concretely. Grain? Walls? Soldiers?"

"It promises that the mountain opens," Ivo said. "That something bound beneath Thornfell for a century is freed. That the freeing breaks a power that would otherwise—"

"A power," Ostrava interrupted gently, "that currently belongs to a woman running from inquisitors through smuggler country, who has no army, no city, no seat at any table, and no obligation to spend herself on us even if she survives. You want us to bet on a stranger's virtue because a book said nice things."

"I want us to bet on the truth," Ivo said. "Because Kestrian's whole reign is built on everyone agreeing not to look at it. He blames the plague on swordspeakers. Our own physicians traced the alchemical signature of the grey-fever to court laboratories two years ago — I've read the reports, they exist, three of the physicians who signed them are dead and the fourth fled to the Saltmarsh. If we surrender Astrid Halloran, we aren't buying peace. We're signing a confession written by a murderer, in our own hands."

Silence, of the heavy kind. Ivo realized his fists had closed on the rail until his knuckles ached, and made them open.

It was Veymar, surprisingly, who broke it. The old man's eyes came back from wherever they'd been, and fixed on Ivo with something almost like pity. "You argue like your mother," he said. "She stood where you're standing, twenty years ago, and argued the same way — that truth was a weapon and honesty a strategy. You know how that ended."

Ivo knew. Everyone knew. His mother had spent her last year in office fighting Kestrian's trade embargo, alone, abandoned vote by vote by colleagues who found her principles expensive. She'd died before the embargo broke her city, but not before it broke her.

"She ended," Ivo said carefully, "on the right side of history. Late. But on it."

"She ended early," Veymar said, "and the city went on without her. That's what cities do, boy. They outlast the people who love them too much. It's their whole genius." He turned to Rellis. "Call the question."

* * *

They called the question at dusk.

Ivo didn't stay to hear it read formally. He stood in the antechamber while the tally came through the doors in the clerk's thin voice — thirty-one for negotiation, five abstaining, four against — and did the arithmetic himself, because arithmetic was apparently all this was. Thirty-one cities willing to hand over a woman none of them had met, to a man none of them trusted, in exchange for a mercy none of them believed in.

Four against. He'd been one of the four. It felt less like courage than like being left standing on a beach after a wave.

"You should eat something."

He turned. Sella Voss, the delegate from Cinderport, stood in the doorway holding two cups of the terrible spiced wine the council served during long sessions. She was fifty, broad, weathered; she'd captained grain ships through pirate waters for twenty years before politics took her. She pressed a cup into his hand whether he wanted it or not.

"Thirty-one to four," Ivo said.

"Thirty-two, effectively. Rellis counts abstentions as yeses when it suits him, and it'll suit him tonight." Sella drank, grimaced at her own wine. "You gave a good speech."

"It changed nothing."

"It changed me, and I voted with you, so technically it changed nothing twice over." She leaned against the wall beside him. Below them, through the antechamber's tall window, Harrowgate's harbor lay in lamplight, fishing boats coming home dark, and beyond the breakwater the black line of the sea. Somewhere out there, east and south, an army was making camp. "Can I ask you something honest, Tanzer? Do you believe it? Truly? The Codex, the prophecy, the mountain? Or do you just need it to be true, because the alternative is handing a person to a butcher and calling it statecraft?"

Ivo looked at the harbor a long time.

"When I was fourteen," he said, "my mother took me to see the Codex. Before the seals. One hour, one candle, one guard at the door who owed her a favor. She turned the pages and told me: most of this is nonsense, Ivi. Kings' horoscopes, weather charms, the usual rot. But some pages — she tapped one — some pages are written in a hand that makes my teeth ache. Like the words know they'll come true and are smug about it." He turned the cup in his hands. "I thought she was being fanciful. Mothers are allowed. But I've read those pages since, and my teeth ache, Sella. Something wrote our future down a hundred years ago, and it wasn't kind about it, and the only person alive who can change what it says is a woman this council just voted to sell."

Sella was quiet for a moment. "Then I'm sorry," she said finally, "for what the council is about to make you do."

"What's that?"

"Whatever your conscience can't live with." She drained her cup. "It always comes to that, in the end. Politics is just the slow process of finding out what your conscience can't live with, and then living with it anyway, or leaving." She squeezed his shoulder once, hard, and walked out.

Ivo stayed at the window until the lamps below burned low.

* * *

The hunters left within the week.

That was the part that astonished him afterward — not the vote, which he'd expected, but the speed. Within four days the council had chartered three companies of freelance trackers through the Smugglers' Guild, issued warrants stamped with the seal of the Compact authorizing "retrieval of the asset Halloran for protective custody," and dispatched a courier to the usurper's camp bearing terms so obsequious that Ivo had refused, formally, to sign the delegation's letter of credence. His refusal was minuted. Nobody cared.

He watched the first company ride out from the walls on a grey morning — eight riders, professional, unmarked, led by a lean Greywater man named Corvess whom Ivo had seen once before, years ago, testifying with perfect calm about a cargo of slaves he'd personally liberated and resold. Retrieval. Protective custody. Words with clean edges and rotten cores.

"They'll reach Thornfell country inside ten days," Sella said beside him. She'd come to stand with him again, or perhaps just to watch; her face gave nothing away. "If the inquisitors don't get there first."

"Then we're racing to rob her," Ivo said. "Us or Duskwell. Either way she loses."

"Either way she loses," Sella agreed, "unless someone reaches her first and tells her what's coming. From both directions." She said it lightly, looking at the riders diminishing down the coast road, and Ivo understood — not all at once, but the way a wall understands it is falling, gradually and then completely — that she hadn't come to comfort him.

He turned. "Sella."

"Eight riders," she said. "Corvess keeps good horses but bad company. The second company leaves tomorrow from the north gate. There's a ford at the Marrow where they'll water — a day's rest, no more. After that, the passes." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were tired and absolutely steady. "I voted with you, Ivo. Four against thirty-one is a vote, but it isn't a plan. So I'm asking you, as the only person in that chamber young enough and stupid enough: do you have one?"

The wind came off the harbor and smelled of salt and tar and the far cold sea. Down the coast road, the riders were specks now, swallowed by the distance, carrying warrants with his city's seal toward a woman whose name was written in a book that made his teeth ache.

Ivo thought of his mother, ending early, on the right side of history. He thought of the antechamber, and Sella's voice: *whatever your conscience can't live with.* He thought about how a council of forty could decide a stranger's fate in an afternoon, and how the decision weighed nothing in any hand that cast it, and how somewhere on a switchback road beneath Thornfell that weight was about to land on shoulders that had never voted for any of it.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "I think I do."

Sella nodded slowly, unsurprised, already turning away. "Then don't pack light," she said, "and don't pack loudly. And Ivo—" she paused at the door, not looking back, "—write nothing down. Your mother wrote everything down. It's how they caught up to her in the end."

The door closed. Ivo stood alone in the emptying chamber, in the groove worn by a hundred years of other people's decisions, holding the shape of one that was finally, terrifyingly, his own.
