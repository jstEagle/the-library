## 9. Bone-Lit Rumors

The miner was dying in the back room of the Waystone Inn in Grayhollow, and everyone in town knew it, and no one said it aloud, which told Astrid something about how this place worked before they'd even crossed the threshold.

They'd come down out of the high passes with Sef three days after the ice bridge, moving slow because Sef's ribs were strapped and every jolt of the road turned them gray-lipped and silent. Grayhollow was a mining town the way a scar is skin — stretched over something that had torn it open. The mountain loomed south of it, Thornfell proper now, its peak lost in cloud, and the whole town seemed to orient itself around that absence the way a wound orients around the blade.

The innkeeper took their coin without questions, which cost extra. The room had one bed and a window facing away from the mountain, and Astrid lay awake most of the night listening to Sef breathe shallowly and to the sound beneath everything else — a hum so low it wasn't a sound at all, more a pressure in the teeth.

"You hear it too," Sef said near dawn. Not a question.

"It's the mine pumps," Astrid said. "Or wind in the shafts."

"It's not."

Astrid didn't argue. They'd stopped arguing with Sef about the mountain somewhere on the second day, after the archivist had spent an hour mapping the sound's direction against the stars like a man triangulating a siege engine. Sef heard things in it Astrid didn't — cadence, maybe. Pattern. *Voices*, Sef kept almost saying, and then not saying.

The dying miner's name was Oskar Venn, and they learned it from the innkeeper's daughter, who brought their breakfast and their gossip in the same breath: old Oskar had come up from the deep workings six weeks ago with his lungs full of something that wasn't stone dust, and he'd been fading ever since, and he'd been asking — for two weeks now, paying coppers he could barely afford — for anyone traveling the high roads. Specifically for anyone who talked to their sword.

Astrid set down their cup. "Who told him about me?"

"Nobody tells anybody anything in Grayhollow," the girl said, with the flat pride of someone stating local geography. "But Tam saw you in the yard yesterday, showing the carter how to true a warped blade, and when you touched the edge you did the thing. The humming thing. Tam's brother works the deep levels. He heard Oskar say if a swordspeaker ever came through, he had something that belonged to them." She shrugged. "Belonged to *them* plural, mind. Like he meant all of you. But there aren't any of you left, are there?"

There weren't. That was rather the point.

* * *

Oskar Venn's room was at the top of a narrow house that leaned against its neighbor like a drunk against a wall. The stairs smelled of tallow and wet stone. Sef waited below — Astrid had asked them to, and Sef, whose pride was still bruised from needing help down a flight of steps in Split Pass, had agreed with a tightness that said they understood why.

The miner lay propped on pillows gone yellow as old parchment. He was a big man going hollow, the flesh falling off his frame the way snow slides off a roof in thaw, and his eyes were the strangest part: too bright, fixed on Astrid the moment they entered, tracking them across the room with an alertness his body couldn't match.

"Swordspeaker," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow.

"People keep saying that word like it's a diagnosis."

"Isn't it?" A wheeze that might have been a laugh. "Sit. No — don't sit. Stand where I can see your hands. Old habit. Down below we learned to watch hands, not faces. Faces lie. Hands are honest."

Astrid stood where they were asked. "Your granddaughter said you've been paying travelers to find me. Coppers you need for medicine."

"Medicine's done buying me anything." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report a closed road. "Six weeks I've been dying and it's the best work I've ever done. Now. Before the pleasantries rot us both — do you know what's under Thornfell?"

"Rock," Astrid said. "Ore veins. Bad air."

"Bad air." He smiled, and there was blood in the creases of his lips. "My grandfather mined the deep galleries, back when the Halloran charter still ran them. He wrote things down. Miners don't write things down, generally — it's bad luck, writing what the mountain says — but he was a strange man. Kept a journal in a language my father couldn't read and I can't either. Drawings, though. Drawings I understand."

He turned his head with effort toward a chest at the foot of the bed. "Open it. Careful. Bottom layer's false."

Under wool socks and a broken pick-head, under a false bottom that lifted on a hidden finger-notch worn smooth by generations of hiding, Astrid found a bundle of oilcloth tied with wire. Inside: a journal bound in hide gone stiff and dark, and wrapped separately, nested in rotted felt, a key.

It was iron, but wrong iron — black as a starless sky, dense past sense, cold in a way that had nothing to do with the room. It was long as a hand, shaped like nothing Astrid recognized: not teeth but grooves, spiraling, as if it were meant to be *turned through* something rather than into it. When Astrid's fingers closed around it, the low hum they'd felt since Grayhollow — the pressure in the teeth, the not-sound — shifted.

Not louder. *Nearer.* As if something far below had leaned closer to listen.

"What is it?" Astrid asked, and heard their own voice go quiet.

"Charter key. One of seven, my grandfather wrote. Black iron, forged from ore that came *out* of the sealed levels — the ones the Halloran company walled shut two hundred years ago and paid everyone to forget." Oskar's bright eyes didn't leave their face. "You're doing the thing again. The humming thing. Your sword knows it, doesn't it?"

Astrid looked down. They hadn't drawn the blade, but they could feel it through the scabbard — that faint sympathetic shiver that had been their gift and their curse since childhood, steel answering speech. Except this wasn't answering speech. Nothing had been spoken.

The sword was answering the *key*.

"Your grandfather," Astrid said carefully. "What did he write?"

"That's the part you won't like." Oskar reached a trembling hand toward the journal; Astrid brought it to him. He didn't open it. Just laid his palm flat on the cover, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Lights," he said. "Beneath the sealed galleries, lights that aren't fire. Veins of candlelight running through rock like marrow through bone — that's how he put it. Bone-lit, he called it. And the light moves. Pools where voices pool. Because that's what it is, swordspeaker. Light made of voice. Of *selves*." His breath caught, steadied. "My grandfather watched the Halloran factors carry crates down the deep stair. Crates that clinked like bottles. And he watched them come back up lighter, and richer, and he asked once, just once, what was in them, and the factor laughed and said—"

"*By the pound*," Astrid said.

Silence. Even the house's settling seemed to stop.

Oskar stared at them. "You've heard it."

"I've—" Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "I heard it on the roads. An echo. My grandmother's voice, bargaining with something under the mountain. I thought it was the road replaying her. Roads remember footsteps, sometimes. I thought—" They stopped, because the next part wouldn't shape itself into words, and Oskar waited with the terrible patience of the dying. "I thought it was memory. Not *merchandise*."

"Memory, merchandise." The miner shrugged, and the shrug cost him. "Same thing down there, maybe. Everything's priced. That's what my grandfather wrote at the end, the last entry, letters getting worse like his hand was shaking. *Everything under Thornfell has a price and the price is always the same thing.*" He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he lay back looking smaller. "You know what I mined, these forty years? Salt. Good honest salt, sold by the pound. And every shift, walking down, I'd pass the sealed gallery doors and feel like a sheep walking past a slaughterhouse, except nobody would tell the sheep which building it was."

"Why tell me?" Astrid asked. "Why pay strangers to hunt down a swordspeaker? What do you want, Oskar Venn?"

"To give you this before I can't." He nodded at the key in their hand. "And to ask you a question, and I'll only ask once, because dying's expensive and I haven't got time to be polite." He pulled himself higher on the pillows, and for a moment the brightness in his eyes was almost fierce. "The Hallorans owned those sealed levels. Owned the charter, owned the factors, owned whatever walked out of the bargain they cut. Are you the last of them?"

"I'm the last of everything," Astrid said. "Including, apparently, the debts."

"Then here's my question." His hand found theirs, closing over the black-iron key, pressing both together until the metal bit cold into their palm. "When you go down there — and you will, that key wants you to, I can see it wanting — when you stand in front of whatever your family made, are you going to be a Halloran? Or are you going to be the thing that ends them?"

The hum beneath the town rose, very slightly. Or perhaps Astrid only imagined it. Perhaps they only imagined that it sounded, just then, like an held breath.

"I don't know yet," Astrid said honestly.

"Good answer." Oskar released their hand and fell back against the pillows, spent, grinning at the ceiling. "My grandfather wrote that the honest ones never know till they're standing in it. Said the liars always know right away." His grin softened into something older and wearier. "Go on, then. Let an old man rest. Come back tomorrow if you want the journal — I'll have my granddaughter read you the parts she can. But take the key tonight, because I want to die knowing it's already walking toward the door it was made for."

Astrid stood a moment longer. The key sat in their palm like a piece of midnight, patient and cold, and far below — through rock and salt and sealed doors and two hundred years of paid-for forgetting — something waited with the courtesy of a creditor.

"Who do I thank?" Astrid asked at the door. "You? Your grandfather?"

"Thank the mountain," Oskar said, eyes already closing. "It let me live long enough to matter."

* * *

Sef was waiting in the inn's common room, hunched over a table with maps spread flat and weighted with cups, pretending — badly — that they hadn't been counting the minutes. Their ribs were strapped, their face was still the color of tallow candles, and their eyes went straight to Astrid's closed fist.

"Well?" they said. "Either you bought something or you stole something. Your face does the same thing either way."

Astrid opened their hand on the table between the maps. The black iron drank the lamplight and gave nothing back.

Sef went very still. Then very slowly, very carefully, they reached across and touched the edge of the table instead of the key, as if the wood itself needed bracing. "Where," they said, in the precise, flattened voice of an archivist confronting a catalog error of apocalyptic proportions, "did you get a charter key."

"The dying miner upstairs."

"There are *seven* of those. Seven, Astrid. I've read fragmentary references in four separate collections — trade charters, a dissolved company's tax rolls, one heresy trial transcript where a witness was executed mostly for knowing the word 'charter.' Every source agrees the keys were recalled and destroyed when the Halloran company folded. Every source. And you're telling me one has been sitting in a miner's chest in Grayhollow for—"

"Two hundred years, apparently. Grandfather's keepsake." Astrid turned it over. The spiral grooves caught no light at all. "Sef. He said there are sealed levels under Thornfell. Galleries the Hallorans walled shut themselves. Lights down there made of voices. Crates carried down and men coming back up *richer*. Sold by the pound." They looked up. "Tell me that matches your fragments. Tell me I'm wrong."

Sef didn't tell them they were wrong. Sef sat back, pressed both hands flat against the table, and did the thing Astrid had learned to recognize as the archivist wrestling a conclusion into submission: eyes unfocused, lips moving slightly, sorting citations like cards.

"It matches," Sef said finally, quietly. "It matches worse than you know. The trial transcript — the executed witness — his testimony was about *sound*. He claimed the mountain sang at certain hours, and that the song had parts, like a choir, and that the parts didn't repeat. A repeating song is machinery. Wind, water, pumps. A song that never repeats is—" They stopped.

"Is people," Astrid said.

"Is people." Sef rubbed their eyes. "I filed that testimony under religious hysteria twenty years ago. I was young and the Inquisition liked clean categories." A pause, and then, with the bitter precision that was Sef's armor: "Kestrian's alchemists brew plagues, his inquisitors burn witnesses, and underneath all of it, apparently, there's been a market running for centuries where you can buy a soul by weight. And the Halloran family held the charter." Their eyes came up, sharp with something that wasn't quite accusation and wasn't quite pity. "Your family built the cellar, Astrid. The question the miner asked you — I heard it in your face before you said a word. So. Are you going to be a Halloran down there, or the thing that ends them?"

Everyone wanted that answer today. Everyone wanted it fast, cheap, settled.

"I don't know," Astrid said again, and found it easier the second time. "That's the honest version. Ask me again standing in it."

To their surprise, Sef nodded — slowly, the way you nod at a map that's finally oriented north. "Good. Certainty's what the Inquisition sells. Doubt's the only currency worth carrying into that mountain." They began gathering the maps, briskly, businesslike, and then paused with a folio half-stowed. "The key. May I?"

Astrid handed it over. Sef held it the way they held everything rare — not touching the metal directly, using a strip of cloth, turning it toward the lamp. The black iron refused the light exactly as before.

"Cold," Sef murmured. "Not temperature-cold. *Warded*. This isn't a key to a lock, it's a key to a *permission*. Something down there issued these deliberately — seven of them, distributed, recalled. Which means whatever's under Thornfell planned for visitors." They looked up, and behind the exhaustion in their face something kindled that Astrid had seen only rarely in three years of roads: the pure, terrible delight of a scholar holding proof. "It's been expecting a Halloran. All this time. It's been *waiting*."

The hum rose again — or Astrid's imagination furnished it — and this time it did not sound like a held breath.

It sounded like a door, somewhere very far below, beginning to open.

* * *

They climbed to the room as the lamplighters worked the street outside, shadows sliding along the shutter-slats. Astrid sat on the floor with their back to the bedframe and the key in their lap, and did not sleep, and did not expect to.

Every vow they'd ever spoken lived in their steel, and every vow had a price tagged to it, and somewhere below their feet the ledger of all those prices sat waiting in a hand that might be their own grandmother's. *Sold by the pound.* They turned the phrase over like the key, testing its edges. Somewhere under Thornfell, someone had weighed a soul on a scale and written a number beside it, and the number had purchased an oath, and the oath had made Astrid Halloran a swordspeaker, and every wonder of their life had been bought with change from that first transaction.

*Am I the coin,* they wondered, *or am I the purchase?*

The key grew no warmer in their hands. It didn't need to. It had time.

Below, the hum continued — patient, layered, never repeating. And sometime in the smallest hours, Astrid realized they had stopped hearing it as dread.

Somewhere in that choir was a name. Maybe their own. And whether it was a debt to be paid or a prisoner to be freed, Astrid Halloran was going down to find out — and the mountain, patient as interest accruing, already knew they were coming.
