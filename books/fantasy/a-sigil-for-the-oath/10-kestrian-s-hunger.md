## 10. Kestrian's Hunger

The vault beneath Vaelmoor had no windows, which Kestrian considered its finest feature. Windows admitted weather. Weather admitted time, and time was the one creditor he had spent ninety years learning to cheat.

He descended the spiral stair alone, as he always did, waving off the guards at the bronze door with a flick of two fingers. They were good soldiers, his inquisitors' pick of the palace watch, and they believed — because he had arranged for them to believe — that the vault held confiscated weapons awaiting trial inventory. Let them think it. A king's appetite should never be common knowledge. Appetites could be taxed; hungers could only be fed or hidden.

The stair was cut from the mountain's root, and the deeper he went the warmer the stone became, until by the last turn he could feel it against his bare soles like the flank of a sleeping animal. He had ordered the carpet removed years ago. He liked to feel it breathing.

The door at the bottom was iron faced with silver, and silver, unlike iron, remembered. It had been poured and blessed and inscribed by oath-keepers two generations dead, men and women who had imagined a thief like him and failed to imagine him well enough. The wards read intent. His intent was simple and therefore invisible to them: *I am the lawful sovereign of Vaelmoor and everything within my borders is mine.* The wards tasted that truth and let him pass, because technically, legally, in every way their makers had thought to codify, it was one.

He wondered sometimes what they would have made of the distinction between *mine* and *owed*. Nothing, probably. Oath-keepers had never been subtle people. That was why they were so easy to harvest.

The lamps lit themselves as he entered, wicks catching on breaths of old vow still hanging in the air like pollen. The vault was long and low, ribbed with buttresses, and along both walls ran the racks: four hundred and eleven blades, each in its leather sleeve, each tagged with a lead seal and a clerk's neat hand. *Confiscated under Proclamation Nine. Confiscated under Proclamation Twelve.* Swordspeakers did not go quietly into the archives. Their steel went here instead, and here it stayed, and here it waited, and here —

Here it sang.

Kestrian stood in the doorway a moment, as he always did, letting the sound wash over him. To anyone else the vault would have been silent. But he had not been anyone else for a very long time. What he heard was a chord — hundreds of voices braided into steel, each blade holding the residue of every vow ever spoken through it. *I will hold this bridge. I will guard this child. I will come back.* Small vows, mostly. People rarely swore great things into swords; great things required great payments, and even swordspeakers were thrifty with their souls. But small vows, aged decades in the dark, distilled — ah, they distilled beautifully.

He crossed the floor slowly, dragging his fingertips along the rack-leather the way other men trailed fingers along the shoulders of lovers.

There. A short cavalry saber, taken from a woman named Ostrey who had held a river ford for six hours against three times her number before Kestrian's alchemists' plague found her lungs. Her last vow was still thick in the metal: *I will not yield this crossing.* He drew the blade halfway from its sheath and pressed his lips to the flat.

The vow came into him like cold wine.

It was not words, exactly. It was the shape of a kept promise — the ache in her arms, the mud sucking at her boots, the certainty that the ford would be held because she had said it would be and her saying had made it true. For a moment Kestrian *was* Ostrey at the ford, and the moment was sixty years deep and absolutely intact, and his body drank it the way a cracked jar drinks rain. His spine straightened. The tremor in his left hand, the one the physicians called palsy and he knew better, stilled. In the polished breastplate of the ceremonial armor mounted near the door, a man looked back at him who was perhaps forty. Perhaps thirty-eight. Handsome in the way that well-fed things are handsome.

Then the vow guttered. It always guttered. Residue was residue; the woman herself was ash in a pauper's trench outside the city, and all that remained in the steel was the echo of an echo. He slid the saber home and felt the hunger settle back into its seat behind his sternum, patient, enormous, already leaning toward the next rack.

Four hundred and eleven blades. He had been rationing himself at nine a night, rotating through the racks like a man pacing out a larder, and lately nine felt like nine sips from a drained cup. He took twelve tonight. It changed nothing except the arithmetic of how soon the vault would be empty, and empty it would be, because confiscations had slowed. There simply weren't many swordspeakers left to strip.

*One*, said the arithmetic. One, if the reports were true.

* * *

He dined afterward in the small chamber above the vault, alone, on food he barely tasted. Hunger for vows did not displace ordinary hunger; it sat beside it, the way a wolf sits beside a dog, similar enough in outline to fool the unwary. He ate the quail because eating was habit and habit was armor, and he drank the dark Vaelmoor wine because it dulled the edge of the thing behind his sternum without ever quite blunting it.

Maren Duskwell arrived at the third glass. She did not knock — she knocked everywhere else in the world, he had watched her do it, a precise double rap that announced *functionary, harmless, please ignore* — but down here, where the mountain's warmth rose through the floor, she came in quiet. She understood, in whatever compartment of her soul she kept locked even from herself, what these rooms were for. Or she understood enough. It was one of the hundred reasons he valued her.

"Report," he said, and did not look up from the quail.

"The smuggler roads are being combed. Dusk patrols on every pass below the snowline." She stood at parade rest, hands folded. "We found the waystation at Colder's Halt burned three nights ago. Deliberate. Before we reached it."

"So she knew."

"Someone knew. The innkeeper's family is missing. Either they ran ahead of us or someone moved them." A pause, precisely measured, the pause of a woman deciding how much truth a sovereign can absorb. "And there is the matter of the archivist."

"Sef." He said the name the way he said all names that mattered — lightly, tasting it.

"Excommunicated from the Harrowgate scriptorium eight years ago. Keeper of restricted access for eleven years before that. Three years of travel warrants issued under research credentials, all of them tracing the same circuit — Thornfell, the salt caves, the Halloran holdings." Maren produced a thin folder and set it on the table between them. "My reading is that Sef has been looking for Astrid Halloran since before either of us knew Astrid Halloran mattered."

"And your conclusion?"

"That the archivist knows something about the Halloran oath that the archives do not." Her jaw tightened fractionally. "That the prophecy pages are real."

Ah. There it was. Maren Duskwell, inquisitor-general, terror of two provinces, and still — after all his careful cultivation of her cynicism — a woman who wanted the world to have a spine of meaning running through it. He had tried to burn that out of her early, with assignments designed to show her that meaning was a currency and nothing more. She had executed every assignment flawlessly and kept the want anyway, folded up somewhere behind the competence. It made her predictable. It also made her magnificent at her work. He had never decided which he preferred.

"The pages are real," Kestrian said. "I have known that longer than you have held a commission."

Something crossed her face — not surprise; she was too well-made for surprise — but a recalculation, quick as a card sharp's glance.

"You've read the Codex."

"I have read *of* the Codex. Reading it requires touching it, and touching it requires permissions I granted to exactly one living person." He smiled at her, and enjoyed watching her understand, layer by layer, what he was telling her. "Sef was mine once too, Maren. Everyone who matters has been mine at some point. It is the burden of sovereignty."

"What does the Codex say?"

"It says," Kestrian said, setting down his fork, "that there is a door under Thornfell, and behind the door is the source. The well the swordspeakers have been drinking from for a century without ever finding the spring." He rose, and walked to the window — the one window in the hunting lodge above the vault, facing north, facing the black mass of the mountain on the horizon like a head bowed over the land. "You have seen what a single sword can do in a swordspeaker's hand. You watched Halvatt break the siege lines at Corren's Field with four words. Now imagine the *well*. Imagine what it costs to buy a drink from it, and imagine what I could buy with a kingdom's treasury."

"With a soul," Maren said quietly. "The prophecies say it runs on souls."

"Do they?" He turned, genuinely pleased. "You *have* been reading."

"I read everything, majesty. It's the work."

"It is the work," he agreed. "And the work says the Halloran girl carries the key. Not a key of iron — a key of blood. The bargain that opened the door runs in her veins, which means the door opens *for* her, which means whoever holds her holds the spring." He came back to the table and rested both hands flat upon it, and let her see, for one unguarded second, the hunger looking out through his eyes with nothing dressed over it. Maren did not step back. He noted that, filed it away. Bravery or blindness or some alloy of the two; with her it was always impossible to say, and he suspected she engineered the ambiguity deliberately. Clever creature.

"She is to be taken alive," he said. "Unharmed. Unsworn-to — no bindings, no gag-charms, nothing that touches her voice. If her voice is damaged, Maren, the door may as well be bricked over. Do you understand the shape of the order?"

"Alive, unharmed, voice intact." She recited it flatly.

"And bring me word the hour you have her. Not the day. The *hour*." He straightened, and the hunger folded itself back behind his face, and he was again the reasonable, tired, patient father-of-his-country the portraits showed. "There is one more thing. A contingency. If the Halloran girl reaches the mountain before we take her—"

"She won't."

"If she does," he continued, with the mildness of a man correcting a child's arithmetic, "then the vault becomes relevant. All of it."

Maren's eyes went, involuntarily, downward. Toward the racks. Toward the four hundred and eleven sleeping voices.

"You want the blades moved to Thornfell."

"I want everything moved to Thornfell." He smiled. "A cannon, Maren. You have heard the engineers talk about the oath-cannon — the prototype they built from the Corren's Field salvage? Crude. A toy. But scale it, feed it properly—" he gestured vaguely at the floor, at the mountain of stored vows beneath their feet, "—and you have a weapon that doesn't break walls. It breaks *oaths*. It unmakes the promises a thing is built on. Walls. Wards. Wills." He let that sit. "If the door opens and what's inside proves uncooperative, I intend to ask very forcefully."

For just a moment — a half-breath, gone before it finished arriving — something moved behind Maren's eyes that was not ambition and not duty. He catalogued it and let it go. Every instrument developed hairline fractures eventually. The art was using them before they spread.

"Dismissed," he said warmly. "Bring her to me."

She saluted and left, her bootfalls fading up the spiral, and Kestrian stood alone with the quail going cold and the wine untouched now, listening.

Down through the floor, faint as tide through a hull, the vault was singing. Four hundred and eleven small kept promises, holding their notes in the dark, waiting to be drunk.

Not enough. He had known it for years the way a drowning man knows the waterline, but tonight, with the mountain visible even from this far south — a smudge of darker dark on the northern horizon — the knowledge had teeth. A kingdom of residue. A pantry of echoes. And beneath Thornfell, behind a door bought and paid for by a dead man's bargain, the *source*: ten thousand voices, whole, unspent, singing in a cage.

His mouth watered. Actually watered, standing there alone at forty-something in a body maintained like a lie, at the thought of it. Ten thousand. He did the mathematics the way other men prayed.

The Halloran girl would come to the mountain. Prophecies saw to it that their subjects always did — that was the beauty of prophecy; it was a leash disguised as a road. And when she opened the door, he would be there, with his army and his cannon and his appetite, and he would drink until—

Until. The word opened under him like a stair going down forever, and for the first time in ninety years Kestrian looked at the bottom of it and felt something adjacent to fear. Not of dying. Dying was a problem with solutions; he owned several. But of the *still being hungry*. Of drinking ten thousand souls and feeling the wolf lean forward, patient, enormous, and ask:

*And next?*

He closed his eyes. Down in the dark the vault sang on, sweetly, insufficiently.

"Light the beacons," he told the empty room, in the voice he used for orders that would be repeated by others within the hour. "Every garrison between here and Thornfell. And get me Maren's dispatch schedule. She moves tomorrow."

Above him, far to the north, the mountain waited with its door and its spring, and Kestrian — sovereign, cheat of death, keeper of four hundred and eleven borrowed promises — began, at last, to plan his descent.
