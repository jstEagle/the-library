## 12. The Unsworn Word

The cave Sef found us was old salt, hollowed by some sea that had never existed this far inland, and the walls sweated light when the fire caught them — a soft amber glow, like looking into the throat of a lantern. We'd been running for two days since the ford. Two days of my mother's face missing from behind my eyes like a window painted shut.

I sat with my back against a pillar of rose-colored salt and held my sword across my knees and did not sleep.

The vow I had broken at the river — *I will not leave you behind*, spoken to Sef on the second night out of Vaelmoor, sworn in carelessness because it cost so little — that broken word still echoed in me. Not painfully. That was the terrible part. It had come loose cleanly, the way a tooth comes loose, and where it had been there was now a socket. A small dark absence shaped exactly like my mother's face. I kept reaching into it the way a tongue worries a gap, expecting to find something, finding only smooth nothing.

And yet.

At the ford, when the echo-singer's voice had been unraveling me thread by thread, when every oath I carried was being picked apart like wet rope — I had spoken a word that was not an oath. A word no one had bound, no one had paid for, no one had ever said in quite that way. *Stop.* And the mountain under the river had listened. The water had gone still as hammered tin. The singer's voice had cracked like ice.

I had told myself it was adrenaline. A fluke. The oath's power bleeding through a crack in its own rules.

Sef didn't believe in flukes. Sef believed in marginalia.

"Again," they said now, sitting cross-legged opposite me, the Codex open on their lap though neither of us looked at it. They had barely slept either. There was salt dust in their gray hair and ink on three fingers and the particular brightness in their eyes that meant they'd been reading all night without turning pages.

"I don't know how."

"You do know how. You did it at the ford. You simply don't know what you did." They tapped the Codex. "There's a word in here — Aldric's hand, the oldest stratum of notes. *Unsworn.* He underlined it four times and then crossed it out and then wrote underneath: *the house does not permit what it has not sold.* Which means the house permits everything else."

"It's not a house."

"Everything is a house if you live in it long enough." Sef leaned forward, firelight carving their face into planes and shadow. "Astrid. Every swordspeaker who ever lived spoke through purchase. Vow made, memory paid, steel answers. Clean transaction. But you broke your own system and something answered anyway. Do you understand what that means? You have a door the rest of them never got. Or—" and their voice went careful, "—a door someone left open for you. On purpose."

The salt walls ticked as they cooled. Somewhere above us, miles up, the road we'd fled was crawling with inquisitors and council hunters both, two nets converging, and here we sat in the belly of a dead sea talking about doors.

"If I try," I said slowly, "and it works, and I don't understand it — what does it cost?"

"That's the experiment, isn't it."

* * *

We started small.

The first thing I unswore was a stone. There were hundreds scattered across the cave floor, salt-bright, none larger than an apple. I picked one up and held it in my palm and tried to remember what it had felt like at the ford — not the word itself but the shape of the speaking. Oaths had a grammar I knew by heart: intention first, then price, then steel. This would need no steel. No price. Just—

*Move.*

The stone rippled.

It didn't fly from my hand. It *melted*, briefly, like wax remembering it had once been liquid, flowing between my fingers in a warm silver thread before cooling back into solidity — a different shape now, a little bird, crude but unmistakable, wings folded. Warm. It stayed warm in my palm long after it should have cooled.

Sef made a sound I had never heard from them before, somewhere between a scholar's gasp and a prayer cut short. "Say it again—"

"No." My heart was going hard against my ribs. "One at a time."

But I was already reaching for the next thing, because wonder is a hunger and I had been starving for months — starving on faded oaths, on memories paid away until whole seasons of my childhood stood empty as gutted houses. Here was a magic that asked nothing. That answered like the mountain itself had been waiting, leaning close, saying *yes, yes, what else, ask me anything.*

I unspoke a crack in the wall. The fissure sealed with a sound like a sigh, salt knitting to salt.

I silenced the fire for ten seconds — not extinguished it, *silenced* it, the flames leaping soundlessly, and in that hush I could hear something beneath the cave, far down, a vast slow breathing I chose not to think about.

I reshaped my sword's chipped edge back to true, and the blade sang one clear note and went quiet, and Sef wrote for an hour without stopping.

By the third day I had learned rules, or thought I had. Unsworn words wanted precision; vague intent gave vague results. They wanted solitude of meaning — I couldn't speak one while holding an active oath in mind, or the two tangled like fishing line. And they came easiest in the deep places, the older stone, as if the mountain's memory made my words more legible.

On the fourth day, I cut my forearm testing whether I could silence a wound.

I drew the knife slow and deliberate, a shallow line, blood welling bright against the salt-light. Then I looked at it and said, quietly, *never happened.*

The wound closed. Not healed — closed, the skin smoothing over as if the cut were being unwritten, and with it went the pain, and with the pain went—

I staggered. Caught myself against the salt pillar.

Because for half a heartbeat I couldn't remember doing it. The cutting. My arm bore no mark, no sting, and my memory of drawing the knife was already fraying at the edges like cloth rubbed thin — I knew I had done it because Sef was staring at me with wide eyes and there was a bloody knife on the ground, but the *doing* of it was going, going, a candle guttering, and I grabbed at it desperately—

"—Astrid!"

"It's fine." My voice sounded far away to me. "I'm fine. I remember. I remember the knife."

Did I? Or did I remember Sef telling me about the knife, afterward, which would feel identical from the inside?

Sef crossed the space between us and took my wrist, turned my unmarked forearm over, pressed thumb into flesh where the cut should scar. Nothing. Their hands were shaking.

"The mountain isn't taking payment," they said. "That's what I keep getting wrong. It's taking *authorship*. When you unswear a thing, you're not just changing it — you're editing the record of it. And the record is *you*, Astrid. Your past is just a story you tell forward. Change the story enough and—"

"Don't." I pulled my arm free. "Don't finish that."

They didn't. But they looked at me the way archivists look at documents they suspect are forgeries — lovingly, and with dread.

* * *

I should have stopped there.

Here is what I remember of the fifth day, and I want to be honest in this account even if honesty costs me the reader's trust: I remember choosing not to stop, and I cannot fully remember why. I have reconstructed the reasons since — the hunters behind us, the prophecy ahead, the need to be strong enough to survive Thornfell's descent — and they are good reasons, defensible ones. But when I search for the moment of decision itself, I find a smooth place. A polished absence. And I have begun to suspect that some of my decisions are like that now: real decisions, mine, worn featureless by words I spoke without counting.

The fifth day I tried memory.

Not rewriting anyone else's. Mine. A test, I told myself. Controlled. There was a memory I treasured and had never spent: the last summer with my grandmother, before her dying and the oath, before everything. She was teaching me to gut fish on the bank of the Mirren, and she laughed at something — I could never remember what, only the laugh, big and unguarded, a laugh she let almost no one hear — and the sun was on the water and I was nine years old and completely happy.

I held it the way you hold a soap bubble.

And I said: *show me what I've lost.*

Nothing happened to the memory. It sat intact, sunlit, perfect. For about a minute I felt foolish.

Then other things started arriving.

They came like fish rising in a dark pond — surfaces breaking. A kitchen I had no name for, copper pots, a woman humming with her back turned. A boy's handwriting in the margin of a book, letters slanting left. Snow falling upward — snow falling *upward*, I remembered snow falling upward, which was impossible, which meant it wasn't mine or wasn't weather or wasn't real. A voice singing a lullaby in a language I recognized and could not speak, each note landing in me like a key in a lock I hadn't known my chest contained.

"Wren," I said aloud, without deciding to.

Sef's head snapped up. "What did you say?"

"Nothing. I don't— I said a name." My mouth tasted of salt. "It's not a name I know. It's a name I *almost* know. Like a word on the tip of the tongue except it's a whole person on the tip of the tongue."

The visions kept coming all afternoon, and I stopped fighting them and started cataloguing instead, because cataloguing was Sef's discipline and I had borrowed it like a coat. Kitchen-woman. Left-slanting hand. Upward snow. Lullaby. A staircase of black iron seen from below, descending forever. A feeling, recurring in nearly every fragment, that I can only describe as *being watched from inside by someone patient.*

When the sun-cycle dimmed and the salt-light faded, I sat among the fragments like a man sorting coins from three different kingdoms, and Sef watched me, and finally I said the thing we had both been thinking.

"Some of these are real. Some of them are mine." I turned my hands over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. "But I can't tell which. That's the horror of it, Sef. Not that the power costs something. I always knew it would cost. The horror is that it pays in counterfeit. It gives me memories that feel more vivid than my life — more *real* than my life — and maybe they're Wren's, maybe they're the mountain's, maybe they're nothing but beautiful lies the stone dreams up to feed me. And every time I use an unsworn word, the border gets thinner. My mother's face was taken at the ford. Fine. Clear theft. But these—" I closed my fists. "These are smuggled in. Planted. And I'm starting to build myself out of materials I can't verify."

Sef was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Down below, the mountain breathed its vast slow breath.

"There's a page," they said at last, very carefully. "In the Codex. Marginalia again, Aldric's hand, but later — decades later. I didn't show you because it seemed like madness and we had enough of that. It says: *the bargain was not one soul. Count again.*"

My blood went cold and then colder. "Two souls."

"At least two. The second name is smudged. Deliberately, I think — blotted with something that eats ink." Sef met my eyes. "I showed you the ledger of prices in the oath-rite. One soul, freely given by the heir, generation after generation. But if Aldric bought *two* — if he sold two souls into that court and only ever recorded one — then half of everything you are might belong to someone the world was never allowed to meet."

Wren. The name rose in me again, unbidden, and this time it brought a taste with it: rain on hot stone, and grief, enormous and specific and not mine.

Or mine. That was the whole unbearable question.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. Near dawn I climbed to the cave's mouth, where the salt gave way to living rock and cold air poured down from the peaks, and I stood looking north toward Thornfell — a black massif stitched with early stars, patient as a debt.

And I decided — or something in me decided, and I no longer fully trusted the difference — to stop experimenting.

Not because of fear. Because of arithmetic. Every unsworn word traded authorship for power, and I was about to walk into the one place in the world where knowing exactly who I was might be the only weapon that mattered. If Aldric Halloran waited at the bottom of the Hollow Stair, and if the Codex's date had already come due, then I needed my self whole — even the broken parts. *Especially* the broken parts. A forged coin spends easily, but it buys nothing real.

So I made myself a rule, and I spoke it aloud to the mountain so it would hear me mean it:

"No more unsworn words. Not unless lives depend on it. Not even then, if I can help it."

The mountain hummed. I felt it through my boots, faint and satisfied, like a merchant watching a customer swear off spending — right up until the next shop window.

I was turning back into the cave when I heard it.

Voices. Far off, down-valley, carried on the thin cold air — too many voices for travelers, moving in cadence, and beneath them a sound I knew from the ford and would know in my grave: the high, sweet, wrong harmonic of an echo-singer being driven hard, forced to sing a searching-song.

They had found the region. Inquisitors, sweeping the valleys note by note, listening for oaths the way owls listen for mice.

And threaded through the searching-song, faint but distinct, another voice answering it — human, female, giving orders in a tone I recognized even at this distance, because I had heard it across a river while it tried to take me apart.

Maren Duskwell was coming. And she was not alone.

Behind me, in the dark of the salt cave, the Codex lay open on Sef's bedroll — and I would learn only later, much later, that its next page had already begun to darken with fresh ink, spelling out a name that was not mine, and a chapter titled in a hand I almost recognized:

*Maren's Doubt.*
