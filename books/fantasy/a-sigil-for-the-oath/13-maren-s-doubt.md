## 13. Maren's Doubt

The cells beneath Vaelmoor's Hall of Inquiry were cut from the same gray stone as the rest of the palace, but they smelled different — colder, older, like the inside of a well that had never known sun. Maren Duskwell had walked these corridors for eleven years and had never once thought about the smell until tonight. Tonight she noticed everything.

The prisoner waited in the third cell, the one with no bench, because prisoners who sang were not permitted furniture. Echo-singers could turn anything into an instrument — a stool's legs, a chain's links, the hollow of their own cupped hands — and a song in the dark was worth more than a key. So the Inquisition kept them standing, in rooms designed to swallow sound, and waited for their voices to wear out.

This one was young. That was the first wrongness Maren registered as she stepped through the door and let it seal behind her. The file on her desk said *Vessin Corrane, taken at the ford of the Senn, age indeterminate.* But the creature — the *person*, some traitorous part of her insisted — looked barely twenty. Thin wrists. A face still soft at the jaw. Eyes that had already gone somewhere else and did not intend to come back.

Two of her interrogators stood against the far wall, hands folded, faces arranged into professional blankness. She dismissed them with a flick of two fingers. They hesitated — protocol required three present for a singer — but her rank was written into their bones by now, and they went.

She heard the door seal. She heard her own breathing become the loudest thing in the world.

"You're Vessin Corrane." Not a question. Files began conversations; questions invited them.

The echo-singer's head came up slowly, the way a drowned thing rises. "You're the one they call Duskwell." The voice was hoarse from disuse, but under the hoarseness Maren caught it — the doubled quality, the faint second tone riding beneath the first like a reflection in moving water. Singers' voices always sounded to her like two people agreeing to be one.

"And what do they call you?"

"Nothing, lately." A ghost of a smile. "That's rather the point of me."

Maren pulled the single stool — hers, permitted, iron-legged so it couldn't hold a note — from the corner and sat, setting the leather folder across her knees. She did not open it yet. Eleven years of this work had taught her that the folder was a prop; the real instrument was patience.

"You were taken at the Senn ford," she said. "Traveling without writ. Toward Thornfell."

"Toward the mountains, yes."

"With coin minted in Harrowgate and letters sealed with the free cities' council wax."

"The council pays poorly," Corrane said. "But it pays."

Maren studied the thin face. "For what?"

"For singing, Inquisitor-General. What else does anyone pay me for?"

It went on like that for an hour. She circled; the singer drifted. Who sent you — the council, or someone within it? Which delegate? What were you to sing, and where, and to whom? Each answer came back smooth and useless, a stone worn round by long handling. *I sing where I'm told. I sing what I'm given. I don't ask what the words are for; asking is how singers get sold.*

Maren had broken harder subjects than this. She had broken generals and archivists, a saint once, or what passed for a saint in the salt provinces. But those had all wanted something — mercy, release, revenge — and wanting was a door. This one seemed to want nothing except to stand in the dark until the dark finished its work.

So she changed the shape of the room instead.

She stood, crossed to the door, and struck it once with the flat of her palm — the signal that meant *continue without me* to whoever watched beyond it, and meant nothing at all to anyone else. Then she turned back, and she did the thing she had done only twice before in eleven years: she took off her gloves.

Her hands were plain, broad-knuckled, scarred at the left thumb from a training accident decades ago. Ordinary hands. She held them up so the singer could see them bare, and she watched something flicker behind those faraway eyes — recognition, maybe, of what it cost a woman like Maren Duskwell to be ungloved in front of a stranger.

"I'm going to tell you what happens next," Maren said quietly. "In about half a bell, my people will come back through that door and take you up to the binding chamber. You've seen one. You know what we do there. We will put a quench-collar on you and ask our questions again, and each answer you give under the collar will cost you a memory — not one of yours. One the collar takes at random, because random is cheaper. And when your memories run thin enough, you'll start losing skills. Then names. Then the trick of being a person at all, and we'll have a very quiet, very obedient husk that used to know songs." She let that sit. "Or. You can talk to me now, freely, and I give you my word — my sworn word, and I am told my word binds even where oaths fail — that you go to the soft cells in the east wing. Warm meals. A window. No collar. No more questions from anyone but me."

The silence stretched so long she counted her own heartbeats. Forty-seven of them.

Then Vessin Corrane began, very softly, to hum.

Maren's whole body went rigid. It was the first rule of singers: never let them find a note. But the hum wasn't a working — it carried no power she could feel, no resonance crawling along the stone. It took her several seconds, listening past her own fear, to understand what it was.

A lullaby. An old one, northern, the kind grandmothers sang over cradles in villages the usurper had burned for harboring swordspeakers. Maren knew it because her mother had sung it, and because her brother Tomas had demanded it every night until he was seven, and because she had not thought of either fact in nineteen years and now could think of nothing else.

"Stop," she said.

Corrane stopped. "Just checking something." The faraway eyes found hers and stayed, for the first time, actually *present*. "You flinch like someone who used to be sung to. That means somewhere under all that gray wool you're still a person who remembers being small." A pause. "I'll take the window. And I'll tell you the truth, Inquisitor-General, because you asked nicely and because — " the ghost-smile again, bitterer now — "because I've been carrying it since the Senn ford, and it's heavy."

Maren sat back down. Her hands, ungloved, felt strangely naked on the folder.

"Talk."

"I was hired six months ago," Corrane said. "Through intermediaries, through intermediaries' intermediaries — the usual architecture, so deep no one at the bottom knows who stands at the top. My instructions were simple. I was to make my way toward Thornfell by stages, letting myself be followed. When the right people found me — and they would find me, I was told, because I was bait — I was to sing a particular song in their hearing. A working-song. It unravels oaths, loosens vows, makes bound things stutter." The singer's gaze slid away. "I never got to sing it for the ones I was meant for. Your people took me at the ford instead. Which means, I suppose, I've been singing it all along for the wrong audience."

"You were bait for the swordspeaker." Maren kept her voice level. "Halloran."

"Halloran, yes. Or whoever came digging after the prophecy. Bait, and net, and bell — I was to ring myself loudly enough that everything hunting and hunted would come running to the same clearing, and then the clearing would be closed." Corrane shifted weight off raw feet. "That part I understood. What I didn't understand — what I made the mistake of understanding only last month — was the *second* set of instructions. The ones underneath."

"There are always instructions underneath."

"Not like these." For the first time the singer's composure cracked, and what showed through was not fear exactly but a kind of appalled clarity, the look of a person who has finally read the fine print on their own sale. "Do you know what an echo-singer actually does, Inquisitor-General? People think we repeat sounds. We don't. We *carry* them. Every word ever spoken near us lives in us, layered, and we can bring any layer forward if we're given the right key. It's not magic the way swordspeech is magic. It's just... terrible memory, wearing a throat."

Maren's fingers had gone cold on the folder. "And?"

"And I carry orders." Corrane's voice dropped, and here at last the doubled tone rang clear, both registers speaking in unison, which Maren knew — everyone in the Inquisition knew — meant the singer was giving testimony their own body considered true. "Spoken to my handler in my presence, in a walled garden in Vaelmoor, eight months ago, by a man whose voice I would know anywhere because I've been made to memorize it. He said: *"When the Halloran opens the mountain, the legions move. Not before. Harrowgate first, then Cael Port, then the salt towns. Burn the granaries before winter. Leave nothing with walls standing. If any soul escapes the mountain unbound, the order stands regardless — the cities die whether or not the prize is taken."*" The singer drew breath. "He said it twice, for accuracy. I remember both times."

The cell was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the palace continued its ordinary night: boots on marble, a distant door, the clink of the changing of some guard who believed, presumably, in the righteousness of his work.

"Those are not the Lord Protector's orders," Maren said. "The Protectorate's directive is recovery of a strategic asset and stabilization of—"

"I know what the proclamation says." Corrane's voice had gone gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "Traitors, plague, the sword-oaths poisoning wells. I've heard it recited in market squares in nine towns. But this wasn't the proclamation. This was Kestrian himself, in his own garden, to his own man, saying what happens to Harrowgate after. And there was a phrase he used that I've turned over four hundred times since, trying to find another meaning in it, and there isn't one. He said: *'The mountain is a granary too, Duskwell's people will fetch the key, and then we eat.'"*

Maren stood up. She did not remember deciding to stand.

"That's impossible," she said, and heard immediately how it sounded — not like judgment but like prayer. "Kestrian has spent eight years building the Protectorate. Courts. Roads. The grain reform alone saved—"

"Saved whom?" Corrane asked softly. "Have you been to the salt towns lately, General? Have you counted what the plague actually killed, and cross-checked it against what the alchemists' ledgers say was brewed?" The singer's head tilted. "No. Of course not. You're the sword arm. Sword arms aren't shown ledgers."

The folder slid from Maren's knees to the floor. She left it there.

"You'll forgive me," she said, and her voice was steady, which cost her more than screaming would have, "if I don't accept the testimony of a captured operative repeating a secondhand quote. Memory-workings can be planted. Voices can be forged. You may believe every word you're carrying and still be carrying a lie someone sewed into you years ago."

"All true," Corrane agreed. "Ask me for proof."

"What proof could you possibly have?"

"Give me a note," the singer said. "Any note. Hum it."

Every instinct screamed against it — the first rule, never let them find a note. But Maren had spent eleven years trusting her instincts, and her instincts had brought her to this cell, ungloved, at the hour of the third watch, listening to a lullaby she hadn't heard since childhood. She hummed a middle tone, low and tuneless, giving the singer nothing to work with.

Vessin Corrane took the note and did something with it. Not a song. More like turning a page — the tone bent, split, and became two tones, then four, weaving into a chord that should not have fit in a human throat. And inside the chord, suddenly, unmistakably, was a voice Maren knew.

*"The mountain is a granary too. Duskwell's people will fetch the key, and then we eat."*

Kestrian's voice. Not an impression — the actual timbre, the slight wet click at the back of the palate, the way he swallowed his final consonants like a miser swallowing coins. Maren had knelt before that voice at seventeen, sworn herself to it at nineteen, built her entire adult life as a load-bearing wall beneath it. She knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, and it was coming out of a starving echo-singer's mouth in a cell that smelled like a well.

She had her knife at Corrane's throat before she knew she'd drawn it.

The singer didn't flinch. "There," Corrane whispered, the chord dissolving. "Now you've heard him yourself. Do you feel it? How heavy it is?" The faraway eyes were calm. "Kill me if you need to. I'd rather that than the collar. But you'll still know what you know, General. That's the cruelty of my kind's gift. Once a voice is truly heard, it can't be unheard."

Maren lowered the knife. Her hand was shaking. She watched it shake with a kind of clinical detachment, as though it belonged to a subordinate who would shortly be reprimanded.

Eleven years. Eleven years of clean, efficient work — the ford ambushes, the archive seizures, the quiet disappearances of people who spoke too loudly about old oaths. She had told herself a story the whole time, the way every tool tells itself a story: *I keep order. Order keeps people alive. Someone must hold the line while the world remakes itself.* And beneath the story, unexamined, the load-bearing assumption: *the man at the top wants roughly what I want.*

The assumption had just spoken in its own voice and called the free cities food.

"My brother," she heard herself say, and did not know why she was saying it. "My younger brother. He died in the plague year. Cael Port. He was fourteen." Nineteen years of silence around that sentence, broken now in a stone cell in front of the last person who should have heard it. "They told us it came off a swordspeaker's blade. That the oath-magic curdled the water."

"They told everyone that," Corrane said. "It's a good story. Swordspeakers make excellent monsters — rare enough to fear, scarce enough to blame. But alchemists brew plagues, General. Alchemists keep ledgers. And the man who ordered the burning of the granaries before winter doesn't strike me as someone who mourns."

Maren retrieved the folder from the floor. Her movements were precise, automatic, the muscle-memory of a career performing itself while the woman inside it stood very still and bled.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out flat and merciless. If the singer lied, Maren could walk out of this cell, file her report, and sleep — imperfectly, but she had slept through worse. If the singer told the truth, then everything Maren was — general, inquisitor, sworn blade of the Protectorate — was an instrument aimed at a slaughter, and had been for years, and the slaughter had a date, and the date was tied to a swordspeaker opening a mountain.

Either way, she had to leave this cell. The difference was what she did after the door sealed.

"General?" Corrane said quietly.

Maren looked at the singer — at the raw feet, the wrists, the terrible patient eyes — and made the only decision available to a person who had just discovered she might be a weapon: she would not yet decide what she was. She would only refuse, for one night, to be used.

"The window," she said. "As promised. East wing."

"And my report to the Lord Protector's office? I'm told I'm material evidence in the Halloran matter. There's a request pending for my transfer to the—"

"You'll be logged as quarantined," Maren said. "Plague-suspicion. No transfers. No visitors. No one touches you until I clear it personally." She paused at the door. "How long can you hold a voice? If someone tried to make you sing that order again — for someone else's ears — could you refuse?"

Corrane smiled, and for a moment looked every day of whatever age they actually were. "General, I once held a bishop's dying confession for six years rather than give it to the man who caused it. Voices keep. Ask me again sometime, when you've decided what you want kept."

Maren struck the door twice — the release signal — and waited for it to swing inward, and did not look back.

* * *

The report took her until dawn, and it was the finest piece of mendacity of her career precisely because almost none of it was false.

She wrote that the captured echo-singer had yielded confirmation of the Halloran connection: the singer had been deployed as bait along the Thornfell road by agents of the free cities' council, corroborating the theory that the council sought to reach the swordspeaker first. She wrote that the singer's memory-workings had proven partially degraded — exposure to the ford's oath-unraveling residue, she suggested — rendering further testimony unreliable and possibly compromised by council counter-intelligence seeding false orders in captured assets. She recommended indefinite quarantine of the asset pending purification protocols, and noted that she had assumed personal oversight of the interrogation, as the material touched on matters of the Lord Protector's strategic confidence and should pass through no other hands.

Every clause defensible. Every clause a lock.

She read it through three times, then signed it — *M. Duskwell, Inquisitor-General* — and watched the sealing wax cool, thinking: *this is the moment. This is the hairline.* Somewhere in the space between what she knew and what she had just written lay a crack no wider than a signature, and she had chosen, for tonight, to stand on both sides of it at once.

The lie would hold. That was the horror of it — how easily it held, how little friction the machine offered, how smoothly the gears accepted a report that concealed a genocide-in-waiting because the concealment was phrased in the correct bureaucratic dialect. The machine did not want the truth. The machine wanted paper shaped correctly, and Maren had spent eleven years becoming fluent in its grammar.

What frightened her was not that she had lied. She had lied before; lying was a tool, and tools were neutral. What frightened her was the relief.

She sat in her office above the Hall of Inquiry with the lamp turned low and let herself, for the first time in nineteen years, actually remember her brother properly. Not the official grief, the polished memorial version — Tomas, taken young, lamented appropriately, folded away. The real one: Tomas at six, insisting the lullaby be sung *with the wolf verse*, though the wolf verse scared him; Tomas at ten, whittling a wooden sword and declaring he'd be a swordspeaker someday, and their mother crying in the kitchen afterward for reasons she wouldn't explain; Tomas at thirteen, asking Maren — home on her first leave in uniform — whether the Protectorate was good, really, underneath. And Maren, nineteen and already armored, answering: *underneath, everything is just structure, little brother. Good is what structure protects.*

Tomas had looked at her for a long moment with those grave dark eyes and said, "That's not an answer."

It had taken her nineteen years and one echo-singer to hear him.

She opened her desk's bottom drawer — the one locked with a key she wore on a chain inside her shirt, the drawer her subordinates believed held confiscated relics — and lifted out a slim packet of papers she had assembled over the years without ever admitting to herself why. Discrepancy reports she had flagged and buried. Supply requisitions that made no sense unless grain was being moved *out* of the salt towns ahead of schedule. A physician's affidavit, purchased at cost, estimating the plague's true death toll in Cael Port at nearly triple the official figure. Small stones, each one explicable alone, each one nothing. Together, stacked in the dark of a locked drawer, they had a shape she had refused to look at directly for four years.

She spread them across her desk now, beside the false report's fair copy, and looked.

Outside, the sky over Vaelmoor was going gray at the edges. Somewhere north, a swordspeaker she had spent a year hunting was walking toward a mountain full of sold souls, carrying a prophecy, pursued by everyone including — in a sense that Maren was only beginning to understand — herself. Kestrian wanted the mountain opened. The moment it opened, the legions would move, and Harrowgate would burn, and somewhere in the ash would be delegates like the Tanzer boy, idealists who still believed the council's votes meant something.

And Maren Duskwell would have helped put every torch in those fires. Not by malice. By *grammar*. By writing reports in the correct dialect and never once asking what the sentences were for.

She thought of the singer's phrase: *once a voice is truly heard, it can't be unheard.*

She thought of her brother: *that's not an answer.*

She took a fresh sheet of paper. Not an official one — plain stock, bought with her own coin, the kind that left no requisition trail. She dipped the pen, hesitated, and wrote a single line in her precise, unornamented hand:

*Kestrian's order, verbatim, per captured echo-singer Corrane: burn the free cities when the mountain opens. Verification needed. — D.*

She did not address it. She did not sign it fully. She folded it small and did not yet seal it, because a letter with no recipient is not treachery; it is only a question, and questions, unlike orders, can wait.

She held it over the candle flame for a long moment — long enough that the wax began to soften along the fold — and then, instead of burning it, she blew out the candle and slipped the folded paper into the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart, where the chain to her desk-key also lived.

Then she rose, put on her gloves, and went down to the east wing to check on her prisoner, because that was what she told herself she was doing, and because she needed to walk, and because the corridors were the only place she could think.

The east wing was quiet at this hour. She passed the soft cells with their warm lamps and their barred windows showing a sliver of courtyard sky, and stopped at Corrane's door. Through the observation slit she saw the singer sitting against the wall beneath the window, knees drawn up, awake — of course awake — and humming almost silently, lips barely moving, holding some voice in trust the way other people held breath.

Corrane's eyes opened and found the slit, found her, without surprise.

"Well?" the singer mouthed, or perhaps only breathed. "Decided yet?"

Maren did not answer. She stood at the slit for a long moment, one hand pressed flat against the inside of her coat pocket, over the folded paper and the key and the hairline crack running through the whole architecture of her life.

Then she turned and walked back up toward the waking palace, where the day's first orders were already being drafted in her name — and among them, she knew without having seen it, would be new movement orders for the legions, staging maps for the Thornfell approaches, the machinery of the harvest beginning to turn. The mountain had not opened. The swordspeaker had not been taken. But the machine did not wait for certainties; it prepared for probabilities, and it expected its Inquisitor-General to prepare alongside it.

Let it expect, she thought, climbing the stairs into the gray morning light. Let it draft its orders. She had a question in her pocket over her heart, and a voice in a cell below that could not be unheard, and a brother's verdict ringing in her ears after nineteen years:

*That's not an answer.*

Soon — soon, but not yet — she would have to find someone worth asking it to.
