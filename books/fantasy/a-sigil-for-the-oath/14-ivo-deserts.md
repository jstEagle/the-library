## 14. Ivo Deserts

The seal on the hunt order was still warm when Ivo Tanzer decided to betray the Free Cities of Harrowgate.

He stood in the council chamber's anteroom, the parchment in his hands, reading it a fourth time as though repetition might change the words. It did not. *By decree of the Council of Harrowgate: all hunters commissioned under Article Nine are instructed to apprehend the swordspeaker Halloran and deliver them, alive, to the custody of the usurper's envoy, as surety for the armistice negotiations.* Alive. As surety. As a coin to be pressed into Kestrian's palm while the man's legions sharpened their edges on the eastern hills.

They were going to hand Astrid Halloran to the man who had made swordspeakers into plague-carriers and traitors. And they had voted for it. Nineteen to eleven. Ivo had stood and argued until his voice cracked, and Delegate Morven had smiled at him the way one smiles at a child who has mistaken a game for a war, and the vote had come down anyway, and the chamber had smelled of lamp oil and old velvet and the particular rot of respectable men doing an unrespectable thing.

He folded the order into quarters. Then eighths. Then he stopped, because folding it smaller would not make it smaller.

"You could still sign," said Morven, passing behind him with a sheaf of her own commissions, her rings clicking against the parchment. "Article Nine requires the dissenting delegate's signature for the record. It's a formality. Your objection is noted either way."

"I know it's a formality." Ivo set the order on the table. "That's why I'm not signing it."

"Delegate Tanzer." Morven paused at the door. She was sixty, silver-haired, and had buried a husband and two sons in the Border Wars, and she spoke to him with the gentleness one reserves for the young and the doomed. "You argued well in there. Better than well. But well-argued is not the same as won, and a delegate who leaves the chamber when he loses the vote is a delegate who has chosen his conscience over his city."

"Yes," Ivo said. "That's exactly what I've chosen."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he would remember afterward that she had not looked angry, or even disappointed. She had looked like a woman reading the first page of a book she already knew the ending of.

"The hunters leave at dawn from the Eastgate," she said. "If a delegate were to, say, take a personal holiday in the southern hills, no one would stop him. And if a delegate were to be found in the company of a traitor swordspeaker, the council would have no choice but to strike his name from the rolls, revoke his stipend, and open an inquiry into his family's holdings." She adjusted her shawl. "The Tanzer shipping interests, I believe. Your father built those."

"My father built those," Ivo agreed. "And my father would say a name is worth more than a fleet."

"Your father," said Morven, "would be right. Which is why you're wrong. Good day, Delegate."

She left. The anteroom was very quiet. Somewhere below, in the council's map room, clerks were inking the hunters' route in red — the red that meant *pursuit*, the red that meant *blood on our hands but not on our floor*. Ivo looked at the folded order for a long time.

Then he picked it up, and he did not leave it on the table after all.

* * *

He told himself, riding out of Harrowgate through the Southgate at an hour when only bakers and thieves were awake, that he was not deserting. Desertion implied an army. He was a delegate, not a soldier; he had a commission of argument, not of arms. What he was doing was — he searched for the word as his horse picked its way down the switchbacks — *correction*. He was correcting an error. The council had made a mistake, a catastrophic, cowardly mistake, and someone had to unmake it before it got Astrid Halloran killed or worse, and since the council would not, then the council's most junior delegate from the river districts would have to do it himself, alone, with no plan beyond *ride fast, find them first, warn them, and figure out the rest on the road*.

It was, he admitted somewhere past the third milestone, a terrible plan. It was the kind of plan that got junior delegates killed in the first chapter of a tragedy. But the alternatives were all worse. He had spent three days in that chamber listening to men weigh a human life against a treaty, and every one of them had used the same arithmetic — *one life, weighed against ten thousand; surely the scale tips; surely any reasonable person* — and Ivo had sat there with his hands flat on the table thinking: *that is exactly the arithmetic Kestrian used to justify the plague. That is exactly the arithmetic that turns cities into the thing they swear they are fighting.*

You did not fight a man who sold souls by becoming a city that sold people. It was that simple. It had taken him three days and one vote to see it, and now he could not unsee it, and the seeing had a taste like iron.

The proof was wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, under his coat, where it rode against his heartbeat. He had taken it from the council's own intelligence office — not stolen, exactly; he had signed the requisition ledger with his own name, because if he was going to ruin himself he was going to do it honestly, in ink, in his own handwriting — a dispatch intercepted from Kestrian's field command, coded in the usurper's private cipher, which the council's cryptographers had broken two months ago and which the council's war party had been quietly sitting on ever since. It was orders for the Third Legion, countersigned by Kestrian's own hand: *Upon the opening of the mountain, proceed to the reduction of Harrowgate, Vaelmoor's claims to the contrary notwithstanding. Leave nothing that votes.*

*Leave nothing that votes.* Five words in a dead general's cipher, and they had outweighed nothing in that chamber, because eleven delegates had read them and nineteen had decided not to.

Ivo rode south with the paper against his chest and his father's name in his mouth like a stone he was chewing. *Tanzer.* His father had built a shipping house on the principle that a Tanzer's word, once given, was cargo that never sank. His father was dead four years now, and Ivo wondered, galloping past the flooded gravel pits where he'd learned to swim, whether the old man would have understood this — the son riding out alone to warn a stranger because a room full of reasonable people had voted to do otherwise — or whether his father would have called it vanity, the particular vanity of the young who have never yet had to choose between their conscience and their people.

He decided, somewhere past the fifth milestone, that his father would have understood. He had to decide that. A man could not ride toward ruin on a maybe.

* * *

Finding them was harder than he'd hoped and easier than he'd feared.

The council's hunters had a route — the red ink on the map room floor, which Ivo had memorized in the last hour before he left, standing over the clerks' shoulders with a cup of tea he never drank. The route assumed Astrid would take the low roads: the river valleys, the ford country, the soft going. But Ivo had listened to the swordspeaker talk in that chamber — no, that wasn't right, Astrid hadn't been in the chamber; Astrid had been a name, an abstraction, a *surety* — but Ivo had read the intelligence reports, all of them, the ones the war party waved and the ones they didn't, and the reports said the Halloran woman thought like a smuggler. Smugglers did not take the low roads when the low roads were being watched. Smugglers took the roads that didn't exist.

So Ivo turned east where the hunters went south, and bought a map from a one-eyed cartographer in a river town who laughed at him for asking after the old salt roads — "those aren't roads, boy, those are rumors with footpaths on them" — and then sold him one anyway for three times its price, and Ivo paid it gladly, because the rumor-map showed a thread of a track climbing into the hills toward Thornfell, a track that appeared on no official survey, and that was where he would have gone, if he were a hunted swordspeaker with the whole world sharpening itself behind him.

He rode for six days. He slept in ditches and hay barns and once, memorably, in a shrine to a river god whose statue had been beheaded by iconoclasts a century ago, so that he spent the night under the blind gaze of a headless stone figure with water dripping somewhere in the dark, and dreamed of his mother singing, which she had not done in life often enough, and woke with his face wet and could not have said why.

On the sixth day he found the aftermath.

It was a ford, or had been — the river had shifted since the maps were drawn, leaving a gravel bar and a stand of drowned alders — and the signs of the fight were everywhere, written in the language Ivo had learned to read from his father's harbor masters: scuffed stone, a boot-print half-flooded, the particular churned mud of many feet and a struggle. And blood. Not much. Enough. He crouched at the water's edge and read it the way he'd read tide-marks as a boy, and what it said was: *two people, ambushed, outnumbered, gone south and east, moving fast, one of them hurt.*

There was something else. He found it snagged in the alder roots — a scrap of gray cloth, military weave, inquisitor's gray — and pinned to it, driven through into the root-wood by a knife, a strip of parchment with writing on it in a hand that slanted hard to the right, urgent, the ink feathered where a wet thumb had smeared it:

*They used a singer. We got out. It cost more than I can say. If anyone reads this who is not our enemy — the mountain is the only door left. — A.H.*

Ivo stood in the cold shallows with the note in his hand and felt something in his chest that was not quite hope and not quite fear, but was made of the same material. *It cost more than I can say.* He knew, from the reports, what swordspeech cost. He had read the old accounts. Every vow paid in memory. He looked at the note and thought: *what did it cost her, and what did she choose to lose, and would I have had the courage to choose at all?*

He rode faster after that. His horse objected. He overruled it.

* * *

He found them on the ninth day, in the foothills below Thornfell, and he found them because they almost killed him.

He had stopped at a spring to water the horse — a gray mare with a mean streak and excellent endurance, his one extravagance — and he had just pulled the bit loose when the voice came out of the rocks above him, and the voice was not a voice. It was a *weight*. It pressed on him like deep water, like a hand on the back of his neck, and every joint in his body tried to fold at once, and the last thing he saw clearly was a sword-point emerging from between two boulders above the spring, followed by an arm, followed by a face he knew from a hundred council briefings and had never once imagined seeing at the business end of a blade.

"On the ground," the swordspeaker said, and the word *ground* landed on him like a dropped anvil, and Ivo Tanzer, delegate of the river districts, went down into the gravel with his cheek against the wet stone and his arms out, and thought, distantly: *this is the most expensive way anyone has ever said hello.*

"Ivo Tanzer." The voice was different now — the weight gone out of it, leaving something hoarse and human underneath. "Harrowgate council. You signed the hunt order."

"I refused to sign the hunt order," Ivo said into the gravel. "There's a record. Check the record."

Boots crunched around him. A second figure — slight, gray-cloaked, with the ink-stained fingers of a man who had spent his life in archives — crouched and went through Ivo's saddlebags with the efficiency of a border inspector. Papers. The false travel writ Ivo had bought in the river town. Coin. The one-eyed cartographer's rumor-map, which made the ink-stained man go very still.

"Sef," said the swordspeaker. "Look at his coat. Left side."

A hand, careful, found the oilcloth packet against Ivo's ribs. Drew it out. Unwrapped it.

Silence, then. The long kind. Ivo lay in the gravel and listened to the spring water and the mare cropping grass behind him, indifferent to the fates of delegates, and he counted his own heartbeats and got to forty before the swordspeaker spoke again.

"Read it to me," Astrid Halloran said. "The last line. The part about Harrowgate."

Ivo turned his head as far as the sword's pressure allowed. Up close, the last swordspeaker looked terrible — a healing cut along the jaw, a burn on the left hand, eyes rimmed red with exhaustion, the kind of tired that lives in the bones and pays no rent anywhere else. But the eyes themselves were steady, and they were fixed on him with a suspicion so complete it was almost a form of respect.

"*Upon the opening of the mountain*," Ivo recited, "*proceed to the reduction of Harrowgate. Leave nothing that votes.*"

"Say the countersignature."

"Kestrian. His own hand. The cryptographers broke the cipher in the spring. The war party sat on it." He swallowed. "I voted against handing you over. I lost. Nineteen to eleven. And then I took this out of the intelligence office under my own name, because if I'm going to hang I'd like it to be for something, and I rode south for nine days to find you before the hunters did, and I have been lying in ditches and eating hard bread and talking to a horse, and I would very much like to stand up now, if the anvil-word could be lifted."

The sword did not move. The gray-cloaked man — Sef, the disgraced archivist, the name was in the reports too — looked up from the dispatch with an expression Ivo couldn't read. "Astrid," he said quietly. "This is real. This is the field cipher. I catalogued three of these during the exile. This is genuine."

"I know it's genuine," Astrid said. "That's what frightens me. Why are you here, Tanzer? Truly. Not the speech. The truth. Nobody rides nine days into the hills for a stranger. What do you want?"

And there it was — the question he had been avoiding for nine days, lying in his ditches, the question that had been riding behind him like a second rider. What did he want. He had told himself *duty*, *correction*, *conscience*, all the respectable words, the words that looked well in a requisition ledger. But the truth was smaller and stranger and he found, lying in the gravel with a sword at his neck, that he could finally say it.

"I sat in that chamber," Ivo said, "and I listened to nineteen people do arithmetic with your life, and I realized I have spent my whole career being reasonable, and being reasonable is how the worst things happen. Nobody does evil in Harrowgate. We *vote* for it. Weigh it, debate it, minute it, and vote for it, and everyone goes home feeling grave and responsible." His voice cracked and he let it. "I don't want anything from you. That's the truth. I want to be the kind of person who, when a room full of reasonable people votes to feed someone to a tyrant, gets up and walks out the door and does the unreasonable thing. I've never been that person. I'd like to find out if I am. That's all. That's the whole of it."

The silence stretched. The spring murmured. Somewhere above them a bird complained about the disturbance.

Then the weight lifted — not the sword, which withdrew smoothly, but something else, something in the air itself, as though a held breath had been released by the mountain. Astrid Halloran stepped back and sheathed the blade, and stood swaying slightly, and Sef was there at their elbow in a moment, a steadying hand, and Ivo understood that the swordspeaker was far closer to the edge of exhaustion than the voice had let on.

"Get up," Astrid said.

Ivo got up. His knees reported their objections. He brushed gravel from his cheek and found, to his surprise, that his hands were steady.

"You look like a clerk who lost a fight with his own desk," Astrid said.

"I feel like one."

"Good. Clerks who look like that are usually honest." Astrid looked at him a moment longer — measuring, deciding — and then, in a voice that had lost its edge and found something drier underneath: "You rode nine days. Through hunter country. With Kestrian's own handwriting against your chest."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because someone had to know. Because you're walking into that mountain thinking the free cities are a refuge, a door, a *somewhere to run to*, and they're not. There is no somewhere. The council sold you, the usurper wants the mountain, and the hunters are three days behind me on the low roads." Ivo took a breath. "You have no allies. I'm sorry. I'm the closest thing, and I'm a deserter with a horse."

Astrid laughed. It was a short, cracked sound, more surprise than humor, and it seemed to surprise them most of all. "A deserter with a horse," they repeated. "Well. That's more than I had yesterday."

Sef had spread the rumor-map on a flat stone and was already tracing the thread of track toward Thornfell with an ink-stained finger. "The drowned chapel," he murmured, half to himself, eyes distant with the particular look of a man reading a page no one else can see. "Astrid — the Codex marginalia. The chapel on the old salt road. If the ledger exists, it's there. And if the ledger exists—" He stopped. Looked up. "We need to know what was sold. Before we go down. Before *any* of them go down."

Astrid was quiet for a moment. Then they looked at Ivo, and there was something new in the look — not trust, not yet, but the ground trust gets planted in.

"Can you fight, Delegate Tanzer?"

"No," Ivo said honestly.

"Can you ride hard, read a map, talk your way past a checkpoint, and keep a secret?"

"Yes to all four."

"Then you're the most useful person I've met in a month." Astrid turned toward the mountains, where Thornfell stood up against the afternoon sky with its summit lost in cloud, and Ivo followed the look and felt, for the first time since the council chamber, that the stone in his chest had turned to something that might, in time, be called resolve. "Eat," Astrid said, tossing him a wrapped ration from the mare's pack. "We move at dusk. And Tanzer — the hunters behind you. How many?"

"Twelve. Led by a tracker named Corvane. He's good."

"How good?"

"He found a smuggler's road that isn't on any map," Ivo said. "I only found you because I stole the map from the same office he did."

Astrid and Sef exchanged a glance, and Ivo saw it pass between them — the same thought, arriving at the same moment, in the same silence.

*Then he knows where we're going.*

Far above, at the tree line, a crow lifted off a dead pine and flew toward the mountain, and the light was beginning to fail, and the drowned chapel — whatever it held, whoever's name was written in it — waited in the dark below Thornfell like a sentence no one had finished reading.

"We move now," Astrid said. "Dusk is a luxury we just stopped having."
