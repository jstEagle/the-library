## 15. The Ancestor's Ledger

The chapel had drowned a century ago, and the water had never quite finished the job.

It lay in a fold of the Thornfell foothills where a spring had risen and swallowed everything — nave, bell tower, the little graveyard with its tilted stones. The roof had collapsed inward, so that the whole ruin sat beneath a skin of black water like a face under ice, and through that water, when the sun angled right, you could see the chapel's bones: arches furred with weed, a font brimming with clear cold light.

Sef found it because Astrid's maps said it was there. That was the thing about Astrid Halloran's smuggler's maps — they didn't mark roads so much as they marked absences. Places to be unseen. The drowned chapel of Saint Verre was the largest absence for forty miles, and after three days in the open hills with Maren Duskwell's hounds somewhere behind them, absence was the only shelter left.

They waded in at dusk, boots filling immediately, and crossed the nave by way of the fallen rafters, which made a kind of bridge just above the waterline. Ivo went first, arms out for balance, a boy from a council chamber learning very quickly what wet wool weighed. Astrid came last, sword slung across their back, and paused halfway across to look down into the flooded dark below the beams.

"There are candles down there," Astrid said quietly.

Sef looked. Far beneath the surface, wedged in the cracks between flagstones, were stubs of tallow candle — dozens of them — burned down to pools of wax, each pool hardened around something small and pale. Not stones. Sef squinted and wished they hadn't. Teeth. Someone had come here, over years, over decades maybe, and burned candles on the drowned floor, each one anchored in a tooth.

"Offerings," Sef said. "For the dead who don't stay bought." They kept their voice level, because Ivo was watching them both, and one of the three of them had to pretend this was ordinary. "Chapels flood. People leave gifts anyway. It means nothing."

"It means someone knew," Astrid said.

But they moved on, to the one dry place in the ruin — the old vestry behind the altar, raised a hand's width above the flood line, its door swollen shut until Astrid put a shoulder to it. Inside: dust, a rotted lectern, a window of thick green glass gone opaque with grime. And on the floor, arranged with terrible neatness, a dead man.

He had been dead long enough that he was mostly leather and suggestion, but his clothes had been good once — an archivist's coat, the kind Sef had worn for thirty years before the exile, with the double rows of brass buttons and the chain for reading glasses still hanging at the collar. Beside his hand lay an oilcloth packet, tied with wire, and his other hand rested flat upon it like a man holding down a map in wind.

Ivo made a small sound and stepped back into the doorway.

"He came here to hide it," Sef said. Their own voice sounded far away. "Or to die with it."

"You know him?" Astrid asked.

"No." And then, because lying to Astrid had become harder than any vow Sef had ever broken: "Yes. In the way we all knew each other. He was Vellus Corane. Keeper of the western annex. When I was exiled, he was one of the ones who voted for it." Sef crouched, slowly, joints complaining, and did not touch the body yet. "He sent me a letter three years ago. One line. *The margins remember what the pages were made to forget.* I never answered it. I was too proud, and too frightened, and I told myself it was a trap."

Astrid crouched beside them. Up close, in the green half-light of the sealed window, their face looked carved rather than drawn — the swordspeaker's stillness, the stillness that came from having paid for every flinch. "And now?"

"Now he's dead in a drowned chapel with my name's question in his hand, and I'm going to find out why."

Sef unwound the wire. Inside the oilcloth was a book.

Not the Codex. Smaller — a ledger, bound in grey calfskin gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked into segments like something that had tried to bend and been refused. Sef knew the binding style instantly, the way you know your own handwriting: Undercourt work. The mountain's own bookbinders used no glue, no thread; they stitched with sinew cured in something that smelled, even now, faintly of iron and honey. Books bound that way were not made to record. They were made to *hold*.

"This isn't archive stock," Sef said. "This is a price-book. A ledger of sales." Their hands had begun, without permission, to shake. "Archivists talk about these the way sailors talk about the deep places. A ledger bound in sinew keeps its own accounts. You don't read it. It reads *you*, and it checks your arithmetic."

"Then don't read it," Ivo said from the doorway. His voice was thin. "Burn it. We've run three days on rumors and a madwoman's prophecy page, and every mile it gets worse. If that book is another—"

"Ivo." Astrid didn't raise their voice. They almost never did; the oath had taken whatever part of them shouted. But the word landed like a hand on the boy's chest. "We came down off the pass because Sef said the marginalia pointed here. So let Sef read the margins."

Sef opened the ledger to the first page, and the smell rose up — iron and honey and underneath it, faintly, church incense, as if the book remembered being carried here. The paper was vellum, but wrong; it had the grain of skin that had known weather, and along one edge, faint and regular, the ghost of follicles.

They began to read aloud, because some things needed air to be true.

*Received of Aldric Halloran, of the Halloran freehold above Thornfell, one soul entire, unencumbered, freely surrendered —*

"No," Astrid said.

"—in payment of the Oath of Speaking, to endure in his bloodline while the debt stands. Witnessed under bone-light. Sealed in—"

"*No.*" Astrid was on their feet. The chapel water shivered in rings around the rafters. "That's not — my grandmother swore the oath. Her mother before her. It's a vow. It's *spoken*, it's a tradition, it's—" They stopped. Sef watched them arrive at it, watched the certainty collapse the way a snowbridge collapses, all at once and silently. "It's a purchase. We're not oath-keepers. We're *collateral*."

"The Codex says the same thing," Sef said gently. "Different words. The prophecy page names a stolen soul. I thought it meant metaphor. Archivists always think everything is metaphor until the ledger bites them." They turned the page. "There's more."

There was more. The entry continued in the careful, clerkish hand of some Undercourt functionary — and then, cramped into the margin in a different hand, older, pressed hard enough to groove the vellum, a second voice had annotated the sale. Sef recognized the letters from the Codex's marginalia, the ones they'd spent three years learning to read. Aldric Halloran's own hand.

*It was not my soul. Understand this, whoever finds this. I did not sell myself. I could not — the court does not take the willing; willing souls taste of nothing. It must be taken or traded, and it must belong to you utterly. So I sold what was mine utterly. My blood's souls. All of them. Every child of my line, unto the last generation, sold before birth, to buy the speaking.*

"They sold us before we were born," Astrid whispered. "Every generation. We've been paying our own price since before we had mouths to speak with."

"And the oath collects the difference," Sef said. "Memory for magic. I always thought that was the nature of swordspeech — a tax on the tongue. It isn't a tax. It's an *installment*. Each vow spoken into steel draws down against an account that was opened before any of you existed." They heard their own voice go flat and archival, retreating into procedure the way it always did when grief arrived without papers. "You haven't been spending yourselves, Astrid. You've been spending an inheritance that was never given. Only sold."

Ivo sat down slowly on the lectern's base. Nobody told him it was rude to sit in a vestry. Nobody cared.

* * *

They made camp in the vestry because there was nowhere else, and because moving at night past the ford where Maren's net waited would be suicide dressed as courage. Astrid took first watch out of habit. Sef worked by the green window's faint light with the ledger open across their knees, and Ivo slept badly against the wall, muttering fragments of council procedure at his nightmares.

The ledger's later entries were the worst part, though Sef would not have believed that possible at the first page.

The Undercourt kept meticulous books. Each generation of Hallorans got its own line — a date, a name, a valuation. Souls entered the account like grain entering a granary. Some lines were annotated *collected* (Sef did not learn what collected meant, and made themselves not wonder), others *deferred*, others — most — *active*, meaning the person lived and spoke oaths and paid in memory, drawing down a balance they'd never agreed to hold.

And there, near the end, in ink that was barely a century old:

*Astrid Halloran. Active.*

Below it, the valuation column held a figure so large that Sef checked it twice against the exchange tables printed in the back matter — tables that priced a plowman's soul at eleven marks and a bishop's at four hundred. Whatever formula the Undercourt used, Astrid Halloran's number dwarfed them all. A soul priced not for what it was but for what it was *doing*: the last active account, the final collateral on a debt now centuries old. The linchpin soul. The one whose collection would close the book.

Tonight's date. The Codex hadn't lied about the timing either. The ledger wanted settling.

Sef closed the book and sat in the dark with their hands flat on the cover, feeling the sinew binding hum faintly against their palms, like a cat purring, like a snare tightening. This was the moment, they understood, where an honest archivist confessed everything. And Sef had been many things — exiled, disgraced, proud, frightened — but honest only in bursts, like summer rain.

Still. The margins. Vellus Corane had died carrying the question to them, and the least Sef owed the man was accuracy.

"I have to show you the rest," they said to Astrid across the dark vestry. "But you need to hear how I know it's the rest, and not forgery. Because I need you to trust the book, and you shouldn't trust anything tonight, least of all me."

Astrid turned. In the dark their eyes caught the window's green light and held it. "Go on."

"When I was keeper of the Codex annex, my heresy — the crime they exiled me for — wasn't theft. It was annotation. I wrote in the margins. Corrections, cross-references, doubts. The Codex is a prophetic text, and prophecy survives on vagueness; it cannot bear a footnote. So when I found entries that contradicted each other, I *said so*, in ink, on the sacred vellum, and the conclave stripped my keys and named me unbound." Sef's mouth twisted. "Three years I've told myself I was punished for honesty. But here is what I actually learned in those margins, Astrid, and why I sought you out, and why none of this was chance: the Codex has two hands. The prophecy pages are one hand — formal, distant, the court's voice. The marginalia are another. Older. And the older hand doesn't write predictions."

"What does it write?"

"Corrections." Sef reopened the ledger and turned to the final leaf, holding it toward the green glass. "Look at the last entry. Look carefully."

Astrid leaned close. Sef watched them read it, watched the stillness of their face acquire a hairline fracture somewhere behind the eyes.

The final entry was dated — the ledger's own convention — *at settlement*. Two souls were listed. The first was Astrid's, the great number, the linchpin. And beneath it, in the same clerkish hand, was a second line.

*A second soul, surrendered with the first, per the original bargain: one soul entire, plus one soul unnamed, surrendered in fee.*

The name field beside it was smudged.

Not faded. Smudged — deliberately, recently, by a thumb dragged sideways through wet ink. The smudge was fresh. The smudge was *this century*. Someone had opened this ledger within living memory and erased a name, and the erase was imperfect, and beneath it, if Sef tilted the vellum against the green light at exactly the right angle, the ghost of the original letters rose like a bruise through skin: two strokes, possibly three, the shape of a short name.

A name shaped, Sef realized with a cold that started in their spine and finished in their teeth, like the beginning of a name that started with *W*.

"Aldric sold two," Astrid said. Very quietly. The words came out level, which was worse than shaking. "Two souls. One named. One—" Their hand rose and hovered over the smudge without touching it, the way you hover over a burn. "One unnamed. A fee. A *fee*, Sef. What kind of soul is a fee?"

"One that belonged to the seller absolutely," Sef said. "Those were the terms — you saw them. Willing souls taste of nothing. It must be taken or traded, and it must be *yours*." They heard the sentence finish itself in the silence, in the sound of Ivo waking, in the drip of the flooded nave, and hated every word of it. "A child of your line, Astrid. Sold alongside you. Before either of you could be asked."

"But I don't—" Astrid's breath went ragged and reassembled itself, the discipline of a hundred paid-for vows hauling it back into line. "I have no brother. No sister. My grandmother spoke of one pregnancy. One birth. Me."

"Then where," Sef asked, as gently as the question allowed, which was not gently at all, "is the second soul? Sold, accounted, valued, settled — but never collected, never deferred, never marked active. It exists in this book, Astrid. Everything in a sinew-bound ledger exists. So it exists somewhere."

Outside, far off across the flooded hills, something howled — not wolf, not dog, but the tuned, harmonic cry of an echo-hound running a scent of *speech*, hunting the residue of spoken vows. Maren's hounds. Closer than yesterday.

Astrid stood. They took the ledger from Sef's hands with strange care, the way you take a wounded bird, and held it against the window light for a long moment, looking at the smudged name, at the bruise of letters rising through the vellum.

"Wren," they said.

Sef went still. "What did you say?"

"Nothing." Astrid lowered the book. Their face in the green light had gone somewhere Sef couldn't follow — somewhere behind their own eyes, behind a hundred sold memories, staring at a gap shaped exactly like a person. "I said nothing. That name just — arrived. Like remembering something I never learned." They turned toward the door, toward the dark and the hounds and the mountain waiting beyond it with its bone-lit court and its meticulous books. "Get your things, Sef. Both of you. We move before dawn."

"Where?"

"Wherever this ledger was written." Astrid's hand closed around the door's iron ring, and beneath their fingers, faintly, the metal sang — one clear note, unbidden, the sound of steel recognizing speech it hadn't been paid to carry. "Because whoever smudged that name wanted it forgotten. And I want to know why I can almost say it."

Behind them, unnoticed, the ledger lay open on the vestry floor, its sinew stitching humming softly to itself — checking, and finding, at long last, its arithmetic beginning to balance.
