## 17. The Army Moves

The road to Harrowgate had been built for pilgrims, and it died under soldiers.

Maren Duskwell watched it happen from the ridge above the Vaelmoor gate, where the legions poured out in columns of grey and iron-rust red, ten thousand boots striking the old flagstones in a rhythm like a heart learning to beat too fast. Behind them came the supply wagons, the oath-cannon on its cradle of chained blades, the alchemists' carts with their canvas shrouds lashed down against prying eyes. Ahead of them, three days' march to the north, the free cities waited behind walls that had not been tested in a generation.

And between the two, choking every road and river ford and sheep-track like blood in a narrowed vein, the refugees.

She had seen them from the moment the army cleared the city outskirts — families with handcarts, drovers abandoning flocks they could not move fast enough, a woman carrying a loom's shuttle and nothing else, as if that one object were the whole of what she'd been. They moved at the pace of the slowest child. They moved because rumor moved faster: Kestrian marches, Kestrian burns, Kestrian takes swordspeakers apart to see how the vows are stitched.

"Ten thousand," said Captain Orla Venne beside her, reading her thoughts or her face, which amounted to the same thing. "Plus train. We'll eat Harrowgate's granaries bare inside a week if we take the city intact."

"If we take it intact," Maren said.

Orla said nothing. That was its own answer.

Maren pulled her gauntlets tighter and went down the ridge to where the order was waiting for her. She had known it was waiting since dawn; you learned to smell paper in the usurper's army the way horses smelled weather.

* * *

The field headquarters had been pitched in a burned-out tithe barn, and Kestrian's seal sat on the dispatch case like a fat black spider. Maren broke the wax alone. She had asked for privacy and been granted it, which told her everything before she read a word. Orders meant for witnesses came with an audience. Orders like this came with closed doors.

She read it twice.

The refugee columns moving ahead of the army were slowing the advance — clogging fords, fouling wells, halting the baggage train to beg. More to the point, they were slowing it *predictably*, and the army's forward scouts reported that among the third column, camped tonight at Marlow Ford, there were travelers who did not beg. Travelers who watched the roads. Council hunters, most likely, tracking the same quarry the inquisitors tracked.

The order was simple. At first light, Maren would take her wing of inquisitors and the flame-carts and burn the column at the ford. All of it. The council would learn that harboring their hunters among civilians bought those civilians ash. And Harrowgate would learn what awaited cities that debated surrender too long.

There was a second paragraph. It was the kind of paragraph that existed so the man signing could tell himself the document said something else. *Collateral losses are regretted and will be memorialized.* Memorialized. Maren imagined the plaque. *Here lie four hundred people who walked slowly.*

She rolled the order closed. Her hands did not shake. That was the part she noticed most, afterward — not the burning, but the steadiness. She had trained her hands out of shaking years ago, back when she still believed the shaking was the problem.

Outside, the army stretched past the horizon in both directions, and somewhere in it was a boy named Tam who had joined for bread and now drilled with a pike taller than he was, and somewhere else was the quartermaster's clerk who wept quietly over requisition lists, and somewhere else the chaplains who had stopped preaching anything but obedience. An army was just a country that had learned to walk. Hers had learned.

Maren mounted and rode to brief her wing.

* * *

Marlow Ford at dusk was a country of small fires.

The refugees had made camp along the river's southern bank in the ragged crescent that exhausted people always made — fires close together for comfort, close enough that a spark from one could find the next. Maren stood on the northern rise with her glass and counted hearths instead of souls, because counting souls was a thing she refused to let herself be good at.

Four hundred and eleven fires. Say five to a fire, some more. Two thousand, give or take the ones already dead of the road who hadn't been buried deep.

"They're just people," said Inquisitor Hask, beside her. Young, clever, the kind of faithful that comes from never having been asked to do anything hard for his faith. His voice had a crack in it he was trying to pretend wasn't there.

"Yes," Maren said. Because lying to him would have been a mercy she couldn't afford to hand out, and cruelty dressed as honesty was the only coin she had left to spend.

"And the order—"

"Is the order." She passed him the glass. "You'll take the eastern arc with the second flame-cart. I take the western. We go at first light, when the river mist is thick and they can't see us coming and can't run anywhere dry."

Hask looked through the glass for a long time. When he lowered it his jaw was set wrong, like a door hanging off its hinge. "Inquisitor-General. There are children down there. You can see them. Little — they've got little flags up, some of the families, little white flags, like the old stories say farmers did in the border wars so that—"

"I know the stories, Hask."

"The stories say you *didn't burn the white flag*. That was the whole story. That was the point of the story."

Maren took the glass back. Through it, at the crescent's center, someone had indeed raised a bedsheet on a spear shaft — a family's whole inventory of surrender, offered to an army that hadn't arrived yet. Offering peace to the night air, in advance, hoping it would keep until morning.

"We'll ride down first," she heard herself say. "Before the flame-carts. We'll announce the column is confiscated for military transport. We'll order them off the road, south, away from the river. Anyone who scatters scatters alive."

Hask stared at her. "The order says—"

"The order says what it says. What happens before the order is carried out is my discretion." She was surprised by her own voice, level as a mason's line. Somewhere under the words, something in her was screaming in a very tidy, well-organized way. "Get your arc ready, Inquisitor. If anyone asks why the column wasn't burned at first light, tell them Duskwell delayed to confirm the council hunters' position. That will even be true."

It would even be true. That was the trick of it, she thought, walking back down the rise. You didn't have to disobey. You only had to find the seam between the order and the hour, and stand in it, and call standing in a seam a strategy. She had watched men do it their whole careers in the other direction — finding the seam that let them be crueler than the paper required. The seam worked both ways. It had to. Otherwise the world was only ever what was written on it.

She did not sleep. She sat with the order on her knee and the white flag in her mind's eye and composed, sentence by sentence, the report she would write tomorrow, and hated how easily the sentences came. *Column dispersed without significant loss of life due to timely intervention of inquisition forces.* Clean. Efficient. A sentence with no children in it anywhere.

That was the craft they had given her, she realized. Not swordwork, not interrogation — though she had both. The true craft was this: the ability to hold a horror in one hand and language in the other and use the second to make the first weigh nothing. Kestrian had never needed to break her. He had only needed to hire the part of her that was good at sentences.

* * *

At first light the mist lay on Marlow Ford like a held breath, and Maren rode into it with forty inquisitors and no fire.

The refugees woke to the sound of hooves and came out of their blankets the way small animals come out of burrows — all ears and stillness. Maren stood in her stirrups at the crescent's edge, where the white flag hung sodden and gray, and made her announcement in the flat parade voice that carried without seeming to try.

"By decree of Kestrian, rightful sovereign of the realm, this road is conscripted. The column will disperse southward, away from the ford, effective immediately. Food stores at Thornholt will receive you. Go now. Do not gather again on any military road."

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then the crescent began to come apart — not in panic, worse, in the terrible orderly way of people who have practiced being displaced. Mothers waking children who had learned to wake silently. Old men folding blankets that would be slept in that night on open ground. A man hitching a cart whose axle had been repaired twice with rope.

A woman stepped toward Maren's horse. Gray-haired, straight-backed, with flour still in the creases of her knuckles, as if she'd left her bakery mid-loaf and simply kept walking.

"You're the one they talk about," the woman said. "Duskwell. The one who catches the swordspeaker."

"I'm the one who clears roads."

"My daughter's boy saw one of them once. A swordspeaker. Down in Millbrook, before the plague." Her voice had the mildness of the very tired. "He spoke a vow over our well when it went bitter. Just — talked to it, sort of. Like scolding a dog. And it sweetened." She looked up at Maren, and her eyes were the worst thing on the whole road, because they weren't accusing. They were asking. "Is that what makes us traitors? Is that the crime?"

Behind Maren, the flame-carts waited with their loads of rendered tallow and pitch, and the morning was burning away the mist minute by minute, and somewhere north of here her orders' real authors were counting hours. Every minute she spent being questioned by a baker was a minute the seam grew thinner.

"The road is conscripted," Maren said. "Go south."

The woman looked at her a while longer. Then she turned and called to her family in a voice worn smooth as a stair, and the column flowed around Maren's horse like water around a stone, and not one of them spat at her, and somehow that was unbearable too. Hatred would have been a transaction. This was just — people obeying her, because she was the armed one, because obedience was the last skill the road had left them.

By full light the southern bank was empty. Fires drowned. Four hundred hearths scattered into the hills, alive, carrying with them a new story about the inquisitor-general who burned nothing at Marlow Ford — and Maren knew exactly what that story would cost, and exactly how briefly it would buy anything.

Hask found her at the ford's edge. His face was wet and he pretended it was the mist, and she let him.

"You delayed," he said carefully. "To confirm the hunters' positions."

"I confirmed them. They were never in the column. The intelligence was wrong." She handed him the folded order. "Carry this back to division. Tell them Marlow Ford is clear, the objective is unmet, the quarry was not present, and recommend the flame-carts be reassigned to supply escort, where they'll do less damage."

Hask took it. "Inquisitor-General — thank you."

"Don't thank me." She turned her horse north, toward the army, toward the sentence she would have to write. "Thank me when I've done it twice."

* * *

Kestrian's reply came by hawk at dusk, and it was worse than anger.

Anger would have meant she mattered. Instead the dispatch held two paragraphs in the chancellor's hand, not the king's — the king signed nothing anymore, everyone knew the king's signature lived in a drawer and was applied by stamp — and the paragraphs were courteous the way a knife is clean.

*The Inquisitor-General's discretion is noted. Discretion is a resource, and resources are audited. Be advised that the operation at Marlow Ford has been rescheduled and executed by other assets. Casualties consistent with projections. The General is reminded that her unique value lies in the retrieval of the asset Halloran, and that value is contingent on results. The mountain opens soon. Be there when it does.*

She read it three times. *Executed by other assets.* She made herself picture it — the flame-carts going in without her, the mist, the white sheet on its spear, the crescent of small fires catching one from the next — and then she made herself stop, because the picture was doing something to her hands after all, and she needed her hands.

So instead she did what she did. She wrote the report.

*Column dispersed without significant loss of life due to timely intervention of inquisition forces.* She looked at the sentence. It was true of her morning and a lie about her day, both at once, and it would be filed, and it would become the record, and the record would become the history, and somewhere thirty years from now a student would read it and feel nothing, which was the entire design.

She almost signed it.

Then she took a second sheet, and beneath the official report, in her own smaller hand, she wrote the other record — the one with numbers in it. Four hundred and eleven fires. Estimated two thousand souls, dispersed south. One white flag, unburned. One order received, deferred, executed by others at cost of [she stopped; she did not know the number; no one would ever tell her the number]. Signed, dated, sealed in her personal wax, the one with the Duskwell wolf, and pushed deep into the false bottom of her dispatch case where three other documents already lived, each one a hairline in the stone of her career, each one a small treason she had told herself was merely bookkeeping.

Loyalty cracked along a hairline, her grandmother used to say of frost-split granite — not dramatically, not all at once, but on some cold morning the whole face of the cliff simply comes away. Maren pressed her palm flat against the dispatch case and wondered, not for the first time, how many hairlines she was, and whether the cold morning had already happened without her noticing.

*The mountain opens soon. Be there when it does.*

They wanted Astrid Halloran found because the Codex said the Halloran name opened Thornfell's depths, and the depths held the source — the raw vein of vow-light that Kestrian's stolen blades only sipped from. Maren had read the intercepted fragments. She knew what the usurper intended to do with the source, and she had filed that knowledge in the same place as the rest, the place where knowing and acting were separate rooms.

But lately the rooms had developed a door.

She stood at her tent flap and looked south, where the refugees' scattered fires were kindling one by one across the dark hills — hundreds of tiny lights, persistent, absurd, like stars that had decided to live on the ground. Somewhere out in that darkness was a disgraced archivist and a swordspeaker with a prophecy on their trail, and somewhere beneath a mountain was whatever it was that Kestrian hungered for, and somewhere inside Maren Duskwell was her brother's voice, six years dead, saying her name the way he'd said it when they were children: *Mare. Mare, look.* He had always been pointing at things. Skies, rivers, strange birds. Look, look.

She looked.

The mountain was three days east. And for the first time since she'd taken the commission, Maren Duskwell allowed herself to wonder — cleanly, efficiently, in the same tidy voice she used for reports — what she would find down there besides her quarry.

What she would find besides a soul.
