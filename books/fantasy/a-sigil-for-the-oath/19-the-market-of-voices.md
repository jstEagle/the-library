## 19. The Market of Voices

Ivo Tanzer had imagined the descent into Thornfell a hundred ways since the Hollow Stair swallowed them: as a tomb, as a throat, as a wound in the world that had never quite closed. He had not imagined it would smell like a bakery.

The scent reached them before anything else — warm bread, honeyed wine, woodsmoke, cut flowers — drifting up through the dark in layers so distinct they seemed almost rude. After three days on the Stair, where gravity leaned toward the mountain's heart and their own spoken words fell upward past their ears like startled birds, the smell of ordinary human comfort made his eyes sting.

"That's not bread," Sef said flatly. The archivist hadn't slept properly since the ice bridge; there was a new caution in them now, a way of holding their hands close to their body as though afraid they might be taken and sold. "Don't let it be bread. It's memory. They bake the *memory* of bread down here. It's cheaper than flour."

Astrid said nothing. Since the Split Pass — since the word that had come out of Astrid's mouth unsworn and unbidden and had caught the falling stone like a caught breath — they had been quiet in a way Ivo found worse than any argument. They walked with one hand resting on the pommel of their sword, not gripping it. Resting. Like someone keeping a hand on a dog that might bite.

"Cheaper than flour," Ivo repeated, because repeating things was how he kept from screaming. "You say that like it's normal."

"Nothing under this mountain is normal," Sef said. "Everything under this mountain is *priced*. Learn the difference before it costs you."

The Stair opened.

Ivo had no better word for it. One moment they were descending a spiral of black steps cut for no human stride, and the next the wall simply ended and the dark bloomed outward into a cavern so vast that the lantern light fell into it and gave up. And then the cavern lit itself — slowly, reluctantly, the way a city lights at dusk — and Ivo understood that the lights were not lamps.

They were voices.

Thousands of small flames hung in the air at every height, gold and green and one deep bruised violet, each one pulsing faintly with a rhythm too irregular to be fire and too alive to be anything else. They clustered thick along the cavern walls like ivy, drifted in slow rivers across the ceiling, pooled in the hollows between stalactites. Where they gathered brightest, Ivo could hear — not words, exactly. Fragments. A woman laughing at something told twice. A child reciting numbers. Someone, somewhere far off in the glow, singing.

"Oh," he said. It was all he could manage. "Oh, that's—"

"Don't name them," Astrid said quietly. It was the first full sentence they'd offered in hours. "If you name what you're looking at, it becomes yours to answer for. Just look."

The Market filled the cavern floor below them in terraces, stalls built of pale bone-colored timber — or bone, Ivo thought, and then made himself stop thinking — arranged in winding streets with no discernible logic except appetite. Figures moved among them. Some were human-shaped. Some were nearly. Merchants' lanterns were unnecessary here; each stall was lit by its own cluster of the floating flames, drawn to the goods like moths that had forgotten they were moths.

Vials everywhere. That was the thing that finally made it real to him, more than the lights or the smell or the figures whose shadows bent the wrong way. Vials — thousands upon thousands of them, racked in wooden grids like spices, like apothecary stock, each one holding a curl of colored smoke or a bead of light no bigger than a currant. He saw a stall where a broad-shouldered woman in a mask of polished tin held a vial to a customer's lips the way you'd hold a spoon of medicine to a sick child. He saw the customer's face change — saw decades fall off it, saw the mouth go soft — and then saw the merchant's scale-tips shift, and coin change hands, and the moment end.

"What did she buy?" he whispered.

"A birthday," Sef said. They had gone gray around the mouth. "Or a funeral. Or her mother's hands braiding her hair. It doesn't matter which. What matters is the price." They nodded toward the far side of the terrace, where a narrow door stood open in nothing — a doorway framed in iron, leading nowhere, with a queue of people shuffling toward it. "Look at the exit trade."

Ivo looked. Beyond the empty-framed door, in a dimmer annex of the market, the goods changed. No vials there. Instead: ledgers. Contracts. And people signing them — people who looked lighter afterward, emptier, walking away with the loose-jointed ease of those who have set down something heavy without checking whether they'd need it again.

"They sell forward," Sef said. "Memories they haven't made yet. First kisses. Children's faces. Their own funerals. The Undercourt pays well for futures — they're rare stock." A pause, and then, quieter, with an archivist's terrible precision: "That's how Aldric Halloran paid for your friend's oath. Not with a soul already lived. With a soul still owed."

Astrid's hand tightened on the pommel. Just once. Then relaxed.

"We're not stopping," Astrid said. "We cross the market floor, we find the Warden's road, we keep moving. Touch nothing. Taste nothing. Say nothing you wouldn't want read back to you at a trial." They looked at Ivo, and their eyes were kind and exhausted and frightened, and Ivo hated how much comfort he took from the fright. "Especially you, delegate. You've spent your whole life buying things. This is the first place you'll ever shop where the currency is you."

* * *

The Market accepted them the way water accepts a dropped stone: briefly interested, then indifferent.

Ivo kept his eyes down and his hands in his pockets and failed completely. He couldn't not look. Every stall was a sermon. There was a merchant who dealt only in smells — a rack of stoppered clay pots labeled in a script that squirmed when read — and beside him another who sold *names*, actual names, whispering samples to passersby until they flinched at hearing themselves called by something they'd never been called before and suddenly missed. There was a cage of songbirds that sang nothing but arguments, overheard decades ago through thin walls, and a woman selling silence in graded qualities, from the silence after snowfall to the silence in a house after someone has died, priced accordingly.

And through it all, the vials. Rows of them. Lives in glass.

He told himself he wasn't searching. He told himself that very firmly, right up until the stall with the music.

It was tucked into a crook of the terrace where two bone-timber awnings met, smaller than its neighbors, run by an old man whose face Ivo couldn't hold onto — not because it was hidden, but because it kept being a face he half-knew: his grandfather, then a Harrowgate dockmaster, then no one. On the counter before him lay perhaps thirty vials, and above each one hovered a tiny flame, and from each flame came sound. Thin, private sound. A man's voice telling a joke badly. Rain on a specific roof. And underneath everything, threaded through the whole stall like a bass note—

Singing.

Ivo stopped walking.

It was a lullaby. Not a famous one — not one he could have named if you'd held a knife to him — but he knew it the way you know a staircase in the dark, the way your feet know it, the little rising turn in the third phrase where she always went up higher than the melody strictly required because she believed songs should be generous. His mother's voice. His mother's voice, singing him down to sleep in a rented room over a chandler's shop in Harrowgate the winter his father's ships went missing, when he was six, when the rent was late, when she sang anyway, every night, because it was the one thing she could give him that cost nothing.

Cost nothing *then.*

"How much," he heard himself say.

"Ivo." Sef's hand landed on his shoulder. "No."

"How much," he said again, and the old man smiled with a face that was becoming his father's now, which was impossible, which was obscene, and turned a small brass tag on the counter so it faced him.

The number was written in a currency Ivo didn't recognize — a sigil, a coiled mark — but beneath it, helpfully, in plain trade-script, was the exchange rate. *One hour of remembered warmth, freely given. Or: five years of a future joy, buyer's choice. Or: the face of whoever you love most, held in reserve.*

"There are other versions," the old man said gently, in his grandfather's voice, in his father's, in no one's. "This is the original recording, taken at source. There are copies. Copies are cheaper. But copies taste like copies." He lifted the vial — just the vial, the flame staying obediently above it — and held it to the lantern-light, and inside, the smoke curled and curled, and Ivo could see, or thought he could see, the shape of a room in it. A chandler's shop. Snow at the window.

"He's six years old in this one," the merchant added, tender as a knife. "Listen how her voice changes on the third phrase. She thinks he's asleep. She sings it *for herself.* That's why the copies never satisfy. Everyone wants the part meant for someone else."

"Ivo." Sef again, closer now, low and urgent. "Listen to me. Everything here is priced, and the pricing is the trap. You pay warmth for warmth, and the court keeps the difference, and the difference is always you. That's how they got ten thousand flames in this cave. Not one of them arrived intending to stay."

"I know." His voice sounded very far away to himself. "I know how markets work, Sef. I sat on the Harrowgate trade council for two years. I know exactly what happens when the seller knows what the buyer can't live without." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the whole lesson of my life. I'm the council's youngest delegate because I learned it early. Price the grief, corner the supply, wait."

"And knowing that, you'll still buy it."

"Yes," Ivo said. "Obviously. That's the other whole lesson of my life."

He didn't remember deciding. He remembered the weight of his purse in his pocket — real coin, honest coin, useless here — and setting it on the counter anyway as gesture, as apology, as the last act of a man who still believed in ordinary money. And then the merchant tilted the vial toward his lips, and Sef's grip tightened on his shoulder and did not stop him, and Astrid stood three stalls away with their back turned, deliberately, giving him privacy or refusing witness, Ivo would never know which.

He drank.

* * *

There is no honest way to describe the taste. He tried, later, sitting against a cavern wall with his knees drawn up while Sef watched him with something like pity and Astrid kept watch on the dark road ahead. He tried words like *honey* and *smoke* and *snowlight* and none of them were true.

Here is what was true. Her voice went up on the third phrase. Higher than the melody required. Generous. And beneath it, the specific creak of the chandler's stairs, and the cold coming off the window glass, and the wool of her shawl against his cheek when she leaned over the bed to blow out the candle — and the smell of tallow and her hands, chapped from the washing she took in, and the exact quality of dark in that rented room, which was a safe dark, a dark with a person inside it.

She thought he was asleep. She sang it for herself. And six-year-old Ivo Tanzer, feigning sleep with the skill of a child who has learned that sleeping children don't get cried over, lay still and let her have it, and loved her so much his ribs hurt, and never once in forty years had remembered that he'd been awake.

Now he remembered. Now it was in him again, whole, the way you'd swallow a sun.

He wept in the Market of Voices, standing in front of a stall, in front of strangers and not-strangers and things shaped like customers, and nobody minded, because grief was inventory there. He wept the ugly, honking, graceless way, and when it eased he found the merchant watching him with a face that was, at last, no one's at all — just a mild, professional kindness.

"The rest of her," Ivo said. His voice was wrecked. "The other nights. The other songs. All of it. How much?"

The merchant's expression didn't change, and that was the answer.

"You've paid once," Sef said softly. They crouched beside him, and their eyes behind their smudged spectacles were wet too — Ivo noticed that, filed it away, a disgraced archivist crying in a soul-market over a stranger's dead mother. "Once is a gift. Twice is a habit. There are men in Vaelmoor's upper quarters who spend fortunes down here, Ivo, buying back their childhoods a night at a time, and they are the emptiest men I have ever seen, because the court prices each memory at exactly the value of what remains of you. The poorer you get, the dearer it costs. That's the whole architecture. That's the *design.*"

"Then design me a way out."

"There isn't one out. There's only *through.*" Sef took his arm and hauled him upright, gently, firmly, the way you steady a drunk. "Through, and fast, before you start calculating what else you could spare."

Because that was the horror he couldn't stop, walking. The arithmetic started on its own. *What would one more night cost? What about her voice saying my name? What about the day at the harbor, the last day, the one I've lost entirely — surely the court would sell that cheaply now that I'm diminished?* Each calculation felt reasonable. Each one was a doorframe in a corridor that narrowed.

Astrid fell into step beside him somewhere on the far terrace, matching his pace, saying nothing for a long time. When they finally spoke, they spoke to the road ahead.

"My grandmother taught me that oaths take memories," they said. "Payment per vow. I've known that since I was twelve years old. Do you know what I did? I decided the important memories would survive. I decided *I'd* be the judge of what mattered." A dry, terrible almost-laugh. "Standing here, in this place, I finally understand that's not how it works. The oath takes what it takes, and it took the reason I swore in the first place, and I've been carrying a sword for a purpose I can't remember because the paying and the purpose were the same transaction." They glanced at him sidelong. "So. Was it worth it? The song."

Ivo thought about lying. Thought about the dignified answer, the delegate's answer, the one he'd deliver to a committee.

"She thought I was asleep," he said. "Forty years, and I never knew I was awake for it. Yes. It was worth it. And I'd burn this whole cavern down before I paid twice, and both of those things are true at the same time, and I think that's what this place *is.* Both things. Forever."

Ahead, the market streets began to thin. The floating flames grew sparser, and the ones that remained burned lower, dimmer, drawn downward — Ivo realized with a lurch that the whole cavern's light was flowing in one direction, a slow golden migration toward some deeper chamber, like sparks returning to a fire.

Toward the throne, Sef had called it, back on the ice. A cage of ten thousand bound voices. The source.

The road sloped. The air grew warmer. And from somewhere below — faint, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat — came the sound of a great many voices breathing in unison.

Then, cutting across it, sharp and wrong and utterly out of place:

Boots. Armored boots, on the far stair. Many of them, descending in formation, and beneath the armor-rattle a sound Ivo knew from council war-rooms and had prayed never to hear underground: the loading-song of an oath-cannon's winches, cranking tight.

Astrid went still as a drawn blade.

"They're early," Astrid said. "Kestrian's already coming down."
