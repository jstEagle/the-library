## 20. Warden of the Court

The gate was not a gate.

That was the first thing I understood, standing on the last landing of the Hollow Stair with my lungs full of air that fell upward and my ears still ringing from three days of descending. A gate implies a wall, a boundary between here and elsewhere. This was a threshold in the way a wound is a threshold — a place where the world had been cut open and never quite healed shut.

It stood alone at the center of an immense cavern, black iron taller than three men, worked into the shapes of hands. Thousands of them, palm to palm, fingers interlaced, forming the frame around an opening that glowed faintly from within — not firelight, not lamplight, but that bone-lit glow the dying miner had tried to describe with his last wet breath. Candlelight through marrow. He'd been more accurate than he knew, and it had killed him to know it.

Beyond the gate, I could hear them. Not words. Not yet. But voices the way you hear the sea before you see it — a vast, layered murmur pressing against the iron, ten thousand throats breathing in a rhythm too slow to be sleep.

"They're real," Sef whispered behind me. Their voice had grown smaller the deeper we came; the archivist who had lectured me for weeks about the Codex's provenance now sounded like a child in a cathedral. "Gods below, Astrid. All this time I thought the Undercourt was metaphor. Allegory. The way archivists talk about things we can't verify."

"It's not a court," Ivo said. The young delegate had his hand on his knife and no faith in it whatsoever. His knuckles were white as tallow. "Courts have judges. Laws. This is—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. We all felt what it was: a granary. A place where something precious was stored against hunger.

I drew breath to answer and the mountain took it.

Not violently. That's what I would tell myself later, trying to make sense of the shame — that nothing about it was violent. The air simply moved through me the way a reader moves through a book, turning pages, and somewhere in the turning my next words were lifted out of my mouth before I chose them.

*"Astrid Halloran,"* said the cavern, in my own voice, from every direction at once. *"Daughter of the oath. Bearer of the debt."*

The echo of my name rolled down the stair we had climbed for three days and up toward the distant pinprick of grey daylight, and I understood with sick clarity that everything down here had heard us coming. Had perhaps been hearing us since Vaelmoor. Since the waystation. Since the night my grandmother pressed her failing lips to my forehead and made me kneel before a blade and speak.

Sef's hand found my sleeve. "Don't answer it."

"I don't think answering is optional anymore," I said.

* * *

We crossed the threshold because there was nowhere else to go. Behind us the Hollow Stair had begun, very quietly, to close — stone flowing like slow honey over the steps we'd descended, sealing our retreat with the patience of geology. Ivo saw it first and made a sound like a man swallowing broken glass. I made the decision the way I'd made every decision since the Codex opened: badly, quickly, and forward.

The passage beyond the gate widened by degrees, and the bone-light thickened until I could see where it came from.

Veins. Actual veins, running through the rock overhead like the luminous rivers of some drowned god — branching, forking, pulsing with a pale gold light that moved in waves, and inside each wave, if I let my eyes unfocus just enough, were faces. Not carved. *Present.* Pressed up against the inside of the stone like children at a window, mouths open, singing or screaming or simply saying their names over and over so they wouldn't forget them.

I stopped looking. It didn't help. I could feel them looking back.

"Ten thousand," Sef breathed. They had stopped walking and were staring upward with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person watching their life's work curdle into horror. "The Codex says ten thousand. I thought it was numerology. Symbolic. Archivists always assume numbers are symbolic when the truth is unbearable."

"How long?" Ivo asked quietly. "How long have they been down here?"

Sef didn't answer. Neither did I. The ledger in my pack seemed to grow heavier with every step, its marginalia crawling through my mind: *sold by the pound, sold by the year, sold in perpetuity.* Aldric Halloran's tidy hand. My blood's tidy hand.

The passage opened at last into the court itself, and I will do my best to describe it, though description feels like a kind of betrayal — making beautiful what should only ever be terrible.

It was a hall built inside the mountain's hollow heart, vaulted high enough that the vein-lit ceiling vanished into gloom. The floor was polished black stone that held our reflections too perfectly, so that we walked upon ourselves. Pillars rose in ranks toward a dais at the far end, and upon the dais —

I stopped.

Upon the dais stood a man.

He was tall, straight-backed, dressed in robes of deep green gone grey with age at the hems, and his hair was white but his face — his face was wrong, wrong in the specific way that made my stomach drop through the floor and keep falling. Because I knew that face. I had known it my whole life from the portrait above my grandmother's hearth: strong jaw, deep-set eyes, a mouth that looked like it had once smiled often and now merely remembered how.

Aldric Halloran. Founder of the oath. Dead these two hundred years.

He was looking directly at me, and he was smiling.

* * *

"You came home."

His voice reached me before he took a single step, carried on that strange upward-falling air, and it was tender — that was the worst of it, the tenderness. It was the voice of a grandfather greeting a child returned from a long illness. Warm. Unhurried. Certain of its welcome.

"Astrid Halloran." He descended the dais slowly, and each step rang on the black stone like a bell being tested. "Look at you. Taller than your grandmother. You have her stubbornness in the shoulders — she carried it the same way, like a yoke she refused to admit was there."

"Don't," I said. My hand had found my sword without my permission, and the steel sang faintly against my palm, hungry for a vow, any vow, to give it teeth. "Don't say her name. Don't speak as if—"

"As if I knew her?" He stopped ten paces away, and this close I could see the impossibility of him clearly: skin smooth but for lines of expression, eyes clear and dark and patient as wells. No age touched him. Two centuries had passed him by like weather passing over a window. "Maren Halloran swore her oath across my knee, child. I held the blade while she spoke into it. I taught her father's father to hold a sword. Every vow your line has spoken, I have heard. Every memory they paid, I have kept." He spread his hands, gentle, terrible. "You are the last page of a book I began. Forgive an old man for reading aloud."

Behind me, Sef made a small strangled sound. Ivo's knife was out now, absurdly, a toothpick raised against a mountain.

"What are you?" I asked.

"I am the warden." He said it simply, the way a lighthouse-keeper might say it, or a gravedigger. "The court requires a keeper of terms. Someone must count what comes in. Someone must ensure that nothing leaves." Something flickered behind the well-dark eyes — there and gone, fast as a fish under ice. "And someone must wait. I have waited a long time for you."

"For me specifically." My mouth tasted of copper. "Or for the debt?"

"Child." He sighed, and the sigh moved through the hall and the veins pulsed brighter with it, ten thousand faces pressing closer to their windows, listening. "There is no difference. There never has been. That is the lesson your family refused to learn for four generations, and the lesson that brought you down my stair at last."

"My stair," I repeated. "This is yours, then. This court. These—" I gestured upward, at the veined light, and hated myself for the way my hand shook. "These people."

Something changed in his face then. The tenderness stayed, but a hardness surfaced beneath it, bedrock under topsoil. When he answered, his voice was quieter and far worse.

"No one down here is a person, Astrid. That is the first term of the house, and you will learn it or it will break you." He turned, robes whispering, and began to walk along the rank of pillars, and I realized with a lurch that he was giving us a tour. Hosting us. "They are voices. They are payment rendered and held in trust. What you see in the veins is not suffering. It is *storage*." 

"Not suffering," Ivo said hoarsely. "I watched one of those faces move its mouth. It was saying a name. Over and over. That's not storage. Storage doesn't pray."

Aldric paused. He did not turn around.

"The miner," he said. "The one who gave you the key. His grandfather sold a daughter's voice to buy a winter's coal. She has been saying that name for ninety years." Now he turned, and the compassion in his face was genuine, which made it monstrous. "Would it comfort you to know she does not know she is doing it? The voice persists. The pain does not. We are not cruel here, Astrid. We are *precise*. Cruelty is waste, and waste is theft from the court."

"And the court serves whom?" Sef demanded. Their voice cracked in the middle, scholar's composure finally failing. "The Codex never names the creditor. Four hundred years of marginalia and no name. Who holds the debt, Aldric? Who do you count for?"

For the first time, the warden of the Undercourt looked tired.

"That," he said, "is a question for tomorrow's terms. Tonight you are guests." And the word *guests* settled over the hall like frost, and somewhere in the veins overhead, ten thousand voices went silent all at once — a silence so vast and sudden it rang like a struck bell, and every face turned, in unison, to look at me.

* * *

They fed us.

That detail would haunt me longer than the veins, longer even than his face. A table had appeared in a side chamber — or had always been there; the court had a way of making you unsure whether things happened or had merely been waiting — laid with bread and roasted roots and dark wine, real food, good food, the kind of meal that belongs to homecoming. He seated me at his right hand. He poured my wine himself.

"Do you remember the oath-day?" he asked, as if asking after my health.

"I was seven." I kept my eyes on the cup. "My grandmother was dying. She put her sword flat on the table and told me to put my hands on the steel and speak the Halloran words. I didn't understand what I was agreeing to. I didn't understand anything except that she was frightened, and she'd never been frightened in front of me before."

"She was frightened," Aldric agreed softly, "because she knew what the oath would take from you, and she loved you, and she needed you bound anyway. That is the whole tragedy of our family in one sentence. We are a lineage of people who loved each other and spent each other." He refilled his own cup. "Do you know why she was dying, Astrid?"

"Her heart. Everyone said her heart."

"Her heart was fine." He set down the decanter with a click that echoed strangely, too loudly, as though the room were listening. "She had paid too many memories to keep the ones that mattered. She forgot how to swallow, near the end. Forgot my name — my true name, the one from before the ledger. She sat across from me for eleven years, child, calling me *sir*, because I was a stranger to her and I could never tell her otherwise." His well-dark eyes found mine across the candlelight. "The oath runs on memory. Yours has been running on more than yours for a very long time."

The chamber tilted. Or I did. Sef's hand closed over mine under the table — the disgraced archivist, the liar, the keeper of forbidden pages — and I gripped back hard enough to hurt us both.

"The second soul," I said. My voice came out flat, sanded down. "In the ledger. Two souls, and one smudged past reading. Is that what you're telling me?"

Aldric smiled, and it was the tender smile again, the grandfather smile, and it was the cruelest thing I had ever seen.

"Eat," he said gently. "Drink. You've walked three days downward and you'll want your strength for what I have to show you. The terms can wait until morning."

"There is no morning down here," Ivo said.

"There is here." Aldric rose, and the candles dimmed and relit in sequence as he moved, bowing to his passage. At the chamber's door he paused, half-turned, framed in bone-light.

"You slept badly on the road, I think. Waking at odd hours. Dreams of a childhood that isn't quite yours — a river you never fished, a mother's voice singing words in the wrong key. Rooms in your memory with someone else's furniture." His voice dropped, soft as a confession, warm as a knife going in. "You told yourself it was imagination. It wasn't. She has been waiting in you a long time, Astrid. And now that you're home —"

He bowed, precisely, courteously, the warden seeing his guests to rest.

"— she knows it too."

The doors closed behind him. All of them. I heard locks turning in doors we had never seen, iron sliding through iron throughout the court, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat — and beneath my breastbone, faint but unmistakable, something that was not me stirred, and pressed outward, and listened.

*Wren,* it thought, tasting the shape of its own name. *My name was Wren.*

The candles burned low. None of us slept.
