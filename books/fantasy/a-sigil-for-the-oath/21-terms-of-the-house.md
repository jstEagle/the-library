## 21. Terms of the House

Aldric Halloran had waited four hundred and eleven years for this meal, and he had cooked it himself.

He stood in the kitchen beneath the court — a room no living hand had touched since his own had been young — and watched the venison turn on a spit driven by warm air rising from the mountain's marrow-veins. The firelight moved across copper pans polished so long ago they remembered their own shine. He had hunted the deer in the upper forests two nights past, dressed it with a knife older than the free cities, salted it from a block of grey mineral that wept faintly when struck, as everything down here eventually learned to do.

He was nervous. That was the absurdity of it. He had faced inquisitors, usurpers, the slow centuries themselves without flinching, and now his hands were not entirely steady over a gravy of dark wine and juniper because his great-great-granddaughter's child was sitting at his table.

*Not his table*, he corrected himself. The house had never been his. That was rather the whole of the problem.

He carried the food up the winding stair to the guest hall, balancing the tray with the ease of a body that did not tire, did not age, did not hunger except in the one place where all his hunger lived. Through the door's iron lattice he could hear them: the archivist's low voice laying out options like cards, the young delegate — Tanzer, the boy who had abandoned his council to come — asking questions too fast, and Astrid answering almost none of them.

Almost. Aldric heard the silences between the answers, and those silences were eloquent. Those silences were *his*.

He unlocked the door with the black key that answered only to him and entered carrying supper like a peace offering, which is what it was, and which would not be accepted, which he knew.

* * *

The guest hall had been built to flatter. Its walls were veined with bone-lit channels that glowed the color of candleflame seen through a closed fist, and its long table was cut from heartwood of a forest that no longer existed above ground. Three places were set. Aldric had considered setting four and then remembered, with an old and practiced wound, that there were only three of them, though there should by rights have been four, and that absence sat at every meal he had ever served.

"You're the warden," Sef said. Not a question. The disgraced archivist sat rigid, hands flat on the table, eyes doing what archivists' eyes always did — reading the room, cataloguing the light-sources, counting exits. There were none. Sef would have worked that out already; Aldric could see it in the way their shoulders had stopped pretending otherwise.

"I am the warden," Aldric agreed. "And you are Sef Arandel, formerly First Keeper of the Vellum Codex, exiled for reading aloud pages your office held sealed. I have read your marginalia." He set down the platter. "It is better scholarship than anything your successors produced. You were right about the second ledger entry. I'm sorry you had to be."

Sef went very still. "You've read my—"

"Everything written within a day's walk of the mountain comes to me eventually. Words fall upward here. Did you wonder why?" He smiled, and knew it was the wrong smile, the courteous mask, and could not find another. "Eat, please. It's venison. I shot it myself, which I mention only because I want at least one thing at this table to be simple."

Astrid hadn't touched the chair. They stood with their back to the wall — the same wall, Aldric noted, that held the old tapestry of the Halloran arms, a sword crossed by a quill, woven before either symbol meant anything anyone would fight over. Their sword-hand flexed open and shut. Looking for the speech. Reaching for the oath the way a tongue reaches for a tooth that has stopped aching only because it has stopped being there.

"It doesn't work down here," Aldric said gently. "You've noticed. Your vows go quiet below the third landing. That isn't the wards. That's proximity. You are standing very close to the thing that pays for them, and steel does not spend coin it can see."

"Then say what it is," Astrid said. Their voice was steady. Aldric admired that and hated that he admired it, because admiration had always been the first tool of his captivity — the court had taught him to love the people he sold. "Say it plainly. No hospitality. No venison. What did my family buy, and what did it cost?"

Aldric folded his hands. Around them the bone-light dimmed slightly, the way it always did when truth came into a room, as if the mountain itself leaned in to listen through its own veins.

"What your family bought," he said, "was a voice that the world must obey. Swordspeech. The oath-magic. Every vow you speak into steel becomes law for as long as the steel holds its shape. You know this better than anyone alive, since you are the last one who carries it."

"I know how it works," Astrid said. "I don't know why it works."

"No one has known why for three hundred years. The families kept the oaths and forgot the price. That forgetting was the second purchase — silence, folded into the first bargain like poison folded into meat." He drew a breath he did not need, purely because some habits of the living made truth easier. "The power runs on a soul. One soul, given wholly, held in trust beneath this mountain. It burns slowly — a soul is a very large sum, spent at vow-size — but it burns. And while it burns, whoever speaks the oath speaks with the weight of that soul behind every word."

The hall was silent. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, thunder walked across Thornfell's shoulders.

"Whose soul," Astrid said.

Aldric looked at them. Really looked — at the face he had watched grow in glimpses through scrying-bone and ledger-smoke for twenty-six years, the jaw that was his mother's jaw, the gray eyes that were his own. Love rose in him like floodwater against a door. He had four hundred years of practice holding that door, and it groaned anyway.

"The ledger lists the seller as Aldric Halloran," he said. "That much the Codex records truly. But the Codex is a book of outcomes, not mechanics. A sale requires a buyer, a seller, and goods." He opened his coat and drew out the ledger — the true ledger, black iron-bound, its clasp shaped like a closed mouth. He laid it on the table between the venison and the untouched bread. "The goods are listed here. Turn to the page yourself. I'd rather you read it than hear it. Hearing me say it will feel like accusation. Reading it will feel like fact, and facts are kinder."

Astrid didn't move. It was Sef who reached out — Sef, whose fingers trembled as they had trembled over the Vellum Codex in a smugglers' waystation days ago — and turned the iron clasps back, and opened to the page marked with a ribbon of faded red.

Aldric watched them read. He had imagined this moment ten thousand times across four centuries, and in none of those imaginings had the venison gone cold untouched, and in none of them had he felt this particular shame, which was new, or perhaps not new, merely finally arrived.

"The name is smudged," Sef said at last. "Both names. Yours is clear — *sold: one soul entire, in perpetuity, to the Court Beneath Thornfell, consideration received: the Oath of Speaking Steel, binding upon the line of Halloran until the goods are exhausted.* But—" Their finger stopped on the lower line. "There are two entries under *goods.* Two souls. The second is smudged. Deliberately smudged. This wasn't water damage. Someone rubbed the vellum while the ink was wet."

"Yes," Aldric said.

"Astrid." Sef's voice had gone thin. "Look at the date on the second line."

Astrid looked. Aldric watched them read it, watched the arithmetic happen behind their eyes — the date of the bargain, and then the second date beside it, twenty-six years past, the year of their birth.

"That's—" Astrid began, and stopped, and Aldric saw the exact moment the floor of their certainty gave way, because he had stood on that same floor when it had given way beneath him, four hundred years ago, in this same hall, holding the same ledger.

"The second payment was deferred," Aldric said quietly. "The Undercourt does not lend. It structures. My bargain purchased the oath outright, but the interest — the second soul — was scheduled against a birth. A child born to the Halloran line in whom the oath ran strongest. Born under the sign the Codex calls *the Split Flame.*"

"Me," Astrid said.

"You," Aldric agreed, "and not only you."

* * *

Later — after the plates had been cleared by servants who were not servants, after Sef had filled forty lines of a pocket notebook in a shorthand Aldric pretended not to read, after Ivo Tanzer had asked, with the blunt courage of the very young, whether they were prisoners — Aldric led them up to the gallery above the throne-cage.

He had debated this. He had argued with himself through the small hours the way he argued with himself every night, and lost, the way he lost every night. Secrecy served the court. Truth served Astrid. And he had spent so long serving the court that the chance to serve something else had become a hunger in its own right.

From the gallery, the cage could be seen whole.

It filled the vast chamber below like a chandelier fills a cathedral — if the chandelier were the size of a hill and made of light. Ten thousand points of radiance, each one a bound voice, suspended in a lattice of iron and oath-work, turning slowly, the way stars turn, the way prisoners pace. The sound of them reached the gallery as a hum just below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the wet architecture of the ear. Ten thousand souls murmuring at the edge of audibility. Aldric had listened to that murmur every night for four hundred years. Some nights it sounded like wind. Some nights it sounded like weeping. On the worst nights — and there had been so many worst nights — it sang.

"What am I looking at?" Ivo asked. His voice cracked on it. He was leaning over the rail despite himself, drawn the way moths are drawn, and Sef had a fistful of his collar hauling him back.

"The source," Aldric said. "The reserve. The court's treasury." He let the words sit. "When your usurper's collectors strip oaths from captured swordspeakers, when his alchemists bottle memory in glass vials for sale in the Market below us, where do you imagine the raw material comes from? Nothing is created down here. Everything is *held.* The cage holds. Kestrian wants what the cage holds — not a sip, not a trade, but the whole treasury, drunk at once. He believes it will make him immortal. He is wrong about what it will make him, but he is right that it can be taken, and he is coming to take it."

"How do you know he's coming?" Sef demanded.

"Because his agents are already on the stair." Aldric said it flatly, because there was no kind way. "Nine of them. Inquisitors in plain clothes, descending the far stair since dawn. They move quickly for men carrying an oath-cannon's focusing core in pieces on their backs. They will reach the outer gate by tomorrow nightfall. After them will come the man himself."

"And the doors?" Astrid asked. They had not moved from the rail. The cage-light moved on their face, gold and restless, and Aldric thought: *you look like her, you look like Elenna, you look like everyone I have ever failed.* "What happens with the doors?"

Aldric turned to face them fully. Below, ten thousand voices hummed their subterranean hymn. Above, the mountain ticked and settled around its own ancient bones.

"The doors are mine to open and close," he said. "That is what a warden is. And I am telling you now, as honestly as I have ever told anything: I am going to close them."

He raised a hand before the eruption — Astrid's hand going to a sword that would not answer, Sef's mouth opening around an archivist's objection, Ivo simply stepping forward like a boy squaring up to a bully.

"Hear the terms first. All of them. Then hate me, if hate is what remains, and it will be, and I will deserve it, and I will serve you dinner again tomorrow regardless, because that is also what a warden is."

Silence. Even the cage seemed to quiet, attentive.

"The bargain that fuels your oath cannot be revoked by force. It can only be revoked by its own clause — spoken backward, in full, by one who remembers the original vow perfectly. I remember it. I have recited it forward every night for four hundred years as penance, and I could recite it backward in my sleep, and I have tried, and the words will not take from my mouth. The revocation requires the *seller's blood-line.* It requires you, Astrid Halloran. And it requires a perfect memory of a moment that was sold." He paused. "There is more. There is the smudged name. There is a second person whose childhood lives inside yours like a room behind a wall. But that truth deserves its own hour, and I have stolen enough of your hours tonight."

"So the terms," Astrid said, and their voice was terrible and level. "Say the terms."

"The terms are these." Aldric counted them off, and his hands were steady now, because this part he had rehearsed for centuries. "One: you remain beneath Thornfell, guests of the court, protected. Kestrian cannot harvest what he cannot reach, and behind these doors nothing reaches. Two: your soul stays where it is, burning as it has burned, and your swordspeech continues above ground for as long as the sum lasts — decades yet, perhaps a lifetime. Three: the revocation clause dies unread, the cage stands, and ten thousand voices stay bound, and the world above keeps its magic and its wars and its way of solving things with steel." He met Astrid's eyes and did not look away, though it cost him more than the cannon ever could. "Or: you refuse my hospitality, and I lock the doors anyway — because locked doors protect you from yourselves as much as from him — and we discover together whether love, given four hundred years to practice, can survive becoming a prison."

"You'd keep us here," Ivo said, "to save us."

"I'd keep you here," Aldric said, "because I have watched one Halloran burn for that oath already, from the inside, for four centuries, and I will put my unliving body across any stair that leads another one into that fire." Something cracked in his voice, an old seam giving way. "You think I don't know what I am? I am the man who sold the first soul. I am the reason the cage turns. Every kindness I offer you is paid for out of the worst thing I ever did, and I offer it *anyway,* because the alternative is to stand aside and watch the bargain complete itself, and I have not the courage, and never did, and that lack of courage is the only honest inheritance I ever gave this family."

The bone-light pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Astrid stepped away from the rail. For a moment Aldric thought — hoped, hopelessly, humanly — that they might reach for him. Instead they looked past him, up, toward the ceiling and the stone beyond it and the storm-wrapped peak of Thornfell miles above, and when they spoke it was not to him at all, or not only to him.

"Then here are *my* terms," Astrid said. "Open the doors. Give me the ledger. And tell me the smudged name before Kestrian's dogs reach the gate — because I promise you, warden, whatever you're protecting me from, I'd rather meet it knowing who I am than die comfortable and half a person."

Below them, in the turning cage, ten thousand bound voices shifted at once — a sound like a tide changing its mind — and somewhere far down the Hollow Stair, faint even to Aldric's old ears, nine sets of boots kept walking, patient as debt, toward the gate he had already begun to lock.
