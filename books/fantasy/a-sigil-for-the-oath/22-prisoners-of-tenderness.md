## 22. Prisoners of Tenderness

The cell they gave Sef was beautiful, and that was the worst thing about it.

It was a room carved from pale stone the color of candlewax, and the walls were veined with light — thin threads of gold that pulsed slowly, like something breathing behind the rock. A bed waited, dressed in linen so fine it seemed spun rather than woven. A basin of clear water sat on a stand of black iron. On a small table: bread still warm, honey in a clay pot, a cup of wine dark as garnets.

No bars. No lock Sef could find. The door simply did not open when pulled, and closed without sound when pushed, and beyond it lay a corridor lit by veins of gold that bent, gently, insistently, back toward the court.

*Hospitality,* Aldric Halloran had called it, in that voice like a bell rung underwater. *You are guests of the house.*

Sef had spent thirty years among archives and had learned to read a room the way other people read a page. This room said: *you are kept.* The warmth of the bread said: *we want you comfortable.* The door that would not open said: *we do not need chains for people who have nowhere to be.*

They sat on the edge of the bed — the bed was soft as falling asleep — and pressed their ear against the wall.

And heard voices.

Not one voice. Not two. A murmur, layered, vast, like standing at the mouth of a cave and hearing the sea — except the sea does not say names. The sea does not weep in registers too high for human ears. The sea does not repeat itself, over and over, the way grief repeats itself, the way a prayer repeats itself when no one answers.

Sef pulled back as if the wall had burned them.

"Ten thousand," they whispered to the empty room. "He said ten thousand."

Aldric had not said it, actually. Aldric had said almost nothing since he'd locked the doors — since he'd smiled at Astrid with that terrible tenderness and told them they'd come home. But Sef had been the keeper of the Vellum Codex. Sef had read the forbidden pages aloud and been exiled for it, and if exile had taught them anything, it was this: when an archive goes quiet, it is not because there is nothing left to say. It is because someone is being prevented from saying it.

The mountain was full of archives. And every one of them was screaming.

* * *

They found each other by accident, or by design disguised as accident — Sef never learned which, and suspected Astrid didn't either.

Sef had spent what felt like hours pressing their palms to walls, following the pulse of the golden veins, mapping the cell block by its breathing. The veins converged somewhere below, toward a heat in the stone that had nothing to do with temperature. When Sef pushed open their door — it opened outward now, obligingly, which frightened them more than a lock would have — the corridor carried them, gently, the way water carries a leaf, down and around and down again, until they stepped into a gallery where Ivo Tanzer stood with his back against the far wall and his hand shaking near his belt knife.

"Sef." Relief and suspicion fought openly across his face. "Did you—"

"The door opened for me. Yours too?"

"It opened for both of us," said Astrid's voice, low, from the archway at the gallery's end. They came into the bone-light slowly, one hand braced on the wall, and Sef saw at once that something had happened to them. Their color was wrong. Not pale — *thinned*, as though someone had diluted them slightly, the way you dilute wine to make it last.

"Astrid." Sef crossed the gallery fast. "What did he do to you?"

"Nothing." A pause. "That's what frightens me. He fed me. He talked to me. He showed me—" Astrid's jaw worked. "He showed me a room where my grandmother's voice is kept. In a vial. He let me listen to her for as long as I wanted." Their eyes came up, and they were wet, and furious about being wet. "He knows exactly what hospitality means, Sef. He's had centuries to learn."

Ivo pushed off the wall. "Then we stop being guests. There has to be another way out. Every prison has—"

"This isn't a prison." Sef heard their own voice go flat and archival, the tone they used for cataloguing disasters. "Prisons hold bodies. Look around you. Look at the walls." They turned a slow circle, gesturing at the golden veins threading the gallery's stone, pulsing in slow unison. "This whole court runs on something. Wards don't maintain themselves. Doors don't decide who passes. Light doesn't grow through rock like this unless something is feeding it."

"Feeding it what?" Ivo asked.

Sef looked at Astrid. Astrid looked back, and Sef watched them arrive at the same answer at the same moment, and watched them hate it.

"The sold souls," Astrid said quietly. "The ones bought before mine. The ones bought after. The market upstairs trades in memories in glass — but the market has to get its stock from somewhere." They touched the wall, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking. "The veins are voices, Sef. Aren't they? That's what you heard."

"I heard a thousand murmurs through one wall." Sef's throat was tight. "If the veins run through every wall of this court—"

"Ten thousand," Astrid finished. "Maybe more."

For a long moment none of them spoke. Above them, faintly, through fathoms of stone, came the muffled sounds of the Market of Voices going about its trade — the chime of vials, the pleasant murmur of commerce conducted in other people's pasts.

"We can't fight this," Ivo said at last. It cost him something to say it; Sef could see the young delegate's idealism straining against the words like a dog against a leash. "An army couldn't fight this. If the walls themselves are made of captive souls, then breaking the walls—"

"—is killing them," Astrid said. "Or freeing them. And we don't know which until we try, and we only get to try once."

"No," Sef said.

Both of them looked over.

"No," Sef repeated, and the archivist in them rose up like a tide, thirty years of catalogues and marginalia and forbidden pages read aloud by candlelight all converging on a single certainty. "You're both thinking like soldiers, and this isn't a battlefield. It's a *ledger*. Everything here is recorded — Aldric keeps accounts, the market keeps prices, the Codex kept prophecies. Which means the throne — whatever sits at the center of this, whatever the veins converge on — it isn't just a power source. It's a *record*. Ten thousand souls don't get bound without documentation. Terms. Clauses. A sale is a text, and every text can be read, and everything that can be read—"

"—can be contested," Astrid breathed.

"In law, yes. Maybe in sorcery too. The Halloran oath runs on spoken vows — words with binding force. If the sale of your soul was spoken, Astrid, then it has a shape. A grammar. And grammars have errors." Sef gripped their own hands together to keep them steady. "We find the throne. We find the record. We read it. All of it. Before anyone tries to break anything."

"And how," Ivo said, "do three prisoners find the center of a mountain?"

Sef looked down at their feet. The golden vein beneath the flagstone pulsed once, twice — and then, unmistakably, leaned. Tilted its glow, subtle as a compass needle, toward the archway at the gallery's end. Toward the deep.

They hadn't noticed it before. Or perhaps it hadn't been pointing before. Or perhaps—

Perhaps the voices wanted to be found.

"They'll show us," Sef said, and hated how much the words sounded like hope, and hated more that they felt it anyway.

* * *

The veins led them down through corridors that grew older as they descended — the pale candlewax stone giving way to darker rock, the elegant carvings giving way to tool-marks, then to nothing worked by human hands at all. The air warmed. Somewhere below, water moved with the patience of centuries.

Sef walked last, and used the position to do what archivists do: observe, record, correlate. Astrid moved ahead with the unsteady grace of someone walking through a house that remembered them better than they remembered themselves. Ivo kept glancing back at Sef with the particular anxiety of a man who has staked his life on a scholar's hunch and wants reassurance on an hourly basis.

Sef gave him none. They were counting.

Every hundred paces, the veins in the walls thickened. Every few hundred more, the murmur in the stone grew a fraction louder — and clearer. By the time the corridor opened onto a ledge above a vast descending hollow, Sef could pick individual voices out of the chorus, the way you can pick single instruments from an orchestra heard through a wall. An old man repeating a child's name. A woman singing four notes of a lullaby and starting over. Someone — many someones — reciting what sounded like terms. Prices. Dates.

Sales.

"Oh," Sef said softly. "Oh, you poor things. You've been *reading yourselves* all this time."

"What?" Ivo whispered.

But Astrid had gone rigid at the ledge's edge, staring down into the hollow, and Sef followed their gaze and forgot Ivo entirely.

The hollow was a cathedral inverted — a cavern miles across, its ceiling lost in darkness, its floor lost in light. Because the floor *was* light. At the center of the cavern, sunk into the living rock like a jewel into a setting, stood a structure that Sef's mind refused for three full seconds to name, and then named anyway, and then wished it hadn't.

A throne. Vast, black iron and pale stone, wrought in the shape of interlocking ribs — a cage the size of a chapel, and within it, filling it, pressing against every bar and seam and joint:

Light. Ten thousand points of it. Moving.

They moved like fish seen from a bridge at night, like embers rising, like a city seen from very high up — streets and districts and neighborhoods of small bright souls, drifting, clustering, pressing against the cage's bars in slow tides. And each point of light, as it passed close to the bars, flared briefly, and Sef understood with sickening clarity that the flare was a voice reaching the surface of the binding, the way a drowning swimmer's hand breaks water.

The golden veins in the walls — the veins that lit every cell, every corridor, every gracious room of the court above — ran down the cavern walls like roots, and plunged into the cage.

The throne drank. The court glowed. The guests slept warm under fine linen, eating warm bread, in cells heated by ten thousand stolen lives.

"That's what he wants," Ivo said. His voice had gone thin. "Kestrian. The vault of blades wasn't enough. This is the source."

"He wouldn't drink it," Sef said. The archivist's cold clarity had returned, and Sef was grateful for it, because the alternative was weeping. "Look at the structure. The cage isn't built for consumption — it's built for *containment*. Binding requires stability. If Kestrian cracks it open to harvest, he doesn't get a feast. He gets a flood." They stared down at the drifting lights, at the small flares against the bars, hand after hand after hand breaking water. "He'd get ten thousand unbound voices in a collapsing mountain, and every vow ever sworn on stolen soul-stuff unraveling at once. Including—" they glanced sideways, carefully, "—including yours, Astrid. Whatever holds your oath together is downstream of that cage."

"Then breaking it unmakes me," Astrid said. Flat. Testing the sentence's weight.

"Or completes you. Or frees you. I don't know. The records will tell us, if we can reach them." Sef tore their eyes from the cage and scanned the cavern — the ledges, the stairways spiraling the walls, the small structures near the throne that might have been altars or desks or both. "There. Near the base. Those aren't decorative. Ledgers, or lecterns, or—"

The light changed.

It was subtle — a dimming at the edges of the cavern, a slight quickening in the veins' pulse, the way a house changes its breathing when someone wakes. Astrid felt it first; Sef saw it hit them, a flinch that traveled shoulder to spine.

"Someone's coming up the far stair," Astrid said.

"You can feel that?"

"I can feel the *doors*." Astrid's hand went to their sword hilt — an old habit, and Sef knew now it was worse than useless here, knew that the blade's oaths were downstream of the very cage they were looking at. "Three of them. Heavy boots. Iron. And something else—" Astrid's face twisted, listening to a frequency Sef couldn't hear. "Something bound. Something that sings."

Below, at the cavern's far side, a doorway opened in the rock — a vertical seam of torchlight spilling down a stair that Sef's maps had never shown, because no smuggler had ever drawn it, because no one who entered had ever left to draw it from outside.

Figures descended. Small at this distance, deliberate, unhurried. Men in the grey-and-scarlet of the usurper's inquisition, moving with the confidence of people who had been *expected*.

And ahead of them, on a lead of black chain, walked a figure whose throat gleamed with a silver collar, and even at this distance, even across the vast hollow, the figure was humming — and the hum slid down into Sef's bones and began, gently, patiently, to loosen things. Knots they hadn't known they'd tied. Promises they'd forgotten making.

Beside them, Astrid made a sound like a man taking a knife wound without flinching, and their sword-hand dropped away from the hilt as if the hilt had gone suddenly, unbearably cold.

"An echo-singer," Sef breathed. "Kestrian sent an oath-unraveler. He's not waiting for us to open the mountain." They grabbed Astrid's arm, pulled, already turning back toward the corridor, toward the gilded cells, toward anywhere but this ledge in full view of the descending torchlight. "He's coming to take the cage himself. And we're standing in his way."

Behind them, far below, the first of the inquisitors' boots rang on the stair — and ten thousand small lights pressed against the bars of the throne, all at once, all together, like a held breath.
