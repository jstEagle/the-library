## 23. Maren Descends

The Hollow Stair did not announce itself. That was the first lie of it.

Maren Duskwell had spent eleven years learning how power announced itself — the trumpets before a decree, the seal pressed into hot wax, the click of her own boots in a marble corridor timed so that lesser officials knew to look busy. Power, in Vaelmoor, was a language of entrances. But the entrance beneath Thornfell was a crack in a hillside behind a dead miner's cottage, half-hidden by bracken and a slab of stone that had been moved recently enough to leave fresh earth on its underside.

Someone had gone in ahead of them. The Halloran woman. The archivist. Possibly the council deserter, Tanzer, whose name now appeared on warrants in three cities with a bounty that would buy a vineyard.

Maren stood at the lip of it for a long moment, torch in one hand, sword at her hip, and let herself feel — cleanly, without apology — the thing she had trained out of most of her officers: reluctance.

"You don't have to do this personally," said Lieutenant Corvas behind her. He was young, ambitious, and had learned to read her silences the way monks read scripture. "We could send down scouts."

"Scouts come back wrong from places like this." She said it flatly, because it was true, and because saying true things aloud had become, over the past weeks, a private discipline she practiced like knife-drills — not because anyone required it, but because if she stopped she suspected she would forget which direction the blade pointed. "And I don't delegate what I'm not willing to carry myself. Form up. Torch spacing ten feet. Nobody speaks below the first landing unless I ask you to."

"Why not speak?" Corvas asked.

Maren looked into the dark of the stair, where her torchlight bent strangely — not downward, as light should, but inward, leaning toward some center the eye couldn't find. "Because I don't know yet what's listening."

She went down first. It was, after all, the only direction left.

* * *

The stair itself was a marvel her reports would never adequately describe, and Maren had built a career on describing things adequately. It spiraled down through the mountain's root like the inside of a shell, steps of black stone worn smooth by centuries of feet, and gravity here leaned toward the center of the descent rather than straight down, so that walking felt like wading through a river that flowed sideways. Words behaved worse. When Corvas muttered a curse two landings above her, it arrived at her ear from *below*, rising past her boots like a bubble through water, arriving faint and inverted, as though the mountain were repeating it back in a mocking whisper.

She had been given her orders in Kestrian's own hand, sealed with the double-crown sigil: *Retrieve the swordspeaker alive. Open the way to the source. Let nothing else leave.* The last clause she had read four times. *Let nothing else leave.*

Inquisitor-general was a title that came with a certain architecture of deniability. She had signed orders she didn't read. She had burned archives she hadn't inventoried. She had told herself, for eleven years, that the machine ran on her competence and not her complicity, and that these were different things.

Then, three nights ago, in an interrogation cell beneath the Vaelmoor garrison, a captured echo-singer had looked at her with eyes gone milky from years of binding and recited, in a voice that wasn't hers, the text of Kestrian's contingency order: the razing of Harrowgate. Of every free city. Not occupation. Not annexation. Ash, and then resettlement by loyalists, and then histories written by men who would call it mercy.

Maren had filed no report. She had walked out of the garrison, requested this assignment personally, taken six soldiers instead of sixty, and told herself she was doing it to control the outcome.

She was beginning to suspect there was another word for what she was doing, and that the word was *defection*, and that she would know for certain when the moment came and she either gave the order or didn't.

* * *

The voices started at the ninth landing.

At first she thought it was the acoustics — the strange upward-falling air carrying sounds from somewhere far off, a market perhaps, a settlement in the deep places. There were stories; she'd dismissed them. But the sounds weren't distant. They were *close*. They were all around. They were coming out of the walls.

Not screams. That was the horror of it, later, when she tried to explain to the tribunal — that it was not screams. It was conversation. A thousand conversations happening simultaneously in the stone around her, overlapping like rain: someone counting coins, someone singing a lullaby in a dialect she almost recognized, someone arguing about the price of rope, someone repeating a name over and over — *Tamsin, Tamsin, Tamsin* — in the patient tone of a person trying to hold onto something.

"Bound voices," said Sergeant Ilse quietly. She was the oldest of the six, gray at the temples, and had served under Maren long enough to be permitted observations. "The miners' tales. Sold by the pound, they say."

"Superstition," said Corvas automatically.

"Corvas." Maren kept her eyes on the wall, where her torchlight seemed to catch faint movements beneath the surface, like fish under ice. "Shut your mouth and listen."

He listened. Everyone listened. And in the listening, the mountain seemed to notice that it was being heard, because the voices grew — not louder exactly, but *nearer*, pressing toward the surface of the stone the way faces press against a window. A man laughing at a joke. A child reciting numbers. An old woman praying, in the old cadence, the pre-usurpation cadence that had been outlawed for a decade.

"They're people," Ilse said. Not a question.

Maren didn't answer. Because answering meant deciding what they were, and deciding what they were meant deciding what Kestrian was, and she had spent three nights standing at that particular door without opening it.

The door opened itself at the eleventh landing.

* * *

It came out of the wall the way warmth comes out of a hearth — gradual, then total. One moment the voices were ambient, a weather of sound. The next, one voice separated from all the others with the terrible clarity of a bell struck in an empty church, and it said:

"Maren?"

Her body knew the voice before her mind refused it. Her body turned. Eleven years of discipline, of controlled movement, of never showing surprise to any subordinate — her body threw all of it away in a single heartbeat and turned toward the wall like a girl turning toward a window.

"Danny?" she said. Then, hating herself for the diminutive even as she said it, because he had been twenty-two when he died and no one should be called Danny at twenty-two, dead or otherwise: "*Daniel.*"

"I can't see you." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the black stone and from inside her own skull, and it had the slightly flattened quality of a voice remembered rather than heard. "Is it dark? It's always — I don't know where I am. Mare, I don't know where I am."

Daniel Duskwell had died seven years ago of river-fever in a garrison hospital at Coldspring, and Maren had held his hand while it happened, and she had buried him in the officers' plot because their father's name still carried enough weight for that, and she had visited the grave every year on the feast of the First Frost until the visits became unbearable, which had taken four years.

That was the official account. That was the account she had given herself.

But there had been a second account, the one that lived in the locked drawer of her memory alongside the echo-singer's testimony and the razing order: Daniel had not fallen ill randomly. The fever wards at Coldspring had been full that season, and full disproportionately of soldiers whose families had debts, whose commissions had lapsed, whose paperwork showed irregularities. Collectors came through the hospitals in those years — quiet men in gray coats with ledger-books, and they paid death benefits to families in advance, oddly, before the deaths, and the families signed things they didn't read. Their mother had been drowning in their father's debts. Maren had been nineteen and already wearing the inquisitors' gray. Neither of them had read what their mother signed.

*Mare,* the voice in the wall said. *I'm cold. Why is it cold? I was — we were going to the ice fair. You promised you'd take me before I shipped out. You never took me.*

"We'll go," Maren said. Her voice came out steady, which astonished her, because inside her chest something was coming apart along seams she hadn't known existed. "Danny. Listen to me. What happened to you? Do you remember Coldspring?"

A pause. In the pause, the other voices dimmed, drawing back politely, a crowd giving room to a grief.

"Coldspring," Daniel repeated slowly, tasting it. "There was a man. Gray coat. He said — he said Mother sold me. He said it like it was a debt being settled. Mare, did Mother sell me?"

"No." It came out of her fast and useless. "No, she wouldn't—"

"He said you signed too." The voice was not accusatory. That was somehow worse. It was puzzled, gentle, a younger brother asking his older sister to explain arithmetic. "He had a paper. He said both names. Did I do something wrong?"

Maren's torch shook. Six soldiers stood behind her in the dark of a buried stairway, watching their commander's shoulders, hearing fragments, understanding more than she wanted them to. Later — much later, at the tribunal — Ilse would testify that in that moment General Duskwell stopped being the usurper's instrument and became something else, and that the transformation had taken less than a minute, and that she had watched it happen and done nothing to stop it because there was nothing to stop. You cannot arrest a woman for grieving. Grief is not mutiny.

"You did nothing wrong," Maren said. She stepped closer to the wall. Up close, she could see them — faint shapes in the stone, like figures sunk in dark glass, drifting. Hundreds. Thousands. Some near the surface, close enough to almost touch. Some far down in the black, tiny as stars. "Danny. I need you to tell me everything you remember. Everything. Can you do that?"

"There are so many of us," he whispered. "So many, Mare. We're stacked like cordwood. Some of them have been here longer than anyone's been alive. There's a woman down deep who says she remembers the mountain being *built*. There's—" His voice caught, frayed, reassembled. "They take pieces of us. Something comes through and drinks, regular as a tax collector, and when it's done you're missing — you're missing the middle of yourself. I've forgotten our mother's face, Mare. I've forgotten my own middle name."

*Duskwell,* she almost said. *Your middle name is Ambrose, after Father.* But she understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between ribs, that telling him would change nothing, because the forgetting wasn't failure of recall. It was removal. Someone had reached into her brother and taken things out, the way Kestrian's vaults held confiscated blades still humming with their oaths, the way the usurper's face stayed unlined year after year while his contemporaries silvered and stooped.

Appetite without end. She had seen the edges of it for years and called it vigor.

"Danny," she said. "I'm going to get you out."

"Mare—"

"I don't know how yet. But I'm going to find the woman who's supposed to fix this — the swordspeaker, the oath-breaker, whatever they call her — and everyone downstairs wants me to bring her to Kestrian, and I am telling you now, brother, in front of God and this mountain and six witnesses, that I would rather burn my commission than deliver one more soul into that man's hands. Including yours. *Especially* yours." She pressed her palm flat against the stone. It was warm. Warm as a living cheek. "Do you hear me?"

For a moment the whole stairway was silent — every bound voice stilled at once, as if the mountain itself were holding its breath around her words. Then Daniel laughed, and it was his real laugh, the one from before, and it cracked her open the rest of the way.

"You always did make promises like they were siege engines," he said. "Go on, then. Go break something. I'll keep. We're very good at keeping. It's the only thing we're good at anymore."

She took her hand off the stone. It left a print in the dust, five fingers, human-shaped, absurdly small against the dark.

* * *

They camped at the twelfth landing because the men needed sleep and Maren needed to write, and writing was how she thought. She sat apart with her journal — the plain one, not the coded report-book — and wrote by torchlight while Ilse kept watch.

*What follows is my testimony,* she wrote. *If anything happens to me below, get this to Harrowgate. To the council, not the garrison. Names, dates, orders in the usurper's hand. My brother Daniel Duskwell, soldier, 4th Foot, recorded dead of fever at Coldspring, Year of the Grey Heron — alive in the stone of Thornfell, bound and sold, sale executed by collectors operating under crown charter with signatures including M. Duskwell (age nineteen, coerced by omission) and E. Duskwell (mother). I did not know what I signed. This is not an excuse. There is no excuse at this scale.*

She stopped. Read it back. Added:

*I am no longer an officer of Vaelmoor. I don't know what I am. Possibly the same thing the swordspeaker is: a person with a debt the mountain intends to collect.*

Below her, faint through leagues of rock, the voices murmured on. Counting coins. Singing. Saying each other's names.

She closed the journal and slept, badly, with her sword unsheathed across her knees — not against enemies, but against the possibility that she'd dream of her brother again and wake having forgotten why.

* * *

Dawn didn't exist underground, but the mountain had its own hours. At what might have been morning, a tremor rolled up through the stair — soft, deep, rhythmic, like a giant shifting in sleep — and the bound voices all cried out at once, a single ragged chord of alarm, and then went silent in a way that was worse than noise.

Ilse was at her elbow instantly. "General?"

"It's not us," Maren said. She could feel it in her teeth. Something vast had struck the mountain from outside, high above, and the shock had traveled down through miles of bone-dark stone to reach the roots. Again — a second impact, closer. A third. Each one followed by that terrible collective cry from the walls, fading a little weaker each time.

"The army," Corvas breathed. "Kestrian's brought the army. Or—" His face changed as the arithmetic finished itself. "The cannon. The oath-cannon. They're firing it into the mountain."

Maren was already moving, taking the stairs down two at a time, torch streaming sparks behind her, six soldiers scrambling after. Because she understood now — had understood since Daniel's voice first touched her — what the cannon was for, what the *source* was, what Kestrian intended to harvest wholesale while the mountain's ten thousand prisoners screamed.

And somewhere below her, descending the same spiral from the other side, moving toward the same cage, was Astrid Halloran — the woman whose retrieval was the last order Maren ever intended to obey.

"Hold together," she whispered to the walls as she ran, to her brother, to all of them. "Hold together. I'm coming."

Deep in the stone, faint as a star through cloud, a voice answered her — not Daniel's, a stranger's, one of the thousands — and the word it said was the word she would carry the rest of her life, up out of the dark, into every courtroom and every testimony:

*"Hurry."*
