## 24. The Smudged Name

Aldric Halloran did not look like a monster. That was the worst of it.

He looked like a grandfather at a hearthside — silver-streaked hair tied back with a plain cord, a wool coat brushed clean of the mountain's dust, hands folded on the table between us as though he had all the patience that centuries could buy. The gilded cell they'd given us had been unlocked an hour after dawn by no clock I trusted; Aldric had simply appeared at the door and said, *Come. You've earned answers, and I've run out of ways to make you earn them.*

So we sat in a long chamber lit by veins of pale gold running through black stone — bone-light, the miner had called it, and now I knew why. The walls were not stone at all, or not only stone. They were something grown, something alive enough to glow, and it made my skin crawl to think of what fed it.

The ledger lay open between us.

I had seen it before, in Sef's drowned chapel, in copies and fragments. But this was the original — vellum gone the color of old teeth, corners soft as cloth from handling. Two columns of names ran down the page in Aldric's careful hand, and beside them, prices. Not coin. Prices in the only currency the Undercourt had ever accepted.

"You told me," I said, "that one soul was sold here. Mine. By your hand, or your bargain, or whatever you call the thing you did." My voice came out steadier than I felt. Wren — whoever Wren was, whatever stirred behind my ribs like a second heartbeat learning its own rhythm — had been restless since we passed the Market of Voices. Restless ever since the drowned chapel, if I was honest. Restless, maybe, since birth. "You told me the ledger listed two."

"I did." Aldric's eyes were gray-green, my grandmother's eyes, my eyes. He didn't flinch. "And you have spent every waking hour since pretending you didn't hear me say it."

Sef sat to my left, quill already out out of pure habit, though there was nothing here to copy that wasn't already burned into us. Ivo stood against the far wall with his arms crossed, the way men stand when they want to be near a door. Nobody touched the ledger.

"Say it plainly," I said. "No riddles. No hospitality. You've locked us in a mountain, Aldric. You don't get to be gentle anymore."

Something moved behind his face — hurt, maybe, or the memory of hurt worn smooth by time into something that only resembled it. He turned the ledger toward me with two fingers, rotating it slowly, the way you'd turn a mirror to face someone who dreaded their reflection.

"The smudge," he said.

I looked. The second name, the one beneath mine, was exactly what Sef had described: a smear of ink where letters should be, as if someone had dragged a thumb across wet script before it dried. Except this ink was centuries dry, and the smear was too deliberate, too contained. It wasn't damage. It was erasure with good manners.

"When the Undercourt takes a soul," Aldric said, "it takes the whole of it. Every memory. Every year unlived. Every name it might have carried." He tapped the edge of the page, just beside the smudge, where faint impressions remained — the ghost of pressure from a pen long lifted. "But souls are not coins, Astrid. They cannot be minted twice. And the oath I bought required more than one life's worth of fuel. It required a life that had never been allowed to begin."

The bone-light pulsed once, slow, like something breathing in its sleep.

Behind my ribs, that second heartbeat stumbled.

"What are you saying?" Ivo asked from the wall. His voice was rough. Good. Someone should sound rough.

Aldric looked at me, and only at me, and said: "Your mother bore twins."

* * *

The chamber did not spin. I want to record that honestly, because everything else in me did. The walls held still; the ledger held still; Aldric's terrible tender face held still. Only the ground tilted somewhere inside my skull, and I gripped the table's edge and found the wood was warm, warm as skin, and hated it.

"A boy and a girl," Aldric went on, because monsters are thorough. "Born in a snowstorm in the high passes, three hours apart. The first child screamed loud enough to crack ice. Healthy. Loud. A fighter from the first breath." His mouth curved, and I realized with distant horror that the expression was pride. "That was you, Astrid."

"And the second," Sef said quietly. Not a question. Sef had read enough forbidden pages to know how these sentences ended.

"The second was quiet. Watchful. Opened her eyes before she cried — the midwife said she'd never seen it." Aldric's fingers traced the air above the smudged line without touching it. "The oath demanded a soul sold *whole*. Not a dying man's leavings. Not a criminal's forfeit. A complete, unspent life. And there is only one moment in any person's existence when a life is entirely unspent." He paused. "Birth."

"No," I said. It came out flat, automatic, the word itself a door slammed shut. "My mother — I remember my mother telling—"

"You remember *being told* you were born alone," Aldric corrected, gently, horribly. "You remember the story because it was given to you, polished, rehearsed. What you do not remember—" and here he leaned forward, and the light moved in his eyes like water over graves, "—is anything from the first years of your life that belonged to *her*. Your sister's first word. Her favorite song. The way your mother hummed it to two cradles instead of one. Those memories were priced into the sale, Astrid. The Undercourt does not take only what is given. It takes what must go so the remainder doesn't grieve itself apart."

"My sister," I repeated. The words tasted foreign. Borrowed. As if I'd picked them up off a stranger's table. "You're saying I had a sister."

"I'm saying you had a twin," Aldric said. "And that when the bargain closed, she did not die. Nothing down here dies, properly speaking." He gestured — a small, encompassing motion that took in the glowing walls, the deep dark beyond them, the throne we hadn't yet seen but all of us could feel, a pressure at the edge of thought like a storm on the horizon of the mind. "She was *folded in*. A soul needs a vessel, even a purchased one, even a partial one. The court is efficient. It wastes nothing."

Sef's quill had stopped moving. I heard it stop — the small scratch of note-taking simply ceasing, the way a heart stops in stories, all at once rather than fading.

"It wastes nothing," Sef echoed. "So it put her—"

"In the vessel closest to hand." Aldric met my eyes. "In you."

* * *

Afterward I would not be able to say how long I sat there. Time in the Undercourt was a dishonest servant anyway; the bone-light didn't dim or brighten, and hunger arrived when it pleased and left when it forgot. I know that at some point Ivo crossed the room and put a hand on my shoulder, heavy and human and slightly too tight, the grip of a man holding someone to the world.

I know that I asked questions. I remember fragments of my own voice, thin and strange:

*"Where is she now?"*

Here, Aldric had said, tapping my sternum through my shirt, once, lightly, like knocking.

*"Is she awake?"*

She has never slept, Aldric had said. She has only ever been unheard.

*"Did my mother know?"*

Your mother signed nothing, Aldric had said, and for one flickering instant his composure cracked and I saw underneath it: a hundred and sixty years of carrying this. Your mother was told her second child was stillborn. She held a wrapped bundle with weight enough to be believed. The court is thorough, and I was thorough on its behalf, and I will answer for it until the mountain finishes with me. But she never knew. Grief was the price she paid instead, and grief was not in my ledger, which means it was the one honest thing in that room.

I remember that last part most clearly, and I hated him for it, because it was the first thing he'd said that I believed without effort, and belief felt like betrayal of everyone the lie had touched.

Now the silence stretched, and I broke it, because someone had to, and apparently that someone was going to be me forever.

"Wren," I said.

Aldric blinked. "What?"

"The name." My mouth knew it before I did. That should have frightened me more than it did — the way the syllables arrived fully formed, worn smooth, like stones from a riverbed I'd never walked. "Her name was Wren. You never said it. None of the records say it. So either you're about to tell me, or—"

"Or it was hers," Sef whispered. "Before the sale. Before the erasure. Names survive in the blood when they can't survive anywhere else." Sef's hands were trembling around the quill. Three years hunting me across half the continent, and here, finally, at the bottom of the truth, the archivist looked less like a scholar than a witness to something sacrilegious. "Astrid. When did you last dream of a bird?"

I opened my mouth to say *never*, and stopped.

Because there it was. Not a memory — a groove where a memory should sit, the negative space Aldric had promised. A small brown bird, plain and quick, perched on a windowsill in a house I couldn't picture, singing a tune I couldn't hear, while snow fell past the glass in a storm I *could* — the storm of my birth, the one true anchor of my earliest self. The wren had been there. In the groove. Waiting.

"She named herself," I said. My voice sounded like it came up from somewhere below the floor. "Inside me. All this time."

"Not all this time." Aldric's tone shifted — caution entering it, the wariness of a man handling a blade he'd sharpened himself. "A remnant. The Undercourt took her *whole*, Astrid, understand that — her future, her voice, her claim to a separate existence. What remains is residue. Echo. An imprint in the wax." His eyes searched mine, and for the first time since the gate, since *you came home*, I saw fear in my ancestor's face. Genuine, personal fear. "It should have stayed residue. But you've been speaking unsworn words. Words the oath was never built to carry. And each time you speak one, you reach down into the fuel that powers you — into *her* — and you stir it."

"Stir it," I repeated. "Like a fire."

"Like a sleeper," Aldric said, "who was buried alive, and has begun, after twenty-six years, to notice the weight of the earth."

* * *

They gave us the chamber after that — or Aldric withdrew, which amounted to the same thing; in this house the difference between permission and inevitability had worn away centuries ago. Sef spread notes on the table. Ivo paced. I sat very still and conducted an inventory.

That was the discipline my grandmother taught me, back before I understood what her lessons cost: *When the world breaks, Astrid, count what's left.* So I counted.

One: my hands. Mine. Scarred at the knuckles, sword-callused, steady enough. Two: my name. *Astrid* — spoken aloud, it rang true, rang like struck steel, rang like the oath itself. Whatever Wren was, I was still me. Probably. Three: my memories — and here the counting faltered, because how do you inventory a warehouse when you've just learned the shelves were rearranged by thieves? The winter festival where I'd gotten lost — had I gotten lost, or had *she*? The lullaby I sometimes caught myself humming in empty rooms, a tune I'd always assumed I'd stolen from some tavern singer — whose throat had it come from originally? The way I flinched at enclosed spaces and loved them equally, claustrophilia and claustrophobia wrestling in the same breast, which I'd always chalked up to a swordspeaker's contradictions—

Two people's worth of instincts, wearing one body like a borrowed coat.

"Astrid." Sef, carefully. "Talk to me."

"I'm trying to decide," I said, "which thoughts are mine. Right now. This one, for instance — the one where I want to burn this room down. Is that me? I've been angry since we got here, sure, but this is—" I flexed my fingers. Warmth gathered in them without being called, without a vow, without a price, and that warmth had a *flavor* now, and the flavor was not entirely unfamiliar. It tasted like standing at a window watching snow. "This wants things. It has a direction."

"That's the unsworn speech," Ivo said. "You've been pulling from deeper since the ice bridge. We knew that."

"We knew you were reaching past the oath," Sef said. Their voice had the careful flatness of a scholar reciting something they'd rather scream. "We did not know the well had a girl at the bottom of it."

The warmth in my hands guttered.

And then — I will write this plainly, because Sef will ask me later to be precise, and precision is the only mercy left in this place — the warmth answered.

Not in words. Not yet. But my right hand rose from the table without my leave, slow as a swimmer surfacing, and pressed itself flat against my own chest, over the heart, over the *other* heartbeat, and the pressure of it felt like greeting. Like a palm laid on a door from the outside. And from the inside, faint as a knock through a wall, faint as a wren's wing against a window in a snowstorm—

*tap*

Once.

Ivo went for his knife. Sef went white. I sat perfectly still and let my own hand rest on my chest and felt my heart slam against my palm like something trying to get out, or something trying to let in, and I could not tell the difference, and I suspected I never would again.

"Hello," I said aloud, to no one, to the room, to the groove in my memory shaped like a whole other life. My voice cracked in the middle of it, split into two registers for half a heartbeat — my low rasp and something lighter underneath, a harmony line I'd been singing solo without knowing it. "Wren."

*tap-tap*

Faster. Eager. Furious. The knock of someone who has waited twenty-six years behind a door and has just learned there is someone on the other side who says hello.

The bone-light in the walls flared — every vein of it, all at once, the whole chamber flushing gold like a held breath — and far above us, muffled through leagues of living rock, came a sound I had hoped not to hear for days yet: the groan of the far stair taking weight. Boots. Many boots. Kestrian's agents descending, drawn by the same flare of power that had answered me at the ice bridge, drawn perhaps by the flare answering me *now*.

Aldric appeared in the doorway, faster than a man his apparent age should move, and the tenderness on his face was gone, replaced by the warden's cold arithmetic.

"They're coming," he said. "And Astrid — whatever you just woke, wake it no further until we choose where it stands. Because I have read every page of that ledger, and I promise you this much:" his eyes dropped to my chest, to the place where two heartbeats argued, "the court wrote terms for one soul reclaimed. It wrote nothing at all for two."

Behind my ribs, patient and furious and finally, finally heard, Wren knocked again.
