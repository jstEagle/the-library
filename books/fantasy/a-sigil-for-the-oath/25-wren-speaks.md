## 25. Wren Speaks

The first thing I remember is warmth that was not mine.

That is how I would describe it, if I had words then — and I did not, not yet. There was a warmth like a hearth in a house I had never entered, a heartbeat that drummed somewhere above me and to the left, steady as a smith's hammer, and I lay beneath it the way water lies beneath ice. Listening. Waiting. Hating, in the small way that things hate before they have names.

Then Astrid opened the door.

* * *

It happened in the gilded cell, on the third night after Aldric locked the doors with his terrible tenderness. Sef had gone to the archives of the Undercourt — she said the court's librarians owed her debts, which meant she intended to threaten them — and Ivo was pacing the corridor outside, arguing with a guard who did not answer. Astrid sat alone on the bed with the black-iron key in one hand and their own reflection in a hand-mirror they'd taken from the washstand.

They were looking at their face the way you look at a stranger's face. Searching for someone else in it.

*I'm here,* I wanted to say. *You've been looking for me your whole life. You just didn't know what shape to look for.*

They set down the mirror. They spoke aloud, because speaking aloud made things true in this place where words fell upward — "Wren. If you're in there. If any of it is real."

And I rose through them like a diver surfacing.

I will tell you what it is like, because no one has told you, because no one has ever been me. It is like being handed a body you have only ever lived beside. Every nerve arrived at once — the itch of wool at the wrist, the sour taste of fear-sweat, the ache behind the eyes from three nights of bad sleep in a gilded cage. I had felt all of these things from underneath, secondhand, muffled. Now they were mine, sharp as new knives.

Astrid's mouth opened. My voice came out.

"I'm here," I said, and heard the difference immediately. Where Astrid's voice ran low and even, worn smooth like a river stone, mine came out quicker, clipped at the ends, a voice that had been rehearsing arguments for twenty-six years in a room with no one to argue with. "Gods below. Gods below, I'm *here.*"

Astrid tried to take the mouth back. I felt it — a pressure like a hand on a door, polite at first. Then less polite.

"Stop," they said, half in my voice, half in theirs, the words tearing down the middle. "Stop — who —"

"You know who." I let them have the tongue, mostly, because I wanted them to hear me clearly and clarity required cooperation. We shared the breath now; we would have to learn to share everything else. "Twenty-six years. That's how long I've been under. That's how long I've listened. Do you want the inventory? Because I have an inventory. I've had nothing but time and an inventory."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. Through our shared nerves I felt Astrid's pulse spike, felt their hands go cold. "Don't do this. Not like this."

"Not like what?" I laughed, and the laugh came out ugly, and I was glad. Let them feel it. Let them feel what twenty-six years of being *fuel* does to a laugh. "Not like the truth? Your grandmother taught you oaths, Astrid. Vows spoken into steel. Payment taken. Well — I am the payment. I am the price that got paid, and nobody asked me, and now you're sitting in the house that was built out of me, complaining that the walls are haunted."

"That wasn't me. It was before me. Aldric —"

"Aldric brokered it. Your grandmother swore it. But *you* spent me." I pushed forward into the hands, flexed our fingers — *my* fingers, ours, I still don't know whose — and held them up to the candlelight. "Every vow you spoke into steel. Every memory you paid. You thought the oath ate your memories. It didn't. It fed them to me. I remember your mother's face, Astrid. The one you lost at the ford. She used to sing off-key on purpose when she thought you were sad, because she'd learned that making you laugh worked better than comforting you. You don't remember that. *I do.* I have been keeping your memories warm while you burned them."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere beyond the door, Ivo had stopped pacing. Through the wood I could hear him breathing slow and deliberate, the way people breathe when they are pretending very hard not to listen.

When Astrid finally spoke again, it was quietly, in their own cadence, and there was something in it I had not expected: not anger. Shame.

"I'm sorry," they said.

And that — that undid something in me. I had rehearsed this conversation ten thousand times in the dark under their heartbeat. In none of my rehearsals had they apologized first.

* * *

We negotiated, that night, the way siblings negotiate over a house neither wants to leave.

Sef returned near dawn with ink-stained fingers and no useful information, took one look at us — at Astrid sitting rigid on the bed, talking to themselves in two voices — and did the most Sef thing possible: she sat down across from us, folded her hands, and said, "Right. Ground rules. One voice at a time. I refuse to archive a shouting match."

"She's taking notes," I said. "Look at her. She's already taking notes."

"I am establishing a record," Sef said primly, uncapping her pen, "because whatever this is, it is going in the Codex margins, and margins demand accuracy."

So we talked. Or rather, I talked and Astrid flinched, and gradually the flinching became listening, and the listening became something harder to name.

I told them about the dark. Not dramatically — Sef kept interrupting to ask clarifying questions, dates and durations, the archivist's reflex — but plainly. What it was like to exist as residue inside another person. To have longings with nowhere to attach. I wanted rain. I had never seen rain except through Astrid's eyes, and even that only in glimpses, moments the oath hadn't bothered to tax. I wanted to be afraid of something honestly instead of borrowing Astrid's fears secondhand. I wanted — and here I made myself say it, made myself look straight through our shared eyes at Sef and mean it —

"I want the soul back. All of it. Even if it unmakes both of us."

The room went very still. The candles bent toward the door, as if even the flames were leaning away.

Sef's pen stopped. "Unmakes," she repeated. "Explain the mechanics. Precisely."

"The ledger lists two souls under one bargain," I said. "One sold, one fused. Pull the sold soul back out of the court's keeping, and the fusion comes apart. There isn't enough of either of us left to survive alone as whole people. I've done the arithmetic in the dark for twenty-six years, archivist. I've had nothing else to do. The sum doesn't work. Somebody ends. Maybe everybody does."

"And you want that anyway."

"I want *out*," I said, and my voice — our voice — shook. "Do you understand what fuel is? Fuel doesn't get tired. Fuel doesn't get to stop. Fuel burns until the fire decides otherwise, and the fire has decided otherwise for twenty-six years, and every single day it burns I am the thing it burns. You all keep talking about reclaiming the soul like it's a treasure. Like Astrid takes it back and becomes whole and powerful and rides off into the unwritten future. Nobody has once asked what happens to the person who *is* the soul."

"I'm asking now," Astrid said.

It was quiet enough that I almost missed it. They had retreated deep inside, letting me hold the mouth, and their voice came from underneath mine like a voice through a floor.

"You're not a person who *is* the soul, Wren. You're my brother. Or my sister. I don't — I don't even know which, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry I don't know —"

"Neither," I said. "Both. I never got to find out. Another item for the inventory."

"Then let me fix it differently. There has to be a way to split it. Sef, the revocation clause — Aldric said —"

"Aldric said it requires perfect recall of the original vow," Sef said. "Spoken backward. And the original moment was sold. Which means—" She stopped. Her pen hovered. I watched her arrive at the conclusion the way you watch someone walk off a ledge they haven't noticed yet.

"It means Wren might be the only one who remembers it," she finished slowly. "Memories sold to the court don't vanish. They're *kept*. Kept things can be found. If any remnant of the original vow survives anywhere in this mountain — in the Market, in the ledgers, in the throne itself — it would surface through the twin. Through the fused soul. Through—"

"Me," I said.

"Through you."

Everyone looked at our body then. At me, looking out of it. And I want to be honest, because honesty is the only inheritance I was ever given: for one moment, I considered lying. I considered saying *there's nothing in me, no remnant, forget it, break the cage, burn the bargain, let the ending come.* Because if the remnant existed, then maybe there was a road where we both lived, and hope is a cruel thing to hand someone who has spent twenty-six years learning not to hope.

But I had also spent twenty-six years watching Astrid pay and pay and pay, and I knew — better than anyone alive — exactly what this family does with its children. It spends them. My whole existence was a receipt for that spending.

I would not become the next entry in the ledger.

"There's something," I admitted. "Deep down. Under everything. A weight. I always thought it was just — me. The shape of being squashed. But it might be the vow. It might be the original moment, pressed flat, waiting."

Sef wrote three lines and underlined one. Astrid breathed. And Ivo, who had given up pretending, pushed open the door and stood there with his delegate's chin up and his eyes wet, and said:

"Then we go find it. Both of you. Whatever's down here that keeps pieces of people — we go get yours back."

* * *

We went down, deeper than the cells, deeper than the drowned chapel, into a corridor where the walls were veined with candlelight through bone and every word spoken aloud drifted up past our heads like bubbles rising through dark water. Aldric found us there. Of course he did. He was the warden; the mountain was his body in every way but the flesh.

He looked at Astrid — at us — with that terrible tenderness that made my borrowed skin crawl.

"You've let it speak," he said softly.

"*Let it,*" I repeated, and stepped forward into the front of our shared body, and watched him flinch. Good. Let the warden flinch. "Interesting choice of word, grandfather. As though I were weather. As though I were a draft under a door. I've been in this family's cellar for twenty-six years, Aldric Halloran, and every one of you has used the same word. *It.* The oath uses it. The ledger uses it. Kestrian will use it, when he arrives with his cannon. And I am done being *it.*"

"Wren." His voice caught on the name like a nail on cloth. He had said it before, in the reveal — but saying it as address, saying it to a face, was different, and I watched a century of warden's composure crack along the same hairline Maren Duskwell's loyalty had cracked, along the hairline everyone in this story cracks eventually. "I named you. Before the bargain. Did Astrid tell you that? I held you for eleven minutes. Eleven minutes, and in those minutes you were the only thing in the world I had not yet sold."

"Then you'll help us," I said. "Because there's a remnant of the original vow buried in this mountain, and you know where the court keeps its bought things. Take us to it. Open every door you locked."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I tell Astrid what I've heard through your wards," I said, and here was the card I had been holding since the night I surfaced, the card I had hated holding, because using it felt like becoming the family that made me. "Every night, the bound voices sing to keep the cage sealed. Ten thousand voices. And lately some of them have started singing *your* name. Asking why the warden's doors only lock from the outside. I've had twenty-six years of listening, Aldric. I hear everything the mountain hears. Shall I recite?"

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the tenderness was still there — but beneath it, for the first time, I saw the other thing. The guilt, old as the bone-lit veins, worn down smooth as a stair by centuries of climbing.

"No need," Aldric Halloran said. "Follow me. And child—" he paused, and did not look back, and his voice was quieter than the mountain, "—when you find the vow, and speak it backward, whatever happens to you after — know that eleven minutes was the truest thing I ever owned."

* * *

Behind Aldric's shoulder, in the dark ahead, I heard it.

Faint. Rhythmic. Rising up the Hollow Stair like a heartbeat that did not belong to any of us.

Boots. Many boots. And beneath them, a sound I had only known from Astrid's nightmares: the groan of oath-steel being loaded into a cannon's throat.

Kestrian was coming down.
