## 26. Kestrian Arrives

The Hollow Stair had a smell, and Kestrian Vael hated that it was familiar.

Cold stone and old tallow, the way his mother's cellar had smelled in the famine years, when he'd been small enough to hide among the salted hams and listen to the men above argue about which children to feed first. He had been eight. He had been hungry for eleven years after that. Every hunger since had been a smaller copy of that one, and every hunger since he had fed.

Now the stair leaned him gently inward, gravity tilting toward the mountain's heart like an usher with a hand at his back, and his hunger rose to meet what waited below the way a dog rises when it scents meat through a door.

Behind him, two hundred inquisitors descended in column, their boots ringing on steps cut by no human hand, each man and woman bound by oath-lanterns whose light did not flicker even when they stumbled. Before him rolled the cannon.

They called it the Tithe. Maren Duskwell had named it — no, that wasn't right. Maren had refused to name it, and so the soldiers had named it themselves, the way soldiers always named the things that frightened them. The Tithe: a breech of black iron forty feet long, mounted on a cradle of chains, loaded not with shot but with four hundred confiscated oath-blades packed hilt-first into its throat like teeth. Each blade still held the residue of the vow that had made it. Four hundred promises, compressed. When the cannon fired, it would speak them all at once.

Kestrian had dreamed about the sound it would make. In the dream it sounded like his own name.

"Your Grace." Captain Ferren of the vanguard, breath fogging though the air was warm as a mouth. "Scouts report the stair opens onto a gallery. There's — light ahead. Not torchlight."

"I know what kind of light it is," Kestrian said.

He did. He had tasted it secondhand for twenty years, sipping from the vault of blades beneath Vaelmoor, drinking the dregs of other people's vows the way a drunk drinks the rinse-water from cups. It had kept him young past sixty, past seventy, while his hair stayed black and his hands stayed smooth and courtiers whispered about alchemy and devilry and were careful to whisper where he couldn't hear. But dregs were dregs. Lately the vault had begun to taste thin, like wine cut three times with water, and his reflection had started showing him things — a gray strand here, a looseness at the jaw there — that he corrected each morning with a draught distilled from a traitor's sword.

The vault was a cistern. Beneath Thornfell was the spring.

*Ten thousand voices*, the miners' rumors said. *Sold by the pound.* He had paid good gold to have those rumors verified and better gold to have the verifiers silenced, and now he walked down toward them with his hunger walking ahead of him like a favored hound.

The gallery opened without warning, the stair spilling them onto a ledge above a cavern so vast the lantern-light simply gave up trying to fill it. And below —

Below, the Market of Voices burned before he had given any order to burn it.

* * *

It had been burning for perhaps a minute. That was the first thing he understood: the fire was already there, already climbing the stalls of glass and bone, and it had not come from him. The second thing he understood, as he stood at the ledge's rail with two hundred soldiers pressing at his back, was that the fire was not entirely unwelcome to him either, because burning light threw more light than steady light, and he wanted to see everything.

The Market spread across the cavern floor in terraces, stall upon stall upon stair, a bazaar built inside a throat. Thousands of glass vials hung from wire and sinew in curtains, each one holding a curl of pale luminescence — a memory, a voice, a year of somebody's childhood — and the firelight moved through all those thousands of vessels at once until the whole cavern shimmered like the inside of a struck bell.

Merchants ran. Some of them weren't shaped right to be called merchants — tall, jointed things in robes, faces like unfinished sculpture — but they ran all the same, clutching armfuls of inventory, screaming in voices that came out doubled, a person's voice and something older speaking together through one throat.

And threaded through the panic, rising out of the burning stalls, out of the cracked vials, out of the very stone: singing. Or weeping. Or both. Freed memories escaping their glass and dispersing into the air like perfume, and every soldier on the ledge had gone still, and Kestrian saw more than one of them mouth along to words they shouldn't have known — a lullaby, a name, a prayer from a childhood none of them had spent in this mountain.

"Hold formation," Ferren said hoarsely, to no effect whatsoever.

Kestrian barely heard her. He had closed his eyes and opened himself the way he always did — the practiced, greedy opening, the cistern-mouth — and what poured into him nearly took him off his feet.

Not dregs. Not residue. *Live* voice, raw from the source, ten thousand years of bargained memory loose in the air because some fool's fire had broken the seals. He drank standing up, eyes shut, hands white-knuckled on the rail, and felt the gray strand at his temple darken, felt the looseness at his jaw pull tight, felt thirty years fall away between one heartbeat and the next. His knees buckled. Two inquisitors caught his elbows and he shook them off without opening his eyes.

More. He wanted more. The wanting was the whole of him now; it had been the whole of him since he was eight years old in a cellar listening to men decide whether he would eat, and he had long ago stopped pretending the wanting was anything other than what it was.

"Your Grace." A new voice, and this one he knew, and knowing it annoyed him. Inquisitor-General Maren Duskwell climbed the last steps from the rear of the column, armor scorched, one gauntlet missing, her face wearing the expression she wore instead of disagreement. "The fire didn't start with us. Someone's ahead of us in the Market. And the wards —"

"I can see the wards, General."

He could. They were beautiful, or had been: a lattice of pale script hanging in the air above the far end of the cavern, ringing a structure of black iron that even at this distance made his teeth ache. The throne-cage. The lattice was failing — he watched a whole arc of it gutter and unravel as he looked, letters falling upward, because words fell upward down here, someone had told him that once and he'd forgotten who. Through the widening gaps in the script, the cage showed plainly: a tower of interlocked bars, and inside it, pressed against every bar, lights. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Packed like fish in a net, pressing outward, and every one of them a soul.

His spring. His cellar full of hams. His.

"The warden's weakening," Maren said. "Whoever set the fire is drawing his strength somewhere else. If the wards fail before we reach the cage —"

"They won't fail before I reach the cage, because we're going now, at the double, and anyone who loots so much as a vial will hang from the stair with his lungs nailed open." He turned from the rail. "Bring the Tithe. If the warden objects to my arrival, the cannon will explain the terms."

Maren hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Long enough that Kestrian marked it, filed it, added it to the small ledger of her hesitations that had been growing longer all winter. Then she saluted and turned to bark the order, and the column began to move down the switchback ramps toward the burning Market, and Kestrian followed his hunger into the smoke.

* * *

The Market burned beautifully and fought back.

That was the thing the reports had never captured — he'd read every interrogation, every miner's confession, every smuggler's testimony extracted at cost, and none of them had said the Market *fought*. But the fire had loosened things, and loose things in this place had edges. As the column descended into the terraces, vials burst overhead in volleys of released memory, and the memories did not disperse politely. A decade of a drowned fisherman's storms hit the front rank like weather. A bride's whole wedding came apart over the inquisitors in a rain of golden light, and men who walked through it came out the other side staggering, weeping, calling names of women they'd never married.

"Screens!" Ferren roared. "Oath-lanterns forward! Burn a corridor!"

The lanterns' binding-light cut channels through the chaos, and the column pushed along them, boots crunching glass. Kestrian walked in the center of the formation because he was the usurper and usurpers walk in the center, and also because the center was where the released voice ran richest, and he drank as he marched, greedily, openly, feeling himself swell with it. Around him soldiers sagged under memories not theirs. He alone grew stronger. That had always seemed correct to him. That had always seemed like the natural order of the world: everyone pays, someone eats.

A merchant-thing barred their path at the third terrace — one of the tall ones, robes smoking, and in its arms a crate of vials it would not drop. Its sculpted face turned toward the column and it spoke, and its voice was a market-cry and a funeral dirge at once:

*"Everything here is priced, kingling. You cannot afford this street."*

Kestrian smiled at it with the smile that had ended careers. "I'm not buying."

He drew the sword at his hip — not an oath-blade, nothing so honest, but a weapon forged in Vaelmoor from melted-down confessions, steel quenched in ink — and he cut the creature down where it stood, and the crate shattered, and forty years of somebody's harvest festival burst over the column like a sunburst, and through the golden light the inquisitors surged forward shouting, and the street was theirs.

But in the moment the creature died, Kestrian felt something slide into the hole it left — a glance, a pressure, attention turning toward him from the far end of the cavern like a searchlight. From the cage. From the throne behind the failing wards.

And with the attention came a voice. Not aloud. Down here nothing needed to be aloud.

*"You are not the buyer either, Kestrian Vael. You are the debt."*

He knew that voice. Twenty years of dreams had taught it to him syllable by syllable, though he had never heard it waking: Aldric Halloran, the warden, the ancestor, the man who had brokered the bargain that built the Halloran oath and then vanished from history into the mountain's keeping. Kestrian had sent collectors after Halloran's descendants for fifteen years. One swordspeaker remained. Somewhere below or above, Astrid Halloran was descending these same depths — his scouts confirmed it, the council hunters confirmed it, the prophecy itself confirmed it, that cursed page in that cursed book — and the prophecy wanted Halloran at this cage tonight.

Well. So did he. Let fate and ambition arrive together; only one of them would leave carrying anything.

"General." He did not raise his voice. He never needed to anymore; the vault had seen to that, and now the Market did too, and his voice carried through the burning terraces like a blade through silk. "The wards fail within the hour. Put your best company on the cage approach. Nothing reaches that iron before I do. Nothing."

"And Halloran?" Maren asked. Her face was smoke-streaked. Behind her, down a side gallery, he glimpsed her pause — just a pause — near a shattered stall where small vials lay scattered, and her gaze caught on one of them, and her hand half rose toward it, and then did not. Another entry for the ledger. "If Halloran reaches the cage first —"

"Then the cannon speaks." He laid his palm, almost fondly, on the Tithe's black breech as the crew wrestled it around the final terrace. Four hundred blades slept in its throat. Four hundred broken oaths, hungry for company. "I did not haul four hundred promises down a mountain to negotiate."

* * *

The wards broke while he watched, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and he had seen cities kneel.

The lattice of pale script had been fraying for an hour, arcs of it dying letter by letter, and now — with a sound like a harp strung with nerves being tuned past breaking — the last ring of Aldric Halloran's ancient protection collapsed inward and fell as light-snow across the cavern floor. The throne-cage stood naked in the dark at the head of a short stair, black iron gleaming, ten thousand souls pressed to its bars, and the sound they made when the wards died was not a roar. It was worse. It was a sigh. Ten thousand voices exhaling at once, hope and terror in a single note, and the note went through the column of inquisitors like a spear and left half of them on their knees.

Kestrian did not kneel. Kestrian walked up the stair.

Up close the cage was a cathedral of bars, taller than Vaelmoor's keep, every bar engraved with a name — he recognized the engraving style instantly, the same hand that had written the Vellum Codex, the same patient mad hand — and behind the bars the lights churned and pressed, and among them, faint but unmistakable, he heard individual voices. A woman counting. A child reciting a lesson. An old man singing a song about the sea that Kestrian's own grandfather had sung, in a kitchen that had burned fifty years ago, and the recognition hit him like wine and he laughed out loud, alone at the bars of the cage while his army watched from below.

All of it. All of it was real, and all of it was here, and it was going to be his.

"Emplacement!" he called down. "Train the Tithe on the cage! Crew to your posts!"

Below, the great gun swung, chain-groaning, its muzzle rising toward the throne like a head lifting to drink.

*"No."*

The voice filled the cavern now — Aldric Halloran, everywhere, in the stone and the smoke and the marrow. *"You may breach my doors, thief. You may burn my market. But that cage is sealed by terms older than your line, and the blood-price of breaking it is written, and you—"*

"Yes, yes." Kestrian rested both hands on the cannon's breech, feeling the four hundred oaths shiver against his palms like hounds scenting the hunt. "The terms. The price. Everything here is priced — your merchant told me. Here is my counteroffer, warden: I am the crown of Vaelmoor. I own every oath ever sworn to me, and I have collected them for twenty years, and I have never once paid for anything, and I am still alive. Fire when ready."

He stepped back. The crew lit the fuse.

And in the instant before the spark reached the charge — in the last clean half-second the mountain would know for a long time — three things happened at once, and he saw all three, because greed has excellent eyesight.

The Tithe fired, and the sound was his name after all, exactly as he had dreamed, four hundred broken vows screaming down the barrel in one voice, and the blast struck the throne-cage and the mountain rang like a bell struck by God.

Far across the burning Market, on a high gallery, a figure with a sword turned at the sound — a swordspeaker's silhouette against the fire, coming fast, with companions, with witnesses, exactly as the page in the Codex had promised.

And at the cage's base, where the blast had cracked the first bar, a single light slipped free — one soul escaping through the gap — and it rose, and it did not flee. It hovered in the smoke between Kestrian and his cannon, and it looked at him, and he knew it, and he had buried it in an unmarked grave outside Vaelmoor nineteen years ago, and it said, in his mother's voice:

*"Hungry still?"*

Then the second shell loaded, the Market roared, and the mountain began, at last, to break.
