## 27. Aldric's Choice

The cannon arrived before its master did.

Aldric felt it coming the way a house feels weather — through his bones, through the walls he had not built but had come to inhabit, through the long slow pulse of the court beneath Thornfell that had been his heartbeat for three hundred years. Something was descending the far stair with iron in it. Iron and oath-steel, melted down and married into one body, and every blade in that marriage screamed as it went.

He stood at the high window of his study — a room he had furnished over centuries with things stolen gently from the world above: a chair of ashwood, a lamp that burned whale-oil when there were no whales left within a hundred miles, a map of coastlines that no longer existed — and he watched the dark of the Hollow Stair, where gravity leaned inward and words fell upward, and he waited for Kestrian.

He had known this would come. He wanted that written somewhere, even if only in the ledger of himself: *he knew*. Not the hour, not the shape of it, but the fact. A warden who guards a treasure learns to measure the greed of men the way a sailor measures swell. And Kestrian's greed had been swelling for years now, visible from below, a tide that would eventually break against these doors.

The doors he had locked behind Astrid Halloran two nights past.

Aldric turned from the window and looked at the ring of keys on his belt. Black iron, all of them, cast from the same pour as the key the dying miner's grandfather had carried up the mountain and hidden in the world above like a seed. One of those keys opened the gilded cells where the last of his line slept, or pretended to sleep, or lay awake listening to the mountain breathe.

One of them opened everything.

*You came home.* That was what he had said to Astrid, standing at the gate with three hundred years of tenderness curdled into something between love and ownership. He had meant it. That was the obscenity of it — he had meant every word. He had watched from the deep places when Astrid swore the Halloran oath over their grandmother's body; he had felt the new vow settle into the old bargain like a stone dropped into a well that had been waiting for stones. He had read the Codex's page for tonight a hundred years before the ink was dry, because he kept the original and Sef's book was only an echo, and echoes run late.

And he had locked the doors anyway.

Because the alternative was the end of him. Because a warden who unlocks the doors is a warden no longer, and Aldric Halloran had been nothing else for so long that the name had worn smooth as a river stone, all its edges gone. He had brokered the sale. He had held the pen while his own hand shook — no. He corrected himself, standing alone in the lamplight, as he had corrected himself ten thousand times across ten thousand identical nights. His hand had not shaken. That was the lie he told to make the nights bearable. His hand had been steady. Steady as a surgeon's. He had written the terms in blood-price because blood-price was binding, and he had chosen each clause with care, and he had taken his commission: the wardenship, the unaging, the right to stand at this door forever while everyone he might have loved was sold by the pound below.

He took the ledger from its shelf.

It was a plain thing, the ledger — oxblood leather gone black with handling, brass corners green with the mountain's breath. He had bound it himself. The first entry was in his own hand, three centuries old and still sharp:

*Bought: one soul, entire, from the line of Halloran. Paid: the Oath, whole and working, unto the said line until the debt be called.*

Beneath it, added later in ink that was not ink:

*Second soul purchased same hour, unnamed at seller's request. Price: silence. Term: fuel.*

Wren. The name that wasn't in the book. The child erased at birth so the elder twin could burn bright enough to carry the oath. Aldric had told himself for generations that the second purchase was mercy — better the remnant fed the flame than wandered nameless — and he had almost believed it, the way you can almost believe anything if you repeat it across enough empty years.

Then Astrid had walked through his gate with Wren already stirring inside them like a second heartbeat learning to keep time, and Aldric had understood that nothing he had ever told himself about mercy had ever been true.

* * *

The screaming of the cannon's components grew louder.

Aldric descended. The court received him the way it always did, corridors of bone-lit stone parting around his steps, the walls veined with candlelight that pulsed when the bound voices stirred. He passed through the Market, or what remained of it. Kestrian's vanguard had come down hours ahead of their master — a wedge of inquisitors in grey lacquer, methodical as locusts — and they had fired the Market's western arcade to drive the vial-sellers into the open. Glass crunched under Aldric's boots. Memory ran in the gutters, silver-thin, evaporating as he watched: a woman's laugh, someone's first snow, the smell of a kitchen in a house that stood above ground and above water both. Free memories, unowned, spilled and wasted, and the inquisitors moved among the stalls collecting only what had price-tags on it.

They saw him and made way. They had orders about the Warden — *do not touch, do not address, do not meet his eyes* — and they obeyed them with the particular rigor of soldiers who have heard what happened to colleagues who didn't.

At the far stair's mouth, they were assembling it.

The oath-cannon came down in nine pieces on nine litters, and the inquisitors fitted it together in the great vaulted chamber before the throne-cage with the competence of people who have rehearsed. It was a monstrous thing: a barrel of layered oath-steel, each stratum a different confiscation — blades taken from swordspeakers purged in Vaelmoor, in Harrowgate, in the salt towns — wound around a core of black glass that drank the light of the bone-veins and gave none back. It did not throw shot. It threw *extraction*. Aimed at the cage of ten thousand voices, it would tear the vows out of the bound souls wholesale, drink them into the core, and leave behind whatever a voice is when you take the vow out of it.

Aldric had examined the schematics once, years ago, delivered to him by a courier who thought he served the court. He had read them twice and then sat very still for a long time.

He knew what was left behind. He had seen it once, in miniature, in the early years — a debtor who defaulted, whose single purchased memory was reclaimed by force rather than lapse. What remained of the man afterward had lived another forty years, ate, worked, married, and was described by everyone who knew him as *a good neighbor*. No one could say what was missing. There was no one left inside to miss it.

Ten thousand voices. Wholesale.

"Lord Warden." The vanguard's captain came to him, helmet tucked under one arm, sweat cutting lines through the dust on her face. She was young. They were all young; that never changed either. "The Lord Protector descends within the hour. We're instructed the court is to be made ready for harvest."

"It has been ready for three hundred years," Aldric said. "That is what I am for."

She nodded, relieved to have the exchange over, and turned back to her work. Aldric stood a moment longer, watching them seat the core into its cradle. Then he climbed back up through the Market, past the fires, past the gutters running silver, and unlocked the first door.

* * *

Sef was awake. Of course Sef was awake. Archivists slept the way cats did — lightly, and offended at interruptions.

Aldric set the lamp inside the cell's threshold and stood in the corridor with the bars between them, and for a moment neither spoke. In the cot beyond, Ivo Tanzer breathed the deep uneven breath of genuine exhausted sleep, one arm flung over his eyes. Astrid's cell was dark further down the row; whether they slept, Aldric could not tell and did not let himself try.

"You," Sef said finally, "have terrible timing."

"I've had three hundred years of practice at terrible timing."

Sef rose, wrapped a blanket around their shoulders, and came to the bars. Up close their face was thinner than it had been on the road, the exile's hollows deeper, but the eyes were the same — the eyes of a person who had read a forbidden page aloud knowing exactly what it would cost, and had decided some things were worth more than keeping. Aldric had exiled them himself, in absentia, through intermediaries. *The keeper who reads aloud shall keep nothing.* He wondered sometimes whether Sef knew.

"The cannon," Sef said. "I heard the fitting. I hear everything; these cells are tuned. You designed the acoustics." Their voice stayed level. "What does it do?"

"It takes the vow out of a soul and leaves the rest." Aldric held Sef's gaze. "You're an archivist. Tell me what a voice is without its vow in it."

Sef's hands tightened on the bars. "Nothing you'd recognize. Nothing that would answer to a name." A pause. "Kestrian means to fire it at the cage."

"At the cage. Yes. Ten thousand souls in one blast, and the core drinks until it cracks, and my lord protector becomes something more than a man sustained — becomes a man *filled*. And the mountain comes down on whatever's left, which will include your cells, unless I move you first. Which brings me to why I'm here."

He drew the ring of keys. Sef watched them with the wariness of someone who had spent three years studying bargains and had learned to see the hook inside every gift.

"Why?" Sef asked simply. "You locked us in. You stood at your gate and called it home and hospitality and protection, and then you turned the lock, and I watched you do it. Why unlock us now?"

"Because I built this house," Aldric said, "and I have just finished understanding what it's for."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Aldric agreed. "It isn't. The answer is a confession four centuries long, and we don't have four centuries. Ask me again after, if after exists. Or don't. You'd be justified."

He put the black key in the lock and turned it. The mechanism had not been turned in — he calculated, and stopped calculating, because the number was obscene — in a very long time. The door swung wide on hinges that whispered.

"There's a stair behind the eastern archive wall," he said. "It climbs to the Salt Gallery and from there to the waystation roads. It predates the court; the court was built around it because whoever built this place wanted one road out that they controlled. I control it. Now you do." He pressed a second key into Sef's palm — small, cold, shaped like a fishhook. "Take the boy. Take Astrid. Go up."

Sef stared at the key and did not take it. "And you?"

"I have an appointment with my lord protector."

"Aldric." Sef used his name deliberately, the way you'd use a lever. "Whatever you're planning—"

"Is four hundred years overdue, and will go easier if the last of my line is a hundred miles above ground when it happens." He closed Sef's fingers over the key. "The Halloran oath runs on a bought soul. Did you find that in the margins? Then you also found the ledger lists two. When you get Astrid clear — when there's fire between them and this place — give them this."

He drew the final page of the ledger from inside his coat. He had cut it free that morning with a razor-sharp piece of the broken Market glass, because the page could not be removed by any other means — the ledger guarded itself, or had; the wardenship was fraying at the edges now that he had begun, actually begun, to mean it.

Sef unfolded it by lamplight. Their lips moved as they read. Halfway down, they stopped, and looked up, and their face had changed entirely — the archivist's composure gone, replaced by something rawer.

"This is a revocation clause."

"Bargains written in blood-price can be unwritten in blood-price. The original vow must be spoken backward, word-perfect, by one who holds the memory of the speaking. I hold it. But I can't speak it — I'm party to the contract; my word carries no weight against my own signature. It must be Astrid. And Astrid cannot remember the moment the vow was spoken, because the moment was part of the purchase." Aldric heard his own voice go quiet. "There is a remainder. The twin. Whatever fragment of Wren survives inside Astrid holds what was cut away. If it can be brought to the surface, and if it remembers —"

"If," Sef said.

"Yes. If. Everything worth doing is an if. I learned that late." He stepped back from the open cell. "Go east. Wake the boy gently; he startles. Astrid will fight you on leaving — let them win the argument quickly, there's no time for pride. And Sef."

Sef looked up.

"When this is done, if it is done — the Codex. Burn it or rewrite it, but stop letting it write you. That was my sin before any other. I let a book decide what I was."

He walked away before Sef could answer, because answers were a luxury, and because he was afraid that if he stopped walking he would sit down outside that cell and stay there, guarding, wardening, being exactly what he had been made into, until the cannon fired and made the choice irrelevant.

Behind him he heard Sef wake Ivo — gently, as instructed — and heard the boy's confused, frightened questions, and heard Sef's low steady answers begin to build the shape of escape.

* * *

Astrid was waiting for him in the corridor.

Not fighting Sef. Not fleeing. Standing in the bone-lit dark between the cells with their sword-hand loose at their side and their grandmother's jaw set hard in their face, and looking at him the way he had imagined, in his worst imaginings, being looked at for three hundred years.

"You," Astrid said.

"Me."

"Sef woke me instead of Ivo. Said you'd want me to hear it straight." Astrid stepped closer. Up close they were so much their grandmother — the same gray eyes, the same stubbornness in the set of the mouth — that Aldric's chest hurt with it. "The revocation clause. The twin. Say it isn't true."

"You know it's true," Aldric said. "You've known since the drowned chapel. You've been carrying a second heartbeat for weeks and telling yourself it was fear."

"Don't." The word cracked out of Astrid with an edge of unsworn power behind it — the mountain shivered, and somewhere down the row a guttering lamp steadied. Astrid blinked, startled at themselves. "Don't tell me what I know. You sold us. Both of us. Before I could walk. Before *either of us* could—" Their voice failed, refound itself lower. "Why are you unlocking doors? What does the man who sold his own blood want now?"

Aldric considered many answers. Most of them were true. None of them were sufficient.

"I want," he said at last, "to be the man who sold his own blood, and then stopped. That's all that's left to want. Everything else — the wardenship, the years, the standing at the gate calling it duty — that was the payment I took, and payments taken aren't the same as choices made. I took mine a long time ago. This is the choice."

From far below, through the bones of the mountain, came a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat: the oath-cannon's core, charging. The bone-veins in the walls flickered, dimmed, flickered again — the court itself feeling the pull.

Astrid heard it. So did Aldric. Neither moved toward the stairs.

"He'll fire it at the cage," Astrid said. "Ten thousand voices."

"Yes."

"And you're sending us up and out while he does it."

"I'm sending you up and out so you live to undo what I did. The cage is mine to answer for too. Let me answer it."

For a long moment Astrid studied him — and Aldric felt the look like weather, like the turning of a season, the assessment of one oath-bound creature by another. Then, slowly, Astrid shook their head.

"No," they said. "We don't split. That's how your bargains work — separate the parties, isolate the asset. I've read the ledger, Aldric. I've had weeks of Sef reading it to me by campfire. Every deal in that book starts with dividing people so they can't choose together." They lifted their chin toward the sound of the charging core. "So we go down. All of us. You included. And you can spend your guilt however you like down there, but you'll spend it beside us."

Something in Aldric's chest — something he had believed rusted shut centuries ago — moved.

"The revocation needs Wren," he said quietly. "It needs a memory neither of you owns alone."

"Then we wake Wren," Astrid said. "Together. Apparently that's the family business — waking things up." A ghost of a smile, fierce and brief. "Come on, grandfather. Show me the way down. And this time, when you call it home—"

"Yes?"

"—mean it differently."

* * *

They went down together: Aldric leading with the lamp, Astrid at his shoulder with the sword loose in its sheath, Sef and Ivo close behind with Sef's hand gripping the boy's arm — Ivo pale but walking, having refused, in a voice that cracked only once, to be sent anywhere alone.

The corridors narrowed. The bone-light thickened until it was less light than substance, veins of warm gold running through stone the color of old ivory, pulsing faster now as the core below drank harder at the mountain's root. Aldric heard the court changing pitch around them — the hum of the bound voices rising from its usual oceanic drone toward something sharper. Fear, or hope, or the place where those two sounds become indistinguishable.

At the last bend before the vaulted chamber, he stopped them with a raised hand and pressed the ledger's final page into Astrid's hands.

"Blood-price cuts both ways," he said. "Read it. Know it. When the moment comes — and it will come badly, moments like this always do — the words must be perfect. Word-perfect, backward, from the mouth of the memory that holds them."

Astrid looked down at the page. Their lips moved. Aldric watched them reach the clause and saw their breath change — the small involuntary sound a person makes when the shape of a life alters underneath them.

Below, a voice like poured gold rolled up the stairwell, rich and amused and bottomlessly hungry, and Aldric knew it the way you know a scar.

"Greetings, Warden!" Kestrian called, delighted, echoing. "Bring out my inheritance. The mountain is mine, and I have such plans for everything inside it."

Aldric closed his eyes for one breath. Opened them.

"Stay behind me," he told them, and walked down to meet his master — with the last of his line at his back, and the revocation burning in Astrid's hands like a coal that remembered being fire.
