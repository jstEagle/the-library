## 28. Revocation

The final page of Aldric's ledger was not paper.

I had assumed it would be — some vellum scraped thin as breath, some parchment cured in the old ways. But when Aldric pressed it into my hands in the ruined antechamber of the Undercourt, while the mountain groaned somewhere above us like a ship taking water, I felt its weight and knew it for what it was: a plate of lead, hammered flat, inscribed with lines so fine they might have been cut by a hair dipped in ink. Blood-price, he had called it. I understood now that he meant it literally. The page was a wound made permanent. It was still faintly warm.

"Read it," Sef said. She stood close enough that her shoulder touched mine, and I took strength from that small pressure — from the fact of her, solid and stubborn and still here after everything the mountain had tried to bury us under. "Read all of it. Every word. Don't skip the ugly parts."

"The ugly parts are most of it," Aldric said quietly. He had not sat down since giving me the page. He stood with his back very straight, this man who had not aged in a hundred years, and there was something terrible in his posture — the straightness of a soldier at attention before a firing squad he has requested himself. "That is rather the point of confessions."

* * *

The revocation clause ran to eleven lines. Sef read them aloud twice, once fast and once slow, because she did not trust herself to have heard them right the first time — and neither did I, because what they demanded was impossible, and I wanted to be sure of the shape of the impossibility before I broke against it.

*Let the sale be undone,* the lead said, in language older than any kingdom above, *by him who sold or by his blood who bears the debt, speaking the vow entire, backward, word-perfect, in the place where it was spoken.*

Backward. Word-perfect.

The original vow. The one Aldric Halloran had spoken on this spot, in this court, a hundred years ago — the bargain that bought the oath, that built every swordspeaker since, that purchased our power with a soul sold by the pound into the throne-cage below. To revoke it, someone had to stand here and speak that vow in reverse, every clause unwinding itself in perfect order, the way a rope unbraids only if you hold every strand.

And I could not remember it. Not one word of it.

Not because it was lost to time. Because it had been *sold*. The memory of that moment — Aldric's voice, the terms, the terrible arithmetic of it — had gone into the ledger along with everything else. It lived now in the cage beneath the throne, in the ten thousand voices Kestrian was even now climbing down the far stair to harvest. The knowledge of how to unmake the sale was locked inside the thing the sale had made.

"It's a trap," Ivo said. He had been pacing the length of the antechamber, restless as a caged animal, and now he stopped, hands knotted. "It's designed so it can't be undone. Whoever wrote this clause—" he gestured at the lead page "—built the lock and swallowed the key."

"Not whoever wrote it." Aldric's voice was very soft. "Me. I wrote it. And yes, Ivo Tanzer, I built the lock, and I swallowed the key, and I told myself for a century that this was mercy — that a bargain which could be casually undone was no bargain at all. That the Halloran oath deserved permanence. That *I* deserved permanence." He looked at his own hands, unlined, obscene. "I was a coward with excellent penmanship. That is the whole of my epitaph."

"Then it's finished." Ivo threw up his hands. "We came all this way, we buried half the Market, we've got an army descending the north stair and a madman with a cannon made of stolen oaths, and the one thing that could stop any of it requires a memory nobody alive possesses—"

"Somebody does."

The voice came from inside my own mouth.

I felt Wren rise the way you feel a second heartbeat start up under your ribs — a warmth behind the breastbone, a pressure at the back of the throat, a cadence entering my tongue that was mine and not mine. My lips moved. The words were shaped slightly wrong, pitched slightly high, the way a twin's voice is wrong and right at once when you hear it across a room.

*"Somebody remembers,"* Wren said through me. *"You're forgetting what we are, Astrid. You've been forgetting it since Thornfell. I'm not a passenger. I'm the part of us that didn't get spent. When they sold us — when Aldric sold the soul that fuels the oath — they couldn't take all of it. A flame needs a wick. They left a wick."*

Sef had gone very still. She was looking at my mouth as though it belonged to someone she was meeting for the first time, and perhaps she was; in all the weeks since the smudged name, since chapter by chapter the truth of the twin had surfaced, she had never heard Wren speak this fully, this *presently*, without me steering.

"Wren," Sef said carefully. "Can you hear me?"

*"I've always heard you. You talk in your sleep. You apologize to furniture."*

A sound escaped Ivo that was halfway between a laugh and a sob. Even now. Even here. I loved him for it.

"The vow," Sef pressed, leaning closer, her archivist's hunger cutting clean through her fear. "The original vow. Do you remember it? All of it? Word-perfect?"

And here Wren faltered. I felt it — a stutter in the borrowed voice, a coldness spreading through the shared space of our skull like water finding a crack.

*"Most of it,"* Wren said. *"I remember most of it. I was there, Astrid. Not born yet, not separate — but there, folded into the soul he sold. I remember the court lit with bone-light. I remember his voice shaking on the fourth clause. I remember—"*

The coldness spread. I felt Wren reaching for something and finding the reach too short, a hand closing on air where air should have been substance.

*"—there's a gap. Near the end. The sealing clauses — the ones that bind the sale shut. I remember that they existed. I remember their rhythm, almost. But the words themselves were the first payment taken. He paid them out of us before he'd even finished speaking them. Clever bastard. He made the vow eat its own tail."*

Silence in the antechamber. Somewhere far above, muffled through fathoms of stone, came a sound like thunder deciding to become permanent: the oath-cannon, being positioned. Being loaded. Kestrian's people worked quickly when they smelled a harvest.

"So it can't be done," Ivo said again, quieter now. "Word-perfect means word-perfect. One syllable wrong and—"

"And the clause fails," Aldric finished. "Or worse. A revocation spoken imperfectly doesn't undo the sale. It *renegotiates* it. On terms set by the court." His eyes went to the dark doorway beyond, where the bone-lit corridors of the Undercourt stretched away toward the throne-cage. "And the court, at present, is burning. Desperate courts make desperate terms."

* * *

But Sef was not listening anymore. Sef had that look — the one I'd first seen at a smuggler's waystation a lifetime ago, when she'd opened the Vellum Codex to a page dated that night and shown me my own name written in century-old ink. It was the look of a woman whose mind had just left the room to go do something dangerous without her body's permission.

"Astrid," she said slowly. "When the sale was made — the memory went into the ledger. Into the debt. Where does a debt live?"

"In the cage. In the sold soul. In—"

"In *you*. In both of you. You're the debtors. The whole line of you — the oath runs on the debt, that's what Aldric told us, that's why your vows take memories, every payment services the principal." She was talking faster now, pacing, hands carving the air. "So the missing clauses aren't destroyed. Nothing down here is ever destroyed — that's the first law of the Undercourt, the miners' stories, everything's priced and stored and kept. They were *transferred*. Paid into the account. Which means they exist somewhere in the court's records, in the market's stock, in the—"

"The vials," I breathed.

The Market of Voices. Half of it ash now, Kestrian's breach-fire having gutted the bazaar where memories hung in glass like preserved fruit. But markets keep vaults. Markets keep their ledgers. If the sealing clauses had been sold — if Aldric had paid them into the court's own coffers —

"They'd be in the warden's inventory," Aldric said. He had closed his eyes. When he opened them, there was something in his face I hadn't seen there before: not guilt, not tenderness, but *shame* — the raw, skinless kind. "The vault beneath the chapel. I kept it myself. A hundred years of payments, every memory ever surrendered against the Halloran debt, catalogued and shelved. Including—" his voice caught on something "—including the first installment. Mine."

* * *

Getting there meant crossing the burned Market.

We went in a line — Ivo first with a salvaged lantern, then Sef, then me, then Aldric last, and the order mattered more than anyone said aloud, because none of us entirely trusted ourselves to walk in front of him and watch his face among the ruins he had curated for a century. Glass crunched under our boots in sheets and drifts. The fire had done strange things to the memory-vials: where they'd shattered, the released moments hung in the air as heat-shimmer ghosts, half-second replays flickering in the smoke. A child's birthday. A wedding argument. Someone's last view of a sunset over Harrowgate's western wall. The Market's dead inventory, haunting its own shelves.

At the heart of the burn zone, the vault door still stood. Black iron, unmarked, cool to the touch. Of course it was — the same metal as the miner's key, the same metal as the ledger's final page. The Undercourt built its locks to outlast its arsonists.

Inside: rows. Rows and rows of niches cut into living rock, each holding vials ranked by age, and the oldest niches nearest the back were furred with mineral bloom, centuries of the mountain slowly trying to swallow its own records. Sef went down the rows reading labels in a whisper, her fingers trailing the stone, an archivist in the archive of the damned.

"Here." Her voice cracked. "Astrid. Here."

One niche. Two vials.

Two.

She lifted them into the lantern light, and I saw why there were two, and the floor of the world tilted.

The first was labeled in Aldric's fine hand: *Sealing clauses, IV–VII. Payment rendered in full.* The memory of the vow's ending — the exact words Wren could not recall, the lock's key, sitting in glass a hundred years, waiting.

The second vial held a curl of gold light that moved like breathing. Its label read simply: *Wren Halloran. Remainder. Not for sale.*

"I couldn't do it," Aldric said from the doorway. He had not entered the vault. He stood with one hand braced on the frame as if the threshold itself repelled him. "After the birth — after we fused what remained of the twin into the surviving child — the court demanded the remnant too. Full payment. And I refused. The only refusal of my long coward's life. I told them the remainder was required collateral, that selling it would void the original instrument. Legal fiction. Lies. I just — I could not sell *all* of a child." His head bowed. "So I kept your brother on a shelf."

"My sister," I said.

Aldric looked up.

"Wren is my sister." My voice came out steady, which surprised me, because everything under it was shaking. "I've been carrying her since before I was born. She speaks with my mouth and dreams in my sleep and she is not a remainder, and she is not collateral, and she is not *on a shelf* anymore."

I took both vials.

* * *

We gathered in the antechamber, at the exact center of the sigil worn into the floor — the place where the original vow had been spoken, the place the clause demanded. Above us the mountain shuddered in long slow intervals now, like a sleeper turning toward waking. Below us, faintly, through fathoms of stone, I could feel the throne-cage humming: ten thousand voices, and somewhere among them Kestrian's men already drilling at the outer wards.

There was no time. There is never time, at the end; the mountain sees to that.

"Explain it to me once more," I said. "Exactly. What happens if I speak it wrong."

"You don't want to know," said Ivo.

"I need to know."

Sef answered, because Sef always answered. "An imperfect revocation reopens negotiation. The court sets new terms. With the court in agony and half-burned, the terms will be set by whatever's left of its law — which may be nothing, which may be the cage itself, which may be Kestrian if his people breach in time to claim creditor's rights." She met my eyes. "It could mean the sale transfers to him. Your soul. Wren's. Everything. Forever, with interest."

"And if I don't speak it at all, the cannon fires within the hour and everyone in that cage becomes fuel for a tyrant's immortality." I turned the two vials in my fingers. Gold light, patient light. "So. No pressure."

*Astrid.* Wren rose gently this time, not seizing the tongue but resting against it, a hand on a shoulder rather than a hand on a throat. *Let me say it.*

"You remember the opening clauses. The middle. Most of it."

*I remember being sold. There's no forgetting the shape of it. But the ending — the seal — that's the vial. That's the part we bought back.* A pause, and then, with something that in a separate throat would have been a laugh: *We can't do it alone, either of us. I'm missing the beginning. You're missing the end. Isn't that just like the family.*

I looked at Sef. At Ivo. Last, longest, at Aldric, who watched me with the expression of a man watching the verdict of a trial he'd waited a century to lose.

"If we do this," I said, "and it works — the sale revokes. The oath dies. Every swordspeaker's power ends with it, including mine. And the debt dissolves — which means the cage loses its legal claim on every voice inside it. All ten thousand. Free." I swallowed. "And Wren and I — what happens to us? If the soul comes home whole? Two people in one body, one life, one set of—"

*Nobody knows,* Wren said simply. *Isn't it wonderful?*

"That," said Sef softly, "is the first honest sentence anyone's spoken in this mountain."

I uncorked the first vial. Memory poured into me like cold water into a hot glass — I gasped, staggered, and suddenly I was standing in this chamber a hundred years ago, seeing it through eyes not mine, hearing a young man's voice crack on the fourth clause, feeling the terrible exhilaration of a bargain struck with eternity. And beneath that memory, arriving a half-breath later, came Wren's — the same moment from inside the sold soul, the darkness and the light and the sound of a name being signed away.

The two memories lay against each other like matching halves of a broken seal.

They fit.

"Oh," whispered Sef, who saw my face and understood before any of us said it. "Oh, that's why it needed *both* of you. The vow was spoken by one person, but it was *made* of two — the speaker and the payment. Only a voice holding both sides can wind it backward. That's why the clause said *his blood who bears the debt*. Singular. It never imagined the debt would grow up and learn to share."

I raised the second vial. Wren's remainder — a hundred years on a shelf, and still it leaned toward me through the glass like a child toward a window.

"Together?" I asked — out loud, in front of witnesses, because some vows should never be silent.

*Together,* said my sister.

I drank.

* * *

What rose in me then cannot be properly told, but I will try, because the mountain deserves a witness and I am what remains of one. Imagine every word you have ever spoken arranged in a corridor, running backward into darkness. Now imagine walking it — no, being walked — while a second set of footsteps keeps pace beside yours, and between you, you constitute one complete traveler. The opening clauses came up out of Wren like water finding its level, and I carried them forward; the middle we spoke in braided unison, her cadence and mine crossing so tightly that Sef swore afterward she heard one voice; and when we reached the point where the young Aldric's courage failed — where the sealing clauses had been devoured as payment — I opened the second vial's gift and the ending was *there*, whole, remembered from both sides of the transaction, and I spoke it backward into the bone-lit dark:

Every promise unmaking itself in reverse order. Every *I swear* becoming *I unsay*. The rope unbraiding strand by strand while the mountain held its breath around us, and the sigil beneath our feet kindled white, and the ledger on its stand nearby began, page by page, century by century, to bleed its ink upward off the vellum like a tide going out—

On the final word, the world cracked — not the mountain, but something underneath the mountain, something older than bargains — and every light in the Undercourt went out at once.

In the absolute dark, small and clear, I heard the throne-cage begin to open.

And above us, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Kestrian's men cheering — because whatever had just broken in the deeps, the usurper felt it too, and mistook it for his harvest beginning.
