## 29. The Cannon Fires

Maren Duskwell had burned a refugee column on the road above Harrowgate, and she had hated herself cleanly, efficiently, the way a surgeon hates an amputation — with precision, without indulgence. She had thought that was the worst thing her hands would ever do.

She was wrong.

The oath-cannon sat at the mouth of the Hollow Stair like a dead god's finger, its barrel forged from three hundred confiscated blades welded vow-to-vow, each one still faintly warm, still whispering. She could hear them as she walked the line of inquisitors checking the breech-chains: a low chorus of broken promises, muffled by iron. Kestrian stood beside it with his hand flat against the metal, and Maren watched him breathe it in.

He was younger than he'd been that morning. That was the horror of it — you could watch it happen, the lines around his eyes smoothing like wax reworked by a thumb, his hair thickening from ash-grey back to brown at the temples. He fed as he touched it. He was always feeding now.

"Inquisitor-General." He didn't look at her. "Your report."

"The stair is cleared to the fourth landing. The locals—" she almost said *the people*, caught herself, "—the resistance is scattered. The swordspeaker and her companions went down ahead of us. Two days' lead, perhaps less. The mountain slows them."

"And speeds us." Kestrian turned, and for a moment his face did something terrible: it smiled with genuine warmth. "You've done well, Maren. Better than well. When this is finished, when the throne-cage is emptied into the vaults at Vaelmoor, I will not forget who brought me the key to it."

*When this is finished.* She held his gaze because looking away would be worse than anything else she could do. "And the free cities?"

"Harvested," he said pleasantly. "In time. Every oath sworn in every market square from here to the sea — do you know how much that is? Centuries of kept promises, sitting in steel and stone like grain in a silo. The mountain is only the granary door." He patted the cannon's barrel the way a farmer might pat an ox. "But first, the wholesale cut. A thousand souls in one draught. The collectors have been selling these voices by the pound for a hundred years, and no one ever thought to simply take the whole shelf."

Maren thought of her brother. Tam. Dead eleven years, drowned in a canal in Vaelmoor, or so the record said — *sold*, more likely; she had heard his voice down in the dark two nights ago, among the bound ones, thin and repeating, calling her name in a child's cadence because that was when he'd been taken, before he'd ever grown. She had filed no report about that either.

"Your orders?" she said.

Kestrian's smile widened. "We fire from here. The cage sits at the mountain's heart; the oath-light will rise up the stair like sap up a trunk. My artificers have built the siphon-vaults to catch it. Nothing needs to be carried out by hand. Elegant, yes?"

*Nothing needs to be seen,* Maren translated. Nothing human would go down there and stand in front of what they were doing. Even the usurper's appetite had learned shame, or efficiency — with him there had never been much difference.

"Elegant," she said.

* * *

They fired at dusk, though dusk meant nothing under the mountain except the moment the bone-lights along the stair dimmed on their slow cycle, veins of candle-glow pulsing through pale stone.

Maren stood thirty paces back, hand on her sword, watching the artificers spin the ignition-wheel. The chains sang. The barrel of welded blades began to glow at the seams, and the whispers inside it rose — she heard distinct words now, fragments of vows, *I will return, I will protect her, I will never leave* — three hundred small promises screaming their way toward one enormous purpose.

Kestrian raised his hand.

For one absurd instant, Maren considered whether she could reach him before the wheel completed its turn. Eleven paces. Two guards. His reflexes were those of a man drinking youth by the gallon. No.

The wheel locked. The cannon spoke.

There was no boom, not at first. There was a *pulling* — every candle in the cavern bent sideways, every breath in her lungs dragged outward, every inquisitor staggering toward the muzzle as if the mountain had inhaled. Then the light left. It went *down*, through the rock, through bone and salt and the deep buried architecture of the Undercourt, a lance of stolen vow-fire punching toward the heart of Thornfell — and the mountain answered.

The crack came up through Maren's boots before she heard it. Then she heard it: a sound like the world's largest door splitting along a seam, deepening, widening, running away beneath them in branching lines. Dust fell from a ceiling that had not shed dust in ten thousand years. Somewhere far below, something that had been silent for centuries began — very faintly — to scream.

Not one voice. Thousands.

"Oh," said one of the young artificers, staring at his gauges, delighted. "It's working. Look at the draw — we're pulling raw—"

The gauges shattered. All of them, at once, in a spray of glass and mercury that opened the boy's cheek to the bone. He didn't even cry out. None of the bound voices below were screaming in pain exactly, Maren realized. They were waking. The blast hadn't harvested the cage — it had *cracked* it, cracked the mountain with it, and now ten thousand souls that had been drugged into sleep for generations were surfacing all together into agony, and their agony was rising up the Hollow Stair like floodwater.

Kestrian swayed beside the cannon. For a heartbeat Maren thought he'd been struck — then she saw his face and understood. He was *drinking*. Raw vow-light was flooding back up the barrel faster than the siphon-vaults could catch it, and he stood in the spill of it with his arms half-raised, mouth open, eyes rolling white, growing — not taller, but *denser*, his outline smearing at the edges like ink dropped in water. His hair went from brown to black to a color that had no name.

"Maren." His voice came from everywhere at once. "Maren, it's *magnificent*."

She drew her sword.

She did not decide to. Later, examining the memory — and she examined it many times, in the years after, in testimony and in dreams — she could never find the decision. There was only the moment before, when she was standing at attention, and the moment after, when her blade was bare and her body had already chosen, some part of her older than her commission, older than her ambition, older than Tam's funeral.

An inquisitor nearest her — Vessic, good with locks, kind to horses — saw the blade and frowned, confused rather than alarmed. "General?"

"The vaults aren't catching it," Maren said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Look at the draw readings. We're not harvesting. We're cracking the cage open, and everything inside is coming *up*. Everything." She raised her voice over the rising wail. "Every soul the collectors ever bought. Every voice sold by the pound. Do any of you know someone who died strangely? Someone whose body was found but whose face was wrong afterward? They're in that flood. And when it reaches the surface, it reaches Vaelmoor."

Vessic looked at the cannon. At the king. At the thing that had been the king, swelling gently at the edges, drinking.

"Stand down the ignition crew," Maren ordered. "Now. We cannot fire a second charge into a cracked cage."

"She can't," said the thing that had been Kestrian, still smiling, still everywhere. "But I can."

The second firing needed no wheel. The cannon simply *obeyed* him now, the welded blades groaning into alignment, drawing power out of the air itself, out of the waking voices, out of the inquisitors' own minor oaths of service — Maren felt hers tug, the vow she'd sworn at her commissioning (*to serve the crown's justice*) pulled taut across her chest like a wire.

So she broke it.

"I resign," she said clearly, into the pull, into the wire, into the faces of forty inquisitors. "I resign my commission. I name Kestrian a usurper and a soul-thief, and I release you from your oaths to him — hear me, all of you: you are released. What follows is not treason. It is *arrest*."

The wire snapped. She felt it break — a small cold snap behind her sternum, a vow coming loose — and with it, horribly, a memory peeled away, payment for the unsworn breaking: she lost the smell of Tam's room, the cedar and wet wool of it, gone as if a page had been lifted out of a book. She had paid memories before, buying escape for the swordspeaker at the ford, buying her brother's face. This one she paid standing still, on purpose, and it was the easiest yet.

Because forty inquisitors were looking at her.

Some looked away. Six — she counted them forever after: Vessic, the Corvane sisters, old Brandt, a scarred woman named Ilse, a boy called Fen — six moved to stand beside her, swords out. The rest froze in the terrible arithmetic of soldiers everywhere: orders against eyes, career against conscience, the muzzle against the man.

Then the flood arrived.

It came up the stair not as light but as *sound* — ten thousand voices reaching the upper galleries at once, and the sound was not noise, it was recognition. Maren heard her own name in it, heard *Tam, Tam, Duskwell, he's here, he's drinking us, stop him* — and she saw the frozen inquisitors hear their own names too, mothers and fathers and drowned children and deserters, everyone anyone had ever quietly sold or failed to mourn properly, all of them surfacing together through the cracks in the world.

Three more inquisitors crossed to Maren's side. Then five. Then a dozen, weapons dropping, men and women falling to their knees not in surrender but in grief, sobbing names into the dust.

Kestrian screamed. It was the first honest sound she had ever heard him make. The drink had become drowning — too much too fast, ten thousand furious souls pouring into a vessel built for theft, and he blazed at the mouth of the stair like a bonfire wearing a crown, his outline no longer human, his voice layered now, speaking with a hundred stolen throats: *"Mine, mine, I bought it, I PAID—"*

"He'll tear the mountain down on all of us," Brandt shouted. "General — the swordspeaker — she went down toward the cage. If anyone can close this—"

Maren was already moving.

She took the stairs three at a time, six loyal inquisitors at her back, descending into the cracking dark toward the heart of Thornfell — toward the swordspeaker she had hunted across half the world, whom she had cornered at a ford and cost a mother's face, whom she had been ordered to bring alive to a man who wanted to eat her whole.

Below her, the mountain groaned. Above her, Kestrian's burning shape tore free of the cannon's frame and followed, flowing down the stair like molten crown-metal, hungry even now, *hungriest now*.

And far beneath them both, at the bottom of everything, Maren heard a new voice rise through the ten thousand — clear, doubled, two voices braided into one, a swordspeaker and someone else, someone who had waited a very long time to speak —

—and the voice said a single word that made the whole mountain hold its breath:

*"Enough."*
