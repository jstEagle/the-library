## 30. Climbing the Cracking Dark

The mountain screamed, and Ivo Tanzer learned what it meant to be inside a throat.

He had been running — that was all he could say of the last hour, all his body would admit to knowing: stone under his boots, dark ahead, Sef's lantern swinging like a dying star. Then Kestrian's cannon had fired, and the world had folded in on itself. The blast came not as sound first but as *pressure*, a fist of air and light that picked Ivo up and set him down ten feet away against a wall that was no longer where he remembered walls being.

When his ears stopped ringing enough to hear anything at all, the Hollow Stair was coming apart.

Above them — which was below them, because gravity here leaned toward the mountain's heart and spoken words fell upward — the great spiral of steps cracked like river ice in spring. Chunks of bone-white stone sheared away and drifted, slowly, terribly, toward the center of everything. The candlelight veins that lit the deep places flickered, guttered, flared. Somewhere far off, behind them, down in the drowned chapel and the Market of Voices, glass was breaking by the thousand.

"Up." Sef's voice found him through the dust. The archivist's face was gray with powdered stone, their spectacles cracked across one lens, and they were already hauling on Astrid's arm. "Ivo. *Up.* If the throne-cage cracks open while we're standing still—"

"I'm coming."

His legs answered before his courage did. That, he would think later, was the whole secret of it. Courage was just legs that kept answering.

* * *

They climbed through a world in ruin.

The stair had been made — or grown; Ivo no longer trusted any distinction the Undercourt observed — from vertebrae of something vast and patient, each step broad as a table, spiraling upward into darkness that breathed. Now the vertebrae were splitting along seams older than kingdoms. Dust fell upward past their faces in slow gray curtains. And through it all came the voices.

That was the worst part. Not the cracking, not the falling. The *voices*.

Ten thousand bound souls caged in the throne above them had felt the cannon's blow like a hammer on a hive, and now they were waking, and waking hurt. Their cries poured down the stairwell in waves — grief, confusion, fury, and beneath it all a sound like ten thousand people saying *oh* at once, the sound of prisoners remembering there had once been a sky.

Ivo climbed with his hands over his ears for the first hundred steps. It didn't help. The voices weren't outside him. They were in the stone, in the marrow of the place, in the wet dark of his own skull.

"Don't listen," Astrid said. They were three steps above him, hauling themselves hand over hand along the inner rail, and their voice had a strange doubled quality Ivo had been hearing since the chapel — two cadences braided into one throat, Astrid and the one called Wren speaking together like a bow drawn across two strings. "Don't listen to *what* they're saying. Just know they're saying it. That's how you keep your feet."

"How do you—" He coughed dust. "How do you know that?"

Astrid looked back at him. In the bone-light their face was half shadow and half flame, and for a moment Ivo saw both of them there — the swordspeaker who had walked out of a prophecy, and the twin who had never gotten to be born, wearing the same eyes.

"Because I spent twenty-five years doing it," Astrid said. "Come on."

* * *

The second tremor caught them at a landing where the stair split around a pillar of fused vertebrae, and this time the mountain did not scream so much as *shift*, a vast muscular settling, like a giant rolling over in pain.

The landing tilted. Sef went down.

Ivo saw it happen in pieces: the archivist's boot skidding on a film of fine powder; the lantern leaving their grip and spinning away into the dark, its light whirling like a dropped coin; Sef catching the rail with one hand, sliding, the whole weight of them swinging out over the drop where gravity leaned hungry toward the center of the world.

"*Sef!*"

Ivo threw himself flat. The stone bit his palms. Below him Sef dangled, boots kicking at nothing, their free hand scrabbling at smooth bone that offered nothing to hold. Three years of searching, Ivo thought absurdly. Three years of exile and forbidden pages, and it ends hanging off a staircase inside a dying god's spine.

"Sef! Give me your other hand!"

"I can't reach!"

"Then I'll come to you!" He swung his legs over the edge before his mind finished objecting. The pull of the deep places took hold of his ankles like cold water, gentle and absolute. He stretched until his shoulder screamed. His fingers found Sef's wrist — slick, thin, astonishingly strong for a man who lived among books.

"I've got you."

"No," Sef said, and their cracked spectacles had fogged with fear-breath, but their voice was level, almost apologetic, the voice of a man reading a difficult passage aloud. "No, you don't. You weigh nothing here and I weigh everything. You'll go over with me. Let go, Ivo. Find another way up. Tell Astrid the marginalia — tell them about the revocation clause, the blood-price, tell them—"

"Shut up," Ivo said. "Shut up, I'm not letting go."

"You must—"

"I deserted the Council of Harrowgate." He heard his own voice crack, and hated it, and kept talking anyway, because talking was the only rope he had. "I burned my commission. My father disowned me by letter — a *letter*, Sef, he couldn't even do it to my face. I have lost every single thing I had except being useful to two people climbing a broken staircase, and I am not letting go of the one thing left."

Below them, boots rang on stone.

Astrid came down the outside of the rail like something that had been born on cliffs — or like someone borrowing the skill of everyone who ever had. They braced against the pillar, hooked one arm through the balustrade, and reached down with their sword-hand bare and open.

"Sef," Astrid said. "The Codex. Page one. What does it say?"

Sef blinked dust from their eyes. "*All oaths are debts,*" they recited, automatic, the scholar's reflex stronger than terror. "What—"

"And page four hundred?"

"'...and every debt may be forgiven, though forgiveness costs the lender.'" Sef's face changed. Something loosened in it. "Oh. *Oh.* You want me to let go of the railing."

"I want you to stop holding on to the wrong thing." Astrid's open hand waited, steady as a written line. "Take mine instead. Trust is just an oath nobody wrote down. Jump."

And Sef — disgraced, exiled, three-years-searching Sef — closed their eyes, released the rail, and fell six inches into Astrid's grip.

They hauled him up between them, all three gasping on the tilted landing, and none of them said anything clever. There wasn't time. Above them the cracking grew louder, and through the new-made gaps in the stairwell wall they could see, for the first time, what they were climbing toward.

* * *

The throne-cage hung in a cavern like the inside of a heart.

It was a lattice of black iron and white bone, vast as a cathedral turned inside out, and within it moved lights — thousands upon thousands of small warm flames, packed tight, swirling in slow constellations. Each one a soul. Each one a person somebody had sold, by the pound, by the vial, by the name.

And wrapped around the cage, clinging to it like frost on a window, was Kestrian.

Even at this distance Ivo could see what the usurper was becoming. He had seen the man twice in Harrowgate, once from a delegate's bench: a handsome, silver-templed figure, youth held on his frame like dye held in cloth. That cloth was tearing now. The stolen vows of confiscated blades had sustained him for decades, but raw vow-light — drunk straight from the cage through conduits of iron and screaming — was more than a body was built to hold. Kestrian swelled with it. His silhouette blurred and thickened, arms too long, spine curving, mouth opening wider than mouths should, and the light he swallowed shone *through* him, outlining ribs that might have been his own or might have been growing new ones.

"He's going to drink it dry," Sef whispered. "If he drains the cage before Astrid reaches it—"

"Then there won't be a cage to break," Astrid said quietly. "Or a mountain either. Or, possibly, a world with Thornfell in it. Wren says the foundations are vows. Every load-bearing thing down here is a promise somebody made. He's eating the architecture."

The final flight of stairs lay ahead — perhaps four hundred steps, spiraling up the cavern wall toward a gatehouse of fused vertebrae that had, until tonight, been sealed. Between them and it, the stair crossed open gallery after open gallery, and through those galleries came sounds that made Ivo's stomach fold.

Inquisitors. Maren Duskwell's white-and-black column had entered the far stair hours ago, and now fragments of them were everywhere in the collapse — cut off, leaderless in some cases, in others still moving with terrible discipline toward the same gatehouse. And among the freed echoes and the falling stone moved things worse: oath-wights, the confiscated blades' residual promises given crude shape, stumbling constructs of iron and grievance that struck at anything warm.

"We fight," Astrid said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't bravado either. Ivo heard what was underneath it: *we fight, and I am nearly empty, and every word I spend now is a word I'll need later.* Since the cage-crack, Astrid's swordspeech had been a guttering lamp — one unsworn word spent on the ice bridge, another on Sef, the well of sold soul draining faster with each miracle.

"No," Ivo said.

Astrid and Sef both turned.

"Not you." His hands were shaking. He noticed, catalogued it the way his father had taught him to catalogue weakness in an opponent, and found — to his genuine surprise — that he didn't care. "Not unless you have to. You're the key to whatever happens up there. Wren's the memory of the vow. If either of you falls on these stairs, everything everyone has paid is wasted. Sef carries the ledger knowledge. So it's me." He drew the short council-issue blade he'd carried since Harrowgate, the one honest piece of steel on his whole traitor's body. "I'm not a swordspeaker. I'm not even a good fencer. But I'm a *delegate*, and delegates know exactly one thing: how to stand in front of something ugly and delay the vote."

Sef made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Astrid looked at him for a long moment, that doubled gaze measuring him, weighing him — and then nodded, once, the way you nod to an equal.

"Together, then," Astrid said. "But you take point only when I say. Deal?"

"An oath freely given," Ivo said, and was startled to feel the mountain hum faintly under his boots, as if it had heard.

* * *

The first oath-wight came at them on the two-hundredth step.

It had been a duelist once — you could see it in the way the construct moved, the elegant ruined geometry of a fencing master's muscle-memory bound forever into scrap iron and a promise to win. Its blade was a bar of confiscated steel, still humming with the dead vow. It came up the stairs at them without haste, because it had never known hurry and never would.

"Astrid—" Sef began.

"I said I'd take point." Ivo stepped forward.

Everything his tutors had drilled into him arrived all at once, useless and useful: distance, angle, the lie of the feint, the truth of the retreat. The wight cut; he was not there; the blade took a chip of stair instead and the whole step lurched loose beneath them. He gave ground, gave it again, giving ground the way water gives, and the wight followed, patient as arithmetic, onto the exact slab Ivo had noticed was cracked.

"Nothing personal," he said, and hammered his pommel down on the fissure.

The slab broke. The wight dropped, silently, into the dark, still holding its en garde, and Ivo stood shaking over the gap feeling sick and victorious and certain that somewhere his father was turning in a chair he hadn't left yet.

"That," Sef said faintly, "was not in any book I've read."

"There's a lot of the world not in books." Ivo wiped his mouth. "That's why I started reading your margins instead."

* * *

They fought four more times, and Ivo stopped counting the steps and counted only breaths — in for the climb, out for the swing. Sef turned out to carry a stiletto of archive-silver and a startling knowledge of anatomy gleaned from banned medical folios; Astrid spent two words in all that stretch, one to silence a wight's humming blade mid-air and one — whispered, anguished, costing something visible — to turn a collapsing gallery into a bridge long enough for them to cross. Each word left Astrid grayer. Each word left the doubled voice thinner, as if Wren were being used up alongside.

And always above them Kestrian drank.

By the final gallery, the usurper had stopped resembling a man entirely. He was a column of appetite now, a tower of swallowed light with a face surfacing in it occasionally like a drowning swimmer — sometimes young, sometimes ancient, sometimes both at once, the decades he had stolen all trying to leave at once and being forced down again. The cage's constellations were dimming where he fed. Ivo watched a cluster of small flames near the iron gutter and thought of the Market of Voices, of vials, of prices, of Ivo's mother singing — the moment he'd bought back and wept over, the taste of her kitchen, her off-key rising note. Ten thousand mothers. Ten thousand songs being drained through one greedy mouth.

"Almost there," Astrid breathed.

They were fifty steps from the gatehouse when the mountain exhaled.

Not a tremor this time. An exhale — a slow, tectonic sigh that rolled up from the roots of Thornfell, and Ivo understood, with the strange clarity exhaustion grants, that the freed voices had done something. That ten thousand souls waking in agony had pushed, together, against the walls of their prison, and the prison — and the mountain around it — had breathed out under the pressure.

The gallery floor rippled like a struck drumhead. The gatehouse ahead groaned, tilted, and began, majestically, horribly, to fall — directly into the path, sealing the last approach in tons of ancient bone.

And out of the dust and the drumming, from a side gallery below, came the ring of boots in formation, and a voice Ivo knew from wanted bills and council briefings, hoarse now, stripped of its official music:

"Halloran! *Halloran!* Take my hand at the rail — I can get you to the gate from the west spur — *move!*"

Inquisitor-General Maren Duskwell stood on a shattered balcony with her white cloak torn to ribbons and her sword drawn — pointed, Ivo saw with a lurch, not at them but back down the gallery, at her own advancing column.

Astrid stared. Sef swore softly in three dead languages.

Ivo Tanzer, ordinary man, delegate, deserter, son of a letter, tightened his grip on his plain honest blade and started running for the rail.

"Fifty steps," he panted. "Someone catch us if we fall."
