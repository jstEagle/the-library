## 31. The Throne of Ten Thousand

The last stair gave out beneath Ivo's boot with a sound like a snapped rib, and after that there was no more climbing—only the throne-cage filling the world.

Astrid had imagined it a hundred times since Sef first described it in whispers through a gilded cell door: a cage of voices, ten thousand strong. Nothing imagined came close. It rose from the center of the Undercourt like a heart cut from the mountain and hung back in its own hollow, a lattice of black iron and older things—bone, glass, chains of script that moved like slow silver worms—and inside it, *light*. Not candlelight, not oath-light. The light of voices pressed together until they had nowhere to go but into glow. Ten thousand sold souls, packed to the density of suns, humming a chord so vast and so patient that Astrid felt it not in their ears but in their teeth, in the wet of their eyes, in the old scar tissue where the Halloran oath had been sworn into their wrist.

The mountain's bone-veins all ran here. Astrid could see them now, the luminous threads Aldric had walked among for three centuries, converging like rivers toward a sea that was not allowed to move.

"It's singing," Wren said.

Not aloud. Never quite aloud anymore—the twin voice lived behind Astrid's sternum now, a second pulse, a second set of eyes looking out through shared sockets. But Astrid heard it as clearly as their own thought, and had stopped flinching from it somewhere between the Market of Voices and the cracking dark.

"They're always singing," Astrid said. Their own voice came out raw. The climb had cost them: Ivo's arm was bound with a strip of Maren's cloak, Sef was gray-lipped and leaning against a fallen pillar, and Astrid's left hand would not fully close. "That's what a soul sounds like when you don't let it finish a sentence."

Below them, across a floor of shattered chapel tile, Kestrian drank.

He had stopped pretending to be a man somewhere on the far side of the oath-cannon's second firing. He stood at the cage's base with both hands buried to the wrist in a vein of vow-light he'd torn open like bread, and the stolen years went into him visibly—a shudder along his jaw, a thickening of the light behind his eyes, his shadow spreading across the tiles wider than his body had any right to cast. Inquisitors ringed him at a careful distance, the smart ones already edging toward the exits, the loyal ones too far gone in awe to move.

Maren Duskwell stood closest of all, blade drawn, her face the face of a woman who had decided something irreversible and was simply waiting for the mountain to agree with her.

"Astrid." Kestrian did not turn. His voice had changed too—layered now, one voice laid over another over another, the way a chorus sounds when it's been made to hold a single note past all decency. "You're just in time. Do you know what this is? Truly?"

"Ten thousand people," Astrid said.

"Ten thousand *oaths*." He turned then, and Astrid made themselves look. His skin had gone translucent at the temples; under it, faint as watermark, vows moved. Other people's promises, swimming in him like fish under ice. "Every swordspeaker's bargain, every market contract, every whispered vow these mountains ever swallowed and kept. The Hallorans found the well, child. Your grandmother's grandmother drank from it and called it magic. I am merely drinking *deeper*."

"You're drinking prisoners," Wren said—through Astrid's mouth, both of them speaking at once, the double voice rolling off the tile. Astrid let them. It seemed only fair that Kestrian hear how many of them there were.

His smile widened. "And what are you? Two souls in one coat, is the rumor. One sold, one smudged. You should understand me better than anyone, Astrid Halloran. We are both vessels. The difference is that I *chose* my fullness."

"The difference," Astrid said, "is that everyone in you is screaming, and no one in me is."

That landed. He blinked, slow, like something reptilian processing warmth, and for half a breath the layered voice fell silent and only Kestrian remained—younger than his years, emptier than his youth, a man who had once looked into the same well and felt thirst instead of horror.

Then the mountain shook again, deep and structural, and dust sifted down through the bone-veins like snow falling upward in reverse, and there was no more time for philosophy.

* * *

Sef reached them first, hauling themselves along the pillar line with an archivist's economy—not a wasted step, not a wasted word. "The revocation clause needs a speaker," they rasped. "But listen to me, both of you, *listen*. That's not the only door. Look at the cage's anchor-script. Look at the base."

Astrid looked. Through the haze of vow-light they saw it: the lattice did not merely stand on the floor of the court. It was *written* onto it—rings upon rings of binding script, the same moving silver text that chained the whole Undercourt together, flowing out of the cage's roots and into the mountain's bones. Every freed voice Astrid had heard echoing up the Hollow Stair, every memory in every vial in every burning stall of the Market—it all drew its power, its *permission*, from this one anchored point.

"It's not a prison," Sef said. "It's a *keystone*. Pull the keystone—" They didn't finish. They didn't have to. The whole architecture of the Undercourt, the trade, the warden's office, the tidy commerce of souls by the pound, stood on this cage standing shut.

"And if it opens," Ivo said quietly, from where he'd braced himself against the pillar, cradling his bad arm, "everything comes out at once. Ten thousand voices. All the power that's been stored here for three hundred years." He swallowed. "In whose hands?"

"In no one's," said Wren.

"In *ours*," said Kestrian.

He had crossed the floor while they spoke. That was the obscenity of him now—he moved like spilled water, fast and wrong, and suddenly he was ten paces away with vow-light still dripping from his fingers, and the inquisitors were fanning wide behind him, and Maren had shifted her stance without seeming to decide to.

"I'll be plain with you, swordspeaker," Kestrian said, and his voice was almost gentle, which was worse than the layering. "You cannot beat me. Feel it. Feel what I've taken in tonight alone. You could speak your little steel-words until your tongue rots and it would be a moth against a bonfire. Unless—" and here he gestured, almost courteously, at the cage, "—unless you take what's yours. Your soul is in there, Astrid. Sold by your own blood, yes, bought cruelly, yes—but it is *yours*, and it has been steeping in that light for a century. Reclaim it and you will not be a moth. You will be the fire. You could unmake me with a syllable. You could take Vaelmoor in an afternoon. You could take everything your free cities keep begging the world not to take from them."

Astrid's heart did a thing Astrid hated it for doing.

It *wanted* that.

Not abstractly. Not in the noble way of imagining justice. It wanted it the way a starving body wants bread—immediate, animal, below all argument. Because Astrid could feel the gap where their soul should be, had been feeling it their whole life without knowing its name: the memories that weren't theirs, the flat places, the way oaths came easy because some essential part of them had been pre-spent. And one ring of that cage, one thread of that light, was *theirs*. Their own self, waiting. Whole. Complete. Powerful enough to stop Kestrian with a word and protect every city that had voted, only weeks ago, to sell them to the usurper's hunters.

One act of reclamation, and Astrid Halloran would never be powerless again.

"Do you feel it?" Kestrian murmured. He had come closer still. Behind him Maren took one long stride, and an inquisitor's hand closed on her shoulder, and she let it, biding. "The pull. I feel it too, you know. Every day of my reign. That's all ambition is, in the end—a soul that knows exactly where its missing piece is kept, and walks toward it forever."

"That's not ambition," Wren said. "That's hunger wearing its shoes."

Kestrian laughed—a real laugh, brief and human and horrible. "Little ghost. You were sold before you drew breath. You never even got to *want* anything. And now you stand at the door of everything, and you tell me you don't want in?" He spread his dripping hands. "Look at it. LOOK AT IT."

Astrid looked.

And the cage looked back.

It happened the way it had been happening for days now, the boundary wearing thinner: the hum of the ten thousand resolved, all at once, into distinct voices. A woman counting her children's names like beads, over and over, because repetition was the only shape left to her. A soldier reciting a promise to a farm he'd never reached. An echo-singer holding one perfect note that had been her last act of defiance before the collectors came. A miner. A midwife. A boy who sounded, unbearably, like Ivo Tanzer might have sounded at seven. Ten thousand sentences, each interrupted at its best moment, each held mid-word for a century or more.

And beneath them all, threaded through the lattice like a signature through a page, Astrid felt their own soul. Not screaming. Worse—*waiting*. Patient as a held breath. It knew its owner had finally come downstairs.

*Take us,* it did not say, because souls don't speak. But it leaned. The whole cage leaned, a degree, a heartbeat's worth, and every voice inside brightened at once with terrible hope, and Astrid understood with total clarity that if they reached out—even halfway, even hesitantly—the cage would pour itself into them gladly. All of it. Every voice, using theirs as the door.

Power enough to kill Kestrian. Power enough to do *good* with it. Power enough to remake the free cities into something safe forever, because nothing unsafe could survive being spoken at.

The temptation was not that it was evil. The temptation was that it was *right*.

"I can end him," Astrid said aloud. Their voice cracked in the middle. "I can end him and free them after. Take it now, win the war today, break the cage tomorrow when it's safe. That's not greed. That's tactics."

"It's the same sentence they're trapped in," Sef said softly.

Astrid turned. The archivist had slid down the pillar to sitting, gray-faced, hands pressed flat to the tile as if reading it. "Mid-word, Astrid. That's what a cage is. *I'll free them after.* Every warden who ever locked a door told himself that. Your ancestor built this place telling himself that—he was going to use the power for good, the ledger says so in his own proud hand. Three hundred years later he's still telling himself that, and the sentence still hasn't ended." Sef looked up, and their eyes were wet and furious and kind. "You taught me that. On the roads. You said an oath isn't the words, it's the debt. Don't swear this one."

"The free cities voted to hunt you," Ivo said. His voice shook. He wasn't arguing; he was grieving ahead of time. "They sent hunters. If you take that power, they'll never ask you for anything again—they'll only ever obey, or plot, and either way you'll be alone on top of the world. I know what that costs. I watched my father pay it in a smaller country." He pushed himself upright, swaying. "You'd win everything, Astrid. And you'd never again be someone who could be trusted with nothing."

"Enough." Kestrian's patience had run its course; the layered voice crashed back in like a tide. "Sentiment is not a strategy. The mountain is dying around us, girl. When it goes, the cage breaks anyway—ungoverned, unwitnessed, ten thousand raw souls detonating under Thornfell and burying every living thing above it. Only a *hand* can open it safely. Mine or yours. Choose, or I choose for you, and I promise you my choosing involves drinking."

He raised one hand, and the nearest vein of vow-light bent toward him like a stalk of wheat toward the sun.

And that was when the shaking started—not the mountain's shaking. Astrid's.

Wren was rising.

Astrid felt it happen the way you feel a wave gather under a boat: a swell from somewhere deeper than muscle, pressure behind the eyes, the taste of iron and rain. For weeks the twin had surfaced in moments, in cadences, in sudden fierce opinions—and Astrid had learned to share, the way siblings learn to share a room. But this was different. This was Wren coming all the way up, taking the shared throat and the shared hands and the shared heart, and Astrid felt their own grip loosening and made the hardest choice of the night:

They let go.

*Say it,* Astrid offered, inward, outward, everywhere. *If you have the words, say them.*

And Wren—who had never drawn breath, who had been sold unnamed, erased, smudged, spent as fuel for a sister's magic before either of them could cry—Wren stepped forward into the space between the cage and Kestrian, wearing Astrid's body like a borrowed coat that fit perfectly, and spoke.

"No."

The word fell onto the tile of the ruined court and rang there. Not steel-speech. Not unsworn speech. Just a voice—one voice, small and absolute, the first fully-owned sentence of a life that had lasted a century inside someone else's.

Kestrian actually paused. Even now, even swollen with a thousand stolen vows, something in him recognized the rarity of it. "No?"

"No," Wren said again, steadier, tasting the word. "We don't want in. Do you hear me? We don't want *in*." They lifted Astrid's hand—not toward the cage, but toward the faces behind its bars, the glow, the ten thousand mid-word sentences. "I spent my whole existence being someone's fuel. Being the missing piece of other people's power. My sister's sword. Your crown. This mountain's ledger." The voice cracked, and it was Astrid's grief and Wren's grief in the same breath and neither could have said whose. "If we take it—if we open this cage by reaching *in*, if we drink even one mouthful—we're you. We're just you with better reasons. And I would rather be nobody, free, than somebody, full."

"Wren." Kestrian's voice dropped low, coaxing, horribly sincere. "You were never given a name until a month ago. You were never given *anything*. This is the only inheritance you will ever be offered. Take it, and no one can ever sell you again."

"That's why we won't take it," Wren said. "Because as long as power is the price of safety, *someone* will always be for sale. Always someone smaller. Always a twin, a miner, a brother—" and here, across the floor, Maren Duskwell flinched as if struck, "—someone whose whole life fits in a vial, priced by the pound. Break the arithmetic. Here. Now. With us standing in it."

Kestrian looked at Wren for a long moment.

Then he sighed, and it was the sigh of a man closing a ledger on a bad account. "Pity," he said. "I did offer."

He turned to his inquisitors. "Hold the swordspeaker's body. Carefully—the vessel must survive the transfer. If the ghost won't reach into the cage, then I'll reach into the *ghost*, and take what's mine by right of purchase. Aldric signed the sale. A signature doesn't expire."

Steel cleared leather in six places at once.

And Maren Duskwell moved first—not toward Astrid, but sideways, putting herself in the path of the two nearest inquisitors, her blade low, her voice carrying across the ruined court with the crisp authority of twenty years of command:

"The order stands rescinded. By me. Right here, right now, before witnesses." Her eyes found Astrid's—or Wren's; by then there was no telling. "Whatever you're going to do," she said, "do it fast."

* * *

Later—though it could only have been seconds—Astrid surfaced inside their own body again, and found Wren still there, shoulder to shoulder, the way two people stand at a door they mean to open together.

Around them the court had become a single ringing collision: Maren against her former inquisitors, Ivo swinging a broken stave with his good arm and a smuggler's dirty cunning, Sef shouting coordinates and weaknesses in archivist's shorthand, and beyond them all Kestrian striding for the cage with his arms spread wide, laughing, ready to drink the whole well if he couldn't be handed the cup.

There was no plan left. There was no revocation clause memorized, no ritual, no clever archivist's door. There was only the cage, and the choice, and the two of them.

*Together?* Astrid asked.

*Together,* said Wren. And then, with the ghost of a grin Astrid recognized as their grandmother's: *On three.*

They raised their joined hand—not toward the cage's lock, not toward its script, but toward the keystone-anchor where three centuries of binding flowed into stone.

And they spoke, with one voice made of two, the smallest, oldest, most dangerous word any of them had ever said:

"*Open.*"

The cage heard.

The whole mountain heard.

And ten thousand voices, held mid-word for three hundred years, finally, gloriously, began to finish their sentences.
