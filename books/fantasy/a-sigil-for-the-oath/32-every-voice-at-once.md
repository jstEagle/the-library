## 32. Every Voice at Once

The throne-cage hung in the heart of the mountain like a wasp nest made of light.

Ten thousand cells of glass and bone, each one holding a voice — Astrid could see them now, truly see them, the way they had never been able to see anything before. The vow-light that Kestrian drank poured off the cage in slow rivers, and inside every cell something pressed against the walls: a face, a name, a half-finished sentence. A woman with river-mud on her hands. A boy no older than eight, mouthing a word over and over that might have been *mother* or might have been *mercy*. An old man whose mouth moved without sound because he had been sold so long ago he had forgotten how loudness worked.

Astrid stood at the foot of the dais with their sword — their useless, oath-dead sword — still drawn, and understood for the first time what the Halloran oath had always been.

Not power. Plumbing.

Their whole life, every vow spoken into steel, every memory paid out like coin, had been a tap screwed into the side of this cage. The magic had never been theirs. It had been stolen goods passing through them, laundered through the family bloodline, and somewhere below their ribs, behind a wall of sold memory, the twin who should have lived their life beside them had been the price of admission.

*Wren,* Astrid thought, and felt the thought answered — not in words, but in pressure, like a hand pressing palm-to-palm against the inside of their chest.

Kestrian stood on the far side of the chamber, and he was barely a man anymore.

The cannon's blast had torn the throne-cage open along one seam, and raw vow-light spilled from the wound like marrow from a cracked bone. He had waded into it. He was drinking it straight, both hands raised, mouth open, and the light went into him and made him *more* — broader, taller, his shadow thrown huge and wrong across the cavern walls — and less, all at once. His skin had gone translucent at the edges. Through the skin of his throat Astrid could see the vows moving, hundreds of them, thousands, coiling in him like eels in a barrel. Stolen courage. Stolen grief. Stolen promises made by people who had died centuries ago trusting that a spoken word meant something.

"Look at me," Kestrian said, and his voice came from everywhere, from every blade he'd ever confiscated, resonating off the iron in the walls. "I am every oath ever sworn in Vaelmoor. I am the loyalty of ten thousand soldiers and the love of every bride who ever said *always*. What are you?"

"Astrid Halloran," Sef said quietly, at Astrid's shoulder. Blood ran down her temple from the climb; she didn't wipe it away. "And that's answer enough."

Ivo stood on her other side, holding a shard of broken stair-iron like a knife, breathing hard, alive, impossibly, after the fall. Maren Duskwell was somewhere behind them in the smoke with her sword turned against her own order, and the mountain groaned around all of them like a ship going down.

The seam in the cage split wider.

*Now,* Wren said — and it wasn't a suggestion, it was a decision, arriving whole the way a stone arrives at the bottom of a cliff. *Now, while it's breaking. Before he drinks it all.*

"If we break it," Astrid whispered, and their breath shook, "we lose everything. The soul. The swordspeech. All of it. We'll be—"

"We'll be *us*," Wren said. "One of us. Small. Unwritten." A pause, and then, gentler than Astrid had ever heard them: "You've spent your whole life paying for your words, sister. Speak one for free."

Across the chamber, Kestrian laughed — a sound with too many voices in it, a choir forced to laugh in unison. "Come then, last swordspeaker! Come take back your little piece of me. I'll swallow you too. I've swallowed kingdoms' worth of—"

He reached into the breach in the cage.

And the mountain finally lost patience.

* * *

It began as a sound Astrid had heard only once before — on the night their grandmother died, when the dying woman's last held breath left her body all at once. Ten thousand voices exhaled together. The cage did not shatter so much as *surrender*, every cell failing in the same instant, glass and bone and binding-script coming apart like frost off a windowpane in sunlight.

The freed voices hit the chamber like a flood hitting a valley.

They were not gentle. Astrid had imagined liberation would sound like music, like choirs, like peace — but these were voices that had been held under for a century, some for three hundred years, and they came up screaming, singing, cursing, laughing, weeping, all at once, in every language the free roads had ever carried. The noise was not heard so much as *endured*, a pressure that flattened the air, that pressed Astrid to their knees with their hands over ears that were useless, because the voices weren't coming through the ears at all anymore. They were coming through everything. Through the stone. Through the blood. Through the place where Wren lived.

Through *them*.

Sef was shouting something — Astrid saw her mouth move, saw Ivo drag her back from the flood of light pouring across the floor — but no sound reached Astrid. There was no room in the world for any other sound. The mountain filled with the freed dead and the freed taken and the freed sold-by-the-pound, and every single one of them, in the first wild instant of having a self again, looked for someone to blame.

They found Kestrian.

He stood hip-deep in the flood with his arms still raised to drink, and for the first time since the Hollow Stair, the usurper of Vaelmoor looked afraid.

"No," he said. "No — you're *mine* — I bought — I have receipts, I have *warrants*, I have the law of—"

The voices closed over him like water over a dropped stone.

What happened next happened fast and slow at once. Fast, because it took less time than a heartbeat. Slow, because Astrid saw every part of it, the way you see everything in the moment you expect to die.

The stolen vows in Kestrian's body woke up.

Every promise he had ever drunk — every soldier's loyalty, every widow's faithfulness, every child's oath on a mother's grave — recognized its thief. Astrid watched them turn in him, watched the eels in the barrel become knives, watched the man who had consumed ten thousand oaths be claimed by ten thousand creditors in the same instant. His own stolen youth began to leave him the way heat leaves a corpse. The translucent edges of his skin grayed. His hair went white from the temples inward, then silver, then nothing. His fine coat hung off a frame that was bending, shrinking, desiccating, centuries arriving in a single breath like a debt called due.

He looked at Astrid across the flood. And there was a moment — Astrid would carry it, would choose to carry it — where the thing looking out of Kestrian's collapsing face was just a man. Old, terrified, hollowed by an appetite that had never once fed him. His lips moved.

*"More,"* he mouthed. Or maybe *"mother."* At that distance, at that end, they might have been the same word.

Then he came apart. Not dramatically. Crumbling, like chalk, like old bread, like everything a person is when every borrowed thing is taken back at once. What settled onto the floor of the Undercourt was a drift of ash in the shape of ambition, already scattering in the wind the freed voices made.

The chamber went quiet in stages, like a storm walking away.

* * *

But it wasn't finished.

The voices filled the mountain, and they needed somewhere to go, and the only speaker standing at the center of them — the only throat in Thornfell with an oath-shaped hollow in it, the last tap in the last line of plumbing — was Astrid.

They rose to their feet without deciding to. The flood of the freed pressed in around them, and Astrid felt each voice find the shape of their selling, their binding, their theft — and then find, underneath it, the mark of the house that had built the tap. *Halloran,* the voices said, in ten thousand tones, some grateful, most not. *Halloran. Halloran. Yours is the mouth. Yours is the door.*

"I don't have any power," Astrid said aloud, and heard their voice crack apart in the vastness. "The oath's dead. I broke it. There's nothing left to speak *with*—"

*There's us,* said Wren — and Wren was not alone anymore. Behind Wren, behind Astrid's own fractured heart, stood every voice in the mountain, patient as weather, waiting for the door to be opened or kept shut. *We can't unmake him ourselves. He drank us; we're tangled in what he became. But a word freely given — a word with no price attached, no sale behind it, no stolen soul under it — we can stand behind a word like that. If you'll say it. If you'll let us through.*

"And what does it cost?" Astrid asked, because they were their grandmother's grandchild, and cost was the first language they'd ever learned.

*Nothing,* Wren said. *That's the point. That's the whole point. It costs nothing, and that's why it will work. Everything he built was bought. Nothing bought can survive a gift.*

Astrid looked back once — at Sef, on her knees in the drifting light, watching with the expression of an archivist seeing a text resolve itself; at Ivo, gripping his iron shard, tears cutting clean lines down his dusty face; at the breach in the world where Kestrian had been.

Then they opened their mouth, and let the mountain speak through them.

The word was not in any language Astrid knew, and yet they knew it perfectly, the way you know your own name. It was the first word ever spoken into steel, recovered now from the bottom of a century of theft. It meant, as nearly as any living tongue could hold it: *unmade, and forgiven, and unmade again.* It meant: *what was taken returns to its owner.* It meant: *no.*

Astrid said it.

The mountain said it with them — ten thousand voices, one word, and the word went out through the stone of Thornfell and up through its roots and into every confiscated blade in every vault in Vaelmoor, into every oath Kestrian's regime had ever bound, into the cannon itself, which sighed apart into scrap on the stair above. The usurper's works unwove themselves like a knot shown its own pattern. Somewhere far overhead, Astrid knew, the vaults were emptying, the oath-blades falling dark and honest and ordinary, mere steel at last.

When the word finished, the silence afterward was the largest thing in the world.

Astrid stood in the middle of it and waited to feel the power arrive — the reward, the harvest, the wages. They had been paid in memory their whole life; some animal part of them braced for the bill.

Nothing came.

Nothing was *taken*, either. No memory tore loose. No name went missing from inside them. Wren was still there, quiet now, settled — a presence leaning against the inside of Astrid's ribs like a sibling sharing a blanket, watching the same view. The sword in Astrid's hand was just a sword. Their head was just their head, full of holes, full of two lives braided badly together, full of grief and dust and the strange lightness of a debt discharged.

"Halloran," Sef said hoarsely, from very far away, getting closer. "Astrid. Can you hear me? The mountain's coming down."

She was right. Above them, through the wound the cannon had torn, the roots of Thornfell groaned and shed stone. Dust fell upward past them, toward the center, toward the emptied throne. Freed voices were streaming out of the chamber in bright rivers, following the old paths of the Hollow Stair, flowing up and out toward a sky none of them had seen in a hundred years.

Astrid sheathed the sword — their sword, no one else's, good for cutting bread and nothing holier — and turned to Sef and Ivo, and found they were smiling, though their face was wet.

"Yeah," they said. "I hear you." They took Sef's arm, and Ivo took theirs, and behind them the throne of ten thousand stood empty and dark, a cage with no birds, a debt with no debtor. "Let's not be here when it finishes falling."

They climbed.

And the mountain, emptying itself of everything it had ever swallowed, let them go.
