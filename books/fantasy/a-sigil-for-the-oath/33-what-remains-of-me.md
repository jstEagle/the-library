## 33. What Remains of Me

The first thing I knew was the taste of dust and candle smoke, thick on my tongue like a coin held too long in the mouth. The second thing was the silence — not the silence of an empty room but the silence after a choir stops, the air still ringing with everything it had held.

I was lying on my back under a slab of green-veined stone, and I could not remember how I had come to be beneath it.

That should have frightened me more than it did. Once — a lifetime ago, which is to say three days ago — I would have reached for a memory to steady myself, some anchor from before: my grandmother's kitchen, the smell of tallow and iron, the first vow I ever spoke into steel. But the shelves inside me had been ransacked. Whole rooms stood empty. Others held furniture that belonged to no house I recognized — a red-haired woman laughing over a loom, salt wind on a cliff I'd never climbed, a name repeated softly in the dark: *Wren, Wren, come inside now.*

Those were Wren's. I knew that now the way you know your own hand in bad light — by shape, not certainty.

I pushed at the slab. It didn't move.

*Hello,* said something quiet inside me, in a cadence that was mine and not mine, the way a sister's voice is your mother's voice and entirely her own. *Don't panic. We're pinned.*

*We,* I thought back, and felt Wren's presence settle closer, unhurried, like someone pulling up a chair. Not a passenger anymore. Not a fire burning through me. Just — there. A second heartbeat with opinions.

*You broke the cage,* Wren said. *I helped. You were very dramatic about it.*

*I don't remember being dramatic.*

*No. You wouldn't.* A pause, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. *It cost. Everything costs here. But we're alive, and he isn't, so I'd call it a fair trade even if the accounting hurts.*

I tried to take stock of myself the way Sef had taught me to take stock of a manuscript: what survives, what's water-damaged, what's been cut out with a razor. My body reported in piecemeal — ribs bruised, one ankle wrong, left hand scraped raw from climbing shattered stair. My mind was worse. I reached for Kestrian's face, the way he'd swelled with drunk vow-light until his skin went translucent as parchment held to flame, and found only an impression: *large, hungry, falling*. I reached for Maren Duskwell turning her blade on her own inquisitors, and got a flash of white cloaks and then nothing, a hole shaped exactly like a betrayal redeemed.

But other things remained, absurdly intact. The way smugglers whistle when they think no one's listening. The exact weight of a good whetstone in the palm. Grandmother Halloran's dying whisper — *say it true or don't say it* — still lodged behind my teeth like a seed.

And underneath all of it, where swordspeech used to live, there was nothing. Not emptiness exactly. More like a room after the fire's gone out: warm ash, the shape of where the heat had been.

I tried, experimentally, silently, to speak a vow into the stone above me. *Open.*

Nothing answered. The stone stayed stone. For the first time since I was eleven years old, kneeling before my grandmother while she bled out on the flagstones and made me swear, the world declined to obey me.

I laughed. It hurt my ribs, and I laughed anyway, and Wren laughed with me, and the sound came out strange — two people laughing through one mouth, harmonizing with themselves.

*Well,* Wren said. *At least we're not boring.*

* * *

They dug me out by lamplight. I heard them before I saw them — Sef's voice, hoarse and lecturing, the way it always went when they were frightened: "—told you the load-bearing sigil was on the east column, nobody listens to archivists, this is why civilizations fall—" and then Ivo Tanzer's younger voice cutting through: "Less history, more lifting."

A wedge of darkness shifted. Lamplight poured through the gap and fell across my face, and I flinched from it like something exhumed.

"Astrid." Sef's face appeared upside down above me, gray-streaked hair hanging loose from its knot, cheeks streaked with soot and worse. Their eyes did the thing they always did — catalogued me in one sweep, top to bottom, checking for damage the way they checked a folio for mold. "Oh thank every unwritten page. You're breathing."

"Barely," said Ivo, wriggling into the gap beside them. He looked terrible — a gash above his eyebrow crusted black, his fine council delegate's coat ruined beyond any tailor's redemption, hands blistered from climbing. He looked, I thought, happier than I had ever seen him. "Don't move. There's rubble on your legs that we need to negotiate with, and it's not in a talking mood."

"It never is," I rasped. My voice sounded like gravel dragged through honey. "How long?"

"Nobody knows," Sef said. "Time's been behaving badly since the cage broke. Hours? A day? The Market's clocks all stopped at different times, which either means catastrophe or Tuesday." They wedged their shoulders under the slab. "On three. And Astrid — don't push with magic."

"I can't," I said.

Sef went very still. Even in the bad light I saw the word land on them like weather. They didn't say anything — Sef never wasted words on things already understood — just adjusted their grip and counted, and Ivo heaved, and the slab ground up and away with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and then there were hands under my arms and the cold open dark of the ruined Market all around me.

I had seen it whole, once, on the way down: a bazaar of glass and bone-light, ten thousand vials of memory glowing like trapped fireflies, merchants of the dead calling their prices in whispers. Now half of it was rubble. The ceiling had cracked along its spine and let in a slow drift of pale dust that fell through the lamplight like snow that couldn't decide to land. Vials lay broken everywhere, their contents spilled — and where a memory spilled, it played itself out against the floor in ghostlight. A child learning to skip stones. An old man's wedding day, flickering sideways, the bride's face worn away. A dog. Someone had sold the memory of a dog, and now the dog ran in circles through the wreckage forever, chasing a stick no one would throw.

I sat among the ruins of the market of voices, and for a long time I couldn't do anything but breathe.

"Here." Ivo pressed a water flask into my hands. His fingers were shaking. So were mine. We drank like it was a competition.

"The others?" I managed. "Maren? The bound voices — did they—"

"Gone up," Sef said quietly. They lowered themselves onto a fallen merchant's stall beside me, wincing, and I realized with a small shock that Sef was old, actually old, and had climbed a mountain's skeleton twice in three days for me. "All of them. When the cage shattered, they went through the crack in the ceiling like — like sparks up a chimney. You'll see."

"See?"

"You're under Thornfell's crown right now. The whole sky's doing something it shouldn't." Ivo's voice had the careful brightness of someone holding grief and wonder in the same hand. "Come look. When you can stand. No rush. Well—" he glanced at the settling rubble, "—some rush. But a kind rush."

*He's terrified,* Wren observed. *They both are. They keep looking at you like you might not be you.*

*Am I?*

Wren considered this with the seriousness they brought to everything, which was considerable. *More than you've ever been. Less than you were yesterday. Both of those are good.*

I tried to stand. Failed. Tried again with Ivo's shoulder under my arm and made it upright, and the three of us picked our way through the spilled memories toward the great fissure in the Market's roof, stepping carefully around the running dog, who paused to look at us with borrowed eyes full of borrowed joy before resuming its endless chase.

* * *

The climb to the fissure took most of an hour and cost me two rests, a moment of vertigo so complete I forgot Sef's name mid-sentence — called them "archivist" like a stranger, and watched the hurt cross their face before they smoothed it away with professional grace — and one long stop where I simply sat down on a broken stair and shook, because I had reached for my mother's face again, the memory I'd paid at the river ford to buy our escape, and confirmed what I already knew: gone. Sold to the dark. Spent on a bridge and a running start.

"She had your chin," Sef said quietly. They had settled beside me without asking, the way they always did, offering proximity instead of pity. "I've seen the portrait in the Halloran papers. She had your chin and Ivo's terrible posture."

"That's specific."

"I'm an archivist. Specificity is the only prayer I know." They were quiet a moment. "When we get somewhere with walls and ink, I'll write down everything anyone remembers about her. Every anecdote. Ivo's met her cousins; they gossip beautifully. We can rebuild her out of other people's recollections. It won't be the original manuscript." Their jaw tightened. "But it will be a faithful copy. That matters. I've staked my entire disgraced life on copies mattering."

I looked at them — this exile who had read forbidden pages aloud and lost everything, who had hunted me across three years of roads for reasons they'd confessed with shaking hands, whose loyalty I had doubted and doubted again and doubted wrongly — and I found, searching for gratitude, something larger and stranger. Recognition. We were the same kind of ruined thing, Sef and I. Keepers of texts that had cost more than they promised.

"Sef," I said. "The Codex. My name was in it. Tonight's date, the stolen soul, all of it."

"Yes."

"What does it say now?"

They smiled — small, exhausted, and lit from underneath by something I hadn't seen in them before. Hope, maybe. Or its stubborn cousin.

"Ask me tomorrow," they said. "When I've dared to look."

* * *

We came up through the fissure onto a ledge of raw granite, and the sky over Thornfell was on fire, and it was beautiful, and I started to cry.

Not the aurora of the far north, though it moved like one — curtains of green and violet and gold streaming up from the mountain's shattered crown and unfurling across the whole night sky, rippling, folding, brightening and dimming in rhythms too deliberate to be weather. As I watched, the lights pulsed in patterns that almost resolved — spirals, then branching lines like a family tree, then, unmistakably, briefly, a wave: the kind you make with your hand. Ten thousand hands. Ten thousand voices saying *hello, hello, hello* to a sky they'd been locked out of for a century.

"They're going up," Ivo whispered. He had his hat off. I hadn't seen him take it off; he must have done it on the climb, out of respect, ahead of time. "Since the mountain cracked. One by one, or in bunches. Some of them linger. Look — there, the gold one, it's been circling the summit for hours. Sef thinks it's looking for someone."

"Or waiting to be sure," Sef said. "Some debts need witnessing before they release."

I stood on the ledge with my ruined body and my ransacked memory and watched souls leave the world's cellar through its new skylight, and I understood that I was watching what my whole life had been spent to prevent and preserve at once — the oath that made me, the oath I broke, the power I refused because refusing it was the only use of it that wasn't theft.

*Do you regret it?* Wren asked. Not challenging. Genuinely asking, the way family asks at the end of hard days.

I took inventory one last time. Swordspeech: gone. Half my childhood: gone, or Wren's, or traded for strangers' lives. Mother's face: gone, though Sef would build me a copy. Grandmother's voice: kept. The weight of a whetstone: kept. Two friends digging me out of a collapsed hell: kept. A sibling sharing my skull, quiet as a cat in a sunbeam: kept.

*No,* I told Wren, and meant it down to the ash. *Ask me again when I try to cook something and burn it, and I may revise.*

Wren laughed, and this time it came out of my own mouth unbidden — a short, startled bark of laughter that made Ivo jump and Sef turn.

"Sorry," I said, wiping my face. "That was — there are two of us in here now. You'll have to get used to the acoustics."

Sef studied me with those cataloguing eyes, and whatever they saw, they nodded slowly, the way they nodded over a text whose provenance finally checked out. "Two names," they said. "One road. I'll want to write all of it down, eventually. When you're ready. Nothing written until it's yours to give."

"Everything here is priced," I quoted softly.

"Not between us," Sef said. "That was always the heresy. That's why they burned my library." They looked up at the streaming lights, and their voice went rough. "Free transmission. Free memory. Free voices. The whole underground economy of the soul, undone by one stubborn person refusing to spend the last coin."

"I had help."

"You had a lot of help," Ivo agreed. Then he winced, pressing a hand to the gash above his eye. "Speaking of which — someone should probably check whether Maren made it out. And whether the council hunters below us know the war's over, because Ivo-the-delegate has some very awkward testimony to give, and Ivo-the-corpse would give considerably less of it."

Above us, the aurora pulsed — and then changed.

The slow, waving hellos stopped. All at once, every curtain of light bent inward, streaming down toward the summit like iron filings snapping toward a lodestone, gathering, condensing, growing brighter and brighter until the whole sky seemed to pour itself into a single point above the broken peak. The air hummed against my teeth. The granite under my boots vibrated with something that was almost — almost — language.

*Sword,* Wren breathed, and for the first time since waking, their presence flared, urgent, reaching. *Astrid. Something's coming down. Or coming home.*

The light gathered. Held. And spoke.
