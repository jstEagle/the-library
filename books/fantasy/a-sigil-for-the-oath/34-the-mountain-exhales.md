## 34. The Mountain Exhales

Maren Duskwell had spent eleven years learning to read the sky the way other people read ledgers — for what it owed, for what it concealed, for the exact moment a debt came due. So when the mountain above them began to glow, she read it the only way she knew how.

It was not lightning. She had called lightning down on three villages and knew its signature: the flat white flash, the smell of copper and scorched thatch, the way the world went silent a half-second before the thunder confessed. This was nothing like that. This light came from *inside* the rock, seeping up through a thousand fissures like marrow lit from within, and then it rose — rose out of Thornfell's shattered crown in slow ribbons, green and gold and a violet so deep it looked like the sky had been wounded, and it unspooled across the heavens in banners a mile wide.

The freed voices, Sef had called them, back when they were still a theory in a marginalia-stained book. Now they were weather.

Maren stood in the mouth of the Hollow Stair with ash in her hair and her brother's name caught in her throat, and she watched the aurora pour over the peak, and she understood, with the cold clarity that had made her the youngest inquisitor-general in the usurper's history, that the world had just changed its ledger. And that she was standing on the wrong side of the balance line.

Beside her, one of her own inquisitors — Vess, a solid, unimaginative man she had trusted with a hundred ugly orders — made a small sound. Not a gasp. A *recoil*.

"That's not natural," he said. "That's the work of the traitor. That's the swordspeaker's doing. General, we should—"

"We should," Maren said, "shut up and watch."

He shut up. They all did. Forty inquisitors on the mountainside, forty pairs of eyes lifted to the light, and not one of them — not Vess, not the hard-bitten veterans of the Vaelmoor purges, not the young ones who still polished their commission seals like talismans — not one of them could look away, and not one of them could say what they were seeing was evil. The light was too beautiful for the word to fit in their mouths. Maren watched them realize it, one face at a time, the way frost creeps across a window: the understanding that they had spent years hunting a woman who could make *this*, and calling it treason.

Her brother's voice was still in her throat. It had been in her throat since the Stair, since she'd heard it among the bound ones — Tam, sold to the Undercourt at fourteen for the crime of humming in the presence of a tax collector's wife, his voice one of ten thousand caged in the throne beneath the mountain. She had spent nine years telling herself he had died of the fever, had buried an empty coffin, had wept at the grave with proper and proportionate grief. And he had not died. He had been *priced*. By the pound, the dying miner had said, and Maren had put a blade through the man's door in fury before she'd understood the man was telling the truth.

Now the light was full of voices. She could hear them, faintly, the way you hear a crowd through a cathedral wall — not words, but the texture of them, the vast plural hum of people who had been held and were not held anymore. Somewhere in that hum, if she listened — and she could not stop listening — Tam was singing. He had always sung. He had sung while he chopped wood, while he mended nets, while their mother scolded him, and Maren had forgotten, in nine years of inquisition work, that a voice could be an act of joy rather than evidence.

The aurora reached its widest spread, a canopy of green fire from horizon to horizon, and beneath it the mountain exhaled — a long, tectonic sigh, dust rising off the slopes in silver sheets, and Maren felt it pass over her like the breath of something enormous that had finally been allowed to sleep.

"General." Vess again, quieter now. "The capital will have seen this. The Regent's— Kestrian's spires have watchers on the towers. They'll know the mountain fell."

"Kestrian won't be told anything," Maren said. "Kestrian is a smear on the floor of a cavern two miles under our feet. I watched him age four hundred years in the time it takes to sneeze." She turned to face her forty inquisitors, and she saw the question in all of their faces, the question they were all too disciplined to ask: *what do we do now, with no one left to give us orders?*

It was, she reflected, the first honest question anyone had asked her in years.

She unhooked her commission seal from her belt.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of the Market of Voices, because the Hollow Stair had collapsed behind them and the only way out was down and through, and because Maren refused to climb back up into the light until she had decided what she was carrying out of the dark.

The Market had burned. Kestrian's breach had seen to that — the oath-cannon's first shot had turned three rows of stalls into slag, and the fire had done the rest. But fire, Maren noted as she walked the ash-choked avenues, had not destroyed the Market so much as *opened* it. Glass vials that had held memories for sale lay shattered by the thousand, and their contents had not died with the vessels. The memories had evaporated into the air of the cavern, and now the whole ruined bazaar was haunted by them — little weather systems of other people's lives. A gust of someone's first snowfall drifted down one lane. The smell of bread, sharp and specific, rose from a cracked case near a burned awning, and a young inquisitor named Pell stood in it with tears running silently down his face, and when he saw Maren watching, he didn't wipe them away.

"I can smell my grandmother's kitchen," he said. "It's not even mine. It's some dead miner's grandmother. General, why does it feel like mine?"

"Because it was sold to someone who never got to have it," Maren said. "And now it's free, and it doesn't care whose it was." She looked down the long avenue of ash and glass. "That's what we were guarding, Pell. All those years. We were the lock on a door that kept this from happening."

Pell said nothing. But he took off his commission seal, the way you take off a hat in a church, and set it on the ashes, and walked on bareheaded.

Maren did not take hers off yet. Not yet. There was an order to things, and she had spent her whole life inside that order, and even when the order was monstrous she had moved through it precisely, because precision was the only integrity available to a person inside a machine. You could not stop the machine. But you could decide, at every single juncture, whether your part of it was true.

Her part had stopped being true the day she'd filed no report. The echo-singer in the cells below Vaelmoor, the one who had recited Kestrian's true orders in her flat, ruined voice — *when the swordspeaker opens the mountain, raze the free cities; leave no harbor, no council, no name* — Maren had sat with that transcript for one full night, and in the morning she had written *nothing of operational value* in the report line, and she had known, signing it, that she had just become a traitor. It had felt, at the time, like the smallest and most private treason in the world. A blank line on a page. No one would ever know.

Now the whole sky knew. The whole sky was *made* of it.

She found the swordspeaker near the burned chapel — the drowned one, though the water had drained when the mountain cracked, leaving the pews standing in black mud like the teeth of something patient. Astrid Halloran sat on the chapel steps, wrapped in a blanket, looking like a woman who had been unwritten and was waiting to see what got written back. Sef the archivist crouched beside her with the Vellum Codex open on their knees, and a young man — the council delegate, Tanzer, the one who'd deserted the hunt — was trying to feed her soup she didn't appear to notice.

Maren walked down the aisle of mud, and every step of it felt like walking toward her own trial.

"Swordspeaker," she said.

Astrid looked up. Her eyes were strange — not empty, not full, but *shared*, as if two people were looking out through one face and had agreed to take turns. "Inquisitor-General," she said. "You turned your blade on your own people in the cavern. I saw it."

"I did."

"Why?"

It was not an accusation. That was the terrible thing — Astrid asked it the way a physician asks *where does it hurt*, and Maren found that the answer came out of her without permission, the way Tam's voice had come out of the cage.

"Because my brother was in that cage," she said. "Because Kestrian's collectors sold him when he was fourteen, and I spent nine years working for the man who priced him, and because when the cannon fired I understood that there was no version of my life in which I stayed loyal to that and remained a person. That's all. It's not virtue. I want that on the record. I didn't have a revelation. I had a *bill come due*."

Astrid studied her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "The record. You mean you intend there to be one."

"I intend to make one," Maren said. "In Harrowgate. In front of the council, and the tribunals, and anyone else who'll listen. Everything. The razing order. The plague — Kestrian's own alchemists brewed it, I can name the laboratory and the men who ran it. The refugee column I was ordered to burn, and the fact that I obeyed." Her voice did not shake. She had trained it not to, and she hated it a little, that even her confession would be delivered in the voice of a functionary. "I'm going to surrender my commission on the steps of the Council Hall and testify against the regime I served, and then they can do with me what they like. I'm not asking for your forgiveness, swordspeaker. I'm telling you so you hear it from me before you hear it from a crier."

Sef looked up from the Codex with an expression Maren couldn't read — the archivist had been exiled for reading forbidden pages aloud, and there was something in their face now that recognized a woman about to do the same thing with her own life.

Astrid said only: "The council sent hunters after me. They may not receive you gently."

"No," Maren agreed. "They may not. That's rather the point."

* * *

The road to Harrowgate took nine days, and the world came apart and reassembled itself along every mile of it.

They saw it first in the villages: people standing in their doorways at dusk, looking north toward Thornfell, where the aurora still burned — weaker now, a green thread rather than a banner, but *there*, persistent, like a lamp left in a window by someone who intended to come home. The free cities had spent three years under Kestrian's shadow telling themselves the mountain was only a mountain. Now it was a signal fire that would not go out, and every peasant who squinted at it understood, in the wordless way of people who have lived under occupation, that somewhere the thing they had been afraid of had died.

The roads filled. Not with armies — with *witnesses*. Farmers who had seen inquisitor columns pass. Women whose sons had been taken for the tithe of voices. Old men who remembered the free councils and had kept the old civic seals buried in their gardens, and who now dug them up and wore them openly, on strings around their necks, like amulets against the return of the dark. By the fifth day, Maren's forty inquisitors — thirty-one now; nine had slipped away in the night, and she had not stopped them, had in fact marked their names in her book with a small note: *chose to run rather than choose* — were being fed by villages they had once taxed. It was the most humiliating thing she had ever endured, and she endured it with her spine straight and her seal still on her belt, because she had decided it would come off in Harrowgate, publicly, or not at all. A private renunciation was just desertion with better grammar.

On the eighth day, at the crossroads town of Ferrow's Bend, they found the refugee column.

Or what was left of it. This was the column Maren had been ordered to burn as bait for the swordspeaker — the order she had obeyed, with her clean and efficient hatred, three weeks and one lifetime ago. She had done it from the air, from a drake-lift, with incendiary lances, and she had told herself the standard things: it was a legitimate target, it carried smugglers, the deaths were on the usurper's ledger and not hers. She had filed the report. She had slept that night.

Now she walked the length of it on foot. The survivors had buried their dead in rows along the roadside, cairns of fieldstone, hundreds of them, each marked with whatever the bereaved had had — a scrap of cloth, a carved name, a child's shoe on top of one. The town had planted a wooden post at the head of the rows, and someone had burned letters into it with a poker, and the letters said:

*THEY DIED AS BAIT. REMEMBER THE ORDER, NOT THE FIRE.*

Maren stood before the post for a long time. Vess came to stand beside her, and she was aware of her inquisitors behind her, and of the villagers watching from their doorways, and she understood that this was the moment — not Harrowgate, not the Council Hall, but here, at a burned fieldstone field, in front of the people the order had been *about*.

She took off her commission seal.

It was heavier than she remembered it being. Eleven years of wearing it and she had never once felt its weight, and now it dragged her hand down like an anchor. She held it up so the villagers could see it — the black iron disc, the crossed quill and sword, the Regent's sigil that was now no one's sigil at all.

"On the third of Frostmonth," she said, in the voice she used for official pronouncements, the voice that carried across parade grounds, "I, Maren Duskwell, Inquisitor-General of the Vaelmoor Commission, received a written order to burn this column. I obeyed that order. I am the reason these stones are here." She let the silence hold. "I am going to Harrowgate to testify to this and to everything else, under whatever law the free cities choose to revive, and I will accept whatever they decide I am owed. I am not asking you to forgive me. I am asking you to *come* — come to Harrowgate, all of you, and watch, and make them do it in the open. Accountability that happens in a back room is just a quieter version of the crime."

For a moment, nothing. Then an old woman came out of the nearest doorway, walked to the post, and touched the burned letters once, gently, the way you'd touch a grave.

"We'll come," she said. "We'll bring the list of names."

By morning, the column of inquisitors marching on Harrowgate had grown into a procession — inquisitors walking bareheaded at the front, and behind them three hundred villagers carrying the cairn-lists, and behind them more, joining at every crossroads, the way tributaries join a river that has finally decided which sea it's going to.

Maren marched at the front with her brother's name in her throat and her empty belt, and she thought: *this is what it costs, and this is what it buys, and I have finally, at thirty-four years old, made a trade I can account for.*

* * *

They could see Harrowgate's walls from the ridge on the ninth day, and they could see, gathered before its gates, the thing Maren had known was coming and had marched toward anyway: the council's militia, drawn up in formation, pikes leveled — not at the procession, but *ready*, uncertain, the militia of a city that had sent hunters after a swordspeaker and was now watching that swordspeaker's enemy walk up its road wearing the face of penance.

At the gate stood the council's marshal, an old campaigner named Brakkus whom Maren had drunk under a table twice at a peace conference that now felt like it had happened to someone else. He looked at the procession — the villagers, the cairn-lists, the bareheaded inquisitors, the woman at the front — and his face went through several calculations and arrived somewhere between disbelief and dread.

"Duskwell," he said. "You have a lot of nerve."

"I have a lot of testimony," Maren said. "The nerve is incidental." She stopped before the gates, and behind her the procession stopped, three hundred voices going quiet in the cold morning air. "Marshal, I surrender my commission to the free cities of the Harrowgate Compact — the lawful authority it was never actually sworn to, which is precisely the problem. I request, formally, that I be taken into custody pending tribunal. And I request that the tribunal be public, and that these people—" she gestured at the villagers with their lists of names, "—be given standing to speak. Not as petitioners. As *prosecutors*."

Brakkus looked past her at the crowd, at the wooden post they'd carried the whole way — they had taken the post itself, the burned-letter post, eight men bearing it like a relic — and something in the old marshal's jaw changed.

"The council will have to vote," he said.

"Then let them vote in the open," Maren said. "That's the whole point. That's the *only* point."

They took her into custody — gently, which she had not expected, and which she distrusted more than chains. They took her seal, and Brakkus held it in his palm and looked at it a long time before dropping it into an evidence box, and Maren thought: *there it is. Eleven years, in a box, with everything else the regime touched.* They gave her a cell with a window, which she understood was not mercy but theater — the council wanted the woman who had turned coat to be seen to be treated well, in case the wind blew the other way again. She sat on the cot and watched the light through the bars, and far to the north, over the mountains, the green thread of the aurora still burned.

On the second night, a guard — young, nervous, one of the ones who'd worn a civic seal under his uniform for a year — unlocked her door and said, "There's people asking after you, General. Not to hurt you. The ones from the burned column. They're camped outside the walls and the council can't make them leave and they're telling anyone who'll listen that you're the one who stopped to read the names." He shifted. "And there's a delegate. Tanzer. Says he's got documents for your tribunal. Says you'll want them."

Maren sat up. "Ivo Tanzer is in the city?"

"He came in an hour ago, half-dead off the mountain road, and walked straight into the council chamber, and ma'am—" the guard's voice dropped, and there was something in it that Maren recognized, the sound of a young man watching history decide which way to tip, "—he's got the razing order. The original. With the Regent's seal on it. And he's telling the council that the first witness they call should be you."

Maren looked out the window, north, at the green light over the mountains, and she felt the hairline along which her loyalty had cracked widen into an open door.

"Then tell the council," she said, "I'll be ready at dawn. And tell them to open the doors wide. The whole city is going to want to hear what we were ordered to do — and this time, no one is going to file it away."
