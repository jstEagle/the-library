## 35. Trials and Testimonies

The Hall of Concord in Harrowgate had been built to hold a thousand voices in argument. It had never been built to hold silence.

Ivo Tanzer stood at the witness rail and felt that silence pressing on him from all sides — the galleries packed shoulder to shoulder, the delegates' benches full for the first time since the war's end, even the clerks in their ink-stained alcoves leaning forward with quills poised and forgetting to write. Above the dais hung the Concord Banner, its nine cities stitched in silver thread, and beneath it, on a plain wooden chair, sat the first defendant: Quartermaster-General Ossen Vayle, who had signed the requisitions for the alchemist plague and then, when it burned through Vaelmoor's poor quarters, signed the orders to seal the doors behind it.

He looked smaller than his reputation. A grey man in a grey coat, hands folded, waiting.

"Delegate Tanzer." The presiding magistrate was an old woman named Corvel, her voice dry as ledger paper. "You have asked to speak before the charges are read. The council permits unusual things this season." A ripple that was almost laughter moved through the hall and died. "Speak."

Ivo's mouth was dry. He had rehearsed this on the road from Thornfell, in waystations, in the ruins of the Market where he'd helped dig Astrid out of the rubble with his bare hands bleeding. He had rehearsed it again last night until Sef told him — gently, the way archivists tell you a document has been misfiled — that if he said it one more time it would sound like recitation, and recitation is what liars use.

So he didn't say what he'd planned.

"I came here eight months ago," he began, "to argue that we should surrender one person to save ourselves. You'll find the transcript. I stood where I'm standing now and I said the prophecy was a gamble we couldn't afford, and that handing over the swordspeaker would buy us peace with Vaelmoor." He let himself look up at the galleries, at the faces. A woman held an infant against her shoulder; the child wore a scrap of black mourning-cloth, as half the city did. "I want that entered into the record too. Not because it flatters anyone. Because the same machinery that made me argue for that trade is still sitting in these benches, and if we don't name how it works today, we will build it again tomorrow."

Corvel's quill-hand paused. "Name it, then."

"It works by making the horror arrive already priced." Ivo turned toward Vayle. The old quartermaster watched him with the flat patience of a man who had audited worse. "No one in Kestrian's government ever ordered an atrocity. They ordered a *requisition*. A *containment protocol*. A *logistical adjustment*. Delegate Harl can confirm — he sat beside me when a courier delivered the razing order for the eastern refugee column, and it was titled 'Fuel Reallocation, Column Seven.' Seven hundred people walking out of the burning lowlands with everything they owned on their backs, and somewhere a clerk wrote *fuel*."

The silence deepened. Somewhere in the upper gallery a man began quietly to weep, and no one shushed him.

"You were not there," Corvel said. "At the column."

"No. I deserted before that. That's why I'm alive to testify." His voice caught and he hated it, and let it. "But I carried the order itself out of Vaelmoor. Sealed, countersigned, bearing the usurper's privy seal. I gave it to Magistrate Corvel's investigators six weeks ago. I'm asking the court to read the *title* aloud into the record. Just the title. Let everyone hear what genocide sounds like when it's been through the accounting office."

Corvel looked down at the document unrolled before her. For a long moment she did not touch it, as though it might still be warm.

"'Fuel Reallocation,'" she read, "‘Column Seven. Authorization for total expenditure. By command of Kestrian, First of His Name.'" She set the paper down as if it weighed more than the banner overhead. "Entered into record."

Ivo stepped back. His knees felt strange, loose, the way they had on the ice bridge when Sef went down and the world tilted. He had thought speaking the truth would feel like setting something down. Instead it felt like handing a live coal to a room full of people and trusting them not to drop it.

Vayle did not look at him. Vayle looked at his own folded hands, and Ivo realized, with a small cold shock, that the man was composing his defense already — and that the defense would be arithmetic. So many lives weighed against so many years of stability. And the terrible thing, the thing Ivo would carry out of the hall and into his dreams, was that the arithmetic was not stupid. It was only monstrous. Those were different failures, and the court needed to learn to tell them apart, or it would hang the monsters and promote the arithmeticians.

* * *

Sef testified on the fourth day.

They had dressed him in his old archivist's grey — someone had found the robe in an evidence locker, confiscated three years ago along with everything else he'd owned when they expelled him from the Codex tower — and he wore it like armor, or like an accusation. His hands shook when he took the oath to tell the truth. They steadied the moment he opened the satchel and lifted out the Vellum Codex.

Even from the delegate benches, Ivo could see the change that came over the hall. Everyone had heard of the book. Almost no one had seen it. It lay on the evidence table looking exactly like what it was: an old book, vellum gone the color of weak tea, corners soft with handling, utterly unremarkable except for the way the air around it seemed to hold its breath.

"State your name and former office," Corvel said.

"Sef Ardanis. Keeper of the Vellum Codex, third chair, Tower of Records, Vaelmoor. Formerly," he added, and something dry flickered under his voice, "before I read pages I was forbidden to read aloud."

"Describe the Codex for the record."

"It is a book of prophecies approximately one hundred and twelve years old, though portions may be older. Its authorship is unknown. Its binding is human skin — we confirmed this in my second year, and I wish the record to reflect that I objected to continuing to handle it after that confirmation, and was overruled." He said it evenly, but Ivo saw his knuckles whiten on the cover. "It writes itself. New entries appear in pigment consistent with the original inks, dated forward. Most entries concern dynastic matters, harvests, wars. Mundane futures. It is, in a sense, the most boring terrible object in the world."

"And its relevance to these proceedings?"

"The usurper consulted it." Sef turned a page with exaggerated care, showing the court a spread. Even at a distance Ivo could see the marginalia, cramped and furious. "These annotations are Kestrian's own hand — verified against his sealed correspondence. He was searching for the swordspeaker lineage. He believed, correctly, that the Halloran oath ran on a soul sold beneath Thornfell, and that whoever controlled that source controlled every vow ever sworn into steel. Every oath-blade in his vault. Every soldier's loyalty purchased through bound speech. He wasn't building a tyranny, Magistrate. He was harvesting one that had been growing underground for a century."

A murmur ran the length of the benches. Corvel struck it down with one look.

"You claim the Codex foretold events during the recent conflict."

"I claim better than that. I claim it foretold them accurately, dated to the night, including—" Sef stopped. Ivo watched him choose, deliberately, not to look at the place where Astrid sat among the honored witnesses: thin, scarred, half their memories sold or shattered, sitting very straight. "Including the date the swordspeaker's name appeared on its own page. The entry was written before the events it described. I handled the ink myself. I am prepared to swear to its antiquity and its accuracy both, which should be impossible, and which is precisely why the book was locked away."

"Then answer me this," Corvel said, and leaned forward, and the whole hall leaned with her. "If the future could be read — if it *was* read — why did it fail? Why does the book now end?"

Sef closed the Codex gently, like closing a door on a sickroom.

"Because it ends where choice begins," he said. "The prophecy required a soul to be reclaimed and a power to be wielded. Both were possible. Both were written. What was not written — what cannot be written — was refusal." He looked out across the hall, at the mourning-cloth, at the orphaned child, at the delegates who eight months ago had voted to hunt an innocent woman across the mountains. "You are all living in the margin the book couldn't fill. I suggest you take notes."

* * *

They convicted Vayle on the sixth day, and two of the seven alchemists, and the warden of the blade-vaults, and eleven officers whose names meant nothing to history and whose signatures meant everything to the dead. Four were sentenced to the wall. The rest to labor in the rebuilding of the lowlands they had burned — Corvel insisted on that, over the louder delegates' objections, saying that a state which only knew how to kill its criminals had learned nothing from the one it had just buried.

Kestrian himself there was nothing to try. What remained of him was a drift of ash in the ruined Market beneath Thornfell, and a name that mothers in the free cities had already begun using to frighten children, which Ivo privately thought was a second execution, and a deserved one.

On the ninth day, the council turned from the past to the future, and called Astrid Halloran to the floor.

Ivo watched them walk down the center aisle and understood, with a lurch, how much the mountain had cost them. They moved carefully, favoring the left side, where the blast had cracked ribs the healers said would knit. Their sword rode at their hip out of habit, but Ivo knew — everyone in that hall who mattered knew — that the blade was silent now. Whatever words lived in it had flown skyward with the auroras, ten thousand voices streaming free over Thornfell while the mountain exhaled. Astrid Halloran was, by every measurement the old world possessed, nothing. No oath. No swordspeech. Half a self, learning to be whole.

The council offered them a chair anyway. The high one, beside the magistrate's bench, draped in the concord-silver.

"Halloran," Corvel said, and her dry voice softened by perhaps one degree, which from Corvel was a eulogy of feeling. "The council has debated long. There are those who wanted you tried alongside the officers — you carried an oath-blade, you spoke binding words, you are the last of the order that built the vaults and the collectors and the market of voices." She raised a hand against the outcry from the galleries. "There are others who wanted you crowned, or canonized, or married into three separate noble houses before sundown. The council has, for once, done neither."

She drew out a scroll, but did not open it.

"We ask instead for something older than either. We ask you to keep oaths."

Ivo saw Astrid go very still.

"Not with magic. The age of bought vows is ending, and good riddance — we will spend a generation untangling the ones that remain, and your testimony alone will not be enough to do it. No. We ask you to stand in the courts, and at the border-fords, and in the councils, and witness the promises free people make to each other. To hold them to those promises with nothing but memory and law and your own sworn word. To be," and here Corvel smiled, thin and real, "the first keeper in history whose power consists entirely of being trusted."

The hall waited. Ivo found he was gripping the bench rail hard enough to hurt.

Astrid stood for a long moment, looking at the chair, at the silver, at the nine stitched cities overhead. When they finally spoke, their voice carried to the back wall without any magic in it at all.

"I spent my life paying for power with pieces of myself," they said. "I won't do that again, and I won't watch anyone else do it. If the office is what you say — witness, not wielder — then I'll take it. But I want it written into the charter that no oath-keeper may ever accept payment for a vow again. Not coin. Not memory. Nothing. An oath costs exactly what it says, and no more."

"Moved and carried," Corvel said, before the shout of assent finished echoing. "Charter amendment one. The clerk will enter it."

And that was when the messenger arrived.

Ivo noticed her first because of the mud — road-mud, highland mud, the orange-brown streaked with mica that only came from the Thornfell passes, and no legitimate traffic had come over those passes since the mountain cracked. She was young, a courier's badge on a torn cloak, and she crossed the hall floor at a dead run until two guards caught her arms and she shouted over them, breathless:

"Magistrate! From the Hollow Stair — from the survey team on the lower stair!" She wrestled something from inside her cloak: a message tube, seals broken, resealed with the expedition's wax. "The lower chambers aren't empty. The throne-cage is destroyed, yes, the voices are gone — but there's *someone* down there. Alive. Unaged. He was the warden, he says. He says—" she faltered, reading from the courier's memorized script, "—he says his name is Aldric Halloran, and he asks for his descendant. He says he has one page left to give, and he doesn't think he has long."

The silence that followed was different from every other silence the hall had produced that week. Heavier. Older.

Ivo turned and looked at Astrid, and saw grief arrive on their face like weather — the grief they had already carried once, standing in the drowned chapel over a confession they'd read aloud so the mountain would remember honestly, the grave they'd dug beside the emptied throne. Grief without forgiveness.

"He was buried," Astrid said quietly. "I buried him myself."

"Magistrate," the courier said, "he also says—" and she swallowed, "—he says the page isn't for the court. And that the smudged name in the ledger was never the only thing he hid."

Corvel closed her eyes briefly, a woman weighing the cost of a truth before it had even been spoken. Then she opened them.

"Halloran. It seems the mountain isn't finished with you."

Down in the benches, Sef had gone white as vellum, and was already reaching for his satchel.
