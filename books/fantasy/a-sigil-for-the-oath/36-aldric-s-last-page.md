## 36. Aldric's Last Page

The Hollow Stair was quieter now.

Astrid felt it before they saw it — the absence of pressure that had lived in the stone for a century, the lean of gravity toward the court's heart, the sense of being *expected*. The mountain had exhaled its ten thousand voices into the sky above Thornfell, and what remained was only rock: honest, dumb, indifferent rock. Astrid had spent their whole life inside an oath that hummed. Walking down the Stair without it was like walking after a fever breaks — the world the same shape but lighter, and somehow harder to trust.

Sef had offered to come. Ivo too. Maren had stood at the mouth of the Hollow Stair with her commission folded in her fist and said nothing at all, which from Maren was nearly speech.

Astrid went alone.

*Some doors you have to open with your own hands,* Wren said. Not spoken aloud — never aloud anymore — just a settling, like a second weight leaning against the inside of Astrid's ribs. Since the throne-cage broke, Wren's presence had gone quiet, not gone. A sibling sharing a house. They didn't speak often. But they spoke for this.

*I know,* Astrid answered, and kept descending.

The black-iron key still worked on the outer gate, though there was nothing left to lock out. The wards Aldric had maintained for three hundred years were ash in the stone, and the great doors swung at a child's push. Beyond them the Market of Voices lay gutted — Kestrian's oath-cannon had burned through it like a hot wire through wax. Vials crunched underfoot, their contents long since evaporated into smoke or freedom or nothing. The awnings were charmed lace once; now they hung like moth wings. Here and there a glass vial had survived intact, sealed memories glowing faintly, ownerless. Astrid stepped around them carefully. Someone would come back for these. Someone should.

The drowned chapel was next, where Sef had cracked the ledger's marginalia by candlelight while the mountain shook around them. The water had drained when the cage broke — all the Undercourt's plumbing of grief running backward, Astrid supposed, or simply no longer needed. The chapel floor was black mud and white bone-silt. Astrid's boots left prints that would be the first prints anyone had made here in centuries.

And then the court itself.

It was smaller than Astrid remembered. That was the thing no one warned you about horror: memory inflated it, gave it cathedral proportions, and when you came back the evil fit inside an ordinary room. The dais where Aldric had sat his warden's vigil was just a dais. The throne — the *cage*, the ten-thousand-voiced engine of the Halloran oath — was a heap of shattered silver lattice, torn open down the middle like a ribcage pried apart by hands. Scorch marks fanned out from it where Kestrian's cannon had struck. Freed light had eaten the rest.

Astrid stood in the doorway a long time.

"You came home."

The words rose so clearly in memory that Astrid's hand went to a sword that wasn't there. They'd left it above. There was nothing left to speak to. But the echo of Aldric Halloran's voice — terrible tenderness, three hundred years of guilt wearing a grandfather's face — still lived in this room the way heat lives in a hearthstone after the fire dies.

"Well," Astrid said aloud, to the empty court, to the broken cage, to the man who wasn't in it anymore. "I came back. Different reason this time."

Wren stirred. *He can't hear you.*

"I know."

*Then why talk?*

"Because the mountain can." Astrid crossed the floor, boots ringing on stone that no longer leaned toward anything. "Because somebody should say true things in rooms where lies were spoken, or the walls learn the wrong language."

That was Sef's philosophy, actually. Astrid had stolen it shamelessly over six months of shared roads, and Sef had pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of gift.

Aldric's body lay where he had fallen — not far from the throne, close enough to touch it, turned toward the breach in the silver as if he'd been trying to climb inside when the end came. Or trying to hold it together. Astrid would never know which, and had decided, on the long road back from Harrowgate, that not-knowing was a burden they were willing to carry rather than guess wrong about.

Kestrian's people had wanted to take the corpse. Maren's testimony mentioned it — the inquisitors' remnant, before she turned her blade on them, had orders to retrieve "the asset's remains." What remained of the regime had wanted Aldric Halloran's body for study, or trophy, or worse. Astrid had refused. It had been the first thing Astrid had demanded in the trials' chaotic opening weeks, standing powerless before the council of Harrowgate with no swordspeech and nothing but a name everyone suddenly knew: *he is mine to bury. He is my blood. Whatever he did, he did it wearing my face's history, and he goes into the ground by my hand or not at all.*

Ivo had argued the legal case. Sef had produced documents. Maren had simply looked at the council until they agreed.

Now Astrid knelt beside the body.

Death had been thorough with him. Three hundred years of borrowed time had come due all at once in that final hour, and what was left was small — a dried husk in fine clothes, a mummy wearing a warden's collar, the age Kestrian had died with settled into him posthumously like floodwater finding its level. His face was still recognizably the face from the gate. Still recognizably, if Astrid was honest, a face related to theirs. Grandmother Halloran's brow. Astrid's own jaw, worn by centuries.

"Hello, great-great-grandfather times more than I can count," Astrid said quietly. "You absolute bastard. You poor, monstrous old man."

Both things. That was the sentence Astrid had been turning over for months, on roads and in waystations, in the letters Sef wrote and Astrid read aloud to make sure the words stuck. Both things. Villain and victim. The man who brokered a sale of souls and the man the sale had imprisoned; the architect of the cage and its longest-standing prisoner; the tender host who locked the doors and the trembling servant who, at the very last, unlocked them and pressed a page of blood-price into Astrid's hands with fingers that shook.

Grief without forgiveness. Astrid had learned the phrase from a mourner in Harrowgate, some ordinary woman weeping for a son conscripted into Kestrian's legions, and had recognized it immediately as the exact shape of the thing in their own chest. Grief without forgiveness. You could carry love for someone and refuse to excuse them in the same breath. The two did not cancel. They cohabited, the way Wren cohabited in Astrid, the way every hard thing cohabited in a person who had learned that hearts were bigger than they looked.

Astrid unrolled the shroud they'd carried down — plain wool, bought in Thornfell with smuggler's coin, because Aldric Halloran would be buried in wool like any traveler and not in the silk of courts.

But first: the reading.

That had been the plan from the beginning, formed somewhere on the road north while Astrid stared at fires and couldn't sleep. Sef had found it in the ledger's final pages during the weeks of testimony — Aldric's confession, written across decades in different inks, different hands steadying and failing, a document begun in pride and ended in ash. Sef had copied it fair. Then Sef had looked at Astrid over the copy and said, in the archivist voice that meant a principle was at stake:

"It has to be read aloud. In the place it happened. Words spoken into stone are remembered by stone — your grandmother taught you that, and the road proved it, and everything under this mountain proves it. If his confession stays on paper, it's evidence. Read aloud down there, it becomes *record*. The mountain will keep it whether anyone else does or not."

So Astrid drew the fair copy from their coat, weighted it open with two pieces of shattered silver from the cage — a symmetry Sef would have appreciated — and began to read.

* * *

*"I, Aldric Halloran, being unable to die, set down the truth because death will not take my confession for me."*

The first entry was dated in a year Astrid had to sound out like a child. The ink was brown-black, confident, the hand of a young man drunk on survival. Astrid read it in the empty court and the words fell upward slightly — habit of the place, the Stair's old physics dying slowly — and each one seemed to hang in the air a moment before sinking into the walls.

*"The famine took the village. The plague took the city. I walked to Thornfell because there was nowhere left to walk to, and beneath the mountain I found the court, and the court made me an offer no starving man refuses: power beyond the reach of kings, in exchange for a soul."*

"Not yours," Astrid murmured. "You didn't even sell your own."

*"Not mine,"* the confession continued, as if the stone had repeated the question down three centuries. *"The court required a soul of Halloran blood, unbaptized by any vow, unnamed. My wife had borne twins that spring. One of them I named and kept. One of them I carried down the Hollow Stair in a basket lined with wool, and I told my wife the child had been stillborn, and I let her grieve, and I sold her grief-child to the Undercourt for the price of our family's greatness."*

Astrid's voice caught. They let it catch. Sef had said — Sef always said — *don't perform the reading, but don't flinch from it either; the stone knows the difference between recitation and truth.* So Astrid breathed, wiped their face with the back of one glove, and read on.

*"The oath worked. Of course it worked. Every vow spoken into steel thereafter ran on that stolen fire, and the Hallorans became swordspeakers of legend, and I was fed from the court's table and aged not at all, and my descendants died gloriously in wars I funded, and I told myself I had saved us."*

The handwriting changed. The next entry was decades later, the letters thinner, the ink cheaper.

*"My wife died knowing. She found the ledger. She did not scream at me. That was worse than screaming. She said: 'Every vow your blood swears is paid for by a child who never got to swear anything.' Then she stopped speaking to me entirely, though we shared a house for nineteen years, and on her deathbed she said only one word to me: 'Wren.'"*

Astrid stopped.

The name landed in the silent court and did not echo. Some names are too heavy to bounce.

*Yes,* Wren said, very quiet. *Go on.*

*"She named the sold twin,"* Aldric had written. *"In her last hour she chose the name the child should have carried, and she made me promise — she made me swear, knowing what oaths cost me — that if ever the bargain could be undone, I would undo it, whatever it cost the House. I swore it on her grave. And then I spent two hundred years looking for loopholes, because I am a bargainer, it is all I have ever been, and I could not bear the price of keeping faith."*

Astrid read the middle years quickly, because they were unbearable slowly: entries cataloguing every swordspeaker the oath had consumed, every memory taken as payment funneled downward to feed the court, every rationalization wearing thinner. Aldric had watched generations of his own descendants pay installments on his crime. He had watched Astrid's grandmother — *Maren Halloran, named for no one, fierce as weather* — begin to suspect the truth. He had watched the usurper rise in the world above and smelled in Kestrian's hunger something worse than his own sin: his sin, perfected, industrialized.

The final entries were recent. The hand shook. The ink was the blood-price red of the revocation clause.

*"The last swordswpeaker came down my stair tonight,"* one read, misdated, misspelled, human. *"She has her grandmother's eyes and she looked at me the way my wife looked at me, and I heard myself say 'you came home' as though I had any right to the word home. I locked the doors. God forgive me, I locked the doors, because locking doors is the only prayer I know."*

And the last entry, written in the hours before Kestrian breached the court:

*"Tonight I unlocked them. Whatever comes of it — whatever the girl chooses, whether she saves herself or saves the others or dies in the choosing — I want the record to show that Aldric Halloran did one unambiguous thing in three hundred years, and it was small, and it was late, and it was only this: he opened a door he had closed. Let the mountain weigh that against everything else. Let the mountain do the arithmetic. I cannot make it balance. No one could. That is what I understand now, at the end: there is no price at which a soul becomes affordable. There is no interest rate on a child. I built my House on a number that does not exist, and the House has finally, correctly, fallen."*

Astrid closed the copy.

The silence afterward was enormous. Not empty — enormous. The court held the words the way a bell holds a strike, humming somewhere below hearing, and Astrid understood that Sef had been right: the stone had taken it in. This room would remember Aldric honestly now. Not the legend. Not the monster alone. The whole ruinous, pitiable truth of him, pressed into rock like a flower nobody loved.

"There," Astrid said hoarsely. "The mountain remembers. That's more mercy than you gave, and less than you hoped for. Seems right."

*It does,* Wren agreed. A pause, then, softer: *Read the part about Grandmother again sometime. Not today. Sometime.*

"Yes."

* * *

The burying took an hour.

There was no shovel below the mountain, so Astrid used a sword — not their old oath-blade, which had gone dark and dead when the cage broke and now hung above the mantel in a rented room in Harrowgate, a relic of a self half-erased. This was a digger's blade, bought cheap, sharpened cheaper. The floor of the court near the broken throne was old stone over older earth, and Astrid cut into it the slow, sweating, unmagical way, and thought about how much of the last year had been exactly this: work done by hand that magic used to do, and how strange, and how — there was no other word — *owning* it felt.

The grave was shallow. Astrid wrapped Aldric in the wool, tucked the fair copy of the confession into his folded hands — the original stayed with Sef, entered into the trial record, because the free cities needed paper truth as much as the mountain needed spoken truth — and lowered him in.

"No words," Astrid said to the shrouded form. "I considered a blessing. I considered a curse. Neither one is honest. You get the truth instead, which you already got, read aloud, witnessed by stone. That's your rite. That's all you get."

A pause.

"Grandmother named you in her heart, I think. Not Wren — you don't get that name, it was never yours, it belongs to someone you stole it for. But she must have called you something, in nineteen years of silence, in the privacy of her own skull. Whatever it was, I hope it wasn't kind. And I hope — " Astrid's voice cracked and they let it, kneeling in the dirt of the Undercourt, grieving without forgiving, "— I hope she also, once, at the very end, called you something that hurt her to say. Because that's the worst punishment there is, isn't it? Being loved by someone you've wronged past the point of pardon."

They filled the grave. They marked it with a piece of the shattered cage — a length of silver lattice driven into the earth like a fence picket, ten thousand voices' prison repurposed as one man's headstone. Astrid looked at it a long moment.

"If anyone ever finds this," they told the mountain, "the marker's honest too. He built the cage. Now he rests under a piece of it. That's the whole story. Don't gild it."

Then Astrid stood in the emptied court of Thornfell, alone for the first time in a year, and let themselves feel the size of the quiet.

No oath humming in the blood. No prophecy waiting with tonight's date on its tongue. No ancestor at the gate saying *you came home*, no usurper drinking light, no inquisitors, no hunters, no net. Just a woman-shaped person — two people-shaped, really, one house — standing in the wreckage of the machine that had made them, having buried its maker, holding nothing but a dead sword far above and a live grief that would not fully heal and did not need to.

"We can go up now," Astrid said.

*We can,* Wren said. And then, because siblings share houses and houses hold truths: *Thank you. For doing it right. He starved me before I ever got to be hungry. But you buried him like I was a real thing he'd killed. That matters more than I know how to say.*

Astrid pressed a hand flat over their own sternum, over the quiet second heartbeat-that-wasn't, and said nothing, because nothing needed saying between the two of them anymore.

They turned for the stair.

Halfway across the ruined Market, stepping around another surviving vial — this one glowing a warm amber, some stranger's summer afternoon preserved in glass — Astrid stopped.

Because the vial wasn't ownerless.

Someone had scratched a mark into the soot on the shelf beside it. A fresh mark. Days old at most. And Astrid knew the mark, because they'd seen it chiseled above a door in Harrowgate and stamped on council writs and branded into confiscated oath-blades in Kestrian's vaults:

The sigil of the free cities' new Office of Restitution. The bureau charged with cataloguing the Undercourt's remnants, returning sold memories to their families, accounting for every soul the mountain had held.

Which meant someone from the surface had been down here recently.

Which meant, unless the Office had grown remarkably efficient in Astrid's absence, someone was in the mountain *now* — deeper than the Market, down where the only things left were a grave hours old and a stair leading further beneath Thornfell than any map had ever charted, into the dark below the court, the dark Aldric's confession had mentioned exactly once, in a single line Astrid hadn't thought about until this moment:

*The court was not the bottom. Nothing sold is ever kept where the buyer can see it.*

From far below — far, far below, down a stair that shouldn't exist — came the sound of a voice, singing, rising upward through the stone the way words had always fallen in this place.

Astrid picked up the vial, held the warm amber light against their palm, and started walking toward the sound.
