## 37. Learning My Own Face

The first thing I learned about myself after the mountain was that I could not cook.

Not couldn't-well. Couldn't at all. I stood in Sef's kitchen in Harrowgate — a narrow room with a window that looked out on the archive's back wall and a stove that smoked if you looked at it wrong — holding a knife over an onion like it was a small hostile animal, and I realized I had no memory of ever having cut one.

"I've eaten," I said aloud, to the room, to Wren, to whoever was listening inside my own skull. "I must have eaten for twenty-six years."

*You burned porridge once,* Wren offered, from the quiet place where they lived now. Not a voice, exactly. More like a memory that had opinions. *You were nine. You cried about it.*

"Did I?"

*I did,* they said, and then, softer: *Or I would have. It's hard to say whose crying that was.*

I set down the knife. Outside, Harrowgate was rebuilding itself plank by plank and law by law; somewhere across the city, Maren Duskwell was testifying before a tribunal in a voice that never wavered, and Ivo Tanzer was learning to be a delegate instead of a deserter, and Sef was — well. Sef was standing in the doorway watching me threaten an onion with the expression of a man cataloguing evidence.

"You held that sword like it was part of your arm," he said. "You spoke a mountain open. And you can't dice an onion."

"The sword came with instructions." I slid the onion toward him. "The onion didn't."

He took up the knife without comment and showed me: knuckles forward, tip anchored, let the blade do the falling. It was such a small thing to be taught. It went into me like water into dry ground. I thought: this is what it's like, learning something I get to keep.

That was the season of small lessons. That was the year of my second childhood.

* * *

Sef kept a room in the archive that smelled of dust and lamp oil and old glue, and in it we built what he called, without any irony he was willing to admit to, the Astrid Project.

It began with a table. Then the table grew shelves. Then the shelves grew boxes, and the boxes filled with everything anyone had ever written down about me or to me or near me: letters from my grandmother Halloran, three of them, brittle as moth wings; a smuggler's ledger from the Thornfell roads with my name entered eleven times in my own handwriting; a child's copybook, recovered from a cousin twice removed who had nearly thrown it out, full of my eight-year-old letters marching crookedly across the page. *A is for Astrid. A is also for apple.*

"That one's yours," Sef said, tapping the copybook. "No question. The hand matches your ledger entries."

"And this?" I held up a letter in a different hand, rounder, quicker, addressed to *my darling Wren* and signed by someone named Tamsin Vale.

Sef was quiet a moment. "We don't know. There's no Wren in any record I can find — no birth entry, no death entry, nothing. Which is itself a kind of record. Someone worked hard to erase them." He turned the letter over in his long fingers. "But the paper's the same stock as your grandmother's letters. Same village stationer, almost certainly. Whoever Wren was, they were real, and they were loved, and then they were gone so thoroughly that even the ink gave them up."

Inside me, something shifted its weight, the way a person shifts in a chair when their name is spoken in another room.

*Tamsin made plum cakes,* Wren said. *For market days. She let me lick the spoon.*

"Plum cakes," I said out loud, and Sef looked up sharply. "Wren remembers. Tamsin Vale. Market days. The spoon."

He wrote it down. He always wrote it down. That was our covenant, unspoken but absolute: anything Wren surfaced, anything I dredged up, anything we guessed — it went on paper, dated, sourced when we could. Because the mountain had taught us both what happens to memory when no one writes it down. It gets sold. It gets smudged. It becomes someone else's fuel.

"We're not recovering you," Sef said one evening, ink on his fingers, lamplight catching the grey coming in early at his temples. He'd been disgraced once for reading pages aloud; now he read my life aloud every night, and no one had ever looked less exiled. "I want to be honest about that. We're not excavating a true Astrid from under the rubble. Some of it's gone. Sold, spent, burned paying vows I'll never fully itemize. What we're doing is—" he searched for the word, and I watched him find it and decide not to soften it— "authoring. You have sources. You have gaps. You get to choose which sentences go in the gaps."

"That's not a self," I said. "That's a forgery."

"It's a biography," he said. "Every one ever written is a forgery of a life. The only difference is whether the forger admits it." He tapped the table. "Your grandmother gave you an oath and called it identity. Aldric sold half of you and called it destiny. The Codex wrote your name on a page and called it fate. You've spent your whole life being authored by other people, Astrid. Forgive me if I think you've earned a turn with the pen."

I sat with that for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us, the archive's night-staff moved through the stacks like patient ghosts.

"All right," I said finally. "Then I'm choosing this: I want to know if I liked plums before Wren told me about the spoon."

Sef smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one that made him look twenty years younger and slightly criminal. "We'll buy some tomorrow. Empiricism."

* * *

Empiricism, it turned out, tasted excellent.

We bought plums from a fruit-seller on Cordwainer Row who remembered my face — "You used to come through with the Thornfell caravans, didn't you? Always paid fair, always rude" — and her testimony went into the project too, because apparently I had been rude to fruit-sellers, and I decided, sitting on the archive steps with juice running down my wrist, that I would keep that. Rude to fruit-sellers. Fair pay. It felt like mine. It might have been Wren's. It might have been neither; it might have been something the road had rubbed onto me like grease. It didn't matter. I chose it, and a chosen thing is yours no matter where you found it.

Other things were harder.

Arguing, for instance. I discovered I had been a good arguer with a sword in reach and a poor one without. When Ivo came to dinner — he came often, bringing bread and city gossip and the particular exhaustion of a young man rebuilding a government — and disagreed with me about the tribunal's sentencing guidelines, I heard my own voice drop into a register I recognized from the descent, from the stair, from the court: the register that expected obedience. Ivo flinched. Actually flinched, set down his fork.

"Sorry," I said. "That was — I wasn't asking. I was pronouncing."

"You were swordspeaking," Sef said quietly, from the stove. "Without the sword. Without the power. Just the habit of it."

I sat with that all night. The power was gone — truly gone; I had tested it in the first weeks, standing on the summit speaking vows into empty air until my voice cracked, and the mountain had only hummed, indifferent, a door that no longer recognized my key. But the shape of the power stayed. The posture of it. Twenty-six years of being the thing people obeyed leaves grooves in a person, and I was learning to walk beside my own grooves without falling in.

So I practiced arguing badly. I practiced losing arguments — to Ivo about tariffs, to Sef about whether the Codex should stay locked (he won; I still think he cheated with citations), to a cobbler about whether I needed new boots (the cobbler was right). Losing, I found, was a skill like any other, and I had never once been allowed to learn it.

*You lost to me constantly,* Wren said one night, apropos of nothing, while I lay awake listening to Harrowgate's rain. *Arm wrestling. Chess. Who got the last plum cake. You sulked magnificently.*

"Were we children together, then? Really? In the same house?"

A pause. Wren's pauses had texture now; I had learned to read them. This one was careful, the way Sef's were careful when he was about to tell me a page was missing.

*I don't have childhoods,* they said. *I have weather. Sometimes there's a kitchen, and Tamsin singing, and someone small across the table from me who sulks when they lose. I can't tell you if that someone was you-before-me or just a shape I borrowed. But Astrid — whoever they were, they liked losing to me. They liked it because losing meant I was real enough to win.*

I lay in the dark and let that settle into me like sediment.

"Then I choose that too," I said. "I choose to have liked it."

*You can't just choose things into your past.*

"Watch me."

And Wren laughed — actually laughed, a sound with no memory under it, brand-new, ours — and the rain went on, and I slept better than I had any right to.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the maps.

Sef spread them across the project table one gray afternoon: the smuggler's roads I had run for a decade, drawn in my own hand, annotated in my own shorthand. Waystations marked with symbols I no longer knew the meaning of. A stretch of the Salt Road annotated *ask Marta re: the dog.* A cave system beneath the switchbacks labeled, simply, *do not sing here.*

"Why not?"

"I was hoping you'd remember," Sef said.

I closed my eyes and reached for it and found the specific smooth absence I had come to know so well — not darkness, not forgetting, but a gap shaped exactly like a knowing. Somewhere in the bargain's accounting, that knowledge had been currency. I had sung in that cave, or refused to, and the knowing of why had paid for something.

"Leave it," I said. "Some doors I'd rather not reopen just because I can."

"Astrid Halloran, declining information." Sef raised his eyebrows. "The mountain falls and the world turns upside down."

"The mountain fell so I could decline things." I traced the Salt Road with one finger, feeling the map's ghost under my skin — muscle memory of switchbacks, of caravan rhythm, of which stones lie. That much survived; the body keeps what the soul spends. "Mark it *unknown, possibly dangerous, owner's choice.* That's honest."

He wrote it. And that, I was beginning to understand, was the whole discipline of it: honesty about the gaps. Not filling them with invention and calling it truth, not leaving them raw and calling it purity, but marking them, naming them, living around them the way a tree lives around a lightning scar. I would never be whole. Wholeness had been sold off in installments before I was old enough to sign anything. But a person is not a purse to be emptied or refilled. A person is a story that keeps deciding what it's about.

I cooked, that winter. Badly, then adequately, then — once, memorably, for Ivo and Sef and Maren, who arrived straight from the tribunal looking like she'd been flayed and stitched back by her own hand — well. A plum cake. Tamsin Vale's recipe, reconstructed from Wren's weather: *cinnamon, too much butter, the spoon.* Maren ate two slices in silence and then said, "My brother would have liked this," and no one made it strange, because we had all lost people to the same appetite, and grief shared at a table is a different substance than grief carried alone.

I argued. I lost. I learned the names of my neighbors, and forgot them, and learned them again, and discovered that forgetting without magic behind it is just the ordinary human condition, and that everyone else has been living with it all along, patiently, without prophecy.

I wrote letters. To the freed voices' keepers in the southern settlements, though most had scattered like sparks and wanted no keeping. To the cousin with the copybook. One, unsent, to a grandmother ten years dead: *I know now what the oath cost. I know you thought you were saving me. I am going to decide you were right, because it's kinder than the alternative, and kindness is a choice I get to make now.*

And every evening, Sef read the day's additions aloud, and the project grew, and I grew with it — not recovered, not restored, but *drafted*, revised, argued over by committee (me, Sef, and a sibling made of weather), and slowly, slowly becoming legible to myself.

* * *

On the last night of winter, Sef didn't come to the kitchen.

I found him in the archive instead, in the project room, standing very still over the table. On it lay the Vellum Codex.

I hadn't seen it since Thornfell. We'd agreed it would stay sealed — Sef had been exiled once already for opening it, and neither of us had the stomach for what it had done to my name. But there it sat, open, and Sef's face in the lamplight was the face he'd worn at the waystation the night he'd shown me my own name on tonight's date: wonder and terror sharing a chair.

"Sef."

"I was checking the binding," he said, in the voice of a man who knows how guilty he sounds. "For the tribunal exhibit. I swear that's all. And Astrid — look."

I looked. The prophecies ended, as he'd told me they did, at the night the cage broke. Every page beyond it was blank. I had known this. He had told me this, weeks ago, in a report delivered like a diagnosis.

But that wasn't what he was showing me.

Beyond the blankness, at the very back, in the gutter of the final gathering — faint, brown, unmistakably fresh — there was writing. New ink. Ink that had not been there when the mountain fell, because Sef had checked, he said, he had checked three times.

Five words, in a hand none of us knew:

*The next oath is unwritten.*

And beneath it, smaller, in the same hand — a date.

Tomorrow's.
