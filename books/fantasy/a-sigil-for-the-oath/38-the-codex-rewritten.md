## 38. The Codex Rewritten

The Vellum Codex had survived three exiles, one inquisition, a river crossing that should have drowned it, and the fall of a mountain. Sef carried it out of Thornfell the way other people carried children — against the chest, one arm curled beneath, flinching at every jostle of the road.

Now, six weeks after the mountain exhaled its ten thousand voices into the sky, Sef sat in the archive room of Harrowgate's Chantry of Records with the book open on a table scarred by two centuries of scholars' knives, and turned past the last written page.

Blank.

They turned again.

Blank.

The lamplight lay flat and yellow across vellum that had never once, in all the years Sef had kept it, refused to show them something. The Codex was not a book, properly speaking. It was a wound in the world through which the future bled backward, and every page after tonight's date had always held *something* — a name, a date, a fragment of some catastrophe or coronation decades hence, inked in pigments that predated their own making. Sef had spent eleven years as its keeper reading pages that described things no living person had done yet. They had been exiled for reading those pages aloud.

And now: nothing. Page after page after page of clean, empty skin, soft as the inside of a wrist.

Sef sat back. The chair creaked. Somewhere below, Harrowgate's bells rang the third hour after midnight, and the sound came up through the floor like a pulse.

"Say something," they murmured to the book.

The book said nothing. It never had. That had always been part of its horror — prophecy without commentary, fate without a face. But silence had never been one of its answers before. Silence was new. Silence was *earned*.

Sef pressed two fingers flat against the blank page, half expecting warmth, a tremor, the faint resistance of ink forming beneath the skin like a bruise rising. Nothing. Just vellum, cool and patient and finished.

"You're done," they said aloud, and heard their own voice crack on the second word. "Aren't you. You're finally done."

* * *

They did not sleep. Instead they did what archivists do when confronted with a miracle: they tested it for fraud.

By dawn they had laid out the full apparatus of their old office — the pigment charts, the magnifying lenses ground from Thornfell crystal, the reference folios of dated inks they'd assembled over fifteen years and smuggled out of Vaelmoor in a false-bottomed chest. They scraped micro-shavings from the final written page — the one bearing Astrid Halloran's name and the date of the mountain's breaking — and compared them against shavings from pages a century old, fifty years old, ten.

Identical. Bone-black and rust-red and that strange violet made from crushed lichen that grew only in lightless places, all of it aged uniformly, all of it old as the book itself. Whatever hand had written Astrid's doom had written it a hundred years ago, in ink mixed before Astrid's grandmother drew breath.

Then Sef turned to the first blank page and scraped that too.

Nothing. Not blankness concealing invisible script — they tried heat, they tried ash-rubbing, they tried the old archivist's trick of tilting vellum against raking light to catch the ghost-grooves of a stylus. The page had never been touched. It wasn't hiding anything. It was simply, honestly, empty.

Which meant the Codex hadn't run out of pages. It had run out of *future*.

Sef set down the lens and looked at their hands. Old hands now, or at least older than they'd been three years ago, when they'd first read a forbidden page aloud in the Vaelmoor archive and been cast out for it — *the swordspeaker's name will surface on the night the mountain opens its eye*, they had told a room full of colleagues who preferred their prophecies catalogued and unspoken. Three years hunting a person they'd never met, guided by a sentence in a dead language, and now that person was alive and powerless and sleeping two floors above the Chantry guesthouse, and the book that had orchestrated the whole miserable pilgrimage had nothing left to say about any of it.

There was a feeling in Sef's chest they couldn't immediately name. It took them most of a pot of cold tea to identify it, because they had never once felt it in the presence of the Codex.

It was relief.

* * *

Astrid came down mid-morning, moving carefully the way they still did, as though the stairs might demand payment. Six weeks of relearning a body without swordspeech, and they still reached sometimes for words that used to reshape stone — Sef had watched them stop themselves mid-syllable at a stuck window, a stubborn jar, a door swollen shut with rain. Then they would do the thing with their hands instead, plainly, like everyone else, and look faintly astonished each time that plainness worked.

They were carrying bread and two apples and wearing the expression of someone who had slept, actually slept, which still looked foreign on them.

"You didn't come up," Astrid said. "Ivo bet me you'd fallen asleep at the table again. I said you'd be awake and furious about something."

"I'm awake," Sef said. "I'm not furious." They hesitated. "I don't know what I am. Sit down."

Astrid sat. They had learned, in six weeks, to read Sef's moods the way sailors read weather, and whatever they saw made them set the apples down slowly. "It's the book."

"It's the book." Sef turned it around on the table so the blank pages faced them. "Go on. Look."

Astrid looked. Sef watched their face go through it — confusion first, then the quick darting search of someone checking whether this was a trick, then something quieter and more dangerous settling behind their eyes. Their hand rose and hovered over the vellum without touching, the way people hover over the faces of the dead.

"It's empty," Astrid said. "All of it?"

"Everything past the night the mountain broke. I've been at it since midnight. Every test I know. The last written page is real — same century-old pigment, same hand, same everything. And then—" Sef spread their palms over the emptiness. "Nothing. No more future. It just stops."

Astrid was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had an odd gentleness. "You spent three years following this thing. It dragged you out of your life, got you hunted, nearly killed you twice that I know of and probably more that I don't. And now it's blank and you're sitting here looking like—" They searched. "Like a man whose jailer died."

"That's exactly what it looks like," Sef said. "Yes."

"And underneath that?"

Sef laughed, short and surprised. "Underneath that is the problem." They pulled the lamp closer. "Astrid, do you understand what this book *was*? Not what it said — what it was? Every event it recorded happened. Every single one, across a hundred years. Wars, famines, the plague Kestrian blamed on your kind — it's in there, dated forty years before his alchemists brewed it. We used to tell ourselves the Codex *saw* the future. But seeing implies distance. This thing didn't see. It *scheduled*. And everything it scheduled served one arrangement — the sale under Thornfell. The oath, the swordspeakers, the usurper's rise, Maren Duskwell's whole career, the council's cowardice, even Ivo's little rebellion — I've found all of it in these pages now that I know where to look. It wasn't a window. It was a ledger. And ledgers get balanced."

"So when we broke the cage—"

"The account closed. The contract ended. And the instrument of the contract—" Sef touched the blank page one more time, almost tenderly. "Stopped writing. Which means, and I want you to hear how enormous this is, which means there is no next line. There is no page somewhere with Kestrian's heir on it, or a new usurper, or your death, or mine. The future isn't hidden from us anymore, Astrid. It's *unwritten*. Nobody has written it. Nobody is going to write it for us."

Astrid stared at the emptiness, and Sef watched something move through their face that they recognized from the mountain — the vertigo of a person whose certainty has collapsed and who is deciding, right there, whether to grieve it or be glad.

"My whole life," Astrid said slowly, "I thought my choices were the pen. That I was writing my story. And then I found my name in here, dated before my birth, and I learned I was the ink." They looked up. "You're telling me the inkwell's dry."

"I'm telling you the inkwell's dry, the pen's snapped, and the vellum—" Sef turned the book fully around, open to the first untouched page, and pushed it across the table until it sat between them like a border crossing. "The vellum is right here. Blank. Waiting."

* * *

The others arrived by evening, because word traveled fast in a city rebuilding itself and both of them were terrible at keeping secrets.

Maren Duskwell came first, still wearing her commission's grey despite having publicly surrendered it a month ago, because habits of dress outlast the institutions that made them. She stood at the doorway of the archive room with her arms crossed and looked at the open Codex the way she looked at everything now — as evidence.

"That's the book that wrote me," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd found her own name in it during the trials, on a page dated thirty years back: *the inquisitor-general will burn the refugee column at Marrow Ford.* She had testified to it herself, flat-voiced, while Kestrian's officers went pale. "Is my page blank too?"

"Every page is blank," Sef said. "Past the mountain."

Maren was silent. Then she crossed the room, put one finger on the empty vellum, and left it there for a long moment, as if taking a pulse. "Good," she said finally, and her voice caught on it, and she pretended it hadn't. "Then whatever I do next is actually mine." She straightened. "The tribunal reconvenes tomorrow. They'll want your testimony on the Codex's provenance, Sef. Bring it. All of it — including the parts that make you look bad."

"I always bring those parts."

"Yes," Maren said, and for the first time in Sef's memory, the former inquisitor-general almost smiled. "That's why you're still alive."

Ivo Tanzer arrived an hour later, breathless, having run from the council chambers when a runner reached him — because of course Sef had sent a runner; some habits were load-bearing. He was twenty-four and had the exhausted luminous face of someone who had testified about a razing order and watched half the council try to pretend he'd invented it. He barely glanced at the book. He looked at Sef.

"Is it true? It's stopped?"

"It's stopped."

Ivo let out a breath and dropped into a chair like a puppet with cut strings. "Then the council can't cite it anymore. Do you know how many votes this week were 'the Codex foretells stability under a strong regency'? Half of them. They've been quoting a *dead book* at each other for a month, trying to make it say Kestrianism was inevitable." He laughed, ragged. "It wasn't inevitable. Nothing's inevitable. Gods, that's terrifying."

"That's the proposal," Sef said.

Four faces turned toward them. Sef realized they'd been waiting all day to say this, rehearsing it against the blank pages, and now that the moment had come their mouth went dry anyway. Archivists were not, as a profession, accustomed to proposing things. They preserved, they catalogued, they testified. Proposing was for politicians and prophets, and Sef had been exiled for being too much of the latter already.

But the blank page sat there, and nobody else was going to say it.

"Here is what I propose," Sef said. "We keep the book."

Maren's eyebrow went up. Ivo frowned. Astrid went very still.

"Not as an oracle — it's done, it'll never write another word, and if anyone claims otherwise I'll personally scrape their ink. We keep it as what it actually was: a record. A hundred years of a bargain, written down in advance by the thing that profited from it. Every entry is testimony now. Evidence of how fate gets manufactured by whoever holds the pen." Sef turned the Codex toward the light. "And then — and this is the heresy, so brace yourselves — we continue it. In our hands. In ordinary ink. Plainly. As history."

Silence.

"You want to write in it," Maren said flatly.

"I want to *write in it*, yes. Dated entries. What actually happened, verified, signed. Starting with the mountain. Starting with the ten thousand names, if the freed voices can be recovered — and I believe they can; the Market vaults survived, and every vial is a memory somebody sold, which means every vial is a page of somebody's history we could give back." Sef's voice was steady now, gathering speed the way it only did around documents. "No prophecy. No dates ahead. Only dates behind. A book that records what free people chose, after the last unfree book closed. Let Harrowgate keep it. Let the cities argue over it. Let some archivist in a hundred years open it and find — for the first time in its existence — that every word in it is true *and already happened*."

"That's not a holy book," Ivo said slowly.

"No. It's better. It's a *ledger of the living*." Sef looked at Astrid. "Your family's oath ran on purchased souls. Your ancestor bought power with futures. This is the antidote, Astrid. The Hallorans wrote fate. We write history. Someone has to hold the pen who doesn't intend to profit."

Astrid reached out and rested their hand on the blank page — really touched it this time, palm flat, the way they'd once touched steel to speak oaths into it. Sef watched them and understood that they were feeling for something: the old pull, the hunger of the vow, the voice that used to answer. Finding nothing but paper.

"When I was small," Astrid said quietly, "my grandmother told me the Halloran gift was that our words came true. It took me thirty years to learn the truth — that our words came *prepaid*, and the price was paid by people who never got a vote. Including me. Including—" their hand pressed slightly harder on the page, on the sibling settled quiet inside them, "—including Wren." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and level. "If you write in that book, Sef, write this on the first page: *no entry in this book was bought.* Can you promise that?"

Sef thought of their exile, their three years of hunting, the ways they had used Astrid as much as saved them — arriving at that waystation not by chance but by design, a truth confessed on a salt-cave floor and never fully forgiven. They thought of every archivist who had ever told themselves that recording power was different from serving it.

"No entry bought," Sef said. "No entry forged. No entry written by anyone who stands to gain from it. Witnessed signatures on every page. And when I die, the office passes to someone the cities elect — not appoint. Elect." They took out their pen knife, the one tool no archivist surrendered, and held it over the blank vellum. "May I?"

Astrid nodded. Ivo leaned forward. Maren, arms still crossed, gave a single slow nod that from her was a benediction.

Sef uncapped the ink — ordinary ink, oak-gall black, bought that morning from a Harrowgate stationer for four coppers — and on the first page after a century of prophecy, in a careful clerk's hand, wrote:

*Night of the Mountain's Breaking. Ten thousand voices held beneath Thornfell were freed. The usurper Kestrian, who fed on bound vows, was unmade by a word spoken with all of them behind it. The last swordspeaker, Astrid Halloran — and Wren, who was owed a life — gave up the power rather than wield it. Recorded by Sef, formerly keeper of this book, who spent three years mistaking its sentences for destiny. This page cost no one anything.*

They blotted it. Signed it. Passed the pen to Astrid, who added their name beneath — and paused, and added a second name beside it, smaller, in a different cadence of handwriting that Sef had seen only once before, on a scrap of paper Astrid had written half-asleep.

Then Ivo signed. Then Maren, after a long moment, wrote her name and beneath it, unprompted, three words that made the room go still: *witness, defendant, freed.*

The candle guttered. Outside, Harrowgate's bells rang out the hour, and the sound was only bells.

"There," Sef said, and their voice was not entirely steady. "The future is officially unwritten. From tonight forward, this book knows only what we dare to tell the truth about."

Astrid was looking at the fresh ink, at the two names drying side by side, and something in their expression made Sef ask, "What?"

"Tomorrow," Astrid said slowly, "the tribunal rules on what happens to the roads. To the waystations. Whether anyone keeps them safe now that the oaths are gone." They looked up, and there was something kindled in their face that no prophecy had ever put there — something chosen. "I think I'm going to speak at the hearing. Not swordspeech. Just speech." A pause, and the ghost of a smile. "Come with me? Both of us. The book should see it."

Sef closed the Codex gently on its first honest page and picked it up — lighter now, somehow, than it had ever been.

"We'll be there," they said. "Both of us. And afterward—" they tapped the cover, once, like knocking on a door that finally opened outward, "—afterward, we'll write down whatever you said. Word for word. Plainly. So that a hundred years from now, when someone reads it, they'll know it wasn't fate."

They blew out the lamp, and in the dark the blank pages waited, patient as morning, for everything that had not yet happened.
