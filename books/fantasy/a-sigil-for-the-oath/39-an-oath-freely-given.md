## 39. An Oath Freely Given

The mountain had been quiet for a season, and I still woke some mornings listening for it to stop.

That was the thing no one warned you about — not Sef with all her archives, not Maren with her inquisitor's eye for the shape of a lie. When you have carried power like a knife between your ribs long enough, its absence feels like a wound that hasn't decided to close. I would reach for swordspeech the way a tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth, and find nothing there but me: Astrid Halloran, memory-fractured, half a soul and half again, standing on her own two feet without anything beneath them but stone.

It was enough. Most days it was even good.

But the summit was calling, and I had been putting it off since the thaw.

* * *

We climbed on the last morning of winter, when the snow on Thornfell's shoulders had gone soft and grey as old wool. Sef led the way up the pilgrim path because she'd memorized every route up the mountain from a book she'd been exiled for reading, and because she liked being useful more than she liked admitting it.

"You know," she said, pausing at the switchback where the path bent around a shrine so weathered its god was only a suggestion of elbows, "there are forty-one recorded ascents of Thornfell before the mountain fell silent. Thirty-eight of them ended badly."

"What happened to the other three?" Ivo asked. He was carrying most of our supplies because he was young and stubborn about it, and because he still moved like a man proving something to a council that had already forgotten his name.

"They wrote books," Sef said. "Which, depending on your view, is worse."

Maren brought up the rear. She still walked like an inquisitor — heel first, weight centered, hands loose near her belt out of habit rather than need. She hadn't carried a blade since she'd surrendered her commission on the steps of Harrowgate's hall of judgment, in front of three hundred people and every scribe the free cities could muster. She said she didn't miss it. The way she said it made me think she missed it exactly the way you miss a limb in cold weather: an ache where the weight used to be.

"Forty-two," she said now, quietly, looking back down the way we'd come. The valley below was a wash of pale light and woodsmoke, the free roads stitched across it like seams holding something together. "You're the forty-second."

"Only if I write a book," I said.

"Oh, you will." Sef smiled without turning around. "Everyone who survives something this large writes a book. It's practically an oath."

I laughed, and the sound went up the mountain ahead of us, thin and clear in the cold air, and somewhere far below us — or far within; I'd never learned which direction the mountain thought of as *down* — something answered. Not words. Not anymore. Just a hum, low and vast, like the note a bell keeps after everyone has stopped listening.

The freed voices. Ten thousand of them, or ten thousand and one, depending how you counted what Wren and I had become. They had poured out of the throne-cage and through the cracking dark and up into the sky, and the free cities had seen them go as auroras, green and silver over Thornfell like a banner unfurling. But not all of them had left. Some had stayed. Some had chosen the stone the way other souls choose rivers or wind — and they lived in the mountain now the way warmth lives in bread.

They knew my footsteps. That was the strangest part. Every time I climbed, the hum rose to meet me like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

The summit itself was smaller than I remembered. Grief does that — shrinks the places where big things happened, folds them down to hand-size so you can carry them. The scorch-mark where Kestrian's oath-cannon had cracked the peak had healed over with new stone, smooth and pale as scar tissue, and the crater's lip was furred with the first stubborn lichen of spring. The wind up there came off the high snowfields and tasted of nothing at all: clean, empty, waiting.

I stood at the center of the old scar and found my knees were shaking.

"Astrid." Ivo's voice, careful. "We can come back another day."

"No." I pressed my palm flat against the new stone. Cold bit through my glove. "No, it has to be now. It has to be while I'm still afraid of it."

"Why afraid?" Maren asked. She asked the way she'd asked everything since her conversion — precisely, without mercy, but with a kind of respect underneath, the way you handle evidence.

I took my hand off the stone. "Because the last vow I spoke on this spot cost me everything I had. And because every vow I've ever spoken, someone paid for. My grandmother paid in memories. Aldric paid in centuries. Wren paid in—" I stopped. Swallowed. "Wren paid in existing."

Sef set down her pack and began unpacking it with the unhurried ceremony she brought to all important things: the ledger-book, the ink, the little folding stool she refused to be ashamed of. If you were going to witness history, she believed, you should at least be able to sit comfortably and take accurate notes.

"So don't pay," she said.

"It doesn't work like that. It never worked like that. Words into steel, steel into binding, binding into blood—"

"That was the old bargain." She looked up at me, and behind the archivist's dryness there was something fierce and certain, three years of exile burning clean. "Astrid. I've read every version of the Vellum Codex. Do you know what I found in the margins last month? Underneath the prophecy? Underneath all of it?"

I shook my head.

"There was an older text. Scrubbed out. Someone — Aldric, I think, or whoever taught him — scraped the vellum and wrote over it. But vellum remembers. I held it under the right light." Her voice dropped, and the wind seemed to lean in with me. "The original words weren't a spell. They were a promise. *I will hold what is broken until it heals.* That's all the oath ever was, at the bottom, before someone turned a promise into a purchase. Power wasn't the price, Astrid. It was the interest."

The hum beneath my feet deepened by a fraction. Like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice.

* * *

We made ready the way you make ready for small things, because that was the whole point.

No circle drawn in salt. No blade unsheathed. No blood, no sigil, no invocation out of any codex, vellum or otherwise. Sef sat with her book open to a blank page. Ivo stood at my left shoulder with his hood back, letting the wind pull at his hair like a boy at a festival. Maren stood at my right with her empty hands folded, and I noticed she'd taken off her gloves — bare fingers against bare air, nothing held, nothing hidden.

"Tell me if I start to glow," I said. "Or float. Or speak in a voice that isn't mine."

"Define 'isn't yours,'" Maren said. "Given recent history."

"Wren will tell you. You'll hear it. There's a second heartbeat in the cadence."

Wren stirred at that — not rising, just turning over, the way a sibling turns toward a conversation happening in the next room. Since the cage broke, Wren lived in me the way a tenant lives in a house they've finally been given the deed to: present, quiet, occasionally rearranging the furniture of my moods. We were learning each other. Last month I'd caught myself humming a lullaby I had no memory of learning, and known whose it was. Last week Wren had flinched from a smell of hot iron and I'd stood in the market holding their fear in my chest like a bird, until it calmed.

*I'm here,* I said inwardly, which was not speech at all, just attention, just room made.

*Obviously,* Wren said, dry as Sef. *Get on with it. You're stalling.*

I was. I looked at the sky — hard blue above the snowfields, one hawk riding the thermal off the eastern face — and at the valley, and at the three people who had walked into hell's antechamber beside me for reasons that had never once been profit.

"I don't know if anything will happen," I told them. "I'm not asking for power. I'm not asking for anything. That's — that's the entire point. A vow that takes nothing and gives nothing except what I already am. If the mountain ignores it, fine. If the mountain laughs, fine. I'll write a book about it and Sef will edit."

"Brutally," Sef agreed.

"But if it hears —" My voice caught. I let it catch. A vow freely given ought to be allowed its cracks. "If it hears, then I want it witnessed properly. Not by gods. Not by courts. By these three."

I knelt. Not because kneeling was required — I was done with required — but because the stone was cold and honest and I wanted to feel it through my knees while I spoke.

And then, because I had spent my whole life speaking vows into steel, and this time there was no steel, no magic, no sold soul to fuel the syllables — I simply spoke. Into air. Into morning. Into whatever was listening under the scar.

"I, Astrid Halloran," I said, "and Wren, who is mine and I am theirs, do make this promise, freely and without price:

"To walk the free roads, and keep them free — not with a sword that speaks, because I don't have one anymore, but with these feet and this stubbornness and whatever help my friends consent to give.

"To guard the voices that chose to stay — not to bind them, never to bind them, but to stand between them and anyone who comes with a ledger and a cage.

"And to remember imperfectly, and forgive myself for it, and build a self out of what remains instead of mourning what was taken."

I stopped. The wind moved. Somewhere below, a raven complained about something.

"No payment," I said, quieter. "No price asked, none accepted. Take it or leave it. Either way, it stands."

Silence. Long enough that Ivo shifted his weight. Long enough that Sef's pen hovered unmoving over her page.

Then the mountain answered.

Not the way it used to answer swordspeech — that crack-and-thunder, the pressure of ten thousand bound throats forced into agreement. This was different. This was the hum I'd heard all season rising slowly through the rock, through my knees, through my ribs, until it wasn't outside me at all but inside, resonant, warm as a hearthstone in winter. The lichen on the scar trembled. Loose snow shivered downslope in bright ribbons. And the note — the great, patient, underground note — swelled once, gently, the way a crowd breathes in before singing, and held.

Approval. Not command, not contract. Approval. The difference mattered more than I could say. A contract owns you. Approval just — agrees with you. Walks beside you.

Tears were freezing on my cheeks before I noticed them. Wren pressed close against the inside of my chest, and I felt their wonder as heat, and underneath the heat something older and rawer: *we're allowed. We're actually allowed to want things and have them.*

"Yes," I whispered to them, and to the mountain, and maybe to myself. "Yes. That's the whole idea."

* * *

We stayed on the summit until the sun cleared the eastern ridge and the snow went gold. Sef read back what she'd written and made me correct two words — she insisted vows be transcribed exactly, "or future archivists will invent motives for you, and they will always choose the stupid ones." Ivo cried a little and pretended it was the wind, and we all let him pretend, because that was a kindness the mountain itself would have endorsed. Maren said nothing for a long time, and then, at the edge of the path down, she stopped and put her bare hand flat on the stone the way I had.

"For Tam," she said, too low for anyone but me to hear — though I suspect the mountain heard, and I suspect the mountain kept it. Her brother. Sold by Kestrian's collectors years ago, one voice among ten thousand. Whether he'd gone up with the auroras or stayed in the stone, she'd never stop listening for him. "Whatever's left of him. I'll keep the roads too."

The hum under her palms rose, brief and gentle, like a hand laid over hers.

She straightened, embarrassed, inquisitor-blank again. "Well. Let's get down before the weather does."

* * *

Halfway down the pilgrim path, Sef stopped so abruptly Ivo walked into her pack.

"Problem?" he asked.

She was staring at the little roadside shrine — the one with the god of suggested elbows — with her head tilted like a woman hearing a wrong note in a familiar song. Snow had slid from its roof in the night. Beneath the snow, where decades of weathering should have shown only blank stone, there were letters.

Fresh-cut. Sharp-edged. Glinting with a faint, unmistakable sheen of vow-light.

Sef brushed the stone clean with her sleeve, and I read the words upside down and felt the ground tilt gently under everything I'd rebuilt:

**THE FIRST PROMISE WAS NEVER THE LAST. WHO HOLDS THE ROADS MUST ANSWER WHEN THE OTHER MOUNTAINS SPEAK.**

Below it, a date. Not tonight's date.

Tomorrow's.

"Well," Sef said faintly, uncapping her ink. "It seems the Codex isn't finished with us after all."
