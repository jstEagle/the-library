## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The waystation at Thornfell Cross smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who drank to forget and succeeded only in remembering louder. Ada Nystrom had been coming here for three years, long enough that the innkeep no longer watched her hands, long enough that the regulars had stopped noticing the pale-eyed woman in the corner who ordered one cup of small beer and nursed it until the candles burned low.

She liked being unnoticeable. It was the closest thing to a virtue her gift allowed.

The merchant sat three tables away, a broad man with salt-and-pepper whiskers and the soft, unfocused stare of someone who had drunk himself to the exact border of sleep. His name was Aldous Crane, though he hadn't given it. She knew it the way she knew most things about him—by the shape of his memories leaking through his skin like heat through a shuttered window. Grief made people leak. Grief was a cracked jug, and Ada had learned to hear every drip.

His daughter's name was Lira. She had died eleven months ago, fevered, on the road between Greywater Ford and the coast, and Aldous Crane carried the memory of her last morning everywhere he went because he could not bear to set it down and could not bear to hold it. He carried it the way a man carries a stone in his boot: limping, refusing to stop, refusing to take the boot off.

Ada finished her beer and crossed the room.

*Rules first.* She always said them in her head, an old litany worn smooth as a river stone. *Take only what is freely drowning. Take only what the taker can survive losing. Never take twice from the same soul. And never—never—take what isn't already trying to leave.*

It was not morality, exactly. It was craft. A memory torn whole out of a sound mind left a wound that showed—a slackness in the face, a stutter where certainty used to live—and wounds brought questions, and questions brought the kind of attention that got hedge-witches drowned in horse troughs by sensible villagers. But a memory already loosening, already half-sunk in grief or drink or despair, could be lifted like a coin from the bottom of a shallow pool, and the pool would barely ripple.

Aldous Crane's memory of his daughter's last morning was not merely loose. It was drowning. It wanted to go.

Ada sat down across from him without asking. That was part of the ritual too; asking made it theft, and she needed it not to be theft tonight.

"You're the memory-woman," he said. His tongue was thick but not stupid. "They say you buy sorrows."

"They say wrong." Her voice came out flat, practiced. "I don't buy anything. I *lift*. There's a difference."

"What difference?"

"A buyer takes what you'd rather keep. I take what you'd rather be rid of." She set both hands flat on the table between them, palms down, the way a physician might lay out his instruments. "Your daughter. The last morning. You've relived it four hundred times since the fever took her—I can see the edges of it fraying from here. Another year of this and there'll be nothing left of you but that morning wearing your body like a coat."

Crane flinched as if struck. Around them the waystation roared on—dice clattering, a fiddler mangling a reel, rain beginning to hammer the shutters—but between the two of them the air went still and private, the way air does around a deathbed.

"Can you truly—" He swallowed. "They say you take it whole. That afterward a man remembers she existed but not the end. Not the—" His voice broke on the word he couldn't finish. *The coughing. The little gray lips. The way she asked for water and then stopped asking.*

"I take what you give me," Ada said. "You'll keep her birthday. Keep her laugh, if you ever had the sense to notice it while she lived. Keep her name." She leaned forward. "What goes is yours to name, within reason. Most people choose the sickbed. Some choose the funeral. One man gave me his wedding day and wept with gratitude after, which told me more about his marriage than I cared to know."

That startled a broken half-laugh out of him, which was good. Fear tightened the mind; laughter loosened it. She had learned that from a smuggler named Maro, years ago, back when she still thought of herself as an apprentice learning tricks instead of a woman perfecting a hunger.

"How much?" Crane asked.

"For a dying-child memory?" She named a figure that would have bought his mule, his cart, and a season's lodging. He didn't blink. They never blinked at that part. It was the other price they haggled over—the one she couldn't put in words, the piece of themselves that left with the sorrow whether they understood it or not. Take a man's worst night and you took some of the mortar between his days. She had seen it: the merchant who sold her his grief and thereafter smiled too easily, agreed too readily, became a slightly hollowed version of the man he'd been. Lighter. Less.

*Lighter,* she thought, as she always did. *Everyone wants to be lighter. Nobody asks what the lightness is made of.*

"Done," Crane said.

"Then look at my hands."

He looked. Everyone did eventually; there was something in them that drew the eye even when they were empty, two pale ordinary hands scarred faintly at the fingertips with marks like old burns, though she had never touched fire. The sigils lived under the skin there, coiled and sleeping. She felt them stir now, the way dogs stir when their master reaches for the leash.

"Think of the morning," she said softly. "All of it. Don't push it toward me. Just—hold it up. Like showing me a picture."

And he did, because grief-stricken men always did, because the truth no one admitted was that they were desperate to show someone, anyone, the thing that was killing them.

The memory rose in him like heat shimmer. Ada saw it before she took it—that was how it always began, the lifting preceded by a seeing, the thief forced to appraise before she stole. A grey dawn in a rented room above a dyer's shop. A girl of nine propped on pillows, hair stuck to her forehead with sweat, insisting she felt *much better, Papa, truly*, and managing a smile that would have fooled anyone who hadn't spent eleven months memorizing his daughter's face. A bowl of broth going cold. A window with rain on it, the same rain, it seemed, that fell outside the waystation now, eleven months later and forty miles east.

*Don't,* Ada told herself, as she always told herself. *Don't look at the details. Details are how they get into you.*

She looked anyway. She couldn't help it. The girl pointed at a crow on the windowsill and called it a bad omen and laughed at her own joke, and Aldous Crane had laughed too, God help him, because he'd believed her, because children recovered, because the fever had broken for the neighbor's boy just the week before—

Ada breathed in.

The taking itself took less time than a heartbeat. Her fingers brushed the backs of his hands—not skin to skin, never quite, but close enough that the gap between filled with a pressure like held breath—and the sigil passed. She felt it slide up her arm and settle somewhere behind her sternum, cold and heavy and *bright*, a sealed lamp of someone else's worst hour, joining the hundreds of others she carried in the dark vault of herself.

Across the table, Aldous Crane exhaled. His shoulders dropped an inch. His face, for the first time since she'd begun watching him, went genuinely calm—not empty, not yet, the hollowing would come later, in the weeks after, when he reached for the morning and found only smooth water—but calm the way a man is calm after setting down a load he has carried so long his spine has grown around it.

"She liked crows," he said wonderingly. "Did she? I feel like she liked crows."

"She liked crows," Ada confirmed quietly. "The rest is yours to rebuild."

He paid without counting the coin. He thanked her. He walked out into the rain lighter than he'd come in, and Ada sat alone at the table and pressed her fist against her mouth and hated herself with the efficient, familiar precision of long practice.

*Three hundred and six.* This one made three hundred and six. She kept count. She didn't know why—there was no absolution in arithmetic—but the number mattered to her the way a scar matters, as proof that something had happened and had cost something.

The fiddler played on. Dice rattled. The world did not care, and that was somehow the worst of it.

* *

The upstairs room she rented at Thornfell Cross was narrow and clean, with a bed, a basin, a shuttered window, and a floorboard she trusted. Ada bolted the door, moved the board, and lifted out the oilcloth packet that held everything she owned that could not be carried in a coat.

Coin, mostly. Papers of passage under three names. A knife she'd never had occasion to use and kept anyway, because a thief who cannot defend her hoard is only storing treasure for the next thief. And beneath all of it, wrapped in its own square of cloth, the thing she never sold, never traded, never spoke of:

Her cache. Her own stolen goods.

Every memory-thief needed a hiding place, and the clever ones hid their takings outside themselves—in sigils inked onto vellum, pressed into wax, sunk in river mud—for a simple reason. A sigil carried in the flesh weighed on the soul. Ada had known thieves who staggered under thirty, fifty, a hundred borrowed years, walking bent like pack-mules, faces crowded with lives they'd never lived. She carried more than any of them and stood straight, because she alone had learned the trick of putting her own past away.

She unwrapped the cloth with hands that did not shake, because they never shook anymore, and counted the sigils stored there the way a miser counts coin: quickly, ritually, touching each one.

There were eleven. Eleven pale scars on scraps of cured hide, each one humming faintly against her fingertips like a trapped moth. Eleven memories she had cut out of her own skull and locked away, because keeping them inside had been unbearable and living without them had proved only slightly less so.

Eleven, and not one of them older than her fifteenth year.

That was the arithmetic that haunted her, on nights like this, in rooms like this, with the rain talking nonsense against the shutters. Three hundred and six memories taken from others, and every single one filed neatly in the vault of her body, catalogued, retrievable, hers. But her own childhood—her mother's voice, her father's face if she'd ever had one, the village she must have been born in, the first time she'd discovered what her hands could do—all of it sat in these eleven scraps of hide, exiled, and beyond them lay a blankness that was not forgetting. Forgetting was soft-edged. This was a wall. Somewhere behind that wall was a girl who had become a thief, and Ada had searched that wall for fifteen years and never once found a door.

Other thieves lost their early years the natural way, the way everyone did, in fragments and fog. Ada had checked. In the taprooms where her kind drank, she'd asked, casual as asking after weather: *How far back do you remember?* And they'd answer, *Four, maybe five. My mother's kitchen. Snow. Why?*

Snow. Kitchens. Mothers' voices.

Nothing. She had nothing. The earliest thing she owned outright was a rain-soaked road at fifteen, a fence named Maro teaching her which merchants leaked and which were sealed, and the bone-deep knowledge—unexplained, sourceless, absolute—that she must never, ever let anyone know what her hands could do until her hands were strong enough to protect her.

She repacked the cache and sat on the edge of the bed and did the other thing she did every night, the penance, the audit. She opened the vault.

Not far. Never far. Just a crack, enough to let one sigil speak.

Tonight it was the dyer's daughter. Lira, aged nine, calling a crow a bad omen. The memory sat behind Ada's sternum like a swallowed coal, and she let it warm her the way she let all of them warm her, briefly, guiltily. This was the secret under all her other secrets, the one she'd admit to no fence, no client, no god: she *kept* them. She was supposed to be a broker, a cutpurse of pain, moving sorrow from overflowing hands to empty ones—there were buyers, God knew, rich old women who paid dearly to feel a dead child's laugh again, exiles who craved a homeland's taste, and Ada told herself she performed a service, grief recycled, nothing wasted. But the truth was that she skimmed. She lingered. She lived in other people's pasts because she had none of her own, and every night she cracked the vault and visited, a squatter in borrowed years.

Somewhere out there was a childhood with her name on it. Eleven scraps of hide away, or gone forever. She no longer knew which frightened her more.

Outside, the rain thickened. Downstairs, a door banged and voices rose—one of the drovers, drunk, shouting about the roads, about soldiers, about something happening at Greywater Ford. Ada ignored it. Soldiers were permanent weather in Thornfell; you learned to walk around them like puddles.

She pinched out the candle and lay down in the dark, and in the dark the sigils murmured their nightly murmur, three hundred and six whispers settling around her like a congregation: a fisherman's terror of deep water, a widow's last argument with her husband, a deserter's shame, a bride's cold feet on a winter stair, Lira's crow laughing on the sill. Other people's yesterdays, keeping her company in the endless present tense of herself.

She was almost asleep when she heard the boots on the stairs.

Heavy boots, unhurried, two sets, climbing past the second floor to the third, where the drover was still shouting about Greywater Ford—and stopping. Coming back down. Stopping at her door.

Ada was out of bed and through the window and onto the stable roof before her conscious mind finished the sentence, barefoot in the rain, the cache strapped to her ribs, three hundred and six souls whispering *run*.

Below her, through the shutter slats, lamplight moved in her rented room. A voice, clipped and official, asked the innkeep a question she was too far away to hear.

But she caught the answer, floating up through the rain, and it froze her to the roof tiles.

"No, Captain, no one by that name. A memory-woman, you say? No, sir. Nothing like that comes through here."
