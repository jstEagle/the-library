## 2. Proclamation at Greywater Ford

The trouble with Greywater Ford was that everyone knew everyone's business, and Ada Nystrom had spent five years making certain her own was not among it.

She came down from the Thornfell road in the grey hour before dawn, hood up, pack light, boots wrapped in wool to quiet the gravel. The town sat where the Vess river flattened into a crossing wide enough for carts, all slate roofs and smoke-threads and gulls arguing over fish guts on the shambles' steps. Market day. Banners already going up along the timber-framed guildhalls—blue and silver, the crown's colors—and Ada felt the old instinct rise in her throat like bile: *leave now, cross the ford, be gone before the bells.*

But she needed salt. She needed rope. And most of all she needed coin, because the last fragment she'd sold—a cooper's memory of his wedding day, three years gone and fading anyway—had bought her a week of bread and a night under a roof that smelled of tallow and other people's dreams.

So she stayed. She told herself it would only be an hour.

She was wrong by exactly as long as it took a crier to climb the steps of the assize hall.

---

Ada heard him before she saw him: the flat brass blast of a herald's horn cutting through market noise, the sudden hush spreading through the square like oil across water. She was standing at a spice-seller's stall with a twist of saffron in her palm, weighing its price against her hunger, when the crowd began to turn—all faces swinging toward the hall steps the way iron filings swing toward a lodestone.

The crier wore crown livery. That alone would have gathered eyes. But it was what hung from his belt—a scroll case sealed with black wax, black wax being reserved for proclamations of hunt and forfeiture—that made the whole market go still enough to hear the river.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third," the crier called, voice trained to carry over battle, "and by writ of the Lord Chancellor Ives: let it be known throughout the Marches of Thornfell."

He unrolled the proclamation. Ada did not need to hear the words. She had been dreading them for months, ever since the first rumor came down the smuggler roads—that the crown had finally noticed what memory-wielders were, what they could take, and how easily they could walk through a man's mind and out again carrying his secrets like a stolen purse.

"*Let every wielder of the forbidden art of mnemosurgery surrender themselves to lawful authority,*" the crier read. "*Let every citizen who shelters such a one be judged an accessory. Let every citizen who reports such a one receive the bounty of fifty crowns.*"

Fifty crowns. Someone in the crowd whistled low. A year's wages for a laborer. Ada watched the number land on faces around her—the way it brightened some eyes and darkened others—and understood with cold clarity that from this morning forward, every person she passed was worth fifty crowns richer for betraying her.

"*And further:*" the crier's voice dropped, which somehow made it worse, made people lean in, "*let it be known that the art itself is hereby declared abomination. Any found to bear the mark of the thief's gift shall be taken alive if possible. If not—*"

He paused. He was enjoying this. Ada hated him with a clean, simple hatred.

"*—the crown's inquisitors are authorized to do what must be done.*"

The horn sounded again. Somewhere near the fish stalls a woman began to cry quietly, and Ada thought, absurdly, of the saffron still pinched between her fingers. She set it back on the stall with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when your hands want to shake.

That was when she saw the hedge-witch.

---

Old Maren had traded at the edge of Greywater Ford's markets for as long as anyone could remember—charms against toothache, teas for birthing mothers, little knotted cords said to ease bad dreams. She wasn't a memory-wielder. Ada had brushed past her mind once, years ago, accidentally, in a crowded lane: Maren's gift was small and warm, a knack for easing pain, nothing more. Half herb-craft, half prayer, entirely harmless.

It didn't matter. The crown's writ didn't distinguish.

They came through the crowd fast—four men in half-armor, not crown regulars but the Chancellor's new company, black-and-silver, moving with the particular confidence of men who have just been told the law is on their side no matter what they do. They took Maren at her stall. She didn't even have time to gather her charms; a soldier's boot scattered them across the cobbles, dried lavender and knotted thread and a jar of honey, and the jar broke, and the smell of honey spread sweet and terrible through the square.

"You've got the look of it," the squad's sergeant was saying, hauling Maren up by one arm. She was tiny. Her feet barely touched the ground. "Old women with their potions. We'll see what the inquisitor finds behind those eyes."

"I'm nothing," Maren said. Her voice was thin but steady. "I ease pain. That's all. Ask anyone. Ask—" and she looked out at the crowd, at the faces she had tended for forty years, births and fevers and griefs, and Ada watched her understand, in real time, that no one would meet her eyes.

Because fifty crowns was fifty crowns. Because fear is contagious. Because it is easier to watch than to speak.

"They'll see," the sergeant said. "Inquisitor Vale has methods. You'll show us everything you are, granny."

*Vale.*

The name went through Ada like a blade slipped between ribs—clean, precise, devastating. She kept her face still. Twenty years of practice kept her face still. But inside her head, everything was suddenly noise.

Emeric Vale. Captain now. Of course he was; he'd always been destined for rank, for the straight-backed certainty of service. Five years since she'd vanished from his life without explanation, and now he was here, wearing the Chancellor's silver, hunting people like her with methods—

She should have left at the word *proclamation*. She knew that. She stayed rooted anyway, watching them drag Maren toward the assize hall, watching the old woman's slippers scrape the stones, watching a child in the front row pick up a sprig of scattered lavender and hold it like something precious.

Maren turned her head once, at the top of the steps, and looked back at the crowd—not accusing, just looking, the way you look at your own life from the edge of it. And then the doors swallowed her, and the market exhaled, and everywhere around Ada the great machine of ordinary commerce groaned back into motion, because people must eat, even on the day their neighbors begin to disappear.

Ada bought her salt and her rope. She paid with exact coin, met the vendor's eyes exactly as long as politeness required, and walked out of Greywater Ford at a pace just short of running.

Behind her, the blue-and-silver banners snapped in the wind off the lake road, and somewhere in the assize hall, an old woman was learning what the crown's methods were.

Ada climbed. The mountains took her the way they always did—grudgingly, in switchbacks and scree—but they took her, and by midafternoon the town was a smudge of smoke below, and the wind off the high passes carried nothing but stone and snowmelt and silence.

Except it didn't carry silence. It carried the name.

*Vale.*

Five years ago she had left a letter on his pillow and a hole in his life, and told herself it was mercy. Now the crown had given him a writ and a company and permission to open skulls, metaphorically speaking, and Ada Nystrom—thief of memories, keeper of none of her own—climbed into the Thornfell highlands knowing, with the certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant, that the hunt had only ever been going to end one way.

She walked north, toward Lake Vess, toward the smuggler caves above the waterline, toward whatever waited there.

And far below, in the town she'd fled, a captain in black-and-silver dismounted at the assize hall, and paused, and frowned at something written in chalk on a wall where no chalk should be.

* * *

Captain Emeric Vale had interrogated forty-one suspects in his three years as an inquisitor, and he had learned to distrust the moment when a prisoner stopped being afraid.

Maren the hedge-witch had reached that moment an hour ago. Now she sat in the plain room with its plain table, hands folded, watching him with the mild patience of a woman who had outlived three kings and considered the fourth unlikely to improve matters. Her file lay between them. It was thin. Charms. Teas. Complaints from a rival midwife, ten years old.

"You're not a mnemurge," Emeric said.

"No."

"The writ doesn't require that you be one."

"No," she agreed. "It requires that I have the *look* of one. Which, I'm told, I do." She smiled faintly. "Grey hair and no husband. It's a dangerous combination, Captain."

He almost smiled himself. He didn't. Instead he opened the second document on the table—the one that had actually brought him to Greywater Ford—and turned it so she could read it.

It was a sketch. A sigil, drawn in careful ink: a spiral broken at its center, with seven points radiating outward like a wheel missing its hub.

"Have you seen this mark?"

Something moved behind Maren's eyes. Not recognition exactly. Recognition of *weight*. "Where did you get that?" she asked, and her voice had lost its humor entirely.

"It was found carved into a granary door at Hollowmere. Sixteen years ago. It's been appearing again—fresh carvings, along the Thornfell roads. Smugglers' marks, we assumed. But smugglers don't carve things that make my archivists go pale." He leaned forward. "You know what it is. I can see that you do."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the river. When she spoke, her voice was gentle, almost pitying. "Captain Vale. Do you know why the Chancellor wants memory-wielders hunted? Truly? It isn't fear of thieves. A man can guard his purse. It's that a mnemurge can walk into any lord's or minister's head and walk out with his treasons, his bribes, his sins." She tilted her head. "A gift like that, in the wrong hands, could bring down a government. Or expose one."

"I'm not in the habit of discussing statecraft with prisoners."

"No. You're in the habit of asking about sigils." She pushed the sketch back across the table with one finger. "Then ask yourself this, boy: if the mark frightens your archivists—who are learned men, who know their histories—perhaps the mark isn't a smuggler's sign. Perhaps it's older than smuggling. Perhaps it's older than your crown."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning I'm an old woman with lavender and honey," Maren said, folding her hands again, "and I have nothing more to say, and you may write that down."

He studied her. She held his gaze without flinching, and Emeric Vale, who could read fear the way sailors read weather, found no fear in her at all—which was, in its way, more frightening than fear. People were only calm like that when they believed something larger than the crown was in motion.

He closed the file. "You'll be transported to Thornfell Keep for formal examination."

"I supposed as much." As the guards took her arms, she paused beside him, close enough that only he could hear. "One thing for free, Captain, since you asked so nicely about the spiral. If you're hunting someone who bears that mark—truly hunting them—then pray you find them before the thaw does."

"What does that mean?"

But she only smiled that mild, maddening smile, and the guards walked her out.

---

He stood afterward at the window of the assize hall, watching the market dissolve into evening, and let himself think about the thing he had been not-thinking about all day.

The mark. He'd seen it twice before in his career—once in an archive folio, once scratched into the lintel of a burned-out waystation on the Thornfell smuggler roads. Both times, the same odd sensation: not familiarity, but *echo*, as though his eye recognized a shape his mind had never learned. His superiors called it a cult-sign, decades dead. Fine. Then why did the Chancellor's new writ include it, specifically, in the annex of forbidden symbols? Why had Lord Ives' men been asking about it in every market town from here to the coast?

And why, when he'd stood in the crowd that morning listening to the proclamation, had he felt—as clearly as a hand on the back of his neck—the particular prickle of a thief's craft passing through the square?

He'd turned. Of course he'd turned; that was the training. And he'd caught nothing: a thousand faces, a market in motion, a woman in a grey hood buying salt. Nothing. Except that the crowd's attention had bent, very slightly, very briefly, away from a single point—people unconsciously giving space the way water gives space around a moving stone—and by the time he'd pushed through to the spice-seller's stall, the stone was gone, leaving only a faint disturbance and a vendor saying, confused, "She paid me exact coin, sir, I never saw her face—"

Thieves' marks weren't visible. That was the first thing they taught you. But a skilled mnemurge moving through a crowd left ripples—tiny aversions, tiny blind spots—and Emeric had spent three years learning to feel them, and he had felt one today, in Greywater Ford, at the exact hour the hunt was proclaimed.

Someone had been standing twenty feet from him, hearing their own death read aloud, and had simply... walked away.

He thought about the ripples. He thought about the skill required to leave so few. There were perhaps a dozen wielders in the Marches capable of it, and his lists had names for all of them.

And beneath the lists, beneath the discipline, in the place he did not examine, something older stirred—an image he had not summoned in five years: rain on a smuggler's trail, a laugh low in a hood, fingers laced with his in the dark, a woman who had taught him that a person's memories could be handled gently, like birds, and who had vanished one autumn morning leaving a letter he had burned unread because burning it hurt less than finishing it.

*No,* he told himself, the way he'd told himself for five years. *She's dead, or she's across the sea, or she was never what you wanted her to be.*

He lit a lamp and sat down and wrote his report: *Proclamation delivered without incident. One detainee, low priority. Possible presence of skilled mnemurge in market during proceedings; investigation continuing.*

Then he added the annex item—the spiral, the seven points, the broken hub—and noted that fresh carvings of it had been reported within thirty miles, trending north.

North. Toward the high passes. Toward Lake Vess.

He stared at the word a long time, pen hovering, feeling the echo again, stronger now, pulling at some buried part of him like a hook in deep water.

Outside, the first snow of the season was beginning to fall on Greywater Ford, soft and silent, erasing roads in both directions.

By dawn, Captain Emeric Vale would ride north with twelve men and a warrant signed in black wax. And somewhere above the tree line, unaware of how precisely the hunt was already bending around her, Ada Nystrom reached the smuggler caves above Lake Vess just as the light died—and heard, rising faint and impossible from the black water far below, something that sounded almost like her own name.
