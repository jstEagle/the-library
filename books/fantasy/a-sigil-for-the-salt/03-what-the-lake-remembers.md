## 3. What the Lake Remembers

The cave above Lake Vess had a name among smugglers—Hollowjaw, for the way its mouth gaped over the water like something mid-swallow—and Ada Nystrom had hidden in it twice before, both times with coin enough to buy silence and wine enough to forget why she needed it. Tonight she had neither. Tonight she had only the grey wool cloak on her back, three sigils sewn into its hem like cheap embroidery, and the sound of her own breathing echoing off wet stone.

She had run from Greywater Ford two days ago. Two days of ridgeline and scree, of sleeping in ditches with one eye open, of watching every traveler on the road below for the flash of crown-blue that meant an inquisitor's patrol. The hedge-witch's face still came to her when she closed her eyes—the old woman dragged through the mud by her hair while the criers read the proclamation in voices polished smooth as river stones, *by order of the Crown, all wielders of stolen memory are declared abominations, to be taken alive if convenient*—

*If convenient.* Ada knew what that phrasing meant. It meant the crown would prefer them breathing only as long as breathing served the interrogators.

She knelt at the cave's inner wall where a spring seeped through a crack in the rock, cold and mineral-bright, and drank until her throat stopped burning. Then, because thirst was handled and fear was not, she allowed herself the small ritual that kept her sane on the roads: inventory.

Three sigils in the hem. The merchant's daughter—that was the grief she'd lifted at the waystation, warm still, heavy as a wet coat; she'd need to sell it soon before it went stale, though the thought of parting with it made something in her chest twist sideways. A soldier's memory of a massacre he'd been ordered to commit, taken off him in a card game he'd lost so badly he'd wept. And a sliver of a midwife's hands, decades of catching children, which Ada kept not because it sold but because holding it felt like holding something holy.

That was the arithmetic of her life. That was what her gift had made her: a purse full of other people's yesterdays, spending them piece by piece, never keeping anything that belonged to herself.

Because there was almost nothing that did. She'd discovered that truth young and had built her whole craft around avoiding it. Other thieves of memory hoarded their own pasts like dragons on gold. Ada couldn't. Somewhere between her sixth year and her twelfth there was a hole, smooth-edged and total, and everything she'd tried to pour into it slid out again like water through cupped hands. She remembered learning to steal memories—remembered the old woman who taught her, remembered her first lift, the taste of someone else's birthday on her tongue. But ask her about her mother's face and there was nothing. Ask her where she'd been born and the question fell into the dark without ever landing.

*A thief with no origin,* she thought, pressing her palm flat against the cold stone. *Fitting.*

Below the cave mouth, Lake Vess lay flat and black under a sky rinsed clean of stars by low cloud. The smuggler maps called it bottomless. The fishermen who worked its southern shore called it worse things and wouldn't say them aloud after dark. Ada had crossed it once by ferry and spent the whole passage fighting the urge to look down, as if the water were a pupil dilating around her.

Tonight, with nowhere left to run but up into snowfields that would kill her slower than soldiers would, she found herself looking down anyway.

The water was doing something strange.

At first she took it for moonlight breaking through cloud—but the cloud cover hadn't moved, and the glow was coming from beneath, a pale silver-green luminescence rising through the black like breath rising through a sleeper's chest. It spread outward in slow rings, each ring wider than the last, until the whole visible sweep of the lake was lit from within, and Ada pressed back against the cave wall with her heart hammering against her ribs.

Then the light spoke.

Not in sound—not exactly. It arrived the way memories arrived when she took them, bypassing the ear entirely, blooming behind her eyes with a completeness that made speech seem crude by comparison. But she heard it as a voice anyway, because some animal part of her mind insisted on translating wonder into something with a shape:

***You are far from home, little thief.***

Ada's hand flew to the knife at her belt—a stupid gesture, and she knew it, and the voice seemed to know it too, because the next words carried something that was almost amusement, if amusement could be ancient and sorrowful.

***Steel is no use against me. I am older than steel. I am older than the mountain you are hiding inside. Come to the water, Ada Nystrom.***

Her name. Her *name*, spoken by a lake, in a cave three days' hard ride from anywhere anyone knew her face. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to climb higher into the mountains and keep climbing until the world ran out. But her feet were already moving, carrying her toward the cave mouth across stone worn smooth by centuries of smuggler boots, because—she realized, with a kind of horrified clarity—because the voice had said her name the way her mother must have said it once, in a memory she didn't have, and some starved thing deep inside her was desperate to be known.

She stopped at the lip of the cave, toes overhanging the drop to a ledge of black rock where the water lapped close.

"Who are you?" Her voice cracked on the second word. "What are you?"

***I am what remembers.*** The light pulsed, slow, patient, tidal. ***You have spent your life trading in human memory—little lives, little griefs, a daughter here, a sin there. All of it beautiful and small. But every river runs somewhere, child. Every memory ever held by mortal flesh drains, in the end, into the deep places. I am where they drain. I am the memory of this land, and I have been waiting five years for you to come home.***

"I'm not—" Ada laughed, high and unsteady. "I'm not from here. I don't have a home. I don't even have a—"

She stopped. Because the lake was showing her something.

It came gently, which she hadn't expected; she braced for the violation of a forced memory, the sensation she inflicted on others daily and had never once endured herself. Instead it unfolded like a hand opening: an image of this same shore, centuries ago, when the trees were old-growth cedar and the water stood higher, lapping at stones carved with spirals. People stood on those stones—people, but wrong, tall and slender, skin gleaming with the same silver-green light that now filled the lake, throats ridged with lines that opened and closed like gills. And facing them, human figures in robes stitched with salt-white thread, and between the two peoples a structure of light rising out of the water: seven points, seven burning sigils arranged in a ring, each one humming a note that Ada felt in her teeth.

A pact. A bridge. Two realms holding hands across the surface of a lake.

And then—fire. Smoke. The ring of light shattering like glass struck by a hammer, sigils scattering into darkness, the tall luminous people sinking beneath the waves with their arms raised in grief or accusation, and the humans fleeing inland as the waters rose and rose and rose—

The vision released her and Ada found herself on her knees on the wet rock, gasping, tears freezing on her cheeks. She hadn't known she was crying.

"What," she whispered, "was that?"

***The Salt Covenant.*** The lake's voice had gone grave. ***The pact that bound my people's realm to yours, so that neither would drown the other—so that the waters would keep their borders and the borders keep their word. Seven seals held it fast, one for each promise made at the founding. Five years ago, on a night of fire at a place called Hollowmere, the covenant broke. The seals scattered. My king sleeps his long sleep beneath the waves with his court around him, dreaming of drowning your lowlands, and every winter the thaw comes earlier, and every spring the waters rise higher. This year, the last of the old wards fails. When the spring thaw comes, Lake Vess will not stay a lake. Neither will any of them.***

Ada shook her head, backing away from the edge. "I'm sorry. I truly am. But I'm a thief. I sell other people's pasts for copper. Whatever you think I can do about a drowned kingdom, you've got the wrong woman."

***Have I?***

The light gathered itself, drew inward, concentrated—and rose. It came up the cliff face in a slow column and hung in the air before her, and in the heart of the radiance Ada saw a shape take form: a sigil, intricate as frostwork, seven-angled, burning with a light that hurt to look at directly.

But that wasn't what stopped her breath.

What stopped her breath was where the sigil sat. It hovered in the air between them, yes—but threads of that light ran downward, inward, into *her*, into the space behind her ribs where stolen memories lived in their careful rows. The sigil wasn't floating near her. It was anchored to something inside her, glowing through her flesh like a coal through cloth.

***There it is,*** the lake said softly. ***The first seal. Do you see now, seal-bearer? You have carried it for years. You have slept beside it. You have traded lesser memories around it like a merchant stacking coins on a buried king's crown.***

"That's impossible." Ada's voice came out thin. "I'd know. I know every memory I carry—I catalogued them, I *invented* the catalogue—there's nothing in me that looks like *that*, there's nothing in me that *glows*, there's nothing in me except—"

Except the hole. Except the missing years. Except the smooth-edged dark between her sixth year and her twelfth that no memory would fill, that she had told herself for fifteen years was simply damage, simply loss, simply the price some cruel god had charged her for her gift.

The lake let the silence stretch until the truth had time to arrive on its own.

"No," Ada said. "No. Those aren't mine to give. Those aren't anything—they're just absence, they're just nothing, you can't hide a seal inside *nothing*—"

***Can you not?*** Something moved through the light that might have been pity. ***Who better to guard a treasure than a thief who believes her own coffers empty? Someone hid those years from you, Ada Nystrom—or you hid them from yourself. Either way, the first seal sleeps in the deepest vault you own. And I can show you the door.***

Ada wrapped her arms around herself. The wind coming off the lake was glacial, and she was shaking, and none of it was from the cold. Wonder and dread had collided somewhere beneath her sternum and she could no longer tell which feeling was which. This was the moment she had spent her whole life running from, she understood suddenly—not the inquisitors, not the proclamations, not the gallows waiting for hedge-witches. This. Being *known*. Being claimed by something larger than a market town and a price list. Every rule she'd ever set for herself—never carry what isn't yours to carry, never owe what can't be paid in coin, never, *never* let anyone tell you who you are—all of it crumbling like riverbank in flood.

"If I open it," she said carefully, "if I go digging in my own head for this seal—what happens to whatever else is buried there? What happens to *me*?"

***I do not know,*** the lake answered, and its honesty was somehow worse than any lie. ***No seal-bearer has ever refused the calling. You would be the first.***

"And if I say no? If I walk away right now, up the mountain, and keep walking?"

***Then the waters rise, and thousands drown, and you will live a long time, little thief, because guilt is preservative.***

The light pulsed once, twice, patient as tide. Below, the vast black body of Lake Vess breathed its slow silver breath, and Ada thought of the drowned court sleeping in its depths, dreaming of flood, and of the lowland towns she'd passed through a hundred times selling grief to the grieving—towns with names and markets and children, towns full of memories not yet ripe for stealing.

She thought of the hole in her past. Fifteen years she'd treated it as a wound. Now the wound was speaking to her, and asking to be opened, and telling her it had teeth.

"One memory," she said at last. Her voice steadied as she said it, the way a thief's hands steady over a lock. "You show me the door. I decide whether to walk through. And if what's inside destroys me—if it takes the rest of what I am—then the covenant stays broken and the flood can take us all, because I won't trade myself for the world. I've seen what that trade does to people. I've *sold* what that trade does to people."

***Agreed,*** said the lake, and the sorrow in it deepened, as if it already knew what waited behind the door and wished it didn't. ***Come to the water's edge, seal-bearer. Let me show you the first night you ever stole—from whom, and what, and why. Let me show you ash.***

Ada descended the last few feet of rock and put her hand into the water.

It closed around her wrist like a gentle fist, and the light rushed up her arm—

—and the lake's voice cut off mid-word, and the glow guttered, and every ring of silver-green on the surface of Lake Vess shuddered and flattened at once, as if something vast below had turned over in its sleep.

From the center of the lake, moving against the current, something was rising.
