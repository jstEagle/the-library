## 4. The Drowned Court's Envoy

The voice in the water had not stopped.

Ada had spent the night wedged into the smuggler cave above Lake Vess with her back against cold stone and her boots drawn up to her chest, listening to it. Not words, exactly. Not at first. More like the feeling of a word spoken just below hearing—the shape of a name pressed against the inside of her skull from the other side of a wall. She had slept in caves like this a hundred times, on a dozen lakes, and never once had the water spoken. But then, she had never before watched a covenant sigil burn inside one of her own stolen memories like a coal wrapped in cloth.

*Seal-bearer,* the lake had called her.

She did not want to be a bearer of anything. Bearers were hunted. Bearer was a word that got hedge-witches dragged through market squares by their hair while criers read their crimes aloud in a bored official cadence.

Dawn came gray and slow through the cave mouth. Below her, Lake Vess lay flat as hammered tin, and for one merciful hour she let herself believe the night had been a dream—that the hunger in her gift, the strange resonance when she'd touched the water, was only exhaustion playing tricks on a tired thief. She chewed the last of her waystation bread, which tasted of rye and mildew, and began checking her kit out of pure habit: lockpicks, three vials of tallow-salve, the folded squares of silk where she kept sigils too dangerous or too sad to sell.

Her hand paused over the innermost pocket. The one no buyer had ever seen.

Inside lay the memory she had taken from the lake's surface last night—or rather, the memory the lake had *given* her, pressing it up through her fingers like something surfacing for air. It sat against her palm now as a faint warmth, a sigil unlike any she had ever lifted. Human memories came to her hand with texture: grief was gritty, joy was slick, love was warm and heavy as a sleeping cat. This was none of those things. This was *salt*. Cold, vast, patient salt, and beneath it something singing.

She closed her fist around it and told herself, firmly, that she would throw it back into the lake and walk south to the lowlands where nobody knew her face, and she would find some fat merchant with a guilty conscience and live quietly off his secrets until the hunt burned itself out.

The lake had other opinions.

The water moved.

Not wind-ruffled, not current-shifted—*moved*, all of it at once, a single deliberate contraction like the flinch of a great sleeping animal. The stillness of the lake broke outward from a point near the shore in widening rings, and Ada was on her feet with a knife in her hand before her mind caught up to her body, because in five years on the Thornfell roads she had learned that anything moving toward you in the wilds was either predator or soldier, and both required the same response.

What rose from the water was neither.

It came up slowly, without splashing, the way a body rises in a dream. First the head: a long, elegant skull crowned with dark hair that moved as if still underwater, streaming and settling, streaming and settling. Then shoulders draped in what she took for a moment to be robes of silver-gray silk until the light shifted and she saw they were scales, thousands of them, fine as fish-scale and arranged with the deliberate beauty of court dress. The torso was human—a man's torso, lean, the chest bare except for a gorget of nacreous plate that gleamed like the inside of a shell. From the waist down, where legs should have been, the body tapered into the powerful fluke and ridged flank of an enormous salmon, silver-shot with pink, trailing water that fell upward in defiance of every law Ada knew, droplets climbing back into the lake behind him like beads drawn along a string.

His face stopped her knife halfway to a throwing grip. It was a beautiful face and a grieving one—high cheekbones, wide-set eyes the pale gold of a fish's iris, and an expression of such profound, practiced sorrow that Ada felt, absurdly, as though she had walked uninvited into a funeral and been handed a drink.

He regarded her across forty feet of gray morning air. Then he inclined his head with the precise, economical grace of a courtier acknowledging someone of inconvenient rank.

"Seal-bearer." His voice was the voice from the night—layered, resonant, each word carrying an undertone like sound heard through water. "You are awake. Good. The Court feared you would sleep until the thaw took your lowlands, and we have so little time for courtesies."

Ada did not lower the knife. Her mouth had gone dry. "I think," she said, and was mildly proud that her voice came out level, "you've mistaken me for someone important."

"I mistake nothing." He drifted nearer, not swimming so much as *arriving*, the water bearing him to the shingle below the cave mouth with the deference of a servant presenting a guest. Up close he smelled of deep places—cold stone, old rain, and underneath it the clean iron scent of lake depths. "I am Sef, envoy extraordinary of the Drowned Court of Vess, voice of His Drowned Majesty in all matters touching the dry world. And you are Ada Nystrom, daughter of Maren Nystrom, keeper of the Salt Covenant, and you carry six—" he paused, and his gilded eyes flicked to her fist, to the warmth glowing faintly between her fingers, "—no. Forgive me. One, and the echo of six. You carry the first sigil already, though I see you do not yet know how to open it."

"I don't carry anything." She tucked her fist behind her back like a child hiding stolen sweets, which, she reflected bitterly, was exactly what it was. "I'm a thief. Whatever the water told you about me, it exaggerated."

"The water does not exaggerate. The water is incapable of exaggeration; it can only remember." Sef's sorrowful face did not change, but something in it sharpened, the way light sharpens when cloud slides off a peak. "And the water remembers you, seal-bearer. It remembers a woman who walked its shore last night and touched it with hands full of stolen years, and it remembers the sigil waking inside her at the touch. You were made for this. Or made into this. The distinction has occupied our philosophers for two hundred years and I will spare you the debate."

"Kind of you." Ada edged sideways along the cave wall, keeping stone between herself and the shore, keeping the knife loose in her grip. Envoy. Drowned Court. His Drowned Majesty. Every smuggler's tale she'd ever half-believed was assembling itself around her like a trap closing, and she hated traps more than she feared them. "Look—Sef. I'm flattered. Genuinely. But whatever you think I am, I lift memories and sell them to people who pay. That's the whole of my ambition. I don't bear seals. I don't restore covenants. I've never restored so much as a fence."

"You will restore the covenant." He said it without heat, without threat, the way a man announces that winter will come. "Or the spring thaw will come without it, and the lakes will rise, and the drowned kingdom will take back the lowlands that were pledged to us and taken from us, and the dry folk will learn what it means to break faith with the deep. Two hundred years ago, seal-bearer, your kind swore a pact at the origin-stone. Seven sigils bound the realms together: ours kept the waters gentle, yours kept the borders open and the drowned honored among the living. The pact broke. The sigils scattered. And every year since, the thaw has risen higher. Last spring, Greywater Ford flooded to the eaves. This spring—" he spread his webbed, elegant hands, and the gesture was terrible in its gentleness, "—this spring the water comes for the towns themselves. Unless the seven are gathered and the rite is sung at the origin-stone before the ice breaks."

"How long?"

"Forty days. Perhaps fifty, if the high snows hold."

"And if I say no?"

Sef went very still. Fish-still. The stillness of a thing that lives in a place where movement means death. When he spoke again, the courtly music had drained out of his voice, leaving something older and colder beneath, the sound the lake had made in the dark.

"Then I return to my king and report that the seal refuses, and the Court will conclude that the dry world has chosen war after all. And I will not need to tell you, Ada Nystrom, what the deep does when it chooses war. Your own histories forgot us within a generation—we made sure of it—but the oldest villages on these shores still nail blue charms above their doors and won't say why. Ask them. They remember being afraid, even if they've forgotten the reason."

*Blue charms.* Ada's grandmother had nailed one above her door. Ada had assumed it was for luck.

Her knife hand ached from gripping. She forced her fingers to loosen, one at a time. Fear was a currency too, in her trade—she'd bought fear off soldiers and sold it to gamblers—and the first rule of handling it was to price it honestly instead of letting it spend you. So she priced it. A drowned kingdom. Forty days. An inquisitor with her old lover's face hunting her through the mountains. And now a salmon-courtier asking her to save the world.

"I'm going to tell you the truth," she said, "because it seems rude to lie to a man who's mostly fish. I didn't choose any of this. I woke up one morning when I was small and I could steal what people remembered, and I've been surviving on it ever since. I don't know anything about pacts or origin-stones or drowned kings. I know fences, tollhouses, and which waystations water their beer. If there's a seal inside me somewhere, someone put it there without asking, and I've spent my whole life returning things that were put in people without asking. Why would I become one more of them?"

Something flickered across Sef's mournful features—not anger. Recognition. The look of a courtier who has finally found the argument he was sent equipped to make.

"Because you of all the dry folk should understand what it is to be robbed," he said softly. "Two hundred years ago, the crown of the dry realm stole the covenant's power for itself. Did you believe the hunt for memory-wielders was new? Your kings have feared and coveted the gift since the breaking—they broke the pact *to* take the gift, seal-bearer, and they have been hunting its bearers ever since, hoarding them, burning them, draining them for their inquisitions. Every wielder dragged from a market square this season is a piece of the covenant they are stealing back. And you—" his golden eyes held hers, relentless, pitying, "—you make your living selling fragments of the very power they are murdering people to control. Thief, yes. But whose theft serves the thieves?"

"That's not fair."

"No," Sef agreed. "It is not. Very little of what is coming will be fair. That is why I was sent to speak gently, and I find I am doing it badly." He drew himself up, scales rippling, water sheeting off him and rising home to the lake. "Hear the terms plainly, seal-bearer, so you cannot claim you were coerced. The Court asks. It does not compel—it cannot compel; the seal was made free or it is worthless. Gather the seven sigils hidden in the years you have stolen. Sing the rite at the origin-stone before the thaw. Refuse, and we will flood the lowlands and call it justice, and you may live with the arithmetic of that. Choose."

*Choose.* As if it were a merchant's scale and not her life on both pans. As if she could simply set down one weight and pick up the other.

Ada looked past him at the lake—at the flat gray reach of it, deceptively calm, holding its drowned court and its drowned king and two hundred years of grievance beneath a skin of morning light. Somewhere south of here, Greywater Ford's criers were reading death warrants in bored voices. Somewhere north, Emeric Vale was riding into the mountains with her name in his pocket and iron in his saddlebags. Forty days. Fifty, if the snows held.

"I'm a thief," she said again, but the defiance in it had gone thin, and she heard the terror underneath her own voice, and she knew he heard it too. "Thieves don't save worlds. We rob them while someone else is saving them. That's the whole trade."

"Once," Sef said, "a thief was exactly what the covenant needed. That story is also waiting in your stolen years, seal-bearer, and it is not mine to tell." He turned, the great fluke lifting, water climbing home around him. "The second sigil sleeps west of here, beyond the snowfields. When you decide—and you will decide, because the water chose you and the water is patient—I will rise. Until then, I leave you to your courage. It is smaller than you pretend and larger than you know. Most are."

He sank without a ripple. The lake smoothed over him like a page closing.

Ada stood a long time at the cave mouth, the knife finally lowered, the sigil warm in her fist like a coal she couldn't put down. Forty days. Blue charms over cottage doors. Emeric's face in the crowd at Greywater Ford, and the mark he'd almost seen on her wrist.

Below her, far out on the gray water, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the rings of it reached the shore and touched her boots like fingers counting.

West, then. Through the snowfields.

She packed her kit with hands that almost didn't shake, and started walking.
