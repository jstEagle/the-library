## 5. Seven Sigils, One Thief

The lake kept talking long after Ada wanted it to stop.

She sat at the mouth of the smuggler cave with her boots in the shallows, and the water spoke against her ankles in a language she had no right to understand—yet understood, the way you understand a mother's voice through a wall. *Seal-bearer*, the lake said, or something like it. The word was older than the word. It tasted of salt and of a door closing.

Behind her, in the dark of the cave, something was drying itself on the stones.

"You will catch your death," Sef said, "sitting in me like that."

Ada did not turn around. "You're the lake?"

"I am *of* the lake, as a courtier is of a court." A wet, deliberate sound—scales against granite. "I am Sef, envoy of the Drowned Court, and I have been waiting thirty years for the water to find you. Do not make me wait another night on a cold shore while you sulk."

She turned. In the grey pre-dawn light he was worse and better than her first glimpse of him: a man's torso grafted to the hindquarters of a great salmon, silver-scaled, his skin the grey-green of deep water, gills fluttering at his throat like a nervous man's collar. His eyes were the color of the lake at dusk—amber shot through with black. He wore a courtier's sash, rotted to ribbons, knotted with what looked like drowned men's rings. He held a staff of white coral as if he'd forgotten he was holding it.

"You're real," she said. It was a stupid thing to say. She said it anyway.

"Alas." He lowered himself onto a stone with the care of an old man, tail folding beneath him. "You hoped the lake was madness. Madness would have been kinder, I think. Madness you could drink away in Greywater Ford and forget by spring."

Ada pulled her boots from the water and stood. Her hands were shaking, and she hated them for it. "I'm not a savior. I'm not a *seal* or a *bearer* or whatever the water called me. I'm a thief. I steal memories and I sell them and I keep moving. That's the whole of me."

"Yes," Sef said. "That is precisely why it must be you."

* * *

He told it the way a man recites a debt—without ornament, without mercy.

"There was a covenant," he said. "Before your crown, before Thornfell had a name. The human realm and the drowned kingdom beneath these waters made a pact, sealed at a stone in the high country, witnessed by salt and by blood. The drowned folk kept the lakes from rising past their ancient bounds. The humans kept the old rites, kept the memory of the pact alive in their keeping—handed down, mother to daughter, in a line of covenant-keepers." His gills fluttered. "A pact is a living thing, Ada Nystrom. It does not survive on paper. It survives in *remembering*. Forget it, and it frays."

"And someone forgot."

"Someone *stole*." His amber eyes fixed on her, and she felt the weight of them like a hand on her sternum. "Thirty years ago, the pact broke. Not all at once. It broke in seven places, the way a rope breaks strand by strand. Seven sigils—the seven knots of the covenant—tore loose and scattered. Each one fled into the nearest vessel that could hold it. Not stone. Not water." He paused. "A memory. A memory is a knot of salt and blood, seal-bearer. It is the only vessel in either realm that can hold a covenant-sigil without burning."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "Whose memory?"

"Yours."

The word dropped into her like a stone into a well, and she listened a long time for it to hit bottom.

"I was a child," she said.

"You were the daughter of a covenant-keeper." Sef's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "The sigils did not choose you at random. They fled *home*. And when the pact broke—when the keeper's line was cut and the child who should have inherited the remembering instead—" he made a small, precise gesture with one webbed hand, "—took a knife to her own mind and cut out the years that hurt her. She did not know what she was cutting. She thought she was saving herself."

The cave was very cold. Ada became aware that she had backed up until her shoulders found the stone wall.

"I don't have a childhood," she said. "I've told myself it was the gift. That taking so many memories burned mine out of me, the way a candle burns its own wick. I built my whole life on that story."

"I know," Sef said. "I watched you build it. We have watched you for thirty years, seal-bearer, from beneath the water, hoping and despairing by turns. The sigils are still in you—or in what you stole and *hid*. A memory-thief does not destroy what she takes. She caches it. And a child who steals from herself hides the stolen years so well that even she cannot find them." He tapped the coral staff against the stone, once, like a judge's gavel. "Seven sigils. Seven hidden memories from the years you cut away. Find them, and the covenant can be reforged at the origin-stone before the spring thaw."

"And if I don't find them?"

Sef was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, he did not raise his voice, and that was the most frightening thing he had done yet.

"Then the thaw comes, and the drowned kingdom, unbound, rises with it. The lakes will climb. Vess will climb. Greywater Ford will be a fishing story told by trout. The lowlands—" he spread his hands, taking in the mountains, the world, "—the lowlands will learn what the word *drowned* means in the old tongue. It does not mean death, seal-bearer. It means *change*, and the drowned do not ask permission before they change a thing."

Ada slid down the wall until she was sitting, because her legs had made the decision without consulting her.

"I'm a thief," she said again, but it came out smaller this time.

"Yes," Sef agreed. "And that is the only reason there is hope. A saint would try to fight the drowned. A king would try to buy them. A scholar would try to study them until the water came up his nose. But a thief—" his amber eyes glinted, "—a thief knows how to find what was hidden. Even from herself. Especially from herself."

* * *

She made him wait while she thought, and he let her, folding his tail and going still as a heron, which she suspected was a courtier's trick for making silence do his arguing for him.

Seven memories. Seven years of her own life, cut out and hidden, and she had walked past the hiding places her whole adult life the way you walk past a door in your own house that you have agreed, without ever saying so, never to open.

Her rules. She had rules. That was the thing she told herself made her different from the butchers who stripped people hollow for coin. *Never take a whole life. Never take love. Never take what can't be lived without.* She'd built those rules in the years after Hollowmere, after the granary, after the night she couldn't remember and had stopped trying to. The rules were the fence around the hole where her childhood should have been, and now a fish-courtier from a drowned kingdom was telling her the fence was the cage.

"If I do this," she said. "If I go digging in my own skull for seven pieces of a broken pact. What happens to me?"

Sef's gills stilled. "I don't know."

"You're an envoy. You're supposed to know things."

"I know the covenant, the rite, and the price. The price is written in the old tongue and I will not soften it: a covenant reforged demands a seal that can stand in both realms and belong to neither. What that makes of the woman who stands there—" he shook his head, slow, "—the last seal-bearer was not a woman afterward. She was a door. Doors do not remember what walked through them."

Ada laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "So the reward for saving the world is forgetting I saved it."

"The reward," Sef said, "is that there is a world."

She looked out at the lake. Dawn was coming up behind the eastern peaks, and the water had gone from black to iron to a pale, cold rose, and somewhere under all that water there was a whole kingdom holding its breath, waiting on a thief from Thornfell to decide whether they drowned or not.

"Say I agree," she said. "Say I go hunting through my own hidden years. What do you give me?"

"The finding." Sef unknotted something from his sash—a ring, black with age, set with a stone that was not a stone but a drop of water that had forgotten how to fall. "A sigil-sense. Hold it, and the covenant-sigils will pull at you like hooks in a line. Faintly, at first. Stronger as you gather them." He held it out on his open palm. "And my counsel, for what a drowned courtier's counsel is worth. Which, I am told, is less every year."

"And what do you give me," Ada said, "if I refuse?"

"Nothing," Sef said simply. "I am not permitted to compel you. The covenant was broken by compulsion—your crown's compulsion, your crown's iron and fire. It can only be reforged by a free hand. That is the first law and the last." His amber eyes held hers, and in them she saw the lake at dusk, and something vast and tired and drowning underneath the dusk. "I can only tell you what the water told me, seal-bearer, and the water does not lie: the thaw is coming either way. The only question is what it finds when it arrives."

She took the ring.

It was cold, then warm, then it hummed against her palm like a struck bell heard through a wall—a frequency she felt in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her mind where the stolen years had been cut away. And far off, deep in the buried country of her own hidden memories, something *answered*. One pull. Then another. Then seven, faint as heartbeats through stone, scattered across the dark map of everything she had made herself forget.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, they're *there*."

* * *

That was when the dogs started.

Sef's head came up first—he heard them before she did, gills flaring, the whole long length of him going taut. "Riders," he said. "In the pass. Coming up the smuggler road."

Ada was already moving, years of Thornfell roads folding into her spine. She snuffed the little fire, swept the ash with a pine bough, and was at the cave mouth with her pack before the second bay echoed off the cliffs. She counted the way Maro had taught her, back when Maro was still a friend: dogs first, then hoofbeats, then the high thin note of a hunting horn, and under it all the creak of leather and iron that meant *soldiers*, not smugglers. Smugglers didn't ride in formation.

"How many?" Sef whispered.

"Eight. Maybe ten." She pressed herself flat against the rock and looked down the switchback trail. Grey mist still clung to the pines, but through it she could see them—dark riders climbing the road below, and a banner she knew too well. The crowned eye of the royal inquisition. "Crown men. Not tax collectors. Inquisitors."

"They are hunting memory-wielders," Sef said. "The proclamation. We heard it read at Greywater Ford."

"I heard it read. I was *in the crowd*." Her mouth was dry again. "They burned a hedge-witch's cart with her still in it. They're not taking prisoners for trial anymore."

"Then we go up," Sef said. "The high snowfields. Iron and dogs do poorly above the snowline, and blessed iron—" his hand went unconsciously to his own side, where, she noticed for the first time, a bandage of woven reeds was wrapped, "—does worse."

She looked at him. "You're hurt."

"I was hurt before I met you. The lake's edge is not kind to envoys either, these days." He said it lightly, and it was the first time she'd heard him fail at lightness. "Seal-bearer. The bargain."

"The bargain," she said. "Right."

She made herself say it plainly, because if she didn't say it plainly she never would. "I'll find your seven sigils. I'll carry them to the origin-stone. I'll do whatever the rite needs doing. And in exchange, the drowned kingdom owes me one truth at the end of it—whatever the rite costs me, whatever I become, I get to *know*, before it happens. No door gets made of me in the dark. You understand? I've spent my whole life not knowing what I cut out of myself. I'm not walking into a second hole blindfolded."

Sef regarded her for a long moment. Water dripped from the cave ceiling between them, counting seconds.

"That is not a small thing to ask," he said. "The rite's price may be a truth that breaks you before you can pay it."

"Then it breaks me in daylight," Ada said. "Take it or leave it."

The envoy of the Drowned Court bowed his head—the first time she had seen him do it—and when he raised it, there was something in his amber eyes that had not been there before. Not hope. Something older and more tired that hope grows out of, the way a tree grows out of rot.

"Agreed," he said. "One truth, freely given, before the seal is set. Witnessed by salt." He touched the coral staff to the wet stone, and somewhere beneath them, deep under the lake, Ada felt the bargain *take*—a settling, a click, like a lock accepting a key it had been waiting thirty years for.

"Then we climb," she said.

They went up. Behind and below, the dogs found the cold ashes of her fire and began to sing, and a man's voice cut through the mist, giving orders in a voice that Ada did not recognize—and yet some animal part of her, some scrap of her that had survived the cutting, flinched at the sound of it as if it had heard that voice say her name before.

She did not look back. She took the ring from her pocket and closed her fist around it, and felt the seven pulls, faint and far, like stars through cloud.

*One of them,* she thought, climbing into the snowline with a drowned courtier's wet footprints steaming behind her, *is the night I can't remember. One of them is why I have no childhood.*

*And one of them is going to tell me who that voice belongs to.*

Above her, the snowfields waited, white and silent and full of buried things.
