## 6. The First Memory: Ash on Her Hands

The cache was buried where Ada had buried everything she could not bear to keep and could not bear to lose: beneath the split granite at the head of the Vess falls, where the water's roar drowned out thought and the spray froze silver on the stone in winter. She had been seventeen when she sealed it. She remembered the burying—of course she did; that was the joke of her gift, that she could take a memory out of her own skull and still recall the taking, the way an amputee recalls the limb. She remembered kneeling in the dark with a trowel, wrapping the lead-lined box in oilcloth, whispering the binding words her mother had taught her before the crown taught her mother's neck the rope. She remembered everything about the night except what the box contained.

That was the arrangement. That was the price.

Sef waited at the tree line, half in the shadow of the pines, and the sight of him was still a wound to her sense of the world—a courtier's torso rising from a salmon's tail, scales catching the moonlight like hammered tin, gills fluttering at his throat with each slow breath. He had followed her up from the lake without complaint, dragging himself over stone and root with a patience she found more unsettling than menace. Menace she could fight. Patience, in a thing that had waited a hundred years for her, was its own kind of pressure.

"You buried it deep," Sef said. His voice was the sound water makes in a closed room. "Deeper than the others."

"The others I could look at." Ada knelt at the split in the granite and worked her fingers into the seam. The stone was cold enough to burn. "This one I buried like a corpse. Face down. No marker."

"Because you knew what it was."

"I knew what it *weighed*." The seam gave with a crack; a slab the size of a coffin lid tilted aside, and there it was, just as she'd left it—oilcloth gone stiff and gray, the lead box beneath furred with frost, and around it, in the frozen earth, a ring of soil that had never grown so much as a weed. Five years of nothing daring to root there. She hadn't noticed that when she buried it. She noticed it now.

Sef made a sound low in his chest, not quite a word. Reverence, maybe. Or hunger.

"Don't," Ada said, without looking up. "Whatever you're about to say—about destiny, about the covenant, about the drowned king's grief—don't. I'm doing this because the thaw will drown the lowlands and I have a rule about being responsible for dead strangers. One rule. It's a short list. Don't make it shorter."

"I was going to say," Sef replied, "that you should not open it alone."

Ada lifted the box. It was heavier than lead had any right to be, heavier than it had been at seventeen, as if the thing inside had been growing all this time in the dark, feeding on the soil's refusal. She set it on the flat stone and broke the wax seal her own thumbprint had pressed into it five years ago. The hinges made a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, on a bed of black salt, lay a sigil.

Not the covenant sigil—not yet. This was her own work, cruder, the way her stolen memories always took form: a small tablet of gray glass, clouded at the center, and through the cloud a glow like coals under ash. Orange. Deep. Breathing.

It was the largest thing she had ever taken from anyone, and she had taken it from herself.

"You were a child," Sef said quietly. "Children should not be able to do this."

"Children can do anything if it's the only way to stay alive." She lifted the tablet. It was warm. That was wrong—everything in the box should have been winter through—but the glass sat in her palms like a living thing, like a bird with a broken wing, and the glow at its center pulsed in time with her own heart, and she understood suddenly, with a lurch, that it had been waiting for her hand. That it had always been waiting.

"The lake showed you the first covenant sigil," Sef said. "It lives inside this. Inside the memory. To take the sigil, you must take back what you stole. All of it. Every year you buried."

"And if I don't want it back?"

Sef's gills fluttered. "Then the sigil stays asleep, the covenant stays broken, and in the spring the lakes rise and the lowlands learn to swim." A pause. "You said you had a rule."

Ada looked at the tablet. The cloud at its center swirled, and for a moment—she would swear this later, to herself, in the dark—it looked like smoke.

* * *

She did it the way she did all her work, because craft was the only religion she had left. She sat cross-legged on the flat stone with the tablet in her hands and the falls roaring behind her, and she breathed until her pulse slowed, and she pressed her thumbs to the glass the way she pressed them to a client's temple, and she said the unbinding words.

*What was taken, return. What was buried, wake. What I could not carry, let me carry now.*

The world went to ash.

Not gradually. There was no fade, no gentle surfacing into the past. One breath she was on the cold stone above Lake Vess, and the next she was *there*, and her body remembered before her mind caught up—she was small, she was seven, her hands were wrong, her hands were *covered*—and she screamed, or the child screamed, and the two screams were the same scream, and the memory took her like the lake takes a stone.

Hollowmere. The granary.

She was crouched in the shadow of the tithe-barn, and the night was red at the edges, and the air tasted of pitch and burning wheat and something else beneath it, something older and fouler, like a marsh drained after a century. She knew the taste. She had spent five years not knowing she knew it. *Salt and rot. The smell of the covenant tearing.*

Across the yard, the granary burned.

It had not caught. It had been *set*—she could see the men now, five of them, six, moving with lanterns and long-handled flails, and they wore no livery she recognized but they moved like soldiers, and they were not trying to put the fire out. They were feeding it. They had broken open the granary's undercroft and they were pouring barrels down into the dark, and the barrels did not hold grain. She was close enough to see. She was close enough that she would spend the rest of her life not-remembering this: the barrels held salt, black salt, packed around bundles of reeds and river-clay tablets marked with sigils she was too young to read but her blood was not too young to *know*—and the fire took them and the sigils *screamed*. She heard them. Everyone in Hollowmere heard them, though only the children and the dogs would admit it afterward: a sound like a choir drowning, rising out of the burning undercroft, and the lake a mile away answered it with a sound of its own.

The water in the millrace ran backward.

Ada—the seven-year-old, the child in the memory, the girl whose hands were covered in ash because she had climbed the barn roof to watch the stars and the first tremor had shaken her down into the soot—Ada watched the covenant break. She did not have a word for it. She had a *feeling* for it, the way you feel a bone snap in another room: the whole night lurched, something vast and patient and old gave way, and every tablet in that undercroft—seven tablets, she could see them now, seven glows in the firelight, the crown's men prying them out of the granary's foundations where they had slept beneath the town for three hundred years—every tablet went dark at once, like eyes closing, like a door slamming, like a promise spat upon.

And then the men began to die.

Not all of them. Three. The fire reached the last of the tablets and three of the crown's men simply *stopped*, mid-stride, and their bodies stood there burning without falling, and out of their open mouths came water. Clean water, lake water, pouring out of them as if they had been vessels all along, as if the drowned kingdom had reached up through the broken pact and reclaimed what it could. The other men ran. The fire spread. And in the shadow of the tithe-barn, a seven-year-old girl with ash on her hands did the only thing her gift knew how to do, the thing her mother had drilled into her since she could talk: *when it is too much, take it out. When it would break you, make it a sigil. When you cannot carry it, bury it.*

She reached into her own small chest with hands that had never stolen anything but a cook's headache and a neighbor's grief, and she took.

She took the burning granary. She took the backward water. She took the singing tablets and the standing dead and the taste of salt and rot. She took the whole night, all of it, every detail her mind had recorded in that merciless childish clarity—and because she was seven and imprecise, she took too much. She took the weeks around it. She took the whispered arguments overheard through her mother's door: *they've found where the crown hides them, they've found the granary at Hollowmere, we have to move them, we have to move them tonight.* She took her mother saddling the horse at midnight. She took her mother's face in the doorway, ash-smeared, terrible, beautiful, saying: *stay here. Whatever you hear, whatever you see—stay in the barn, Ada, and no matter what comes, no matter who knocks—do not open the door.*

And she took what came after. The knocking. The voices. The men who were not crown soldiers, who wore no livery, who spoke softly to the child through the barn door and called her by a name she had not heard in five years and could not now hold on to, because the memory was already dissolving as the sigil sealed it away—her mother's name, gone, gone, the price of the taking, the tax her own gift levied on her own heart—and she took the long cold hours in the hay until dawn, and she took the morning when her mother did not come back, and she took the decision, made by a child alone in a burning town, to bury the whole of it beneath the granite at the head of the falls and walk down the mountain and become, over five years, the woman she was.

The memory released her.

* * *

She came back to herself on the flat stone above Lake Vess, on her hands and knees, and she was not screaming, which surprised her; she was making a sound much worse than screaming, a low continuous note like a struck bell that would not stop ringing, and her hands were pressed flat against the stone and the frost beneath her palms had melted in a perfect double print.

Sef was beside her. She did not know how long he had been there. His webbed hand hovered over her shoulder, not touching. Waiting for permission, or fearing it.

"They set the fire," she said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "The crown's men. They found the granary because the tablets were hidden under it, under the town, three hundred years, and they dug them out and burned the bindings and the covenant broke *there*, that night, that was the night—" She stopped. The bell-note in her throat threatened to become something else. "I watched it. I was seven. I watched the crown break the covenant and I stole it from myself so I could survive, and I have spent five years selling other people's grief for bread while the reason the world is breaking sat in a lead box under a rock I could have dug up any day."

"You were a child."

"I was a *witness*." She pushed herself upright. The world tilted and steadied. "And there's worse. Sef, there's worse, and you're going to stand there and tell me it changes nothing, but I have to say it out loud or it will eat through me like the fire ate the undercroft." She turned and faced him, and the falls roared, and the moon sat low and swollen over Lake Vess like an eye refusing to close. "The men who came to the barn. After. The ones who knocked. They weren't crown. They called me by name. They knew my mother. And I took that memory too—I took it the same night, it's in the same sigil, I can *feel* it still in there, a corner of it, a voice through a door—and Sef, I know that voice. Five years later, I know it."

The glow at the center of the tablet had changed. The ash-cloud had burned away entirely now, and what remained was not orange but a deep, clean, silver-blue, the color of the lake at dawn, and through the glass she could see it: a shape, a sigil, geometric and ancient, the first of seven, turning slowly like a key finding its lock.

"Take it," Sef said.

"It's mine. I stole it fair."

"It was never yours. Nothing you ever stole was yours. That was the covenant's mercy in you, girl—the thief's gift turned seal-bearer's duty. Take it. The night is short and the thaw does not wait."

Ada closed her hands around the tablet. It was warm as a living thing, and it wanted her, and she hated it, and she took it. The glass dissolved against her palms like snow in river water, and the sigil rose out of it—a line of silver-blue light, a glyph older than the crown, older than the lakes—and it sank into her chest, and she felt it *set*, felt it seat itself somewhere behind her sternum with an almost audible click, a lock engaging, a first tooth of seven catching.

The silence afterward was enormous. Even the falls seemed to hush.

"Six," Sef said softly. "Six remain. And the next—" His gills stilled, and for the first time since he had risen from the lake, the courtier looked uncertain. "The next is not buried in stone. The keeper at the tollhouse of Whispers holds its location, and the keeper does not take coin."

"I know what she takes." Ada stood. Her legs held. Her hands had stopped shaking, mostly. She looked down at them—at the ash that was not there, at the seven-year-old's hands that would be hers forever now, ghosted over her own. "Secrets. She takes secrets. And I have exactly one worth the price."

She turned toward the mountain pass, toward the tollhouse, and the moonlight caught her face, and for one moment Sef saw what the drowned court had waited a century to see: not a thief, not a seal-bearer, but something between—a woman with a lock engaging in her chest and a name she could not remember and a voice through a barn door that she now, finally, recognized.

Because she did recognize it. That was the horror under all the other horrors, the one she had not said aloud, the one she would carry up the pass like a stone in her mouth:

The man who had knocked at her mother's door, the night the covenant broke, had called her by her true name in a voice she knew.

And the voice belonged to Captain Emeric Vale.
