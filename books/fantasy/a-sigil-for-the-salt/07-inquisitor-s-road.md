## 7. Inquisitor's Road

The smuggler's name was Doss Fenner, and he had been crying for the better part of an hour.

Emeric Vale watched him from across the cell's single tallow candle, letting the silence do what his questions could not. The holding room beneath Greywater Ford's garrison house was a converted root cellar—stone walls sweated with damp, a drain in the floor that smelled of turnips and old blood—and Fenner sat chained to a chair with the particular looseness of a man who had already decided he was going to die here and was only negotiating the terms.

"You're not listening to me," Fenner said. His voice had gone hoarse from weeping. "I told you everything I know about the roads. The passes, the tolls, who pays who. That's worth something. That's worth *something*, Captain."

"It is worth a great deal." Emeric turned the chair around and sat astride it, arms folded over its back. "Which is why I'm confused that you keep leaving out the one thing I actually asked you about."

"I don't know anything about any sigil-thief."

"You knew her mark on sight."

Fenner's jaw worked. Candlelight caught the grease in his beard, the salt crust at his collar. A man of the lowland rivers, Emeric judged, pushed up into the mountains by debt or appetite, the way most of them were. He'd interrogated forty such men since the Proclamation went out, and they all broke along the same seams: fear first, then greed, then loneliness.

"I saw a chalk sign on a waystation door," Fenner said. "That's all."

"You described it."

"Because you held my hand over a brazier!"

"I did." Emeric let that stand. He did not enjoy the work—he had never enjoyed the work—but he had learned long ago that pretending otherwise made it slower, and slowness got people killed. Sometimes the wrong people. Usually the right ones. "A circle broken by seven notches. Drawn in ash mixed with something that shines. You looked at it and went pale as a christened corpse, and you told Corporal Hesk it was the Mark of the Borrowed Years and that no honest man should follow where it led. Those were your words. Then you tried to buy passage south with money you didn't have. So either the mark frightened you, or the woman who left it did."

Fenner said nothing. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the garrison floor; the candle guttered in the draft of the opening door and steadied again.

"Here is what I know," Emeric continued, conversationally, because he had found that smugglers confessed fastest when the questioning sounded like gossip. "In three weeks, this thief has lifted memories at four waystations between Thornfell and the high passes. In each case the seller walked away dazed but whole, paid fairly, and remembered nothing of the transaction's details—which tells me she scrubs herself from their minds afterward. Clean work. Disciplined. She takes grief mostly. Dead children. Dead wives. One man sold the memory of his own funeral, which I confess I didn't know a person could carry."

"That'll be the Vess roads," Fenner muttered. "Grief's currency up there. Half the mountain folk are rich in it."

"And twice," Emeric said, "she refused payment entirely. A widow at Coldbrass Ford. A ferryman whose son drowned in the spring floods. She took the memories off them like pulling splinters, gave them back their coin, and left before dawn." He leaned forward. The chains clinked as Fenner flinched. "Smugglers don't do charity, Doss. Thieves don't do mercy. So either she's a new kind of criminal the crown hasn't named yet, or she's something older wearing a criminal's coat. Which brings me back to the mark."

"I told you—"

"You told me it was the Mark of the Borrowed Years. That phrase didn't come out of nowhere. Somebody taught it to you, or somebody warned you, and both of those somebodies know more than you do." Emeric rose, crossed to the cell door, and knocked once. It opened onto Corporal Hesk's flat, patient face. "Bring the ledger box."

* * *

The ledger box was iron-banded oak, crown-stamped, and it contained everything the hunt had gathered so far: eleven depositions, two confiscated memory-sigils sealed in glass, a charcoal rubbing of the waystation mark, and a map of the high country with red thread pinned from Greywater Ford to the snowline. Emeric spread it all across the cell floor because he wanted Fenner to see it—to understand that the machinery of the crown was not a rumor but a weight, and that it rolled downhill toward whoever stood in its path.

He also wanted to watch Fenner's face while he did it. Faces were testimony too. Five years of inquisitor's service had taught him that the mouth lied first, the hands second, and the eyes never—not entirely, not if you asked the right questions fast enough.

The sigils in their glass jars caught the candlelight. They looked like chips of stained glass drowned in honey, faintly luminous, faintly warm. When Emeric had first held one, at the inquisitors' academy in Carreth, his instructor had made him grip it barehanded until the stolen memory inside began to bleed into him—a stranger's wedding, sunlit, unbearably happy, and then the same wedding seen again through grief years later. He had wept without knowing whose tears they were. That was the lesson, the old man had said. Memory-wielders traffic in the most dangerous substance in creation: other people's hearts. The crown does not hunt them out of cruelty, boy. It hunts them because a person who can take what you love and sell it back to you is more dangerous than any army.

Emeric had believed that, then. He had built five years of his life on believing it.

"Look here." He crouched beside the rubbing of the mark and tapped the seven notches. "Every account agrees on this. Seven breaks in the circle. Now look at the Proclamation." He unrolled the broadsheet—the crown's seal in black wax, the words MEMORY-WIELDERS TO BE SEIZED FOR QUESTIONING printed in stern capitals—and laid it over the rubbing. "Notice what the Proclamation doesn't say. No mention of the number seven. No mention of circles. No description of any mark. The crown ordered a general hunt, Captain Vale, and yet every report that reaches my desk concerns this one thief with this one sign." He glanced up. "You're wondering why I'm telling you all this."

"Aye," Fenner admitted. "I'm wondering when the brazier comes back."

"The brazier is retired. You talked." Emeric gathered the papers slowly, squaring their edges. "Here's what I think, Doss. I think the mark isn't hers. I think she inherited it, the way a daughter inherits her mother's ring. I think there were others once who carried it, and they're dead or scattered, and the crown knows more about them than the Proclamation lets on. And I think our thief is following a road she doesn't fully understand, toward something the crown wants very badly to control." He paused at the door. "Am I warm?"

Fenner stared at him. Then, quietly, with the exhausted honesty of a man past fear: "You're warmer than anyone's been since they started hanging hedge-witches in the market square. There's talk on the roads. Old talk, come back up out of the ground. About the lakes. About the Salt Covenant." He licked his cracked lips. "About a keeper's line that was supposed to be ended twenty-odd years ago."

The cellar seemed to grow colder. Or perhaps that was only Emeric's blood.

"What keeper's line?"

"Wouldn't know, Captain. Wouldn't want to." Fenner wet his lips again. "But if I were you, and I were chasing her—and you are, and you are—I wouldn't go up the east passes after the thaw starts. The water remembers, they say. And it talks to certain folk." He shrugged, chains rattling. "That's all I've got. Hang me or feed me, but stop asking, because I've given you everything and my head's starting to feel like a dropped basket."

Emeric studied him a long moment. Then he nodded to Hesk. "Bread and small beer. Move him to the dry cell." As the corporal unlocked the chains, Emeric added, without turning: "Doss. If she'd wanted to, she could have taken your memories of this conversation and left you drooling in a ditch. Why do you suppose she didn't?"

Fenner was quiet for so long that Emeric thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because she only steals the hurt things, Captain. That's what the roads say. She leaves the rest alone." A ragged laugh. "Makes it worse somehow, don't it."

It did. Emeric climbed the stairs into the grey morning light and found that his hands were not quite steady.

* * *

The garrison house sat on the ford's north bank, and from its upper window Emeric could see the whole sprawl of Greywater Ford waking: fish carts and wool carts, the bridge thick with early traffic, the river running brown and swollen with mountain melt. Two weeks ago they had dragged an old woman through that square—Mother Casselin, the hedge-witch, guilty of nothing worse than selling charms against toothache—and the crowd had cheered, and Emeric had stood at attention in his captain's blue and felt something crack inside him that had not mended since.

He had written a report recommending clemency. Lord Chancellor Ives had returned it unsigned, with a note in his thin, precise hand: *Mercy is a luxury of the secure. Be useful.*

Be useful. That was the whole of Emeric's life now, distilled to two words. He had been useful to the crown since he was nineteen, when his father's debts and his mother's pride had funneled him into the inquisitors' corps the way a millrace funnels water. Useful through the border campaigns. Useful through the purges. Useful, always useful, and never once asked whether usefulness was the same thing as goodness.

And now the crown had pointed him up a mountain after a memory-thief, and he was beginning to suspect—with a dread that sat in his chest like swallowed river stone—that he already knew exactly who she was.

He took out the evidence again, alone this time, and laid it on the window ledge where the daylight could reach it.

The mark. Seven notches in a circle. He had seen it before—of course he had seen it before, though he had spent two weeks telling himself he hadn't, telling himself that marks blurred together in this trade, that a chalk circle in ash meant nothing. But he had seen it scratched into the lintel of a croft door in Hollowmere, years ago, half worn away. Seen it inked small and neat at the base of a woman's thumb, hidden under a leather glove, visible only when she reached for her cup at supper and forgot herself.

Ada.

He said the name silently, because saying it aloud would make it true, and if it became true then everything else became true with it: that the thief working the Thornfell roads was the woman who had shared his blanket roll through one winter and half a spring; who had laughed at his terrible singing; who had traced the bones of his wrist one night and said, half asleep, *you'd hate what I am if you knew*. Who had vanished from their camp above Lake Vess five years ago leaving nothing behind but a cold fire, her bedroll folded with military precision, and a note of six words that he still could not read without his throat tightening:

*Don't look for me. Forget me kindly.*

He had spent a year looking anyway. Then he had spent four years learning how to stop—burying it under rank and duty and the particular numbness of men who are good at their work—and now the crown had handed him a commission that was, in truth, a wound reopened with a surgeon's delicacy. Find the sigil-thief. Capture alive. Those had been Ives's orders, delivered in person, with eyes that lingered a moment too long, as though the Chancellor already knew something about the thief that Emeric did not.

Capture alive. Not seize for questioning. Alive.

He pressed his palm against the window glass until the cold ached. Below, the market was assembling itself for the day, and a crier was mounting the courthouse steps to read the Proclamation again, his voice bouncing off the stone fronts of shops that had stood since before the flood-year. Emeric watched a young soldier in the crowd—one of Hesk's riders, barely shaving—laugh at something a baker's girl said, easy and open, a boy who still thought serving the crown and being decent were the same occupation.

*I'd hate what I am if you knew.* He had assumed she meant the thieving. The gift. The taking. He had told her, that night by the fire, that he didn't care what she was, and he had even meant it, and she had smiled the saddest smile he had ever seen on a living face and kissed him like someone memorizing.

Now he wondered what else she had known. What she had seen coming—the Proclamation, the hunt, the hedge-witches in cart-ropes—and how long ago she had seen it. Five years. She had run five years ahead of this day, and he had spent those five years becoming exactly the instrument designed to catch her.

The bitter joke of it almost made him laugh. Almost.

"Hesk," he called, without turning from the window. The corporal appeared in the doorway, buckling his sword belt. "Ready the company. We ride within the hour."

"East passes, sir? Fenner's talk about the thaw—"

"No." Emeric turned. On the map behind him, the red thread ran from Greywater Ford up the river valleys toward Lake Vess, and beyond the lake, if you followed the old drovers' paths, lay a stretch of high country where the smuggler roads narrowed to a single thread through the pines. A thread with, if his information held, a tollhouse on it. Thieves needed fences; thieves needed passage; and everything that moved through the mountains moved through the Tollhouse of Whispers eventually. "West. The lake roads. She'll want to move fast before the thaw closes the low trails, and she'll need a broker for whatever she's carrying. We can be at the tollhouse in four days if we push."

Hesk hesitated. "Sir—if you don't mind my saying—you've been different these two weeks. Since the mark came in. Slower to decide, quicker to—" He stopped himself with the instinct of a good soldier.

"Quicker to what, Corporal?"

"Nothing, sir."

"Say it."

Hesk's jaw tightened. "Quicker to let her go, sir. Coldbrass Ford. The widow's cottage. We had the lane covered and you called us off because you said the sighting was stale." He met Emeric's eyes with the terrible directness of an honest subordinate. "It wasn't stale. The mud on the fence post was still wet."

The room was very quiet. Down in the square, the crier had finished, and the crowd was dispersing into its ordinary noise.

"Corporal," Emeric said at last, evenly, "if you ever have cause to believe I am compromising this hunt, you will say so to my face, as you just have, and I will thank you for it. And then you will remember that I have served this crown for nine years without a blemish, and that I will bring in this thief if it costs me everything I have." He picked up his gauntlets from the sill. "Saddle the horses."

He did not add: *and pray to whichever god listens that I find her before the Chancellor's own men do, because Ives wants her alive, and I have begun to suspect that alive, in Ives's hands, is a fate worse than anything I would ever allow.*

He did not add it because saying it aloud would be treason, and because he was not yet sure it was false.

* * *

They rode out as the church bells rang terce, twelve riders in crown blue threading the bridge traffic, and the last thing Emeric saw of Greywater Ford was the empty scaffold in the square, built for Mother Casselin and never used, waiting with the patience of carpentry for the next condemned.

By noon they had left the river behind and entered the pine shadow of the western slopes, where the snow still lay deep and the smuggler roads ran white and silent. At the first waystation, Emeric swung down and knelt at the doorpost. Chalk ghosts of older signs layered the wood like geological strata—but near the bottom, fresh, deliberate, drawn in ash that glittered faintly when the light struck it, was the circle with its seven notches.

Beside it, smaller, almost shy: a second mark he had never seen in any report. A little bird, wings folded. A wren in winter plumage.

His breath went out of him all at once. Because he knew that bird. He had drawn it himself, badly, in the frost of a tent flap five winters ago, making her laugh—*that's supposed to be a wren, Vale? Looks like a drowned rat*—and she had taken the stick from his hand and redrawn it properly, quick and sure, saying: *there. Like that. Remember it. It means someone's thinking of you.*

She had left it for him. Knowing he would come. Knowing what he was now.

Emeric straightened slowly, and the cold of the mountains settled into him like a decision. Behind him, Hesk waited with the horses, breath steaming. Ahead, the road climbed through the pines toward the tollhouse and the lake and whatever waited there, and Emeric Vale put his heel to his mount's flank and rode toward the woman he had been ordered to capture, carrying with him the only weapon she might not expect:

the truth that he had never learned to forget her.
