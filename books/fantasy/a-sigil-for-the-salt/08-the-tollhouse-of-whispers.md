## 8. The Tollhouse of Whispers

The tollhouse stood where the smuggler road narrowed to a single file between two shoulders of black rock, and it looked, at first glance, like something the mountain had grown rather than built. Its walls were dry-stacked fieldstone furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Its roof sagged under decades of snow-load. The only sign that anyone lived there was smoke—a thin gray ribbon rising from a chimney crooked as a question mark—and the smell of woodsmoke mixed with something stranger underneath: dried herbs, tallow, and beneath all of it a faint mineral tang, like coins held too long in a sweating palm.

Ada stopped twenty paces out and made herself breathe.

She knew this place. Every thief on the Thornfell roads knew this place. The Tollhouse of Whispers sat on the only passable route between the lake country and the high snowfields, and its keeper took no coin. Coin was metal and metal was mute. The keeper traded in the only currency the mountain respected: what you knew, and what you could bear to lose.

"You've gone pale," Sef said beside her. The envoy had walked out of Lake Vess two days ago wearing a shape that mostly passed for human—long-limbed, gray-eyed, with gill-scars along his throat like the marks of an old surgery—but he still moved wrong, still moved *quiet*, in a way that made horses stamp and dogs whine. "Is the keeper dangerous?"

"The keeper is fair," Ada said. "That's worse."

* * *

The door was unlocked, because locks meant nothing here. Ada pushed it open into a single low room lit by a hearth and by dozens of hanging lanterns of colored glass, each one glowing with a soft interior light that had nothing to do with flame.

Memories. Lanterns of memories, hundreds of them, hung from the rafters on cords of braided hair and hemp, each one pulsing gently with the color of what it held. Amber for childhood summers. Deep red for griefs. One near the door burned a sickly green that Ada did not let her eyes rest on.

At the room's center, in a chair carved from a single stump, sat the keeper: a woman so old that age had worn her down to essentials, like wind wears stone. Her eyes were closed and sunken, the lids smooth as river pebbles. She had been blind longer than Ada had been alive; the smugglers said she'd traded her sight away herself, long ago, for the first secret she'd ever sold.

"Two heartbeats," the keeper said. Her voice was dry and unhurried, like paper turning. "One of them beats in water." A pause. "Come in or go away. The draft is making my tea cold."

Ada stepped inside. Sef had to duck under the lowest lanterns, and when one swung near him its glow guttered, as if the memory inside flinched from the smell of deep water.

The keeper's blind face turned toward him with terrible precision. "You shouldn't be above the waterline, salmon-man."

"I carry a message," Sef said. "And a wound that won't close on blessed iron."

"So I taste. Iron and salt. You're bleeding through your own skin to stay shaped." The keeper clicked her tongue. "The drowned court always did spend its people carelessly." Then her head turned to Ada, and though there were no eyes behind those lids, Ada felt seen—seen more thoroughly than any inquisitor's lens had ever managed. "And you. The seal-bearer walks into my tollhouse after five years away. Sit down, girl. You look like a woman carrying a debt."

"I'm carrying a question."

"Everyone who comes here is." The keeper lifted a clay pot from the hearthstone and poured three cups without looking, the steam rising straight in the still air. "But questions are free. It's answers that cost. So ask, and then we'll discuss the price."

Ada sat on the bench across from her. The lantern-light moved over them both, red and gold and that one uneasy green.

"There's a sigil," Ada said. "A covenant sigil. Salt-white, warm, it sings if you hold it near water. I need to know where the second one is hidden."

The keeper drank her tea. Around them the lanterns pulsed, and in their light Ada saw the room properly for the first time—not just a hoard but an archive, each memory hung with a small tag of bone or birchbark marked in a script she couldn't read. A library of everything people had chosen to give up.

"I know where it is," the keeper said at last. "I've known for thirty years. A frozen shrine above the Vess headwaters, under the glacier's thumb, where a covenant-warden hid it because she couldn't carry what it showed her." She set down her cup. "That answer is worth more than anything you're wearing, everything in your pack, and every lie you've ever told well enough to survive. That answer is worth a secret."

"A secret I know?"

"A secret you *carry*." The keeper's mouth curved, not unkindly. "I don't want information, child. Information rots. I want weight. Give me a memory—one of yours, whole, freely given—and I'll hang it among my lanterns, and I'll tell you where the ice keeps its tooth."

Ada's hands went cold. She had paid this way before—small things, other people's things, the merchant's dead daughter lifted out of his skull at a waystation table while he slept off bad wine. Taking was easy. Taking was what she was *for*. But giving—

"What memory?" she asked, and hated how thin her voice came out.

The keeper tilted her head, listening to something beyond the room. "Ah," she said softly. "There it is. There's the one you guard like a coal in cupped hands. Singing. A woman's voice. Lullaby?" 

"No." Ada was on her feet before she knew she'd moved. The lanterns swayed around her. "No. Anything else."

"Anything else is worth less." The keeper did not rise. She didn't need to; the whole room rose with her, the memories humming faintly, hundreds of small lights leaning toward the argument like a crowd at a trial. "You misunderstand the trade, seal-bearer, so listen once. The road past my door leads onto the snowfields, and the snowfields lead to the shrine, and the shrine holds the second sigil, and without the second sigil the spring thaw drowns Greywater Ford and every town downstream of it. Thousands of heartbeats. I can hear them from here, all that anxious drumming." She tapped her own chest once. "Against that, I ask for one song. One memory. Yours, not stolen—given. Do you understand the difference? A taken memory leaves a scar. A given one leaves a *choice*, and choices are the only thing the mountain respects."

"She's right," Sef said quietly. "It's how the old bargains worked. The covenant itself was sealed with a surrender. Everything since has been cheaper imitations."

"Don't quote pacts at me," Ada snapped. He subsided, gill-scars flushing darker.

She turned back to the keeper. "Why that one? Why does everyone want the same thing? The crown wants my gift. The drowned king wants my obedience. Now you want—" Her voice cracked and she hated that too. "It's a lullaby. My mother sang it. It's the only thing I have left of her that isn't blood or grief."

"That is precisely why it will pay the toll." For the first time the old woman's voice gentled. "Child, do you know what I keep here?" She gestured upward, and the lanterns answered, brightening in a slow wave that rolled across the ceiling like dawn crossing a valley. "Every one of these was precious. That amber one—there, third cord from the wall—that's a man's last morning with his brother before the fever took him. He gave it to me to buy passage for his children through a winter that would have killed them. He wept while he paid. He also slept that night for the first time in a year. Precious things are heavy things, Ada Nystrom. You've spent your life lifting weight out of other people's heads. Maybe it's time you set some down."

"And forget her voice?" Ada's throat ached. "Forget the one thing—the one *clean* thing—I ever had? Everything else in my head is stolen goods. That song is mine. It's the only thing that's actually mine."

"Is it?" The keeper asked it mildly, terribly. "Or is it another thing you stole from yourself, the night you learned what your gift costs, because a girl who remembers being loved cannot survive becoming a thief?"

The silence rang like struck glass.

Because that was the truth under it, wasn't it. Ada had known it for years in the wordless way you know a splinter is lodged deep—she had no childhood. Not lost, not blurred: *absent*, excised, a clean surgical gap from age six to age thirteen, and she'd told herself for five years that whatever she'd cut out had been too terrible to keep. But the lullaby remained. One thread left dangling from the ruined cloth, and she had guarded it like a relic, sung it to herself on cold nights in a whisper, *hush now, salt-child, the tide knows your name*—

Her mother's voice. Her mother, who had been executed by the crown for keeping a covenant Ada hadn't even known existed until a fish-faced courtier crawled out of a lake to ruin her life.

"If I give it up," Ada said slowly, "I stop knowing why any of this matters to me. I'll be doing this for strangers. For Ford and the lowlands and a drowned court I've never seen."

"Yes," said the keeper simply. "That's called duty. Most people acquire it exactly that way—by losing the reasons they started and continuing anyway." She poured herself more tea, steam curling past her blind eyes. "But I'm not a cruel woman, whatever they say at the waystations. So here is my mercy: the trade is honest both directions. While you hold the sigil's location, I'll hold the song safe. And should you come back down this road after the thaw—with the covenant reforged or honestly failed—you may buy it back. Same price in reverse. A secret for a song."

"And if I die on the snowfield?"

"Then I'll hang it high, where the light reaches, and it will be the brightest thing in this house." The old woman smiled with real warmth. "Not the worst fate for a lullaby. Better than dying in a skull no one mourns in."

Ada looked at Sef. He watched her with those gray drowned-court eyes, and for once he offered nothing—no argument, no envoy's counsel. Just witness.

She thought about Greywater Ford. Criers in the square, the hedge-witch dragged by her armpits through the mud, the crowd's faces doing that terrible thing crowds' faces do—relief that it isn't them. Thousands of heartbeats downstream of a melting glacier. She thought about the first sigil, ash-warm against her palm even now, tucked against her ribs in oilcloth. Six more after this. If a lullaby bought the second, what would the seventh cost?

*Everything,* said a voice in her that sounded unpleasantly like the lake.

"Show me how it's done," Ada said.

* * *

It was done standing, facing the keeper across the little table, hands flat on the wood.

"It hurts less if you don't fight it," the keeper said. "Reach for the song. Hold it whole—all of it, every verse, don't try to keep a copy for yourself. The mountain knows cheating, and the mountain despises it. When you have it, push."

Ada closed her eyes.

The lullaby rose easily, treacherously easy, the way a blade comes out of a wound. *Hush now, salt-child, the tide knows your name.* Her mother's voice, low and smoky, a kitchen in Hollowmere, flour on wooden boards, lamplight, rain on the shutters. *The tide knows your name and it sings it again.* Ada's mother had died on a scaffold when Ada was six, and everything after was a gap, and everything before was this: four verses and a hummed refrain, forty seconds of a dead woman's voice, guarded for fifteen years like the last ember of a burned house.

*Don't fight it.*

She pushed.

Giving was nothing like taking. Taking was a scalpel's work, quick and clean, the sigil flaring silver between her fingers and the donor none the wiser. Giving was slow. Giving was watching the lantern-cord reach for her, feeling the memory pull loose root by root—kitchen, lamplight, rain, flour, voice—and understanding, with each tug, that it was not coming back. She tried to cling to the melody alone, just the notes, just the shape, and felt even that slide away like water through fingers, because a song without the voice that sang it was only arithmetic.

The last thing to go was strange: not the sound but the *comfort*, the specific weight of being small and safe and sung to. It detached, drifted up, and hung in the air between them glowing a soft hearth-orange, and then the keeper's hand closed around it—or seemed to—and it was drawn up to the rafters, to a hook near the highest window where the daylight would find it, and it settled among the others and began, very softly, to pulse.

Ada opened her eyes.

The room looked the same. She felt like a different animal. There was a hole where the song had been, and the hole did not hurt—it *ached*, which was worse, an ache with no object, like the phantom pain of a limb. She tested the edges of it the way you test a missing tooth. Nothing. No verse. No voice. She could remember that her mother had sung to her; the fact survived like a label on an empty jar. The contents were gone.

"Well paid," the keeper said quietly. There was something almost tender in it. "You'll want to sit."

"The location," Ada said. Her voice came out level, which she counted as the day's single victory.

"The shrine stands in the cirque below the Whitefang glacier, half a day past the snowline, where three stones lean together like conspirators. Under the center stone. But hear me, seal-bearer—" the old woman leaned forward, and the lanterns dimmed toward her as if the room itself were listening, "—the sigil is not merely hidden there. It's *guarded*, in the old manner. The warden who hid it bound it to her own regret, and regrets don't sleep. You'll see things in the snow. Things you gave up. Things others gave up. The shrine shows every pilgrim the price of what they seek and asks if they mean to pay it twice." She sat back. "Also, you should know: you were followed up the valley road. Four men, iron at their hips, and one of them carries a lens. Crown glass."

Sef went very still. "How long behind us?"

"A day. Less, now." The keeper smiled thinly. "Consider that a bonus, thrown in free. I dislike inquisitors. They come through here sometimes, and they never have anything worth trading—only fear, and fear is common as gravel."

Ada was already moving, packing the oilcloth bundle tighter, checking the knife at her hip. At the door she stopped. Against every instinct, against the ache in her head where the song used to live, she turned back.

"Why do you do it?" she asked. "Trade in this. Sit here alone with a thousand other people's yesterdays."

The keeper tilted her face up toward the rafters, toward the orange glow burning high by the window, and for a moment she looked less like a toll-keeper than a woman standing in a garden.

"Because someone has to keep what people can't afford to carry," she said. "And because every lantern in this house is proof of the same thing, child: that love costs, and people pay it anyway, and the world goes on. Now go. The snow is opening its mouth, and I'd rather not have your blood on my doorstep twice in one lifetime."

* * *

They climbed out of the tollhouse pass as the light failed, onto a shoulder of rock where the trees gave up and the world turned white and blue and merciless. Behind them, far below, the tollhouse chimney smoked its thin gray thread. Ahead, the glacier called Whitefang lay along the horizon like a sleeping jaw.

Sef walked beside her, breath steaming, gill-scars dark with cold. After an hour he spoke.

"You gave up a great thing today."

"I know what I gave up."

"Do you? You gave up the sound of being loved as a child. Do you understand that most people never recover from losing that, even when they keep it?" He glanced sideways at her. "In my father's court we have a saying: *the sea does not remember its waves, yet the shore is shaped by every one.*"

"That's very pretty. Does it help?"

"No," Sef admitted. "But the drowned court says it anyway. We are a sentimental people. We have time to be."

Ada almost smiled. Then she crested the shoulder of rock, and the smile died.

Far below, winding up the valley road they'd left hours ago, torchlight moved. Four points of flame, steady and spaced like soldiers, and above them a glint of glass catching the last of the sun—a lens, sweeping the slopes in slow arcs, patient as a fishing bird.

"They've found the pass," Sef said.

"They've found the pass," Ada agreed. She pulled the oilcloth bundle tight, touched the first sigil's faint warmth through the fabric, and looked north to where three stones waited in the snow like conspirators. Half a night's hard climb ahead, a shrine full of other people's regrets, and the crown a day behind and closing.

She started walking.
