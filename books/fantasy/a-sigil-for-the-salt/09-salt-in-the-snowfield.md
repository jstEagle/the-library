## 9. Salt in the Snowfield

The snowfields above Lake Vess did not forgive. They did not forget, either—that was the trouble. Every footprint they left behind them was a sentence written in a language any tracker could read, and behind them, somewhere down in the white silence, Emeric Vale was reading.

Ada pulled her hood lower and climbed.

The wind up here had teeth. It came off the glacier in long, keening gusts that stripped the heat from her bones and filled her ears with a sound like the lake had made when it first spoke to her—that vast, patient voice beneath the water. She had not slept properly in two days. When she did sleep, she dreamed of ash on her hands, of Hollowmere's granary folding into flame, and she woke with the taste of smoke in her mouth and the first sigil burning cold against her sternum like a coin left too long in a winter stream.

*Six more,* she told herself, and climbed.

Sef moved beside her as though the snow were no obstacle at all, which, Ada supposed, for him it wasn't. His lower body was salmon-sleek and scaled in colors that shifted with the light—slate, then rose, then the deep bruised green of lake water at dusk—but his torso was that of a courtier, narrow-shouldered and elegant, dressed in robes of woven kelp that somehow never froze. His face was the worst part. It was a handsome face, a mournful face, the face of a man who had attended a thousand funerals and wept sincerely at every one, and it made Ada want to both trust him and throw him off the mountain.

"You walk like someone being chased," Sef observed. His voice carried strangely in the wind, arriving whole despite the gale, as if the air itself made an effort for him.

"I am someone being chased."

"You walk like someone being chased *before* the chasing began." He glided alongside her, leaving no prints. "In my court, we had a saying for that. It does not translate. Roughly: *the fish that fears the net swims into it faster.*"

"Your court sounds charming."

"It was," Sef said, and the grief in it was so plain, so unperformed, that Ada looked away. "It was charming. It was flooded with light and song and the king's daughters danced on the drowned roofs of the old town, and now it is dark, and cold, and dying by inches as the covenant rots. So yes. Charming. Past tense."

Ada said nothing. She was learning that about Sef—when he spoke of the drowned court, silence was the only decent answer.

They crested the ridge near midday, though midday up here was a gray suggestion rather than a fact, and Ada saw the shrine.

It stood in a shallow bowl of the snowfield like a bone pushed up through flesh: a circle of standing stones, each taller than two men, each crusted in ice so thick the original shape of them was only a rumor. At the center rose a smaller stone, waist-high, and even from the ridge Ada could feel it—the same pull she'd felt at her own hidden cache, the same tugging behind her ribs, like a fishhook set in her heart.

The second sigil.

"It was a shrine to the covenant, before the breaking," Sef said quietly. "Pilgrims came up from the lowlands every spring. They brought salt from the lakeshore and packed it into the stone's seams, and the salt kept the ice away, and the ice never touched the heart of it." He paused. "The salt has not been renewed in five years. The ice has won."

"Five years," Ada said. "Everything went wrong five years ago."

"Everything went wrong the night a child broke a pact she did not know she was making," Sef said. "The years are only the ripples."

She shot him a look, but his courtier's face gave nothing back, and the wind pushed them down into the bowl before she could decide whether he was mocking her or mourning her.

* * *

The shrine's heart, when Ada reached it, was exactly as Sef had promised: the outer stones wore armor of ice a hand's thickness, but the central altar—when she laid her bare palm against it—was dry, and cold, and *waiting*.

The sigil was inside a memory. She knew that now, understood the architecture of the thing in a way that made her feel ill. Somewhere in the years she had stolen from herself, in the childhood she had carved away to survive, she had taken a memory that held this sigil the way a fruit holds a seed. She had carried it for years without knowing. She had *sold pieces of it*, maybe. Some farmer in the Thornfell roads might be walking around right now with a fragment of the covenant lodged in his skull like shrapnel, and she would never know which farmer, never find him, and the thought made her want to sit down in the snow and not get up.

But this one—the shrine held its own copy. The old covenant-keepers had been thorough. They had seeded their sigils in more than one vessel, and this altar was a vessel.

Ada closed her eyes and reached.

The memory came to her like cold water flooding in.

*Snow. This same snowfield, but the sky is the hard blue of a bell, and there are people here—dozens of them, robed in gray and white, salt-gray and snow-white, and they are singing. She is small. She is holding someone's hand—a woman's hand, chapped and strong, and the woman is singing too, and Ada knows the voice, knows it the way you know your own heartbeat, and she cannot see the woman's face because the memory has been stolen and re-stolen and worn smooth as a river stone, but she knows—*

*Her mother.*

*The song is the lullaby. The lullaby she sold at the Tollhouse of Whispers, the memory she paid the blind keeper, the one she told herself was a fair price for a road forward—and it was this, it was always this, the lullaby was sung here first, at this altar, by covenant-keepers, and her mother was one of them—*

The memory broke apart like ice underfoot.

Ada came back to herself on her knees in the snow, gasping, her palm still pressed to the dry stone. The second sigil rose out of the altar like a bubble rising through dark water: a small shape of light, salt-white, edged in the deep green of lake depths. It drifted to her and sank into her chest beside the first, and the two of them burned there together, twin coins of cold fire, and Ada wept without quite knowing why.

"The lullaby," she said, when she could speak. "My mother sang it here. She was—" Her voice cracked. "Sef. What was she?"

Sef stood at the circle's edge, and for once his mournful face had no performance in it at all.

"She was a keeper," he said. "One of the last. And you are her daughter, seal-bearer, and the covenant remembers its own blood even when the blood does not remember itself." He turned his head, sudden, sharp, toward the ridge they had descended—the way a heron turns toward a fish beneath the water. "And we are out of time."

Ada heard it a heartbeat later: the creak of harness leather. The snort of horses. The soft, carrying crunch of many boots in snow, coming around the southern shoulder of the bowl in a line, spread out, disciplined.

Riders. At least eight. Dismounting at the bowl's rim to come at the shrine on foot, which meant they were good, which meant they were Emeric's.

"They followed us across the open snow," Ada breathed. "How—"

"Your footprints," Sef said. "You write your fears in a language soldiers read fluently."

* * *

There was no cover in the bowl. The standing stones were pillars of ice, translucent, and behind them you were visible as a smudge of shadow—a delay, not a defense. The only way out was the northern slope, steep and wind-scoured, a climb that would take minutes they did not have.

Ada pressed her back against the altar stone and did arithmetic. Eight soldiers, maybe ten. She had a knife, a thief's skills useless on open snow, and a drowned courtier whose elegant torso housed no weapon she had ever seen.

And she had something else.

Her gift. Her curse. The thing the crown was burning people for.

She reached into herself—not into the stolen childhood, not into the sealed vault of her own lost years, but into the *other* cache, the merchant's stock, the inventory of a thief. Memories she had lifted on the smuggler roads, bought and bartered and taken, each one tagged and shelved the way Maro had taught her to tag and shelve stolen goods. She had a cooper's memory of his wedding day. A widow's memory of her husband's laugh. A smuggler's memory of a betrayal in a boathouse.

And she had a soldier's memory.

She had taken it at a waystation near Greywater Ford, from a man who didn't know he'd lost it, a scarred sergeant named Doss who'd drunk too much and slept too hard. It was a small memory, as soldier memories went. Not a battle. Something worse. It was Doss and three other men, and an order that should never have been given, and a farmhouse that burned with people inside it, and the sergeant's own voice in the memory saying, *we follow orders or we hang together,* and the taste of the lie in that sentence, and the shame that Doss carried every day like a stone in his boot.

Ada had taken it because shame-saturated memories sold well to collectors who liked to feel wicked safely. She had never spent it.

She spent it now.

She could not put it in the soldiers' heads—her gift did not work that way, never had. She could only take, and hold, and trade, and show. But she could *show*. She could make it real in the air between them, the way the lake had shown her the first sigil, the way Emeric had once watched her pour a dead man's last morning into a basin of water for a paying client. She cracked the memory open and let it pour out into the snowfield air, wide and bright and undeniable: the farmhouse, the fire, the screaming, the order, the sergeant's voice, the shame—

The lead soldiers stopped as one man.

Ada saw it happen. Saw the memory take them the way cold water takes a swimmer. The point man—a young corporal, cheeks chapped raw—went white and swayed. Behind him, a broad-shouldered trooper made a sound like a man being sick. And at the center of the line, a scarred sergeant—*Doss himself*, of course it was Doss himself, Emeric's own sergeant, because the world was a small cruel circle—staggered as though struck, because he was seeing his own hidden sin standing in the snow in front of his men, wearing his own face.

"What in the frozen hells," someone whispered.

"It's her," Doss rasped. "The memory-thief. She's got— that's *mine*, that's *inside me*—" And the horror in his voice was not of Ada but of himself, of the thing now exposed, of the men around him seeing what he carried.

"Stand fast," the corporal said, but his voice shook.

"Stand *fast*," Doss snarled, and then, lower, only for the men nearest him, only loud enough for a thief's trained ears to catch: "Did you see it? Did you all see what I—" He couldn't finish. He didn't have to. Ada watched the squad's spine crack along the fault line she'd chosen: not fear of her, but fear of *each other*, fear of the memory they'd all just witnessed, fear of what else the thief might have, what else she might *show*.

"Who else has she got things from?" someone muttered. "Who's next? Your wife? Your—"

"Enough." The corporal again, trying to be the officer the moment needed. "Form up. We have orders—"

"Orders." Doss laughed, and it was an awful sound. "You want to talk about orders, boy? You want to know what orders look like when they're finished with you?"

And that was when Sef moved.

Ada hadn't even seen him circle. He came around the eastern stones in a low, flowing rush, half-gliding on the snow, and he wasn't going for the soldiers at all—he was going for the horses. Three quick shapes of motion among the picketed mounts at the bowl's rim, a flash of kelp-green and scaled rose, and the horses screamed and broke. They scattered down the southern slope in a clatter of ice and panic, taking the squad's mobility with them, and the soldiers turned as one toward the sound, toward the impossible fish-man, and someone shouted, "*Blessed iron! It's one of the drowned—blessed iron, now!*"

Ada saw the blade come out. Saw it catch the dead gray light. Saw the blessing-marks along its fuller, ink-dark sigils of the crown's own counter-magic, the kind the Church forged for exactly this, for things from beneath the water.

"Sef!" she screamed.

He was fast, but the snow slowed him where it would have slowed no one in his own element, and the trooper with the blessed blade was fast too, and Ada watched the iron take Sef across the shoulder in a bright, ugly arc. The sound he made was not a scream. It was worse—a long, wet, astonished note, like a bell cracking, like something from the deep being shown the sun. His blood was not red. It was dark and silver-flecked, and where it fell on the snow it *hissed*, and Sef went down against the altar stone with his courtier's face finally, finally showing something unperformed: agony, naked and ancient.

The second sigil in Ada's chest flared white-hot. The first answered it. And Ada Nystrom, who had spent five years being careful, who had survived the smuggler roads by never fighting, never standing, never giving anyone a reason to remember her face—Ada stopped thinking like a thief.

She thought like a storm.

She opened every shameful memory she carried, all at once, and poured them into the snowfield: the sergeant's farmhouse fire, a quartermaster's stolen ledgers, a lieutenant's forged orders, a priest's memory of doubting his own god, a soldier's memory of his captain taking bribes—she ripped them open like packages and let the contents spill, and the squad broke. Not all of them. But three men threw down their arms and simply sat down in the snow, and two more turned on each other with accusations already flying, and the corporal stood in the middle of it with his sword up and his eyes wild, shouting orders into a hurricane of exposed sins.

And Doss—Doss was on his knees, weeping, staring at nothing.

Ada ran to Sef.

He was shivering, which she hadn't known his kind could do. The wound across his shoulder smoked faintly, and the flesh around it had gone gray, the beautiful shifting scales dulled to the color of dead fish. "The iron," he managed. "It doesn't cut, seal-bearer. It *unmakes*. Slowly. I need water. Deep water. Or I need—" His breath hitched. "I need the covenant mended, and I suspect we cannot wait that long."

"There's a lake below the north slope," Ada said. "Glacial. Half-frozen."

"Glacial," Sef repeated, with the ghost of his courtier's smile. "You offer a dying man a puddle."

"I'm offering you the only water I know."

Around them the squad was in full rout, the corporal dragging two men up the southern slope on foot, their horses gone, their discipline gone, their certainty gone. Doss still knelt in the snow. As Ada hauled Sef's arm across her shoulders—as she took his weight, which was strangely little, strangely light, like carrying driftwood—Doss looked up at her.

"You should have left it buried," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Some things, you dig them up, they don't go back in the ground."

"I know," Ada said. "Believe me, I know."

She turned north, toward the ice lake, with the drowned man's blood hissing silver on the snow behind her and two sigils burning in her chest like swallowed stars—and she thought, with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding home: *He'll come now. Not the squad. Not the riders. Him. I showed a squad of his soldiers what shame looks like, and when Emeric Vale finds them sitting in the snow, he will stop hunting a thief.*

*He will start hunting me.*

The wind came down off the glacier, keening, and somewhere beneath its voice—beneath it, the way the lullaby had lived beneath the years—Ada heard the lake below the north slope calling to her.

Calling her down.

Calling her *faster*.
