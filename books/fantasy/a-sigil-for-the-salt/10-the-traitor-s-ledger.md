## 10. The Traitor's Ledger

Maro's house smelled the way it always had—tallow smoke, dried eel, and the sour sweetness of ink gone old. Ada stood in the doorway of his counting room for a long moment before knocking, letting herself remember why she'd come, because once she stepped inside there would be no turning back. The mountains were behind her. Sef waited in the tree line with a wound that blessed iron kept reopening, and the snowfields ahead were impassable without papers, safehouses, and a guide who knew which passes the crown's riders watched.

Which meant Maro. Which meant trust, and she had learned the hard way what trust cost in Thornfell.

She knocked twice, then a third time with her knuckle's edge—the old signal, thief to fence, *friend at the door, not the law*.

The bolt slid. Maro filled the gap in his nightshirt, gray-stubbled, one eye still gummed with sleep. For three full seconds he looked at her without recognition, and something in Ada's chest went cold and quiet. Then his good eye widened, and he grabbed her wrist and hauled her inside so fast she stumbled.

"You're dead," he hissed, barring the door behind her. "Do you understand? You are a *dead woman*. There are proclamations with your description nailed to every tollhouse from Greywater Ford to the coast, and you're standing on my doorstep like it's market day."

"Good evening to you too."

"Don't—" He pressed both hands over his face and dragged them down, and when he lowered them he was older than she remembered. Five years was not so long, but it had worked on him like water on soft stone. "Kitchen. Not the front room. And keep your voice under your breath even there; walls in this town have learned to listen since you left."

The kitchen was unchanged—a black stove, two chairs, a shelf of chipped crockery, and above the hearth the little carved boat he'd never explain to her. She sat where she'd sat a hundred times as a girl of seventeen, eating his burnt porridge while he taught her how to price a memory by the weight of its grief. He put water on to boil without asking, the way he used to, and for one dangerous moment she let herself feel *home*.

That feeling lasted until she saw the ledger.

It lay open on the corner of the table, half-buried under a stack of trade bills. Maro kept everything in ledgers—debts, favors, prices paid and owed—and she knew its green leather binding the way she knew her own hands. But this page was newer. Fresher ink. Her eyes caught on a figure first: **300 crowns**. Then the word beside it, written small and neat in a hand she recognized as well as her own heartbeat.

**Nystrom.**

The kettle began to sing. Neither of them moved toward it.

"Maro," she said quietly. "What is this?"

"It's nothing. Accounts." He reached across the table—not fast, Maro was never fast—but she got there first, sliding the ledger out from under the trade bills and into the lamplight. He didn't fight her for it. That was worse. A man who lied would have fought her.

She read.

It wasn't a single entry but a correspondence, dates marching down the margin in Maro's tidy script. *Inquiry received, crown assessor, re: memory-wielders active on Thornfell roads.* *Provided description per prior arrangement.* *Payment received, 100 crowns, balance on confirmation.* And then, dated eleven days ago, in ink still sharp-edged:

*Confirmed sighting, Greywater Ford. Subject identified as A. Nystrom. Balance due, 200 crowns. Advised crown that subject likely seeks high passes; suggested Vess route.*

Suggested Vess route. The road she had taken. The road Emeric Vale's riders were on now, somewhere behind her in the dark, closing like a hand.

Ada set the ledger down with great care, the way she handled sigils she didn't yet understand. When she looked up, Maro wouldn't meet her eyes. He stared at the singing kettle as though it might save him if it boiled long enough.

"How long?" she asked.

He flinched. "Ada—"

"*How long?*"

"Three years." His voice came out sanded flat. "Since the second winter after you vanished. The crown sent an assessor to every fence between here and the capital. Not asking questions—*offering*. And I said no, Ada, I want you to know I said no the first year. Twice." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as ash. "Then they came back with a list. Names of fences who'd refused. Half of them are dead or in the salt mines now. And I sat right here, in this chair, and did sums. That's all I've ever done, girl. Sums. Three hundred crowns against my neck, against this house, against—" His hand made a vague gesture that took in the whole shabby kingdom of his life. "I'm sixty-one years old. I can't run salt-mine sums."

"The Vess route," she repeated. "You told them where I'd go."

"I told them where any sensible thief would go." Finally he turned to face her, and his eyes were wet, and Ada discovered that pity could be more painful than rage. "I also told them the passes would be snowed shut till thaw. I told them you were cleverer than their trackers. I bought you time with the same breath I sold you, if you want the truth of the arithmetic. It's all I had."

The kettle screamed itself hoarse. Maro moved at last to lift it off the flame, and Ada watched his trembling hands and felt the cold thing in her chest turn over and show her its other side.

Because here was the truth she couldn't escape: she understood him. Completely. She had built a life on exactly this arithmetic—weighing other people's grief against her own survival, coin by coin. She had stolen a merchant's dead daughter not three weeks ago. If anyone on this earth had the standing to condemn Maro Okonkwo's ledger, it was certainly not the woman who sold sorrow by the dram.

And yet.

"They'll hang you anyway," she said. "When they catch me. You know that. Fences who sell names get thanked and buried, in whichever order suits them."

"I know it." He set two cups down, poured, and the tea was the cheap bitter kind he always drank himself, saving the good leaves for buyers. Even now. "I did the sums, Ada. I told you. I'm not going to survive this war between the crown and whatever you've become. Nobody small survives it. All I chose was which way to be small."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low. Or rather, Maro talked, and Ada listened, and between his words she took inventory of the room like a thief, because old habits are older than forgiveness.

He gave her what he had. Papers—a dead man's travel writ, clean enough. The name of a pass-keeper at Cold Saddle who owed Maro nine years of favors. A warning about the tollhouse at Graymere, whose blind keeper had changed allegiance sometime in the autumn and now traded secrets to the crown as readily as to travelers. Ada filed that away; the tollhouse keeper had been on Sef's map of whispers, and apparently the drowned court's intelligence was as outdated as its king.

"And after Cold Saddle?" Maro asked. "Where does this road of yours end?"

She almost told him. The words rose up—*the covenant, the sigils, the lake that speaks, the envoy bleeding blessed iron in your tree line*—and she bit every one of them back. Not because she feared betrayal. The ledger had already done its worst. She stayed silent because some part of her, the part that had survived Hollowmere and five years of stolen years, knew that every person who knew her purpose became a weight she'd have to carry or drop.

"South," she said. "Toward the water."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Maro studied her across the dying lamplight, and then he nodded slowly, as though she'd passed some test he hadn't meant to give. "You sound different. Older. Like the memories finally fit." He smiled, lopsided, the smile that had made a lonely orphan girl believe someone in Thornfell was glad to see her come in from the cold. "I used to tell buyers you were heartless, you know. Made your work seem cleaner. Liar, of course. You've got the biggest heart on these roads, and it's been eating you alive since the day I met you."

"Flattery won't unpick the ledger."

"No. Nothing will." He drained his tea and set the cup down with the finality of a man laying down cards. "So take your payment."

Ada went very still. "What?"

"You heard me." He spread his hands open on the table, palms up—an offering, or surrender, hard to say. "I sold your name for three hundred crowns. You've come back from the dead to find it, and we both know you're not leaving here with nothing but bad tea and a travel writ. So take it. Take what I owe. I know how you pay yourselves, girl—I taught you, God help me, I taught you myself."

"I don't steal from friends."

"Then it's lucky," Maro said gently, "that I'm selling instead."

* * *

She should have refused.

She told herself that all the way through it—that a better woman would have pushed back from the table, taken the papers, and walked into the snow owing nothing and owning nothing, carrying her anger like a lantern. That taking his memory was just another entry in another ledger, and the whole rotten arithmetic of Thornfell would win again, as it always won.

But she thought of Sef in the trees, wound weeping, trusting her with a quest that had already cost her a mother's lullaby. She thought of the road south and every door that might close. She thought of the blind keeper at Graymere, and what secrets cost now that the world had grown teeth. And she thought—if she was honest, and the hour was late enough for honesty—that she wanted it. Wanted one warm thing to carry into the cold. Even if it came at the price of the man who'd given it.

"Tell me what it is first," she said. "I choose what I take. Those are my rules, Maro. You wrote them yourself, in that ledger, the winter I turned eighteen."

He closed his eyes. Nodded.

"It's the day you came to me," he said. "First real job you ever ran, the ferryman's grief—you remember. But that's not the memory. The memory is afterward. You fell asleep in that chair—" he nodded at it, "—with the sigil still cooling in your pocket, and I sat up all night watching you breathe, because I'd promised your mother I'd look after you if ever the roads brought you to me. And I thought, *this child has no idea what she's going to become. This child will break my heart or the world, one of the two.*" His voice caught, held, steadied. "And I loved you. That's the memory. Twenty minutes of an old man loving a sleeping thief. That's what I owe you. Take it and be done with me."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like slow water, like the lake calling from far away.

"Maro," she whispered. "If I take it, I won't remember that you loved me. I'll only remember that you sold me."

"I know." He opened his eyes, and they were clear now, dry, almost peaceful. "But *you'll* be lighter. And I'll get to stop being ashamed of it. Everyone wins, girl. That's the only honest sum I've ever done."

She reached across the table.

Her gift woke the way it always did—a warmth in her fingertips, a taste of salt at the back of the throat—and she laid two fingers to his temple with a tenderness that surprised her. Maro exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a burden carried thirty years.

The memory came away like a coal drawn from a fire: warmth, lamplight, the small rhythm of her own sleeping breath seen from across a room, and underneath it all, steady as bedrock, *I promised your mother. I promised your mother. I promised—*

Ada gasped. The sigil bloomed between her fingers—a small amber square shot through with a thread of gold, warm to the touch, warmer than any grief-sigil she had ever lifted. Love, it seemed, pressed differently than sorrow. Denser. Brighter.

And then it was gone from her mind, as cleanly as a knife through tallow. She sat in a stranger's kitchen, holding a stranger's debt-token, looking at an old man she had known for perhaps an hour. He looked back at her with polite blankness, rubbing his temple, already reaching for the travel writ on the table.

"Papers," he said briskly. "Cold Saddle. Anything else, mistress?"

The cold thing in her chest closed its fist.

"No," Ada said. "Thank you. That's everything."

She took the writ and left the ledger where it lay. At the door she paused—one last look at the kitchen, the stove, the little carved boat above the hearth that meant nothing to her now, nothing at all—and felt the absence where twenty minutes of love had been, an ache shaped exactly like the thing removed.

Outside, the snow had stopped. Sef detached himself from the tree line, moving stiffly, his wounded side held careful, his fish-pale eyes finding hers with unsettling precision.

"You carry a new light," he said. "It burns gold. What did you buy?"

"A friend's friendship," Ada said. "Secondhand."

She tucked the golden sigil deep in her coat, next to the lullaby-shaped hole where her mother's voice used to live, and turned south toward Cold Saddle and the frozen shrine beyond it. Behind her, Maro's lamp went out.

She was a mile down the road before she understood what else she'd carried out of that house—not just the sigil, but the shape of Maro's last words before her gift had silenced him forever: *I promised your mother.*

Her mother. Who Maro had known. Who Maro had *promised*.

Who had died, according to the sixth sigil waiting somewhere ahead of her, with salt on her lips and a covenant on her tongue.

Ada pulled her hood up against the wind and walked faster, toward the snowfields, toward whatever truth her own stolen childhood had buried in the ice.
