## 11. Emeric at the Fireline

The firebreak cut through the black pines like a scar that hadn't healed—a hundred yards of ash-grey earth and dead bracken, running straight as a ruler's line up the mountain's shoulder. Ada had used such cuts before, in her smuggling years. They were the crown's own roads of convenience: burned clear to stop the fires from spreading, and then abandoned, because no one wanted to be seen on ground so naked.

Which was why she should have known better than to cross it alone.

She heard him before she saw him—the creak of a saddle, the deliberate crunch of a boot on scorched soil—and her hand went to the knife at her hip even as she turned.

Emeric Vale sat his grey gelding ten paces away, reins loose, sword sheathed, his inquisitor's coat unbuttoned despite the cold. No riders flanked him. No crossbows waited in the treeline—at least none she could hear, and five years of surviving Thornfell had made her ears honest.

"Captain Vale." She kept her voice flat as slate. "Come to drag me back in chains?"

"I came alone," he said. He dismounted slowly, the way he did everything—deliberate, economical, as if motion were a language he refused to speak carelessly. He left the horse standing. "Ada."

Not *thief*. Not *seal-bearer*. Just her name, spoken in the low voice she had spent half a decade trying to forget, and failing every single night.

"You shouldn't have followed me here."

"You shouldn't have let me see you at Greywater Ford." He took one step closer. The wind moved between them, carrying the smell of old char and new snow. "You were always careless when you were frightened. You touch your collar. You've done it since Hollowmere."

Her fingers stilled against her throat. She hated him for knowing that. She hated herself more for the warmth that bloomed anyway, traitorous as everything else about her.

"I'm not the girl you knew."

"No." His eyes—grey-green, patient, the eyes that had once watched her across a barracks fire while she pretended not to notice—traveled over her face like a man reading a letter he'd received too late. "The girl I knew stole bread and lied about it badly. The woman they're hunting steals memories and lies about nothing at all. That's worse, somehow."

"Is it?" She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as ice on a puddle. "Then arrest me, Captain. Do your duty. The crown pays well for memory-wielders—I've seen the proclamations. Dead or alive, though I suspect alive is worth more to Lord Chancellor Ives."

Something flickered across Emeric's face at Ives's name—something quick and dark, gone before she could name it.

"That's what I've come to ask you about," he said.

"The crown's pay? Negotiate with my captor, is that it?"

"The truth." He said it quietly, but there was iron under it, the same iron that had made him a captain at thirty. "I've been hunting you for four months, Ada. I've read the files. Your mother's execution—filed under sedition, sealed by order of the Chancellor himself. Hollowmere's granary fire—filed under accident, then re-filed under heresy, then re-sealed. Three different stories for one night, each one signed by men who weren't there. I interrogated a smuggler last week who wept when I mentioned your name, and when I asked why, he said—" Emeric's jaw tightened— "he said *she takes the pain out and leaves you lighter, gods forgive me, she left me lighter*."

He stopped three paces from her. Close enough that she could see the exhaustion carved beneath his eyes, the sleeplessness of a man who had been chasing a ghost and finding his own heart in the trail.

"So before I put these chains on you"—he lifted them now, coiled in his fist like a sleeping snake, plain iron, no blessing worked into the links, which was itself an answer to a question she hadn't asked—"I want the truth. From you. Whatever you are. Whatever this hunt actually is. I want to hear it from your mouth and not a clerk's pen."

"And if I lie?"

"You won't." A ghost of the old smile, bitter at the edges. "You never could. It's why you became a thief of true things instead of false ones."

Ada stood in the middle of the firebreak with the wind pulling at her hair and felt the whole shape of her life tilt. Behind her, up the mountain, Sef waited by the shrine road with a wounded flank and dwindling patience. Ahead of her lay the drowned chapel, the third sigil, the long arithmetic of sacrifice she had already begun paying. And here, blocking the path like a question she had dodged for five years, stood the only man whose memory she had never stolen—not because she couldn't, but because she had wanted at least one thing in her head that was hers, honestly hers, kept rather than taken.

"Emeric," she said carefully, "the truth will cost you more than you know."

"My whole life has been paid for by other people's secrets. Let it finally buy something."

She almost smiled. Instead, she reached into the satchel at her hip.

Her sigils clicked softly against one another—small tablets of pressed salt and light, each one a stolen piece of someone's past. Most were coin: griefs sold to grieving families, joys sold to the joyless. But a few were different. A few were evidence.

"This is a soldier's memory," she said. "Corporal Dennel Rask, third company, garrisoned at Hollowmere the winter the granary burned. He's dead now—fever, two years back—but his memory isn't. I took it off a pawnbroker in Saltmarsh who didn't know what he had." She held the sigil up between them, letting the pale light of it play across both their faces. "Do you know how my gift works, Captain? Truly works?"

"They say you take a thing and leave a gap."

"Worse than a gap. I take the memory entire—it comes out of them like a splinter, whole, with all its weight. And I can give it to another. Press it into a willing mind, and they live it. Not watch it. *Live* it. Every sense. Every fear." She met his eyes. "What I'm about to show you, you'll feel from the inside. You'll smell the smoke. You'll hear the orders. And afterward you won't be able to unknow it, because it will be *yours*, Emeric, as much as anything you've lived yourself. Can you carry that?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The wind combed the dead grass flat. Somewhere above them a crow complained and went silent, as if it too were waiting.

"I've carried worse," he said at last. "I carried the order to burn the Thornfell waystation. Forty souls, most of them children of smugglers, and I rode escort for the men who lit it because the writ bore the Chancellor's seal and I told myself the seal was the same as right." His voice didn't shake, but it went very quiet, which was worse. "Show me."

She stepped close—close enough to feel the heat of him, close enough that her hands remembered things her mind had tried to bury—and pressed the sigil to his brow.

"*Remember*," she whispered, and the old word of her gift went out of her like breath.

* * *

Smoke.

He was Corporal Dennel Rask, nineteen years old, and his hands were shaking around a torch that weighed nothing and everything. The granary at Hollowmere rose black against the stars, and around it stood eleven soldiers in the livery of the crown, and before it stood a woman with silver pins in her hair holding a child behind her skirts.

"She won't open the inner store," Sergeant Vael was saying, his voice tight with something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite shame. "Chancellor's orders. If the covenant-keepers won't yield the vault, we burn the stores above it. They'll open it when their children starve."

"The vault's empty," the woman said. Her voice was calm in a way that made the hair on Rask's neck rise. "You know it's empty. The covenant sigils were scattered years ago—we keep nothing here but grain and prayers. Burn our food and all you'll reap is a hungry village and a broken pact."

"The pact was broken," Vael said, "the day the Chancellor decided it was."

Rask remembered wanting to lower his torch. Remembered the sergeant's eyes sliding to his, daring him. Remembered the child—the little girl with her mother's stubborn chin—peeking around the silver-pinned woman's skirts, staring at him with eyes too old for a face so young.

And he remembered the word that started it. One word, dropped casual as a stone down a well, from the rider who had brought the Chancellor's sealed writ: *the seal-child watches*. As if it mattered. As if a seven-year-old hiding behind her mother could threaten an empire.

The torch left his hands.

Afterward—the screaming, the orange dark, the woman dragging the girl into the smoke toward the vault door that would not save them, the sergeant shouting *the seal-child watches* again and again like a prayer or a curse—afterward, Rask knelt in the snow and vomited, and the rider with the writ crouched beside him and gripped his shoulder with terrible kindness.

"You saw nothing," the rider said. "That's an order. Forget it, and the crown will make forgetting easy."

And they did. They brought out a man in a grey hood—Rask's memory slid around the man's face like water refusing to hold a shape—and the man touched Rask's temple and the night began to come apart, the fire folding into fog, the screaming thinning into wind—

But Rask was strong in the way of simple, stubborn men. He held one fragment of it, one splinter of the burning night, buried deep where the hooded man's art could not reach. He held it for six years. And then a thief with kind eyes and a thief's gentle hands had drawn it out of him in a Saltmarsh alley, had looked at what she held, and had wept.

* * *

Emeric came back to himself on his knees in the ash of the firebreak.

He didn't remember falling. His gauntlets were dug into the scorched earth; his breath came in ragged pulls; and the taste of smoke filled his mouth though there was no fire within twenty miles. The sigil lay cold on the ground between them, spent, its light guttered out.

"The girl," he rasped. "In the memory. The seal-child."

"Me." Ada's voice came from very far above him. "My mother was a covenant-keeper. The crown broke the pact that night, Emeric. Not the drowned kingdom. Not thieves like me. *Your* Chancellor sent soldiers to starve a village into surrendering a vault that was already empty, and when that failed, he burned the food anyway and called it heresy after the fact. Everything since—the hunt, the executions, the hedge-witches dragged through market squares—is him trying to bury the evidence. And I'm the last witness. That's why the hunt is for me specifically. That's why Ives wants me alive."

Emeric pushed himself upright. He stood swaying slightly, a man relearning the weight of his own body, and when he looked at her his face was terrible—not with hatred, but with the specific anguish of a soldier discovering that the flag he bled for had been stitched from lies.

"The waystation," he said hoarsely. "Thornfell. Forty souls. I told myself it was law. I told myself—gods, Ada, I built my whole *life* on telling myself—"

"I know."

"You *knew*? All this time—you knew what the crown was, what I was serving—"

"I knew what you were," she said fiercely, stepping toward him. "That was never in question. You think I stayed away five years because I stopped—" She caught herself. The wind filled the silence. "Because it was safer for you not to find me."

"Safer." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I've spent five years thinking you left because I was dull, or dutiful, or because I chose the commission over—" He stopped. Looked at the chains still coiled in his fist. Looked at them for a long time.

Then he opened his hand and let them fall into the ash.

Neither of them moved. The sound of it was very small and very loud, iron meeting dead earth, and Ada felt it land somewhere deeper than her ears.

"I can't unsee that memory," Emeric said quietly. "You warned me, and you were right. It's mine now. Which means I'm a witness too—which means the moment I ride back to camp, some part of me becomes treason." He straightened, and the captain came back into his spine, but changed, reforged around a crack. "There are riders on the north road. Six, under Lieutenant Harrow. Ives ordered them to sweep the firebreaks at dawn. You have until first light, no more. Take the high pass east of the lake—their horses won't manage the scree."

"Why?" The question tore out of her before she could dress it in cunning. "Why let me go? Why not take the bounty, take the promotion, take the clean conscience of a man who did his duty?"

"Because my conscience stopped being clean about eight minutes ago," he said, "and it turns out it was never mine to begin with—it was issued to me with the uniform." He picked up the chains, slow and deliberate, and hung them from his saddle. Evidence of a capture that never happened, or preparation for one that might. Even he perhaps didn't know which. "Go, Ada."

"Emeric—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, breaking her heart with three syllables: "Please."

She went. Four steps. Five. On the sixth she turned, because she was a thief and had never learned to leave a valuable thing behind, and she said the thing she had carried for five years like a stone in her boot:

"When this is finished—if I'm still me when it's finished—I'll find you. Not the captain. You."

He didn't answer. He stood beside his grey horse in the dying light with the chains on his saddle and the smoke of another man's memory in his lungs, and he watched her go, and Ada Nystrom walked into the pines carrying more hope than she had trusted herself to hold since Hollowmere.

Behind her, Emeric Vale mounted up and turned toward the north road and the lie he would have to tell at dawn, and hated himself with a purity that bordered on devotion—because he had traded his duty for her, and would have traded it again, and some part of him understood that this was exactly the kind of weakness the crown had always promised to protect him from, and exactly the kind of strength it could never forgive.

He spurred the horse south, toward the riders, toward the lie.

Above the firebreak, in the deep pines, a shape that was half salmon and half courtier unfolded itself from the shadows where it had watched everything, and Sef's voice was soft as water finding a crack.

"Now you understand," the envoy murmured to the dark, to the girl already climbing, to no one and everyone, "what the seventh sigil will ask of you both. Come. The thaw does not wait, and neither does the chapel."

Far below, unseen, the waters of Lake Vess had begun, very quietly, to rise.
