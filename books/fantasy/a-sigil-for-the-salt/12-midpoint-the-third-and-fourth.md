## 12. Midpoint: The Third and Fourth

The chapel had drowned a hundred years ago and still refused to die.

Ada saw its spire first—a black finger breaking the surface of Lake Vess's southern arm, furred with weed and white with birdlime. The water around it lay flat as hammered tin. Sef swam beside her boat in his strange half-way fashion, salmon-sleek below the waist, his courtier's face tilted up at her through the shallows, and even now, after weeks of it, she could not look at him without her mind trying to choose what he was and failing.

"You're certain it's here," she said. Not a question. She was stalling, and they both knew it.

"The third sigil has sung from that water since before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath." Sef's voice came out of the lake like something remembered rather than spoken. "It is tired of singing, thief. Hurry."

She stripped to her shift, wrapped the six recovered—no, four; four sigils, she corrected herself, the count still felt like an open wound—wrapped the four sigils in oilcloth and bound them to her belt where the water couldn't touch them. The first two sat against her ribs like coals that never quite cooled: ash-smelling Hollowmere, salt-bitter from the snowfield shrine. When she closed her eyes she could feel them humming out of phase with each other, two notes of a chord missing five.

"And if there are bones down there?" she asked.

"There are always bones." Sef sank until only his eyes showed, dark and patient as the lake itself. "Yours will not be among them today. I have seen it."

"You've seen nothing. You've *guessed*, which is what your kind does instead of thinking."

His laugh bubbled up around her ankles, warm despite everything. "Go down, seal-bearer."

She went down.

The cold took her like a hand closing. Below the surface the light went green, then grey, then the color of old grief, and the chapel rose out of it gradually—a roof collapsed into lacework of beams, walls furred with silt-bloom, windows gone to nothing so that the water poured through the nave like a congregation that had stayed after the last service ended. Ada kicked down the aisle between drowned pews. Fish scattered from the altar like startled thoughts.

The bell rope still hung. That undid her more than the bones would have—the small human stubbornness of it, someone's rope, someone's knots, waiting for a hand that had been fish-food for a century.

The sigil was in the font.

Of course it was. She should have guessed. She hung in the green water above the stone basin and felt it before she saw it: a pull behind her sternum, the same wrong-gravity tug she'd felt at the shrine, at the granary's ghost-memory. Her own stolen past calling to itself.

Inside the font, under an inch of silt, lay a memory. Not hers.

She knew that at once, and knowing it frightened her more than the drowning cold did. Every sigil so far had come out of memories she herself had stolen as a child—fragments she'd ripped loose during the night the world burned, hidden inside her own head without understanding what she carried. But this one had a different texture. Older handling. Someone else's theft.

She brushed the silt away.

The memory bloomed against her palm when she touched it, unspooling like ink in water: a young priest, rain on his face, running through the streets of Hollowmere with something cupped in his hands like a dying bird. A covenant fragment. He was weeping. Behind him the granary glowed, and the glow was not fire—it was the covenant tearing, light bleeding out of the seams of the world—and the priest ran because he could not bear to watch, and he carried his piece of the broken pact to the one place he thought holy enough to hold it.

He'd pressed it into the font himself. She watched him do it, watched his mouth move through the water-warped years—*forgive me, forgive me, I cannot carry this*—and then the memory folded shut like a moth closing its wings, and the sigil lay bare in the font: a small disc of grey-green light, carved with the same interlocking wave-and-root pattern as the others.

Ada understood, suddenly and completely, why the drowned chapel hadn't felt haunted.

It had felt *kept*. Tended by grief. A man who couldn't bear what he knew had given his unbearable thing to God, or to the water that stood in for God, and the water had held it faithfully ever since.

Her lungs were burning. She pried the sigil loose—cold, heavier than its size, humming now in harmony with the other three—and kicked for the surface, past the bell rope, past the pews, past the priest whose name no living person remembered but whose mercy had kept a piece of the world stitched together for a hundred years.

She broke the surface gasping, and Sef was there, steadying the boat, and when she held the fourth—third, third, she was losing count—the sigil up dripping, the whole lake seemed to exhale.

"Three," Sef said softly. "Three of seven."

"It wasn't lost." Her voice came out ragged. She climbed over the gunwale, streaming, shaking—not from cold. "Sef. It wasn't lost. A priest brought it here. On purpose. He hid it because he *couldn't hold it anymore.*"

Sef was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped off his elegant, mournful face.

"Yes," he said finally. "That is how covenants break, seal-bearer. Not all at once. One person at a time, setting down what they cannot carry."

* * *

The hermit lived three days north, in the high folds of the Thornfell mountains where the smuggler roads gave up and only goat tracks remained. Sef could not leave the water for long—he grew sluggish and strange past the second night, his scales dulling like old silver—so he guided her to a stream that ran down from the peaks and told her to follow it upstream until the water forgot which way it flowed.

"That's nonsense," Ada said.

"It is directions," said Sef. "There is a spring up there that surfaces, runs fifty paces, and sinks again. The locals call it the Wishing Breach. Your hermit built his hut beside it. He chose it deliberately. A man who wants to forget builds his house near water that practices forgetting."

The stream did exactly what Sef promised. Ada found it on the second morning: a bright thread of meltwater bursting from a rock face, sliding downslope in a chattering rush, and then—simply stopping, pouring itself back into the earth as if it had changed its mind. She stood at the place where the water vanished and felt the hair rise on her arms. Even her gift, her terrible lifelong gift, went quiet here. For ten full seconds she could not remember her mother's face.

Then the feeling passed, and she was Ada Nystrom again, thief and fugitive, and she hated how much of her wanted those ten seconds back.

The hut squatted on the shoulder of the slope above the breach: turf-roofed, smoke-stained, ringed with a fence of antlers and blessed-iron horseshoes that made her skin crawl. A garden of bitter herbs. A line of washing gone grey. And sitting on a stump outside the door, as though he had been waiting for her for thirty years, was a man so ordinary he was almost shocking—a balding, broad-shouldered farmer type with flour still in the creases of his knuckles, as if he'd left a bakery mid-shift and walked straight up a mountain.

He looked at her and began to cry.

"No," he said. "No, no, no. You're her. You're the one from the—" His voice cracked apart. He pressed both flour-white hands over his face. "I told them. I told the crown men. I told the priests. Nobody listens to a madman. They wrote it down as madness. *Delusion of guardianship*, the inquisitor wrote. Delusion! As if I'd *choose* this."

Ada's hand drifted toward her knife. "Who are you?"

"Tomas Aldery." He dropped his hands. His eyes were red-rimmed and terribly, lucidly sane. "Baker. Was. Before." He pointed at her belt, at the bundle where the sigils hummed. "You've got some already. I can hear them. Gods, I can *hear* them, they've been screaming at me through the rock for thirty years, the fourth one, my fourth one—"

"Your fourth."

"I hid it!" He stood up, agitated, pacing, and Ada noticed then the details that didn't fit the baker: the way his shadow fell slightly wrong in the morning light, the faint wave-pattern scarred into his forearm, old and deliberate, a covenant-mark cut by his own hand. "After the burning. After I saw what came through the seam. I took my piece—I was a warden's son, we were supposed to guard them, my father died guarding his—and I ran, and I hid it, and I swore I'd never tell anyone where. And then—" he laughed, wet and awful, "—and then I couldn't *forget where I put it.* That's the joke, thief. That's the whole joke. The hiding works too well. It lives in me now. I can't sleep. I can't bake bread without seeing the light coming out of the grain. Thirty years."

Ada's throat was tight. "You're a warden-blood. Like—"

"Like the seventh." He waved a dismissive, floury hand. "Yes. We're rare. We're *supposed* to be able to hold a covenant sigil without it eating us alive. My father could. His father could. Me—" he spread his arms, the picture of a ruined man, "—me, it turns out, cannot. So every day for thirty years I have sat here next to the forgetting-water and begged it to take it out of me, and it won't, because it isn't mine to give, and every day I have prayed for someone to come and take it *for* me."

He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were the most exhausted things she had ever seen.

"You're her. The seal-bearer. The water told me you were coming—it tells me everything except the one thing I need." He knelt. In front of her. A grown man kneeling in the mud of his own garden, hands open. "Please. Take it. Take the whole memory, I don't care, take all of it—take the burning, take my father, take the light in the granary, take every day of the last thirty years. I want to wake up tomorrow and not know why my arm has a scar. I want to plant cabbages and be *only* a gardener. Please. I'm begging you. Everyone else who ever guarded a piece either died or went mad, and I am so tired of being the second kind."

The wind moved through the antler-fence. Somewhere below, the Wishing Breach chuckled and swallowed itself.

Ada thought of the priest in the drowned chapel, pressing his unbearable thing into the font. She thought of the frozen shrine, raised by hands unknown, a coffin built for a memory no one could live beside. She thought of herself, seven years old, ripping the burning night out of her own head because a child could not survive carrying it—and leaving the wound behind, and spending twenty years bleeding from a hole she couldn't name.

*They were never lost,* she thought. *Not one of them. Every sigil on this mountain was put down by someone who loved the world too much to let themselves break it.*

The quest turned over in her chest like a boat righting. All this time she had thought she was a thief retrieving stolen goods. She wasn't. She was a rag-and-bone woman walking a road of other people's discarded burdens, collecting what they could not carry, and the crown called it heresy and Sef called it destiny and both of them were wrong. It was neither. It was *mercy*. It had been mercy from the very first night at Hollowmere, when a child too small for the task did the only merciful thing she knew how to do.

"All right," she said quietly. "Tomas. Look at me."

He looked.

"This will hurt going out. Less than it hurts staying in. I promise you that much, and I don't lie about my work." She knelt too, so they were level, and took his flour-dusted hands in hers. "When it's done, you won't remember why you're crying. You'll just be crying. Let it happen. Then go plant your cabbages."

He nodded, jaw set, a man walking to his own gallows gladly.

She opened her gift the way she hadn't opened it in twenty years—not to take, not to steal, but to *receive*. Tomas's memory rose to meet her: the granary burning with light instead of flame, his father's body at the seam, the weight of the fourth sigil in a boy's hands, thirty years of nights, thirty years of bread baked with shaking arms. It came out of him like a splinter drawn from a wound, long and slow and shrieking, and he sobbed once, deeply, the sound a house makes settling—

—and then his face went smooth. Blank. Then, slowly, puzzled, he frowned at the sky, at his herb garden, at the stranger kneeling in his mud holding something small and grey-green and glowing.

"Do I know you?" Tomas Aldery asked pleasantly. There was flour on his knuckles and thirty years gone from his shoulders. "Lovely morning. Are you lost? The path down gets treacherous past the breach—mind yourself."

And Ada Nystrom, thief, seal-bearer, breaker and mender of worlds, sat in a madman's garden and wept, because mercy, it turned out, felt exactly like theft from the inside.

Four sigils now. Four pieces of a broken promise, gathered from a font, a shrine, a cache of her own childhood sins, and the freed mind of a baker who would spend the rest of his life wondering why cabbages made him happy.

She stood, wiped her face, and turned east toward the lowlands, where the fifth sigil waited beneath the waters of Lake Vess—and where, on the road below, she could see dust rising. A column of riders. Crown blue.

Moving fast. Moving toward the lake.

Toward Emeric.
