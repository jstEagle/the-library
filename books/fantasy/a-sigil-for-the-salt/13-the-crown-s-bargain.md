## 13. The Crown's Bargain

The Chancellery of Greywater Ford had once been a granary. Lord Chancellor Aldous Ives had chosen it for that reason alone: a building built to hold the kingdom's bread now held its secrets, and secrets, like grain, kept best in the dark and the dry.

He stood at the long window of his study and watched the snow come down over the market square, where three days ago they had burned a hedge-witch whose only crime had been remembering too well. The crowd had been smaller than he'd hoped. Fear, like wheat, needed the right season to ripen. But it was coming. By spring thaw, every village from Thornfell to the coast would know what happened to those who trafficked in stolen years.

Behind him, someone knocked. Two knocks, precise as a signature.

"Enter."

Captain Emeric Vale came in with snow on his shoulders and exhaustion carved into the lines around his mouth. He was a handsome man made severe by duty—dark hair going grey early at the temples, a jaw that looked hewn rather than grown. He bowed exactly as deep as protocol required and not a finger's width further. Ives had always appreciated that about him. Men who gave you precisely what was owed were men who could be measured.

"Chancellor."

"Captain Vale. Sit." Ives did not sit himself. He never sat when delivering orders; standing gave the words weight, and weight was half of authority. "You've been in the mountains eleven days."

"Twelve."

"And your report?"

Emeric's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "We found the smuggler camp at Hollowmere Fork abandoned. Cold fires, but recent. We took two prisoners. One died of his wounds. The other—" A pause, carefully weighted. "He claims the thief he guided is a woman. Small, dark-haired, travels with something that isn't human. Something out of Lake Vess."

Ives turned from the window at last. "And do you believe him?"

"I believe he was terrified enough to tell the truth."

"Good. Terror is honest. It's loyalty that lies." Ives crossed to his desk, where a single document lay beneath a paperweight of black glass—a stone, though few who saw it guessed what kind. It had been dredged from the lakebed forty years ago by a diver who drowned three days later, raving about doors under the water. Ives had paid well for it and better for the diver's silence, which had proven permanent. "Tell me, Captain. What do you know of the Salt Covenant?"

Emeric blinked. Whatever he'd expected, it wasn't that. "Old stories. Nursemaid tales. The lakes were ruled by a drowned court before men came, and there was a pact—the land above the waterline for the crown of man, the depths for the drowned king. Then it broke, and the floods came, and the great lake towns drowned in a single night. Vessmark. Ondrelow. Half of Thornfell's history is just names of places the water took."

"Very good. And what ended it?"

"The stories disagree. Some say the drowned king broke faith. Some say we did." Emeric's voice flattened. "Some say a woman did."

"A woman," Ives repeated softly. "Yes. They usually do say that." He lifted the black glass paperweight and turned it in his fingers, letting the firelight slide across its surface. Inside the stone, if one held it at the right angle, something faintly luminous coiled and uncoiled, patient as an eel. "Sit down, Captain. What I'm about to tell you does not leave this room. Not to your lieutenants. Not to your priest. Not written down anywhere your hand has touched."

Emeric sat. His spine stayed straight as a pikestaff.

* * *

Ives told him a version of the truth.

This was his craft, and he was a master of it: the truth was a rope, and you could hang a man with all of it or none of it, and the art lay in choosing which length served. He told Emeric that the covenant was real. That it could be reforged—that the old texts in the restricted archive beneath the capital spoke plainly of seven sigils scattered when the pact shattered, and of a seal-bearer through whom the rite must pass. He told him that the floods were coming again, that the lakes had been rising these three years past, that the drowned court stirred beneath Vess like a sleeper turning toward waking, and that when the thaw came fully, the lowlands would drown and ten thousand souls with them.

All true. Every word.

What he did not tell Emeric was this: that the covenant could be reforged in either direction. That the seal-bearer was not merely a bridge between realms but a hinge, and hinges turn whichever way the strongest hand pushes. That a covenant sealed in the crown's favor would bind the drowned court into servitude—its waters tamed, its magic harvested, its king a vassal—and that Lord Chancellor Aldous Ives had spent twenty-three years and a considerable fortune positioning himself to be the hand on that hinge.

That the hunt for memory-wielders had never been about heresy at all. It was a net. And nets are thrown only when you know the fish is in the water.

"You look troubled, Captain," Ives said, watching Emeric's face with the attention of a man reading weather.

"It's a great deal to take in, Chancellor."

"It is. Take your time."

Emeric was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, in the converted granary's lower floors, clerks scratched away at the machinery of the hunt—lists of the accused, warrants unsigned, informers' letters in a dozen different hands. Ives found the sound soothing. Empire was mostly paperwork.

"The woman," Emeric said finally. "The thief. You believe she's the seal-bearer."

"I know she is." Ives set the black stone down with a soft click. "Her name is Ada Nystrom. Born in Hollowmere, daughter of Maren Nystrom—you may remember the name. Her mother was executed for covenant-keeping when the girl was seven. The child vanished afterward. Resurfaced five years ago working the smuggler roads as a memory-thief, selling fragments of other people's pasts. She has been gathering the sigils since the autumn. She carries six of them now."

Something moved behind Emeric's eyes—too fast for Ives to name it, though he catalogued it anyway. He catalogued everything.

"Six sigils," Emeric said. "How do you know that?"

"Because I have eyes in more places than you have soldiers, Captain, and because the drowned court has been careless in its desperation. Their envoy moves openly now. Half-salmon, half-courtier, weeping his way up and down the lake roads. The drowned are dying, Vale. Slowly, the way a fire dies when the air is taken from it. They need the covenant remade as much as our lowland farmers need their fields unflooded. Both sides need her." He smiled, thin and cold. "Which means both sides will come to her. And whoever holds her when the rite begins decides what the world looks like on the other side of it."

"And the crown wants that hand to be yours."

"The crown wants the realm to survive. I want the realm to survive *correctly.*" Ives let a flicker of genuine feeling show—not warmth, never warmth, but conviction, which reads as warmth from a distance. "Think of it, Captain. A kingdom no longer hostage to the water. The drowned court bound by terms *we* write. No more drowned villages. No more seasons of flood-famine. Generations yet unborn who will never know what it cost. That is worth any price paid now."

It was even, in its way, sincere. Ives had learned long ago that the most durable lies were built on foundations of true belief. He did believe the realm should survive correctly. It simply happened that *correctly*, in his private grammar, meant *with me holding the pen.*

"What are my orders?" Emeric said.

"Capture her alive. Unharmed. The sigils cannot be taken from her by force—they're woven into stolen memories, and memories die when the mind that holds them is damaged. So she comes to me whole, and willing if possible, and merely breathing if not." Ives drew a folded paper from the desk drawer and slid it across. "Your warrant. Full authority. Any garrison, any road, any door in Thornfell province opens at your word."

Emeric took the paper but didn't unfold it. His thumb pressed flat along its crease. "You said willing if possible."

"If she surrenders freely, the rite goes easier. Coercion frays the seal. The texts are clear on that." Ives watched him carefully now. "Why do you ask?"

"Because a woman who's outrun your hunters for a season won't surrender to a stranger. If I'm to take her alive, I'll need to talk to her first. Alone, perhaps. Before iron comes out."

"However you accomplish it. I care about results, not methods." Ives paused, then added, deliberately casual, "There's one more thing you should know. She was seen last winter in Greywater Ford itself. Selling at the waystation market. A merchant's grief, if the reports are accurate. She's been close to this town for months. Close to *you.*"

The silence that followed had texture. Ives let it stretch.

"You knew her," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

Emeric's face did something complicated and brief, like weather crossing water. "Five years ago. Before she disappeared. Briefly."

"Briefly." Ives nodded slowly, and here he played his deepest card, laid down with the gentleness of a man setting a sleeping child into bed. "Then you understand why I chose you for this. Not despite that history, Captain. *Because* of it. She'll run from riders. She'll run from inquisitors. She might not run from you. And a man who loved her once will understand better than any soldier how to make her stop running."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—Ives thought the captain might refuse. Might stand, might bow, might walk out and take his honor with him. Ives had prepared for that too; there were already two letters drafted, one offering Emeric promotion and transfer to the coast, the other quietly ending his career and opening an inquiry into certain irregularities during the Ondrelow campaign. Men thought themselves free until they read the second letter.

But Emeric only said, "And after? When she's taken. What happens to her?"

"That depends on the rite. On her cooperation." Which was true, in the sense that a knife's future depended on the neck it met. "She may emerge from this the most important woman in the kingdom. Honored. Protected. Remembered forever." All of that could even be arranged, in whatever form remained of her.

Emeric rose. He tucked the warrant inside his coat, against his chest, where a man keeps things he hasn't decided whether to treasure or burn. At the door he stopped, hand on the frame, back still turned.

"The hedge-witch they burned in the square," he said. "Did she steal anyone's memory?"

"She harbored one. That was enough under the edict."

"Of course it was." His voice was perfectly level. "Good evening, Chancellor."

The door closed behind him with a click soft as a lock turning.

* * *

Alone, Ives allowed himself the small luxury of sitting.

He poured a measure of plum brandy from the decanter that lived in the false bottom of the map case and drank it slowly, looking out at the falling snow. In the firelight, the black glass stone on his desk seemed to shift, the pale thing coiled inside it turning over like something dreaming.

He had been young once—younger than Emeric Vale, younger than the thief herself—when he'd first read the restricted texts. He had been a junior archivist with ink-stained fingers and a talent for being unnoticed in rooms where important things were discussed, and he had found the Salt Covenant fragment misfiled among tax ledgers from the reign of a dead king. He had read it four times that night by candle stub, and by dawn he understood two things with perfect clarity: that the covenant could be remade, and that everyone who knew it was content to let it stay broken.

Content. That was the word that had curdled in him ever since. Kings and chancellors and bishops, generation upon generation, all of them *content*—content to lose a village here, a harvest there, to mark the waterline creeping up the church walls and call it God's weather. Content to leave the drowned to rot in the dark and the living to drown by inches, so long as no one had to reach into the water and take hold of anything.

Ives reached into water. That was the whole of him, distilled. Everything else—edicts, hunts, burned witches, spent captains—was reach.

He did not think of himself as cruel. Cruelty was waste, and he wasted nothing. The hedge-witch had been necessary; fear moved populations faster than proclamation. Maro the fence had been useful and would remain useful until he wasn't. Even the girl—even the seal-bearer—he bore no malice toward. She was a key. Keys were not tortured; keys were *used*, and then hung by the door for the next time the house needed opening. If she emerged from the rite diminished, hollowed, a woman with no past—well. The texts were ambiguous on what became of the seal-bearer, and ambiguity, in Ives's experience, always resolved in favor of whoever held the pen.

He finished the brandy and returned to the window. Below, in the square, the snow was filling the scorch-mark where the witch had burned, erasing it inch by inch, so that by morning the stones would look innocent again. That was the thing about snow, Ives thought. It kept no memory at all.

A knock—his night clerk, apologetic, bearing a dispatch tube with the lake garrison's seal. Ives broke it open and read by candlelight: riders sighted on the high snowfield road, signs of a skirmish, blessed iron expended, wounded withdrawn. The captain's party had engaged the thief and lost her. Again.

Ives read it twice, and then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a dry sound, like paper tearing.

"Run, then," he murmured to the snow, to the dark lake far to the north, to the girl carrying six pieces of a broken world inside her head. "Run all the way to the origin-stone. Gather your seven. Make the rite ready. Do all the difficult work, child."

He touched the black glass, and the thing inside it pressed back against his palm, cool and eager.

"Because the last hand on the seal will be mine."

Far to the north, under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, the lakes began, very quietly, to rise.
