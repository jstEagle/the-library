## 14. Sinking the Fifth

The lake took them at moonrise, where the reeds grew thin and the water lay black as poured ink.

"Breathing will be the least of your problems," Sef said. He stood at the water's edge, his scales catching the light in dull copper flashes, his courtier's coat of woven kelp stirring in a wind that did not touch the trees. "But it is a problem. Hold this." He pressed something into her palm—a pearl the size of an egg, warm despite the night's cold, thrumming against her skin like a second heartbeat.

Ada turned it over. "What is it?"

"A gill-pearl. Bite down when I tell you, and not before. It will let you breathe water for the space of six bells. After that, you drown like anything else that came from land." His dark eyes, lidless and patient, studied her face. "You are afraid."

"I'm standing at the edge of a lake at midnight about to walk into a drowned kingdom," Ada said. "I'd be worried if I weren't."

"Fear is honest. The court will value that." He waded forward without waiting, and the water closed over his shoulders as gently as a hand drawing him home.

Ada looked back once—at the pines, at the smuggler road she knew so well, at the whole solid world of dirt and stone and firelight—and then followed him under.

* * *

The descent was nothing like drowning.

That was the first thing she understood, and it unsettled her more than terror would have. The water accepted her. It parted around her body with a kind of courtesy, and the cold was not cold but a pressure, a vast attention pressing in on every side, as if the entire weight of Lake Vess had turned to look at her. Light came from below—not sunlight strained through fathoms but a pale gold luminescence rising from somewhere deep, growing brighter as they sank past shelves of rock hung with white flowering weed.

She had stolen memories of drowning before. A fisherman who had gone under off the Vess piers as a boy and been pulled out half-dead; she had lifted the memory from him for three coppers' worth of salt and kept none of it herself, per her rules. In his memory the drowning had been all panic and burning lungs, the world narrowing to a screaming point.

This was the opposite. The world widened.

Fish moved past her in slow constellations. She saw the skeleton of a boat, ancient beyond reckoning, its timbers soft with rot, a forest growing through its hull. She saw a road—an actual road, paved stones running down into the dark, following the contour of the drowned valley—and understood that Lake Vess had never been a lake at all until the waters rose. It had been a border country. It had been a meeting place.

"The Vale of Mereth," Sef said, and his voice reached her through the water as if air were the stranger medium, sound traveling to her ears clean and resonant. "Before the breaking. Your people called it the lowlands afterward, when the water receded far enough to farm. Then the thaw comes, year by year, and the water takes a little more back. Every spring we rise. Every spring your farmers pray it stops." He glanced at her, and there was no cruelty in it, only old tired arithmetic. "It will not stop. Not unless the covenant binds again."

"How much time?"

"Before the flood swallows Greywater Ford? Before it climbs the river valleys to Thornfell itself?" He counted silently, lips moving over small sharp teeth. "Three years. Perhaps four. The king is dying, and the realm forgets how to hold its shape when he goes."

They passed a bell tower, still standing, its bronze bell furred green with growth. They passed walls. Ada realized with a lurch that the pale gold light below was not some natural phenomenon—it was lamplight. Thousands of lamps, burning underwater in windows, in streets, in gardens where pale coral grew in careful rows.

People lived here. Had always lived here. While the crown above burned hedge-witches and dragged memory-wielders through market squares in chains, an entire civilization had waited beneath the water, patient as sediment.

"They know I'm coming," Ada said.

"They have known for five years," Sef said. "Since the night Hollowmere burned. Since the seal cracked and the first sigil fled into a child's memory to hide." He stopped swimming and hung in the water before her, and up close she could see the wear on him—the frayed edges of his fins, the silvering along his jaw like frost on bronze. "You should understand what waits above the throne. The court has argued for five years about what to do with you. Some want you honored. Some want you opened like an oyster, sigils prised out one by one, consequences damned. And some—" his voice dropped, though there was no one to hear, "—some remember what the covenant was *for*, and fear that anyone holding seven sigils might choose a side instead of a balance."

"And which are you?"

"I am the envoy," Sef said. "I argue for the covenant. It is my whole office and nearly the last thing I love." He turned and swam on. "Come. The king receives you."

* * *

The Sunken Throne sat in a hall that had once, Ada thought, been a temple—or perhaps a marketplace, or perhaps both; the drowned built few distinctions she could recognize. Pillars marched away into gloom on either side, each carved with paired figures: human shapes and drowned shapes, hand in hand, their faces worn smooth by five hundred years of water. Above the throne itself, a frieze depicted two crowns being exchanged—one of iron, one of pearl—between two seated figures whose features had also eroded into anonymity.

Between them, in the carving, a third figure stood smaller, holding both crowns at once. The seal-bearer. Even in stone, even eroded, Ada felt the figure watching her.

The throne was coral grown deliberate and vast, white branches rising into the shape of a seat, and upon it—

She had expected a monster. Half-salmon, like Sef, perhaps, or something older and stranger, some leviathan wearing the trappings of kingship. Instead the King Below was small. Shrunken. A drowned man of immense age, his scales gone grey and translucent as sea glass, his finery hanging on him like rigging on a wrecked ship. His eyes were the color of deep water and just as hard to see into, and they were fixed on her from the moment she entered, unblinking.

Around the throne, the court assembled in silence: dozens of them, courtiers and guards, their forms varying from nearly human to wholly fish, dressed in the grave splendor of a people who had dressed for a coronation and then waited five centuries for it. None spoke. The only sound was the hall itself—the faint groan of water moving through stone, the tick and chime of cooling heat somewhere in the depths.

"Seal-bearer." The king's voice was dry, like weed shifting in current. "You come armed."

Ada's hand had drifted, without her deciding it, toward the satchel at her hip, where four sigils now rode together in their wrapping of oilcloth, warm as coals. She made herself lower it. "Habit."

"Honesty," the king said, "is rarer. Continue in it." He gestured with a hand that trembled slightly, whether from age or fury she couldn't tell. "Four sigils. You hold four of the seven. Do you know what my courtiers said when word came that a thief carried them? That it was fitting. That the covenant broke through theft, and through theft it would mend. They laughed, seal-bearer. We have little enough to laugh at down here."

"I didn't break it," Ada said. "I was a child."

"You were the instrument. The distinction comforts no one lying under the floodline." He leaned forward, and the movement cost him; a courtier started forward and was waved back. "But I did not bring you before my court to quarrel about the past. I brought you because the fifth sigil rests beneath my seat, in the root of this throne, and I cannot reach it. Only the seal-bearer can take it. So take it, and go, and bind the pact, and let me die knowing the water will not rise to drown everything my line preserved."

Ada hesitated. There was something wrong in the room, some note held too long. She had spent her life reading rooms—waystations, markets, back-alley negotiations—and every instinct told her this audience had not yet shown her its true business.

"You're dying," she said carefully. "Sef said so."

"I have been dying since the night the covenant broke. Five hundred years of dying, stretched thin as lamplight oil. My death is not news. It is geography." He smiled, and his teeth were small and needled and sad. "But you are clever, thief. You feel the shape of the trap before you step in it. Good. Then let us speak plainly, you and I, courtier to courtier—for that is what you are, whatever you call yourself. You trade in memory. So do we."

He raised one translucent hand, and the court stirred like weeds in current.

"Here is my offer. Take the fifth sigil. Bind the covenant—but bind it *as we would bind it*. Weight the balance toward the water. Let the lowlands flood as they were always meant to flood; let the border return to what it was before your kingdoms stole it. In exchange, I offer you what no crown above can give." His eyes glittered. "A place. A title. Immortality of a kind—we endure down here, seal-bearer, long past the span of your brief burning lives. And power worthy of the name: the memory of the whole lake. Every secret sunk in Vess since the world was young. Every grief ever drowned. You would be able to *take* them, all of them, at a touch. No more selling scraps in waystations for coppers. You would be the richest thief who ever lived, and the safest, beneath waters no inquisitor can ride into."

The silence after was absolute. Somewhere in the court, scales whispered against scales.

Ada found, to her surprise, that she wasn't tempted the way she'd expected to be. The power was real—the offer was real, that was the horror of it—but her mind went instead to smaller things. Maro's ledger. Her mother's lullaby, sold to a blind keeper for a scrap of direction. Emeric's face at the fireline, lit orange by burning scrub, hating himself as he let her walk away.

She had spent five years learning exactly what it cost to trade pieces of yourself. This king was offering to make her rich in the same currency.

"You're asking me to choose," she said slowly. "Not to restore the balance. To tip it."

"I am asking you to correct it," the king said, and for the first time something moved behind his eyes that was not age—something hot and very old. "Your histories say the covenant was equal. Your histories are written by the winners of a peace. Ask the flooded farms. Ask the villages already lost these last hundred years, the ones your crown calls 'reclaimed marshland.' The water was promised a border. The water has received a shrinking puddle. I have watched my realm starve on the margins of your prosperity for five centuries while your kings grew fat on land that was never lawfully theirs." He drew breath—a strange sight, gills flaring. "I do not ask you for conquest. I ask for the terms as they were written. Is that so monstrous?"

"It's monstrous because it's reasonable," Ada said quietly. "If you'd asked for blood I could refuse you clean."

Something like respect crossed the king's ruined face. Behind him, among the courtiers, she saw Sef close his eyes briefly—as if a hope had died, or been confirmed.

"Take the sigil," the king said at last. "It is yours by right regardless of your answer. I am not so poor a king as to ransom my own realm's mending." He gestured downward, and the coral throne seemed to shift, branches parting like fingers opening. "Reach beneath the seat. And then answer me, seal-bearer. Water or balance. Riches and years, or poverty and the gratitude of no one. Choose."

* * *

Ada knelt at the base of the throne and put her hand into the hollow between the coral roots.

The sigil met her fingers like a live coal wrapped in silk, and she pulled it free—and with it came the memory, because the sigils never came alone. It crashed into her the way they all did, total and immediate:

*A market day in the Vale of Mereth, five hundred years gone. Humans and drowned folk trading across a border-stone, salmon-finned children darting between the legs of farmers, a drowned woman laughing as she haggles over apples, a human smith weighing iron against pearls. Equal. Easy. Two peoples who had learned each other's names. And above it all, faint as a watermark, the sense of the covenant holding—two crowns, one balance, the third figure walking between.*

*Then the memory turned, as the sigil-memories always turned, toward its wound: soldiers on the ridge at dusk. Iron banners. A herald reading terms no one had agreed to. The drowned traders driven back into the water at spearpoint, the border-stone toppled, the first dam raised across the river mouth—and the slow, patient, terrible beginning of the theft.*

Ada gasped and came back to herself kneeling on the temple floor, the fifth sigil cupped in both hands, its light bleeding through her fingers. The court watched her in silence. They had all seen what she saw. It was their founding wound; she had merely borrowed it.

"Now you know," the king said softly. "Now you know why the water rises. Not malice, seal-bearer. Arithmetic. We take back what was taken, grain by grain, spring by spring." He leaned forward on his coral throne, and his voice filled the hall like pressure filling the deep. "So. You have seen the truth of the breaking. Will you mend it truly—or mend it as your crown would wish, with the theft left intact and only the leaking stopped? Power and permanence if you stand with us. Or go back up into the thin air and be hunted, and hated, and forgotten, and leave the terms unjust forever."

Ada rose. The sigils in her satchel had begun to hum—all five now, harmonizing against her hip like struck glass. She thought of the carved figure between the two thrones, holding both crowns, belonging to neither side. She thought of Emeric, and of every rule she'd ever kept: steal grief, sell it, keep none of it, owe no one.

She owed no one. That had been the whole architecture of her survival.

And it was exactly the architecture a thief would build, if she were afraid to be the person the stone carvers had remembered.

"Your offer is generous," Ada said, "and it is a bribe, and I've taken enough bribes in my life to know one when it's wearing a crown." She lifted her chin. "I'll bind the covenant as it was written—both crowns, equal terms. The border restored, the water given its due, the lowlands returned where the old terms say they belong. But I won't weight it for you any more than I'd weight it for Ives. Balance isn't a prize. It's the only thing that keeps either of you honest."

For a long moment the hall was silent enough that she heard her own heart in the water.

Then the King Below began to laugh—a dry, rustling sound, like tide withdrawing over shingle—and it went on until it bent him double against his courtier's arm, until tears of clean lake-water ran from those deep hard eyes.

"Ohh," he wheezed. "Oh, you are your mother's daughter. She refused me too. Word for word, almost—the same stubborn arithmetic." He wiped his eyes and straightened, and for a moment, beneath the ruin of age, Ada glimpsed the king he must have been: vast and bright and terrible as open water in a storm. "Very well, seal-bearer. Balance. May both our realms survive your integrity." He waved a translucent hand, suddenly, brutally tired. "Sef. See her to the shore. And seal-bearer—"

She paused at the edge of the light.

"The sixth sigil," the king said, and all the laughter had drained out of his voice, leaving something that sounded almost like pity. "You should know what you're diving for, before you find it. It is not hidden in a chapel or a snowfield or a madman's keeping. It is the first memory you ever stole. The one you took from yourself as a child, the night the fires burned. When you open it, seal-bearer—and you must open it—you will finally learn who lit the pyre at Hollowmere." His eyes held hers, ancient and sorrowful. "And why the crown has been hunting your kind ever since."

* * *

They rose toward the surface in silence, the drowned city's thousand lamps sinking away beneath them like stars setting in reverse. Ada clutched the fifth sigil against her chest and felt it beating there, in time with her heart, in time with the other four.

At the shallows, where the black mirror of the lake broke into silver ripples, Sef stopped and looked at her.

"You refused him," he said. "No one refuses the King Below."

"He offered me everything I've spent my life pretending I don't want."

"Yes." Something moved across the envoy's worn face—not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "That is why the covenant chose a thief. Only someone who knows the exact price of memory could be trusted with all of ours." He inclined his head, courtly and final. "Go ashore, seal-bearer. And when you sleep tonight, do not open the sixth memory. Sleep first. Eat, if you can. Because after Hollowmere, I do not think you will ever be hungry for anything again."

Ada waded out of the lake into the cold night air, dripping, lighter by one sigil and heavier by one truth, and behind her the water settled smooth and dark as a closed eye.

Above the tree line, far to the south, she could see the glow of torches moving along the mountain road. Riders. Many of them, riding hard through the dark toward Lake Vess.

Someone had told the crown where she was going.
