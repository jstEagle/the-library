## 15. What Ada Stole From Herself

The sixth sigil was not hidden in a chapel or a shrine or the hands of a madman begging to be unburdened. It was hidden in a hollow birch on the northern shore of Lake Vess, three miles from the smuggler cave where Ada had slept as a child, and it was wrapped in oilcloth that had gone stiff and brown with age.

She knew it was there before Sef told her.

They had come down from the snowfields with Sef's wound bound in river-silk and salt-ash, his left arm cradled against his chest like something breakable. He had not complained once. Drowned-folk did not complain, he'd said; they grieved in advance and were spared the surprise. But when they reached the birch stand above the water and Ada stopped walking without knowing why, Sef went very still.

"You feel it," he said. Not a question.

"I feel—" She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, as if the ache lived behind the bone. "Like a hook. Like something in me is pulling toward this tree."

"Then it knows you." Sef lowered himself onto a stone at the water's edge, folding his salmon-silver tail beneath him with the care of a courtier arranging robes. "The sigils were scattered into memories, seal-bearer, but not into strangers' memories. Into *yours*. The covenant chose its hiding places well. Who would think to search inside a thief?"

Ada stood before the birch. Its white bark had been scored long ago by small fingers—or by one small finger, over and over, tracing the same shape until it became a groove: a spiral, broken at the top, the mark her mother had drawn in flour on the kitchen table, in ash on the hearthstone, in the dirt of the garden rows. Ada had thought it a game. A nonsense pattern. She had spent five years stealing other people's pasts without ever once wondering why she could not find her own.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice weather—distantly, as something happening to someone else.

"The cache," she said. "I hid things here. When I was young. I don't remember doing it."

"You wouldn't."

"No," she agreed quietly. "I wouldn't."

The hollow was low in the trunk, behind a flap of bark she pried loose with a knife she didn't remember buying. Inside: a child's hoard. A clay whistle cracked down one side. Seven copper coins worn smooth. And a bundle of oilcloth, tied with string, containing nothing at all—or so it seemed, until her fingertips brushed the cloth and the world tilted sideways like a boat shipping water.

She had stolen thousands of memories. She knew their texture: grief came heavy and slow, like wet wool; joy came quick and bright and slipped through the fingers; shame came sharp-edged and cold. Whatever slept in the oilcloth had none of these textures. It was older than texture. It was the thing beneath all of them, the bedrock her whole life had been built on without her knowing.

"Ada." Sef's voice came from far away, from under the water, from another life. "When you open it, I cannot follow. This memory is warded against drowned-folk. Against everything but its thief."

"Then wait for me." She heard her own voice as if through glass. "Don't let me walk into the lake."

"That," said Sef softly, "is the only vow I have ever made gladly."

* * *

She sat with her back against the birch and unwound the string.

There was no flash of light. No sound. The sixth sigil did not blaze out of the cloth like the others had—it simply *opened*, the way a door opens from the inside, and Ada fell through her own eyes into the past.

* * *

Rain. First, always, rain—the memory began with rain hammering the thatch of a cottage at the edge of Hollowmere, and a woman's voice singing low over a boiling pot. Ada was seven years old. She knew it the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and without evidence. Her hands were small. Her sleeves were too long. The world smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and rosemary, and every detail arrived with an unbearable clarity, because seven-year-olds do not yet know to stop paying attention.

Her mother was stirring the pot. Maren Nystrom had iron-grey hair braided crownwise and a scar across her knuckles from a dog bite years before Ada was born, and she sang songs in no language Ada knew—words that tasted of brine and deep water—and Ada had always assumed they were just songs.

"Mama," said little Ada, "why do we live so far from everyone?"

"Because some doors are better kept shut," her mother said, "and we are the hinges."

"That's not an answer."

Her mother laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and crossed the room, and lifted Ada onto her hip as if she weighed nothing, though Ada remembered being heavy, remembered her mother's back troubling her that winter. Memory, it turned out, lied about weight. Memory lied about almost everything except love.

"Do you know what I am, little fish?" her mother asked, carrying her to the window. Rain crawled down the glass. Beyond it, the dark shoulder of Lake Vess lay half a mile off, black and patient.

"A witch. Old Tam says you're a witch."

"Old Tam says that about any woman who can read." Her mother tapped Ada's chest, right over the sternum, right where the ache lived now. "I am a keeper. There used to be many of us—one for each door between the dry world and the wet. We kept the Salt Covenant. Do you know what a covenant is?"

"A promise."

"More than a promise. A promise stitched into the world so it can't be unpicked. Long ago, before your grandmother's grandmother, men and the folk beneath the water made a bargain. They would keep to the deep; we would keep to the land. Neither would take what belonged to the other. In exchange, the lakes would stay in their beds and the rivers in their channels, and the drowned kingdom would sleep, and dream kindly of us." Her mother's voice had gone quiet, careful, the voice she used near sleeping animals. "And I hold my end of the stitch. That's all a keeper does. Holds the thread."

"What happens if the thread lets go?"

Her mother was silent for so long that Ada wriggled to be put down. When the answer came, it came strangely—not spoken, but *given*, pressed gently into Ada's mind like a coin into a child's palm, the way her mother sometimes shared pictures instead of words:

A wall of water taller than the church spire. Fields turned to brown sea. Cattle swimming. A bell ringing underwater, ringing and ringing, and hands—many hands, grey-green and webbed—rising through the flooded streets not in malice but in terrible, patient duty, gathering what the covenant's breaking made theirs to gather.

Little Ada cried. Big Ada, sitting against the birch thirty years away, felt the tears arrive on her face like visitors she had been expecting all along.

"Hush, hush." Her mother set her down and crouched to her level, and her eyes were frightened in a way she was trying very hard not to show. "That is why we keep it, little fish. So that day never comes. And you must never tell anyone what I've shown you. Never. Not even in play. There are men who collect such knowledge the way magpies collect silver."

"Who?"

"Men with clean boots," her mother said, and smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ada had ever seen, and she was still seeing it when the boots arrived.

* * *

They came on the third morning after, when the rain had stopped and frost silvered the garden rows. Ada woke to voices in the yard—too many voices, and none of them friendly—and climbed onto the window bench in her nightdress to look.

Six men in the crown's livery. Grey coats, white sashes, and a seventh man in black with clean boots and a chain of office, holding a document sealed with red wax. The Chancellor's men. Ada didn't know the word *inquisitor* yet. She would learn it later, learning it forever, the way you learn the name of the disease that killed someone.

Maren Nystrom met them in the yard with her arms bare despite the frost, and Ada watched her mother's mouth move—calm, reasonable, a keeper negotiating the way keepers must have negotiated for centuries—and saw the man in black shake his head once, like a man declining a final meal.

They did not read the charges aloud. Later, Ada would understand that there were no charges, properly speaking. There was a list, and a name on it, and orders that the name be removed. The crown had decided, in some committee Ada would never see, that the Salt Covenant was a superstition worth extinguishing—that the keepers were relics, that the drowned kingdom was myth, and that burning the list would burn the debt along with it. You could not default on a contract you declared fictional.

Her mother looked at the window once. Only once. Their eyes met through the frost-feathered glass, and Maren Nystrom did not mouth *run* or *hide* or *I love you*. She drew one finger through the air: the broken spiral. *Hold the thread.* Then she turned back to the men with clean boots, and lifted her chin, and gave them nothing else.

They took her to Hollowmere square. They built the pyre in front of the granary because the granary's stone walls would keep the fire from spreading, which was the sort of practical mercy committees approve. Half the village was made to watch. Ada got there late—she had run the half-mile from the cottage with her breath tearing, and arrived to see flames already climbing—and she stood at the edge of the crowd between the legs of strangers, and her mother found her in the smoke and did not look away.

That was the part Ada had spent thirty years not remembering. Not the fire. The looking. Her mother held her eyes through all of it, steady as a star, so that the last thing either of them carried would not be pain but recognition. *I see you. Hold the thread.*

And then the granary roof caught, though the wind was wrong for it, though the fire should not have reached it—and the crown's men shouted, and buckets came, and the village's whole winter store went up in a column of black smoke while Maren Nystrom burned, and somewhere in the roaring and the panic, seven-year-old Ada Nystrom did the only thing her blood knew how to do.

She stole.

Not from her mother—there was nothing left to steal from, the memory was ending, ending, ending—but from herself. With the instinct of a creature biting off its own trapped limb, she reached into her own mind and tore loose everything: the yard, the frost, the window, the spiral traced in air, the flame, the looking, all of it, the entire unbearable day, and she folded it into oilcloth and string with hands that glowed faintly silver in the smoke, and she hid it in a hollow birch, and she walked back to an empty cottage and waited to be an orphan, having forgotten in the same hour what she was orphaned from.

But a theft leaves a wound, and a wound leaves a shape, and the shape of what she'd torn out was the shape of the covenant itself. The pact her mother held had passed to her—keepers' burdens ran in the blood, that was the whole architecture of the thing—and a child who did not know she was a keeper, who had cut the knowing out of herself, held the thread of the world's oldest promise with a severed mind.

The thread slipped. That night. While she slept in her mother's bed wearing her mother's shawl, the covenant frayed where her mother's death had left it untended, and the first waters moved, and the drowned kingdom—sleeping, dreaming kindly—stirred and began to wake, and the flood that would someday eat the lowlands took its first slow breath.

Child-Ada broke the pact. Not with malice. With survival. With the small animal arithmetic of a girl who could not carry that day and remain a person.

*You caused it,* the memory said, in no words at all, in the pure grammar of truth. *Every drowned field. Every rising inch. Every year of your thieving, every grief you sold for bread—all of it downstream of the night you saved yourself by cutting out the reason.*

* * *

She came back to herself against the birch tree with her cheeks stiff with dried tears and the sun two hours lower in the sky, and the sixth sigil lay open in her cupped hands like a seed of light, humming faintly, warm as a living thing.

Sef was watching her from the waterline, and for once his courtier's mask was entirely gone. What remained was older and kinder.

"Now you know," he said.

"I caused it." Her voice came out scraped flat. "The flood. The thaw. All of it. I broke the covenant trying not to remember my mother dying."

"You were seven."

"I broke it." She closed her fist around the sigil and felt it sink into her skin like cool water into chalk. Five sigils now rode beneath her breastbone, five points of heat arranged in the broken spiral her mother had drawn, waiting for the sixth's arrival to complete a pattern that was nearly, terribly complete. One place empty. "My whole life, Sef. Every memory I ever took—I told myself I was surviving. Selling other people's pasts because I couldn't bear my own. And it was worse than that. I wasn't avoiding my guilt. I was *made* by it. The gift isn't a curse and it isn't a blessing. It's the crack the water comes through. I'm not a thief who happens to be the seal. I'm the seal that learned to steal."

"Yes," Sef said simply. "That is what it means to be a hinge. Doors are blamed for what passes through them." He dragged himself a hand's breadth closer, tail whispering over the stones. "Your mother held the thread alone for twenty years and it nearly killed her, and when she died the burden fell to a child who had no idea what she carried. You did not break the covenant, seal-bearer. You inherited a broken one. There is a difference, and the rite will know the difference, even if you cannot."

Ada looked out over Lake Vess. Evening was coming down the mountains, and the water lay dark and smooth as poured metal, and somewhere beneath it a drowned king slept on a Sunken Throne, dreaming kindly, or had dreamed kindly once, before a child's amnesia gave his court license to rise.

"One sigil left," she said. "One empty point on the spiral."

"Yes."

"And I know what fills it now, don't I? I felt it the moment the sixth settled." She pressed her hand flat over her heart, over the six burning points. "It's not a place. It's not a shrine. It's—" 

"It is a person," Sef said gently. "And I think, seal-bearer, that you have known which person since the snowfield. Since before."

The lake breathed against the shore. Far to the south, faint as a struck match, a signal fire answered another on a distant ridge—crown riders, moving in the dark, closing on a trail only one of them truly wanted to end.

Ada stood, and shouldered her pack, and turned south toward the origin-stone, toward Emeric, toward the last thing in the world she had left to lose.

"He'll have to be willing," she said. "Whatever it costs. He'll have to choose it."

"Yes," said Sef. "As will you."

"And if neither of us can?"

Sef looked at the black water, at the kingdom sleeping beneath it, at the spring thaw gathering itself in the high snows like an army assembling.

"Then learn to swim," he said, "and pray the drowned are merciful."
