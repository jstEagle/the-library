## 16. Maro's Price

The snow came down like ash over Hollowmere, and Ada walked through it carrying six sigils and no idea where the seventh waited.

They had made camp in the ribs of a dead waystation above the Vess road—Sef coiled beneath a tarp of oiled canvas to keep his scales from freezing, Ada hunched over a scrap fire no larger than a cupped hand. The sixth sigil sat against her breastbone still, warm as a swallowed coal. Her mother's face lived inside it now, and her mother's voice, and the sound the executioner's drum had made on a morning thirty years gone. She did not take it out. Some memories you carry closed, the way you carry a wound.

Sef stirred under the canvas. "You are crying again."

"I'm not."

"Your salt is in the air. It tastes of you." He slid his head out, gills flaring pink in the cold, one amber eye catching the firelight. "In the drowned court we say grief is the only fresh water a human ever gives back to the world."

"That's very poetic for a fish."

"I am a courtier," Sef said with dignity. "Poetry is load-bearing."

Ada almost laughed. Almost. Then the wind shifted and brought something else down off the pass—the iron-and-horse smell of soldiers, faint but real, and beneath it, wrongness. The riders she'd ambushed in the snowfield had been Ives's outriders. They had been *following* someone's directions.

She stood so fast the fire scattered sparks. "Maro."

* * *

Maro kept his ledger house in the crook of a river bend below Greywater Ford, behind a tannery whose stink had kept the crown's men away for twenty years. He was the fence who had taught Ada that a stolen memory could be weighed like silver: heavier if the grief was fresh, lighter if the thief flinched. He had fed her when she was seventeen and starving. He had taught her rules she still lived by—never steal from children, never sell a memory twice, never learn your own face from someone else's recollection of it.

He was also, she now understood, the man who had sold her name.

Or so the ledger had said, before she took his friendship from him as payment and left him kneeling in the snow outside Thornfell. She had carried that cold pragmatism for two hundred miles like a stone in her boot. And then the ambush in the snowfield had come anyway, precise as arithmetic, and Sef had said, *blessed iron does not find you by accident*, and Ada had lain awake all night doing the sum.

If Maro had truly sold her to Ives, he'd have been paid by now. Fences were paid fast. So why did Ives's outriders keep finding roads only Maro knew?

Because he was feeding them. Feeding them *wrong*.

"We turn around," she told Sef. "We go back down."

"The thaw is eleven days away. The origin-stone is four days north."

"Then we'll be three days late." She was already kicking snow over the fire. "Maro taught me every rule I have. I'm not going to let the crown hang him for a betrayal he didn't commit—or for one he tried to undo."

Sef watched her with that mournful courtier's patience. "You understand what this costs."

"Yes." She shouldered her pack. "It always costs. That's the whole covenant, isn't it? Everything costs."

For once, the envoy had nothing poetic to say.

* * *

They came down out of the high country at dusk on the third day, and Ada knew before they reached the tannery that she was too late.

Crows. Too many crows. And the ford road lined with the trampled snow of cavalry, and the tannery yard swept clean except for one thing: a ledger book, lying open in the slush, its pages already bloating with meltwater.

Maro's handwriting filled it—his careful merchant's script, the same script that had priced her first theft at eight coppers and a hot meal. But these pages were not prices. They were a confession, dated and signed, addressed to whoever found it.

*To the seal-bearer, if you live, and to the crown, since one of you will read this regardless.*

*I sold your name, girl. Don't pretend otherwise—I know you read my ledger, and I know you took our years as payment, and I don't blame you. A debt honestly collected. But hear the rest, because you only got half the truth.*

*Lord Chancellor Ives came to me in autumn. He didn't want your name to catch you. He wanted your name to flush you—drive you up into the mountains where the lake is, where the old stone is. He knows what you are. He has known since the granary burned at Hollowmere, because it was his men who set the torch, and he has spent thirty years making sure no one who remembered that night stayed alive long enough to say it aloud. Your mother died because she kept the covenant. You broke it because you were nine years old and trying to save her, and no child should be asked to hold a pact together with their bare hands.*

*I gave Ives true roads for months. Then I started giving him false ones. Every rider he sent chasing shadows was a rider not watching the ford, and every day you gained was a day bought with my neck. An old fence knows how to price himself at last.*

*They took me this morning. I told them the seal-bearer was crossing at Miller's Deep, three days east. It will take them two days to learn I lied and one more to admit it. Spend them well.*

*One thing more, and spend this carefully too. The seventh sigil—you've felt the gap in yourself, the shape of what's missing. It isn't in a place. I heard Ives's clerks talk, the night they thought I was deaf. The seventh sigil walks. The seventh sigil has a heartbeat. Ask yourself who was born under the wardens' star, ask yourself why a captain of the crown keeps dreaming of a lake he's never seen. You'll hate the answer. Love her anyway—that's the rite, girl. That was always the rite.*

*Debts settled. Go with the salt.*

The signature was a single M, and beneath it, in smaller script, almost shy: *Proud of you. Always was.*

Ada knelt in the slush and held the drowning book, and the crows went on arguing in the bare trees, and somewhere downriver the water moved the way water always moves, indifferent and eternal, carrying everything toward the sea.

"They hanged him?" Her voice came out strange. Flat. "A public hanging?"

"No." Sef's head emerged from the reeds at the riverbank, dripping. "I asked the water downstream. The water hears everything that touches it, even blood." He paused. "They beheaded him on the ford bridge at noon. He would not give them a last statement. He smiled instead. The soldiers found it unsettling."

Of course he smiled. Maro had spent forty years weighing other people's griefs and pricing them fairly. At the end, he had finally found an item worth everything he had, and he had gotten full value for it.

Ada opened herself—not her gift, not the stealing, just the plain human thing—and let the grief come through her like floodwater through a breached weir. She let it come without turning it into coin, without wrapping it in a sigil, without trading it or spending it or burying it. Just let it hurt. It seemed like the only honest payment left to make.

When it had passed through her, she did something she had never done with any stolen memory. She pressed her palm flat against the wet pages of the ledger, and she reached into herself, into the cache where Maro's friendship used to sit—a hollow now, a socket missing its tooth—and she poured the grief into the space where it had been. Not a sigil. Not currency. A memorial. His laugh, the smell of tanned hide and lamp oil, the eight coppers and the hot meal, the words *proud of you*. She built them into the hollow like masonry and sealed them there, hers forever, never to be sold.

Some things you do not trade. Some things you become.

She rose. Wiped her face with a sleeve already stiff with old salt. "Eleven days until the thaw. We've lost three. The origin-stone is four days north, and Ives thinks I'm at Miller's Deep, which buys us—" she did the sum Maro would have done, instantly, cleanly— "two days of grace. One, if their scouts are good."

"And the seventh sigil?" Sef asked softly.

Ada looked north, toward the high passes and the black line of the Thornfell range, and thought of Emeric Vale—of his hands, of the scar on his jaw, of the way he had stood at the fireline and let her go and hated himself for it. Of the dream he'd confessed to her once, drunk on cheap wine five years ago, of a lake calling his name in a voice like his mother's. She had laughed at him then. Filed it away as a soldier's fancy.

*The seventh sigil walks. The seventh sigil has a heartbeat.*

"He's at the stone already," she said. "Ives doesn't need to hunt me anymore. He just needs bait." She picked up the ledger, shook the water from it, and bound it shut with cord—evidence, testimony, a dead man's gift. "And I'm going to walk straight into it."

"You said courage replaces cunning," Sef observed, sliding back into the current beside her as they turned north. "I begin to understand why humans drown so often."

"Not drown," Ada said. The sigils against her breastbone had begun, very faintly, to sing—all six together now, a chord with one note missing, a chord straining toward completion. "Seal."

Behind them, far to the east, she could almost hear the false trail dying at Miller's Deep, and ahead the snowfield waited, white as salt, leading home.
