## 17. The Seventh Sigil Is a Person

The origin-stone stood in a bowl of hills where three rivers were born from the same snowmelt, and Ada felt it before she saw it—a pull behind her sternum, like a fishhook set years ago finally tightening.

She had climbed for two days out of the drowned chapel valley with six sigils riding against her ribs. They were not objects, not exactly. When she reached into the cache of stolen memories she carried—her own past, sold back to her piece by piece—the sigils came away glowing: a sliver of ash-light, a drop of frozen lake water, a hymn made visible, a key of black iron, a crown of woven reeds, and the newest, torn weeping from the mad hermit's skull, which looked like a mother's hand. Six fragments of a broken pact, humming against one another inside her chest like birds in a cage too small.

Sef walked beside her, favoring his wounded side. The blessed-iron scar had healed wrong, silvered along his flank where scales should have been, and he moved like a courtier pretending he was not in pain—which, Ada thought, was more or less what he always did.

"You feel it," he said. Not a question. His gills fluttered at his throat, tasting the air. "Even above ground, even in this thin dry air, you feel it calling."

"I've felt it since Lake Vess." She crested the last rise. "I thought it was the sigils pulling toward each other."

"And so they are. And so—" He stopped.

Below them, in the hollow where the rivers began, the origin-stone rose from a ring of dead grass. It was taller than a man, older than language, a single seam of salt-white crystal running through gray granite like a vein of frozen lightning. Snow lay on everything else in the bowl, but around the stone the earth was bare and dark, as if winter itself had agreed to keep its distance.

Ada descended alone. Sef remained at the rim; she understood without being told that whatever pact had made him, it forbade him the stone—or perhaps simply that some places demand to be met unaccompanied.

The air changed as she entered the ring of dead grass. Warmer. Older. Like stepping into a room where someone had just been speaking.

She laid her palm against the granite.

The world went white and then went deep, and the memory that took her was not hers.

*She stood on a shore that no longer existed, under a sky the color of pewter, watching two courts face each other across a line of wet sand. On one side, humans in salt-crusted robes. On the other, shapes that gleamed beneath the shallows—crowns of reed and bone, eyes like lanterns in deep water. Between them, a woman knelt at the base of the uncut stone, carving sigils with a knife of salmon-bone, and every sigil she carved she cut also into her own forearm, because a covenant written only on stone is a contract, but a covenant written on flesh is a vow.*

*The woman looked up. She had Ada's eyes.*

"Mother," Ada said aloud, to the empty bowl of hills, her voice cracking against the granite. "You were the first keeper."

The vision released her gently, the way a tide releases a shell. She staggered back, sat down hard in the dead grass, and laughed once—a short, broken sound with nothing in it of humor.

Six sigils burned beneath her ribs. Her mother's blood ran somewhere in the line that had kept this stone. Of course it did. Of course the gift had never been a curse, never an accident, never a thief's trick stumbled into by an orphan girl on the Thornfell roads. It was inheritance. It was *office*. She had spent five years selling other people's griefs for bread while wearing, all unknowing, the badge of a warden whose duty was to hold two worlds together.

"Very good," said a voice behind her. "Now the difficult part."

She turned. Sef had come down after all, standing at the edge of the bare earth, unable or unwilling to cross it. His lantern-eyes were dimmed to embers, and Ada had learned enough of drowned-court manners to know that meant shame.

"The difficult part," she repeated flatly. "Sef. I have given up my mother's lullaby. I have burned my childhood out of my own head twice over. I have watched Maro die buying me a day. What could possibly be left that you would call *difficult*?"

"There are seven sigils," Sef said. "You carry six."

"Yes."

"The seventh does not wait here. The seventh has been walking the roads of Thornfell for thirty years, wearing a soldier's coat."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the mountains.

"No," she said.

"The covenant was broken in blood and fire at Hollowmere," Sef said, and his voice took on the cadence of a formal recitation, the cadence of a court delivering an unbearable verdict as gently as law allowed. "When a pact of that age shatters, it does not scatter evenly. It seeks vessels. Your mother held most of it, and when she died, it broke along the lines of her own keeping—into memories, because that was her medium, and into you, because you were her heir. But one fragment she hid elsewhere. One fragment she bound into the bloodline of the wardens' protectors, the family sworn to guard the keepers when the keepers could not guard themselves. That family took a new name, generations ago, when the old oaths grew dangerous to speak."

Ada was already shaking her head. "Stop."

"They called themselves Vale."

"*Stop.*"

"He was born a warden," Sef said quietly. "Captain Emeric Vale carries the seventh sigil in his marrow. He has carried it since before either of you drew breath to meet. Every instinct that made him a hunter, every dream that woke him sweating, the way blessed iron warms in his hand though he cannot say why—all of it is the fragment, straining toward the rest of the covenant. Toward you."

Ada stood up. Her legs held, which surprised her. The six sigils had gone still inside her—not dormant, she realized, but *listening*, the way a room goes quiet when a name is spoken.

"That can't be right," she said. "He hunts us. He hunts *me*. His crown pays him to drag memory-wielders to the pyre."

"And yet he let you walk out of a firebreak with your throat uncut," Sef said. "And yet he rides slower than he must, and takes roads that cost him days. You thought it was love, and perhaps it was. But love and the covenant are not strangers, seal-bearer. They may even be the same animal."

She pressed both hands over her ribs, over the humming weight of six recovered pieces of a broken world. "What does the rite require? Tell me exactly. Don't dress it."

"Both bearers must stand at the stone together as the thaw begins," Sef said. "The waters rise; the pact must be remade against them. The six sigils you carry will answer to your blood. The seventh will answer to his. And then—"

"Then what?"

Sef's gills folded flat against his neck. It was, she had learned, the drowned equivalent of closing one's eyes.

"Then the stone will take its toll. A covenant is a surrender, Ada. It always was. Your mother surrendered her flesh. You will surrender something equal. But that accounting comes later, and I will not speak it twice." He lifted his chin toward the southern passes. "First there is the smaller problem. The Chancellor's riders are two valleys behind you. And the captain rides at their head—though I think he no longer wishes to catch you, and no longer knows how not to."

* * *

Emeric found the shrine first because his hands told him to.

That was the thing he had stopped saying aloud, even in his own head: for weeks now, his body had known things before he did. Blessed iron warmed against his skin until he'd taken to wrapping the hilt of his sword in wool. He dreamed in water—always water, always a woman's voice singing through it in a language he almost knew—and woke with the taste of salt in his mouth though the high passes held none. And sometimes, riding at the head of Ives's picked men, he felt a pull behind his sternum like a fishhook set years ago finally tightening, dragging him off the Chancellor's map and toward places no fugitive would sensibly go.

Today the pull led him up a frozen streambed to a shrine so old it had no name, only a ring of standing stones around a basin of black ice, and in the ice, sealed like a fly in amber, a single dried sprig of rowan tied with red thread.

He knew it. That was the horror of it. He had never seen it in his life, and he knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark.

His gloved hand rose without his leave. He tore the glove off. The moment his bare fingers touched the ice, the basin *sang*—a note below hearing, felt in teeth and marrow—and six distant points of heat flared across his chest like brands, arranged in an arc, and a seventh point flared behind his own breastbone, answering them.

"Oh," Emeric said, very softly, to the empty shrine. "Oh, no."

Because he had interrogated enough smugglers, enough hedge-witches, enough frightened old women dragged from market squares, to recognize the architecture of a truth when it assembled itself in front of him. The dreams. The warming iron. The way he had stood at the firebreak with his sword half-drawn and found himself asking questions instead of making arrests. The way he'd let her go and ridden south feeling lighter and filthier than he had since his oath.

Five years ago, Ada Nystrom had vanished from his life without a word, leaving only a cold bed and a rumor that she'd gone up into the smuggler roads. He had built his whole self around that absence—joined the inquisitors because the crown at least gave shape to loss, hunted thieves because one particular thief had taught him that people steal what they cannot bear to lose.

And all along, some piece of him had been walking toward her. Had *been* the road to her.

"She didn't run from me," he said to the ice. His breath fogged and froze. "She ran from *this*."

The ice offered no correction. In the depths of the basin, far down where the black went absolute, something shifted—a shadow with the suggestion of shoulders, of reeds woven into a crown—and was still.

Boots crunched behind him. Sergeant Hollis, young, devout, Ives's eyes among the riders. "Captain? Found anything?"

"A shrine," Emeric said. "Abandoned. Old country superstition." He stepped back from the basin and put his glove on, and marveled distantly at how steady his voice came out. "We turn north at the fork. The fugitive crossed the ridge—we'll pick up her trail on the high snowfield."

Hollis frowned. "The Chancellor's orders place her south—"

"The Chancellor's orders place her *alive*, sergeant, and the Chancellor isn't here to read the snow." He mounted, settled his hands on the pommel, and felt the seventh point of heat pulse once behind his breastbone, patient as a second heartbeat. North, it agreed. Toward the rivers' birth. Toward her.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, and could not have said whether he addressed the woman he loved, the god he apparently served, or the man he had been a month ago who still believed he knew what side he was on.

* * *

They came for the stone at dusk on the last day of the thaw, when the three rivers ran loud with meltwater and the whole bowl of hills rang like a struck bell.

Ada heard the horses before she saw them—but then, she had been waiting since noon. She stood within the ring of bare earth with six sigils burning through her shirt in faint outlines of light, and she did not run, because running was finished. Sef had slid into the largest river at moonrise to summon what help the drowned court could give, which she suspected was less than he claimed and more than she deserved.

The riders came down the slope in a wedge, torches bobbing, and at their point rode a man she had loved in another life—the life before Hollowmere, before the granary fire, before she'd carved her own childhood out of her skull to survive it. He reined in at the edge of the bare ground. So did his men. None of them, she noticed, seemed eager to cross it. Horses balked at the boundary like water at a weir.

"Ada." Her name in his mouth still worked on her like a hand on a wound. He dismounted slowly. Behind him, Sergeant Hollis and a dozen crossbows tracked her, and beyond them the torchlight showed empty saddles where prisoners would have ridden—no bait, no hostage. He had come clean. Or come desperate. With Emeric she was learning those might be the same condition.

"You feel it," she said. Not a question. She watched his face do what faces do when the unspeakable is finally spoken by someone else: relief and ruin arriving together. "Don't you. You've felt it for weeks."

"My hands knew the way here," he said hoarsely. "I found a shrine in the ice and it sang to me, and I have spent every mile since trying to decide whether I'm going mad or being *used*—"

"Both, probably." She held out her hand, palm up. Beneath her skin the six sigils glowed through flesh like coals through cloth. "There's no gentle version of this, Emeric, so I'll give you the ugly one straight. The covenant between humanity and the drowned kingdom broke the night Hollowmere burned. My mother held it. She died holding it. It shattered into seven pieces, and six of them lodged in memories I stole as a child—I've spent this whole winter digging them out of my own head at a price you wouldn't believe if I told you. And the seventh—"

"Don't," he said.

"—the seventh is in you. You were born a warden. The Vale line guarded the keepers, and when the pact broke, my mother hid its last fragment in the bloodline best able to protect it. You're not chasing me, Emeric. You never were. Some part of you has been coming home this entire time."

The torches crackled. Somewhere upslope, a horse stamped and blew. Emeric stood very still at the edge of the bare earth, and Ada watched thirty years of certainty die in his face by torchlight—the loyal officer, the crown's hound, the man who had made hunting people like her into a religion because it hurt less than loving one.

"If I step onto that ground," he said at last, "I stop being the king's man. Whatever happens at that stone, whatever rite you mean to work—you should know what you're asking. Because Ives sent me with orders to bring you alive, and Ives does not spend that kind of patience on a hedge-thief. There's something at the end of this he wants, and he means to reach it through us."

"I know."

"And you're staying anyway."

"The thaw doesn't wait for safe," Ada said. "Nothing about this waits. The lowlands flood at full melt, the drowned court rises or drowns forever, and the stone takes its toll regardless of whether we pay it willingly or it strips it out of us. I would rather choose." She swallowed. "Emeric. Both of us. Together. That's the rite. No one else can stand there. No one else ever could."

For a long moment he looked at her the way a drowning man looks at a rope—wanting it, distrusting it, knowing that grasping changes everything.

Then Sergeant Hollis raised his crossbow, and the night filled with horns.

Not the riders'. Horns from the southern pass—many horns, brazen and disciplined, the sound of a column on the march, and above them a banner Ada recognized from crier's proclamations in half the towns of Thornfell: the crowned fist of Lord Chancellor Ives himself, come at last to collect what his hunt had been designed to flush out all along.

Emeric drew his sword—not at her. At the pass. "Get behind the stone," he said.
