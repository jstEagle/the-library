## 18. Chains of Office

They took him at the ford, in the grey hour before dawn, when the mist still owned the water and his sentries were dreaming of home.

Emeric Vale woke to cold iron at his throat and the smell of tallow and wet wool. He had time for one hand on his sword hilt—time enough to know it was useless—before four men bore him down into the shallows. The river filled his ears, his mouth, his eyes, and for one absurd moment he thought of Lake Vess, of a woman who could pull memories from water like threads from a loom, and he wondered if the river would remember him after he drowned.

He did not drown. They wanted him breathing.

When they hauled him up onto the bank, coughing, wrists already ringed with iron, Lord Chancellor Ives was waiting beneath a lantern, dry-shod, immaculate in grey wool, as though he had been standing there all night simply because the night required witnessing. His face held no triumph. That was the worst of it. Triumph would have meant Emeric had been worth beating.

"Captain Vale," Ives said. "Or should we dispense with the title? The men who carried you out of that camp were yours, once. They wept about it afterward, some of them."

"They should have aimed better," Emeric rasped.

Ives gestured, and a soldier stepped forward with Emeric's own dispatch case—the one from his saddlebag, sealed with his own signet. Inside, when Ives drew out the papers, were six weeks of reports written in Emeric's hand: sightings of the thief, roads closed, villages questioned, and threaded through them all, in language he had believed was careful, a pattern of warnings. *The woman is not to be harmed.* *I will take her myself.* *No bounty is to be posted east of the ridge.*

"You led me to her," Ives said mildly, folding the pages away. "Every mercy you showed her was a trail marker. Every village you spared was a village she'd passed through. You have been the finest bloodhound the crown ever fielded, Captain, and you never once looked down to see whose hand held your leash." He turned, cloak swinging, toward the horses. "Bring him. We are going to the origin-stone."

* * *

They bound his hands in blessed iron—not the crude stuff of manacles but thin wire, wound tight, etched with script that burned like frostbite wherever it touched skin. Emeric learned quickly not to flex his fingers. He rode north in an open cart, flanked by forty crown lancers, and watched the mountains rise around him with the slow inevitability of a tide.

He knew this road. He had walked it five years ago, in another life, with a woman who laughed at the wrong moments and stole things she shouldn't have been able to touch. She had stood on the shoulder of this same pass and told him the stones here were older than the kingdom, older than the crowning of kings, and he had kissed her instead of listening, because he was twenty-six and stupid and certain that whatever came next, they would come next together.

Now the stones watched him ride past in chains.

The origin-stone itself sat in a shallow bowl of land where three valleys met—a black monolith taller than a church spire, furred with moss at its base and scarred near its crown with marks no chisel had made. Pilgrims had once come here; you could see the ghost of their road under the grass. No pilgrims came now. The crown had seen to that years ago, though Emeric had never known why until recently, and the knowing sat in him like swallowed glass.

Ives made his camp in the bowl itself, tents ringing the stone, and set his engineers to work with a diligence that turned Emeric's stomach. Scaffolds went up against the monolith. A platform rose at its base, timber and rope, built to the proportions of every gallows Emeric had ever supervised—and he had supervised many. He understood then what the stage was for, and who was meant to stand on it, and the understanding was so clean and so simple that he almost laughed.

"Bait," he said that evening, when Ives came to inspect the wire on his wrists. "You're hanging me off her own covenant-stone like a lure on a line."

"I prefer *invitation*," Ives said. He crouched so they were eye to eye, and in the firelight his face was open, almost kind, which was how Emeric knew he had stopped pretending. "You've read the old records by now—I know you have, you were in my archive without leave twice last month. You know what she carries. Six sigils. Six fragments of the oldest pact this realm ever swore, walking around inside a thief who has spent her whole life selling other people's pasts for bread money." He straightened. "The covenant can be reforged, Captain. Do you understand what that means? Not merely mended—*controlled*. Whoever stands at that stone when the rite completes holds the door between realms. Holds the water. Holds the spring thaw, the harvest rains, the drowning of cities or their sparing. Every chancellor before me has governed land. I intend to govern weather."

"She'll never let you."

"No," Ives agreed pleasantly. "Which is why she will arrive tomorrow with her sigils burning and her choices narrowed to exactly one: surrender herself and them at my stone, or watch you die in front of a thousand witnesses while she hides in the treeline being noble. People believe they will choose duty, Vale. Almost no one does. It's the one reliable law of human nature, and I have built a career on it." He paused at the edge of the firelight. "She chose you once, didn't she? Before she vanished. I've read your file too. There wasn't much left of it—someone burned your early service record rather thoroughly—but ash keeps shapes."

Emeric said nothing. The wire bit his wrists when his hands shook, so he kept them still.

* * *

They built the gallows through the night, and in the morning the crown's criers went out to the valley towns with a proclamation stamped in red wax: *the arch-traitor, the memory-thief of Thornfell, condemned and to be executed at the ancient stone at sundown, that all the realm might witness justice done upon the source of its sorrows.*

It was a lie so brazen it circled back around to genius. There had been no trial. There was no sentence. But by midday the roads into the bowl were crowded with farmers and fishwives and children on fences, drawn by the twin magnets of spectacle and fear, and every one of them a witness, and every one of them a wall between Ada Nystrom and any hope of stealth.

Emeric stood on the scaffold with the wire eating into his wrists and counted the crowd the way he'd once counted enemy banners: nine hundred, perhaps a thousand. Lancers in a double ring. Crossbows on the scaffolds' corners. Ives at the foot of the origin-stone with six of his clerks, each holding a lead-lined case that Emeric would have bet his remaining years did not contain documents.

The sun went down behind the western ridge and the light turned the color of old brass, and the crowd's murmur sank into a waiting hush, and nothing happened.

Then the hush changed quality.

Emeric felt it before he saw it—a pressure in his chest, in his teeth, in the scar-tissue of everything he remembered—as though the air itself had been tuned to a new note. At the mouth of the northern road, silhouetted against the last light, a woman walked out of the treeline with her arms bare and her hands open, and light poured off her.

Six sigils blazed above her like a constellation refusing to stay in the sky. Emeric had seen her gift work small—a stolen grief cupped in her palm like a coal, a memory traded across a table. This was not small. The sigils turned slowly around her, each one a different geometry of light—ash-grey, salt-white, deep-water blue, bone-yellow, one that hurt to look at directly, one that flickered like the last frame of a dying fire—and the crowd did not scream. The crowd went silent the way animals go silent, the ancient silence of creatures recognizing something older than their fear.

Ada Nystrom walked down the pilgrim road alone, and the light of the stolen pasts lit her face, and she was not hiding.

*Good,* Emeric thought, and hated himself, and thought it again. *Good. Come on, then.*

The lancers moved to intercept. She did not stop, did not slow, did not reach for anything that might have been a weapon. Ten paces from the first rank she spoke, and her voice—level, worn, absolutely clear—carried to the edges of the crowd as though the valley itself were passing it along.

"I'm here for the man on the scaffold. Let him down and I'll stand where you want me. I'll do it willingly, and I'll tell you why that matters more than anything your chains can force." Her eyes found Ives. "Because the rite doesn't take prisoners, Chancellor. It takes *consent*. You can drag me to that stone and nail my hands to it, and it will be exactly as useful as nailing a door shut in a house that's already burned. Ask yourself why your own archives buried the rite's requirements. Ask yourself why no crown in three hundred years has tried this."

Ives smiled the smile of a man watching a fish inspect the hook. "Captain Vale," he called up, pleasantly, "does she always negotiate this well?"

But Emeric was watching Ada, and Ada was watching the stone, and something in her face had shifted—not fear, not resolve, but recognition. She was looking at the origin-stone the way a woman looks at a door she has dreamed of a thousand times and is only now standing in front of awake. And the sigils around her had begun, very slightly, to turn faster.

"The seventh isn't here," she said softly. Almost to herself. Then louder, and there was something new in her voice, something that cracked the level calm open like ice on a spring lake: "Chancellor—you've brought everything you think you need. You've brought your army and your lead boxes and your gallows and your thousand witnesses." She lifted her chin toward the scaffold, toward Emeric, toward the wire gleaming on his wrists, and the light of six sigils caught the tears standing unashamed in her eyes.

"But he's the seventh. Isn't he? He's been the seventh all along—and you've just delivered him to the stone yourself."

For the first time since the ford, Lord Chancellor Ives stopped smiling.
