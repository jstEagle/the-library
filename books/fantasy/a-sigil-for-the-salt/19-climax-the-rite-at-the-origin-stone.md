## 19. Climax: The Rite at the Origin-Stone

The flood came up the valley like a held breath finally let go.

Ada stood on the shelf of black stone above the origin-stone and watched Lake Vess unmake its own shores. The water did not rush; it *arrived*, sliding over fields that had been dry for a hundred years, swallowing fences, swallowing the crown road, swallowing the execution scaffold with its banners and its drums. The soldiers on it scattered like ants from a kicked log. Somewhere below, chains clinked against wood—Emeric, bound to the scaffold's post, left as bait because Lord Chancellor Ives had calculated, correctly, that Ada Nystrom would walk into any trap if the price was him.

She had walked in. She had not come alone.

Six sigils circled her like a ring of small suns, burning through her coat, through her skin, through the stolen years she had carried since Hollowmere. They had stopped feeling like theft somewhere on the drowned chapel's steps. Now they felt like organs—her own, beating.

"Seal-bearer." Sef's voice came out of the rising water, wet and resonant, half-song and half-grief. The envoy surfaced beside the stone, salmon-scaled shoulders gleaming, courtly robes streaming with weed. His dark eyes were fixed on the floodline. "The thaw is faster than I feared. The king's court wakes whether we are ready or not. Begin."

"The seventh sigil," Ada said. Her voice sounded strange to her—too calm, too far away, a voice belonging to someone who had already given everything away. "It's in him."

"I know what lives inside your captain." Sef did not look at Emeric. "I have known since the lake first tasted his bloodline in the water he drank at Greywater Ford. A warden-line, old as the covenant itself, bred into forgetting. The rite will ask him for the only thing he has left worth giving."

Below them, on the drowning scaffold, Captain Emeric Vale lifted his head. Water reached his knees. His uniform was torn at the shoulder where the chains had been wrenched; his face was bruised, one eye swollen nearly shut, and still he found her across all that grey distance as easily as if the world had been built with only two points in it.

"Astrid," he called—her false name, the name she'd worn when they loved each other, the name only he still used. "Whatever you're about to do—do it fast. Ives isn't done."

He wasn't. Lord Chancellor Ives stood on the high ridge above the valley with six riders around him, dry above a drowning world, and even at this distance Ada could see the hunger in the set of his shoulders. He had not come to stop the flood. He had come to catch it.

* * *

She went down to the origin-stone through waist-deep water.

The stone rose out of the flooded meadow like a broken tooth, taller than two men, carved all over with script no human hand had cut—the drowned kingdom's first law, written before there was salt in the sea. Where the water touched it, the carvings glowed a faint corpse-light green, and Ada could feel them through her boots, humming, waiting, patient as only very old things are patient.

She pressed her palm flat against the stone.

The world opened.

Not like a memory this time. Like a wound being looked at honestly for the first time. She saw the covenant whole: two realms, human and drowned, bound by an exchange—memory for mercy, grief for grace. The humans would remember their dead less painfully. The drowned would drink the sorrow and be fed by it, sustained by the sadness of the shore-people the way the sun feeds a field. It had been a beautiful thing. It had been working for four hundred years.

And then a child had broken it, trying to steal back her mother's death.

*Hollowmere,* the stone said, in the voice the lake used, which was also the voice under all lakes. *Ash on her hands. Ash on all our hands. Begin, seal-bearer. Six are yours. Give them.*

Ada gave them.

She opened herself the way she had once opened merchants and smugglers and grieving fathers—but inward, and all the way down. The granary fire came first, roaring up out of her chest, and she let it go. Then her mother's lullaby, sold at the tollhouse, bought back by the stone's grace and surrendered again. The frozen shrine. The drowned chapel's bell. The mad hermit's begging, blessed relief. Maro's friendship, Maro's last memory, Maro's stupid crooked smile. Each sigil tore free of her and flew, trailing light like a comet losing its tail, and struck the origin-stone and *sank*, and where each one sank the carving beneath flared white-hot and a line of the ancient script filled in like ink poured into a crack.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

Water climbed her ribs. She didn't care. Six.

The sixth sigil was the worst—it was the memory of her mother's execution, the thing she had stolen first and kept longest, the seed of everything she was—and when it left her, Ada felt something tear loose that she suspected would never grow back. She screamed, or the flood screamed for her, and the stone drank the sound.

Six lines of script burned complete. The valley floor trembled. The drowned court, miles down beneath Lake Vess, turned over in its sleep like a giant dreaming of waking.

And then the rite went quiet, and waited, and asked.

*The seventh,* said the stone, gentle as a knife going in. *A willing surrender of love. Freely offered. Freely received. Or the covenant reforges hollow, and the hollow thing will drown you all.*

Ada turned, treading water, and looked up the slope at the scaffold.

Emeric was already moving.

* * *

Later, she would learn that he had worked the chain-link loose with a nail pulled from the scaffold's own boards during the hour he'd been left there as bait—working calmly, methodically, while the flood rose around him, because that was who Emeric Vale was: a man who prepared for the next moment no matter how bad the current one. But in the moment itself, all Ada saw was the post splitting, the chains slithering off into brown water, and Emeric swimming—swimming toward the origin-stone with strong, furious strokes, cutting through his own army's wreckage like a man returning to a fire he'd sworn to die in.

"Emeric!" She swam to meet him. "You don't know what it costs—"

"Don't I?" He caught the edge of the stone beside her, hauled himself up until they were chest-deep together, both gasping. Up close his ruined eye made her heart lurch; up close he smelled of chain-rust and river and the particular soap he'd always used, and her body remembered things her mind was about to lose forever. "Ives interrogated me for three days, Astrid. Do you know what I told him? Nothing about you. Do you know why? Because every time I tried to imagine giving you to him, something in my chest—" he pressed his fist there, hard, "—something old and stupid refused. I thought it was love. Maybe it was. Maybe it was a warden's oath written into my blood before my grandfather's grandfather was born. It doesn't matter. It's mine, and I'm spending it here."

"You'll forget me." The words came out cracked. "Everything. The Ford. The way you laughed when I—"

"Astrid." He took her face in both hands, water streaming off his wrists. Behind him the flood roared; above them Ives's riders were picking their way down the ridge, and none of it mattered at all. "I spent five years looking for a thief who vanished. I found out yesterday she was the woman I never stopped—never *stopped*. If forgetting you is the price of saving everyone, then I've gotten the better bargain, and I'll thank you not to insult me by arguing."

Above the ridge, a horn blew. Ives's voice carried thin and cold across the water: *"Take the seal alive! Kill the rest!"*

Emeric kissed her—briefly, fiercely, like sealing a letter—and stepped back into the deep water facing outward, between her and the riders, and drew the knife from his boot.

"Now," he said. "Before I lose my nerve or you lose yours. Take it."

* * *

Lord Chancellor Ives reached the origin-stone as the sixth sigil's light was fading and the seventh was kindling.

He came down the ridge on foot, robes hitched up, two guards drowned behind him already and the rest fallen back, and he was not afraid—that was the terrible thing, Ada saw it clearly as he waded toward them, he was *not afraid*, because Aldous Ives had spent forty years believing that power belonged to whoever was ruthless enough to hold it, and here at last was power lying open in a stone like coins spilled from a cut purse.

He had studied the covenant. Of course he had. The hunt had been his design—flush the last seal-bearer into the open, let her gather the sigils, let her do the impossible work, and take the finished thing from her hands at the end. Why destroy a weapon when you could wait for someone else to forge it?

"Captain Vale," he called, wading closer, hands raised in reasonable conciliation. "Step aside. You've served the crown honorably. There is a place for you in the new order—a *high* place. The Chancellor who holds the covenant holds both realms. Think of it. An end to famine. An end to war. Every mind in the kingdom, gently adjusted toward peace. Order, at last, permanent order—"

"You want to be the lock instead of the bridge," Ada said.

"I want what has *always* been owed to this office." Ives reached the stone's edge, and his composure slipped just enough to show the thing underneath, and the thing underneath was starving. "Give me the seventh sigil, girl, and I will spare you both. That is my final generosity."

"It doesn't work that way," Emeric said quietly. "You'd know that if you understood anything except taking."

Ives lunged past him—one old man's desperate grab, fingers splayed for the glowing script—and touched the origin-stone.

The stone showed him the truth.

That was all it did. That was all it needed to do. The covenant was built on exchange, and exchange requires honesty, and the stone gave Aldous Ives exactly what he had spent a lifetime refusing to look at: every lie he had ever told himself, all at once, in full. The peasants starved so his granaries could speculate. The memory-hunt was never about danger; it was about monopoly. His wife hadn't died of fever; she had died of a medicine he'd diverted to buy a rival's estate. Every kindness he'd ever performed, filed and priced. Every cruelty, justified in his own voice, played back in his own voice, forever, with nowhere left to hide—

Ives screamed.

It was not a human sound. The truth went into him the way floodwater goes into a cellar, filling every sealed room, finding every crack, and Aldous Ives—who had built himself out of secrets the way a bird builds out of stolen thread—came apart along the same seams. His guards later swore he simply knelt down into the water and kept kneeling, kept screaming, until the screaming stopped and there was nothing left to kneel but robes and rings and a chancellor's chain dissolving into the flood like sugar. The stone had not killed him. The stone had only stopped pretending he existed.

Ada felt it happen even through the rite's blaze, and felt—shamefully, humanly—nothing at all.

"SIX ARE BOUND," said the origin-stone, and the whole valley rang with it, fish and birds and soldiers and the drowned king in his sleeping hall all hearing the same words. "THE SEVENTH ASKS. WHO SURRENDERS?"

Emeric turned back to her. Water to his chin now. The knife long gone from his hand, dropped somewhere in the brown flood.

"Me," he said. "Willingly. All of it. Everything she is to me—everything I am to her. I give it freely." He smiled, and it broke her heart clean in two, because it was the smile from Greywater Ford, from the waystation loft, from five lost years ago. "Take it, Astrid. Reforge it. Be the bridge."

"And I give mine," Ada said, and her voice did not shake, and that surprised her most of all. "Every memory of Emeric Vale. Every memory of loving him. I surrender it freely, and I receive his, and let the covenant bind what was broken, and let the waters sleep."

The seventh sigil rose out of Emeric's chest like a spark leaving a bonfire—small, golden, shaped like nothing so much as a ring—and hung in the air between their faces.

He looked at her across it.

"Whoever you turn out to be," he said, "the stranger who saves the world—I hope she knows she was loved."

"I'll tell her," Ada whispered.

They touched the sigil together.

Light took the valley. Not floodlight—*dawn*-light, upward light, the whole drowned sky opening like an eye. The covenant closed its circle with a sound like a bell rung underwater and a door shutting gently in a house where someone had finally come home. Six sigils blazed white on the stone; the seventh sank home between them; the script completed itself, every line filled, every law restored; and far beneath Lake Vess the drowned court sighed, and slept, truly slept, at peace.

The water began to fall.

It drained away through the earth as fast as it had risen, rivers running backward into the lake, fields emerging dripping and steaming, the scaffold standing empty in the sudden ordinary afternoon. On the origin-stone, in the last of the light, two people stood holding each other's hands and forgetting each other in real time—names unraveling, faces softening into strangeness, five years and three months of love lifting off them like mist off a morning field.

Ada felt him go the way you feel a fever break.

She held on to one thing, one image, hoarded greedily in the final second before the rite swept it away: Emeric Vale, soaked and bleeding and radiant, looking at her with recognition and wonder and love, saying *I hope she knows she was loved.*

Then the covenant took its due, and the stranger standing on the stone knew no one at all.

* * *

Down in the shallows, a man in a ruined captain's uniform blinked water out of his eyes and looked around at a flooded valley, a glowing stone, and a woman he had never seen before in his life.

Soldiers were wading toward them through the receding water, calling his rank uncertainly. Someone was shouting about the Chancellor. None of it meant anything yet. He put a hand to his chest, where something ached—an absence shaped exactly like a person—and frowned.

The woman on the stone was watching him. Dark-haired, rain-eyed, a stranger. She looked at him the way people look at a word they can almost remember.

He straightened, out of habit older than memory, and saluted her.

She didn't salute back. She was crying, and he found—he could not have said why—that seeing it hurt worse than anything the chains had done.

"Hold the line," he heard himself say, stupidly, to no one, to everyone, to her. "It's over. We held."

The woman opened her mouth as if to answer.

But the water was whispering now, all around the stone, soft and vast and patient, whispering one name over and over into the quiet it had bought with two people's whole hearts—and neither of them yet knew whose name it was saying.
