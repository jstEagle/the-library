## 20. Love Against Duty

The water came for them like a verdict.

It rose in black sheets over the origin-stone's terrace, pouring down the mountain's throat from lakes that had forgotten how to hold their banks, and the air itself tasted of salt and old iron. Ada knelt in the flood-shallow at the stone's base, her palms flat against the rock, six sigils burning above her like a ring of small suns—ash-white, salt-blue, the green of drowned chapels, the gold of a hermit's mercy, the deep violet of the Sunken Throne, and the red one, the terrible red one, that had been her mother's lullaby once and before that her mother's blood.

Six.

The seventh hovered nowhere. It lived in the man standing between her and the rising water with his sword drawn and his crown-issued coat soaked through to the skin.

"Get up," Emeric said. "Get up and we run. There's high ground past the ridge—Ives is dead or drowning, his riders scattered, we can be over the pass before—"

"Emeric."

"—before any of this matters. Ada. *Ada.*"

She looked up at him and saw that he already knew. She could see it in the way he held himself, the way soldiers hold themselves when they have done the arithmetic of a battle and found no version of it where everyone walks away. He had broken free of Ives's chains with his own blood on the shackles. He had put his body between her and the Chancellor's last desperate lunge toward the stone. And now he stood in the floodlight of six burning sigils, reading them the way he read terrain, the way he read sieges, and arriving at the only answer left.

"The seventh sigil," he said quietly. "It's me."

"Yes."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the origin-stone spoke." Her voice cracked on it. "Since I touched the rock and it told me what the wardens were. What your bloodline was. The Vale family kept the covenant's eastern march for nine generations, Emeric. Your grandmother's grandmother stood where you're standing. The crown forgot. The stone didn't."

The water reached their knees. It was cold in the way of things that had waited five hundred years to be cold again—patient, absolute, indifferent to the small warm lives standing in its path. Somewhere below, in the drowned dark, she could feel the court of Lake Vess stirring, Sef's song threading up through the flood like a wire pulled taut. *Seal-bearer,* the water sang. *Bind or drown them all.*

"There has to be another way," Emeric said. "There always is. There's a breach in every wall, a ford in every river. Tell me the shape of the problem and I'll find the seam."

"That's what I love about you." The words fell out of her like stones out of a split sack. "That's exactly what I love about you, and it's why this is going to break my heart, because there is no seam. The rite needs seven sigils and the seventh sigil isn't a thing I can steal, Emeric. That was the whole design. The covenant-makers were memory-wielders themselves—they knew every trick I know. They built the lock so it couldn't be picked. The seventh binding requires a surrender, not a theft. A *willing* surrender. The sigil can't be taken from you. It can only be *given*—every memory you carry of me, given freely, at the moment of binding. All of them. Every one."

The wind off the flood carried the sound of the lowlands drowning—distant bells, distant screaming, the groan of bridges giving way. Greywater Ford first. Then the river towns. Then, if spring came and the thaw swelled what the autumn floods had begun, everything down to the sea.

Emeric was silent for a long time.

Then he did the most Emeric thing Ada had ever seen him do: he sheathed his sword.

"You knew," he said. "At the fireline. You knew even then, didn't you? When you showed me the crown's memory—the granary, the Chancellor's seal on the order—that some part of you was already walking toward this."

"I hoped I'd find another way."

"Liar." But there was no cruelty in it. He waded closer, and the sigils' light moved across his face, and Ada thought: *I will never see that face again after tonight, and I am memorizing it anyway, because memorizing is all thieves have.* "You're a thief, Ada Nystrom. You lie the way other people breathe. But you've never once lied to me when it mattered, so tell me now. If I give them—all of them, every memory of you, the ferry at Vessmark, the winter in the smuggler roads, your laugh, your hands, the night you vanished and the five years I spent learning to hate you for it—if I give all of that to the stone, what happens to me?"

"You live," she said. "You remember everything else. Your duty. Your name. Your dead. You'll know a woman saved the world at the origin-stone the way everyone will know it. You just won't know her." Her voice failed and she dragged it back by force. "You won't know *us*. To you I'll be a stranger. A sigil-bearer. Someone you salute."

"And you?"

"I'm the seal-bearer. The rite takes mine too." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, wet and thin. "Did you think I'd ask you to pay alone? I've spent my whole life stealing memories because I couldn't bear to keep my own. The covenant is going to cure me of that permanently. Everything I have of you goes into the stone as well. The ferry. The winter. The way you say my name when you're angry and when you're not. After tonight I won't mourn you, Emeric. I won't even know there's anything to mourn."

The water climbed their thighs. Above them the six sigils turned slowly, waiting, patient as tide.

"So that's the price of the world," Emeric said. "Two people stop loving each other."

"No." Ada shook her head, and something in her steadied—not calm, nothing like calm, but a kind of terrible clarity, the clarity she felt at the moment a memory came free under her fingers, the moment before the taking. "That's the *lie* the crown would tell about it. That love is a possession and the rite confiscates it. Listen to me. Listen, because I've stolen ten thousand memories and I know what they are. Love isn't the memory, Emeric. The memory is just where love *lived*. The rite takes the house. It doesn't take whatever built it."

"Is that true, or is that hope wearing a coat?"

"I don't know." She smiled through the water running down her face, rain and lake and tears indistinguishable. "Isn't that magnificent? For once in my life, I don't know, and I'm doing it anyway."

He crossed the last few feet between them and took her face in both hands, the way he had at the ferry landing at Vessmark five years ago, the way he had in the firebreak smoke, and Ada closed her eyes and let herself be memorized in return.

"Tell me how it works," he said against her forehead. "Exactly. I want to choose it with my eyes open."

"I stand at the stone. I open the binding. The sigils come to me and I—" she swallowed— "*give* instead of take, which is the one thing my gift has never done. And you—you have to want it. Not accept it. *Want* it. You have to hold every memory of me in your mind and open your hands. If there's one you clutch, one you hide, the binding fails and the water wins and everyone downstream dies hating us. It has to be total. It has to be free."

"And yours?"

"Mine too. Same rule." She drew back far enough to see him whole. "So don't look at me like that while you do it, because I'll cling to that look and then we'll both drown along with the lowlands."

He almost smiled. It was the worst thing she'd ever seen, that almost-smile.

"One condition," he said.

"We're past conditions."

"Humor a dying man's vanity." He took her hand and pressed it flat over his heart, over the soaked wool and the hammering beneath. "When it's done—when you're the seal and I'm a stranger—let the stone keep one thing. Not a memory. Memories are yours, that's your trade, I won't cheat you of your craft. Just this: let whatever it is that beats in here learn the shape of you. So that if you walk past me on some road, some year from now, I stop without knowing why. That's not a memory. That's not clinging. That's just... leaving the door unlatched."

Ada made a sound she would not have recognized as her own name if someone had spoken it.

"That's not how the rite works," she whispered. "There's no provision for—"

"You're the seal-bearer. Rewrite it." His thumb traced her cheekbone, deliberate, greedy, a man committing a map to memory before burning it. "You told me yourself the covenant-makers built a lock. Locks get rebuilt. Build me a door, Ada. Leave it unlatched."

The water was at their waists now, and the sigils had begun to sing—a chord without mercy in it, pure and vast, the sound of a pact remembering itself—and Ada understood that they had perhaps a minute left in which they were still two people and not yet a sacrifice.

"I love you," she said. "Not the memory of you. *You.* Whatever survives this, wherever it hides, hear me: I loved you with everything I ever stole and everything I ever was, and if the universe has one honest ledger left, it's written there."

"I know." He kissed her once, brief and fierce, tasting of salt. "I've known since the ferry. Now open the stone, thief. Let's prove the crown wrong about what love is."

* * *

She turned to the origin-stone and pressed both palms to its wet face, and the world opened.

Not gently. The stone drank the flood-light and answered with light of its own, and the six sigils descended upon her like a verdict read aloud, each one entering her the way stolen memories always had—but backward, reversed, *given*, pouring out of her instead of in. She felt Hollowmere burn behind her eyes and released it. She felt her mother's lullaby rise in her throat and let it go. The chapel-green, the hermit-gold, the throne-violet, the snowfield-red—each one passed through her like a ship through a lock, and the gates of her opened, and opened, and opened.

"Now," the stone said, in the voice of the lake, in the voice of her mother, in the voice of everything that had ever been patient. "*The seventh. Freely given. Hold nothing back.*"

And Emeric Vale, Captain of the Crown's Inquisition, traitor to his office and faithful to something older than offices, closed his eyes and opened his hands.

Ada felt it happen. That was the cruelty of it—her gift let her *feel* the surrender from the inside, the way she had felt ten thousand strangers' griefs, except this was hers being given. She felt the ferry at Vessmark slip loose from him: the rain, the rope-burn on his palm, the girl with the thief's mark who'd pickpocketed his signet ring and returned it with a kiss. She felt the winter in the smuggler roads go—shared bread, shared blanket, two liars teaching each other honesty by lamplight. She felt the firebreak, the crown's guilt poured into him like poison and mercy at once, his choice to let her walk away, his self-hatred walking beside his love down that long ash road.

Memory by memory, year by year, he unclenched.

And God help her, he did not clutch a single one. She watched for it—watched the way a thief watches hands—waited for him to hide one memory in the cellar of himself, one small theft against the rite, the look on her face or the sound of her name. He gave even that. He gave the way she had never managed to give anything in her life: completely, instantly, without bargaining.

*Love proven by its sacrifice,* the stone sang, *not its possession.*

Her turn.

She gathered every memory of Emeric Vale the way she had once gathered coins—greedily, secretly, hoarded against winters of the heart—and she held them all in her mind at once, the whole impossible treasury of him, and she opened her hands.

It was like dying. It was like being born. It was like setting down a load she had carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine. The ferry went. The winter went. The firebreak went. His voice saying her name in anger went, and in wonder, and in the small hours when names are prayers. Five years of hatred-that-was-love went. The chains, the blood on the shackles, his body between her and Ives's blade—all of it, into the stone, freely, *freely*, until she stood hollow and clean at the center of a roaring light, unable to remember what she was mourning or why her face was wet.

The seventh sigil rose between them—white, white as salt, white as a page before writing—and the stone received it, and the covenant *closed*.

The sound was not thunder. It was the opposite of thunder: a silence so complete that the flood itself stopped mid-surge, hung trembling on the mountainside, and then—remembering obedience older than crowns—turned and began, slowly, mercifully, to run back down toward the lowlands it had come from. The lakes exhaled. Far below, the drowned court sank into a sleep five centuries deferred. The bells of Greywater Ford, still ringing underwater, rang on in clear air.

Ada Nystrom knelt at the origin-stone, alive, unmade, empty-handed, and could not for the life of her remember what she had lost.

* * *

Behind her, a soldier stood up out of the shallows.

Emeric Vale surveyed the receding water, the smoking stone, the sigils' afterglow fading like stars at dawn. He remembered the hunt. He remembered the Chancellor's treachery, the chains, the fight. He remembered a woman—tall, dark-eyed, dangerous, radiant with borrowed light—walking into the trap Ives had set and turning it inside out. He remembered her kneeling at the stone while the world ended around them.

He did not remember her name.

The absence was strange—not a wound, but a scar he couldn't recall earning; a door in a wall of his own mind, unlocked, unlatched, leading nowhere he could follow. He stood in the flood-drained terrace and looked at the stranger who had saved the world, and his heart was hammering, and he could not for the life of him remember why.

She turned. Her face was wet. Her eyes, when they found his, were full of a grief so vast it had eaten its own name.

"Captain," she said, and saluted him—awkwardly, wrong-handed, like someone who had learned the gesture from watching rather than serving. "It's done. The lowlands are safe. Go home."

He hesitated. Something in him—the thing that beat behind his ribs, the thing that had learned the shape of her and refused to forget the lesson—made him stop, made him search her face for a word that wasn't there.

"Will I see you again?" he heard himself ask. And then, bewildered by his own mouth: "Why did I ask that?"

The stranger smiled. It was the saddest smile he had ever seen, though he could not have said who had taught him to recognize sadness.

"Maybe," she said. "If you leave the door unlatched."

She walked away down the mountain road, toward the valleys and the waking world, and Captain Emeric Vale stood in the ruins of the flood with his hand pressed inexplicably over his heart, watching her go, until the mist took her.

Below, far below, in the settling waters of Lake Vess, something ancient stirred in its long sleep and murmured a single word into the dark—a word the water would keep, and carry, and offer back someday, when a certain thief came walking:

*Ada.*
