## 21. The Seal-Bearer

The water came down like a curtain being drawn.

Ada stood at the origin-stone with six sigils burning in a ring around her heart—she could feel them now not as memories but as architecture, as load-bearing walls of something vast and ancient—and she watched the flood that should have drowned the valley fold backward into itself. The lake drew in its breath. Rivers ran the wrong way for one impossible minute, silver threads climbing their own banks, and then the waters settled into their old beds with a sound like a kingdom exhaling.

The seventh sigil was leaving her.

That was the only way she could think of it. Emeric's gift had passed through her hands at the moment of the rite—not taken, *given*, freely and with his eyes open—and now it moved through her like sunlight moving through glass, warming everything it touched on its way to somewhere else. She could feel it unmaking her even as it made her. Every memory it passed through, it took.

Her mother's lullaby went first. She had paid that at the Tollhouse of Whispers years ago—or hours ago; time had gone strange inside the rite—but some echo of it had lived on in the way she hummed when she was afraid, and now even that echo drained away, leaving silence where comfort used to be.

Then Hollowmere. The ash on her hands, the granary roaring, the night the covenant broke—gone, and good riddance, except that losing it felt like pulling a splinter from a wound so old the wound had grown around it. She staggered. The stone caught her. Or perhaps she caught the stone.

*Hold,* said the water, everywhere at once. *You are doing beautifully.*

"I don't know what I'm doing," Ada said, and her voice cracked apart on the words. "I don't know what I'll be."

*You will be what you have always been.* Sef stood at the edge of the flooded hollow, half-submerged, his salmon-silver scales catching the last light, his courtier's face full of an expression she had never seen on him before: awe. *Only more so.*

More memories went. Maro's laugh. The weight of his ledger in her hands. The smell of the Thornfell waystations, woodsmoke and wet wool and other people's grief. Her first theft—the merchant's daughter, the memory she'd carried like contraband until she sold it back to him at triple price because guilt had finally outweighed hunger. All of it flowing out of her and into the stone, and the stone drinking it, and through the stone the covenant knitting itself whole, seam by golden seam, between the world of air and the world of salt.

She felt herself becoming a bridge. That was the truth of it, the plain truth under all the ceremony: bridges are made of things given up. A bridge is a road that agreed to be crossed.

"Emeric," she said, because she wanted his name in her mouth while her mouth still knew it.

He was on his knees ten feet away, soaked to the bone, freed from Ives's chains by his own hands and a stolen knife and five minutes of pure desperate violence that she would never remember he was capable of. He looked at her like a man looking at the sun after a long dark—which was cruel, because in another hour he would look at her like a stranger, and worse than a stranger: like a stranger who had saved the world and whom he owed a debt he could never name.

"I'm here," he said. "I'm not going anywhere."

"You are." She laughed, and it came out broken. "We both are. That's how it works. That's how it has to work."

"Tell me what it takes." He crawled toward her across the wet stone. "Tell me, and I'll pay it. Whatever it is, I'll—"

"You already did." She reached out and touched his face, memorizing it with her fingertips since her mind could no longer be trusted. The stubble. The scar above his eyebrow from the snowfield ambush, the one she'd given him indirectly, the one she'd grieved over in a smuggler cave while Sef bled beside her. "You gave it already. When you chose to stand here instead of running. When you handed me the seventh piece of yourself without asking what I'd do with it." Her thumb traced his cheekbone. "Love isn't keeping, Emeric. I learned that too late and I'm learning it again right now, and in an hour I won't have learned anything at all."

"Ada—"

"Don't say my name." It came out sharper than she meant. "Say it now, while it means something. Say it once."

He said it. *Ada.* Just her name, nothing else, and the covenant took it gently out of her ears even as he spoke, so that she heard it the way you hear thunder from far away—a meaning arriving after its sound.

* * *

Lord Chancellor Ives had stopped screaming some time ago.

The truths had gone into him the way floodwater goes into a cellar—finding every crack, filling every hollow—and what they found there had been too much for any one man to hold. He lay crumpled at the base of the origin-stone, alive, breathing, but emptied of the careful architecture of lies he had built his career upon. The crown's role in breaking the covenant. The executions. The engineered hunt. His own face, reflected in his own ledgers, wearing an expression he had never permitted himself to see.

When the waters receded past him he stirred, and looked up at Ada with eyes that held nothing but a vast, scoured confusion, and asked her—in the voice of a child asking why the sky is blue—"Where am I? What was I?"

"Nobody important," Ada said, and meant it as mercy, and knew it was also a verdict.

Soldiers were coming down from the ridgeline—his soldiers, crown troops who had watched their chancellor walk into the flood-hollow and come out less than a man. They halted at the rim of the stone circle with their crossbows half-raised, and none of them fired, because there was nothing left to shoot at. The flood was going down. The woman at the stone was glowing faintly, like a lamp behind paper, and the lake beyond her lay flat and calm as hammered pewter.

One of them, older, grizzled, lowered his weapon and made a sign against evil. Another simply sat down in the mud, because his legs had decided the matter for him.

And Emeric Vale stood up, straightened his ruined uniform out of pure habit, and turned to face them—because whatever else was happening, he was still a captain of the crown, and someone had to stand between a mystery and thirty frightened men with loaded crossbows.

* * *

The change finished at sunset.

Ada felt it the way you feel a fever break—one moment of burning clarity, and then a strange, clean coolness, and then nothing. No dread about tomorrow. No ache about yesterday. She searched herself the way she had spent her whole life searching other people, turning pockets, prying open locks, looking for anything worth taking or keeping.

There was nothing.

Not emptiness—that was the miracle and the horror of it. Emptiness would have been simple. This was *room*. She was full of room, the way a temple is full of room, built to hold something larger than a single life. The seven sigils were gone into the covenant, yes, but their shape remained in her, the way a mold remembers what was cast in it. She was the seal now. Not a woman carrying a burden—a woman *made of* the burden, annealed and finished and set.

She could feel both realms.

Underneath her, always, patient as tide: the drowned court, sinking at last into true sleep. She felt the dying king close his eyes. She felt Sef bow his head in the deep places and begin the long work of mourning properly, the way his people mourned—slowly, musically, over generations—and she felt, threaded through the grief, something that had not been there in living memory. Peace. The covenant hummed through the lakes and rivers and the secret salt veins of the world, and it hummed *whole*.

Above her, the human realm went on being loud and hungry and forgetful, which was its nature, and she found she loved it anyway, the way you love a dog that chews your shoes. Greywater Ford would wake tomorrow and gossip about the flood. Somewhere in the mountains a blind tollhouse keeper would tell fortunes she couldn't possibly know, and charge double, and no one would mind.

A woman with no past. Known to both realms.

It sounded, in her head, in the voice of someone whose voice she could no longer recall, like a curse. It felt, in the new-made architecture of her, like a title.

* * *

They came to her at dusk—the ones who remained.

Sef rose from the shallows one last time, water streaming from his courtier's finery, and knelt on the mud before her, which no envoy of the drowned court had ever done to a creature of air. "Seal-bearer," he said. "The court sleeps. The king asked—" he hesitated, and for the first time since Lake Vess he looked uncertain, "—the king asked whether you remembered him fondly. I told him the truth. That you remembered nothing. He wept, and slept, and I believe they were the same act." He pressed something into her hand: a small scale, iridescent, warm as a living thing. "So that when you pass our waters, we will know you. You are known to us, Ada Nystrom. You will be known to us when your name has worn off the world."

"Will I—" She stopped. Started again. "Was I kind to you? In the parts I've lost?"

Sef smiled, and it transformed his mournful face entirely. "You called me a fish in a frock coat within an hour of meeting me. And then you carried me bleeding through a snowfield for two days." He slid backward into the water. "Draw your own conclusions, seal-bearer."

Then he was gone, and the lake closed over him like a book.

Maro's memory she kept—no. She checked. It was gone, along with everything else, dissolved into the covenant like salt into soup. But she found she was holding something anyway: a smooth grey stone, river-worn, that her hands apparently had picked up at some point during the rite because her hands, at least, remembered that stones were how you carried the dead when you had nothing else. She put it in her pocket. Whoever she was now, she decided, she was a person who carried stones for dead friends.

The soldiers had withdrawn to the ridgeline, spooked and muttering. The Chancellor had been carried off on a litter, docile as a lamb. And Captain Emeric Vale stood at the edge of the stone circle in the last violet light, waiting, because he was a man who saw a duty through even when he didn't understand the duty.

She walked toward him. Each step she took, she catalogued what she was losing—there was almost nothing left, just this final hour, just his face and his voice and the word *Ada* like distant thunder—but she wanted to walk through it awake rather than asleep. If she was to become a bridge, she would cross herself, consciously, every step.

He saluted when she reached him. Full formal salute, hand to brow, the gesture crisp despite the mud and the torn sleeve and the day he'd had. "Ma'am." His voice was steady. His eyes were not; they kept flicking over her face like a man reading a map of a country he'd dreamed about. "The men are saying you ended the flood. That you... I don't know what they're saying. Half of it can't be true."

"It's mostly true," Ada said.

"The Chancellor—" He grimaced. "Whatever happened to him, the crown will want a report. There will be questions. About you." He paused, and something worked behind his eyes, some instinct with no evidence to feed on, gnawing at the bars of its cage. "Do I know you? From somewhere? Before?"

"No," said Ada, and it was the truest lie she had ever told. "But you're going to keep almost knowing me. For years. It'll be a small ache, right here—" she touched her own sternum, "—that you can't explain. You'll dream of snow sometimes. You won't know why."

"That's..." He exhaled. "Madness."

"Yes." She smiled, and found the smile came easily, which surprised her; she had expected grief to be heavier than this. Maybe grief was still arriving. Maybe it traveled slowly, like weather from a far coast. "Welcome to my whole life, Captain."

He studied her a moment longer, and then—he was a decent man under the uniform, she could feel that the way she'd once been able to feel other people's memories, a warmth with no story attached—he extended his hand instead of demanding her surrender. "Whoever you are," he said, "you saved the lowlands. Saved the crown's own army, probably, if those waters had kept rising. The realm owes you."

"The realm doesn't owe me anything," Ada said. She shook his hand. His grip was warm and calloused and utterly unfamiliar, and utterly familiar, both at once, forever, amen. "But thank you for saying it."

He turned to go. Took three steps. Stopped.

"Ma'am?" He didn't turn all the way around—just his head, silhouetted against the dying light. "There's a town downriver. Greywater Ford. If you need lodging, food—people owe you there too, I expect. And I ride that way tomorrow." A beat. "If you needed an escort."

Ada looked at this stranger with his kind, haunted, empty eyes, and felt the covenant hum beneath her feet like a second heartbeat, and understood that she had just been offered, without either of them knowing it, a beginning.

"I might," she said, "at that."

He nodded once, awkward, and walked up the ridge toward his men, and the twilight took him.

* * *

Alone on the origin-stone, under the first stars, Ada did what she had done her entire life in waystations and market squares and smuggler dens.

She took inventory.

Two realms' gratitude in the bank. One scale, one stone, one set of clothes drying stiff with brine. No mother, no lullaby, no lover, no crimes, no childhood, no name that anyone alive would speak to her face. In trade: the ability to hear water thinking. A standing invitation to a sleeping kingdom. An escort to Greywater Ford, departing at dawn, from a man whose heartbeat she could apparently sense at forty paces and whose face made something ache in her that had no name and no history and no intention whatsoever of going away.

She had spent her life stealing memories. Now she owned none, and the world owed her every one she'd ever get.

Ada Nystrom—whoever that had been—laughed, once, softly, at the symmetry of it. Then she settled against the warm stone, pulled her coat tight, and watched the moon come up over a lake that lay calm as a kept promise.

Tomorrow, Greywater Ford. Tomorrow, the long roads, the grieving strangers, the slow work of learning who she was from scratch, one kindness and one mistake at a time.

Tonight, just stars, and the water whispering below her in voices she understood perfectly, telling her, over and over, in the tone of a nurse soothing a child:

*Sleep, seal-bearer. Sleep. You're known. You're known.*
