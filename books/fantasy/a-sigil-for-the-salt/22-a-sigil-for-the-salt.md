## 22. A Sigil for the Salt

The road into Thornfell had changed its name twice since the thaw.

First the crown called it the King's Way, and sent surveyors with painted stakes to mark it. Then the flood took the stakes, and the surveyors, and the lowland villages that had argued over the road's tolls for a hundred years. After that, no one named it at all. It was simply the road that ran along the drowned fields, past orchards still standing in six feet of green water, their branches heavy with apples no one would pick. The people who lived nearby called them the ghost orchards and left offerings of bread on the fences that broke the surface like ribs.

Ada Nystrom walked it in autumn, when the water had drawn back far enough to show the bones of the world again—fenceposts, doorframes, a church bell hanging crooked from a leaning tower, silent even in wind.

She did not remember any of this.

That was the first thing she noticed each morning, waking in whatever barn or shrine or smuggler's loft had taken her in: the absence where a life should be. Not an emptiness exactly. Emptiness was clean, and what she carried was not clean. It was more like the feeling of a room rearranged in the dark—the shapes were all there, the furniture of a self, but nothing sat quite where her hands expected. She knew how to read. She did not remember learning. She knew the smell of woodsmoke meant safety, though she could not have said why. She knew the taste of salt on her own lips meant *enough*, meant *stop*, meant something she had promised someone, somewhere, with the whole of herself.

She had traded away everything else. Fairly, she believed. She had been told it was fair.

She worked the roads as she went, because working was the only thing that felt like hers—not given, not returned, just chosen. And word had traveled ahead of her, the way word does, faster than any rider: there was a thief on the roads who paid nothing and took nothing. A woman who would sit by your fire and lift the worst hour of your life out of you like a splinter, and ask only for supper and a corner to sleep in.

They came to her in ones and twos at first. Then in lines.

* * *

In the market town of Greywater Ford—rebuilt higher up the hill after the floods, its criers now calling grain prices instead of bounties—a widow pressed a copper into Ada's hand and begged her to take it anyway.

"Take it," the woman said. "I can't sleep knowing I owe you."

Ada looked at the coin. It was old, worn nearly smooth, stamped with a king whose face she didn't recognize—or perhaps she did, and simply couldn't reach the knowing. She closed the widow's fingers back around it.

"You don't owe me," Ada said. "You paid already. Everyone pays already. That's rather the point of me."

She said things like that sometimes—strange, weighted sentences that arrived in her mouth fully formed, like birds flying out of an open window. She had learned to let them go without chasing them. Chasing them hurt.

The widow's grief was a small, ordinary thing: a husband lost to fever, forty years of mornings suddenly ownerless. Ada lifted it gently, the way she'd been taught—by whom, she couldn't say—and held it a moment before letting it dissolve into one of her blank sigils, where it would sleep until she found someone who could carry it better. The widow's face went slack with relief, then confused, then quietly astonished, the way they all did, touching their own cheeks as if checking for rain.

"I still love him," the woman whispered. "Don't I? I still—"

"Yes." Ada took her hands. "It's only the drowning part I take. The love stays. Love is heavier than water. It sinks last."

She didn't know where the sentence came from either. She only knew it was true the way her body was true, deeper than memory.

By evening there were eleven people waiting outside the inn, and Ada worked until the candles guttered. A farmer who had watched his son's cart go under in the spring floods. A girl who could not stop hearing the sound the ice made when it took her brother. An old man, a soldier, who flinched at drumbeats and wanted only the battle gone, not the war, not the friends—he was very clear about that—only the battle.

"Careful," Ada told him, her fingers at his temple. "If I take the battle, some of the friends may blur at the edges. Grief doesn't come alone. It brings company."

He thought about it, jaw working, eyes wet in the firelight.

"No," he said finally. "Leave it. Leave all of it. They died and I lived and that's mine to carry." He gripped her wrist, sudden and fierce. "But take the drums. Just the drums."

So she took the drums. Only the drums. She was very good at precision now; she had the sense of having once been very bad at it, all appetite and accident, a child prying memories loose like loose teeth. Someone had taught her better. She wished, distantly, the way you wish on a star you don't believe in, that she could thank whoever it was.

* * *

*

The road east followed the lake.

She felt Lake Vess before she saw it—a change in the air's weight, a hush under the wind, the particular silence of deep water paying attention. By then she had walked past enough shrines and standing stones and half-drowned chapels to know that the land remembered her, even if she couldn't remember it. Old women touched her sleeve for luck. Children ran beside her asking if she was the Salt Lady. Once, passing a burned ruin in the hills, she had stopped without deciding to stop, and stood a long time with ash-smell in her nose and grief in her throat that belonged to no one—grief with no memory attached, orphaned, crying for parents that weren't coming.

She hadn't gone in. Some doors her body refused to open, and she had learned to trust the refusal.

Now the lake lay grey and vast beneath a sky the color of unpolished silver, and Ada stood at the shore with her boots in the shingle and did not move for a long time.

There was an ache here.

Not grief—she knew grief intimately now, kept a whole shelf of other people's, sorted and labeled like preserves. This was different. This was the ache of a word on the tip of the tongue. Of reaching for a hand in the dark and finding warm air. Something in her chest leaned toward the water like a plant toward light, and she could not have said what it wanted, only that the wanting was old. Older than her work on the roads. Older, it seemed sometimes, than Ada Nystrom herself.

She knelt at the waterline.

This, too, was habit without origin: she touched two fingers to the lake, and the lake spoke.

It always spoke softly. It had never once used words she could repeat afterward—they slid out of memory like fish through fingers—but meaning came through, clear as cold. *Seal-bearer*, the water said, or sang, or remembered at her. *Bridge. Keeper of the hinge between the dry world and the drowned.* And underneath that, threaded through it, something gentler and stranger: gratitude. The lake was grateful to her. It had been grateful for months, all through the receding floods, all through the quieting of the drowned court, and it had waited at every shoreline she passed for her to come stand here and receive it.

"Thank you for what?" Ada asked aloud. Her breath fogged. "What did I do?"

The lake answered with images, because it knew she had no use for names: a stone ring on a hilltop. Waters rising like a wall. Six lights burning in a circle. Two figures—one kneeling, one standing over her with his arms spread wide against the world—

The ache flared so hard she gasped and pulled her hand from the water.

The image dissolved. She knelt there shaking, heart slamming, staring at the ripples closing over the place where her fingers had been. The vision had lasted three heartbeats and she had already lost most of it—the way you lose a dream, the way you lose—

The way you lose something that was taken from you kindly, by someone who loved you, for the sake of the whole world.

She knew that shape. She knew it the way the widow knew her drowned husband's side of the bed. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, over the ache, over the hollow, and whispered:

"What was his name?"

For a long moment the lake said nothing. Wind moved the reeds. Somewhere behind her a curlew cried, thin and rising.

Then the water spoke, and this time it used a word, one word, holding it up to her like a lamp:

*Emeric.*

The name landed in her chest and did not slide out.

Ada stayed on her knees in the shingle while the light failed, saying it over and over, tasting it, learning it the way you learn a song you somehow already know the chorus of. Emeric. It fit a shape inside her that she had never been able to name—that lean toward the east every morning, the way certain songs made her stop mid-stride, the reason she always slept facing doors, as if someone might still come through one looking for her. Emeric. A man-shaped room in her, furnished and dusted and empty, waiting.

She laughed, and it came out cracked, and then it wasn't laughing anymore. She cried at the edge of Lake Vess with no memory to cry about, weeping for someone she had never met, and it was the truest grief she had carried in a year of carrying everyone's, because it was the only one that was hers.

* * *

*

She asked the water what to do, and the water, which was old and patient and had seen empires drown, gave her the same answer it gives to everything:

*Come back. Keep coming back.*

So she built a hut.

It took her three weeks, with help from fishermen who wouldn't take payment and a carpenter whose nightmares she lifted as wages. She built it on the bluff above the shore, facing east over the water, because her hands chose the spot before she understood why and she had long ago stopped arguing with her hands. She planted a garden. She hung a lantern in the window that faced the road down from the mountains—the road that wound out of the high passes where, she was fairly sure, though she could prove nothing, she had once run from soldiers with a half-salmon courtier at her side and a granary burning in her childhood. People still came to her there, grieving pilgrims walking the new roads, and she took their worst hours freely, and the shelf of sleeping sigils in her chest grew crowded and kind.

And every morning she walked down to the water, touched two fingers to the lake, and said the name.

"Emeric."

Some mornings the water only hummed. Some mornings it showed her fragments—a uniform dark with rain; a hand catching hers on a stair; a voice saying *you never could lie to me, Nystrom* in a tone that made her whole body blush without permission. She collected the fragments the way she collected everything else, carefully, in sigils she refused to trade. Her first possessions. Her first kept things.

In spring she started leaving the lantern lit all night.

In summer, a peddler told her that a captain had come back from the wars—or from something; the stories disagreed—and taken a commission patrolling the northern roads, and that he was known for two things: he never hanged a smuggler who surrendered honest, and he had a scar on his brow shaped like a sliver of moon, and he had no memory of the five years before the flood, doctors said, or none worth the telling, and he rode like a man following a map only he couldn't see.

Ada listened to all of it standing very still, with the peddler's tea going cold in her hands.

"His name?" she asked, though her heart had already answered, hammering like a fist on a door.

"Vale," the peddler said. "Captain Vale. Emeric Vale."

The name settled into its room. Locked home like a key.

She thanked him, walked him to the road, watched him out of sight. Then she went down to the lake and stood in the shallows with the water at her knees and laughed until she wept, because it was absurd, it was unbearable, it was perfect: the world had taken everything, both of them, the whole covenant of who they had been to each other, and the world had also—she was certain of it, certain the way the tide is certain—left the road open. He was riding roads. She lived beside one. The lantern was already lit.

That night she wrote on a scrap of paper, because she trusted ink more than memory now, and tucked it into the frame above her door where travelers would see it:

*A thief lives here who steals pain and keeps nothing. If you are missing something you cannot name, knock.*

Then she added a second line, smaller, just for him, though he might never come, though he might ride past a hundred times:

*Ask for Ada. You'll know the name. Part of you will.*

She lit the lantern, and the lake below breathed slow and silver, and far off in the mountains a horse was already, unknowingly, turning south.

Down the road, toward the water. Toward the light.
