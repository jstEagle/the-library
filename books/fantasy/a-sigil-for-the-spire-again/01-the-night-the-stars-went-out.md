## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

Rafael Villalobos had three rules for her life at Thornfell, and she had kept all of them for nine years without breaking one.

Rule one: know your rounds better than your own face. Rule two: speak to no one longer than courtesy requires. Rule three: never, under any circumstance, look up.

The third rule was the hardest, because Thornfell sat high enough that the stars came down almost to the battlements, thick as spilled salt, and every soldier she'd ever served with loved to stand watch and name them. Rafael did her patrols with her chin tucked and her eyes on the road below the gate — the smugglers' road, they called it, though nothing had come down it in years but wind and the occasional nervous goat. She counted stones instead of stars. She knew which ones wobbled. She knew where the moss grew thickest and where the ward-stones hummed when you laid a palm flat against them, low and steady, like a cat that tolerated you.

The ward-stones were humming tonight. That was the first wrong thing.

She noticed it at the second hour past midnight, on her fourth circuit. The hum had changed key — dropped half a tone, the way a singer does when they're tired. She stopped at the nearest stone, a squat grey thing furred with lichen, and pressed her hand to it. The vibration stuttered under her palm like a heartbeat missing beats.

"Hm," she said aloud, because there was no one to hear, and saying things aloud made them smaller.

She finished the circuit anyway. That was rule one. You didn't break pattern just because something felt off; you completed the round, noted the anomaly, reported it in the morning in your flat, unremarkable voice that made sergeants' eyes glaze over. Nine years she'd been the most boring soldier on the Thornfell roster, and boring was armor. Boring was a life.

At the top of the fifth circuit, the wards went out.

Not faded. Not flickered. *Went out* — all at once, along the whole line of stones, the way a row of candles dies when someone opens a door. The silence was enormous. Rafael hadn't realized how much of her nine years had been lived inside that hum until it stopped, and the mountain seemed to lean closer in the quiet.

And then she looked up.

It wasn't a decision. It was older than her rules, older than her careful invisible life — some animal part of her knew what was coming before she did, and made her turn her face to the sky the way you turn toward a sound of something falling.

There were no stars.

All of them, gone. Not clouded — there were no clouds; the sky was clean black from ridge to ridge, a ceiling of polished obsidian, and not one star, not the Sisters, not the Shepherd's Lamp, not the pale scatter of the River that every child in the borderlands learned before they learned letters. The moon was gone too. She checked twice, heart slamming, because surely the moon — but no. The sky was a closed eye.

Rafael stood very still on the wall of the Thornfell gate and felt something in her chest come loose and swing free, like a door swinging on a broken hinge, and behind the door was a dark room she could not see into, and the room was *hers*, and she could not remember why it frightened her.

"Report it in the morning," she said to herself. Her voice sounded strange. Thin. "Flat voice. Boring voice."

But her hands were shaking as she lit her lamp, and she walked the sixth circuit fast, and she did not look up again.

* * *

Dawn came grey and sourceless, light without a sun, the whole world lit like the inside of an overcast pearl. Rafael had slept maybe two hours in the gatehouse chair, boots on, sword across her knees. In those two hours she dreamed of ink.

That was the second wrong thing. She never dreamed. Or rather, she woke every morning with the same blank feeling behind her eyes, like a page torn cleanly out of a book, and she'd long ago decided that was simply how she was built. But last night she dreamed of ink — black ink, a great deal of it, and the smell of hot metal and tallow candles, and a voice reading terms in a language she almost knew, and her own hand moving across something, moving with terrible confidence, and —

She woke with her own name half-formed in her mouth. *Rafael.* As if she'd been reminding someone else of it. As if she'd been signing it.

By full light, the garrison had noticed. She heard them on the walls, the younger ones, voices pitched high: *the stars, the stars, where are the stars, is it the war, is it the court, is it — * and the old sergeant Brannoc bellowing them back to their posts in a voice like a rockslide. The captain sent riders down-valley. The riders came back at midmorning white-faced: no stars anywhere. Not in the valley, not over the sea towns two days south. The whole sky, extinguished, edge to edge.

Nobody said the obvious thing. Nobody needed to. Everyone in Thornfell knew the stories, told low over cards: that the stars weren't lights at all but *eyes*, or knots, or nails — every village had its own word for it — driven into the night to pin it shut above the court beneath the mountain. And everyone knew what happened if the pins started coming out.

Rafael's job was the gate. The gate held. Whatever else failed, the wards on the smugglers' road would hold, because they had to, because she had spent nine years —

Nine years.

She tried, standing there in the gatehouse doorway, to remember her first day at Thornfell. She couldn't. She remembered the roster, the sergeant who'd trained her, the layout of the walls — all the furniture of her life, crisp as inventory. But the *first day*, the arriving, the reason she'd come to this cold end of nowhere — gone. A page torn out. She'd never once wondered about it in nine years. Now the wondering arrived all at once, crowding in like smoke through a cracked door, and she gripped the doorframe and made herself breathe.

"One thing at a time," she said. "Boring voice."

The knock came at dusk.

Three knocks, unhurried, spaced evenly — the knock of someone who expected to be let in and was merely being polite about it. Rafael took her sword off the table before she opened the door, which was rule zero, the rule beneath all rules, the one she'd apparently written for herself somewhere back before the torn-out pages.

The stranger on her step was small and weathered as a fence post, wrapped in a road-dust cloak, with a scar that ran from temple to jaw on the left side like a lightning strike frozen mid-fall. Grey eyes. Hands that stayed very still, the stillness of someone who'd learned the cost of fidgeting.

"Rafael Villalobos," the stranger said. Not a question.

"Gate's the next door down. State your business with the garrison."

"My business isn't with the garrison." The stranger lifted a box.

It was a plain thing, iron-banded wood, small enough to carry one-handed, locked with a hasp that had no keyhole. And it was *singing*. Low, barely audible, a thread of sound that Rafael felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears — and then heard, and then knew, the way you know your own footstep on your own stairs.

It was her voice. Her own voice, muffled and warped through iron and wood, humming a tune she did not know and yet somehow knew perfectly, some half-remembered lullaby in a language she almost spoke.

"Where did you get that," Rafael said. Her sword arm had not moved. Her voice came out level, flat, competent. Underneath it, everything was screaming.

"On the road down," the stranger said. "It's been singing your name since the second valley. I'm called Ivet. And you should open it soon, because the collectors are already on the mountain, and the box doesn't wait for anyone's permission."

"I don't take orders from boxes."

"No." Ivet's grey eyes were steady, and terribly sad, and familiar in a way that scraped at the torn place behind Rafael's eyes. "You never did. That was rather the problem."

The box opened itself.

No click, no creak — the hasp simply lay down flat like a sleeping animal, and the lid rose, and the singing swelled into a single clear note that was unmistakably, appallingly, *her*. And the memory came up out of the box like floodwater through a floorboard.

Candles. Tallow-smell. Hot metal. A table of black glass, and her own hand — younger, steadier, certain — dragging a knife across her palm, and blood welling black in the candlelight, and her own voice saying, calm as a woman reporting a patrol:

*"I sign. Seal the gate. Take what's owed."*

And beneath the words, faint and cold as a stone at the bottom of a well, something vast and patient turning its attention toward her, the way a mountain might notice a single falling leaf.

Rafael came back to herself on her knees on her own threshold, the lamp guttering, Ivet crouched before her holding the box, which had gone silent now — emptied, spent, a shell.

"There were twelve moon-turns left," Ivet said quietly. "When you made the bargain. There are none left now. The stars went out because they've come to collect, Rafa. They've come for your soul — and the gate comes down the moment they take it."

Rafael looked up at the starless sky, and for the first time in nine years, she remembered exactly why she never looked up.

She had sold the dark her light to keep this gate shut. And the debt had come due.
