## 2. What the Box Remembered

The box opened at moonrise, and Rafael did not touch it.

She had spent the day doing what she always did—checking the gate's anchor-stones, oiling the hinge of the great iron door that hadn't swung in nine years, eating bread and hard cheese at her post like a woman with nothing heavier on her mind than weather. Ivet had sat in the corner of the guardhouse through all of it, patient as furniture, the box on her knees. Twice Rafael caught herself watching it. Twice she made herself stop.

"You could just tell me what's in it," Rafael said, the third time.

"It doesn't work like that." Ivet turned the box over. It was small, cedar-dark, bound in dull silver, and it hummed—had hummed since dawn—a low note Rafael felt behind her sternum more than heard. "It's not mine to open. It never was. It's keyed to you."

"Nothing is keyed to me. I'm nobody."

Ivet looked at her then, and there was something unbearable in it—not pity, exactly, but the particular grief of someone watching a person mispronounce their own name.

"No," Ivet said softly. "You really aren't."

At moonrise the box stopped humming. In the silence that followed, the lock clicked open on its own, and light came out—not candlelight, not starlight, because there were no stars to borrow from, but something older, the color of memory itself. Rafael stood up without deciding to. Her legs carried her across the room. Her hands lifted the lid.

And the gate of nine years came down all at once.

* * *

She was standing in snow, and she was someone else.

Taller, or so it seemed—younger certainly, hungry in the way of a person who has been running for months. She wore a soldier's coat with the buttons wrong. Beside her stood Ivet, younger too, unscarred, holding a lantern against the dark under Thornfell mountain, and the dark was *breathing*.

"The wards are failing," young-Ivet said. "By spring they'll be ash. You know what's down there. You've seen what comes through when they thin."

"I know." Her own voice, but harder. Sharpened by things she couldn't yet name. "There's one way to seal it properly. One thing down here they'll accept as payment."

"Don't." The lantern shook. "Rafael. Don't."

But she was already walking toward the candlelight below, toward the court beneath the mountain, toward a figure waiting at the bottom of a stair carved from living rock—and she remembered, in the old self's bones, exactly what she said. She remembered signing. She remembered the knife, the blood, the weight of her own name leaving her body like heat from an open door. She remembered the Broker's face, smooth and delighted, lifting a glass reliquary in which something small and bright swam like a fish in winter water.

*Her soul. Her soul, given freely, to seal the gate for thirteen years.*

And then—snow again, a hedge-witch's crooked hut, coins counted out on a table scarred with burn marks. *"Erase me,"* the young woman said. *"All of it. Leave me the gate. Leave me the duty. Nothing else."* The witch had asked if she was certain. The witch had warned that a soul sold leaves a hollow, and hollows fill with whatever walks past.

*"Good,"* she had said. *"Let it be empty. Empty things want nothing."*

The vision closed like a book.

* * *

Rafael came back to herself on the floor of the guardhouse, and she was screaming, or she had been; her throat was raw. Ivet held her shoulders. Outside, the starless night pressed against the windows like a face.

"You knew," Rafael rasped. "You were *there*. You watched me do it and then you let me forget."

"You paid the witch to erase everyone who knew you. I bribed my way off her list." Ivet's jaw tightened. "Don't mistake that for kindness. I let you become nobody because it was easier than watching you remember."

"How many moon-turns?"

"Twelve spent. One left. The bargain comes due at the next full dark." Ivet helped her upright, hands steady, eyes anything but. "When it does, the gate opens whether you will it or not. Your soul was the lock, Rafael. And locks get collected."

Rafael sat with that. Nine years of patrols, of bread and cheese, of being careful and invisible and *empty*—and it hadn't been peace. It had been a wound dressed as a life. Grief rose in her, huge and stupid, for a self she had chosen to lose: the woman who signed in blood, who ran, who loved loudly enough to frighten people. That woman was in a glass jar under a mountain, and Rafael had spent nine years not missing her, because missing requires a shape and she'd had none.

Then the grief cooled. What remained was harder and more useful.

"Then we go down," Rafael said. "Before the collectors do. There has to be a way to end the bargain."

Ivet went very still. "There isn't. Not one anyone's survived."

"There is now. I've got one moon-turn and no soul to lose twice." She stood, took her coat from its hook—the same coat, she realized now, she'd worn to sign away everything—and buckled it on. "Get your journal. We're taking the smugglers' roads."

* * *

*From the journal of Ivet Marren, entry undated:*

*R. slept two hours by the fire, then woke and asked me questions until dawn. Not the frightened questions. The other kind. Where does the Winding Stair begin. Which toll-houses still take coin instead of blood. How many collectors does the Broker keep. I answered every one. It frightens me how quickly the old her is reassembling—quicker than the witch promised, quicker than is safe. Hollows fill fast once they're opened.*

*I have not told her the rest. That I was the one who brought her to the Broker nine years ago. That the map she followed down was drawn in my hand. When she learns it—and she will learn it, she learns everything eventually—I will deserve whatever she does. Better that than the gate opening. Better almost anything than that.*

*We entered the smugglers' roads at first dark. She walked ahead of me the whole way, and I swear the tunnels knew her. Lamps lit before we reached them. Old ward-marks on the stone flaring faintly gold at her passing, like dogs recognizing a master after years.*

*One moon-turn. Thirteen days, if we're lucky. The collectors move faster than travelers. Last night, on the road below Kettlebridge, we heard the bell they carry—three notes, very far off, very patient.*

*Rafael heard it too. She didn't ask what it was. She only said, "They can smell the hollow in me, can't they. The place where I used to be."*

*I said yes.*

*She smiled. It was the worst thing I've seen in nine years.*

* * *

They made the descent through roads that had no right to exist—stairs spiraling inside the mountain's roots, bridges over caverns where rivers ran upward, toll-gates manned by things wearing the shapes of dead friends. Rafael paid them in memories instead of coin, small ones, cheap ones: the taste of a peach, a dog's name, the color of a dress she'd owned at nineteen. Each payment left her lighter and angrier.

On the twelfth day the tunnel walls changed. Rough granite gave way to fitted black stone, and candles appeared in niches—hundreds of them, ten thousand, burning without smoke in air that did not stir. The bell they'd been hearing for days rang close now, three notes, directly ahead.

"They know we're coming," Ivet whispered.

"Good." Rafael rolled her shoulders, flexed her sword-hand, and found she wasn't afraid. Fear needed a future, and hers had narrowed to a single point of light at the bottom of a stair carved from living rock. "Saves us knocking."

Below, in the candlelit dark, the court beneath the mountain was waiting—and seated at its center, glass reliquary glinting on the table before him, was the Broker, smiling up at her as though nine years were nine minutes.

"Welcome back," he said. "We kept your seat."
