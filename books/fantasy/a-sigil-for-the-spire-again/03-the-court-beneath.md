## 3. The Court Beneath

The smugglers' road ended where all such roads end: at a door that should not have opened, opening anyway.

Rafael had counted the turns of the descent the way she'd once counted stars—by habit, by need. Two hundred and eleven steps down the bone-stair. A river that flowed uphill, black and silent, ferrying them on a barge with no poleman. A corridor of salt where Ivet stopped talking and didn't start again for an hour. And then light—not torchlight, not lanternlight, but candlelight, thousands upon thousands of candles burning in a cavern so vast the flames were just weather.

The court beneath the mountain was beautiful the way an open wound is beautiful, if you squinted and wanted very much not to see it.

Candles floated. That was the first obscenity. They drifted in slow constellations above a floor of polished black stone, and beneath each one, Rafael realized as her eyes adjusted, sat something small and glassy—a reliquary, a vial, a stoppered heart. Every candle was a soul on display, lit from within by whatever was left of the person who'd sold it.

"Nine years," Rafael said quietly. "You said you'd never been here."

Ivet's face did something complicated in the candlelight. "I said you hadn't."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ivet agreed. "It isn't." And walked ahead into the court like a woman arriving somewhere she'd been expected.

* * *

They were expected. That was the second obscenity.

A hall of black stone opened off the cavern, its ceiling lost in candle-haze, and at the far end stood a dais of stacked ledgers—actual books, thousands of them, spines cracked and titles worn to ghosts—and before the dais, waiting, stood the Broker.

Rafael had spent nine years imagining what had taken her soul. She'd pictured monsters. Horns, teeth, a voice like rockfall. What stood before her instead wore a grey clerk's coat buttoned to the throat, and had a face so ordinary it took her three full seconds to understand why her skin was crawling: it was *her* face. Not exactly. Older, tireder, smoothed out around the eyes—but hers, unmistakably, the way a signature is yours even when someone else has practiced forging it.

"You kept my face," Rafael said.

"We keep what's owed against," the Broker said. Its voice was paper-dry and pleasant. "Collateral is displayed. It encourages punctuality in our other clients." It gestured, and the candles overhead shifted, drifting into new arrangements, and Rafael understood with a lurch that they were rearranging themselves around *her*, forming patterns, forming—she knew that pattern—a sigil. The gate-sigil. The one she'd carved nine years ago with blood on her hands and no memory of doing it.

"You're punctual," the Broker said. "One moon-turn remaining. Most clients arrive with hours to spare and weeping. You arrived with twelve days and your boots still clean." Something moved behind its borrowed eyes that might have been approval. "The hedge-witch's work held well. Nine years of nothing. Do you know how rare that is? Most who erase themselves leave seams. Grief leaks through. Guilt. You paid for a clean cut, and someone—" a glance, brief and unreadable, toward Ivet— "*someone* ensured the payment was sufficient."

Rafael turned. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Ivet said, and her voice was steady in the way of someone holding a door shut against a flood, "that the box wasn't sent by accident. And that the road down—I mapped it. For you. Nine years ago, in case you ever came looking."

"You *guided* me here. Before. To this."

"I found you the Broker. Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you asked me to." Ivet's jaw tightened. "You don't remember asking. But you asked. You came to me with the wards failing and the gate cracking and you said, *find me another way, any way*, and there wasn't one, Rafa. There was only this. So I found it. And then I helped you forget it because you begged me to, because you said if you remembered what you'd paid, you'd spend every day of those nine years wanting it back, and a soul that wants itself back makes a weak anchor." Her voice finally cracked, just at the edges. "I did everything you told me to do. Everything. Including hating myself for it after."

The candles above them burned very still, listening.

Rafael looked at her—the scars, the road-worn coat, the locked box hanging now from her own hand like an accusation—and felt fury rise in her chest, huge and clarifying. Fury at Ivet, fury at the Broker wearing her face, fury at herself, at the woman nine years gone who'd made these choices and then paid good money to make them someone else's problem.

But underneath the fury, cold and worse: it all fit. Every piece. The stranger at her door with her own voice humming in a box. The road that opened too easily. The collectors who'd shadowed them but never quite closed in.

She'd been delivered. All of it, arranged. By whom, though—by Ivet? By the woman she used to be?

By the contract itself.

"The reliquary," Rafael said to the Broker, turning away from Ivet because if she didn't turn away she was going to hit her. "Show me."

* * *

It sat on the dais, atop the highest ledger, in a stand of black iron: a globe of glass the size of a fist, and inside it—

Inside it was a light. Not flame. Not candle-glow. It moved like something alive, pressing against the glass from within, and when Rafael stepped close, the light pressed harder, flaring, and she felt it in her sternum like a fist knocking from the inside.

Her soul. Small enough to hold. Bright enough to blind.

"There is the shape of our business," the Broker said, coming to stand beside her, and up close its stolen face smelled faintly of candlewax. "Thirteen moon-turns, fully elapsed at next dark. The gate stays sealed; the debt is discharged; the collateral reverts to us in perpetuity. Standard terms. *Or*—" and here it lifted the reliquary from its stand, cradling it almost tenderly— "there is the amendment. New business, as it were."

"Name it."

"The gate's ward is anchored to your soul. Your soul sits in my hand. Which means, Lady Villalobos, that I hold your gate in my hand." The Broker smiled with her own mouth, and it was the worst thing Rafael had ever seen. "Open the Thornfell gate. Unseal it—one night, one passage, one army, the scale is immaterial. Do that, and the collateral returns to you whole. Walk out under the stars with everything you lost. Refuse, and at next dark we collect, the anchor fails, and the gate opens *anyway*—unwarded, unsealed, screaming wide. You cannot keep it shut by refusing. You can only choose whether we open it politely or badly."

The candles overhead had formed the sigil again, and now the sigil began to rotate, slowly, like a lock considering a key.

Rafael looked at the reliquary. At the light beating against the glass—her light, her nine-years-missing self, frantic, *recognizing her*. She felt it the way you feel your name called across a crowded market.

And she thought: *it knows me. It's been down here nine years, and it knows me, and it's afraid.*

"One night," Rafael said. "One passage. Then the reliquary is mine."

"One passage," the Broker agreed, and extended the reliquary toward her, and every candle in the vast dark court leaned inward like a crowd.

Rafael reached out. Slowed her hand. Let it tremble—let the trembling look like longing, which was easy, because some of it was—and then, at the last possible instant, she did not take the glass.

She took the Broker's wrist.

Its flesh was cool and waxy, and it had not expected weight, had not expected *honesty of motion*, and for one half-second its borrowed face went blank with surprise. In that half-second Rafael wrenched the reliquary free, spun, and drove her elbow back into the place where a clerk's throat would be.

The court detonated.

Candles fell like meteors. Ledgers toppled from the dais in an avalanche of contracts, pages tearing loose and swirling, each page shrieking in a different dead client's voice. From the salt corridor, from the bone-stair, from everywhere at once, the collectors came—tall, grey, faceless things that unfolded out of the shadows like folded letters being opened.

"IVET!" Rafael roared, already running, the reliquary clutched to her chest and beating against her ribs like a second heart trying to get home. "THE STAIR! NOW!"

Ivet was running too—had been running before Rafael moved, because of course she had, because she'd planned this too, or feared it, or both—her knife out, carving a line of light across the floor behind them that the collectors recoiled from like water from hot iron.

Behind them, the Broker rose from the shattered ledgers. It did not shout. It simply held up its wrist, where Rafael's grip had left five perfect bruises, and said, in her own voice, calm as a ledger entry:

"The amendment stands void. Collection begins at dawn."

Above them, all ten thousand candles guttered at once—and relit, every single flame burning now the color of Rafael's eyes.

The bone-stair was two hundred and eleven steps up.

They had until dawn to climb all of them.
