## 4. A Sigil for the Spire

The reliquary cracked on the fourth stair, or maybe the fortieth—Rafael had lost count of both.

The court's screams followed them up through the smugglers' roads, echoing strangely in stone that had been cut to carry sound *downward*, toward the throne. Now it carried everything up instead: the Broker's voice unraveling from music into something rawer, a bell struck wrong. The collectors were behind them. The dark was behind them too, and ahead of them was only more dark, and between those two darknesses Rafael ran with a piece of glass pressed to her chest like a knife she'd stolen from her own ribs.

Inside the glass, her soul glowed a low amber, the color of lamplight seen through honey. It was warm against her sternum. That was the cruelest part. Nine years without it and it still fit, still knew the shape of her, still beat in time with the pulse at her throat as if no time had passed at all.

"Left," Ivet said, breath ragged, one hand on the wall. The scars on his forearm caught the reliquary's glow—old wounds, nine years old, made the night he'd walked her down these same stairs. "There's a landing past the cistern. Then the long climb."

"I remember," Rafael said, and stopped dead so fast he nearly went past her.

Because it was true. She remembered the cistern. She remembered the long climb, the way the stairs narrowed where the mountain pinched, the place where a smuggler named Ossa had scratched a crude sun into the rock for luck. All of it had come back with the box—not just the signing, but everything before it. Her mother's kitchen. The road east. The first time she'd stood on the Thornfell wall and watched the stars wheel over the pass and thought, *I could be happy here.*

She remembered being happy here. That was the thing she hadn't let herself know she'd lost.

"Raf." Ivet's hand closed over her wrist. Above them, faint as breath, something was coming down the stairs. Below them, worse. "Not now."

"No," she said. "Now. Listen to me." She held up the reliquary, and the light moved across his face, and she saw him flinch from it—he had always flinched from it, even before, even when he told her it was the only way. "It doesn't work."

"What?"

"The soul. Mine." Her voice came out flat, gatekeeper-flat, the voice she used for bad news at the border. "I can feel it trying to come back into me and it—it won't take. It's like trying to pour water back into a jar that's changed its shape." She turned her hand, watching the amber light swim. "They don't give things back. Even when you steal them. Even when they're yours. A soul that's been sold isn't a soul anymore, it's a *debt*, and debts have owners, and this one—" the crack in the glass whispered, spread a hair's width, "—this one is about to belong to whoever catches us."

Ivet stared at her. In the amber light he looked suddenly very old, older than the roads, older than the bargain. "Then what do we do?"

"We don't put it back," Rafael said. "We make something new."

* * *

The spire atop the Thornfell gate was smaller than she remembered—or she was larger now, which amounted to the same thing. They came out of the last stairwell into night air that had no stars in it, just a black ceiling pressing close, and the old ward-stone rising from the spire floor like a broken tooth: the sigil she had carved nine years ago, the one that bound the gate shut, already going grey and dead at its edges because the thing it was anchored to had been sitting in a glass box under a mountain, gathering dust.

Her soul had been the anchor. That was the whole trick of it. You couldn't seal a door like that with iron or prayer; you sealed it with something that *wanted* to stay, something that would hold the line forever out of sheer stubbornness. The Broker had explained it gently, the way you explain a price to someone who hasn't read it yet. *Your soul will remain here, at the threshold, keeping watch. It is, after all, what you are best at.*

And it had kept watch. For nine years the gate had held while the woman who'd made the bargain walked her patrols with a hole in her chest and a smile she'd bought secondhand.

Rafael knelt at the base of the ward-stone and set the reliquary down.

"It'll shatter if I use it," she said. "If I try to pour it back in and fail, it breaks, and then my soul belongs to whatever's left of the court when the mountain comes looking for it." She pulled her belt-knife—the same knife, she realized, the same knife she'd been carrying since before the hedge-witch, since before everything, some part of her apparently unwilling to surrender even that—and tested its edge against her thumb. "But a debt can be paid in other currency."

"Rafael." Ivet said her whole name, carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"You told me once there was no other way." She didn't look up. The knife-point found the seam in the ward-stone, the groove the old sigil had worn. "You were wrong. There's always another way. It's just never the cheap one."

She began to carve.

Not the old sigil—that was a soul-sigil, a chain-anchor, dead letter now. This one had to be built from scratch, from the truth of what she actually was, and the truth was this: she did not need a soul to hold the gate. She needed *memory*. Every road she'd ever walked. Every name she'd ever learned at the checkpoint—Ossa and her scratched sun, the hedge-witch whose name Rafael had paid to forget and remembered anyway now, *Maren*, Maren with ink-stained fingers, Maren who had wept while she worked. Every loss. Every lie she'd lived inside for nine years. Every morning she'd woken in the guardhouse knowing something was missing and choosing, again, to stand at her post regardless.

A soul was what you were when nobody watched. Memory was what you were when *you* were the one watching. And Rafael Villalobos had spent nine years doing nothing else.

The knife bit deep. Blood filled the grooves and the ward-stone drank it, and somewhere below—far below, through a mile of living rock—she felt the court feel it. Felt the Broker stop screaming. Felt the attention of an entire subterranean nation swing upward toward the thin bright thread of her pain, and she carved faster.

*Every road,* she thought, laying each one into the stone like a beam in a house. *Every name. Every loss.* The gate took them all. It was greedy for them, hungry, drinking memory after memory—but not taking, never taking, because she was giving freely, and that was the difference, that was the whole alchemy of it. What is given cannot be collected.

The reliquary cracked clean through.

Amber light spilled out across the spire floor—and did not sink into her, did not try to come home. It simply rose, unhurried, like a lamp being carried upstairs, and poured itself into the fresh-cut sigil, and the sigil drank it the way the stone had drunk her blood, and the old grey ward-lines flushed gold, and the gate beneath their feet closed—not with thunder, but with the sound of a lock turning in a door someone has finally decided to keep.

Rafael sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking. Her hands would probably always shake a little now, and she found, testing the thought like a sore tooth, that she could live with that.

Above her, the sky tore open.

One star. Just one—a hard white point straight overhead, burning like an eye opening. Then two. Then a handful, scattered, then dozens, the whole torn ceiling stitching itself back together seam by seam, constellations reassembling out of nothing, until the Milky Way lay across the pass like a spilled ribbon and Rafael was crying without any decision to do so, tears cutting cold lines through the blood on her face.

"They're back," Ivet said. He sounded like a man reading good news he didn't trust.

"One by one," Rafael said. "That's how they'll stay, I think. Nothing comes back all at once. Not stars. Not people."

* * *

Dawn came up thin and blue over Thornfell, and the pass looked exactly as it always had, which felt like a miracle and an insult in equal measure.

Ivet stood at the top of the stairwell with his pack on, and Rafael understood, looking at him, that he had been standing there for some time deciding.

"You could come down," she said. "The guardhouse has terrible coffee and the winter is four months long. But the gate holds now. On me. On everything I remember." She flexed her hands, feeling the strange new weight of them—every year accounted for, nothing filed away, nothing forgotten. It hurt. It would always hurt. That was the cost, and she had signed for it herself, in her own blood, and this time she had read every word. "I'm going to remember all of it, Ivet. Including you."

He was quiet a moment. "That's not forgiveness."

"No," she agreed. "Forgiveness might come later, or it might not. This is just the truth. You led me down there once. You brought me the box. Both things are real, and I'm done pretending either one cancels the other." She looked at him steadily, gatekeeper-steady. "So. Stay or go. But choose it honestly, this time."

Below them, far down the mountain, the first supply wagon of the season was rattling up the pass road, and the driver would want coffee, and the ledger needed updating, and the world—ordinary, starlit, unsealed—was waiting to be let in.

Ivet slid the pack off his shoulder and set it down by the door.

"Show me how the wards read now," he said. "I've got nine years of watch to make up."

Rafael smiled—her own smile, unbought, unburied—and opened the gatehouse door onto the morning.
