## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's supper arrived on silver, and Zephyrine Vane tasted death in the third course.

He had learned to read poison the way priests read scripture — patiently, with the whole of his attention, and with a private understanding that most of what he read would never be spoken aloud. The first course was pheasant in a glaze of mountain honey. Clean. The second, a leek soup that carried nothing worse than an old cook's grudge against salt. He swallowed both and stood at the king's shoulder while His Majesty ate, hands folded behind his back, face arranged into the bland attentiveness that had kept him employed for six years.

The third course was lamb.

Zephyrine took his spoonful as he always did — a full mouthful, chewed properly, because a taster who nibbled like a frightened bird invited questions, and questions were more dangerous than arsenic. The meat was good. Rosemary, juniper, a thread of vinegar. And beneath it, so faint that anyone else's tongue would have missed it entirely: something wrong.

Not bitter. Not metallic. It tasted, absurdly, of *nothing* — a small hole in the flavor, the way a tooth feels after it's pulled. He knew only one compound that ate a taste out of the world instead of adding to it.

Hollowroot.

His heart did not stutter. Six years of dosing himself had taught it better manners than that. But his mind went very still and very cold, the way it did when he stepped onto ice over deep water, because hollowroot was not a court poison. Court poisons were for kings and chancellors — elegant, expensive, traceable to three apothecaries who could be hanged by morning. Hollowroot came out of the Thornfell mountains, out of smuggler caches, out of the world below the Spire. It cost almost nothing. It killed almost anything.

Almost anything.

It did not kill him. That was the entire point of him, though only three people alive knew it.

He set down the spoon, turned to the head steward, and said, "The lamb is off. Send it back."

The king looked up, annoyed. "Off? It smells magnificent."

"Juniper's turned," Zephyrine lied smoothly. "You'd taste it within the hour, Majesty, and I'd rather you didn't spend your evening regretting the kitchen." A small, safe lie about spoiled meat — the kind of lie a taster was *supposed* to tell. The kind that kept everyone comfortable.

Behind his teeth, where no one could see, his tongue had begun to go numb.

The dose was small. Whoever had laced the lamb had been careful — careful enough that a normal man might have felt only a heaviness, a sleepiness, and gone quietly into a nap from which the Spire's physicians would later find it impolite to wake him. Careful enough that it might even have passed for accident, if anyone had thought to look.

But they hadn't laced the whole dish. Zephyrine knew the geometry of poisoned platters the way sailors knew weather. This was a pocket of it — a single spoonful's worth, placed precisely where the taster's spoon would fall. Placed for the left side of the dish, because Zephyrine always tasted from the left. Everyone at court knew that. Everyone watched him do it every night.

Poison meant for the king is spread through the dish. Poison meant for the taster is spread for the taster.

Someone wanted *him*. Someone knew exactly how he worked, exactly where his spoon fell, and exactly what he was.

The numbness crept along his jaw like frost climbing a windowpane. He kept his face pleasant and his breathing even and calculated, with the detached arithmetic that years of self-poisoning had made second nature, how long he had before his hands started shaking. An hour, perhaps less if the hollowroot was fresh. Enough time.

"Perhaps I should retire early," he told the steward, bowing. "If the meal disagrees with me, I'd hate to disgrace the Spire."

No one stopped him. Why would they? Tasters came and went. They were furniture that chewed.

* * *

The servants' corridor behind the kitchens smelled of onions and tallow, and Zephyrine walked it slowly, deliberately, letting the numbness spread while he counted exits. There were four ways out of the underkitchens. Two led up into the Spire proper, where inquisitors moved like well-dressed rats. One led to the courtyard and its guards. And one —

One was a door that officially did not exist.

It sat at the end of the wine cellar passage, behind a rack of casks that had held vinegar since before the usurper's coronation, and it opened onto stairs that went *down*, into the old city beneath the old city, into the warren of tunnels and cellars and forgotten streets that the free cities' people called the Undercroft and the crown called nothing at all, because acknowledging it would mean admitting the crown didn't own everything it stood on.

Smuggler's door. Zephyrine had known about it for years. He'd simply never needed it until tonight, because tonight was the first night in six years that someone had tried to kill *him* instead of his employer.

He found the kitchen hand in the scullery.

The boy — no, not a boy, a man of perhaps forty with flour to the elbows and a face like a closed ledger — was scrubbing a pot with the focused violence of someone trying to scrub away a memory. When Zephyrine entered, the man's shoulders climbed toward his ears.

"You sent back my lamb," the man said without turning. His voice shook only slightly. "Said the juniper was off."

"It was," Zephyrine said. "On the left side."

The scrubbing stopped.

"I don't know what you mean." The man turned then, and Zephyrine saw it all in his face — not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been easier. This was terror wearing guilt's clothing, terror with a family attached to it, terror with debts. Zephyrine had spent six years reading faces across dinner tables; he could read this one like a menu.

"Left side of the dish," Zephyrine repeated softly. "Where the taster's spoon falls. You know how I work. Which means someone told you how I work, which means someone *studied* me, which means this wasn't coin changing hands in a tavern. This was planned." He leaned against the doorframe, casual, though his fingertips had gone distant and cold. "Who gave you the hollowroot?"

"I don't—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and final, and the man flinched. "I've swallowed enough poison tonight to kill a horse twice over. I am not in the mood for the dance. Who?"

Silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the supper went on without its lamb, laughter drifting down through floorboards like snow.

"They have my daughter," the cook said finally, barely above a whisper. "Inquisitors. She stole bread — bread, sir, just bread — and suddenly she's facing the purging courts unless certain arrangements are made." His eyes were wet and furious and hopeless. "They knew things. About you. About your mother. Things I don't understand, but they knew them, and they said one dose, one little spoonful, and Dessa walks free."

Zephyrine's blood — already slowed, already thickening under the poison's weight — seemed to stop altogether.

"My mother."

"They didn't say her name. They didn't have to." The cook's voice dropped further. "They said the Vanes were supposed to be *gone*. All of them. And that you were the last, and that last ones make mistakes, and mistakes can be... tidied."

The numbness reached his lips. He tasted copper where he'd bitten the inside of his cheek.

"Get your daughter," Zephyrine said. "Tonight. Before they change their minds about the arrangement. There's a door behind the vinegar casks that goes down into the Undercroft — take it, take the low road east, and don't come back to the Spire ever again. Do you understand me?"

The cook stared at him. "And you? What will you tell them?"

"Nothing." Zephyrine pushed off the doorframe and straightened, and something settled in him as he did — not calm, nothing so gentle, but resolve, hard and bright as forged steel. "I'm going to tell them nothing at all, because by the time anyone asks, I intend to be somewhere their writ doesn't run."

He turned toward the cellar stairs.

"Master Vane," the cook called after him, and the name — his true name, spoken aloud in the Spire for the first time in six years — landed on him like a hand on his shoulder. "Why spare me? I fed you poison."

"Because whoever holds your daughter holds a hundred others," Zephyrine said. "And because I've spent my life swallowing other people's poisons." He allowed himself one thin smile. "I'd like to spend what's left of it returning them."

Then he went down, past the vinegar casks, through the door that did not exist, into the dark.

Behind him, far above, a bell began to ring.

Not the supper bell. Not the watch bell.

The alarm — and Zephyrine, descending into the Undercroft with poison numbing his jaw and his mother's name burning in his ears, understood with terrible clarity that whatever came next, the quiet life of the king's food-taster had ended somewhere between the third course and the fourth.

The hunt had begun.
