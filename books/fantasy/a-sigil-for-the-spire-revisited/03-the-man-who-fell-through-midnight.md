## 3. The Man Who Fell Through Midnight

Zephyrine climbed the Thornfell road with the prophecy book pressed against his ribs like a second heart.

He had not slept. Sleep, he'd decided somewhere past the third switchback, was for men whose names weren't written in century-old ink. The book rode inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and every time his boot found loose scree he felt it shift against him — *the taster who swallows truth* — and climbed faster.

The pass was still open. That was the only good news the mountain offered. Below him, the Spire's watchtowers smoked with signal fires: three columns, then four, a language he'd spent years learning to read from the tasting table. *Something is coming.* By morning the usurper's riders would hold the low road. By noon, maybe, this one too.

So he walked through the dark with his hood up and his tongue — that famous, cursed instrument — cataloguing the air. Pine resin. Cold stone. Snowmelt and iron. And underneath it all, faint as a lie in a courtier's mouth: ozone. The smell of a storm that hadn't broken yet.

Except there were no clouds.

The road bent around a shoulder of granite, and Zephyrine stopped so abruptly he nearly went down on the scree.

Ahead, wedged between two boulders where the path narrowed to a cart's width, hung a fold of light. Not firelight, not moonlight — nothing in nature fell like that. It stood upright in the air, taller than a man, thin as a knife's edge seen head-on, and it hummed. He felt the hum in his teeth. In the fillings the Spire's physicians had put there years ago, when his immunity training first began to cost him enamel. It was the same sensation as the night in the kitchen, the poison meant for him alone: the world quietly rearranging itself around a fact it didn't want to admit.

Zephyrine's hand went to his knife. His other hand went to the book through his coat.

*The final line names a stranger arriving out of empty air.*

"No," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Absolutely no."

The fold opened.

It didn't burst; it *parted*, the way a curtain parts, and a man stepped through it into the cold mountain night as casually as a guest arriving late to dinner. He was tall, dark-haired, dressed in clothes that were fine once and ruined now — a coat scorched along one sleeve, boots crusted with something grey and ashlike that Zephyrine suspected came from nowhere at all. The man took three steps, stopped, and looked at the mountains as if he'd never seen one before.

Then he looked at Zephyrine, and his face did something complicated: recognition and total ignorance fighting for the same ground.

"You," the stranger said. His voice cracked on the word, unused. "You're real."

"Generally." Zephyrine kept the knife low and ready. "And you're late. The page said midnight. It's been midnight for an hour."

"I don't—" The stranger swayed. Caught himself against the boulder. When he lifted his hand to steady himself, Zephyrine saw it: burned into the palm, pale and precise as a brand, half of a sigil. A curve of interlocking lines, ending in a broken edge, as if the rest of the design had been torn away.

The taste of copper flooded Zephyrine's mouth, and it wasn't fear. It was memory. He'd seen that design once, as a child, traced in candle soot by his mother's finger on the underside of a table while she whispered words he'd been too young to keep.

"What's your name?" he asked.

The stranger blinked at him, slow, like a man surfacing from deep water. "Corvel." A pause. "I think. I'm fairly sure that's mine."

"And how you got here?"

"That's the part I don't have." Corvel looked back at the fold of light — which was already thinning, fraying at its edges like smoke deciding to be nothing. "There was a room. Candles. Someone reading aloud, and their voice kept breaking. Then I was falling, and then—" he gestured vaguely at the entire world, "—this."

The light folded shut and was gone, leaving only the ozone smell and a faint warmth in the stone, like a hearth recently raked.

Zephyrine sheathed his knife. It seemed pointless now. You couldn't cut a prophecy; he'd been trying all day.

"My name is Zephyrine Vane," he said. "I'm the royal food-taster of the Spire. Or I was, until last night, when someone poisoned a dish specifically to kill me, which means someone knows what my blood is for. There's a book that named tonight and described you before you existed here. And you have half a sigil burned into your hand that matches one my mother hid me under tables to avoid learning about." He exhaled. "So. Which of us is having the stranger night?"

Corvel laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter belonged to whoever he'd been before. He flexed his branded hand, staring at it. "It hurts," he said, almost conversationally. "Not the burn. The burn doesn't hurt. It hurts like it's *looking* for something."

"It is." Zephyrine started walking, because standing still on an open pass at midnight was how dead men stayed dead. "Come on. We have until dawn before the army closes this road, and I know a smuggler who owes my family more than she'll ever admit."

"The army." Corvel fell into step beside him, unsteady but moving. "Whose army? What war?"

"That," Zephyrine said, "is going to take longer than we have. Short version: the king on the Spire's throne isn't the king. Hasn't been for twenty years. The real line was erased — records burned, houses struck from the rolls, anyone with the blood scattered or killed. My family kept one thing they couldn't burn." He tapped his own chest, over the book. "And apparently, one thing they couldn't predict either." He nodded at Corvel's palm.

They walked. The scree crunched. Somewhere below, a horn sounded — one long note, answered by another, the army talking to itself in the dark.

"Why me?" Corvel said at last. It wasn't rhetorical. It was the question of a drowning man. "Out of everyone. Why pull some— I don't even know what I am. Why drop me on a mountain next to the one man in the world who knows what this mark means?"

"Because prophecies are lazy," Zephyrine said. "Or cruel. They always pick people who are already halfway there." He glanced sideways. In the starlight, Corvel looked less like a weapon and more like what he was: exhausted, frightened, and holding himself together out of pure stubborn habit. "For what it's worth — I've spent my whole life swallowing poison so a liar could eat safely. Being scripted by someone else isn't new to me. The trick is deciding which lines you'll say."

"And if the script says I'm here to destroy everything?"

"Then we'll rewrite it." Zephyrine said it lightly, and knew, tasting his own words the way he tasted everything, that they were true. "I've had enough of kings writing endings for other people. Haven't you?"

Corvel was quiet for three switchbacks. Then: "I keep remembering pieces. A corridor. Green glass windows. Someone calling my name like it mattered." His branded hand curled closed. "I don't remember who I was. But I remember being *loved*, and then I remember it being taken apart."

"Then we get it back," Zephyrine said. "Piece by piece. Starting with a smuggler captain called Maren Quick, who knew my mother, and who is going to be furious that I've brought her a second problem before breakfast."

Ahead, the pass dipped toward the tree line, and beyond it, far off, a valley held a scatter of lights — a smuggler camp burning low and warm against the dark.

Corvel stopped. "Zephyrine." He said the name carefully, testing it. "The person reading, in the room I fell from. Before the candles went out, they said something. I thought it was nonsense."

"What?"

"They said: *the halves must meet before the passes close.*"

Below them, in the valley, a bell began to ring — fast, urgent, the alarm rhythm Zephyrine had been taught to dread since childhood.

The army had reached the foothills early.

* * *

Corvel had died once. He was almost certain of it.

There had been a moment — falling between the candlelit room and the mountain — when everything stopped mattering, when his own name dissolved like sugar in hot water, and he had understood with total clarity that whatever he had been, he was no longer. Something else had stepped out of that light. Something with his face and a hole where his history should be.

Now he followed the taster down into the trees and tried to inventory what he'd been given instead.

Fragments. That was all. Green glass. A corridor that smelled of beeswax and lilies. A woman's voice — not reading, singing, low, a song about a river — cut off mid-verse. Hands, small ones, pressing something into his palm while an older voice said, urgently, *keep it hidden, keep it hidden, they're coming.* And pain. White, total pain, and then the fall.

His palm throbbed in time with his heartbeat. When he opened his fingers, the sigil-half caught the starlight, faintly luminous, like a scar that remembered being important.

"You're staring at it again," Zephyrine said without turning around.

"It stares first." Corvel flexed his hand. "It pulls. Like a compass needle. Toward—" he rotated slowly, feeling the drag of it, "—east. Deeper into the mountains. Toward something."

"Then east is where its other half lives." Zephyrine crouched at the tree line, studying the valley below, and Corvel watched him work: the economical way he read the camp, counted guards' silhouettes, noted the bell still clanging its alarm. This was a man built entirely of caution, Corvel thought, and yet here he was, fleeing a throne with a stranger and a forbidden book, choosing trust at speed because there was no time for the slow kind.

"Do you trust me?" Corvel asked. "Honestly. You've known me an hour."

"I trust the book," Zephyrine said. "The book says you matter. The book has been right twice tonight, which is twice more than most prophets manage." He rose. "Trust is just evidence, Corvel. I'm collecting yours."

"And what do I collect of yours?"

Zephyrine smiled — the first true one Corvel had seen from him, quick and crooked and gone. "My life. Same as you. Now stay close, walk like you belong to Maren Quick's payroll, and if anyone asks, you're my cousin from the coast and you're simple."

"I'm what?"

"Loud questions get loud answers. Loud answers get us hanged." Zephyrine started down into the camp. "Welcome to the resistance, cousin. It's mostly walking."

But at the edge of the firelight, Zephyrine stopped so suddenly Corvel walked into him. Below, in the open ground between the tents, a figure stood waiting — a woman in riding leathers, arms crossed, silver-streaked hair cropped short, watching them climb down with the expression of someone who had been expecting worse news than this and was almost disappointed.

"Maren Quick," Zephyrine murmured. "She knew my mother. She'll know what the sigil is."

"She looks angry."

"She's not angry." Zephyrine exhaled. "That's her worried face. Save yourself — don't ask why."

They descended into the firelight, two men carrying half a truth apiece, and behind them, high on the pass, torches began to move in disciplined ranks — the army, closing the road they'd left, sealing the mountain like a fist.

The halves had met. Now the passes were closing.

Exactly as written.
