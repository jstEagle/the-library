## 4. What the Usurper Forged

Corvel slept badly, and the dreams were not dreams.

They were memories wearing the wrong coat — scenes that arrived with the weight of lived experience but no door he could walk back through. He woke on the cold stone of a smuggler waystation with Zephyrine's cloak over him and his own palm burning, and for a long moment he lay still, watching shapes assemble in the dark behind his eyes.

A hall of white pillars. A woman's voice reading names from a scroll, and every third name followed by a silence where applause should have been — because those people were dead, purged, struck from the rolls. A child's hand pressed into warm wax. His hand. He knew the small scar on the knuckle; he still had it.

*House Aurelian,* something in him said, tasting the name like a word learned before language. *House of the Dawnspire. Sworn keepers of the—*

The memory folded shut like a fan.

He sat up. Across the room, Zephyrine was already awake, sitting against the wall with his arms around his knees, watching Corvel with the particular attention of a man who tested everything before he trusted it. Poisons, promises, strangers out of folds of light.

"You talked in your sleep," Zephyrine said.

"What did I say?"

"Names. A lot of them. Then you said *the seal is split, not broken.*" Zephyrine's voice stayed level, but Corvel had spent one day with him and already knew: level was how this man sounded when he was most afraid. "That mean anything to you?"

"It means I remember things I have no right to remember." Corvel flexed his left hand. The sigil-half sat burned into the palm, a crescent crossed by three lines, and it had not faded since he'd stepped out of nowhere two nights ago. If anything it had darkened, ink settling into skin that had always been waiting for it. "I remember a hall. A house. A woman reading names off a scroll and leaving gaps where people used to be."

Zephyrine went very still. "Gaps."

"Silences. Like the record itself had been cut with shears."

The taster unfolded himself and came to sit across the dead fire pit. In the grey pre-dawn light his face looked older than it had on the road — or maybe just more honest about it. "My mother kept a book of household recipes," he said slowly. "Every recipe started at page nine. Pages one through eight were razored out. She told me it was water damage." He laughed once, without any humor in it. "I believed her for twenty years. I taste poison for a living, and I believed her."

"The usurper's men did that?" 

"The usurper's men do that everywhere. Archives. Parish registers. Gravestones." Zephyrine rubbed his jaw. "There's a whole industry of forgetting running ahead of his armies. They call them scrubbers. They don't kill you if they can help it. They kill the idea that you ever existed."

Corvel looked down at his palm. The crescent caught the grey light.

"I think," he said, "that someone did that to me. Not to my records. To me."

* * *

They walked north through the morning, following the smuggler road Maren Quick had described — a goat-track disguised as nothing at all, doubling back along a river gorge so that anyone watching from the heights would count them twice and lose them once. Zephyrine moved like a man who'd memorized every stone, which, Corvel gathered, he had. Years of it. Tasting poison by day, learning outlaw roads by night, all in preparation for a run he hadn't known he'd need to make.

Around midday they stopped above a mining town called Brackenfell, and what Corvel saw there rearranged something inside him.

The town was burning. Not all of it — just the records-house, the chapel, the school. Three buildings, chosen with care, and soldiers working through the streets with ledgers under their arms, going door to door. From this distance the sound didn't carry, but Corvel could imagine it: polite knocks, polite questions, *any family named Vane? any kin of the old houses? any heirlooms, any seals, anything with a crest?*

"They're ahead of schedule," Zephyrine breathed. Beside him, all his careful calm had cracked open. "Maren said they wouldn't close the passes for another week. That's the Third Column. That's the *Vanguard of Reclamation.*" He spat the title like a bad vintage. "If they're at Brackenfell—"

"Then they're erasing as they march," Corvel said. "Not after. With."

Because he understood it suddenly, wholly, the way you understand a taste — instantly and below thought. This was not an army looting. This was an army editing. Every town between here and the free cities was a page, and the Vanguard was moving down the manuscript with blade and fire, cutting out anything that contradicted the usurper's story: that his line had ruled for three hundred years, that no other claim had ever been, that no house had stood before his house stood.

And underneath that understanding, colder: *they are looking for something. For someones.*

For a sigil split in two. For a taster who swallowed truth. For a man who fell through midnight carrying half a seal burned into his hand.

"We can't go through Brackenfell," Zephyrine said. "There's a high path, two days, exposed—" He stopped. Looked at Corvel properly. "You're not listening."

"I'm listening."

"No. You're somewhere else. Where?"

Corvel closed his eyes and let the fragments come, because fighting them only made them worse. A map room lit by green lamps. A man with a soft voice explaining terms — *when the seal is joined, the claim dissolves, and the council will have no choice* — and Corvel agreeing, though to what, exactly? Had he agreed? Could a man agree to be a weapon and then be surprised when he cut?

"I think I was sent," he said quietly. "I think someone on the other side of wherever I came from put me on this road on purpose. Gave me half a sigil. Pointed me at you." He opened his eyes. "Or at the usurper. I don't know which. I don't even know if *I'm* the weapon or just its handle."

Zephyrine was silent for a long moment. Below them, smoke rose off the burning records-house in a straight, disciplined column, like a candle being snuffed.

"My uncle taught me one thing about poisons worth remembering," the taster finally said. "It doesn't matter what the poisoner intended. What matters is what's in the cup now. You drink it or you don't." He shouldered his pack. "Whatever you were sent to be, you're standing on a mountain road with me, and the pass closes in days. So decide what you are *now*, and let the rest be history someone else razors out."

It was good counsel. It was also merciless, and Corvel loved him a little for that.

* * *

That night, sheltering in a hollow beneath an overhang while sleet needled past, Corvel dreamed again, and this time he did not fight it.

He stood in the white-pillared hall, whole and present, and the woman with the scroll was reading the last name on it. *Aurelian.* Just the name, no first name, because the first name had been cut away. And the hall full of people turned to look at him — hundreds of faces, patient, expectant — and every one of them was Zephyrine's face, and every one of them was waiting for him to say whether the name was his.

He woke with tears frozen on his cheeks and his palm blazing.

The sigil-half had changed. Faint lines now extended beyond the crescent's edge, ghost-traces reaching toward the wrist, as if the mark were trying to complete itself without permission — trying to draw the other half of itself out of the air, out of memory, out of blood.

*The seal is split, not broken.*

Somewhere south of them, a man wore the other half. Or carried it. Or had killed everyone who knew where it was and buried it under a new throne's foundations.

And somewhere behind him, in whatever fold of midnight he'd fallen through, someone was watching to see what he would do with the edge he'd been given.

Zephyrine stirred at the mouth of the hollow. "Road forks at dawn," he murmured, half-asleep. "East goes to Maren's salt camp. West goes into the Vanguard's teeth. Choose fast."

Corvel watched the ghost-lines crawl another inch toward his wrist and understood, with grief he could not fully claim and fear he could not put down, that the choosing had never really been his to make.

Only the drinking.

"Dawn, then," he said. "East. We find the smuggler captain — and we find out whose blood opens this thing."

Far off to the west, thunder rolled where no storm should be. Or perhaps it wasn't thunder at all, but the sound of an army closing a pass.
