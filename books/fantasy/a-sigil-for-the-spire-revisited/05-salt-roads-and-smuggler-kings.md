## 5. Salt Roads and Smuggler Kings

The harbor at Saltmere stank of brine and lies, which Zephyrine had always found comforting. Lies meant commerce, and commerce meant someone willing to move two fugitives east without asking why they smelled of smoke and inquisitor's lantern-oil.

Corvel walked beside him with his hood up and his burned palm hidden, though Zephyrine noticed he kept flexing the hand like a man testing whether a wound still hurt. Three days on the Thornfell road, and the stranger had stopped flinching at his own reflection. That was progress, or surrender. With Corvel it was hard to tell.

"The Salt Roads," Zephyrine said, nodding toward the crooked line of taverns leaning against the seawall. "Every captain here will swear she's never carried anything but salt and prayer. We want the one whose cargo hold smells like neither."

"And how do we find her?"

"We don't." He pulled Corvel into the shadow of a drying rack hung with fish that had seen better decades. "We let her find us. In Saltmere, standing still too long is how you get found by the wrong people."

They didn't have to stand long.

She came out of a doorway that had no business fitting her — broad-shouldered, gray-streaked braid coiled at her neck like a rope ready for work, a scar splitting one eyebrow into two skeptical halves. She looked at Zephyrine the way a woman looks at a debt she'd half-forgotten and half-hoped was dead.

"Well," said Maren Quick. "You've got your mother's way of standing in doorways like you own them."

Zephyrine's breath went somewhere below his ribs and stayed there.

"You knew her."

"Knew her? Boy, I ran salt with Sera Vane for six years before you were weaned." Maren's eyes narrowed, traveling over him — the travel-worn coat, the shadowed face, the stranger lurking behind him like a bad omen wearing a hood. "And now you come down out of Thornfell looking like a man the Spire wants dead, with company nobody sent for. So either you're in trouble—"

"I'm in trouble."

"—or you're *the* trouble. Same price either way. My cabin. Now. And if you're followed, you swim."

* * *

The cabin of the *Kindly Tide* was small enough that three people in it constituted a crowd and four a mutiny. Maren poured something amber into tin cups and waited until Zephyrine drank first — an old courtesy between people who understood poisons professionally.

Then she saw Corvel's palm.

He'd unclenched it to take the cup, and the firelight caught the broken sigil burned into his skin: half a circle, half a star, lines that seemed to shift when looked at sideways, as if the ink were still deciding what it wanted to be. Maren went very still. Stillness, Zephyrine had learned, was what dangerous women did instead of screaming.

"Where did you get that?" she asked Corvel.

"I woke up with it," he said. "That's all I know. A fold of light on a mountain road, and then nothing."

"Nothing." She set down her cup. "Zephyrine Vane. Do you know what your family was? Not what the tax rolls say — cooks and clerks, quiet folk, forgettable folk. I mean what they *were*."

"My mother sold spices."

"Your mother *carried* spices. Among other things." Maren leaned forward. "The Vanes were sigil-keepers. Sworn to a house that ruled these lands before the usurper's grandfather rewrote every ledger, every genealogy, every gravestone in three kingdoms. House Aurelian. You won't find the name anywhere now. That was rather the point of the purges."

The word landed in Zephyrine's chest like a coin dropped down a well — he heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom.

"Sigil-keepers," he repeated.

"Split in two when the house fell. Half sealed in bloodline — passed mother to child, dormant, so no inquisitor could ever burn it out of you. The other half—" she nodded at Corvel's palm "—cut free and hidden with the last loyal servant of the old house. Your mother used to sing you to sleep with a lullaby about it. *Two hands make the light, little love.* Thought it was nonsense myself. Then this man walks out of empty air wearing half of it on his hand, and you come walking right behind him, and suddenly I'm a believer in my old age."

Zephyrine thought of his immunity built poison by poison. Of the prophecy book's page: *the taster who swallows truth.* His whole life had been a locked room, and everyone but him had been carrying the key around in their pockets.

"Why tell us?" Corvel asked quietly. "You could sell us. The usurper pays well for Vanes."

Maren laughed — short and sharp, like a hatchet thrown.

"Boy, the usurper's men burned my sister's village for harboring salt-runners. I've spent thirty years moving contraband under his nose out of spite and profit, and tonight spite is winning." She stood, and the cabin seemed to stand with her. "You're not cargo anymore. You're crew. That means I feed you, I fight for you, and if the inquisitors come, we scatter to the rally points and trust each other to be where we said we'd be. That's the whole religion of the salt roads. Can you live under it?"

Zephyrine looked at Corvel. Corvel looked back — wary still, scripted still, but with something new underneath: the same vertigo Zephyrine felt, the sense of two men discovering the world had been holding doors open for them their whole lives.

"We can live under it," Zephyrine said.

"Good. First lesson of crew: everyone hauls." Maren threw him a coil of rope. "Even prophecy. Especially prophecy."

* * *

For two days the *Kindly Tide* ran east along the coast, then turned inland up a river the maps politely declined to name. Zephyrine learned knots. Corvel learned to stop apologizing for existing. Maren's crew — a dozen smugglers with names like Brackish Tom and Sister Coin — taught them both dice games designed to separate travelers from their money, and Zephyrine discovered that losing gracefully was its own kind of immunity.

It was, he realized on the second night, the first time in his life he'd eaten a meal he hadn't tested first.

Sister Coin had shoved a bowl of stew at him, he'd taken a spoonful out of pure exhausted habit, and then frozen — waiting for the numbness, the bitterness, the telltale almond ghost. Nothing came. Just lamb and turnips and someone else's cooking, eaten among people who had, without ceremony, decided he belonged.

"You all right?" Corvel asked.

"Someone made me dinner," Zephyrine said, "and I didn't check it for poison."

"Is that good or bad?"

"It's the strangest thing that's ever happened to me, and I watched a man fall out of the sky."

Corvel smiled. It transformed his face — the wariness cracking to show something young underneath. "My crew," he started to say, then caught himself, hearing it. Zephyrine heard it too.

Neither of them corrected it.

* * *

The raid came at dawn, as raids prefer to.

Zephyrine woke to Maren's boot on his chest and her hand clamped over his mouth, which in smuggler dialect meant *shut up and be grateful*. Above deck, voices — not the crew's rough salt-talk but clipped, official cadences. Lantern-light through the deck boards. The particular silence of soldiers being polite before they stop being polite.

"Inquisitor's skiffs," Maren breathed. "Three of them. They boarded the river barges downstream — they're sweeping the whole waterway. Someone talked."

"Rally points?" Corvel whispered.

"Mule caravan leaves the north bank within the hour. You two are mule drivers. Congratulations on your promotion." She pressed a purse into Zephyrine's hand and, unexpectedly, gripped his shoulder — brief and fierce. "Your mother once carried a man exactly your weight through a burning customhouse. Family trade, keeping people alive. Go make her proud, and I'll see you at Hollow Ford or not at all."

They went over the side into the reeds while Maren marched up onto her deck to lie magnificently to men with warrants. Behind them, Zephyrine heard the first crackle of torches touching canvas, and Sister Coin's voice rising in a smuggler's curse so inventive it should have been studied.

The mule caravan scattered north along a drover's track, twelve animals and forty frightened souls pretending to be tanners and pilgrims. By midday the inquisitor's riders were visible on the ridgelines behind them — black cloaks, unhurried, the confidence of hunters who knew the quarry was tired.

Corvel stumbled beside a mule named after a saint, clutching his burned hand. "It's *singing*," he said through his teeth. "Since the raid started, the sigil — Zeph, it knows something's coming. It's pulling north. Hard."

"North is the Undervault."

"North is *Hollow Ford* first," Zephyrine said, "and after Hollow Ford, whatever the sigil wants. But look at the ridge."

Corvel looked. The riders had stopped. One of them — ahead of the others, riding alone, cloak the deep charcoal that marked rank — sat her horse on the skyline and simply watched them go. Making no signal. Giving no order.

"An inquisitor with mercy in her heart would be a useful rumor," Zephyrine murmured. "But I've survived too many dinners to believe in convenient rumors."

Behind them, far to the south, the usurper's signal fires had begun to bloom along the passes like a rash — one, then three, then a chain of burning stars marching east. Closing the high country. Sealing the roads ahead of them.

Corvel's hand pulsed light through his fingers, steady as a heartbeat now, pointing north.

"She's still watching," he said. "The rider."

"Then let's give her something worth watching," Zephyrine said, and drove his mule faster into the darkening hills.
