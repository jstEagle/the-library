## 6. The Poisoner's Confession

The chapel had no roof.

That was the first thing Zephyrine noticed as Maren's crew scattered into the tree line behind him — the stars overhead, naked and indifferent, pouring down through the broken vaulting like cold water. The walls still stood, old Thornfell granite furred with moss, and one window survived: a lancet of colored glass depicting a saint whose face the weather had eaten away. Whoever the saint had been, they'd spent a century watching nothing but sky.

Zephyrine ducked through the doorway and pressed his back against stone that was colder than it had any right to be in high summer. His lungs burned from the climb. Somewhere below, torchlight bobbed through the pines — inquisitors, at least six, moving with the patient discipline of people who had done this a hundred times and never once lost their quarry.

Corvel crouched beside him, breathing hard, his ruined palm cradled against his chest. In the starlight the burned sigil-half looked almost wet, as if the mountain itself were weeping out of the man's skin.

"They split up," Corvel whispered. "Two to the north wall. The rest are circling."

"Then we have perhaps four minutes of being alive," Zephyrine said, "and I intend to spend them badly."

He moved along the chapel's inner wall, fingers reading the stone the way he read a dish — texture, temperature, the faint wrongness that meant something hidden underneath. His mother had taught him that. *Everything conceals, little swallow. Your job is to taste through the lie.* Behind the fallen altar, where the moss grew in a pattern too deliberate for moss, his fingertips found it: a seam, a hinge, a door no wider than a coffin.

A smuggler's door. Of course. Every sacred place in the Thornfells had one; holiness and contraband had always shared the same roads.

Inside was a cell barely large enough for two men to stand, smelling of dry rot and, beneath it, something sharper. Something Zephyrine knew intimately. Bitter almond under smoke. Hemlock cut with ashroot.

Poison. Old poison. Weeks old.

"You smell that?" Corvel asked.

"I've smelled it every night for eleven years," Zephyrine said quietly. "I know its name before I know my own."

* * *

They did not have four minutes. They had three, and then boots on stone, and then the door of the chapel boomed open and lantern light carved the nave into long yellow blades.

"We know you're in there, taster." A woman's voice. Not the inquisitor who'd hunted them from Salt Roads down — someone newer, harder, with an accent from the capital's court district. "And we have your uncle."

Zephyrine went very still.

"He came to us three nights ago," the voice continued, conversational now, almost kind. "Fled the kitchens with our mark already on him. He's told us everything. Where you'd run. Which smuggler roads. He's very cooperative, your uncle. He only asks one thing." A pause, delicate as a dropped spoon. "To see you die quickly. He says he owes you that much."

Beside him, Corvel's hand found his arm. "It's a trap to make you come out."

"Everything is a trap," Zephyrine murmured. "The question is whether it's mine or theirs."

He thought about the kitchen hand. The slight man with flour permanently in the creases of his knuckles, who had worked three years beside Zephyrine without ever once meeting his eyes. Who had laced the king's honeyed figs not with enough poison to kill a king — the dose was wrong, deliberately wrong, a taster's death and no more — but with enough to kill the taster, if the taster hadn't spent a decade building immunity the way other men build muscle.

Whoever had designed that poison had needed to know exactly what Zephyrine was. Exactly what his blood could survive.

Family knew things like that.

Zephyrine pushed the coffin-door open and walked out into the lantern light with his hands empty and raised.

* * *

They bound him to the saint's own pedestal, which struck Zephyrine as either blasphemy or honesty, he couldn't decide. The inquisitor — tall, gray-eyed, her white-and-gold surcoat immaculate despite three days in the mountains — circled him slowly while two of her people dragged a figure from the treeline and threw him down among the fallen roof tiles.

Zephyrine looked at the man on the ground and felt the world tilt sideways.

Older. Thinner. Flour gone from the knuckles, replaced by inquisitor's rope. But he knew the set of those shoulders, the way the head hung as though apologizing to the earth. He'd known them across a dinner table twenty years ago, before the purges, before the Vanes scattered like seed on wind.

"Aldous," Zephyrine said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "You look terrible."

His uncle flinched as if struck. "Don't." The word cracked. "Don't use that voice. I poisoned you, boy. I sat in that kitchen for three years waiting for the order, and when it came I mixed it myself, and I measured it for *your* body, because they told me—" His breath hitched. "Because they showed me what happens to Vanes who hesitate."

The gray-eyed inquisitor watched this with the detached interest of a scholar observing an experiment. "Tell him the rest, Aldous. Tell him why."

Aldous Vane lifted his head. In the lantern light Zephyrine saw his eyes properly for the first time — his mother's eyes, worn down to the socket by something worse than age.

"The Vanes kept the sigil," Aldous said. "Your mother's line, my line, back nine generations. Sworn to the house the histories erased. We didn't just carry proof the throne is stolen, Zeph — we *were* the proof. Living, breathing, walking documents. As long as a Vane drew breath, the usurper's claim had a crack in it." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "So he decided to stop us drawing breath. Quietly. One family, one kitchen, one poison at a time. Your mother first. Then your father. Then me — except they didn't kill me. They gave me a choice. My life for yours. And they made sure I understood that refusing wouldn't save you, only prolong everyone's suffering."

"And the second half?" Corvel stepped forward from the shadows where he'd been ignored. "Where is it?"

Aldous stared at him, and something moved across his ruined face — recognition, or terror, or both. "You have his hands," he whispered. "You have the old king's hands." Then, to Zephyrine: "Under my tongue. Where a taster should know to look."

* * *

Later — much later, in the space between one heartbeat and the next that Zephyrine would spend the rest of his life remembering — he would try to reconstruct how he got free. The inquisitor's people had loosened his bonds, he thought. Had turned away. Had been, all of them, looking somewhere else at the crucial moment, in the way soldiers look elsewhere when their orders disgust them and their officer pretends not to notice.

What mattered was this: Zephyrine stood over his uncle with the man's life balanced on the edge of a stolen knife, and every year of grief in his body screamed the same single syllable.

*Do it.*

His mother. His father. Eleven years of tasting death nightly so no one would guess he was the last of a erased line. The kitchen with flour in its corners. The figs. The bitter almond. All of it converging here, on an old man's throat, and no law in any kingdom would call it murder.

"Do it," Aldous said, closing his eyes. "It's cleaner than I deserve."

And Zephyrine felt the knife hand tremble — not with reluctance, he realized, but with hunger. That frightened him more than anything the usurper had ever built. Because vengeance, he understood suddenly, was also a poison. It required the same discipline as his trade. The same careful dosing. And once you started taking it nightly, you built tolerance to the thing that made you human.

He thought of the prophecy book. *The taster who swallows truth.* Not the truth he'd expected — not the forged lineage, not the erased house. This. That justice and revenge wore the same face right up until the moment you chose between them, and the choosing was the only thing that mattered.

Zephyrine flipped the knife, caught it by the blade, and offered it hilt-first to his uncle.

"Then take it yourself," he said, "if death is what you want. I won't give it to you. I need you alive, Aldous. I need you to stand in front of the free cities' council and say what you just said to me, with their scribes writing it down. You're not the end of our family's story." He swallowed. "You're its witness. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and you're going to live long enough to understand it."

For a long moment his uncle didn't move. Then his shoulders broke — not the apology-to-the-earth slump of before, but something deeper, a collapse of twenty years of coerced silence — and Aldous Vane wept into the rubble of a roofless chapel while his nephew knelt beside him and did not touch him, because some forgiveness has to be earned at distance first.

When it was done, Zephyrine held out his hand. "Open your mouth."

Aldous opened it. Zephyrine reached under his tongue — *where a taster should know to look* — and drew out a small object wrapped in waxed silk. Unwrapped it by starlight.

Half a sigil. Its broken edge matched, precisely, the burn scar on Corvel's palm.

When he carried it toward Corvel, the halves began to sing.

Not sound, exactly — a resonance in the teeth, in the breastbone, a hum like a struck bell heard through water. The scarred palm glowed through the skin, and where Zephyrine's half hovered near it, light crawled between them like dawn deciding whether to break.

"It knows the other half," Corvel breathed. "It's been waiting—"

The gray-eyed inquisitor cleared her throat.

She had not moved. Neither had her soldiers. But her sword was sheathed, and her expression had shifted from scholar to something more complicated — a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"That light," she said slowly, "will be visible from the pass. From three camps. From everywhere." She looked at Zephyrine, and then, strangely, at Aldous, and something in her jaw tightened. "My brother died in the purges. Year three. They said he was a tax evader. He was nine." She straightened. "You have until dawn's first watch before I'm required to report this. After that, every blade in the Thornfells comes down on this valley."

Maren Quick materialized in the chapel doorway as if summoned by the mention of dawn, which, knowing Maren, she probably had been. She took in the scene — the weeping uncle, the glowing sigil, the inquisitor standing suspiciously idle — and grinned like a knife.

"Well," she said. "Looks like the family reunion's over. Everybody who's coming to change the world, on my feet. The Undervault's a day's march north, and the pass closes tomorrow night."

Behind her, far down the valley, the mountains lit briefly orange — beacon fire, signal fire, the armies talking to each other in the old language of flame.

The pass was already beginning to close.
