## 7. Where the Pass Closes

Corvel felt the pass close before he saw it.

It came as a change in the wind, a taste of ash and hot iron rolling down from the high saddle where the Thornfell peaks pinched together like a throat. Then the sound — not battle, nothing so honest, but the deep industrial groan of ballistae being winched into position and the wet thud of thousands of boots finding a single rhythm. The usurper's army had reached Greywater Col before dawn, and by midmorning the sky above the pass was black with signal smoke: *sealed*.

He stood at the tree line with Zephyrine and Maren Quick and watched the road die. Watched it happen in real time — the last merchant wagons turned back at spearpoint, the tollhouse put to the torch so no one could shelter there, the pickets stretching east and west until the mountains themselves became the only gate left in the world.

"There it is," Maren said. She spat, deliberate and slow, an old woman's benediction on everything she hated. "Greywater Col, shut tighter than a miser's fist. Twenty years I've run that pass. Storms couldn't close it. Avalanche couldn't close it. One man with a forged bloodline and forty thousand idiots, and suddenly it's shut."

"We knew they'd move fast," Zephyrine said. He was pale — Corvel noticed he was always pale now, since the chapel, since his uncle — but his voice was steady the way rope is steady, frayed and load-bearing all at once. "We knew the passes would go."

"Knowing's not walking through." Maren turned on him, and Corvel saw her take in the sigil-half wrapped at Zephyrine's wrist, then the scar on her own knuckles where she'd gripped something too hard for too long. "There's one other way. You know that. And you know why nobody uses it."

"The Undervault," Zephyrine said.

Maren laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "The Undervault. The drowned mine. The place that ate the Calloway crew whole in my grandmother's day, and the Hollis brothers after them, and every fool since who thought the water was just water." She looked at each of them in turn. "I'm not saying it's cursed because I'm stupid. I'm saying it's cursed because everyone who's gone in has stayed in."

Corvel flexed his left hand inside its wrappings. The broken half of the sigil burned there like a coal that never cooled, and had burned hotter every mile east, hotter as the army closed the passes, as if the two halves of some old truth were calling to each other through stone and water and the dark.

"How far under?" he asked.

"Half a league of flooded galleries," Maren said. "Runs beneath the col itself, old silver workings, sealed a hundred years back when the water came in. Comes up on the far side at a place called the Weeping Stair, if it comes up anywhere at all." She shook her head. "No. Find another way. Go around. Wait out the winter."

"Wait out the winter," Zephyrine repeated softly, "while the usurper burns every record, every grave, every witness between here and the free cities. While his inquisitors finish what they started with my family." His jaw set. "The council sits in six days. If we're not there—"

"I know what happens if you're not there." Maren's voice cracked like a whip. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "Gods help me. I knew your mother, boy. I've known what the Vanes carried since before you were born. And I still say no."

She said no. But she didn't walk away, and Corvel watched her stand there hating herself for the arithmetic she was already doing behind her eyes, and he understood something then about smugglers that no prophecy had bothered to mention: their loyalty wasn't to routes or cargo or coin. It was to people. To the strays they collected the way other captains collected figureheads.

* * *

The crew voted around a cookfire that night, and the vote was no.

All of them. Brannic, who'd taken an inquisitor's bolt in the shoulder for Zephyrine at the caravan raid and never once complained. Old Sefa, who read the water better than anyone Corvel had ever seen. The twins, Piet and Rho, who hadn't spoken a full sentence in his presence but had silently re-wrapped his bandaged palm twice a day as though tending it were a religious duty. All of them looked at the dark mouth of the Undervault two valleys over, looked at Maren, and said the same word.

"You asked them," Zephyrine said afterward, stunned. "You actually let them choose."

"That's the whole point of a crew, boy. If you order them into a cursed hole, you get mutiny or corpses. Usually both." Maren stared into the fire. "Three stay anyway. Me, because I'm too mean to drown. And whoever else is fool enough."

"I am," Corvel said.

Everyone looked at him. He kept looking at the fire, at the way the flames bent when the mountain wind gusted, bending but not breaking, and he heard himself say the rest of it in a voice that sounded calmer than anything inside him deserved. "The sigil halves are strongest near each other. Whatever's under that col — whatever drowned those crews — I think it's connected to what we're carrying. I think the mine was sealed for a reason, and I think the reason is us."

"That's not comforting," Sefa said.

"It's not meant to be."

Brannic stood up, wincing, hand pressed to his bandaged shoulder. "Then I'm third. Someone has to carry you two out when the water gets ideas." He grinned, and the grin cost him something — Corvel could see it did — and Corvel understood that this was what sacrifice actually looked like. Not the bright moment in songs. A man choosing to hurt, quietly, on purpose, because the person next to him mattered more than the fear.

Maren looked at her three fools and rubbed her face with both hands. "Six days," she said finally. "If you're not at the Weeping Stair in six days, I'm assuming the curse got you, and I'm telling your story wrong on purpose so it's more interesting."

* * *

They went in at first light, when the mountain's shadows were longest and even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

The Undervault's mouth was a square-cut wound in the hillside, framed in masonry older than the Spire, older than any kingdom Corvel had fragments of memory for. Above the lintel, worn nearly smooth by a century of runoff, ran an inscription. Zephyrine raised a lantern to it and read aloud, slowly, translating the old script with the archivist's care he'd learned from a dead friend.

"*Here the house that was buried shall keep its burial,*" he said. "*Till the halves remember.*"

Silence. Water dripping somewhere deep below, counting out seconds none of them wanted counted.

"That's about us," Zephyrine said. Not a question anymore. Nothing had been a question for days.

"Yeah," Corvel said. "It's about us."

They descended. The galleries sloped down through dry rock for the first hour, and then the air changed — went cold and mineral and strange — and the water began. Black, utterly still, filling the corridor from wall to wall, rising past their knees within a hundred paces, past their waists within five hundred. Brannic went first with the lantern held high, testing every step with a pole, and Corvel walked behind Zephyrine with one hand fisted in the back of the food-taster's coat, because if the current took anyone it would be the man who couldn't swim, the man whose whole body was a ledger of poisons survived and roads memorized and nothing else, no armor, no weapon, only truth.

The sigil grew hotter with every step.

By the time the water reached their chests, Corvel's palm was no longer burning. It was singing. He could feel the half wrapped at Zephyrine's wrist answering it through the flood, two notes of the same chord vibrating through the water itself, and the ripples they made were wrong — moving against them, circling them, arranging themselves in patterns too regular to be accident.

"Corvel." Zephyrine's voice was thin. "The water."

"I see it."

"Do you see—" Zephyrine stopped. Brannic stopped. Ahead of them, the flooded gallery opened into a vast chamber, a cathedral of drowned pillars, and the black water there was glowing. Faintly. From below. As if something at the bottom remembered light.

And then the visions took him.

Not gently. The sigil flared white-hot against his palm and the world inverted, and Corvel was standing in a throne room that no longer existed, banners of silver-and-green on every wall, a king with his mother's grey eyes setting the broken sigil into two hands — one of them his own, younger, unscarred. *Keep it divided,* the king said. *A whole sigil can be stolen. Divided, it must be chosen.* And then fire, always fire, the usurper's founding conflagration, archives burning, names being struck from rolls with pen-strokes as final as blades, and Corvel watched a clerk erase his own house from history while soldiers held the doors shut against screaming—

—and then the visions turned forward, which was worse.

He saw the war the usurper wanted. Saw the free cities' walls under catapult fire. Saw grain stores burning in three harbors at once, plague-carts rolling through gates that had been opened by bribed councils, the Thornfell passes running red with conscripts from lands that had never been asked. He saw the usurper on a throne built from the melted regalia of erased houses, and he saw the price of failure written in every frame: not defeat, not death, but *unmaking*. An entire century of quiet lives, of smuggler roads and tavern tables and children who'd never learned what had been taken from them, dissolved back into the lie, forever.

He came back to himself coughing floodwater, held up by Brannic's arm, Zephyrine's grip crushing his sleeve.

"What did you see?" Zephyrine demanded. "Corvel. What did you see?"

"Everything they lose if we fail." His voice came out scraped raw. He looked at the glowing water, at the drowned pillars, and understood at last why the crews had never come back up. Not the water. The *weight* of it. No one could hold this much truth and climb. "Keep moving. Six days."

They swam the cathedral of pillars in darkness, holding the lantern high, holding each other higher, and when at last the gallery tilted upward and their feet found stone again and the air came sweet with pine, Corvel turned once to look back down into the dark.

Far below, faint as a dying star, the light in the water still glowed.

Waiting, he thought, for someone else foolish enough to come and remember with it.

Then Zephyrine shouted from up-slope — joy, sharp and disbelieving — and Corvel climbed toward the sound, toward daylight, toward the Weeping Stair.

Where the light of morning fell across a figure waiting on the rocks above. Armor the color of dried blood. The white sash of the inquisitor's office.

Drawn steel catching the sun.
