## 8. Inquisitor's Mercy

The water followed us out of the Undervault like a debt collector.

Zephyrine came up the last of the flooded stairs with Corvel's arm locked in his grip, both of them streaming mine-water and shaking with cold they didn't have time to feel. Behind them, the vault's mouth breathed out its long exhalation of drowned air — iron and rot and something older, something that had watched them walk its throat for three days and let them pass.

Maren's three volunteers came after. Brannic first, hauling the rope, his shoulder wound bound in sailcloth gone brown. Then little Wren, coughing lake water out of her lungs and swearing about it with a smuggler's fluency. Last came Old Tam, who had not spoken since the visions took him somewhere past the second gallery, and whose eyes now held a look Zephyrine recognized from mirrors: the look of a man who has seen what failure costs and cannot unsee it.

The sun was coming up over the Thornfell shoulders, gray-gold and indifferent. Below them the valley opened toward the free cities — forty miles of river road, then safety, then the council, then everything.

Above them stood the inquisitor.

She had been waiting. That was the first thing he understood, standing there dripping on the shale with nothing left in him but the sigil halves — one burned into Corvel's palm, one wrapped in oilcloth against Zephyrine's ribs. She had been waiting because she did not need to chase them anymore. The vault had been the only door, and they had just walked through it.

"Inquisitor Sera Voss," Corvel said quietly. He'd heard the name whispered down the whole length of their flight, the way sailors whisper of storms.

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I've known since Saltmere."

* * *

He'd seen her twice before this morning, though she hadn't known it either time.

Once in the Spire, years ago, at the edge of a tasting hall — a young woman in gray-and-silver, standing too straight for her rank, watching the usurper's court with an expression Zephyrine had spent his life learning to read on other faces. The expression of someone cataloguing exits.

And once, three days ago, across the burning market at Saltmere, when the inquisitors put the smugglers' quarter to the torch. She had ordered the fires. Zephyrine had watched her give the order. And he had also watched her, afterward, stand alone in the ash for a long moment, holding something small — a child's shoe? a charm? — before closing her fist around it and riding on.

Hounds could grieve. That didn't make them less dangerous. But it made them something.

Now she came down the slope alone, which was itself a kind of message. No soldiers at her back. No crossbows on the ridgeline, or none visible. Her blade was drawn — a long inquisitor's saber, ceremonial and entirely functional — and her grays were travel-stained, and there were new lines around her eyes that hadn't been in the tasting hall all those years ago.

"Zephyrine Vane," she said. Her voice carried in the thin mountain air without effort. "Royal food-taster. Sworn servant of the Spire. Fugitive. Poisoner of your own king's table, by some accounts."

"By false accounts," Zephyrine said. "Though I appreciate the poetry."

"The man beside you is either the prophesied stranger or a very good forgery of one." Her blade did not waver. Neither did her eyes, which moved between them with the patience of someone reading a document line by line. "You carry the sigil of House Aurelan. Both halves. You mean to take it to the council."

It wasn't a question, so Zephyrine didn't answer it. Corvel shifted beside him — Zephyrine felt the tension come up through the grip on his arm — and said, "You've read it too. The prophecy book."

Something crossed Voss's face then, quick as a fish under ice.

"Every inquisitor reads everything," she said. "That is the job. We are the usurper's memory, when he would prefer there be none." She took another step down the shale, close enough now that Zephyrine could see the sweat at her temple despite the cold. "Page ninety-one. 'The taster who swallows truth shall carry the broken light to the council of the free.' Page ninety-four names the stranger out of empty air. Page ninety-six—" her voice caught, fractionally, and she mastered it the way Zephyrine mastered his tongue against poisons, visibly and at cost, "—page ninety-six lists what will be done to any hand that helps you. It is a very thorough book. He wrote it into law before I was born."

"He wrote his own defeat into law," Corvel said, "and called it prophecy."

"He wrote it," Voss said, "and then he spent thirty years killing everyone who might have been able to read it correctly." She stopped three paces away. The blade hung at her side now, point-down, no longer aimed. "My family could read it correctly."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way people tell things they have rehearsed alone for years.

Her grandmother had kept a scriptorium in the old city — a copyist's shop, licensed, dull, respectable. When the purges came, they came for record-keepers first, before anyone understood why. The usurper was not destroying books. He was destroying *readers*. People who knew what the old genealogies meant, who could trace a lineage back past the forged charter and see where the ink changed hands.

"They burned the shop with my grandmother inside it," Voss said. "My mother survived because she was already married and using another name. She taught me letters. She taught me never to let anyone know how well I read." A short breath, almost a laugh. "So I became an inquisitor. The safest place to hide a dangerous mind, I thought, was inside the machine that hunts them. I have spent eleven years filing reports full of deliberate errors, misdirecting raids, losing documents. Eleven years of being very good at my job in ways that looked like loyalty."

"And now?" Maren asked. Zephyrine turned — he'd almost forgotten the smugglers behind him, forgotten anyone else existed in the world but the blade and the choice it represented. Maren Quick stood with her arms folded, wind pulling at her gray-streaked hair, watching Voss the way she watched weather. "Now you've found them at last, alone, with orders to bring back their heads. Why aren't we dead yet?"

"Because of page ninety-one," Voss said.

Wren frowned. "What's on ninety-one?"

"'The hound that turns shall buy the hour with her own chains,'" Voss recited. Her voice went flat and even, the voice of someone quoting a sentence that had lived in her head so long it had worn a groove there. "'And her chains shall be lighter than her silence.'" She looked up, and met Zephyrine's eyes, and he saw that she was afraid — truly, deeply afraid — and doing it anyway. "I have read that line every night for eleven years, Master Vane, wondering if it was about me, hoping it wasn't, ashamed of hoping it wasn't. My mother died believing the machine could be slowed from inside. I have slowed it. It has not been enough. The army is six hours behind me. The pass is closed behind you. There is exactly one way left to the council, and it runs through the next two hours of whatever happens on this hillside."

"So turn," Zephyrine said. His heart was hammering and he made his voice stay level, taster-calm, the voice that had faced down kings' plates for nine years. "Turn, and ride with us."

"No." She said it gently, and that gentleness frightened him more than the saber had. "If I vanish, they send more hounds — hounds who haven't read anything but orders. If I die heroically resisting you, same. There is only one version of this where the hunt actually stops." She reversed the saber and offered it, hilt-first, across the space between them. "Arrest me."

* * *

The silence lasted long enough for the wind to change.

"You're mad," Brannic said flatly.

"I'm a page of a book," Voss answered, "and pages don't get to choose where they fall. Only whether they're read." She kept the saber extended. "Listen carefully, because I will only say this once. Take my weapon. Bind my wrists — properly; my own men will check. Leave me alive at the mouth of the vault with a note saying you took me hostage and forced me to open the pass seal. They'll believe it. I have a reputation for competence, not for mercy, and competent officers get captured, not converted. While they're dragging me back to the Spire for interrogation—" the ghost of a smile touched her mouth, "—and they *will* interrogate me, slowly, because I am valuable, and I will feed them errors the way I have fed them errors for eleven years — you will have a day's lead on a road they think is sealed. By the time they understand, you'll be inside the council walls, and the sigil will be speaking for itself."

"And what happens to you," Corvel said quietly, "when they finish understanding?"

Voss finally lowered the saber. She looked at him — really looked, the way people look at a legend they've stopped believing in and started meeting — and something in her face eased, some burden shifting to a different shoulder.

"Then I will be a footnote," she said. "In a better book than his."

Zephyrine thought of his uncle in the chapel, kneeling in candle smoke with murder in his coerced hands, and how mercy had felt like breaking a bone that needed breaking. This was that again. The universe, apparently, intended to keep teaching him the same lesson until he learned it properly: that justice outside the law still required you to look at people — all people, even the ones sent to kill you — as more than obstacles.

He stepped forward and took the saber. It was heavier than it looked.

"Inquisitor Voss," he said formally, "you are under arrest. In the name of the free cities, whose law outranks every crown." He said the words and felt them settle into truth as he spoke them — not a performance, not a fiction. A fact being born.

Her wrists, when he bound them, were steady. Her pulse, when his fingers brushed it, was not. He tied the knots well anyway. Craft mattered. Respect mattered.

Maren was already moving, snapping orders — Wren to scout the road south, Tam and Brannic to rig the hostage-note and a credible scene, herself to stand over Voss with a drawn knife and practice looking menacing, at which she needed no practice. The machinery of escape spun up around them, and hope, which had been a candle in a draft all this way, suddenly had walls around it. A plan. Hours. A chance.

Corvel crouched beside Zephyrine as he finished the last knot, and spoke low enough that only Zephyrine heard.

"She's giving up everything. For a prophecy. For strangers." His palm — the half-burned palm — flexed once. "Why does everyone keep doing that?"

"Because it's contagious," Zephyrine said, and discovered he was smiling, tired and cold and certain. "Come on. We've got a day's lead and forty miles, and when we reach the council—"

Behind them, down the valley road, dust rose against the dawn. Not six hours. Not two. An outrider, moving fast, already close.

Voss saw it too, and laughed — a real laugh, brief and bright and utterly fearless.

"Well," she said. "You'd better run."
