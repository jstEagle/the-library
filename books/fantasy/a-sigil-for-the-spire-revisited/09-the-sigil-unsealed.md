## 9. The Sigil Unsealed

**Zephyrine**

The free cities had built their council hall the way a merchant builds a fortress: beautiful enough to be underestimated, thick enough to survive what came through the gates. Zephyrine stood in its eastern gallery with Corvel at his shoulder and Maren Quick's smugglers fanned along the balconies like crows who'd decided, for once, to roost indoors.

Below them, in the round of white stone where the council sat, the usurper's herald finished reading.

"—and His Radiant Majesty, Aldric the Second of That Name, offers clemency to any city that opens its gates before moonrise. Those that refuse will be remembered as rebels. Their names will be struck from every ledger. Their children will be raised ignorant of them. This is mercy."

Zephyrine's mouth did what it always did when he heard poison described as mercy: it watered.

He knew the herald's voice from the Spire. Serle Vosk had tasted wine with him a dozen times, laughing about the vintage while soldiers sharpened things in the courtyards below. Now Vosk stood on council marble in cloth-of-gold, and behind him, visible through the great doors, the usurper's army filled the terraced streets — ten thousand men in ash-grey, torches already lit against the dusk they'd brought with them like luggage.

Corvel's palm was bleeding again. Zephyrine could smell it under the incense — copper and something older, something that smelled the way lightning smells before it lands.

"It's time," Corvel said.

"I know."

"You keep saying you know and then standing very still."

"That's how I do everything important." Zephyrine turned. In the gallery's shadow, the three smugglers Maren had kept — Brannic with his crossbow, the twins Osla and Fen with knives and no patience — watched him with the particular attention of people deciding whether to follow a man off a cliff. "When I step out there, everything I am becomes evidence. The taster. The Vane. The poison in my blood and the sigil in it. There's no road after this one. You understand?"

Brannic spat over the rail, ceremonially, toward the army outside. "We understood at the Undervault, boy. We're just here for the view."

Osla grinned. "And the looting."

"And the looting," Brannic conceded.

Maren wasn't among them. She was holding the south gate with the rest of the crew, buying the hour it had taken to get here, and Zephyrine carried her last words like a coin in his shoe: *My mother kept your mother's secrets. Finish the job.*

He looked at Corvel. The man who had fallen out of empty air four days ago looked back — hollow-eyed, half-remembered, the burned half-sigil in his hand glowing faintly through the bandages now, as if impatient.

"Together," Zephyrine said.

"Together," Corvel agreed, and then, because he was still somehow himself underneath whatever the prophecy had made him: "If this kills us, I want it noted I was right about the shortcut."

"The shortcut nearly drowned us."

"Noted for both sides."

They went down the stairs into the light.

* * *

**Corvel**

The council chamber was a bowl of faces, and every face turned when they entered — the usurper's herald mid-peroration, twelve councilors on their dais, and beyond the open doors, ten thousand soldiers who could not hear a word spoken at this distance but could certainly see two filthy men walk onto the marble uninvited.

Serle Vosk broke off. Recognition moved across his face like weather.

"The taster," he said softly. Then, louder, pitched for the room: "Councilors, behold. A fugitive servant and a nameless vagrant interrupt the Majesty's generosity. Seize—"

"His Radiant Majesty," Zephyrine said, and his voice carried the way voices carry in rooms built for negotiation, clean and cold, "has a forged claim. And I have proof. Would the council like to see it, or would they prefer to be surrendered by their own herald before they've voted?"

Silence. The kind with weight.

One of the councilors — an old woman with a moneylender's squint, whose name Corvel didn't know but whose rings said she mattered — leaned forward. "Proof," she repeated. "Of what, exactly?"

"That Aldric is no Aldric. That the house he claims descends from was erased — records burned, names struck, bloodline hunted — because the true line survived somewhere no one thought to look." Zephyrine lifted his right hand. The sigil-half scarred into his palm caught the lamplight, dark lines gone silver. "Every Vane carried half. Sworn keepers. My mother died keeping it, and I swallowed poisons for six years so no one would ever think to look at the food-taster's hands."

Vosk laughed. It was a good laugh; Corvel could see why the usurper used him. "A kitchen servant's scar. Councilors, is this what rebellion has come to? Palm-reading?"

"Then look at mine," Corvel said.

He unwound the bandage.

The half-sigil burned into his palm was awake — it had been awake since the Undervault, since the halves had first sung to each other through forty feet of drowned stone — and when he held it up beside Zephyrine's, the resonance became visible. Light crawled between their hands like a thread seeking its other end. Not firelight. Not sorcery as the crowd would name it. Something older and more patient: recognition.

The old councilor stood up.

"That mark," she said. Her voice had changed. Moneylender's squint or not, she suddenly looked like a woman doing arithmetic across a century. "I saw that mark once. On documents my grandmother hid during the purges. Documents she died for." She pointed at the joined halves. "That is the seal of House Aurelian. The true line. The line Aldric's grandfather burned out of every archive in the Spire."

"Grandmother's stories," Vosk said, but the laughter had thinned.

"Then let the story testify," Corvel said, and took Zephyrine's hand.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

He had expected pain. He had braced for it the way he braced for every dish that might kill him — breath held, body made ready to be a battlefield.

There was no pain. There was only *opening*, as if the two halves of the sigil were not seals but doors, and they had been waiting a hundred years for someone to push them both at once.

Light went up out of their clasped hands like a well reversing.

It did not burn. It *revealed*. Zephyrine felt it pass through him, through the marble, through the walls, out into the terraced streets where ten thousand soldiers stood — and everywhere it touched, it showed truth. He felt it leave him and understood, with the strange clarity of the poisoned body finally surrendering, that this was the thing his bloodline had been keeping all along. Not a symbol. A witness.

Above the council chamber, in the air over both armies, the light assembled itself into shapes everyone could read.

A lineage, written in fire that cast no heat: House Aurelian, seven generations tall, ending in a final name that hung above the city like a star refusing to set.

*C O R V E L.*

Beside it, in letters that guttered and blackened as the light examined them, the usurper's claimed descent — and then the forgery itself, exposed line by line. Altered dates. A marriage record rewritten in a different ink than the rest of the page. A dead child's name borrowed and worn like a stolen coat. The light held each fraud up for the whole city to see, patiently, thoroughly, the way Zephyrine had once held a spoonful of suspect broth to the lamp.

In the chamber, Vosk had stopped speaking entirely.

Outside, ten thousand soldiers were looking up at the sky over their own camp, and the sky was telling them their king was a counterfeit.

"He's lying," Vosk whispered. Then louder, finding his voice: "Trickery! Sorcery! This is exactly the—"

From the doors behind him came the sound Corvel would later describe as the loudest quiet Zephyrine had ever heard: the usurper herself entering the council hall, ash-grey cloak, iron crown, flanked by her personal guard. Queen Aldric the Second of That Name — and the sigil-light, reaching her, did not accuse. It simply showed. Above her head, briefly, honestly, it displayed the plain record of who she actually was: a warlord's granddaughter from a minor house, clever and ruthless, who had found an empty throne and a gap in the archives and stepped into both.

She looked up at her own unmasking the way a chess player looks at a board after a blunder.

Then she did the thing Zephyrine had known she would do, because he had spent years learning how power behaves when cornered.

"Burn them," she said quietly, to her guard captain. "All of them. The council, the taster, the pretender. Burn the hall if you must."

The guard captain — a grey-bearded veteran whose face Zephyrine had seen at court a hundred times, always behind someone else's shoulder — did not move.

"Captain," the usurper said.

"Highness." His voice was steady and terribly sorry. "My men can see the sky from here too."

And there it was, rolling through the open doors and down the terraced streets: not a battle cry, not a riot, just ten thousand soldiers simultaneously declining. Spears grounding. Torches being passed backward, away, handed off until they sputtered on wet stone. The usurper's army dissolving not into rebellion but into *witnesses* — men who had come to take a city and instead found themselves attending a funeral for a lie.

The usurper looked around the hall at her own guard, at the council, at the light still burning above Corvel's name, and Zephyrine watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had arrived at years ago in a tasting chair: when the dose fails, there is nothing left but the taste of it.

"Archives can be rewritten again," she said. It was almost gentle. Almost admiring. "You know that, taster. Paper burns."

"So do kings," Zephyrine said. "But paper comes back. Ask your own clerks — they're the ones who taught me that a forged lineage is just a confession you haven't been caught in yet."

Her hand went toward the knife at her belt—

—and stopped, because Corvel had stepped forward, into the light, and the whole hall saw the true heir of House Aurelian look at the woman who had hunted his family's ghost across a century and choose, visibly, deliberately, not hatred.

"You'll stand trial," Corvel said. "Here. Before the cities. Not the crown — there is no crown. The council will judge what you did, and I will not ask them for your death, and I will not ask them for your pardon either. You'll live inside the truth now. Same as the rest of us."

The light began to fade, the lineage dissolving upward like smoke returning to its fire, and in the last of it Zephyrine saw Corvel's face — the man with no memory of how he'd come, carrying half a broken seal in his skin — and realized the prophecy's last page had never named a king.

It had named a stranger. What he did with the name was his own.

The usurper's blade clattered to the marble. Whether it was surrender or simply a hand that had stopped believing in itself, no one in the hall would ever quite agree — and that argument, Zephyrine suspected, would fill taverns for a generation.

Then Maren Quick's voice came down from the south gate, raw and triumphant, carried runner by runner into the chamber:

"Gates are open! Passes are clear! Somebody tell the strays dinner's on!"

Corvel looked at Zephyrine. Zephyrine looked at Corvel.

"Well," Zephyrine said. "You heard the captain."
