## 10. A Table Set for Strays

The council chamber smelled of cedar and old arguments, and for the first time in my life I was not afraid of a room full of powerful people.

I should have been. Half the free cities' delegates had arrived armed, the other half arrived having already decided to be insulted, and between them sat the usurper's herald with a scroll of surrender terms he clearly expected to read aloud before lunch. The high windows threw bars of light across the floor like the bars of a cage nobody had bothered to lock yet.

Corvel stood at the center of it all, wearing a borrowed coat that fit him badly, holding half of a sigil that fit him perfectly.

"You're asking us to believe," said the delegate from Saltmere, a woman with a voice like gravel poured slowly, "that this man is the last heir of House Aurelian. That the throne in the Spire has been occupied for thirty years by a forgery. On the testimony of a *food taster*."

"On the evidence," Corvel said, "of your own eyes."

He looked at me. It was such a small thing, that look. We had rehearsed nothing. There had been no time, and besides, we both knew how it would go: some things you cannot rehearse, you can only survive them once.

I stepped forward and set my half of the sigil on the table beside his.

"House Vane kept this for six generations," I said. "My mother kept it under the hearthstone of a house that was burned for keeping it. My uncle kept it inside his own skin rather than give it up. I kept it in my blood, because the Vanes swore an oath not to a crown but to the truth underneath whatever crown happened to be sitting on it." I let my gaze travel the room. "I have tasted poison for a living for eleven years. I know what things are made of. This—" I touched the two halves where they lay, cold metal warming at my fingertips, resonating faintly like a struck bell remembered rather than heard, "—is true."

"And if we join them?" the Saltmere delegate said. "What then? A new king? A war?"

"No," said Corvel.

And here is what I will remember until I die, the moment I would keep even if every other memory were stripped from me: he did not hesitate. He did not look at the throne-shaped silence in the room, did not weigh it, did not let it weigh him. He picked up both halves of the sigil, pressed them together above the table, and the light came.

It was not fire and it was not thunder. It was more terrible than either. It was *clarity* — the light ran out through the chamber and through the open doors into the square beyond, where the usurper's army stood in ranks and the free cities' levies stood opposite them, and it showed everyone, all at once, the same thing: the forged lineage, the ink still wet on documents three decades old, the erased house, the erased people, the small neat murders that had built a dynasty out of someone else's name.

In the square, I am told, ten thousand soldiers saw the truth and lowered their spears. In the chamber, the usurper's herald drew steel and shouted orders that no one obeyed, and then sat down heavily, like a man who has just watched the floor of the world be pulled out from under him and found sky beneath.

"There is no king," Corvel said quietly, into the ringing silence. "There never needed to be one. There's a council, and there's a reckoning, and there's work. I decline the crown. All of it. Every version anyone's offering me." He turned the joined sigil over in his palm — the palm that had carried its broken half burned into the skin since the night he fell through midnight onto a mountain road. As we watched, the burn faded, leaving only unmarked skin, as though the metal had finally finished saying what it came to say. He smiled, crooked and tired. "I've been somebody's weapon once already. I'd rather be nobody's anything and my own man."

The Saltmere delegate studied him for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, rough, surprised out of her.

"Well," she said. "That's the first honest thing anyone's said in this chamber in a century."

* * *

They gave Inquisitor Sera Voss her amnesty on the third day, in front of witnesses, which is the only kind of amnesty worth having.

She took it standing, in her grey coat, hands visible, blade surrendered — she had insisted on surrendering it publicly, said the symbolism mattered, said she'd spent nine years making the law a thing people flinched at and she intended to spend the rest of her life teaching it to hold still and tell the truth. She read the prophecy book into the record herself, the whole thing, including the line about the stranger out of empty air, while Corvel sat in the gallery looking deeply embarrassed.

Afterward I found her on the council steps, watching the city go about its business in the pale spring light.

"They'll want you back," she said, without turning. "The Spire. They'll want the man who swallowed truth for them for eleven years to come swallow something else. Ambassador. Chancellor. Something with a seal on it."

"I know."

"You'll say no."

"I already have."

She glanced at me then, and there was something almost soft in the hard lines of her face. "What will you do instead?"

I told her. Her eyebrows went up. Then, slowly, she grinned — the first time I'd ever seen her do it, and it transformed her entirely, made her look like a girl playing hooky from an execution.

"A tavern," she said. "You fought your way through assassins, curses, a drowned mine, and an army, and you're going to open a *tavern*."

"Every dish tested," I said. "None poisoned. That's the whole philosophy. You'd be welcome, Voss. Any day. Any table."

She looked out at the street again. Somewhere below, children were shouting about something; a cart rattled past; life continued, unpoisoned, ordinary, miraculous.

"Give me a month to put the order in order," she said. "Then I'll come taste whatever you're pouring."

* * *

Maren Quick helped me find the building, because Maren Quick could find anything that floated, rolled, or stood still long enough to be useful.

"It used to be a tollhouse," she said, kicking the door open onto a low room with a stone hearth and a view straight down the valley road toward the Thornfell passes. "Smugglers used to pay off the tollman here before the pass closed. Good bones. Bad roof. Terrible ghost stories, which is excellent for trade." She walked the room like she was pacing a ship's deck, hands behind her back, appraising. "Your mother stood right about there once. Told me if anything ever happened to her, find the boy, teach him the salt roads."

"You never told me that part."

"You never asked the right questions. That's a taster's failing — always tasting what's in front of you, never asking what's under the table." She thumped the wall twice, satisfied. "We'll need barrels. And a sign. What are you calling it?"

I hadn't decided. I'd been carrying the question around for weeks like a stone in my shoe. But standing in that room, with the mountain wind coming through the broken shutters and Maren waiting with her arms crossed and her whole skeptical heart showing, the answer arrived so simply I laughed out loud.

"The Vanes' Rest," I said. "No. Wait." My mother's voice in my head, dry as ever: *a Vane keeps what matters, boy.* "The Keeping Room."

Maren considered it. "Sounds like somewhere you'd hide a sigil."

"That's the idea."

* * *

We opened in autumn, when the passes were still clear but the first snows were being discussed in the tones people use for weather they don't trust.

Corvel came down from the mountains the week before, hauling timber for the benches on a mule cart, refusing help, enjoying the refusal. He'd taken a cottage on the ridge — close enough to see the road, far enough that no one could make him a symbol without climbing. Delegates wrote to him anyway. He answered perhaps one letter in ten, always the same way: *Take it to the council. That's what it's for.* He kept the joined sigil on his mantel, doing nothing, being itself, which he said was the most honest job it had ever held.

Uncle Tam came too. That took longer to say than to happen.

He arrived on a rainy evening with a bundle of his belongings and a face like a man presenting himself for sentencing, and I stood in my own doorway and discovered that mercy is not a single decision you make once in a mountain chapel — it is a decision you make again every morning, smaller each time, easier each time, until one day it isn't a decision at all, it's just breakfast. I handed him an apron. He cried into it, briefly, and then became, over the following months, the finest bread-baker on the mountain road, as though twenty years of guilt had been fermenting in him all along, waiting for flour.

Voss arrived exactly one month after the amnesty, hung her sword on the peg by the door — where it stayed, gathering honest dust — and took up permanent residence at the corner table, where she drank small beer, corrected everyone's grammar, and slowly learned to laugh.

The strays found us the way strays always do: by rumor, by road, by the smell of tested food drifting down the valley. A taster dismissed from some petty lord's table who'd been dosed one time too many and survived. Smugglers between runs, Maren's people mostly, paying in stories and odd cargo. A runaway archivist with a satchel of banned books she was very slowly, page by page, copying into legitimate circulation. Two of the usurper's former soldiers, young and hollow-eyed, whom Voss interviewed at the door for an hour before waving them in with the words "they're done being that, let them start being this."

House rules were simple, chalked above the hearth in my own hand:

*Everything is tested. No one poisons anyone. Strays eat free.*

Corvel carved those rules into a plank of Thornfell pine himself, and we hung it where the light from the west window caught it at sundown, so that every evening the words glowed like they'd been written in the same light that had unmade a false throne.

On the night of our first winter closing-in, when the snow finally came down thick and soft and the road emptied, I stood at the bar and looked at my kingdom.

Tam pulling loaves from the oven. Voss arguing geography with a smuggler captain who'd sailed rivers that didn't exist. The runaway archivist reading aloud to anyone who'd listen, which was everyone. Maren dealing cards with marked decks she pretended weren't marked, cheating cheerfully and losing anyway. And Corvel at the corner table, no crown anywhere within fifty miles, laughing at something — really laughing, the laugh of a man who fell out of nowhere and landed, against all odds, in a family.

Eleven years I swallowed poison so that a liar could eat safely. Now I fed the truth to strangers for coin and kindness, and no one at any table had anything to fear.

A Vane keeps what matters. Turns out what matters was never a sigil.

The door banged open on a gust of snow and lamplight — a travel-stained figure in the gap, stamping cold from their boots, blinking at the warmth, at the full tables, at the chalked rules glowing over the hearth. Everyone looked up.

I reached for a clean spoon, and smiled.

"Evening, stranger," I called. "Sit anywhere. Everything's tested. Nothing's poisoned. First bowl's on the house."
