## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The market of Hollowvale smelled of wet stone and tallow and, faintly, of other people's lives. Rosalind Winterbourne had learned to read that last scent the way a hound reads blood: the sweetish tang of a wedding remembered too often, the copper bite of grief still wet, the dry papery smell of memories so old they had begun to crumble at their edges. Tonight the air carried all three, and beneath them something new—something scorched, like paper held too long above a candle.

She moved through the stalls with her hood up and her hands tucked in her sleeves, a thin woman of no particular color, the kind of person eyes slid over and minds declined to keep. Invisibility was not a gift in Hollowvale. It was a trade, and Rosalind had paid for it in full.

"Memories bought, memories mended, memories sold," droned the crier at the market gate, a fat man with a voice like a bell full of honey. "Gentlefolk, the winter's dark is long. Fill it with something lovely that never happened to you."

The stalls glowed with their wares. Glass vials of first snowfall. A jar of a mother's voice singing, sold by the spoonful to orphans who could not afford the whole song. An old soldier's last battle, going cheap because no one in a magic-dead valley wanted to remember what magic could do.

Rosalind did not buy. She never bought. Buying left a trail, a ledger, a name. She took.

The dying stranger sat in the shadow of the tannery wall, where the market's lanterns gave out and the mountain's cold began. He had chosen his spot well—she would have chosen it herself—but he had made the mistake of the very sick, which was to stop watching his own hands. His coin purse sat in his lap like a sleeping bird. His eyes were half-closed, and his lips moved around words with no voice left to carry them.

Rosalind crouched beside him with the practiced sympathy of a woman offering help.

"Water," he managed.

She gave him water. It was not kindness, or not only kindness. The dying drank, and the drinking loosened them, and what lived behind their eyes came loose with everything else. She set two fingers, light as moth wings, against his temple, and she *pulled*.

It was like drawing thread from a spindle. His memories came to her in a rush of heat: a road of white gravel under a sky with too many stars. A tower. A tower that was not a tower but a *spire*, black and jointed like the leg of some drowned god, standing in a ring of stones that hummed. Fire that did not burn—fire that *wrote*, crawling across a map in lines of gold, tracing a path from the mountains down, down, into the valley where she stood. And a name, spoken in a voice like a struck bell, spoken over him by someone whose face was a blur of white robes:

*Heir of Vael. Carry it. Carry it or it dies with the blood.*

Rosalind snatched her hand back as if the man's temple had been a stove.

He opened his eyes. For a moment he looked straight at her, and she felt the terrible, vertiginous sensation of being *seen*—truly seen, catalogued, kept. His mouth shaped a word. It might have been *found*. It might have been her name, though no one in Hollowvale knew her name.

Then his eyes went soft and empty, the way eyes did when she finished, and his head tipped against the tannery wall, and the market noise flowed over him like water over a stone.

Her hands were shaking. That was new. In eleven years of taking, her hands had never shaken.

She lifted the coin purse—it was the work of a heartbeat, the work she had done a thousand times—and slipped into the crowd, and the crowd, bless its incurious heart, parted around her like she was smoke.

* * *

She did not open the purse until she was home, in the rented room above the chandler's shop, with the door barred and the candle shuttered to a blade of light. Hollowvale had no wards. Hollowvale had nothing—the whole valley was a dead zone, a wound in the world's magic where the Order's scrying failed and mages' spells guttered out like wet wicks. That was why she had come here. That was why everyone with something to hide came here. The valley forgot you, and the world forgot the valley.

She poured the purse out onto the table. Coins, mostly—silver, a few gold, enough to buy her passage over the pass and a season of silence beyond it. And a scrap of leather, folded into a square no bigger than her palm.

She unfolded it, and it caught fire.

Not with heat. With *light*. Lines of gold crawled up from the leather like vines finding a trellis, and she knew them, knew them the way you know a face from a dream—because she *did* know them, because they were already in her head, spilled there by a dying man's last pull of memory. The map showed the valley, the pass, the white road, and at its heart a spire drawn in strokes that pulsed like a heartbeat.

*Heir of Vael.*

"It isn't mine," she said aloud, to the candle, to the shuttered dark. "You gave it to the wrong woman."

But the map burned on, patient as a debt, and somewhere beneath her ribs, in the place where she kept what she stole, something answered it. Something that had not been there an hour ago. A small, locked room in her mind, and behind its door, a name being spoken over and over, and the name was hers, and the name was not hers, and both things were true.

She thought of throwing the map in the chandler's furnace. She thought of it for a long time. She was still thinking of it when the knocking started.

* * *

Three knocks. Even. Unhurried. The knock of someone who had never in their life been kept waiting.

Rosalind went still, the way she had taught herself to go still as a child, when stillness meant safety. Below, the chandler's dog—half-wolf, suspicious of everything—did not bark. That was worse. That was much, much worse.

She moved to the window and put her eye to the shutter's seam.

Two figures stood in the street below, and the street bent around them the way a crowd parts around a funeral. They wore grey, unrelieved grey, cloaks and hoods and gloves, and on each breast was stitched a circle with a sliver cut from it: the Thirteenth Moon, the moon that was never allowed to fill. They did not knock at doors. They did not speak to the chandler, who stood in his doorway with his face gone the color of tallow. They simply stood, and the street stood with them, and the whole market's noise seemed to have been swallowed.

One of them lifted its head. Even at this distance, even through wood and seam and shadow, Rosalind felt the gaze pass over her window, hesitate, and move on.

*The valley is dead,* she told herself. *No magic crosses the borders. They can't scry here. They can't find—*

The grey figure turned. Not toward her window. Toward the tannery wall. Toward the shadow where a dying man had sat with a coin purse in his lap and a burning name in his mouth.

They would find him. They would find the place where he had been, and they would find the shape of what had been taken from him, because a theft left a hole, and a hole had edges, and edges could be read. She had never thought of it that way in eleven years. She thought of it that way now, with her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

The second figure produced something small and pale—a slip of paper, a list, a warrant—and held it up to the lantern light. And though Rosalind could not see what was written on it, she saw the chandler read it. She saw his eyes lift, slow and terrible, to the window of the room above his shop.

She had lived in Hollowvale for six years, and in that time the chandler had never once looked at her window. No one had. That was the whole arrangement, the whole quiet covenant of the valley: *we do not see you, you do not exist, everyone stays safe.*

He saw her now.

Rosalind took the map, took the coins, took the small locked room in her mind with its name that was and was not hers, and began, very quietly, to pack.

Below, the grey figures started up the stairs.
