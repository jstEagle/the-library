## 2. Three Against the Thirteenth Moon

The passes above Hollowvale were carved by water and time into a throat of gray stone, and Rosalind climbed them in the dark with the stranger's memories burning behind her eyes.

She had left before dawn, taking only what she could carry: bread gone hard as slate, a skin of well water, her mother's knife. The valley's magic-dead silence clung to her like a wet coat for the first mile, that familiar nothing pressing against her gift, and then—somewhere past the standing stone that marked the border—the world opened. She felt it like a sound after deafness: the low hum of living magic in the grasses, in the wind, in the distant glitter of frost on high rock. It made her teeth ache. It made the stranger's memories stir.

They were not quiet. Stolen memories never were at first; they sat in the mind like stones dropped in a pond, rippling outward until she learned their edges. But these had edges like knife-work. A corridor of white marble. Thirteen moons carved into a door. A woman's voice saying *heir* in a tone half reverence, half sentence of death. And always the map, drawn in light behind his eyelids: the Spire of Vael rising from a caldera in the far north, its peak crowned with something that glowed even through the filter of a dying man's recollection.

She stopped at midmorning to eat and found her hands shaking. Not from cold. From the memory of the hunters' faces in the market—two figures in ash-gray cloaks asking after a man who had died in an alley with his hand pressed to hers. *A stranger. Dying. Did he say anything? Give anyone anything?* She had lied so smoothly it frightened her. Twenty years of being no one had made her a master of nothing, and nothing, it turned out, was a very useful thing to be.

Until now.

Now there was a name stitched into borrowed thought—*heir of Vael*—and names, unlike coin purses, could not be fenced or forgotten. The Order would come to the valley eventually. They would ask sharper questions. And Rosalind Winterbourne, who had spent her whole life invisible, would become the most visible woman in the world.

She walked faster.

* * *

The archive at Greyhollow was less a building than a wound—a library half-swallowed by the mountain, its shelves spilling down scree slopes, its books weathered to the color of bone. Cassian Vey lived in the deepest alcove, where the frost couldn't reach, surrounded by ledgers no one had wanted in thirty years.

Rosalind knew him by reputation only. The exiled archivist. The man who had catalogued the Order's own histories and been cast out for noticing what they said.

"You're not a scholar," he said when she found him, without looking up from a page. He was thin and gray-templed, with ink stains up both forearms like old bruises. "Scholars knock. You have the look of someone who steals."

"I need to know about Vael."

That got his attention. He set down his pen with exaggerated care, the way a man disarms himself. "No one needs to know about Vael. That is rather the point of Vael."

"The Spire. The sigil. What it costs to claim it." She crouched so their eyes were level. "I've seen it. In someone else's head."

Something flickered across his face—not greed, exactly, but the hunger of a man who has spent decades starving for exactly this. "Whose?"

"A dying man's. The Order wants it back. Or wants me dead for having it. I genuinely don't know which."

Cassian rose, joints cracking like green wood, and began pulling volumes from the shelves with sudden purpose. "Then you'd better sit," he said, "because the sigil of Vael doesn't grant power, girl. It *is* power—all of it, every working ever woven, compressed into a single mark. To claim it is to burn out everything you are and be refilled. Every stolen memory you carry—" he glanced at her sharply, "—and I'd wager my last book you carry many—would be kindling. That's the cost. You don't spend coin. You spend yourself."

Outside, far below in the valley haze, Rosalind saw it: a thread of riders on the north road, cloaks the gray of woodsmoke.

"Can you travel?" she asked.

"Can I—" He blinked at her. "You're inviting me? You don't know me."

"I know you'd rather die than let them burn those books. So do I. Come on."

He came. He brought eleven volumes and complained about the weight of every one.

* * *

Wren did not want to be found. That was the first thing Rosalind learned about the child.

They caught up to her two days later, in the high pass where the pines gave way to bare granite—a small figure huddled in a hollow of rock, maybe nine years old, hair cropped raggedly short, watching them with eyes that shimmered faintly silver when the light hit wrong. Mage-child. The Order's word for such children was simpler: *unregistered*. Their word for what happened next was simpler still: *correction*.

"I'm not going back," Wren said, before either of them spoke. Her voice was steady but her hands, Rosalind noticed, were trembling around a slingshot held like a sword.

"We're not taking you anywhere," Rosalind said. "We're running the same direction."

"People say that." The silver flared. "People say lots of things."

Behind her, Cassian lowered himself painfully onto a boulder and opened one of his books, as though they had all the time in the world. "In 612, the Order corrected forty-one unregistered children at Thornmere," he said mildly, to the page. "There is a record. I copied it before they burned the original. If you come with us, I will teach you to read, and then no one can ever tell you what happened to people like you without your knowing whether they lie."

Wren stared at him. Then at Rosalind. Then, slowly, she uncoiled from her hollow like a bird deciding a branch was safe.

"You steal things," she said to Rosalind. It wasn't a question. "I can smell it on you. Old thoughts. Someone died near you."

Rosalind's chest went cold. "Yes."

"Good," said Wren. "Maybe you'll steal mine someday. They're heavy."

Nobody answered that. Nobody knew how.

* * *

*Interlude — Wren*

Wren counted the grown-ups' breathing at night to make sure they didn't stop.

The tall ink man breathed slow, like a bellows with a leak. The thief woman breathed like she was hiding from the sound itself—shallow, careful, ready to go silent if anyone came looking. Wren understood that kind of breathing. She used to breathe like that in the cellar under the millhouse, before the men in gray found her and she ran.

The thief woman dreamed loud. Wren could feel it leaking off her like heat off a hearthstone—white corridors, a burning map, thirteen moons. Once, in the dark, the woman whispered a name that wasn't hers, and Wren lay very still and pretended to sleep, because some doors you don't knock on even when they're glowing.

But here was the thing Wren knew and didn't say: the dreams were getting stronger. And whatever was inside the thief woman was waking up.

* * *

On the fifth night, the dreams stopped being the stranger's and started being instructions.

Rosalind woke on her feet at the edge of camp, frost in her lashes, her mouth full of words she hadn't chosen: *the caldera gate opens on the thirteenth turn. The heir enters alone or not at all.* Below her, the northern horizon held a smudge of light that might have been dawn and might have been the Spire itself, patient as a lit fuse.

"You walked in your sleep again." Cassian stood wrapped in his cloak, watching her with tired, kind eyes. "You said the same thing you said last night. And the night before."

"It's using me to navigate." She hugged herself against the cold. "It's teaching me how to get there. Cassian—if I claim it, everything goes. Everyone I've ever taken a memory from. Everything I've taken. All of it burns to feed the sigil. Including—" her voice caught, and she hated it, "—including whatever's happened since I met you. Since we found her."

He was quiet a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the rocks.

"You've already decided," he said.

"I haven't decided anything."

"You decided when you woke us instead of walking on alone. People who mean to claim absolute power don't wake their witnesses." He turned back toward the fire, where Wren slept curled around her slingshot like a talisman. "Get some rest, Rosalind. Whatever you choose—we'll be there."

*We*, he'd said. As if it were settled. As if three fugitives on a mountainside were a thing that existed, a thing with a name.

Rosalind looked at the sleeping child, the shabby archivist, the small stubborn fire against all that dark—and felt, for the first time in twenty years, the terrifying weight of being *seen*.

At first light, they crested the final pass, and the caldera opened below them like a cupped hand.

And in the cup, ringing it, camped the entire Order of the Thirteenth Moon.
