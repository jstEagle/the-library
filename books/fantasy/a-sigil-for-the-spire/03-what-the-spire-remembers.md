## 3. What the Spire Remembers

Cassian had catalogued ten thousand ruins in his years with the Archive, and he had learned that every one of them kept a kind of weather. The aqueducts of Sorrel Keep sweated salt. The drowned library at Meremont exhaled the smell of old ink whenever the tide went out. But nothing in his careful, cross-referenced life had prepared him for the weather of the Spire of Vael.

It remembered.

That was the only word for it. As they climbed the last ridge on the eve of the thirteenth turn, the air around the mountain grew thick and particular, like walking into a room where someone had just been weeping. The stone of the pass was warm under his boots though frost silvered the grass beside it. The wind came down off the peak carrying scents that had no business existing: candle smoke, rain on hot slate, a woman's perfume gone to ghost, bread from an oven cold a thousand years. The Spire did not simply stand above them. It *recalled* them, everything that had ever happened beneath it, and it let the memory leak out like light through a cracked shutter.

"Stop smelling it," Rosalind said, not unkindly. She had gone pale since dawn, and she kept one hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding something shut inside her. "You look like you're falling in love with a mountain."

"I am taking notes," Cassian said, and patted the satchel where his salvaged notebooks lived—the whole ruined archive of him, the thing he'd been exiled for knowing too well. "A man should fall in love accurately."

Wren laughed from somewhere ahead, high and bright, and the sound bounced back off the rocks changed—older, deeper, a laugh in another voice—and Cassian saw Rosalind flinch as if the echo had touched her.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen pillar carved all over with names. Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, curled against Rosalind's side like a comma against a word, and for a while neither of the adults spoke. The fire was small. The Spire's weather pressed close, warm and grieving.

"Cassian." Rosalind's voice was very quiet. He had learned her silences by now—the silence before she stole something, the silence after she gave something back—but this one was new. This one sounded like a door being unlocked from the inside. "There's something I should have told you at the valley's edge. I told myself I'd tell you a hundred times."

He waited. Above them, through the thinning trees, the Spire caught the moonlight—not the twelve moons they'd been born under but a thirteenth, faint and wrong, a sliver of light that existed nowhere else in the sky. Tomorrow night it would reach its fullness. Everyone who had ever written about Vael agreed on that much, and on almost nothing else.

"The sigil isn't just power," she said. "The stranger's memories showed me. It doesn't sit in you when you claim it. It *replaces* you. Every borrowed thing goes back to its owner. Every stolen memory returns." Her hand was still at her chest. "All of them, Cassian. Everything I've ever taken. And everything I—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I didn't only steal from travelers. In Hollowvale, when the loneliness got bad, I took things from people I loved. Little things. A first snowfall. A mother's voice singing. I sold most of them back, but some I kept, because keeping them was the only way I knew how to be a person."

"Wren," he said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "You have memories of Wren. Of me."

"I have everything." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And if I claim the sigil, it takes them. All of it. I would stand there holding the power to end wars and I wouldn't know why my hands were shaking. I wouldn't know either of you. I'd be full of fire and empty of everything that ever mattered, and I'd call that victory because I wouldn't remember enough to call it anything else."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, far down the dark mountainside, a bird cried out once and was answered by silence.

"Why are you telling me now?" Cassian asked, and hated how much the question cost him. "Why not at the valley? Why not at any of the hundred nights since?"

"Because tomorrow you'll try to stop me." She said it without accusation, which was worse. "And I needed one more night where you didn't know. One more night of this." She gestured—at the fire, at Wren's sleeping weight against her, at the small warm architecture of the three of them. "I wanted to be selfish a little longer. I'm sorry. That's the whole confession. I'm sorry, and I'd do it again."

He should have been angry. Some part of him noted, distantly, that anger was the correct response, filed away like a mis-shelved folio. But what he felt instead was the terrible tenderness of recognizing himself in her—an archivist who hoarded knowledge until it cost him everything, a thief of memories who hoarded love the same way. They were both curators of things that were never truly theirs.

"You could destroy it," he said. "Shatter the sigil. Walk away."

"And leave the Order free to dig it out of the rubble? Leave them hunting children like Wren forever?" She shook her head. "Claiming it is the only way to end it. That's the trap. That's always been the trap."

* * *

They reached the gates at dusk on the thirteenth day, and the Spire opened for them like a throat.

Not gates, precisely—Cassian corrected himself even as he thought the word. Gates implied something built to keep people out. These were two pillars of black glass standing apart in a courtyard of white stone, and between them the air shimmered with the same grief-warmth as the mountain's weather, only concentrated, only *louder*. Carvings covered every surface: spirals within spirals, and at their centers, over and over, a single mark—a circle broken by thirteen rays, one ray longer than the rest, reaching past the others toward darkness.

Wren stopped dead in front of it. The child had been chattering all morning—about the moons, about whether Spire ghosts would play cards, about the honey-bread Cassian had promised in a weak moment three days ago—and now the chatter cut off like a snipped thread.

"That's my dream," Wren whispered. "The mark. I dream it every time the thirteenth moon comes. I never told anyone. I never *could* tell anyone." Small fingers rose toward the carving and stopped an inch short. "How does a rock know my dreams?"

"It's not a rock," Cassian said softly. "It's a record. The oldest one in the world." And he understood, suddenly, with the clarity that had gotten him exiled, why the Order wanted mage-children erased. Not because they were dangerous. Because they *remembered*. Because a child who dreamed the sigil's mark was a child the sigil might choose.

Rosalind grabbed both their hands. "Through the pillars. Quickly. Once we're inside—"

The ambush came out of the evening itself.

They had not hidden; that was the horror of it. Grey-cloaked figures had simply been part of the courtyard's shadows, kneeling among the white stones like mourners at a grave, and when the thirteenth moon's light finally cleared the ridge and struck the pillars, they rose. Twelve of them. No—thirteen, the last unfolding from behind the gate itself, tall and sexless in a mask of polished bone, and when it spoke its voice was many voices speaking slightly out of step.

"Heir of Vael." The masked one looked at Rosalind, and Cassian watched her go rigid under the stranger's name. "We felt your inheritance wake in the valley. We have walked behind you across two hundred miles. You led us home."

"No," Rosalind breathed. "No, I checked the passes, I—"

"You checked the roads," said the mask. "We do not travel roads."

What happened next happened fast and happened badly. Cassian threw his satchel's weighted strap at the nearest cloaked figure and bought exactly one heartbeat. Rosalind moved like the thief she was, low and fast, shoving Wren toward the pillars—but the thirteenth figure was already there, between the child and the gate, and its bone mask tilted with something horribly like gentleness.

"Little moon," it said to Wren. "You dreamed us here too."

Wren screamed—a sound with light in it, actual light, sparks bursting off the child's skin—and then the grey cloaks closed, and the scream was muffled, and Wren was gone into the dark beyond the courtyard as if the mountain had swallowed its own echo.

Cassian caught Rosalind around the waist before she could run after them into thirteen blades. She fought him for one wild second, then went still, breathing like a bellows, watching the place where Wren had been.

"They'll use the child," the masked one said, retreating backward into shadow with terrible courtesy. "As bait. As leverage. As a key, if it comes to that. Come to us before the moon sets, heir, or we will open the Spire with blood that has no business opening anything." A pause, and the many voices softened into something almost human, almost pitying. "Or claim the sigil and save us all the trouble. Either way, you will forget why you came."

Then the courtyard was empty, and the thirteenth moon stood full over the pillars, and the Spire's weather turned cold for the first time since they'd arrived.

Rosalind pulled free of Cassian's arms. Her face in the strange light was the face of someone reading a map of a country she'd sworn never to enter.

"You knew," Cassian said. His voice cracked on it. "About what claiming it costs. You knew, and you still came, knowing they'd take her, knowing—"

"I came to trade myself for her." She was already walking toward the pillars, toward the light, and she did not look back. "Everything I've stolen, everything I am—it's worth more to them than one child. So I'm going to give it to them. All of it."

The gate-pillars hummed as she approached, and the carvings began, very slowly, to glow.

"Cassian," Rosalind said, stepping into the light that would take her name away, "if you follow me in, make sure someone remembers to grieve me properly. I won't be able to do it myself."
