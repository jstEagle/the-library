## 4. A Sigil Broken, A Name Kept

The Spire breathed.

That was the only word for it, Rosalind thought as she climbed the last of the winding stair. The stone around her expanded and contracted like the ribs of a sleeping animal, and beneath her bare feet she felt a pulse—not blood, not heartbeat, but something older: the slow tidal pull of the sigil, dreaming in the chamber above. Thirteen turns of the moon it had slept. Tonight it would wake, one way or another.

Her hands were shaking. She curled them into fists and kept climbing.

The memories pressed at her from every direction now. The stranger's burning map had been only the first key; since the valley, since the passes, since Cassian's firelight and Wren's small warm weight asleep against her shoulder, the inheritance had been flooding into her—centuries of Vael's stolen children, their names and griefs layered inside her skull like sediment. She knew things no living person should know. She knew the taste of rain on the Spire's western terrace. She knew how it felt to be taken.

The chamber opened before her without a door.

It was round as a held breath, walled in pale stone shot through with veins of silver light—the sigil itself, vast and intricate, carved across the floor and up the walls until architecture and inscription became indistinguishable. At its center knelt Wren.

The child was unharmed. That was almost worse. Wren knelt with hands folded and eyes closed, and around her the sigil's light moved like something feeding.

"Wren." Rosalind's voice cracked on the name.

"Don't." Wren didn't open her eyes. Her voice was thin, stretched. "It listens through me. It knows where you're standing."

"Then let it listen." Rosalind stepped onto the sigil, and the light rose around her ankles like warm water. "Let it hear everything."

"She came," said a voice from the far side of the chamber, "as the map promised."

The woman who stepped out of the shadowed arch was tall and grey-robed, her face lined but not old, her eyes the flat silver of moonlight on ice. Rosalind knew her from the stranger's memories—from *all* the memories now, because this woman appeared in them again and again across decades, always the same, always watching. First Mother of the Order of the Thirteenth Moon.

"You've been waiting for me," Rosalind said.

"I have been waiting sixty years." The woman walked the sigil's edge without disturbing it; the light parted around her boots as if recognizing its own. "I was born in this room. Did your ghosts tell you that? I was the last child taken before the Spire was sealed. They meant me to be the vessel. The war was already dying by then—there were no mages left worth fighting over—and so the sigil waited, and I grew, and I learned that a thing that waits can be *taught* to wait forever."

"And then you founded an order to guard it."

"I founded an order to keep anyone from waking it." The First Mother's voice did not rise, and that was somehow more terrible. "Every child we erased, every mage we burned—we were pruning the garden so nothing would grow that the sigil could use. And now here you come down the mountain carrying six hundred years of stolen heirs in your head, and you ask me if I've been waiting."

Rosalind looked at Wren. "She isn't part of this."

"She is entirely part of this. She's a mage-child. She's exactly what the sigil has been starving for." For the first time, something moved behind the silver eyes—not cruelty, but exhaustion, ancient and total. "Kill her, and the vessel fails. Claim the sigil yourself, and you become what I refused to become: a god with a war in her fist. Or walk away, and in thirteen years some other heir will find the map, because maps are patient and people are weak."

"There's a fourth choice," Rosalind said quietly.

The First Mother went very still. "There is no fourth choice."

"You were a stolen child," Rosalind said. "You know what it is to be taken, to be *used*, to be shaped by someone else's purpose. You built an entire order out of that wound. But I—" she laughed, and it came out ragged, "—I spent my whole life stealing memories from strangers because I couldn't bear my own. I know what it is to hoard. To hold everything and belong nowhere." She spread her hands. "You taught the sigil to wait. I'm going to teach it to let go."

She knelt beside Wren and took the child's face in both hands.

"It'll hurt," she whispered. "Everything I'm holding—it all goes out. Every memory I ever stole, everyone I ever touched, they get it back. And mine go too. All of them. Even the ones that made me love you."

Wren's eyes opened, wet and furious. "Then don't."

"Sweetheart," Rosalind said, "that's the whole point of family. You give things away and trust they'll give back." She pressed her forehead to Wren's. "Hold on to Cassian. He'll pretend he's annoyed. He isn't."

She turned to the sigil and drove her palms flat against the burning silver.

*Break.*

* * *

It did not shatter like glass. It shattered like dawn.

Light poured out of the Spire in a silent flood, and with it poured everything Rosalind had ever taken. In Hollowvale, a widow woke weeping with her mother's laugh restored to her. On the mountain road, a carter blinked and remembered his son's face after eleven blank years. In a cell beneath the Order's fortress, a disgraced archivist gasped as forty lifetimes crashed home into him—every archive he'd memorized, every memory he'd carried for Rosalind when she'd asked him to hold just one, *just this one, in case I lose myself*—

Cassian sat up in the dark, hand pressed to his chest, and whispered, "Oh, Rosalind. You foolish, brave girl."

In the chamber, the sigil unraveled vein by vein, six centuries of hoarded power going out into the world like dandelion seeds, finding the people they'd been torn from, or their children, or their children's children. The war died unborn. There would be no army of heirs now, no weapon, no chosen one. There was only a woman kneeling in dead silver light, empty.

The First Mother watched, and tears cut clean lines through the dust on her face. When it was done, she knelt too—knelt in the ruins of her life's terrible vigil—and said, "Thank you," in a voice like a girl's.

Rosalind barely heard her. She was searching herself, frantically, the way you pat your pockets after losing something vital. Names gone. Faces gone. The stranger's map, the burning pass, the market, the valley—even her own childhood, even her mother's voice—all scattered to the wind, given away, gone.

Except one.

One memory remained, glowing warm at her center, refusing to scatter. She hadn't chosen to keep it. It had chosen her.

A cold evening in the foothills. A fire too small. Cassian grumbling about rationed tea while secretly giving Wren the larger cup. Wren falling asleep mid-sentence, mid-lie about not being tired, her head tipping against Rosalind's shoulder. And Rosalind sitting very still, terrified to move, thinking: *oh. Oh, this is what it is. This is what I've been stealing from other people all along.*

They found her there when they came up—Wren running ahead, whole and loud and furious, punching Rosalind weakly in the arm before collapsing into her embrace; Cassian leaning in the doorway with his arms crossed and his eyes suspiciously bright.

"So," he said. "You gave away everything."

"Almost everything."

"Do you remember us?"

Rosalind looked at them—at the angry child and the insufferable man, at the two people who had become hers not by blood or theft or accident but by choice, made daily, remade each morning.

"No," she said, and smiled through the tears. "But I kept the moment I decided to stay. Apparently that was enough. Now—" she stood, wiped her face, and looked out the broken crown of the Spire toward the world below, where lights were kindling in answer to returned memories, village by village, like stars coming back on, "—someone should probably tell them all what happened. And I'd very much like to be the one who does it."

Wren slipped her hand into Rosalind's empty one.

"Then let's go home," the child said. "Whoever we decide that is."
