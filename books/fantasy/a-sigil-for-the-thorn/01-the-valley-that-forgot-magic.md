## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The wards hummed when you pressed your ear to the boundary stones. Not a sound, exactly — more a feeling in the teeth, like biting foil — but after eleven years of walking this border I'd learned to listen with my whole skull. That morning the stones sang their usual nothing, and I told them so.

"Still boring," I reported to Odile, who was crouched ten paces down the line, poking at a rabbit snare she'd definitely set on garrison time. "Third stone from the split oak. Dull as ever."

"That's what we pay you for." She didn't look up. Odile never looked up when she was lying about snares; it was her tell, and I loved her for having exactly one. "Dullness is the product. You're our premier dullness inspector."

"Premier and only."

"Scarcity drives up the price." She finally glanced over, gray-eyed and unbothered, a woman built entirely out of patience and rabbit stew. "Anything else?"

I considered telling her about the dream I'd had three nights running — the one where the valley's sky was a lid, and something underneath me knocked politely, like a guest who knew they weren't welcome. But dreams were not patrol findings, and I had a reputation as the sort of mercenary who didn't waste a captain's time with poetry. So instead I said, "Deer crossed at the shallows last night. Big tracks. And somebody's been smoking on the north path, which means Fen's been sneaking out again, which means Fen's going to get an earful."

"Which means *you're* going to give Fen an earful, because you're the only one he listens to."

"Fen listens to Bram."

"Bram feeds him. That's not listening, that's conditioning." Odile reset her snare with a flick of her wrist and stood, knees popping like green wood. "Walk me back? My feet are old and my gossip is fresh."

We walked the border road together, the two of us, under a sky so clean it looked scrubbed. Thornvale in late autumn: birches gone gold to their elbows, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the terraced apple orchards heavy with the last stubborn fruit. Somewhere behind us a mill wheel turned its slow idiot circle. It was, by any honest measure, a beautiful place to be stationed, and I had spent eleven years being grateful for it in the specific way of a person who suspects gratitude might jinx things.

Here is what you need to know about Thornvale, if you're the kind of reader who likes your maps annotated: it is a valley in the eastern reaches of the Aurellian Empire, population roughly four hundred souls and one garrison of nine, warded along its entire rim by standing stones older than any empire, any kingdom, any song anyone still remembered all the verses to. The wards kept magic out. That was the story everyone told. Magic couldn't cross the boundary, and neither could anything that carried magic in its pockets, which meant no sorcerers, no curses, no imperial battle-mages looking to requisition our apples. We were a quiet backwater, blessedly dull, and the Empire's witch-hunters had no reason to come sniffing because there was nothing here to hunt.

That was the story. I want to be clear that on this particular morning, I believed every word of it.

I was twenty-six years old, a sergeant of the Thornvale garrison by courtesy and a sellsword by trade, sworn — insofar as anyone was sworn to him anymore — to Vethra, the Silent God. There used to be prayers for Vethra. There used to be a whole liturgy, once: the god of thresholds and quiet places, the one who stood watch while others slept. Fifteen years ago the prayers stopped working. No lightning, no visions, no warm feeling in the chest. Just silence, and then more silence, until the priests stopped pretending and the shrines went unswept except by whoever felt guilty that week. Most people let go. Gods died all the time; the Empire had a machine in its capital that ran on the corpses of them, or so the scandalous pamphlets claimed before the hunters burned the pamphlet-sellers too.

I hadn't let go. I'm not sure I'd call it faith. It was more like the habit of talking to someone across a table who has stopped answering — you keep talking partly from loyalty and partly because the alternative is admitting the chair is empty. Every morning I pressed my thumb to the little iron sigil I wore at my throat — a thorn inside a circle, worn smooth by other thumbs before mine — and said the old greeting. *Good morning, Veth. Nothing's happened yet.* And every morning the sigil stayed cold, and I went about my day.

It's possible this says something unflattering about me. I've decided not to look into it.

* * *

The garrison house sat at the valley's heart, a squat stone thing that had been a tollhouse, a granary, a fever-house during the pox year, and was now mostly a place where nine people ate too much and complained about each other with real affection. I could smell supper from the gate — onions, rosemary, the scorched-bottom bread that meant Bram had gotten distracted arguing with Ilsa again.

Bram ran the kitchen the way other men run armies. He was sixty, built like a rain barrel, bald as an egg, and he had fed me through a broken collarbone, two fevers, and the worst winter of my life without once making me feel like a charity case. When I'd washed up on Thornvale's doorstep at fifteen — orphaned, broke, carrying everything I owned in a sack and a dead god's sigil around my neck — it was Bram who'd put a bowl in front of me and said, "Eat first, explain after," and I have been explaining after ever since.

"You're back early," he said, not turning from his pot. "Border misbehave?"

"Border's perfect. Border's the best-behaved thing in this valley besides you."

"I heard that," said Captain Ilsa Reyes, from the corner table where she did paperwork and performed the ancient military ritual of Looking Busy. Ilsa was fifty-eight, silver at the temples, with a voice like a door closing gently. She'd commanded the garrison longer than I'd been alive in any meaningful sense, and she ran us less like soldiers than like a family she refused to admit was one. "Report, Quintana."

"All quiet. Wards humming standard nothing. Deer sign at the shallows, big buck, worth a hunt if we want fresh meat for the harvest feast. And Fen's been sneaking up the north path to smoke, presumably, unless he's taken up pipe-weed for medicinal reasons."

"He's sixteen," Bram said, wounded. "Everything's medicinal at sixteen."

"Send him to me," Ilsa said. "Not tonight. Tomorrow. Let him sweat a little — it builds character." She made a note, which meant the matter was closed and also filed somewhere in the labyrinth of her head, where nothing was ever forgotten and grudges expired on schedule. Then, without looking up: "You look tired, Marguerite."

"I always look tired. It's my face."

"It's your face doing something else today." Now she looked up, and her eyes did the thing they occasionally did — that long, weighing look, like she was checking a load-bearing wall for cracks. I'd caught her doing it maybe twice a year for eleven years. I'd never asked about it. Some part of me understood, even then, that Ilsa Reyes kept this garrison standing the way you keep an old roof standing: by never, ever probing the beam that creaks.

"Bad dreams," I admitted, because lying to Ilsa was possible but exhausting. "Sky falling off. Knocking under the floorboards. The usual nonsense."

"Hm," she said, which was either sympathy or a data point. With Ilsa it was usually both.

Fen came in through the back door smelling exactly like cheap pipe-weed and guilt, saw me, and went the color of a beet. I let him marinate. That's half of mentorship: timing.

* * *

Supper that night was the good kind of loud. Nine of us around two shoved-together tables — me, Ilsa, Bram, Odile, Fen, the twins Petra and Anselm who handled farrier work and bickering in equal measure, old Doc Halloran who'd retired here from some war he never named, and Wren, our newest, a former caravan guard with a laugh that could strip paint. The stew went around twice. Anselm told a story about a mule that may have been true and was certainly funny. Petra accused him of stealing the punchline from a ballad. Fen laughed so hard at the impression Odile did of Ilsa ("Quintana. Report. *Properly*.") that he choked on bread and Bram thumped him with the easy violence of a man who has raised children and buried friends and refuses to confuse the two.

And I sat in the middle of it with my bowl and my cold sigil and thought, the way I thought perhaps once a season, *this is the whole treasure. This is it. Don't get greedy.*

Because that was the arrangement I'd made with the world, somewhere in those first raw years: I would take this — the noise, the stew, the people who knew how I took my coffee and which of my scars was from what — and I would not ask why the god I served had gone silent, or why a valley this peaceful needed a garrison at all, or why Ilsa looked at me like a wall she was listening to. Contentment, I'd decided, was like the wards themselves. Best appreciated from the inside, never tested.

Restless contentment. If that sounds like a contradiction, congratulations, you understand me better than I'd like.

Later, when the dishes were done and the others had drifted to beds or cards, I climbed the watchtower for the midnight shift. It was my favorite hour and I pretended it wasn't. The valley below breathed woodsmoke and frost. Above, the stars came out in their usual extravagant numbers — we had no city lights to drown them, just the mountains' black shoulders on every side — and I did what I always did on watch. I counted them, lazily, in no order, and talked to Veth.

"Quiet night," I told him. "Fen smokes. Odile's snares are on garrison time. Ilsa's worried about something, or about someone, and I'd bet the garrison purse it's me, though I can't imagine why, seeing as I am extremely well-adjusted." The sigil lay cold against my sternum. "Fifteen years, old man. You could at least clear your throat."

Nothing. Of course nothing. The stars wheeled. Somewhere below, Bram's banked fire ticked as it cooled.

I remember thinking, right at the edge of sleep-on-my-feet drowsiness, that the sky looked wrong somehow — too many stars near the horizon, too few overhead, though when I actually checked, the count came out fine. I remember deciding I was tired. I remember the wards humming their tooth-hum under my boots, steady as a heartbeat, steady as they'd been for eleven years and, I assumed, for centuries before.

I remember being content. God help me, I remember being *content* — balanced on whatever unseen thing this whole valley rested on, never once asking what it was, or what would happen on the night it moved.

Three nights later, the stars started going out.
