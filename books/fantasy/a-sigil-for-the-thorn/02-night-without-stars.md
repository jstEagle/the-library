## 2. Night Without Stars

There are three things a mercenary on border watch in Thornvale learns to expect: cold that finds the seams of everything, the particular boredom of watching nothing happen, and the sound of Odile's boots coming up the watchtower stairs when she wants your rations.

I had all three that night, and then I had none of them, and I have spent every hour since trying to work out which loss I regret most.

The cold was there first, at least. It always is. Thornvale sits in the mountains like a stone in a boot — everyone who lives here has made their peace with the ache of it or left, and those of us who stayed wear the ache like a uniform we were issued before we could consent. My hands were gloved, my cloak was oiled against damp, and my feet had gone from *painful* to *theoretical*, which is the natural order of a winter watch.

The boredom was there too. It's a specific flavor of boredom, valley-watch boredom. You count the ward-stones along the ridgeline because there's nothing else to do. Eleven visible from the tower, each one carved with Vethra's sigil — the closed eye, the folded hand — and each one glowing with a faint grey light that hasn't changed in brightness or color in my entire life. Not once. The wards are the single most reliable thing in Thornvale. More reliable than the harvest. More reliable than Captain Reyes's moods, and she's been moody since before I was born. More reliable, if I'm honest, than any god I've ever prayed to.

I'd counted them four times. Eleven. Eleven. Eleven. Eleven.

The third thing — Odile's boots — did not come, which should have been my first warning. Odile always comes up the stairs around midnight, ostensibly to check on me, actually to steal the dried plums Bram packs for whoever draws the long watch. That night the stairs stayed empty. Later I would learn she'd fallen asleep over her maps with her ink pot open and a blot spreading across the western passes like a bruise. At the time, I just thought: *quiet night.*

Quiet nights are what we're paid for. Quiet nights are the whole job. You stand on a stone tower above a valley where magic cannot enter and cannot leave, you watch eleven grey lights do nothing, and you collect wages that wouldn't buy glory but will buy bread. I've done it for nine years. I've been good at it for eight of them. There was a stretch in my second year where I wasn't good at it, but that's not this story, mostly.

So: cold, boredom, no Odile. And overhead, the stars, which were doing what stars do, which is burn indifferently at everything below them without taking sides.

I remember thinking, specifically, that the stars were indifferent. I remember it because it was the last complete thought I had for a long time, and I wanted it recorded somewhere that before everything went wrong I understood the universe correctly. The stars don't care about Thornvale. They care about nothing. They hang out there past the wards, past the ridgeline, past the reach of any empire or god-machine, burning their slow indifferent fires, and they have never once looked down.

Then they started going out.

* * *

It didn't happen all at once. That's the part nobody believes when I tell it. People want the sky to fail dramatically — a snuffing, a darkness rolling in like a shutter. But it happened politely, one star at a time, the way candles go out in a room someone is walking through.

The first one was low in the east, over the pass. I noticed it because I'd been using it as a landmark for hours, the way you do without thinking, and when I glanced back it simply wasn't there. No cloud. No haze. The patch of sky where it had been was black, and the stars around it were still burning, close enough that its absence looked like a hole punched in cloth.

I told myself it was cloud. I told myself it was my eyes. I told myself several increasingly creative lies, and then the star beside it went out too, and then two more, and I stopped lying to myself because I ran out of material.

Here's the thing about watching something impossible happen: your body knows before your mind does. My heart was already hammering like I'd run the ridge twice. My mouth had gone dry enough that my tongue clicked against my teeth. Some animal part of me, older than the wards, older than the garrison, older than the whole arrangement of my quiet little life, had stood up inside my chest and said *wrong, wrong, wrong,* and it took my rational mind another full minute to catch up and agree.

By then the dark was spreading across the sky like ink through water. Stars going out in clusters now, dozens at a time, whole constellations erased between one breath and the next. I knew their names — Odile taught me the mountain ones, Fen knows the silly ones, the ones shaped like heroes and beasts — and I watched them vanish by name. The Shepherd. The Broken Crown. The Sisters, who I'd secretly thought of as Bram's stew pots ever since he pointed them out drunk at midsummer.

Gone. Gone. Gone.

And here is what I did, as a trained professional soldier of the Thornvale garrison, sworn to protect the valley and its people: I stood very still and whispered, "No."

Not a command. Not an alarm. Just *no,* small and useless, the way you say it when a mug slips off a table. Like the sky might hear me and reconsider.

Below me, the valley slept. Lamplight in maybe thirty windows. Smoke rising straight from Bram's chimney because he banks his fire late. Somewhere down there, Fen was asleep in the barracks with their boots on again — they never learn — and Odile was face-down in her maps, and Ilsa was awake, I'd bet anything, sitting up with tea she wouldn't drink, because captains sleep like cats and she'd felt something too. I know now she had. We all felt it, that night, though none of us said so until later, when saying it was already too late to matter.

The last star to go was almost directly overhead. It hung alone in a field of absolute black — no clouds, no moon, just emptiness where the entire sky used to be — and it burned on for what felt like a full minute, stubborn, refusing, and I found myself rooting for it the way you root for anything small and doomed.

Then it went out, and the world had no ceiling anymore.

I have stood in caves. I have stood in windowless cellars during storms. I know what enclosed darkness feels like. This was different. This was the sky turned into a cellar. The whole valley sat under a lid of nothing, and every hair on my body stood up, and the silence got so thick I could hear my own pulse in my ears and the creak of the tower stones settling beneath me.

That's when I heard the wards.

* * *

You can't normally hear the wards. They hum, technically, at some pitch below hearing — Odile swears dogs can hear them, and the dogs do act strange near the border stones, so maybe. But that night I heard them the way you hear a rope under strain: not a sound exactly, more a feeling transmitted through the soles of your boots, up through your shins, into your teeth. A note being held too long. Something singing itself hoarse.

I leaned over the parapet and looked east, toward the first ward-stone on the ridge.

It was flickering.

Eleven grey lights, steady for my whole life, and the easternmost was guttering like a candle in wind that wasn't blowing. Grey, then bright, then grey, then — and this is the detail that still wakes me at night — then it flashed *white.* Clean white, hard white, the white of lightning or of a forge at full heat. For half a heartbeat the whole sigil on the stone lit up, the closed eye, the folded hand, sharp-edged and unmistakable, and I swear to whatever's left to swear to that the eye looked open.

Then the second stone flickered. Then the third. Down the ridgeline, one after another, the wards began to stutter like a line of lamps going out in a house where someone is running through the halls ahead of something.

"Ring the bell," I said aloud, to nobody, because saying it made it real and real meant I had to move. "Ring the—"

The wards shattered.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. They didn't fade. They didn't crack. They shattered — all eleven at once, a sound like the biggest bell in the world being struck with the biggest hammer, except the sound was wrong, it came from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground and the air and from inside my own skull, and the grey light that had held the valley for three hundred years burst outward off the ridge in a wave I could *see*, rippling away into the dark like rings off a drowned stone.

And then the silence after it. Worse than the silence before. A silence with a shape, the shape of a held breath.

Something crossed the boundary.

I never saw it. I want that on the record too. People ask, and I say I never saw it, and they look disappointed, and I understand why, but the truth is the truth: whatever came over that ridge in the moment after the wards died, it passed behind the tower, or above it, or *through* it, and all I got was a pressure in the air like the moment before lightning strikes, and a smell — hot iron and crushed herbs, incense and blood — and a sensation along my skin like walking through a spiderweb I couldn't brush off.

And heat.

Heat bloomed under my skin.

I need you to understand what that means for someone like me. I am twenty-six years old and I have lived my whole life in Thornvale, where magic cannot enter and cannot leave, and I have prayed to Vethra since I was old enough to kneel — the silent god, the god who watches with closed eyes and holds out an open hand — and in twenty-six years of praying I have received, in total, nothing. Not silence even. Silence would have been an answer. Nothing. The prayers go up like arrows into fog. Every child in the valley grows up learning the litanies and every adult quietly stops believing and keeps reciting anyway, because the words are the tradition and the tradition is the walls and the walls are home. I stopped expecting an answer around fifteen. I kept kneeling because Bram's stew tastes better after chapel, which is as honest a theology as I've ever needed.

And now there was heat under my skin. In my chest, first — a coal dropped behind my sternum — and then spreading outward, down both arms, pooling in my hands, and my hands were *glowing.*

Faintly. Grey-gold, the color of the ward-light, seeping up through the skin of my palms like light through a shuttered window. I held them up and stared at them and they shook, and the glow shook with them, and underneath the terror — and believe me, the terror was there, the terror was enormous, the terror was a cold hand closing around my spine — underneath all of it was something else.

Awe. Dread's twin. The feeling you get at the edge of a cliff, when the drop is beautiful and fatal and calling your name.

Because I knew, in the way you know things at three in the morning when the sky has gone out and the walls have broken — the way you know things without evidence, without permission, without wanting to — I knew the wards hadn't failed on their own. Wards don't shatter from outside. Everyone says they keep magic out. Everyone is wrong; Ilsa will tell us how wrong, soon enough, with a face like a woman confessing to a murder. The wards were a seal, and seals break from the inside when what they contain gets strong enough to push.

And something in me had pushed.

"No," I said again, and this time it wasn't about the stars. "No, no, no—" I pressed my palms against the parapet stone, trying to smother the light like smothering a flame, and the light bled through my fingers and onto the stone, and where it touched, frost melted. In deep winter, at the top of a watchtower, frost melted under my hands and ran in little silver threads down the rock.

Somewhere below, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then all of them, the whole valley's dogs at once, a chorus aimed at the sky, at the ridge, at me — I'll never know which, and it doesn't matter, because they were right about all three.

I fumbled for the alarm rope. Three pulls means riders sighted; continuous ringing means the wall is breached. My hands were shaking so badly the first pull came out ragged, and the bell answered with a cracked bronze voice that sounded as frightened as I felt, and lights started coming on in the windows below, one, then five, then all of them, the whole valley waking up under its new lid of starless black.

I rang the bell and I glowed and I whispered prayers to a god I'd stopped believing in, and for the first time in my life —

for the first time in my life, something listened.

It wasn't words. Words would come later, fragmented, dying, terrible and tender. What came then was simpler: a warmth in the coal behind my sternum, turning over like a sleeper disturbed, and a feeling that arrived fully formed in the center of me, the way you wake knowing it snowed:

*At last. At last. At last.*

The bell rang. The valley woke. The empty sky pressed down.

And far to the east, over the pass where the first star had died, I saw torchlight. Many torches. Moving downhill toward Thornvale in a line as neat and patient as stitches.

They weren't hurrying.

Whatever they were, they knew exactly what they were coming for.
