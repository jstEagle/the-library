## 3. What the Wards Were For

I have commanded the Thornvale garrison for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned that the hardest part of leadership is not sending people to die. It's deciding which lies to keep, and when the bill for them comes due.

The bill came due at dawn, the morning after the stars went out.

I knew it before anyone told me. I woke in my quarters above the armory with my heart hammering like a recruit on a locked door, and I lay there staring at the ceiling beam — the one Bram carved notches into every year on the anniversary of nothing in particular, because he says a garrison needs traditions even stupid ones — and I thought: *it's finished.* Thirty years of quiet. Thirty years of a debt deferred. And now the collectors were crossing the pass.

I dressed. Buckled on the sword, though there was no enemy yet that a sword would answer. Went down through the cold corridors of the fort, past the shrine alcove where Vethra's sigil had been painted over three generations ago and then, in a fit of sentiment by my predecessor, painted back on — badly, so the god looked mildly embarrassed — and out into the courtyard where the morning was already going wrong.

Marguerite stood at the well with their sleeves rolled up, washing ash off their forearms. Ash that hadn't been there yesterday. They looked up at me, and I saw it plain as a brand on their face: they'd felt something. Something under the skin, something new, and they didn't have a word for it yet.

"Captain," they said. "Did you see the sky?"

"I saw it."

"The wards—" Their jaw worked. Marguerite is not a person who struggles for words; they're a person who chooses them carefully, like coins counted twice. "They broke. All along the border, all at once. Like glass. And something came through. Or something came *out*. I can't tell which, and that frightens me more than either."

"It should," I said, before I could stop myself.

Their eyes narrowed. Twenty-six years old, sworn to a silent god, sharper than any blade in my armory, and they caught it instantly — the wrongness in my voice, the confession I hadn't made yet standing behind my teeth.

"Captain. What do you know?"

*There it is*, I thought. *Thirty years, and here's the bill.*

"Get the others," I said. "Bram, Odile, Fen. The map room. Not the chapel." Old habit. Some things you don't say under a god's eyes, even a silent one. "And Marguerite — bring yourself. That's the most important part."

They stared at me a moment longer, and I watched them decide to trust me, the way you watch someone step onto ice they've already heard cracking.

Then they went to do as I asked, and I stood alone in the courtyard under a starless morning sky, feeling every one of my fifty-four years settle onto my shoulders like wet chainmail.

* * *

The map room smelled of tallow and old paper and Bram's cooking drifting up from below, which felt obscene given what I was about to do. I'd had Odile bar the door — she did it without asking why, which is Odile all over; she reads a room the way other people read weather — and I laid my hands flat on the great table, on the map of the valley I'd been staring at for two decades without ever once seeing it honestly.

Fen sat perched on a stool, sixteen and trying very hard to look like someone who belonged in serious meetings. Bram filled the doorway because Bram fills every doorway; the man is built like a siege engine that learned to make soup. Odile leaned against the wall, arms crossed. And Marguerite stood at the table's edge, arms also crossed, watching me with the patience of a hunter who has decided not to spook the quarry.

"You're going to tell us something," Marguerite said. "You've got your lying voice."

"I don't have a lying voice."

"You have a voice you use when you're choosing not to lie but not telling the whole truth. It's this one. You're using it now."

Gods, I love that child. It makes what I did worse, somehow, that I love them.

"All right," I said. "No preamble. Preamble is just cowardice with better posture." I pulled the lamp closer, so its light fell on the valley's borders, on the thin inked line where the mountains rose. "You all know the founding story. Every child in Thornvale knows it. A thousand years ago, the last mages of the old world fled the empire's purges and hid in this valley, and they raised the wards to keep magic *out* — a shell against a hostile world, so we could live plain lives, safe from sorcery and witch-hunters alike. Yes?"

Nods all around. Fen recited it half under their breath, the way children do, the catechism of home.

"That story," I said, "is true the way a coin is true. It has two faces, and we've only ever been shown one." I turned my hands over on the table. "The wards weren't built to keep magic out. Magic doesn't work that way. You can't wall off a force of nature any more than you can wall off the wind. No — the wards were built to hide something *inside*. To keep the empire from finding it. The valley isn't a fortress, Marguerite. It's a lockbox."

Silence. Bram stopped fidgeting, which for Bram is the equivalent of drawing steel.

"What thing?" Odile asked quietly. She always asks the question everyone else is avoiding. Best scout I ever had.

"A god," I said.

I let that sit. Outside, the garrison was waking — boots in the yard, the creak of the gate hinges, ordinary sounds that suddenly sounded like the last minutes of an ordinary life.

"Vethra," I went on, "wasn't always silent. Wasn't always dying. When the founders came here, the god came with them — or they came with the god, the histories argue about which, and I've read both and found neither convincing. But the empire wanted Vethra dead. Not worshipped, not fought — *dead*, or as near to dead as a god gets, because the empire had begun building its own god by then, a machine-fed thing, and it couldn't tolerate competition. So the founders did the only thing left. They bound what remained of Vethra into the valley itself, warded it beyond finding, and swore the garrison that kept the wards to silence about what they guarded." My mouth tasted of copper and old shame. "We weren't mercenaries hired to patrol a border. We were tomb-guards. Every captain of Thornvale, for a thousand years, has known it and passed it to the next. My predecessor told me the night before she died. And I've known for thirty years, and I've told no one."

"Why?" Fen burst out. Then, reddening: "Why not tell us? Why keep — Captain, that's our *whole lives* you've been—"

"Yes," I said. "It is. And I'd do it again, and I'll tell you why before you finish being angry at me." I looked around the table. "Because the empire doesn't forget. Because the day anyone outside these walls learns what sleeps here, they come, and they don't come to talk. Because ignorance was armor. You can't torture out of a person a secret they never had. I kept you ignorant the way you keep a blade oiled — not because I think you're weapons, but because I know exactly what's hunting us."

"And now?" Odile said. "The wards are broken. Someone knows."

"Someone always knew," I said. "That's the part I haven't said yet. The part I should have led with, if I were braver." I turned to Marguerite. "The night you were born, I wasn't yet captain. I was a sergeant with a bad knee and a worse temper, and I was called to a farmhouse on the east terraces because a midwife had sent word of a difficult birth. What she actually meant — what the old captain understood the moment she arrived — was that the valley's binding was failing. Vethra's remnant, the spark the founders had woven into the land itself, had thinned to almost nothing after a thousand years. Gods don't die clean, Marguerite. They fray. Ours had frayed to a single thread, and threads need somewhere to live."

I held their gaze. It took everything I had. Their face had gone very still, the stillness of deep water.

"They bound it into a newborn," Marguerite said. Flat. Not a question.

"A newborn whose mother had died bringing them into the world," I said, "and whose father had walked out of the valley two years prior and never sent word. A child no one would come looking for. The old captain made the choice. I helped hold the warding-candles. I was twenty-four years old and I told myself I was saving a valley." I swallowed. "We told ourselves the god chose freely. I still don't know if that's comfort or a lie. Perhaps both. Perhaps that's all theology ever is."

Marguerite didn't move. Didn't speak. The lamplight moved on their face, and I saw the heat there — faint, wrong, beautiful — a shimmer beneath the skin like sun through amber. Since last night, since the wards shattered, whatever lived in them was waking, and I could see it, and gods forgive me, some traitor part of me was *glad*, because I have loved this valley my whole adult life and I will burn before I watch it starve.

"Say it plainly," Marguerite said at last. "All of it. Don't ration the truth now, Captain. You've spent my whole life doing that."

"The spark in you woke last night because the wards died," I said. "The wards died because they were tied to the spark — they were its shell, its camouflage, and camouflage fails when the thing hidden starts to glow. Which means the empire's hunters felt it too, somewhere far away, the way you feel a fire through a floor. And they are coming. Not maybe. Coming. Inquisitors, witch-hunters, whatever pretty name the empire gives its butchers this century. And when they arrive, they will ask for one thing." I put my hand flat on the table, next to theirs, not touching. "They'll ask for you."

* * *

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then Bram said, "Over my dead body," in the mild tone he uses for discussing bread, as if it were already settled logistics rather than sentiment.

"Bram—"

"No, Captain, I mean it in the planning sense. Over. My. Dead body. There's a ditch between the east wall and the orchard that fits roughly forty hunters, I've measured it for other purposes, and I'm happy to be the reason it serves this one."

"That's the spirit," Odile murmured, "that's exactly the measured, proportionate response I've come to expect from this garrison."

"You'd rather roll over?" Bram said. "Odile, I've fed these people for fifteen years. I'm not handing the recipe book to the first man with a fancy banner."

"We don't even know who's coming—"

"We know enough. Witch-hunters. The captain said the word 'inquisitor,' which in any language means a man who enjoys his work."

Fen's voice cut through, young and shaking and stubborn: "But it's Marguerite's choice. Isn't it? Whatever the spark is — it's *in* Marguerite. Nobody gets to decide but them." They looked at Marguerite with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself at twenty-four, holding those candles: the look of someone realizing that love has a price tag and checking whether they can afford it.

Everyone looked at Marguerite.

And Marguerite — gods help me — laughed. Short, hard, humorless, the sound of a person finding the one joke the universe left within reach. "So let me assemble this," they said. "My god isn't silent. My god is *me*. My entire life — every unanswered prayer, every empty shrine, every time I knelt in the dark and got nothing back — that wasn't absence. That was me, talking to myself. Praying into my own chest." Their hand drifted up, involuntary, and pressed flat against their sternum, and the light under their skin brightened faintly where they touched it, like a coal remembering air. "And the price of that discovery is that the most powerful empire in the world wants to crack me open like a walnut."

"I won't let them," I said.

"You can't stop an empire, Captain."

"I've stopped empires smaller ways," I said. "One border at a time. One day at a time. It's the only way anything's ever been stopped." I straightened, and I felt the old oath move in me, the one spoken over candle smoke thirty years ago, the one I'd carried like a stone in my boot ever since. "Hear me, all of you. Yesterday you were a border garrison, and today you are what you always were — the guard of a god's grave, except the grave walks and talks and washes ash off their arms at my well. Nothing about your duty changed last night except that now you know its name. The empire will come with banners and legal language and a parley flag, and they will ask politely, and then they will stop asking. And this garrison — *this* garrison — will answer the way Thornvale has answered for a thousand years."

"With silence?" Odile asked, one eyebrow up.

"With steel," I said. "The silence was always just how we loaded it."

Bram grinned like a landslide. Fen sat up straighter. And Marguerite looked at me across the map of the only home either of us had ever known, and I braced for rage, for the betrayal to finally break over me like I deserved.

What I got instead was quieter and worse. "You held the candles," they said. "When I was born. You were *there*."

"I was."

"Fifteen years I prayed and you knew. You knew why I heard nothing." Their voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, low and level, which from Marguerite is far more dangerous. "Every festival. Every vigil. You watched me kneel."

"Yes," I said. "And I would kneel beside you and hate myself, and do it again the next year, because the alternative was painting a target on a child. I am not asking forgiveness, Marguerite. I'm asking you to believe that everything else I've done — every drill, every patrol, every hour I spent teaching you to fight — I did to keep you alive long enough to survive this exact morning. Whether you forgive me or not, that part was true."

Something shifted in their face. Not forgiveness — I hadn't earned it, might never earn it — but something adjacent. Recognition, maybe. The look of a soldier assessing another soldier's position and finding it defensible.

"The hunters," Marguerite said. "How long?"

"Days. Perhaps less. The wards breaking will have been felt as far as the capital."

"Then we have work to do," they said, and turned for the door, and paused with their hand on the frame. Without looking back: "Captain. The candles. Next time there's a decision about my life — there won't be candles you hold without me. Swear it."

"I swear it," I said, "on Vethra," and heard Bram huff — the old god's name in an oath, after all these years — and Marguerite nodded once and was gone, boots ringing on the stair.

* * *

I dismissed the others — Odile to the walls, Fen to run messages, Bram back to his kitchen, because a garrison that eats well fights well and I intended to feed ours for whatever came — and I stayed alone in the map room with the lamp and the lie I'd kept warm for thirty years, now cold in my hands.

Under the table's edge, scratched into the wood by some bored soldier decades before me, someone had carved a crude little sigil. Vethra's mark. The closed eye and the open thorn. I'd looked at it ten thousand times and never once thought about who drew it, or whether, somewhere under the paint and plaster of this fort, the god I'd helped bury had ever been listening after all.

Outside, the morning went on. Boots, harness, the well-chain clanking. Ordinary sounds, borrowed sounds.

And far off, past the eastern ridge where the pass came down through the snowline, Odile's horn sounded — one clear note, then two — the signal for riders on the road.

Not many. Just one, by the spacing of her calls. One rider, coming slow.

I blew out the lamp, because the daylight through the arrow-slits was enough, and it seemed suddenly important to stop hiding things behind small flames.

"Well," I said to the empty room, to the carved sigil, to whoever or whatever was listening. "Showtime."
