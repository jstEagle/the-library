## 4. The Hunter at the Gate

There is a particular smell to a witch-hunter's honesty, and it smells like oiled leather and lye soap and nothing else. We are trained to scrub ourselves of scent — incense, perfume, the smoke of hearth-fires we've slept beside — because a tracker who can't smell her own lies has no business smelling anyone else's. I have been scrubbing myself for eleven years. Some mornings I look at my hands in the basin and they seem like a stranger's hands: clean, capable, and entirely without history.

The morning Inquisitor Corvane's outrider arrived at Thornvale under a parley banner, I was already awake, because I am always already awake, and I was standing at the eastern wall of the garrison with a cup of tea going cold in my fist, pretending to admire the valley.

It is a valley worth admiring, which is precisely what made my work here so tedious. Green to the ridgelines, terraced orchards climbing the slopes like green staircases, a river running down the middle of everything with the smugness of water that has never once been asked to do anything difficult. The wards hummed along the borders the way they always did — not a sound, exactly, but a pressure behind the ears, like the moment before a storm that never arrives. I had spent three weeks mapping that pressure. Three weeks of walking the perimeter with instruments disguised as surveyor's tools, charting a ward-lattice so old and so seamless that whoever built it had either been a genius or had simply had more time than anyone alive should be granted.

And then the stars went out.

I don't write that lightly. I stood on the wall the night it happened and watched them vanish one by one, west to east, like candles being pinched by a careful hand, and every hair on my body stood up and informed me, in the old tongue of the body, that the world had just changed its mind about something fundamental. The wards shattered an hour later. I felt it go through me like a bell struck inside my own ribs. And beneath my feet, beneath the whole sleeping valley, something *woke* — a heat, a pulse, a thread of living light pulled taut from somewhere near the garrison and running out past the mountains toward a horizon I couldn't see.

I knew what it was before I knew what it was. That's the ugly truth of being good at your work. The Order trains you until knowing happens faster than thinking, and then you spend the rest of your life answering for what you know.

* * *

The outrider came up the river road at midmorning, alone, white banner snapping on a lance butt. Grey cloak, grey horse, the high collar of the Order turned up against a wind that hadn't started yet. I watched from the wall while Captain Reyes went down to meet him, and I made myself breathe slowly, because the thing I'd felt in the night was still there — still threaded through the valley, still anchored somewhere uncomfortably close to where I was standing — and it had gotten *brighter* since dawn. Whatever had woken was waking further.

"Tracker Vane." Captain Reyes didn't look at me when she said it. She had a way of addressing people slightly to their left, as if the person she was really talking to stood just behind them. "You'll want to hear this."

"I generally do, Captain."

"Don't get clever."

"Cleverness is the only thing keeping me warm in this valley, ma'am."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth and died there. She was a hard woman, Reyes, weathered like fence posts, but I'd learned in three weeks of watching her that the hardness was load-bearing. She carried this garrison the way a roof carries snow — silently, and with a very specific idea of how much weight was too much.

The outrider gave his name as Brother Cassel, Second Chair of the Inquisition, and he said it with the mild apologetic tone hunters use when they want you to forget that the Second Chair burns people. He dismounted, bowed to the captain, and produced letters of parley sealed in grey wax. All perfectly correct. All perfectly polite.

"The Order seeks information," he said, "regarding anomalous births in this region. Records suggest certain irregularities dating back some thirty years. Births out of season, so to speak. Children born under circumstances the parish rolls found... inconvenient to describe."

"Thornvale keeps no parish rolls," Reyes said. "We keep muster rolls. If a child is born here, we note the mother's name and whether she wants pickles or plums."

Brother Cassel smiled the way frost smiles on a window. "A charming custom. Nevertheless, the Order's interest is administrative only. A census. Nothing more."

Behind his eyes, behind the politeness, I could see the arithmetic running. Hunters are taught to count everything: doors, wells, children, weapons, the distance between buildings, the number of heartbeats between a question and its answer. Cassel was counting the garrison now, and I was counting him, and somewhere in the middle of all that counting, the bright thread in the valley pulsed once, gently, like a sleeper turning over.

Cassel's head tilted. A fraction. The way a dog's head tilts.

He felt it too.

* * *

Here is what the Order teaches its trackers about the spark of a god, in the last year of training, in a room with no windows, by a lecturer whose hands shook: gods do not die cleanly. When the empire unmade the old pantheon — and we were taught to say *unmade*, never *murdered*, though the difference is a matter of grammar rather than conscience — the divine fire did not go out all at once. It scattered. It hid. It took refuge in bloodlines, in birthmarks, in the occasional inconvenient infant born under circumstances the parish rolls found difficult to describe. The god-machine in the capital runs on what the Order harvests. It has run on less and less every year. Everyone below the rank of Inquisitor pretends not to know why the machine-city's lights dim a little more each winter.

Everyone pretends. That is the empire's true state religion: pretense, with lye soap and grey cloaks.

I was fourteen when they took me. Not from Thornvale — from a village two hundred miles south, a place I have deliberately forgotten the name of, because forgetting is also a skill the Order teaches, though not on purpose. They tested every child in the district that autumn. I remember the testing apparatus: a bowl of black water, a silver needle, a prayer in a dead language. Most children touched the needle and nothing happened. When I touched it, the water glowed faintly, briefly, the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

Not enough to make me a vessel. Enough to make me useful. The Order does not waste material. They trained my sensitivity instead of harvesting it, the way you might keep a songbird rather than eat it, if the songbird could tell you where other birds were hiding.

So yes. I know exactly what woke beneath Thornvale the night the stars went out. I know what a spark feels like when it's dying — I've knelt beside three of them in my career, held the bowls of black water while the light drained out of children too young to understand what was being taken. This one wasn't dying. This one was *burning*. Fifteen years of silence and then, in a single night, a blaze.

And I stood on the wall of the garrison, next to Captain Reyes, listening to Brother Cassel ask his polite questions, and I said nothing.

* * *

"You'll dine with us," Reyes said, when the questions wound down. "Parley rules. You'll be hosted and watched in equal measure."

"Naturally," said Cassel.

"We're having stew."

"I'm sure it will be memorable."

It was Bram's stew, so it would be. Bram cooks the way other men fight — with total commitment, visible joy, and a certain amount of spatter. His stew contained approximately one cow, nine onions, and a quantity of pepper that suggested a personal vendetta against blandness. I sat at the long table in the garrison hall with a bowl of it, across from Brother Cassel, who ate politely and sparingly and asked his questions between spoonfuls with the timing of a man who had rehearsed.

"And you, Tracker Vane." His eyes came around to me, mild as milk. "You've been in the valley some weeks now. Surveying?"

"Mapping the old boundary stones," I said. "For the imperial land registry."

"Find anything interesting?"

"Stone," I said. "Mostly stone. Stone is what boundaries are made of."

He laughed — a real laugh, or a perfect copy of one — and the table laughed with him, because the garrison had decided over the past three weeks that I was one of theirs, or nearly, and laughter at a guest's joke is a courtesy extended even to guests you intend to watch all night. Odile, the scout, grinned at me sideways. Young Fen, all elbows and appetite, asked Cassel if the Order really wore armor under their cloaks, and seemed genuinely disappointed by the answer.

And Marguerite Quintana sat four places down the table, eating stew, saying little, watching everything.

I had met them properly only twice. Once in the yard, sparring — they moved like water finding its level, economical, patient, the kind of fighter who wins by letting you exhaust your opinions first. Once on the wall at dusk, where we'd exchanged perhaps forty words about the weather, both of us lying about how much we liked it. They had a face you could read for hours and learn nothing: dark-eyed, watchful, with the settled quality of someone who has made peace with a life most people would find too small.

They were the anchor of the thread. I had known it since the second night, when I walked the perimeter and the ward-pressure bent around the garrison like current around a keel. I had confirmed it on the night the stars vanished, when the heat bloomed and every line of light in the valley ran to or from the room where Marguerite slept.

Now they sat four places away, passing bread, and the spark in their blood burned so brightly that I could feel it warming the side of my face like a hearth, and Brother Cassel sat across from me doing arithmetic, and the whole question of my life narrowed to the width of a single silence.

Say it. Say *the vessel is here, four seats down, pass the word, reinforcements by week's end.* Eleven years of training leaned on me like a hand on the back of my neck. The Order rewards obedience the way rivers reward rain — totally, inevitably, without memory. I knew what happened to trackers who withheld. There was a woman named Hesk, my first instructor, who hesitated once over a vessel in the fen country. They didn't burn her. They made her watch, and then they made her file the report, and then they let her live with it, which the Order considers the more efficient punishment.

Marguerite laughed at something Odile said. It was a small laugh, surprised out of them, quickly smoothed over — the laugh of a person unaccustomed to being funny. The spark flared with it. I swear it did; the light in their blood answered their amusement like a struck tuning fork, and for one absurd moment the entire hall seemed to lean toward them, every candle flame bending a degree off vertical.

Cassel saw it. I watched him see it — the flicker in his eyes, the spoon pausing half an inch above the bowl. But he was a Second Chair, not a tracker; he felt the shape of the wrongness without its name. He looked at the candles. He looked at Fen, at Odile, at Reyes. He did not look at Marguerite, because the Order teaches its officers to hunt with indirection, and looking directly at a thing is how you lose it.

He looked at me instead. Trackers notice things. It was the natural next step in his arithmetic.

"Well, Tracker?" he said pleasantly. "You've a sensitive nose, I'm told. Smell anything unusual in the valley lately?"

Every eye at the table turned to me. Reyes's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Odile raised an eyebrow. Fen stopped chewing.

Four seats down, Marguerite Quintana looked at me with those unreadable dark eyes, and beneath their skin the last living spark of a murdered god burned like a coal wrapped in flesh, and waited, as far as I could tell, to see what I would do.

I thought of Hesk. I thought of the black water and the silver needle and the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. I thought of the machine-city's lights dimming each winter, and the hungry thing beneath them that my whole life had fed without ever once feeding *me*.

"Stew," I said. "Bram uses too much pepper. Otherwise, nothing."

Cassel held my gaze a moment longer than comfort allows. Then he smiled his frost smile and returned to his bowl, and dinner went on, and everyone pretended, in the fine old imperial tradition, that nothing had happened at all.

* * *

I found Marguerite afterward, in the yard, because I am a tracker and I cannot help finding people, and because the conversation coming was one I could not survive avoiding.

They were checking the gate mechanism by lamplight — oiling pins, testing the counterweight — work done slowly and well by someone who intended to keep doing it for years. The spark moved under their skin when they reached, a soft gold shimmer visible only to me, or to anyone of my training, or possibly soon to everyone, if it kept brightening.

"Your inquisitor asks a lot of questions," Marguerite said, without turning around.

"He's paid by the question."

"And you? What are you paid by?"

"Lies," I said. "Mostly in kind."

That got me a look — level, unhurried, the look of someone weighing a stranger on scales they kept behind their eyes. "The stars went out," Marguerite said. "The wards broke. Something crossed the border in the night, and your master arrived the next morning asking about babies. You've been walking our borders for three weeks with instruments that aren't for measuring land. So." They set down the oil rag, folded their arms, and gave me the whole of their attention, which I imagine is what it feels like to be audited by a very patient god. "Who are you, Sable Vane, and what did you come here to find?"

And there it was. The fork in the road, worn smooth by eleven years of walking the same direction.

I could lie. I am extremely good at lying; it is the one art the empire perfected. I could give them a cover story stitched from half-truths, walk out of this yard clean, and put a knife in this valley's back the day Corvane's reinforcements crested the pass.

Instead I heard myself say: "I came to find a spark. The last one. And I found it, and it's you, and I told a Second Chair of the Inquisition it was nothing, and I don't fully know why."

The lamplight guttered. Or it didn't gutter — it *leaned*, all of it, every flame in the yard bending toward Marguerite like grass toward sun, and beneath their skin the gold shimmer rose bright enough to throw shadows.

"That," Marguerite said quietly, "makes two of us who don't know why."

Above the eastern ridge, where the stars had gone out, the sky was still black and empty. But I noticed — and I knew Marguerite noticed, because their breath caught — that far off, past the mountains, in the direction the thread of light ran, a single star had come back.

One star. Burning like a promise, or a warning.

Corvane would see it too. And whatever he decided it meant, he was not a man who traveled anywhere without an army close behind him.
