## 5. Blood Remembers

I told myself I was going up into the hills for the usual reasons. Patrol. Habit. The particular restlessness that comes from sleeping in the same bed for thirty years and dreaming of a door you've never opened.

I told myself that about four times, which is how I knew it was a lie.

The morning after the stars went out, Thornvale had done what Thornvale does: it got up, complained about the weather, and pretended nothing had happened. Odile came back from the eastern ridge at noon reporting that the sky was "normal, boring, and full of clouds," which she delivered with the tone of a woman personally insulted by the heavens. Bram burned the porridge, which was also normal, though I caught him staring into the pot like it might contain answers. Fen asked me eleven questions about the wards before Ilsa told them to go muck the stables, and even Ilsa's voice had a crack in it she hadn't bothered to hide.

And me? I'd slept maybe two hours, and every one of them had been full of heat. Not fever-heat. Something under my skin, coiled behind my sternum like a second heart that had only just remembered it was supposed to be beating. When I woke, my hands were glowing.

Not blazing. Not even bright. Just a faint amber warmth in the creases of my palms, the way a banked fire looks when you lift the ash. It faded when I sat up. It faded when I told it to. It faded, most alarmingly, when I prayed — which is the wrong direction for prayer to work, in my considerable experience. Prayer is supposed to go up. This came *out*.

So: the hills. Alone. Where nobody could see whatever I was about to do, and where the sheep wouldn't talk.

The path past the old shepherd's cairn was one I'd walked since I could walk, and I took it now with the specific care of a person trying to look casual for an audience of absolutely no one. The morning was grey and damp, the kind of valley morning where the mist sits in the hollows like it's paid rent. My breath fogged. My boots squelched. Everything was reassuringly ordinary, and I clung to that ordinariness like a railing on a cliff path.

Here's what I knew, which was almost nothing:

One. The stars had gone out. All of them, at once, in the deep hours of the night, and then the wards — the invisible something that had ringed this valley since before my grandmother's grandmother — had shattered. I'd felt it go. Everyone had, though only Odile had been awake enough to describe it, and she described it as "like the whole world flinching."

Two. Something had crossed the boundary in the same moment. I'd seen it from the wall — or seen the *shape* of it, a wrongness in the mist, moving north toward the high passes. Ilsa had ordered a watch doubled and told us all to stop gawping.

Three. Since that moment, I had been warm in a way I had never been warm in my life. I grew up in this valley. I know every flavor of cold it can serve. I have never once, in thirty years, felt heat come from *inside*.

Four — and this was the one I kept trying to set down and kept picking back up — the silent god I had prayed to every morning and every night for fifteen years, the god who had never once answered, whose silence I had built my whole stubborn devotion around like a house built around an empty chair — that god had felt *closer* since the stars went out. Closer the way a fire feels closer when you open the door of the stove.

I didn't like any of these facts. I especially didn't like four.

* * *

I stopped at the shelf of granite above the shepherd's cairn, where the mist thinned and the whole valley opened out below me: rooftops, smoke, the garrison's crooked banner, the fields going gold at the edges. My valley. My whole world, laid out small enough to fit in a cupped hand.

I sat down on the cold stone, because if you're going to have a crisis, you may as well have it with a view.

"All right," I said to the empty air, because talking to yourself is only madness if nobody answers, and I'd had fifteen years of excellent practice at unanswered. "Let's review."

I held up my hands. Ordinary hands. Scarred knuckles, a missing fingernail on the left from an incident with a hay rake that I do not discuss. I turned them over. I looked at the lines of my palms the way fortune-tellers do, as if the answer might be written there in some alphabet I'd skipped in school.

Nothing. Just hands.

"Right," I said. "So. Either the wards coming down did something to me, or the wards coming down did something that was always in me, or I've finally cracked, and honestly, option three is looking restful."

The air didn't answer. The mist didn't stir. Somewhere below, a dog barked at nothing, which felt thematically appropriate.

I closed my eyes and did the thing I'd done every morning since I was fifteen: I reached for Vethra. Not with words — words had never worked — but with the shape of attention, the way you lean toward a sound so faint you can't be sure it's real. Fifteen years of reaching into silence. Fifteen years of silence answering.

I reached.

And the silence *leaned back*.

I don't know how else to say it. There was no voice, not yet, not then. There was a pressure, a presence, a warmth behind my ribs that suddenly had *direction*, like a hand pressed flat against the inside of my chest. My eyes flew open. My heart was going like a drummer boy who's just been told the battle starts now.

"Okay," I said, standing up, because sitting felt suddenly like a tactical error. "Okay. That's — no. That's new."

The warmth spread. Down my arms, into my hands, pooling in my palms the way it had at dawn. I could see it now, faintly, even in the grey light: a glow under my skin, gold and slow, like embers breathing.

Every sensible instinct I owned said: *put it down, Marguerite. Walk away. Go tell Ilsa. Go tell someone. This is exactly the sort of thing that is not solved alone on a hill.*

Every sensible instinct I owned has been overruled by my stubbornness approximately nine hundred times, and this was to be number nine hundred and one.

Because here's the thing about fifteen years of unanswered prayer: you don't stop wanting the answer. You just stop admitting you want it. And now the silence had a temperature, and the temperature was *warm*, and I was so tired of cold.

I held out my hands, palms up, the way I'd seen the old devotional woodcuts show Vethra's priests holding them, back before the empire burned the woodcuts and the priests and, eventually, the god.

"Whatever you are," I said quietly, "if you're in there — I'm not giving you back."

The heat surged.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because I've turned it over so many times the edges have gone smooth, and I want to keep the truth of it sharp.

I did not cast anything. I did not speak any word of power, because I don't know any. I did not make a gesture, unless you count flinching. What happened was that the warmth in my palms *recognized something* — a shape, a permission, a door it had been standing in front of for thirty years — and the air above my hands caught fire.

Not burned. *Caught.* The way a wick catches. One moment there was nothing but damp morning air, and the next there was a flame the size of an apple, hovering an inch above my bare skin, burning nothing, fed by nothing, steady as a candle in a windless room.

I screamed. I'll admit it. Not a war scream — I have one of those, it's very intimidating, Odile helped me develop it. This was the other kind. The kind you'd be embarrassed to have Odile hear.

I shook my hands. The flame stayed. I flapped my arms like a startled goose, which I also admit. The flame stayed, patient as a well-trained hawk, and somewhere beneath my panic I registered that it didn't hurt. It didn't burn my skin. It was warm the way sunlight through a window is warm. It was warm the way — and this thought arrived whole, uninvited, and devastating —

It was warm the way the chapel had felt, once, when I was fifteen, kneeling at Vethra's empty altar, and for half a heartbeat I'd have sworn the stone under my hands was alive.

The flame guttered when my concentration broke, and went out, and I stood on the granite shelf in the grey morning with my arms still half-raised, breathing like I'd run the ridge line twice.

"Vethra," I said.

And the god answered.

* * *

It was not a voice, exactly. It was more like — you know how you can feel someone standing behind you before you hear them? It was that, but for meaning. Words arrived in my head the way water arrives through a cracked wall: not poured, but *seeping*, in fragments, in pieces, some of them broken mid-syllable, some of them in a language I shouldn't have known and somehow did.

*— little thorn —*

I went very still.

*— hear me. at last. at —* the presence flickered, guttered like the flame had, *— at last, at — forgive. I am not — I was not always —*

"Vethra?" My voice cracked on it. Fifteen years I'd said that name into the dark, and I'd made my peace with the dark. "Is that — are you *there*?"

*— here,* said the seeping, the pressure, the warmth behind my sternum flaring so bright I gasped. *— here, here, in the — in you. I am sorry. I am so — the wards. They hid us both. I could not — you were a child, I could not wake you, they would have —*

"They?" I was crouching now, hands pressed to my temples, talking to the inside of my own skull on a hillside like a madwoman, and I could not have cared less. "Who's *they*? Vethra — the wards came down last night. Something crossed. Is that them? Are they coming *here*?"

The presence shuddered. I felt it the way you feel a building shudder, deep in the stone.

*— hunters,* it said, and the word came through whole and sharp as a splinter. *— they follow the spark. they have always followed the spark. every — every vessel. every child. they —*

The voice broke apart. I felt it break, felt the warmth in my chest stutter like a dying candle, and something in me went cold and furious at the same time, which is a combination I don't recommend.

"Stay," I said. Stupid thing to say to a god. I said it anyway. "Stay *with* me. Don't — you don't get to do this again. Fifteen years, Vethra. Fifteen years of nothing. You don't get to come back for one morning and fall apart."

*— not falling,* the god said, and there was something in the fragment that might have been, of all things, humor. Faint. Frayed. But there. *— falling implies I was ever — up. little thorn. listen. they will come for —*

"For me." I said it flat. I'd known it since the flame caught, maybe since the stars went out. Knowing a thing and saying it out loud are different animals, and the second one bites. "The spark. I'm the spark. That's what Ilsa isn't telling me. That's what the wards were *for*."

*— the wards were a cradle,* Vethra said, and the tenderness in it undid me more than the fear had. *— I chose. do you hear me? I chose you. I was dying and they were hunting me and I chose — the smallest, the quietest, the one who prayed to a silent god —*

I was crying. I noticed it with a kind of distant irritation, the way you notice rain. Fifteen years of devotion, and I'd told myself it was discipline, habit, stubbornness, anything but love, because love for a god who never answered is a humiliating thing to carry. And now the god was here, dying, fragmented, seeping through the cracks of my own skull, and it had *chosen* me, had hidden in me, had listened to fifteen years of my grumbling prayers from inside my own chest, and every one of those prayers I'd ever muttered — *you could at least answer, you silent bastard* — came flooding back, and the god had heard *all of them*, and I felt, unmistakably, amusement.

"Oh, don't," I said wetly, laughing despite everything. "Don't you dare laugh at me. I suffered."

*— you complained,* Vethra said, *— beautifully.*

And then the presence flickered again, harder, and the warmth in my chest dimmed like a lamp running out of oil, and the humor in it turned to something older and much worse.

*— little thorn. I am not — there is not much of me. the spark keeps me. you keep me. but the hunters — the machine they built — it eats gods. it has eaten —* a long, broken pause, and when the voice came back it was thin as thread. *— I am the last. you are the last of me. when they come — and they will come —*

"They'll have to get through the garrison first," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "They'll have to get through me. And I'm *very* annoying."

The warmth pulsed once. If a dying god could be proud of you, I think that's what that was.

*— you must not let them take all of it,* Vethra whispered. *— the spark. whatever they promise. whatever they — the machine does not save. it only —* and then the voice went, not gradually but all at once, like a snuffed candle, and I was alone on the granite shelf with the mist and the sheep and a valley full of people who had no idea what was sleeping in their mercenary.

I sat there for a long time.

The mist burned off. The sun came up thin and pale over the eastern ridge — and I noticed, distantly, that I didn't feel the cold anymore. I sat bare-handed on frozen stone in a valley morning and felt *warm*, and the warmth had a heartbeat, and the heartbeat wasn't mine.

Fifteen years I'd prayed to a silent god, and I'd thought the silence was the hardest thing a person could carry.

It isn't. The hard thing is hearing the answer, and learning that the answer has been dying in your care the whole time, and that everything you love is standing between it and the people who killed all the others.

I got up. I wiped my face on my sleeve, because I'm a professional. I looked down at Thornvale — at the smoke, the banner, the crooked little roofs, Bram's chimney already going, Fen probably already in trouble, Odile probably already on a ridge watching for the thing that crossed in the night — and I made myself a promise on that hill, quiet and specific and unbreakable:

Nobody was burning my valley. Nobody was taking my god. And I was done praying to empty air, because empty air, as it turned out, had been praying back.

I was halfway down the path when I saw the rider on the valley road below, moving at an easy canter toward the garrison gate, and above the rider's head, on a plain wooden pole, a parley banner — white cloth, no sigil, which somehow made it worse.

They hadn't waited for the thing in the mist.

They'd sent someone to ask questions first.

I started to run.
