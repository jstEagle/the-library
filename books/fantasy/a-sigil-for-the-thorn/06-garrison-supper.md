## 6. Garrison Supper

### Bram

Bram Oduya had cooked for kings once — well, a margrave, which was a king with worse posture and better tableware — and he would tell anyone who stood still long enough that the margrave's kitchens had taught him nothing except that rich people thought pepper was a personality. Thornvale had taught him everything else.

He stood at the great hearth of the garrison hall with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, stirring the pot the way he'd stirred it for eleven years: slow, clockwise, and with an air of profound grievance, because the stew could tell the difference. Everyone said so. Odile claimed the barley came out sulky if you stirred counterclockwise, and while Odile also claimed to have once outrun a landslide downhill, her cooking opinions had a suspiciously good track record.

The pot tonight was mutton, root vegetables, the last of the good onions, and a scandalous quantity of thyme that Marguerite had brought back from the border patrol still smelling of sun. Border herbs. Ward-grown. Bram didn't put much stock in the valley's superstitions about its own soil, but he'd be lying if he said the thyme from inside the wards didn't taste like it was trying harder.

"Supper's not ready," he announced to the room at large, before anyone had asked, because the trick to feeding soldiers was to establish dominance early.

"You always say that," said Fen, who was already hovering by the bread board with the expression of a boy watching a door he intended to slip through.

"And I'm always right."

"Yesterday I saw you hand Odile a bowl straight off the fire."

"That was charity. She looked faint."

"She'd been in the saddle since dawn."

"Precisely. Faint." Bram flicked a spoonful of gravy at him — missed, deliberately, because a hit would have ended the game too soon — and turned back to the pot.

The thing about Thornvale, he thought, ladling out portions with the mechanical precision of a man who had memorized every appetite in the room, was that nobody here was supposed to end up anywhere. A mercenary garrison in a valley the maps forgot was where soldiers went when their luck ran out or their consciences caught up with them, and usually both. Bram himself had arrived with nothing but a knife roll, a debt, and a margrave's warrant for his arrest that he kept framed above his bunk as a joke, because if you couldn't laugh at the people hunting you, they won twice.

Eleven years later the warrant hung crooked, the debt was somehow larger, and the garrison of Thornvale sat down to supper together every single night like it was a law of nature.

Ilsa took her seat first, as she always did, at the scarred head of the table. Then Odile, boots off — a miracle, noted by all — then Marguerite, quiet, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and the hills. Then Sable.

Bram's ladle paused, just slightly, over the stranger's bowl.

The hunter had been at the gate three days now under that polite little parley banner, asking questions no one wanted answered, and the captain had ordered a bed made up for them and a place at the table, because Ilsa Reyes had two settings for every situation: war and hospitality, and she never seemed entirely sure which one applied until the knives came out. So Sable ate with them. Sat with them. Watched them with those grey eyes like a person counting spoons.

Bram gave them the smallest portion and the biggest heel of bread, on principle.

"Fen," he said, sliding into his own chair at last, "say the words."

"Do I have to?"

"You have to. It's tradition."

"We made it up last winter."

"Traditions have to start somewhere. Kings don't make traditions. Cooks do. Say it."

Fen sighed the sigh of the deeply oppressed, raised his cup of watered cider, and recited: "To the valley that forgot us, and the table that remembered."

There was a general clatter of cups. Even Sable lifted theirs, after a heartbeat's hesitation, and drank, and Bram watched them do it and thought: *there*. Whatever else the hunter was, whatever polite questions they carried in their pockets like stones, they had drunk to the valley now. That meant something in this hall. That meant something even to the people pretending it didn't.

### Odile

Odile Vasse had been a scout for nineteen years, and the first rule of scouting was that you noticed things, and the second rule was that you pretended not to notice most of them, and the third rule — the one that mattered — was that you always knew exactly how many exits there were.

The garrison hall had four: the main door, the kitchen door, the cellar hatch, and the window behind Bram that stuck unless you lifted it just so, which Odile did, every meal, without looking, the way other people crossed themselves.

She was noticing several things tonight.

One: Marguerite kept flexing their left hand under the table, the way a person does when their fingers feel wrong, too warm, like they've been holding them near a flame. Odile had spent three days in the high meadows after the stars went out, and she had come back down the mountain faster than she'd ever descended anything in her life, and she had seen what the broken wards looked like from above — a ring of dead light around the whole valley, like ash on a wound. She hadn't told anyone the full shape of it yet. Some things you let settle first, like silt in a jar.

Two: Sable wasn't eating. Oh, the hunter moved food around the plate beautifully, arranged a fork through it with real artistry, but Odile had watched enough nervous recruits push stew in circles to know appetite theater when she saw it. Sable was watching the room the way Odile watched treeline. Counting. Not exits, though. *People.*

Three: Fen had seconds on his plate and was going to pretend he hadn't, and Bram was going to pretend not to see, and this small conspiracy happened nightly and was the closest thing either of them had to tenderness, and it was, honestly, the best thing Odile knew about the world.

"So," she said, propping her boots — recently re-donned, she wasn't an animal — on the bench across from her. "Border report, since everyone's pretending they don't want to ask."

Marguerite looked up. "It was quiet."

"It's never quiet anymore, Quin. The stars went out. The sky owes us an explanation."

"The sky doesn't explain itself. That's why it's the sky."

"I got within sight of the north ridge." Odile let that sit. Let the table go still around it, the way she'd learned to drop bad news — gently, flatly, like setting down something breakable. "Where the ward-stones are. There's frost on them. In midsummer."

"Frost," Ilsa said.

"Frost in a perfect circle, six feet out from each stone, and nothing growing inside it, and — this is the part I liked least — birds won't cross it. I sat and watched for an hour. They go right up to the line and turn. Like there's a wall we can't see."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the evening bell rang the hour, and Odile counted the exits again out of pure habit.

Then Bram, magnificent, immovable Bram, stood up and said: "Well, the birds can be superstitious if they like. There's pie." And he set a plum pie down in the middle of the table like a gauntlet thrown at the feet of the apocalypse, and the moment broke, because in this hall the pie always won.

Fen reached for it. Odile smacked his knuckles with a spoon without looking, because some reflexes were sacred.

"Sable," Ilsa said, mild as milk. "You've been quiet."

The hunter's grey eyes came up. "Your cook guards that pie like a border crossing. I'm choosing my moment."

A laugh went around the table — real laughter, loosening shoulders — and Odile filed away the fact that Sable could be funny on purpose, which was somehow more alarming than the frost.

*Later*, she promised herself. Later she'd take Marguerite aside, or maybe Ilsa, maybe both, and say the rest of what she'd seen on the ridge. But tonight the pie was on the table, and Fen was telling a story with his hands, and the valley that forgot magic was doing what it did best, which was forgetting, warmly, together, over plums.

* * *

### Fen

The best seat in the hall, in Fen Tarrow's professional opinion, was the corner bench, because from there you could see the whole table and the whole fire and the whole door, and nobody could get behind you. He'd learned that in his first month, back when he still flinched at slammed doors, and he'd never given the seat up and never would, and if Bram ever tried to reassign him Fen would simply lie down in front of the bench and refuse to move until the cook surrendered out of embarrassment.

He was seventeen, he'd been at the garrison two years, and he was, officially, the worst recruit Captain Reyes had ever accepted, per Captain Reyes, who said it fondly, mostly.

"—so then the goat," Fen was saying, gesturing with a spoon, "the goat gets into the supply tent, right—"

"Oh no," said Odile.

"—and it eats the *entire* bag of sugar—"

"That goat was a menace," said Bram, pointing his knife. "That goat was a criminal."

"—and then it gets into the *pepper*—"

The table lost it. Even Ilsa laughed, the short rare laugh she rationed like ammunition, and Fen rode the wave, because this was the story that never failed, the founding myth of his own arrival: the day he'd chased a runaway goat through half the valley and been hired on the spot by a captain who said, afterward, wiping tears from her eyes, "Anyone who'll run that far after a creature that stupid has soldier in him somewhere."

What he didn't say — what he never said, because it wasn't funny and because some things belonged only to you — was that before the garrison there had been the workhouse, and before the workhouse the road, and before the road a mother who'd gone to sleep in a ditch outside Thornvale one October night and hadn't woken. That the garrison hadn't taken him in out of kindness. That Ilsa had taken him in because the alternative was turning a fifteen-year-old loose with no name and no trade in a valley with nowhere to go, and Ilsa Reyes did not do that kind of arithmetic. He'd watched her face when she decided. He'd known, even then, what he was being saved from.

So when Odile asked him — casually, between bites, the way she asked everything — "What would you miss, Fen? If you had to leave tomorrow," he understood it wasn't casual at all. Nothing Odile said was casual. She was the most careful talker in the valley.

He considered giving a joke answer. The pie. Bram's singing, which was criminal in the other direction. The goat's memory.

"This bench," he said instead.

The table went quiet in the good way.

"And the bell," he said. "And Bram counting heads at supper like we're sheep that wander. And — " he shrugged, ears going hot, seventeen years old and furious at his own honesty — "all of it. All of you. Obviously."

"Obviously," Marguerite echoed softly, and smiled at him, and Fen felt the smile land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

Bram cleared his throat loudly and began stacking plates with unnecessary violence, which was how the cook said *you're welcome* and *don't look at me* and possibly *I love you too*, depending on the night. Tonight, Fen suspected, it was all three.

### Bram

Later, when the plates were scraped and Fen had been sent to bed protesting and Odile had vanished out the sticking window on some scout's errand of her own devising, Bram stayed behind to bank the fire, as he always did, and found the hall not empty.

Sable stood by the hearth, watching the coals.

"Cook," the hunter said.

"Hunter." Bram picked up the poker. "You left half your supper."

"I wasn't hungry."

"Everyone's hungry. They're just sometimes hungry for the wrong thing." He jabbed the coals into a tidy orange sleep. "You want to ask me something. You've been circling it since the bread course. Ask."

Sable was silent a moment. Firelight did strange things to that grey face — aged it, softened it, neither comfortably. "Why do you feed a stranger who might be here to hurt you?"

"Because the stranger might not be. And because my mother fed strangers, and her mother before her, and because the night you stop feeding people who frighten you is the night you become the sort of person worth frightening." Bram set the poker down. "Also, because Ilsa told me to, and I argue with her maybe twice a year, and I used one up in spring over the price of salt."

Something flickered across Sable's face that might have been the ghost of a smile. "You love them," the hunter said. It wasn't quite a question. "All of them. This ragged little garrison at the edge of nowhere."

"They're not at the edge of nowhere," Bram said. "They *are* somewhere. That's the whole point of them." He gathered the last cups onto his tray. "You'd know that, if you'd ever had a table."

He said it plainly, without cruelty, the way you'd mention weather, and walked toward the kitchen door, and stopped there with his hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth, hunter — whoever sent you, whatever they think is in this valley — they've made a mistake. People always do, when they mistake a family for a fortress." He glanced back. "Fortresses fall. Families dig in."

In the kitchen, alone, Bram began washing dishes, and did not think about frost on ward-stones, or birds refusing a line they could not see, or the way Marguerite Quintana had flexed their left hand all through supper like a person holding something warm that no one else could feel.

He thought about it anyway. He was a cook, not a fool.

Outside, far off toward the pass, a horn sounded — once, low, and wrong, a note that didn't belong to any garrison signal — and in the silence that followed, every soul in Thornvale who was awake heard it, and every one of them thought of someone else at the table, and no one slept well at all.
