## 7. Sable's Bargain

I have lied to inquisitors before. It is not a skill I recommend acquiring; the hours are terrible and the retirement plan involves fire.

But lying to Inquisitor Corvane was different from lying to the fat provincial ones I'd cut my teeth on. The provincials lie to themselves constantly — about their piety, their competence, their hairlines — so they are easy to fool. Corvane lies to nothing. He is a man who has sanded himself down until only function remains, and functions do not mislead each other. When he asked me, on the second morning of his parley, whether I had sensed anything anomalous at Thornvale's border, he already knew the answer. He was asking to see what I did with my face.

So I gave him my face.

"Nothing," I said. "The wards are dead stone. Whatever used to live here died with them."

He watched me for four full seconds. Corvane counts seconds the way other men count money, except he spends them freely because he knows he'll get them back with interest. Then he smiled — the small, dry movement of a man acknowledging a colleague's craft rather than believing its product — and said, "Thank you, Sable. You've been very thorough."

That *thank you* sat in my stomach all day like a swallowed nail. Because I knew what came after thorough. Thorough meant he trusted me to be useful, which meant he'd stopped watching me closely, which meant he'd already decided what the truth was and didn't need me to confirm it. Inquisitors don't investigate. They arrive at conclusions and then collect evidence the way a man collects debts — selectively, and with violence held in reserve.

Corvane knew. Of course he knew. The stars had gone out over half an empire that night, and every hunter's compass in the Order had swung toward this valley like a needle finding north. The only reason we were sitting in a parley tent drinking the garrison's thin cider instead of burning the palisade was arithmetic: Corvane had twelve hunters and Thornvale had forty garrison soldiers who knew the ground, plus whatever the spark inside Marguerite Quintana might do if cornered. He was waiting for reinforcements. Three hundred of them, marching up the river road, eleven days out if the weather held.

Eleven days. And everyone in that valley thought the polite questions about "anomalous births" were just imperial bureaucracy doing its slow, stupid dance.

* * *

I found Marguerite at the archery butts behind the barracks, shooting alone in the grey end of afternoon. That was itself worth noting. Mercenaries shoot in pairs or not at all; solitude on a range usually means a person is arguing with a god or with themselves, and given recent events, Marguerite had cause for both.

They loosed, missed left by a hand's width, swore with real feeling, and nocked another arrow without noticing me leaning on the fence. Good instincts, bad attention. Or maybe their instincts had decided I wasn't a threat yet, which was more flattering than accurate.

"You're gripping too high," I said.

"I'm gripping fine."

"You're choking the bow like it owes you money. Loosen your fingers. Let the wood decide when to let go."

Marguerite glanced at me sidelong — those dark eyes doing their careful accounting — and adjusted their grip by exactly one degree, which was their way of accepting help without admitting to it. They loosed again. The arrow struck near center.

"Huh," they said.

"The bow likes you better when you stop trying to control it. There's probably a lesson there." I let that sit for a moment. "Can we talk? Somewhere without ears?"

"There's nowhere in Thornvale without ears. Bram's kitchen alone has four."

"Then somewhere where the ears are friendly."

They studied me properly then. I want to say something noble about how trust passed between us in that moment, but the truth is uglier and more honest: we looked at each other and both did the same calculation, the one where you weigh a stranger against a cliff edge and ask yourself how much you'd mind falling together. Marguerite finished first. They always would, I think. It's the mercenary habit — decisions made fast so you can spend the saved time surviving them.

"The old watchtower on Crag Hill," they said. "Halfway up. Nobody goes there since the roof fell in."

* * *

The tower smelled of bird droppings and thirty years of weather, and through its broken crown you could see the whole valley laid out like something precious someone had left unattended. Fields going gold at the edges. Smoke from the garrison kitchens standing straight in the still air. The border stones circling everything, gray and mute, dead as teeth.

Marguerite climbed up beside me onto the fallen lintel and sat with the ease of a person who had grown up on these hills. For a while neither of us spoke. I have learned that silence is a tool with two edges, and I handed this one over deliberately. Let them think I was nervous. I wasn't. I was terrified, but nerves and terror wear different faces, and I've practiced the wrong one for years.

"Ask your question," Marguerite said finally.

"How do you know I have one?"

"Because witch-hunters don't climb hills to admire views, and because you keep glancing at my hands."

I hadn't known I was doing that. Annoying, being read by someone whose entire training was in hitting things. I folded my hands into my sleeves and made myself look at the valley instead.

"Fine," I said. "No question. A statement, then. Inquisitor Corvane knows what you are."

The wind moved through the broken roof. Down in the valley, a dog barked twice and gave up, the way dogs do, having opinions but no follow-through.

"He suspects," Marguerite said carefully.

"He doesn't suspect. Suspecting is what he does to people he intends to release. He knows. He felt the wards break from sixty miles away — we all did, it's why I was assigned here. He's asking his gentle questions about birth records because he needs the vessel identified beyond doubt before the tribunal signs the order, and because—" I paused, and chose honesty over comfort, which cost me something physical, like setting down a weight I'd carried so long it had grown into my shoulder. "—and because he wants to give you time to run. Running confirms. It saves him a trial."

Marguerite was quiet. Then: "Run where?"

"That's the right question. Most people ask *why*."

"I'm a mercenary. Why is a luxury. Where keeps you alive."

Something loosened in my chest that I refused to name. "There's a pass. Old shepherd road, northwest, above the tree line. The maps call it the Hollow Stair because the empire erased it after the Binding Wars — they erased anything that led out of valleys like this one. But roads don't die just because men forget them. I know it. Every switchback, every shrine-ruin, every place to hide from aerial scrying. I could take you over it. Eleven days' march, and you're beyond the Reach, into the free cantons, where the Order's writ ends at the last cairn."

"Why?" Marguerite said. And there it was — *why*, the luxury, deployed at precisely the moment it mattered. Their eyes were steady on mine, patient as winter. "You're one of them. Hunter's braid, Order signet, the whole costume. Why would you burn all that for a stranger?"

Because you feel like a hearth-fire under my skin whenever you're within ten feet of me, and I haven't been warm since I was six years old. Because I have spent nineteen years hunting children like the one I used to be, and I am done. Because yesterday you laughed at Odile's terrible joke and something in my chest unclenched for the first time in a decade.

"Because Corvane is wrong about one thing," I said. "He thinks the spark is a resource. A thing to be extracted. I've stood in the machine-hall beneath the capital. I've seen what they feed it. If you go to the capital, they will hollow you out over months, politely, with prayers, and what's left afterward isn't a person anymore. I've emptied the buckets. Don't ask me to describe them."

"And the Order lets you just... walk away? Over a pass? Hunters travel in pairs at minimum. Corvane will assign you to escort duty himself, won't he — the tracker who found nothing, sent to make sure the nothing stays nothing."

"He will," I agreed. "Which brings me to the price."

Marguerite's mouth curved, humorless. "There it is."

"There's always a price. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something larger." I turned to face them fully, on the cold stone, with the valley glowing below us like a thing already mourned. "I can't desert alone. Deserters are tracked by blood-scent for a year. Alone, I'm caught inside a month, and worse — caught means questioned, and questioned means I tell them everything, including this pass, including you. My discipline breaks under the rite of truth. I know because I've administered it. So I need—" I made myself say it, plain, no varnish. "Help. Your garrison. Not to fight — to cover the leaving. A diversion the night we go. Something loud enough and far enough from the pass that the pursuit starts pointed the wrong way. Odile's good enough to fake a raid trail. Fen's young enough to be believed as a panicked witness. And I need someone to hold Corvane's attention for the first three days while we're climbing, because he'll know within a day that I'm gone and he'll guess the direction."

"And you're telling me this instead of just doing it, because—"

"Because if I take your people into danger without their knowledge, I'm no better than the empire that took me." The words came out flatter than I intended. True things often do; they don't perform well. "And because you deserve to choose. Everyone in this story has decided things about you without asking. Ilsa. The Order. The god, maybe. I'm asking."

* * *

Marguerite looked at the valley for a long time.

I let them. This was theirs to carry, and I understood — not sympathetically, but structurally, the way you understand a bridge by knowing where the load sits — what the view was costing them. Those fields. That smoke. The dog with its abandoned opinions. A whole life built on top of a secret they'd never consented to, and now the bill coming due in someone else's handwriting.

"My mother was born here," Marguerite said at last. "My grandmother. Probably her mother too. All of us born inside a cage someone built to hide us, and none of us told." A breath. "Ilsa's going to have to explain that eventually. But not tonight. Tonight I'm deciding about you."

"Fair."

"It's not fair. It's necessary. There's a difference and mercenaries know it." They turned. "Here's my problem, Sable. Everything you've said checks out. The pass exists — Odile's grandfather herded sheep over something up there before the maps went blank. The Order's methods check out against every horror story my old sergeant ever told. Even your fear checks out; liars perform fear better than that. So either you're true, or you're the best trap the empire ever baited, and I can't tell which from the outside. Nobody could."

"So what do you do?"

"What I always do. I test the ground before I put weight on it." They held up fingers. "One: you tell me your name. Not your Order designation — your name, the one somebody gave you before the Order renamed you."

The question hit harder than any blow Corvane could have thrown. I sat with it. Nineteen years of *Tracker Sable*, of the single word worn smooth as a river stone from use, and underneath it—

"Ashka," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable like dry wood. "My mother called me Ashka."

Marguerite nodded slowly, filing it away, and I understood they'd never use it carelessly. "Two: the diversion happens only if the garrison agrees. I don't own them. I'll bring it to them straight, your offer and your risk both, and if Bram votes no, we find another way or we don't go."

"They'll vote yes."

"They might surprise you. They're sentimental idiots." A ghost of a smile. "Three. If this is a trap — if you deliver me to Corvane — I won't kill you. I want that clear. I'll just leave you alive somewhere the Order can find you wearing my testimony carved into your skin about what you sold. Vethra's faithful are supposed to be silent, but I hear we make exceptions for traitors."

It was, I reflected, the most romantic threat anyone had ever made me, and I told them so.

"Don't flirt while negotiating," Marguerite said. "It's how empires win."

"Everything's how empires win, according to people who've never run one." I stuck out my hand, palm open — a soldier's grip, no ceremony. "Ashka Sable. Deserter as of the moment you shake it. No going back for either of us."

They looked at my hand. At me. And then Marguerite Quintana did the thing that decided me, more than any speech could have: they hesitated. Honestly, visibly, weighing it — trusting me imperfectly, with their eyes open, choosing anyway. That's the only kind of trust worth anything. The certain kind is just obedience wearing a nicer coat.

They shook.

Their hand was warm. Warmer than a hand should be, warm in a way that had nothing to do with blood, and I felt the spark recognize me through the contact — a faint pulse, like a second heartbeat arriving late — and I saw them feel it too, saw their breath catch.

"It does that around you," they said quietly. "Started two days ago. Around you specifically. Should I be worried?"

I should have lied. A good liar would have lied.

"No," I said. "Hunters are trained to carry a null — a sliver of dead godstone in our sternum charms, so vessels can't sense us coming. Mine broke the night the stars went out. I haven't replaced it." I met their eyes. "I could tell you that was tactical. Keeping my options open. It wasn't. I just couldn't make myself do it. I'd been feeling you for two days before I ever climbed this hill, and I didn't want it to stop."

The wind came through the broken roof again, carrying woodsmoke and autumn off the fields below. Marguerite didn't let go of my hand. Neither did I.

"Well," they said at last, rough-voiced. "Then we'd better move fast. Reinforcements, you said. How long?"

"Eleven days. Maybe ten."

"Then tomorrow night, I talk to the garrison. And Sable—"

"Ashka," I corrected. "If we're doing this, do it properly."

A pause. Something moved across their face that I chose, selfishly, to read as tenderness.

"Ashka," they said, testing the weight of it. "Tomorrow night, then. Pray your gods I can convince a room full of sentimental idiots to burn their own peace treaty."

"I don't pray," I said. "But I'll stand outside the door and listen, which is close enough."

We climbed down from the ruined tower as the light failed, two liars-turned-partners descending toward a valley that didn't yet know it had eleven days to decide what it was willing to lose. Behind us the border stones stood in their circle, gray and dead.

Ahead, in the barracks, lamplight was kindling window by window — and in Captain Reyes's office, I noticed, a lamp burned late, and her shadow crossed the glass twice, pacing.

She already knew something too. I was starting to understand that in Thornvale, everyone knew something, and the knowing was arranged like a card game where every player held one card and no one held the deck.

Tomorrow night, the cards would hit the table.

I only hoped that when they did, the garrison would forgive me for what I was about to cost them — and forgive Marguerite for what they were about to choose.
