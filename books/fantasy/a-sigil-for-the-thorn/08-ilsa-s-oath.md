## 8. Ilsa's Oath

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a commander's office at night, after the lamps have been trimmed low and the reports have been read twice for lack of anything better to do. It is not peace. Peace is a thing I remember only secondhand, the way you remember the shape of your mother's hands. This silence is the kind that waits. It has been waiting thirty years, and tonight it finally caught me.

Marguerite sat across from my desk with their arms crossed like a barricade, which was fair. I had summoned them at an hour no one wants to be summoned, in the tone no one wants to be summoned in, and they had come anyway, because that is what my people do. They come when called. They always have. That is precisely why what I had to say next was going to cost me everything.

"You already told me," Marguerite said. "The wards weren't built to keep magic out."

"I told you part of it." I turned my cup on its saucer, a full rotation, then another. A captain of Thornvale garrison learns early that her hands need somewhere honest to be. "Part of a truth is worse than a lie, in my experience. A lie, once discovered, is finished with you. Part of a truth follows you around for decades, clearing its throat."

"So clear yours."

Gods, but they were Ilsa Reyes's soldier to the bone. Direct as a spear shaft. They got it from me, which meant I could hardly complain about being on the receiving end.

"Thirty years ago," I said, "I helped kill a god."

* * *

Here is how it happened, or here is how I carry it, which may amount to the same thing after enough winters.

I was twenty-two, newly sworn, third sword in a garrison of nine, and Thornvale was not yet mine to protect so much as mine to be posted to — the end of the world, a green pocket between two gray ranges where nothing ever happened except weather and Bram's experiments with pepper. The empire was a rumor we paid taxes to. Magic was a story grandmothers told about the mountains, the way they told stories about wolves. Neither had been seen in living memory, and everyone agreed this was simply how the world was now.

Except it wasn't. The world had been *edited*, and I didn't know it yet.

The old captain then was a woman named Hessa Vane, who drank more than she slept and prayed more than she drank, which by Thornvale accounting made her a saint. On the night it happened she woke me past midnight, put a finger to her lips, and led me up through the hills behind the garrison to a shrine I had walked past a hundred times without seeing.

That was the trick of it, I learned later. Not-seeing. The valley was very good at it. We all were. It was bred into us the way good eyesight is bred into hawks, generation over generation: look away, look away, nothing here, nothing here. The wards did not merely keep magic out. They kept *attention* out. Ours included.

Inside the ruin, someone was dying loudly.

I want to be precise about that, because people imagine gods die quietly, like candles. This one did not. What lay in the shattered heart of that shrine was light the way a scream is sound — too much of it, in the wrong register, hurting something deep in the ear even though there was nothing to hear. And beneath the light, a voice, and the voice said — I can still recite it, word for word, thirty years later, which tells you something about what it carved into me —

*"They are coming. Take what is left of me and hide it where their machine cannot smell it. Hide it in blood. Hide it in flesh. Hide it in a life, and let the life be loved, because love is the one ward I cannot build myself."*

Hessa knelt beside the light. She had a bundle in her arms. A newborn, hours old, born that night in the garrison's own infirmary to a mother who had died bringing him into the world, which meant the child belonged to no family and to all of us, and Hessa — pragmatic, devout, terrified Hessa — had understood before any of us what the god was asking.

Because it wasn't asking. Let me be clear about that too. People want the story where the god begged and the woman refused and was compelled, because complicity with a clean conscience is easier to sell afterward. No one begged. Vethra stated terms the way a dying man states a will, and Hessa Vane, captain of Thornvale, looked down at that squalling scrap of infant and made a decision in less time than it takes to draw a blade.

"The machine," she said. "You mean the empire's god-machine. The one in the capital."

*"It fed on me for a thousand years,"* the light answered. *"Now it will starve, and when it starves, the empire will turn over every stone in creation looking for what it lost. If they find me, they will take me back and I will burn for them another thousand years, and every vessel between now and forever will be a lamp they light themselves with. Hide me small. Hide me mortal. Hide me loved."*

And Hessa — I watched her do it, I stood three paces away holding the lantern and doing absolutely nothing, which is the role I would play in this story for the next thirty years, the one holding the lantern — Hessa laid the baby against the light and spoke words I did not know and have never found in any book since. Old words. Valley words, maybe, from before the editing. The light went into the child the way rain goes into dry ground, and the screaming stopped, and the shrine went dark, and the stars came back out overhead like nothing had happened.

The baby stopped crying too. He looked up at Hessa with those unfocused newborn eyes, and he yawned, and Hessa burst into tears, which I had never seen her do and would not see her do again until the day she died.

"What did we just do?" I asked her.

"We just bought a valley," she said. "Pray we never learn the price."

* * *

In the present, my office lamp guttered and I trimmed it, mostly to keep my hands busy, entirely because my hands wanted to shake.

Marguerite hadn't moved. They were watching me the way Odile watches a trail that doesn't sit right — patient, total, missing nothing. I had the sudden absurd awareness that I had taught them that patience myself, on patrol, years ago, correcting their stance in the wet grass. Everything I'd built had grown up inside the lie I was about to hand them.

"Say the name," Marguerite said quietly. "Or don't. But stop circling it, Captain."

"His name was Aldric," I said.

Something crossed their face — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. Aldric Quintana. Dead of fever at nineteen, eleven years back, buried on the hill with the rest of ours, mourned by the whole garrison because everyone mourned Aldric; he'd had that way about him. Marguerite's older brother. The brother who used to carry them on his shoulders to market. The brother whose boots Marguerite still kept oiled, I realized, and had always assumed it was grief.

It was not only grief.

"He was born the night the god died," I said. "He was the vessel. He carried Vethra's spark his whole life and never knew it — none of us told him, per Hessa's design. The spark slept in him. Fed on his living, I think, on being loved by a whole garrison, exactly as the god asked. And when he burned with fever at nineteen—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I took a breath and dragged it back into line. "When he burned, the spark nearly burned with him. It panicked. It flared. Half the valley felt heat under their skin that night and blamed the weather."

Marguerite's hand had gone to their own sternum. Without meaning to. The way you touch an old scar.

"And then it left him," they said. "Before he died."

"It moved. Yes." I turned the cup again. Full rotation. "A spark that has lived in one body knows the road to the next body in that body's line. Blood remembers. Hessa knew it would happen someday; she'd built the whole arrangement on it. What she never wrote down — and I've cursed her for thirty years for not writing it down — was whether the spark chooses the moment or the vessel does. Whether Aldric sent it to you, or whether it simply fled a burning house."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me," I said. "Because if he chose, then your brother spent his last conscious act deciding you were strong enough to carry what killed him young. And if he didn't choose, then I spent thirty years letting you walk around inside a sentence you never agreed to. Either way, Captain Reyes of thirty years ago should have told you at nineteen. At fifteen. At ten, maybe, the first time you cut your palm on a fence wire and the wound closed wrong-fast. I saw that, Marguerite. I saw it and I filed it under 'valley oddity' because filing it under 'my fault' required a courage I did not have."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, Fen was laughing at something Odile had said, bright and unguarded, and the sound of it went through me like a splinter. That was the other half of the arithmetic, the part I couldn't leave out. Every year I kept the secret was another year the garrison stayed whole, stayed safe, stayed a family — because the wards held, and the wards held because the spark stayed hidden and quiet and unremarked. Tell the truth early and the empire finds out early. The truth had a body count attached. I had chosen the lie the way you choose a tourniquet, knowing it kills the limb to save the rest, and I had told myself for thirty years that the limb was my own conscience and a fair price.

Sitting across from Marguerite Quintana, I finally understood the limb had never been mine.

* * *

There was a middle night I have never told anyone about, and I suppose if I am emptying the ledger, I should empty all of it.

Eleven years ago. Two days after we buried Aldric. I went up to the shrine ruin alone, drunk on Bram's plum spirit and grief, and I screamed at the dark that Hessa's arrangement had failed, that the price had come due, that a boy was dead and it was our doing, ours, mine, and what kind of god hid itself in children and let them die for it.

And the dark — the dark answered.

Not Vethra. Something of Vethra, the way an echo is something of a voice. Faint, frayed, half of it already across whatever distance divides a spark from its god. It said: *"He did not suffer. I took the suffering with me. Ask the one who carries me now whether they dream of falling, and tell them the ground is not real."*

I asked. Marguerite, seventeen years old, furious and hollowed out with grief, admitted — once, late, on watch, thinking I wouldn't remember — that they dreamed constantly of falling and never hit bottom, and hated that the falling felt almost like flying.

I never told them the ground was not real. I never told them what the echo had said. I filed it. I filed everything. Thirty years of lantern-holding, and I told myself it was duty.

Duty is the word cowards carve over courage on their own headstones. I know, because I've had thirty years to practice the carving.

* * *

"So Corvane knows," Marguerite said at last. Their voice was level in a way I recognized from the mirror: levelness as load-bearing structure. "The inquisitor. He knows what's in me."

"He suspects. He's certain enough to stall instead of strike, which for an inquisitor is a confession." I set the cup down and made myself meet their eyes. "He'll wait for reinforcements because Corvane does nothing that might fail. When they come — days, not weeks — he'll ask for you politely. Then impolitely. Then he'll burn the valley down to the root rather than report failure to the machine that feeds him."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because you deserve to run toward something instead of being handed over," I said. "And because I owe you a debt thirty years old, and debts to the dead are cheap. Debts to the living are paid in coin that bleeds."

For a long moment Marguerite said nothing. I let them. A commander learns when silence is hers to spend and when it isn't; this hour it wasn't.

"My brother," they said finally. "You let me grieve him as a stranger. You stood at his grave with me and you knew."

"Yes."

"You let me pray to Vethra my whole life. Fifteen years of prayers into silence, and you knew the god was *in the room*—"

"In your blood. Asleep. Dying, maybe, or maybe just dreaming. Marguerite, I don't know what fifteen years of silence means for a god. I know what it meant for a girl who needed answers and got weather instead."

"That's not forgiveness, Captain."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. I'm not asking for it tonight. Forgiveness is a thing you earn in installments, and I've barely begun paying. What I'm asking is smaller and harder. I'm asking you to believe that everything else was true. The patrols, the training, the suppers, the name on the roll, the way I'd have taken a blade for you before you could blink. None of it was built on the lie. Only the foundation was. And foundations—" my voice cracked clean through this time, and I let it, "—foundations are exactly the sort of thing a captain is supposed to check."

Marguerite stood. Walked to the window. Stood looking out at the garrison yard, where the lamps of home burned in their familiar little constellations, and I watched their shoulders and waited for the verdict the way I had once waited for Hessa's, three paces back, holding the lantern.

"When the reinforcements come," they said, without turning, "whose side are you on? The valley's? Or the oath you swore to Hessa?"

I laughed, short and bitter, because it was either laugh or weep and I had spent my weeping ration decades ago.

"Child, I have spent thirty years discovering that those were never the same side. Hessa's oath was to hide you. Mine—" I stood, joints announcing themselves, old soldier's litany, "—mine is newer than hers. Mine is to the people in that yard. All of them. Including you, including especially you, including the parts of you I helped bury alive under a secret."

They turned then. Their eyes had the heat in them now, literal, banked, the ember-light I'd pretended not to see for years whenever they were angry. God forgive me — whichever god is listening, and I suspect these days it's only the one I made.

"Then help me get them out," Marguerite said. "All of them. Before Corvane's reinforcements arrive."

Outside, far off toward the pass, a single red flare climbed into the dark and bloomed there, hanging, obscene — the signal Odile's outriders used for one word only.

Riders.

Not ours.
