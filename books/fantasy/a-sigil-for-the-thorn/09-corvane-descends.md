## 9. Corvane Descends

I have always found that the best way to break a man's resolve is to arrive politely.

Inquisitor Corvane does not thunder. Thunder is for priests who have never watched a man die and mistaken it for revelation. I arrived at Thornvale's gate on a grey morning with two hundred riders at my back, banners furled, armor oiled to a modest shine — enough force to be undeniable, not so much as to be theatrical. Theatrics invite resistance. Resistance invites blood. Blood stains the soul in ways that are difficult to launder before one stands before the Machine.

The gate sergeant who met me had the look of a man who had already been awake for three days. Good. Tired men negotiate.

"Inquisitor," he said. "Captain Reyes asks that you wait at the boundary stone."

"Captain Reyes may ask," I said pleasantly, "and I may decline. Lead on."

He led on.

* * *

You should understand something about me, since you have been kind enough to open this account of my life: I did not choose this work because I hate witches. Hatred is a luxury of men who do not know what failure costs. I chose it because I was eleven years old and standing in a bread line in the capital when the Machine first faltered, and I remember what the city became in the six days it went dark.

Not dark in the sense of lamps. Dark in the sense of meaning.

The wards that shield the empire's roads failed first. Then the granaries' preserving sigils, then the healing houses. Then the prisons. Then the thing under the Palace of Records that has no name because naming it would require admitting it exists. Six days. Eleven thousand dead by official count, which means perhaps thirty thousand by any honest one. And through all of it, the Machine itself — the great engine beneath the capital, older than the dynasty, older than the empire's founding lie — lay silent as a dead hearth, and every priest in the city pretended not to notice that their god had stopped breathing.

It breathed again on the seventh day. No one ever told the people why. I was eleven years old, and I stood in that bread line and watched a woman beat another woman to death over a half-loaf, and I understood, in the way children understand things they will spend their whole lives refusing to unlearn, that our god is a furnace and we are its fuel, and that everything beautiful about the empire — the roads, the peace, the long golden afternoon of it — is bought with someone's burning.

Most men spend their lives refusing to unlearn such things. I made a career of learning them thoroughly.

So no, I do not hate witches. I pity them, the way one pities firewood. It is not the wood's fault that there is a cold room waiting.

* * *

The garrison received me in its hall with a courtesy so strained it nearly sang. Captain Ilsa Reyes sat at the head of the table like a woman presiding over her own funeral banquet and determined to enjoy the food. I knew her name from the old rolls — Reyes, veteran of the border actions, decorated twice, passed over for promotion for reasons the paperwork described as "temperament" and the gossip described as "honesty." An honest soldier posted to guard an empty valley for twenty years. The empire wastes its best that way, which is perhaps why it endures.

"Inquisitor Corvane," she said. "We weren't expecting you until spring."

"I'm given to understand the stars went out here some nights ago," I said. "I try never to keep an appointment with a miracle. They're terribly unreliable about waiting."

A young officer by the window shifted her weight. A boy in garrison colors — sixteen at most, with the particular rigid stillness of someone trying very hard not to look at something — stood near the door. And at the table's edge, half in shadow, sat a mercenary whose file I had read four times on the road north. Marguerite Quintana. Sworn to Vethra. Twenty-odd years born and bred inside these wards, which was precisely the point of them.

I smiled at each of them in turn, the way one smiles at a locked chest while deciding whether to pick it or simply burn the house around it.

"You'll want to see the ward-stones," Reyes said. "There's damage—"

"There's devastation, Captain. Let us use honest words; they're cheaper in the long run. Your valley's wards — raised, I am told, by the founder's covenant, maintained at imperial expense for three generations without once being asked why — shattered nine nights ago. Something crossed your boundary that night. Something came out." I folded my hands. "Or something woke up inside."

"Nothing came out," Reyes said. "Nothing woke. We've patrolled every mile of the border."

"Then your patrols are better than your stars, because mine went out too, Captain. Every star above this valley, for the length of a night. Do you know how much power that requires? Neither do I. That is rather the problem." I let the silence sit a moment, then softened — one must soften; a hammer applied continuously only teaches the nail to hold harder. "But I am not here to accuse. I am here to ask questions. Politely. You'll find I prefer polite questions. They get answered more often."

* * *

That evening I walked the garrison yard alone, which is to say accompanied by four hunters whom everyone politely pretended were scenery. Sable was among them. My tracker. My finest and most troublesome acquisition.

Sable had come back from the parley three weeks past with a report so meticulously empty that it could only have been carved with great care. *No anomalies observed. Population stable. No signs of working.* Beautifully written. I have read lies in a hundred hands and Sable's are the finest in the order — which is exactly why I assigned Sable here, and exactly why I knew, before I ever crossed the boundary stone, that there was something in this valley worth finding.

One does not tell one's tracker this. One lets them discover that you know, slowly, the way frost discovers a window.

"The vessel," I said quietly, watching the yard. "Your assessment."

Sable came to my shoulder. My tracker has the gift of stillness; even breathing seems to be optional. "No anomaly," Sable said. "As reported."

"Mm. The wards fell, Sable. Wards do not fall for nothing. They fall for *something*, or they fall *from* something. Which?"

A pause. Long enough to be counted. "I don't know, Inquisitor."

"Liar," I said fondly, and turned away before the flinch could fully form. Cruelty is a tool, but one uses it sparingly, like saffron. "Sleep well. Tomorrow we begin the census. House to house. Every birth record, every baptismal roll, every midwife's ledger going back thirty years."

Thirty years. Because I had done the arithmetic on the ride north, and the arithmetic was elegant. The Machine feeds on what the old texts call the *anointed residue* — the spark that pools in certain rare bloodlines, vessels born perhaps once a generation. The last confirmed vessel was rendered unto the Machine twenty-nine years ago. A girl from the river provinces. She lasted eleven months. Since then the Machine has been fed substitutes — prisoners, heretics, poor souls swept up in the purges — and it has grown thinner with every year, the way a body grows thin on bread when it is starving for meat.

And now, in the same season, the wards over a hidden valley fail, and the stars go out, and somewhere in this garrison a mercenary sworn to a silent god walks around with heat under their skin that they cannot explain.

The empire calls it providence. I call it arithmetic. Providence is merely arithmetic that flatters God.

* * *

I will confess what keeps me awake in strange beds, since confession is supposed to be good for the soul and mine requires all the help available.

I have fed the Machine. That is not a phrase one uses lightly, but I will use it plainly because plainness is the only honesty left to a man in my office. Twice in my career I have stood in the feeding chamber beneath the capital and administered the rite myself. The chamber is older than language, they say. The Machine fills it — a vast dark presence you feel more than see, a pressure behind the walls, a weight in the teeth. When the vessel is brought in, the whole room leans toward them. Not mechanically. *Hungrily.* There is no other word for it, though the liturgy offers several, all of them lies.

The first was a man who had burned a chapel. He screamed for the length of it. They usually do. The second was a woman who did not scream at all. She looked at the Machine — at the dark that leaned toward her — and she said, very clearly, "You're hungry." And then she said, "Poor thing," and I have not slept entirely well since.

Because she was right. That is the horror I carry, the one the empire cannot afford for me to carry: the Machine is not a devourer. Devourers enjoy. The Machine is *starving*. It takes and takes and is not fed, the way a man dying of thirst drinks seawater. Everything we pour into it goes down like a stone into a well, and the well does not rise, and the priests sing louder to cover the sound.

If the Machine dies, the wards die. The roads die. The granaries rot, the plagues walk, the provinces remember they were conquered rather than joined. I have seen the projections the Archivists pretend not to keep. Fracture within twenty years. War within forty. The long golden afternoon ends, and night comes down on ten million people who never asked to be warm at anyone else's expense.

And if the Machine lives, it lives on vessels. On girls who say "poor thing." On whatever child in this valley has spent their whole life inside a lie built of wards and silence.

I have made my peace with this. I want to be clear about that. I am not a man at war with himself; I find such men tedious and dangerous. I looked into the well, I understood the arithmetic, and I chose the empire. Ten million against one. Any honest mind makes the same choice; the dishonest ones simply refuse to say it aloud.

But I will tell you the thought that visits me at night, the small cold visitor: *one day the Machine will finish starving, or finish eating, and either way the arithmetic will have been wrong all along, and every girl who said 'poor thing' will have been poured into a furnace for nothing.*

I put the thought out like a candle. It always relights.

* * *

On the third morning I gave them their day.

I assembled the garrison in the yard — all of them, cooks and scouts and the boy Fen who stood too straight — and I spoke from the steps of the hall in the voice I save for such occasions: reasonable, regretful, absolutely final.

"People of Thornvale," I said. "I know what you believe this valley is. A quiet place. A forgotten posting. A valley where magic cannot enter, and therefore never needed to leave. I am here to tell you that this belief is a kindness someone paid for, and the payment has lapsed." I let them murmur. Murmuring is healthy; it is silence one fears. "Nine nights ago your wards broke. Whatever they concealed is now legible to every instrument in the capital. Reinforcements are coming — more than you have seen, I promise you, and less polite than I am. So I offer you the only mercy arithmetic allows.

"One day. At sundown tomorrow, the vessel will be surrendered to my custody — alive, unharmed, and treated thereafter with every dignity the rite permits. In exchange, Thornvale stands. Your captain keeps her command, your families keep their homes, and no hunter's torch touches a single roof in this valley." I paused, and gave them the smile that my own novices privately call the wolf's courtesy. "Refuse, and I will take the vessel anyway, and I will bill the difference to this valley in ash. I say this without anger. I bear none of you ill will. I am simply the man who arrives when the accounting comes due.

"One day," I said again. "Choose."

* * *

They dispersed in that particular silence a crowd makes when it is pretending it hasn't heard. Reyes saluted — crisp, correct, her face a fortress with nothing behind the gates but more fortress. The boy Fen stared at me with the naked hatred of the young, which I found almost touching; it is the last luxury age takes from us. And Marguerite Quintana stood at the edge of the formation and met my eyes across the yard, and did not flinch, and did not look away.

There it was, then. The heat under the skin. I have hunted vessels for twenty years and I can smell the spark like a hound smells rain — not literally, of course; the order tried to breed such hounds once and got dogs that howled at their own handlers. But there is a way a certain person stands when they are carrying something enormous and have just learned its size. The shoulders set differently. The eyes go quiet.

Quintana had those shoulders. Had them, I would wager, for nine nights exactly.

I inclined my head to them, slightly — one professional acknowledging another — and turned back to my officers.

"Sable," I said. "A word."

My tracker came. Stillness walking.

"Tomorrow at sundown," I said, "the vessel comes to me. If it does not, this valley burns, and you and I both know you have grown — attached — to things here that burning would complicate." I kept my voice gentle. Gentleness is the sharpest thing I own. "So I am asking you, as the finest instrument I possess: between now and sundown, find out where it runs. Because it will run, Sable. They always run. And I would very much like to be wrong about everything else, but never about that."

Sable said nothing. Sable's silence, I have long suspected, contains entire libraries.

"One day," I repeated, mostly to myself, and looked up at the grey sky where the stars had gone out and not yet returned, and felt beneath my feet — faint, patient, terrible — the pull of the well that never rises, calling me home to feed it.

Somewhere in this valley, a person who said nothing yet was deciding whether to run.

I hoped, for all our sakes, they would run far.

I hoped, for mine, they wouldn't run fast enough.
