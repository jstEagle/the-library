## 10. The Leaving

I had a plan. It wasn't a good plan — good plans involve supplies, routes, and at least one person who knows you're going — but it was mine, and I'd made it in the dark like all my best decisions.

Leave before the fourth bell. Take the north trail past the old kiln where the wards used to hum loudest, because nobody patrolled there anymore and because sentimentality is a poor tracker. Over the pass before Corvane's reinforcements closed the southern roads. Alone.

The alone was the whole point. That was the part I kept having to explain to myself, the way you explain things to a stubborn animal: *Look. Look at the arithmetic.* One person moving fast leaves no dust. One person caught gives them nothing to burn. Every soul that came with me was a soul Corvane could put on a list, and Corvane struck me as a fellow who kept very tidy lists.

Besides — and here's the part I didn't say out loud even to myself — if Vethra's spark could be cut out of me, or burned out of me, or bled out of me on some imperial altar, then everything it touched was a liability. The garrison. Sable, who'd already given up more for me than I'd earned. The valley itself, which had spent thirty years being forgotten on purpose and deserved better than to be remembered violently.

So: alone. Quietly. Like a coward with excellent posture.

I packed by feel. Bread from the loaf Bram had left cooling on the windowsill of the kitchen — I told myself he'd have offered, which is the kind of theft that feels like friendship — a skin of water, the short knife Ilsa had given me when I was fourteen and still thought knives solved most problems. My cloak. Vethra's sigil, drawn small on scrap parchment, folded into my breast pocket over the place under my sternum where the heat lived now, constant, patient, like a cat that had decided whose house this was.

The spark pulsed once when I touched the sigil. Not warning. Something closer to amusement.

"Don't," I muttered. "You don't get an opinion. You're luggage."

The heat flickered again, and I swear to every silent god that it laughed.

* * *

The garrison yard was empty in the way yards are never actually empty. Odile's snares hung along the east fence, their lines catching starlight — no, not starlight, there weren't any stars yet, just the moon doing its best with what it had. Fen's practice targets leaned against the well housing, straw men with straw hearts, each one stabbed through the chest with the earnest fury of someone who'd been taught to aim for the center and took it personally. Bram's smokehouse breathed its slow breath of salt and applewood.

I stood in the middle of it for longer than a deserter should.

Fifteen years I'd slept behind these walls. Fifteen years of Bram's terrible puns and Ilsa's terrible silences and Odile's terrible gambling debts, which she swore were strategic. Fifteen years of devotion to a god who never answered, which everyone said was very holy of me and which I privately suspected was just stubbornness wearing vestments. And now the god had answered, and the answer was *run*, and running meant leaving the only family I'd ever chosen in the path of whatever came looking for me.

The arithmetic was simple. Arithmetic always is. It's the carrying that kills you.

I got one boot past the gate.

"You're going the wrong way."

Fen sat on the wall above the gatepost, knees drawn up, a blanket around their shoulders like a small disgruntled bat. Nineteen years old, all elbows and conviction. In the dark their eyes were too bright, the way they'd been too bright since the night the stars went out — since the night I'd come back from the hills smelling of smoke I hadn't lit, and Fen had looked at me with the particular expression of someone doing sums they didn't want to finish.

"Fen." I kept my voice level, patrol-voice, the one that meant *nothing to see here*. "It's the middle of the night."

"Which is why you're sneaking out in full kit with three days' bread." They slid off the wall and landed without sound; Odile had trained them better than she knew. "You're going north. Past the kiln."

"The kiln's a ruin."

"So are you, lately." They stepped into my path, arms crossed. Under the blanket their shoulders were set in the way they got before sparring matches they expected to lose. "You didn't tell anyone."

"That was rather the idea."

"I know." And there it was — not anger, worse, understanding. "I know that was the idea. I've been watching you do the arithmetic for two days. You think you're protecting us by subtracting yourself."

"Subtracting myself," I said, "from a problem I caused."

"You didn't cause it. You were *born*, which apparently counts as a crime now, which means we're all accomplices, so—" Their voice cracked, and they hated it, I could see them hating it. "So stop deciding our sentences for us."

Somewhere behind them, a door opened.

I should have known. Of course I should have known. You cannot sneak out of Thornvale garrison; you can only be permitted to believe you did, and the permission had been revoked the moment Ilsa saw me pocketing bread like a man with a conscience.

Bram came first, because Bram always came first when food was involved, and he was holding the rest of the loaf and a wrapped parcel that smelled of smoked pork and dried apples. Behind him, Odile, dressed for travel, bow slung across her back with the casual insolence of a woman who'd never once packed in a hurry. Then Sable, who had simply appeared the way Sable appeared, as though they'd been standing in shadows their entire life and the shadows had finally agreed to lend them out.

And last, leaning on her cane in the doorway of her quarters, gray-haired and immovable as the mountains she'd once climbed without one, Captain Ilsa Reyes.

"Well," I said. "This is humiliating."

"You packed light," Odile observed. "That's the first smart thing you've done all week."

"Bram, those are rations—"

"For four people," Bram said placidly, handing the parcel to Fen. "Five, counting the horse, and the horse eats feelings mostly, so it's four."

"There is no horse."

"There will be. Odile stole one."

"*Requisitioned,*" Odile said.

"It was the quartermaster's own saddle horse," Bram told me gravely.

"*Requisitioned,*" Odile repeated.

I looked at them, all four of them, arranged in the yard like a verdict. "No."

"No?" Fen echoed.

"No. Absolutely not. None of you are coming." I heard my own voice go hard, and I let it, because hardness was the only tool I had left that hadn't cracked. "Corvane wants one thing in this world, and I'm it. Every mile you walk beside me is a mile you're walking into a pyre. This isn't heroics, it's math—"

"Gods, the *math* again," Odile groaned.

"—and the math says one runner lives longer than five."

"The math," Sable said quietly, "also says Corvane burns the valley whether you're in it or not."

The yard went still. Sable rarely spoke in groups; when they did, it landed like a dropped blade. They stepped forward into the moonlight, and up close their face had the terrible gentleness of someone who'd done this arithmetic professionally, for years, for the other side.

"He doesn't need you found, Marguerite. He needs you *gone*. A valley that harbored the vessel is a valley that failed the empire. If you run alone and vanish, Corvane makes an example of Thornvale to justify himself. But if Thornvale's garrison stands between him and you — openly, with witnesses, with blood — then he has a war on his hands, and wars need sanction, and sanction needs time." They paused. "You're not subtracting yourself. You're subtracting us. Those aren't the same sum."

I wanted to argue. I want that noted somewhere, in some ledger of honest intentions: I genuinely wanted to argue, and couldn't find the flaw.

"That's assuming we fight," I managed.

"It assumes we choose," Ilsa said.

She came forward slowly, cane tapping stone, until she stood close enough that I could smell the liniment she pretended she didn't need. Thirty years she'd carried something, I'd known that much — the weight of it in her shoulders, in the pauses before certain words. She looked at me now the way she'd looked at me my first day in the yard, a scrawny orphan with a chip on my shoulder and nowhere else to be.

"I bound a dying god into a newborn child," she said, "to keep an empire's furnace fed and a valley forgotten. I chose for you before you could choose for yourself. It was the only mercy available and it was also a theft, and I have owed you that debt since before you could walk." Her jaw tightened. "I will not compound it by letting you walk out that gate alone. Whatever's coming, you'll face it surrounded, because that is what I built this garrison to *be*. Not soldiers. Not a wall. A family with pikes."

Bram sniffed. Odile elbowed him.

"And besides," Ilsa added, dry as kiln ash, "you'd be dead by the pass. You pack like a sentimental idiot. Who taught you to fold a bedroll? Certainly not me."

"I was distracted!"

"By your own martyrdom, yes, it's very distracting."

Fen was grinning through the tears they were pretending weren't happening. Odile was already redistributing the rations with the ruthless efficiency of a woman dividing a gambling pot. Sable watched me, and in their watchfulness was the question underneath everything: *do you trust me enough to let me stand where you'd fall?*

The heat beneath my sternum pulsed, warm, insistent. Not laughing anymore. Something almost like hope, which was frankly worse.

"If any of you die," I said finally, "I will be insufferable about it. For decades. I'll haunt you. I'll write ballads. Bad ones."

"Oh no," Odile said. "Anything but the ballads."

"Verse after verse. Unrhymed."

"We ride at dawn," Bram declared, "before he can workshop lyrics."

"One condition." I pointed at Fen. "You're nineteen. You stay behind Odile at all times, you sleep when she tells you to sleep, and if I say run, you *run*, and we argue about it later over whatever's left of my dignity."

Fen straightened like something being knighted. "Yes, Captain Quintana."

"Don't call me—"

"Yes, *Captain*," the whole yard said, including Ilsa, who absolutely knew better.

I looked at the gate, open now, the north trail black beyond it. Somewhere over those mountains a god lay dying in pieces, and an empire sharpened itself against the morning, and I was about to lead the only people I loved straight into the seam between them. The sensible thing had been to leave alone. The sensible thing had died in the yard tonight, trampled by a stolen saddle horse and a parcel of smoked pork.

"All right," I said. "Dawn."

"Dawn," they echoed, and the word sounded less like agreement than like a vow.

Behind us, far to the south, past the broken line of the old wards, the horizon flickered — once, twice — pale and wrong, like lightning that had forgotten thunder. Odile's head came up. Sable's hand found their knife.

"They're early," Sable said.

The dawn we'd promised each other had just gotten shorter.
