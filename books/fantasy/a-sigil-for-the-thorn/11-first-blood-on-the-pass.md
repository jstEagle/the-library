## 11. First Blood on the Pass

I've been a scout for eleven years, and in that time I have learned three things worth passing on. One: the mountain always knows before you do. Two: never trust a bird that goes quiet all at once — birds are cowards, and cowards are honest. Three: if Bram ever offers to cook trail rations again, refuse politely and then run.

It was the birds that told me, on the second morning of the climb. We'd come up out of Thornvale through the shepherd's cut, past the last of the ward-stones with their old carvings worn to suggestion, and into the pass proper, where the world narrowed to a throat of grey stone and pine. The company moved slow because we had to. Fen was carrying half her body weight in supplies she refused to give up, Ilsa was moving like a woman made of stubbornness and bad knees, and Marguerite kept stopping at odd moments to press a hand against their own ribs, listening to something none of the rest of us could hear.

And the birds went quiet.

All of them. At once. Like someone had drawn a curtain over the whole sky.

I crouched by the trail and put two fingers in the dirt, which is a thing scouts do mostly to look impressive, but sometimes it actually works. The ground had a tremor in it. Faint. Rhythmic. Horses, maybe, or men in boots marching in step, still hours back down the valley floor.

"Odile." Sable's voice behind me, low. The defector hunter had taken to ranging wide on our flanks without being asked, moving through the trees like they were apologizing to them. "You feel it too."

"Someone's climbing our stairs," I said. "Outriders, by the weight of it. Light company. Maybe eight, ten riders."

Sable closed their eyes for a moment, and I watched them do the hunter thing — the thing they hated doing, reaching back into training they'd spent years trying to drown. When they opened their eyes they looked older. "Corvane doesn't send outriders to fight. He sends them to bleed us. Slow us down so the main column can catch up while we're standing around being heroic."

"Wonderful," I said. "So we get to choose between running faster and fighting anyway."

"We could split," Fen offered, coming up the trail with her pack straps creaking. She was trying to sound calm about it and mostly managing, which for Fen is a triumph. "Some of us go ahead with Marguerite, some of us—"

"No," said four voices at once, mine included, and then we all looked at each other, and nobody explained it, because it didn't need explaining. We were not splitting up. That had been settled somewhere around the night we'd all stood in the garrison yard and decided that if one of us was walking into exile, all of us were. Found family isn't a sentiment. It's a tactical doctrine.

Marguerite came up last. They had that look on them — the new one, the one that had arrived along with the heat under their skin. Half listening to us, half listening inward. It made me want to snap my fingers near their ear, and also made me want to stand between them and anything coming up the pass, which was inconvenient, since standing between people and danger was technically my entire job description.

"How far?" they asked me.

"If they push hard? Contact by late afternoon. If they're smart, they'll wait for the narrows, where we can't spread out."

"And are they smart?"

"They're hunters," Sable said quietly. "They're smart."

* * *

We picked our ground the way you pick a campsite: wrong options first. Too open. Too steep. Too much loose scree that would announce every movement for half a mile. Then Odile found it — I found it, let me be accurate, history will remember this — a place where the pass kinked around a shoulder of granite, with a fallen pine wedged across the lower slope and boulders stacked above it like some giant had gotten bored halfway through a game. A killing ground, as Ilsa said grimly, or as I preferred to think of it, a very selective tollbooth.

The plan took shape around the cook fire we didn't light. Simple plans are best; complicated plans give everyone a chance to be clever, and cleverness in combat is just death with better vocabulary.

Bram would hold the low ground at the log, because Bram was the widest thing in the company and had once stopped a runaway cart with his shoulders, an event he described afterward as "a bit of a bump." Fen would be with him — low, behind the log, where her bow could work the gap between rocks. Odile and I would take the high stones. Marguerite would be in the center, protected, and hating it. Sable would range forward and count them as they came. And Ilsa —

Ilsa would stand wherever she damn well pleased, because she was Captain Reyes, retired in the sense that a cliff retires from being a cliff.

"Bram," I said, while we were setting positions, "you know you don't have to be the wall. You could take the high ground with me. Let Fen anchor the log alone."

"Fen anchors nothing alone," Bram said, in the tone he used for saying the stew needed more salt, which is to say, the tone of natural law. He was checking the strap on his old shield, a battered tower of oak and iron that he'd carried since before I joined the garrison and which had more dents than the garrison door. "She's quick. Quick needs something slow behind it. I'm slow. It's arithmetic."

"You're going to make me do math? On a mountainside?"

"Somebody has to. You certainly won't." But he grinned when he said it, that big slow grin of his, and clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to relocate something. "Besides. Someone has to keep an eye on the girl. She eats when she's nervous, and she'll eat everything."

"I heard that," Fen called from below.

"You were meant to!"

That was Bram all over. Twenty years cooking for soldiers, twenty years standing in doorways between trouble and other people's children, and he talked about both like they were chores he'd volunteer for twice. I didn't tell him any of that. You don't say things like that to a man before a fight. You save them. You hoard them like coins for after, for the fire and the cups and the long evening when everyone's alive and embarrassed about having been afraid.

I'm telling you now instead. I hope that counts.

* * *

They came at the hour when the light goes amber and long, which is the hour hunters favor, because it shines into the eyes of anyone looking downhill. There were nine of them, mounted until the trail steepened, then afoot, moving in the economical pairs that Sable had warned us about. Grey cloaks, pale sigils stitched at the shoulder — the eye-and-anvil of the god-machine, the mark of an order that fed people to iron and called it prayer.

I lay among the stones above the trail with my bow strung and my heart doing something embarrassing in my chest. Beside me, Odile breathed slow and even, counting them the way I counted everything: habitually, obsessively, out loud under my breath. "Nine. Two flankers left, one right, rest in file. They're good. Watch the lead one — see how he checks the treeline before the trail? That's not a conscript."

"They're hunting children and cooks," I muttered. "Let them check trees."

"They're hunting a god," Sable said. "In their eyes, that makes it holy work." There was something in their voice I couldn't name yet — grief wearing armor, maybe. They raised one hand, held it flat: *wait*. Wait was the whole plan. Wait until the lead pair passed the log, until the file compressed into the kink of the pass, until there were too many of them too close together to miss.

Below, Bram sat on the log like a man resting his feet, whittling a piece of pinewood, enormous and unhurried. Fen was invisible, which was the point of Fen. Somewhere behind Marguerite's position, I knew, Ilsa was leaning on her spear with the expression of a woman prepared to be extremely disappointed in the afternoon.

The lead hunter reached the log. Stopped. Looked at Bram.

"Hold," the man called, and his voice carried flat and cold up the rocks. "By authority of the Inquisition, stand aside, cook. No one wants your blood on this stone."

"That's funny," Bram said pleasantly, not moving, knife still working the wood. "Because I've been standing here all day hoping somebody would ask me nicely."

The hunter's hand went to his sword. And the world went to pieces in the usual way, which is to say quickly, loudly, and according to plan right up until it didn't.

Fen's first arrow took the second man in the file through the thigh. My shaft caught the left flanker as he tried to swing wide around the granite — a hit, not a kill, but he went down screaming and screaming men are contagious. The file broke toward cover exactly the way Sable said trained men would break, bunching into the kink, and Odile and I poured arrows into the knot of them while Ilsa rose from behind her rock like the answer to a question no one should have asked and put her spear through the shield of the nearest hunter and the man behind it, both at once, somehow, because Ilsa had always cheated at physics.

For perhaps thirty seconds, we were winning. I remember thinking that. I remember thinking, *this is easy, this is almost insulting,* which is the exact thought you are never allowed to have, the universe being a jealous audience.

Because the lead hunter hadn't gone for cover. He'd gone for Fen.

He'd marked her the moment her arrow flew — the smallest silhouette, the youngest hands — and he crossed the open ground faster than any of us could draw, sword low, and came over the log at her like winter coming over a fence. Fen scrambled back, bow swinging uselessly, and the blade came down and she got her quiver up to block it, which saved her arm and cost her every arrow she had, spinning away across the dirt—

—and Bram was there.

I will see it for the rest of my life, however long that is. Not the blow itself. The *choosing* of it. The way this huge, gentle man turned sideways into a swordsman's line without hesitation, without ceremony, without even dropping his whittling knife, putting his body and his old dented shield between the blade and the girl the way he'd stood between carts and children, between winters and the garrison's thin soup budget, between everything sharp and everything soft, for twenty unremarkable years.

The sword went through the shield. Through the shield and into him, deep, below the collarbone, angling down.

The sound Bram made wasn't a scream. It was worse. It was surprise. A short, punched-out grunt of a man who cannot believe the arithmetic has come out this way.

Then Odile shot the hunter through the throat, and I shot the hunter next to him, and whatever else happened in those next few minutes happened without my full attention, because I was already sliding down the rocks on my heels, already shouting words that weren't words, already kneeling in the blood pooling fast and black in the shadow of the log.

* * *

There is a specific silence that falls over a company after a fight goes wrong. Not quiet — quiet is peaceful. This is the silence of people breathing through their teeth. The surviving hunters fled down-trail, dragging their wounded, and nobody chased them, because chasing requires deciding that anything matters more than what was happening at the log.

Bram was propped against the fallen pine, gray-faced, one hand pressed over the wound beneath his collarbone while Sable knelt over him with the field kit open and their hands — hunter-trained hands, hands built for ending things — moving with desperate precision in the opposite direction. Fen was beside him, gripping his free hand, talking to him in a rapid cracked stream of nonsense: "—and you're fine, you're absolutely fine, you big idiot, why did you DO that, I had it, I had him—" and Bram, blood bubbling faintly at the corner of his mouth, actually laughed.

"You did not have him, little bird," he rasped. "He had *you*. I merely corrected the sum."

"Don't you dare do arithmetic at me right now."

"Then stop crying on my hand. You'll spoil the bandage."

Marguerite knelt on his other side, and I saw them do the thing I'd been dreading and hoping for in equal measure since the stars went out: they laid both palms over the wound, and the heat came off them like sun off a summer road. The spark. Vethra's last living ember, banked in their blood for thirty years, pouring a thread of itself outward through their hands.

Bram's color came back a shade. Just a shade. His breathing steadied from wet rattling to merely labored.

"It's holding," Marguerite said, voice shaking. "The bleeding's slowing. It's — the spark can knit him, I think, if I keep—"

"No." Sable's voice cut flat across the clearing. Everyone turned. Sable's face was terrible with knowledge. "Listen to me. What you felt when the wards fell — that was the spark waking up. Every mile you spend it, it dims. And there is a starving machine in the imperial capital that needs what's left of it, and a shrine on the summit of these mountains that needs it more, and you are *not* going to pour a dying god into a cook's chest to patch a wound that stitching and time can close. Not when the whole reason we're on this mountain is that the world is about to need every ember you have."

"The whole reason we're on this mountain," Marguerite said quietly, dangerously, "is that I refused to let my family pay for my choices alone. So don't ask me to watch one bleed out over arithmetic."

"Then don't," said Bram.

His voice was weak, but it carried, the way his voice had always carried over a noisy mess hall. He lifted his hand off Fen's and pressed it, gently, against Marguerite's wrist. Pushing the healing warmth away from himself.

"I've been stabbed, girl. I've also been burned, frozen, trampled, and poisoned by my own cooking, and here I sit. What I am not is a vessel for gods. Save the miracle for something that deserves it. Stitch me up, feed me that awful willow bark tea, and let me complain about the quality of field medicine, which is my right as senior cook." He paused. Blood loss was making his grin lopsided. "Besides. If you heal me clean, I'll never live down how scared I looked."

Nobody laughed. That was the cruelty of it — the joke was good, and nobody could laugh, and Bram's eyes drifted shut with the effort of staying awake while Sable worked needle and gut-thread through flesh that the spark's warmth had given a fighting chance.

Ilsa stood apart, spear planted, watching the trail below with red-rimmed eyes that missed nothing. When she spoke, her voice was captain-flat, and only someone who'd served under her for eleven years would have heard the fracture running through it.

"They'll report back to Corvane by nightfall. He'll know we fought, he'll know we bled, and he'll know exactly where we are. Outriders don't travel without a signal plan." She turned and looked at each of us in turn, lingering last on Marguerite, whose hands were still glowing faintly, whose eyes were wet and furious. "So we move tonight. Bram rides the mule, and he hates it, and he lives. We make the high switchbacks before dawn, and we do not stop again until the tree line breaks."

"And after that?" Fen asked. Her voice was small. Her hands, I noticed, were still stained with Bram's blood, and she hadn't tried to wipe them.

Ilsa looked up the pass, toward the white shoulders of the summits hidden in cloud. Toward the shrine neither of us had seen and all of us had begun to believe in.

"After that," she said, "we find out what a half-alive god wants from the people who carried it home."

Above us, for the first time since the night the stars went out, I saw it — faint, between the clouds, high on the peak where the snow never melted.

A light. Small and gold and steady.

Like something waiting to be lit.
