## 12. The God Beneath the Snow

I want it on the record that when I imagined my death—and a mercenary imagines their death the way other people imagine retirement—I did not picture it happening in a ruined shrine full of snow, mid-conversation with a god who'd spent fifteen years pretending to be dead.

But then, I also didn't picture the stars going out, or the wards shattering, or Bram bleeding out in my arms on a mountain pass, so at some point you have to admit your imagination was never up to the job.

* * *

We'd climbed for two days since the ford of broken pines where Odile lost the hunters in a rockfall she still described, with visible satisfaction, as "my best work." Two days of switchbacks and thin air and Sable walking like someone who knew exactly how far behind us Corvane's outriders were and didn't like the number. On the third morning the storm found us, the kind of white screaming wall that makes the mountains feel personal about killing you, and Odile—scout's instinct, gods bless her—spotted the dark shape of a roofline jutting from a drift like a rib from a whale.

A shrine. Old. Older than Thornvale's founding, older than the wards, maybe older than anything except the mountain itself.

"Vethran," Sable said quietly, brushing snow off a lintel stone, and there it was: the closed eye, the thorn-wrapped sigil, worn shallow by centuries of weather. The empire had burned every standing temple they could find. This one they'd never found, because the valley forgot magic so thoroughly that we forgot to look.

Inside, it wasn't much. A collapsed half-dome, one wall still standing, an altar stone furred with frost. Wind moaned through a gap in the roof with a sound almost like breathing. We got a fire going—Fen's hands shook until Odile put hers over them and said, "Cold is just weather, love, not a verdict"—and then the others slept, exhausted, while I took first watch, because I'd been taking first watch since I was fourteen and old habits are the last thing to die.

That's when the spark woke up.

* * *

It had been quiet since the pass. Since Bram. Quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Now it moved under my ribs like something turning over in its sleep, and heat bloomed through me—not the firelight's borrowed warmth but mine, generated, *owned*, and beneath my skin I felt that familiar prickling, the sense of a door somewhere inside me rattling in its frame.

*I know this place,* the spark seemed to say. Or maybe that was wishful thinking. Fifteen years of silence will make you hear voices in wind. But the wind through the gap in the roof really did sound like breathing, and the altar stone really did have Vethra's sigil cut into it, deep and deliberate, and when I laid my palm against it—

The world went white and silent, and I fell inward.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about communing with a god: it feels like drowning in reverse. Like being pulled up out of water you didn't know you were drowning in, gasping into an air you'd forgotten existed.

I stood—not stood, *was*—in a place that wasn't a place. Snow underfoot that didn't feel cold. A sky without stars, but not empty the way the night the wards broke had been empty; this darkness was *full*, dense with presence, like the moment before lightning. And across all of it, faint as a candle seen through ten miles of fog, something watched me with the patience of mountains.

"You're alive," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "You're actually alive. Ilsa said you died. Everyone said—"

*A spark survives,* said the voice, and it was Vethra's, no question, the same voice I'd heard as a child before it went silent, only thinner now, frayed at the edges, a tapestry unraveling thread by thread. *A god does not die easily. Dying takes time. The empire has been very generous with time.*

"Fifteen years," I said. "You went silent fifteen years ago. I prayed every day. I lit candles. I—" My throat tightened. Wry narration, Quintana. Keep it together. "I thought you'd abandoned us."

*No.* The word landed like a stone in still water. *I went into you.*

The snow-field tilted. Or I did. Same thing, probably.

"The wards," I said slowly, working it through, feeling each piece click into place with the ugly satisfaction of a lock finally turning. "Ilsa said the wards weren't built to keep magic out. They were built to hide something inside. She looked at me when she said it, and I thought—"

*You thought correctly.*

"I'm a hiding place." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm a *cache*. Twenty-eight years walking around Thornvale complaining about patrol rations, and the whole time I was carrying—"

*Not a cache.* The voice sharpened, and for a moment the darkness around us flared, and I saw—or sensed—the shape of the thing speaking to me: vast, wounded, diminished beyond reckoning but still, unmistakably, *vast*. *A vessel is not a box, child. What I gave you, I gave willingly. What I asked, I asked willingly. You were seven years old. You remember.*

And I did. Gods help me, I did.

A fever, when I was seven. The worst anyone in the garrison had ever seen. Three days burning, and Ilsa sitting by my cot the whole time, refusing relief, refusing sleep, her hand on my forehead like she was holding something down—or holding something *in*. And on the third night, right at the edge between fever and sleep, a voice. Not Ilsa's. A voice like cold air over high snow, saying: *Little thorn. Grow well. I am sorry for the weight.*

Then nothing. Fifteen years of nothing.

"I thought I dreamed it," I whispered.

*Children are told their memories are dreams. It is how adults survive having lied to them.*

"That's bitter, for a god of silence."

*Silence is not peace. Silence is everything unsaid, waiting.*

* * *

I sat down in the snow-that-wasn't-cold, which I suspect was less sitting and more my mind deciding it needed a posture for this conversation, and I made myself ask the question I'd been circling since the night the stars went out.

"Why? Why hide in a baby in a forgotten valley? Why not—rebuild? Find another way? You're a *god*."

The darkness around us contracted, the way a living thing contracts around a wound.

*Because I was dying,* Vethra said. *Truly dying, for the first time in the long history of my long life. Do you know what the god-machine is, Marguerite Quintana?*

"Sable says the empire feeds it. Prisoners, sometimes. Heretics. That it eats... faith, somehow. Souls."

*Simpler than that, and worse. It eats divinity. It was built from the corpse of a murdered god—do not ask me which; even I do not know its name anymore, it has been dead so long—and a machine built from murder can only be fed by murder. For nine hundred years the empire has hunted down every god it could find, torn out what divine fire remained, and poured it into the furnace. Every pantheon of the old world, devoured. Every small god of stream and hearth and harvest, devoured. When the supply ran low, they invented heresy to manufacture more fuel. I was one of the last. Perhaps the last.*

"And the ancient war," I said. "The one everyone whispers about. The war that'll come back if the gods return."

The silence stretched so long I thought the communion might be failing. Then:

*There was no war.*

The words dropped into me like ice water.

*There was no war because there was no one left to fight it. Call it a war if it comforts you—a war implies two sides, battles, honor, defeat in fair combat. What happened was a slaughter that lasted nine hundred years, conducted against beings who could not raise armies, and finished with a lie. The empire wrote the history afterward: the gods grew proud, the gods made war upon mankind, mankind in its righteousness destroyed them. Children learn it in school. Hunters swear oaths to it.* A pause, and the voice went quieter, and somehow that was worse. *I watched it happen, little thorn. I hid in deep places and I watched, century after century, as my kin were dragged into furnaces. And I told myself I was waiting. Being prudent. Biding my time. The oldest lie of the survivor.*

"So when they say the spark could reignite an ancient war—"

*They mean: if a living god exists, the truth of what they did becomes undeniable. The machine cannot digest me whole. If I burn free, every altar in the empire rings like a struck bell, and every citizen learns in one terrible hour that their righteous history is a butcher's ledger. THAT is the war they fear. Not armies. Arithmetic.*

I sat with that. The snow around me glittered with a light that had no source. Somewhere far away, in the real world, my body was presumably sitting against an altar stone while Fen and Odile slept and Sable kept watch, and none of them knew I was being told the end of the world was actually a bookkeeping scandal.

"They want my blood for the machine," I said. "Corvane. If I give it—if I let them pour me into that furnace—it keeps the machine fed, and the empire holds together, and nobody learns anything, and everyone in Thornvale lives."

*Yes.*

"And if I carry you over the mountains and give you back your whole self—"

*The bell rings. The arithmetic begins. Empires are fragile things, little thorn; they are mostly story, and stories die of a single contradiction.* Another pause, longer. *I will not pretend to you that I am neutral. I am a murdered god's memory wearing a spark for a coat. I want to live. I want to burn free. I have wanted it for nine hundred years and I am ashamed of how much I want it.*

"But?"

*But I chose you,* Vethra said, *and choosing means your will weighs more than mine. Hear me now, and believe me, because I have watched liars for nine centuries and I know exactly what they sound like: I chose you because your mother prayed at my last hidden altar the night you were born, and her prayer was not for power or safety. It was 'let her—'* the voice stumbled, corrected itself, '*let THEM be someone who chooses, when the choosing comes.' So I answered. I poured what remained of myself into her newborn child and I asked Ilsa Reyes—one human being, one oath-keeper—to keep the secret, and I went silent, and I waited to see what would grow.*

The snow-field blurred. Or I did. Definitely me, that time.

"Someone who chooses," I repeated. "That's a hell of a thing to build a person out of."

*It is the only thing worth building a person out of. Everything else is furniture.*

* * *

When I surfaced, my face was wet and frozen in streaks, my palm was bonded to the altar stone by a thin film of ice, and the fire had burned down to coals. The wind through the roof gap still sounded like breathing. I worked my hand free, flexed fingers that ached, and stared at the sigil under the frost.

The spark turned under my ribs, warm and patient. Waiting. It had been waiting twenty-one years. It could wait a little longer while its idiot vessel figured out how to feel.

Sable materialized beside me the way Sable always did, soundless, folding down onto the altar step with a canteen. They looked at my face for a long moment.

"It talked to you," they said. Not a question.

"It talked to me." I took the canteen. Drank. The water tasted like snow and iron. "Sable. The ancient war. The one your order trains for, prays for, organizes its whole miserable existence around—"

"There was no war," Sable said flatly. "I know."

I stopped drinking. "You *know*?"

"Hunters above a certain rank know. Corvane knows. We're taught it as a test of faith, actually—they tell you the truth and watch whether you flinch. The war is a doctrine, not a history. Doctrine doesn't need to be true. It needs to be useful." Their jaw worked. "I flinched. I've been flinching for eleven years. It's why they keep me tracking instead of promoting me."

Eleven years of knowing, and carrying it alone, and hunting anyway. I thought about saying something about that. Decided Sable would rather walk over hot coals than be pitied in front of a sleeping garrison.

Instead I said, "Vethra chose me. Willingly. My mother prayed for it—that I'd be someone who chooses."

Sable was quiet for a while. Outside, the storm screamed against the standing wall, and inside, the coals ticked and settled, and Fen muttered something in their sleep about bread, which under the circumstances felt about right.

"Then the choice is real," Sable said finally. "Not prophecy. Not destiny. Just—you, deciding."

"Just me, deciding." I laughed, low, so as not to wake them. "Gods. Ilsa bound a dying god into a baby and hoped it'd grow up sensible. Imagine her disappointment."

"She's not disappointed," Sable said. "She's terrified. There's a difference."

* * *

I should have slept after that. I didn't. I sat watch till dawn, and dawn came gray and grinding through the storm-gap, and with it came Odile, moving fast, snow crusted in her eyebrows, wearing the particular expression she wore when the news was bad and she'd already decided how bad to say it was.

"Tracks," she said. "West ridge. A lot of them. Too many for outriders." She met my eyes. "That's the main column, Marguerite. Corvane's not chasing us anymore. He's herding us."

Behind her, Sable had gone very still, and I felt the spark flare under my ribs like a second heartbeat, and somewhere south of us—days away, weeks away, coming all the same—a starving god-machine turned its blind attention toward the mountains, toward the shrine, toward me.

Toward the last of the last.

"Well," I said, standing, knees cracking, handing Odile back the canteen. "Good thing I've got a decision made."
