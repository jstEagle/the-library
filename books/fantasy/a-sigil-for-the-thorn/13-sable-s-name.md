## 13. Sable's Name

I have been many names. That is what the Order does to you before they do anything else — they take your name, because a name is a hook, and hooks are where old loyalties hang. They gave me a number first, then a designation, then a title, and by the time I was fourteen I answered to *Tracker* the way a dog answers to a whistle: without thinking, without wanting.

My name — my real one, the one I was born with in this valley that smells of wet stone and woodsmoke even now, even here, even on the wrong side of everything — was Sable Ashdown.

You can laugh if you want to. Marguerite did, when I finally said it out loud, though not unkindly. It's an absurd name. A valley name. The kind of name given to children whose parents expect them to grow turnips and die of a bad winter at sixty, having never once seen the sea.

The hunters took me when I was six years old. Which means I have spent twenty-one years learning to hunt people like me, which is a sentence I have never once managed to say without my voice doing something strange around the edges of it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. I always get ahead of myself when I try to tell this story. Corvane used to say it was my worst habit as an interrogator — that I sympathized too fast, reached for the subject's story like a starving man reaching for bread. He thought he was insulting me. He was mostly right.

* * *

We made camp in the hollow below the shrine-ruin, because Odile said pursuit would come up the western trail and Bram's wound meant we couldn't push past dark anyway, and because Marguerite looked at the ruin with an expression I'd learned to read by then — the expression of someone who has just spoken with a god and hasn't finished digesting it.

Bram slept propped against his pack with Fen sitting vigil beside him, both of them pretending the vigil was casual and fooling absolutely no one. Odile took first watch and told the rest of us, with great precision, to *actually sleep*, which in Odile's language means she will personally wake you with a boot if she catches your eyes open.

Which left Marguerite and me at the small fire, and left me with no excuse left, and left the truth sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal.

They were sharpening their knife. Of course they were. Marguerite Quintana sharpens their knife the way other people pray, and maybe it is praying; I've watched enough of both to know the difference between them is smaller than anyone admits.

"You're staring," they said, without looking up.

"I'm deciding something."

"That sounds ominous."

"It usually is." I turned the little tin cup in my hands. It was Bram's cup. Everything we carried belonged to someone else now; that's what a company is. "I told you I'd tell you how I know the pass. And how I know Corvane's tells, and how to read hunter hand-sign, and why I flinch at church bells."

Marguerite set the whetstone down. Slowly, deliberately, the way you put down a weapon so someone knows you've put it down. "You don't have to."

"I know." I laughed, or something adjacent to laughing. "That's the problem. You keep not making me, and it turns out I can't stand that."

* * *

Here is what happened, as best as I can tell it, and I have told it perhaps four times in my life, each time worse than the last, so forgive me if I arrange it badly.

The valley had wards. The wards hid the valley. But wards are not walls — they are more like a held breath, and a held breath can be noticed. Twenty-two years ago, an Order survey team charted an anomaly in the Thornvale region: a region that *resisted being charted*. Maps came back smudged. Birds flew in circles. One cartographer went mad, or claimed to, and the Order does not waste claims of madness.

They sent a quiet team. Not hunters — not yet. Auditors. Men and women with ledgers and polite questions, who arrived in the valley under the pretext of a census and stayed eleven days.

I was born in the eighth year of those audits' aftermath, when the Order had already decided the valley warranted a standing observation post, and the observation post had decided the most efficient way to watch a warded valley was from inside it, wearing its face.

My mother sold eggs. My father mended roofs. There was nothing anomalous about either of them, and there was nothing — *nothing* — anomalous about me. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in this telling: I was an ordinary child. The Order did not take me because I glowed, or prophesied, or floated three inches above my cradle.

They took me because of a ledger entry and a quota.

Inquisitor Vell, whose name I still remember the way you remember a tooth that was pulled — you keep checking the gap — ran the observation post, and the post's budget required results, and results required *subjects*, and the Order's doctrine held that magic leaves residue, and residue runs in bloodlines, and therefore any child born within a warded zone within seven years of warding was *presumptively marked*. Presumptively. It's a beautiful word. It means *we decided before we looked, and looking was optional.*

Six children were taken from Thornvale that year. I was the youngest. I am, as far as I know, the only one still alive.

They trucked us north in a grain wagon. I remember the wagon. I remember being sick, and a girl named Wren holding my hair back, and I remember arriving at the Scholae and the gates closing behind us with a sound I have heard in dreams ever since — not slamming. Closing. Deliberate. The sound of a decision being finished.

Then they gave us numbers, and burned our clothes, and told us our parents had died in a fever, and began the work.

* * *

"The work," Marguerite repeated quietly, when I got there. The fire had burned down to a red eye. "What was the work?"

"Making us useful." I shrugged, and hated how easily the shrug came, twenty-one years polished smooth. "The Order doesn't believe in destroying what it can repurpose. We weren't evidence of the wards' failure — we were instruments. Children raised inside a warded culture could be sent back through warded country and *not be rejected by it*. Do you understand? The wards didn't just hide magic. They hid the valley. And anything of the valley carried the valley's scent. So they raised us to smell like home to places that had forgotten us."

"And they sent you back here."

"Three times." I held up fingers, because if I didn't give my hands a task they'd shake, and I would rather die than let them shake in front of Marguerite Quintana. "Census verification. At nine, twelve, fifteen. I walked into Thornvale with an auditor's satchel and a cover story about being an orphan of the lowland fever, and people fed me. Fed *me*. A woman named — her name was Marta Ossler — she gave me honey cake and told me I had my mother's chin, and I wrote it all down. Every kindness. Every face. Every door that opened. I handed it to Vell in a sealed report and he commended my objectivity."

Marguerite said nothing for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, Odile shifted on her watch, a soft scrape of boot on stone, and I catalogued it automatically — threat assessment, direction, weight — and hated that too, the way the training lives in you like gravel in a wound.

"I knew a Marta Ossler," Marguerite said at last. "Everyone did. She kept bees."

"She kept bees," I agreed. My voice came out flat. I was proud of that, in the moment. I am less proud of it now. "She died two winters ago. In her bed, of age, surrounded by — I checked the records. Surrounded by neighbors. She lived a whole life, Marguerite, and part of the reason the Order never touched Thornvale's elders was that a nine-year-old drew them a map of who loved whom."

"That wasn't you."

"It was my hand on the pen."

"There's a difference."

"Is there?" I heard it rise in me then, the thing I keep caged, the thing Corvane called my *flaw* and my *gift* depending on the day. "Because I've tried that arithmetic every night for twenty-one years and it never balances. Six children taken. Three returned as instruments. Two dead — Wren died at sixteen, they worked her until her heart quit, and they buried her under a number. And me. And every year I told myself *I'll defect when it matters*, and every year the defectors I helped were strangers, because it was easier to save people who weren't — "

I stopped. Because Marguerite had reached across the fire and taken my wrist. Not gently, exactly. Firmly. The way you grab someone who is walking toward a cliff in the dark.

"Sable." My false name. My Order name. They said it and I flinched, and they saw me flinch, and they understood, gods help me, they understood instantly, because Marguerite has always been quick in the ways that matter and slow only about their own worth. "Say the other one. The real one."

And that is the moment I have been circling since I sat down at this fire, and I find, having arrived, that it costs more than everything before it combined.

"Sable Ashdown," I said. My voice cracked on *Ashdown*, split like green wood, because the last person to say it aloud was my mother, at the wagon, running after us down the egg-road until the trees took her. "My name is Sable Ashdown. I was born under the third chimney on Lark Lane, and I liked the honey cakes better than the plain ones, and I cried at thunder until I was five, and I am — I am the thing they made out of what was left."

* * *

Marguerite didn't say *I'm sorry*. I want that noted too, because everyone says *I'm sorry*, and sorry is a coin so worn you can't read its face anymore. What they said was:

"Marta Ossler would have wanted you to have the honey cakes."

And I laughed — really laughed, ugly and sudden, tears coming with it whether I bid them or no — because it was absurd, and true, and it broke something open in me that had been calcifying since I was six years old in a grain wagon.

"I hunted my own origin," I said, when I could speak. "Do you understand what the Order built? They took children *of* the hidden places and taught them to unhide them. Every skill I have — every single one — exists to do to other valleys what was done to mine. And I was *good*, Marguerite. Commendations. Vell used to show my reports to new trackers. *Objectivity beyond her years.*"

"*Their* years," Marguerite corrected softly. "If we're doing names properly."

I stared at them.

"You don't have to," they said. "But I noticed. Years ago, in the garrison, the way you said things. And I noticed you noticing me noticing, and neither of us said anything, and I decided it wasn't mine to ask." A pause. The whetstone lay forgotten between them and the fire. "So. If we're doing names properly."

*Sable Ashdown.* I turned it over. I had not been offered it as a gift since before I could write. The Order gave it back once, mid-training — a cruelty dressed as mercy, *your birth name, tracker, tell us how it makes you feel* — but this was different. This was someone holding out their open palm and saying: whatever fits, wear it.

"Sable," I said. "Just Sable, mostly. She, some days. Neither, others. The Order hated that too. Called it instability." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a coat that fit. "It's the only thing they ever called unstable that I've decided to keep."

"Good," Marguerite said, with such simple finality that something in my chest went loose. Then, wry, because they are who they are: "For the record, I also cried at thunder until I was five. Fen still does, though they'd gut me for saying so."

"Fen cried at thunder during the skirmish on the pass and reloaded crossbows while doing it. I'd call that growth."

"They'd call it tactics."

"We're going to be insufferable together," I realized aloud. "The two of us. Trading intelligence about the children we love like rival spymasters."

Something crossed Marguerite's face at *together* — hope and fear in equal measure, the exact mixture I recognized because I was drinking it too — and they looked down at the fire, and the silence stretched, and it was the good kind of silence, the kind with a question living inside it.

I should let it live, I told myself. I should let it sleep.

I have never once in my life done the sensible thing at the crucial moment. Ask Vell. Ask the Order. Ask every commander who ever signed off on my file with the note *excellent instincts, poor discipline.*

"Sable," Marguerite said, at the same moment I opened my mouth, and we stopped, and almost laughed, and they gestured — *go* — and I should have taken the out.

"When this ends," I said, "however it ends — the machine city, Corvane, the spark, all of it — when it ends, I won't go back to the Order. Whatever's left of me after that is yours. If you want it. That's — " I breathed. "That's the plainest sentence I own. I've been carrying it since the gate, since I stood in front of you lying to Corvane's face and realized I'd rather burn than hand you over. Love against duty isn't a hard choice, Marguerite. Nobody tells you that. The duty dies easy. It's the *hope* that's hard. Hope that there's anything left of me worth choosing."

Marguerite set down the knife entirely. Took my hand — the one that had shaken, that had stopped shaking, that shook again now — and pressed it between both of theirs, and their skin was warm, warmer than it should be, spark-warm, and they said:

"You absolute idiot. You've been worth choosing since you lied to a witch-hunter with your heartbeat steady as a metronome. Since you warned me in the granary with half the garrison ten feet away. I've been trying to figure out how to say it without ruining everything, because I'm — Sable, I carry half a dying god and a war on a leash. Everyone who loves me becomes a target. I've been doing the arithmetic."

"And?"

"And I'm bad at math." They smiled, tired and blazing at once. "Stay. However it ends. Stay."

Above the ruin, through a break in the clouds, one star showed itself — the first since the night everything shattered — a single cold point of light, like a god cracking one eye to check on us.

Odile's voice drifted down from the rocks, dry as chalk: "If you two are quite finished, there's movement on the western trail. Torches. A lot of them."

We looked at each other. Hands still joined. The war, patient as ever, waiting for its turn.

"Together, then," Marguerite said, and stood, and pulled me up after them, and we went to wake the others.
