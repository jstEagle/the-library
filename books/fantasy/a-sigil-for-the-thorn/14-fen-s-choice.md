## 14. Fen's Choice

I have been told, on several occasions and by nearly every member of the Thornvale garrison, that I talk too much. Captain Reyes once described me as "a windmill in a storm," which I took as a compliment until Odile explained that the mill grinds nothing when there's no grain. Bram just says my mouth runs faster than my feet, which is objectively false, because my feet are very fast. It's how I became a scout.

The point is: I know what people expect from me. They expect noise. They expect the youngest one to chatter, to fidget, to fill silence like water fills a boot. And so, when the three riders came out of the tree-line at the ford below Greywatch with their grey cloaks and their hunter's knots tied at the shoulder, I did the smartest thing I have ever done in my short, frequently-lectured life.

I stopped talking.

* * *

Let me back up, because the story technically starts two days earlier, around a fire that smelled like pine smoke and Bram's terrible lentils.

We were camped in the high hollow below the pass — Marguerite, Sable, Odile, Bram with his bandaged ribs, and me. Ilsa was already a day behind us and a world ahead of us, marching into Corvane's custody so the rest of us could walk away. Nobody had said her name since the ford. You could feel where she'd been in our company the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Everyone kept touching the gap.

Sable had spread a map across a flat stone and weighted the corners with rocks, and we were arguing about routes the way families argue about anything — loudly, affectionately, and without anyone changing their mind.

"The shepherd's track," Odile said, for the third time. "It's steep but it's unwatched."

"It's steep enough to kill Bram's horse," Sable said, "and if it kills his horse it kills Bram, and then Fen will cry, and I can't scout and console at the same time."

"I don't cry," I said.

"You cried at the midwinter feast," Odile said. "Over the plum pudding."

"That pudding had *meaning*, Odile."

Marguerite laughed — actually laughed, the first time since the ford — and something in my chest went loose and warm. That's the thing nobody tells you about fear. It doesn't replace everything else. It sits underneath it, like riverbed under river, and all your ordinary joys run across the top of it and look almost normal.

Then Sable went still. Not scout-still. Prey-still. Their head tilted, listening to a sound I couldn't hear yet, and they said, quietly, "Horses. Below us. More than two."

That's when I learned the second smartest thing I've ever done, which was to shut up and stay shut up while everyone else made decisions. They doused the fire. They moved Bram first, because Bram moves slowest and complains loudest and loves us anyway. Odile took the rear. Sable took point. Marguerite took my elbow for exactly three seconds — I counted — and said, "If we're split, you go to the cairn on the ridge and wait one night. One night, Fen. Then you go home."

"Home is wherever—"

"One. Night."

And then the world came apart the way worlds do, which is to say suddenly, stupidly, and with a great deal of shouting.

* * *

They came through the trees on the switchback above us, not below — outriders who'd circled wide, three of them, grey-cloaked, with the coiled-wire sigil of the hunter orders stitched at their shoulders. I heard Odile shout a warning. I heard Bram roar something that was probably a battle cry and possibly just pain. I felt Marguerite grab my collar and hurl me sideways off the trail, down into the bracken and scree, and then hands were on me — not Marguerite's, wrong hands, gloved and hard — and I was rolling, and the sky was spinning, and someone put a knee in my back and said,

"Got a young one."

I want it noted for the record of history that I did not scream. I did the other thing. The thing I'd never done before, the thing that surprised me more than the hands did.

I went limp, and I whimpered, and I let my lower lip do what it does.

Because I'd spent eighteen years being the loud one, the funny one, the youngest one, and I knew — the way you know your own hand — exactly what these hunters would see when they looked at me. A kid. Scrawny, dirty, terrified, sniveling. Baggage. Someone's little brother dragged along on a fool's errand, worth a question and not a rope.

"Please," I said, and cracked my voice in the middle of the word like a dropped plate. "Please, I didn't — I'm not — they made me come, I didn't want—"

"Shut it," said the one holding me. He hauled me up by the arm. He was maybe thirty, weathered, with a hunter's knot tied sloppily, like he'd re-tied it a thousand times without thinking. His companions came out of the brush leading their horses, and one of them had blood on his sleeve that I prayed wasn't Bram's, because Bram was supposed to be running the other way with Odile cursing at him to move faster.

"Where are the others?" the sloppy-knot one asked.

"I don't—" I hiccupped. Genuinely. Fear does half the work for you; you just have to get out of its way. "There were five of us, we're going over to my aunt's in Lowmarsh, my sister ran off with a — with a man, and my mother sent us to fetch her home, and then those people found us on the road and said they'd — they said if I didn't come along they'd leave me for the wolves, please, I just want to go home—"

It was beautiful. I say that with no modesty whatsoever. It was the performance of my life, built on a foundation of absolute truth: I *was* terrified. I *did* want to go home. Every lie I told was load-bearing truth wearing a mask, and I could feel it working, feel the sloppy-knot one's grip loosen from *prisoner* to *annoyance*.

"Lowmarsh is south," he said.

"We got turned around." I wiped my nose on my sleeve. "The mountains all look the same."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then at his companions. And this is the part I'll carry to my grave, the part that changed the shape of me: the youngest one among them — a woman with a scar through her eyebrow and eyes like a winter pond — looked at me and *believed me*. Not because I was good. Because she wanted to. Because she was tired and cold and far from her own fire, and a sniveling boy from Lowmarsh meant this whole miserable errand was a waste, and gods forgive me, I gave her permission to think so.

"He's nothing," she said. "A tagalong. Corvane wants the vessel and the defector, not somebody's lost little brother."

The sloppy-knot one spat. "Corvane wants everyone brought in. You know how he is."

"You want to drag him over the pass?" She gestured at the scree, the darkening sky, the distance. "Tie him, he slows us. Cut him loose, he tells his village a story. Which is worse?"

They argued. I stood there with my wrists bound in front of me, sniffling on schedule, and inside my skull I was doing the fastest thinking of my life. They thought we were five. They thought our route was south toward Lowmarsh. So I fed them the thing they'd already decided, sweetened it, made it theirs.

"They were talking," I volunteered, quavering, "about a — a chapel road? South? I heard them say south, and the big one said 'the ford at Marrow,' I swear that's all I know, please, my mother's going to kill me, she's going to kill me twice—"

"Marrow ford's a day south," one of them muttered. "Could be."

"Could be a lie," said the winter-pond woman. But she said it without heat, and I watched her decide, right there in front of me, that checking south was cheaper than dragging a crying boy north. People don't lie well to each other, mostly. But they lie beautifully to themselves, and if you stand in the middle of a man's own wish, he'll walk into it like a doorway.

They cut my bindings. They took my knife, which stung, and my ration-bag, which stung worse, and they pointed me downhill toward the lowlands with a shove and an order to run and not look back.

So I ran. And at the top of the rise, where the trail bent, I did not look back — because I'd promised myself I wouldn't give them the satisfaction — but I listened, the way Odile taught me, with my whole body. Hooves turning south. Three sets, fading.

Then I turned north, into the dark, toward a ridge and a cairn and whatever was left of my family, and I did not stop shaking until dawn.

* * *

Here is the thing I haven't said yet. The thing I keep circling like a dog around a wound.

While they held me — in those few minutes between the ropes and the cutting — the sloppy-knot one reached into his coat to check something, some token or warrant, and his coat fell open, and I saw what he carried against his chest.

It was small. Smaller than a lantern. A box of black iron, no bigger than a loaf of bread, seamed with wire, and through the seams there was a light. Not firelight. Not lamplight. A light that moved *wrong*, that leaned, that strained — and as I stood there bound and babbling, the light leaned *toward me*. Toward my chest. And the box made a sound. Not a sound in the air. A sound in the teeth, in the fillings, in the wet dark behind the eyes: a thin, endless, grinding hunger, like the world's largest belly cramping around nothing.

The hunter felt it too. I saw his hand press flat against the box, absently, the way you'd press a hand to a crying child. Comforting it. Or holding it down.

And the light strained toward me, and the sound ground through my bones, and for one heartbeat I understood — really understood, in my body instead of my head — what Marguerite has been carrying since the night the stars went out. Something enormous and dying wanted what was in their blood, wanted it the way I wanted air when I was face-down in the scree, and it had sent men with wires and warrants across the mountains to take it, and it would never, ever be full.

I'd known the facts. Facts are cheap. I'd sat around fires hearing about the god-machine the way you hear about a storm at sea — real, dangerous, far away. But the machine wasn't far away. It was in that box. It was *hungry in front of me*, hungry enough that its hunger leaked through iron, and I was standing close enough to feed it, and the worst part — the part that wakes me up at night, Bram snoring two feet away, safe as a kitten in a basket —

The worst part is that for half a heartbeat, some small animal part of me wanted to open the box. To make the hunger stop. Even knowing it wouldn't stop. Even knowing it would eat me and keep grinding. Hunger that size makes you feel sorry for it. That's the trap of it. That's how empires work, I think, though I'd never say that to a grown-up. They make you pity the thing that's eating you.

I told Marguerite all of this later, or most of it, sitting against the cairn with my knees pulled up like a much smaller child than I'd been two days prior. Marguerite listened the way they always listen, completely, like you're the only weather in the sky. When I finished, they didn't say *you're brave* or *it'll be alright*. They said:

"Now you know why we can't go back."

And I nodded, because I did know, finally, in my teeth. Going back meant waiting for the hunger to arrive in earnest. Going back meant the valley becoming a pantry.

"I lied to them," I said. "About Marrow ford. If they check, they'll find nothing, and then they'll know I lied, and they'll come back angry."

"Then we'd best be gone before they finish checking," Marguerite said, and squeezed the back of my neck, gentle, and that helped more than any speech.

* * *

We caught up with Odile and Bram at the second cairn — Bram alive, furious, and complaining about his ribs with such vigor that Odile said she was considering surrendering him to the hunters as a public service. Sable arrived an hour after that, ghosting out of the treeline with three days' beard and news: the outriders had indeed ridden south, found an empty ford at Marrow, and turned back east toward Corvane's main column.

Which meant my lie had bought us time. Maybe a day. Maybe less, once someone asked the winter-pond woman why she'd let a sniveling boy go, and she started wondering herself.

One day. We'd need two to clear the pass.

"There's a shortcut," Sable said, looking at me in a way I didn't love. "Through the old shrine ruins under the snowline. It cuts half a day. It's also exactly the kind of place hunters watch, because it's holy ground and holy ground makes them nervous."

"Nervous how?" Bram asked.

"Nervous like men whose god is a machine," Sable said. "Nervous of anything that isn't."

Everyone looked at Marguerite. Marguerite looked at me — at me specifically, not past me, not over me, at me — and asked, "You led them south. You bought us the day. What do you think?"

Nobody had ever asked what I thought about a route before. Odile planned, Sable scouted, I ran messages and complained about the food. My opinions on movement were decorative, like the plum pudding. And now here was the vessel of a dying god, the person the entire empire was hunting, asking the garrison baby to weigh in on whether we should walk into haunted ruins to save half a day we might not otherwise have.

I thought about the box. The leaning light. The grinding, patient hunger. I thought about the winter-pond woman deciding I was nothing, and how long that decision would hold, and how fast we could move if we moved now instead of circling wide.

"Take the shortcut," I said. My voice only shook a little, and none of it was performance. "They'll be watching the open trails. Nobody watches the places that scare them. And if we meet anything in there—" I swallowed. "Well. We've got a god with us, don't we? Half of one."

Marguerite smiled. It was a tired smile, and a scared one, and it was aimed at me like sunlight.

"Half of one," they agreed. "Best offer anyone's made all week."

We broke camp in the grey light before dawn. And as I shouldered my stolen-back pack — Odile had retrieved my rations from an unconscious mule line, don't ask — I caught Sable watching me with an expression I'd never gotten from them before. Respect, maybe. Or the beginning of it.

"Not bad, Lowmarsh," they said quietly.

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning you lied to armed men with your wrists tied and your voice cracking, and they believed every word." Sable shouldered their own pack. "That's not a scout's trick, Fen. That's a survivor's. Where'd you learn it?"

I thought about the answer — about eighteen years of being the smallest voice in a garrison full of soldiers, learning to make people laugh so they'd listen, learning to read a room the way Odile reads a trail, learning exactly what every single person I loved expected of me and how to be more than it.

"From you lot," I said.

Ahead of us, the mountain rose white and silent, and somewhere beneath the snow waited the shrine ruins, and beyond them the pass, and beyond that a war we were walking toward with our eyes open. Somewhere behind us, three hunters were discovering that Marrow ford was empty, and a woman with a winter-pond stare was starting to wonder about a sniveling boy who'd been too easy to believe.

One day of lead. Maybe less.

I adjusted my straps, checked my knife — Odile's spare, my old one gone, a debt I intended to collect — and started up the slope beside my family, toward the god under the snow.

For once in my life, I didn't say a word.

I didn't need to.
