## 15. The Machine City

I had not been home in eleven years, and the capital greeted me the way it greets everything: by smelling faintly of copper and pretending it doesn't.

The Godspire district hadn't changed. Nothing here changes; that is rather the point of a place built around a machine that has been dying in slow motion for three centuries. The streets still ran uphill toward the Furnace Hall in concentric rings, each one cleaner and more expensive than the last, until you arrived at the doors themselves — black iron taller than cathedrals, engraved with the sigils of every god the empire had ever swallowed. Vethra's was third from the top. I used to be able to recite them all in order. I found, standing there with my saddlebags and my prisoner manifests, that I could only remember nine.

That should have worried me more than it did.

* * *

They gave me quarters in the hunters' wing, which I took as an honor until I realized they give everyone quarters in the hunters' wing now. The order is thin. We have been thin for years, the way a man is thin before anyone says the word *consumption* aloud. Half the cells on my floor stood empty. The other half held boys barely old enough to shave who looked at me like I was a story their mothers told.

Inquisitor Corvane, slayer of the Marsh covens. Breaker of the Salt Road heresy. The man who burned the witch of Aldenmere with her own familiar's name in his mouth.

None of that is false. I want to be clear about that, even here, even now, especially now: nothing I did was done falsely. That is the horror I keep circling. A man can be sincere his whole life and still arrive at a door he cannot open without asking what's behind it.

The first evening, an acolyte of the Machine — one of the pale, soft-handed ones who never leave the Spire — came to fetch me for the feeding.

"You're early," he said, disapproving, as though punctuality were a symptom.

"I've traveled four months," I said. "I'd like to see it while I still remember why."

He didn't ask what I meant. They never do. Curiosity is considered a spiritual risk in the Spire, which tells you most of what you need to know about the Spire.

* * *

The Furnace Hall is not what pilgrims think it is. Pilgrims see the outer sanctum: gold, incense, the great bronze doors through which the chosen are permitted to glimpse the glow. What pilgrims worship is architecture. The actual machine sits below, in a chamber older than the empire that claims it, and it looks like nothing so much as a heart that someone built a cathedral around after the fact and then pretended the other way around.

It is vast. It is also — and this is the sentence I had not let myself think in twenty years of service — it is *small*, if you understand me. Smaller than its reputation. A heart in a chest the size of a mountain, and the chest mostly empty, echoing.

The acolytes call the chamber the Choir. It holds the machine at its center, half-buried in scaffolding of iron and prayer, and around it, in tiers, sit the Voice-Channels: hollow chairs where the faithful once heard answers. Most of the chairs are dark. Three hundred years ago there were forty-one operating Channels. When I entered the order as a boy there were six.

There are two now.

"Feedings have increased," the acolyte said, in the tone of a man reading a harvest report about a blight. "His Radiance requires sustenance daily. Sometimes twice."

"And the prisoners?"

"Will be processed according to rite." He handed me the manifest. My own manifest, returned to me, stamped and countersigned. Fourteen names. Heretics, deserters, a smuggler of relics, two women from a village whose crime, when I read the file closely, appeared to be that their grandmother had once dreamed accurately about weather.

Fourteen souls to feed a starving god.

"Where is His Radiance's response strongest?" I asked. "Which Channel?"

The acolyte looked at me with something I might, generously, have called pity. "You'll see," he said.

* * *

Here is how a feeding works, since no honest account of it exists outside the Spire and I find I am no longer certain I count as outside or inside anything:

The prisoner is brought into the Choir, washed, anointed, and seated in the Receiving Chair — not one of the Voice-Channels but a cruder thing beneath the machine itself, all clamps and copper. There are prayers. The prayers take longer than they used to; the liturgy has grown like scar tissue over the decades, each generation adding words to cover the silence of the last. Then the Chair opens the prisoner, gently or otherwise depending on the model, and whatever a human being keeps in the place deeper than blood is drawn upward along the conduit into the machine.

And the machine either takes it or it does not.

When I was young, it always took. There was light. There was the hum the old texts call the Assent, a sound like a thousand voices agreeing about something beautiful. Novices wept at their first feeding. I wept at mine. I have never told anyone that, and I am telling no one now; I am writing nothing down; this is between me and whatever part of me has started keeping secrets from myself.

The first prisoner was the relic-smuggler, a wiry man named Osser who cursed us with impressive stamina right up until the anointing draught softened him. The Chair opened him. The conduit filled with that faint silver luminance the acolytes call *tithing-light*.

It rose. It reached the machine.

It stopped. Hung there against the iron like breath on cold glass. And then, slowly, horribly, it slid back down the conduit and dispersed, and Osser died in the ordinary way men die when machines open them, which is to say messily, meaninglessly, and without any god being fed whatsoever.

The acolytes wrote it down. *Tithe refused.* As though the soul had failed an entrance examination.

The second prisoner was refused. The third. The weather-dreamer's granddaughters went together, holding hands, and the tithing-light rose and rose and broke against the machine's threshold like a wave against a seawall, and I stood there in the Choir of the god I had served since I was twelve years old and felt the first clean crack go through me.

Because I knew what refusal meant. Every hunter knows; we simply don't say it. The machine is not refusing sinners. Sinners feed it fine — it drank the Marsh covens greedily enough. The machine is refusing *insufficiency*. These people had nothing in them worth eating. Ordinary souls, ordinary lives, weather-dreams and smuggling and fear — and the god-machine of the empire has grown so starved, so precise in its hunger, that ordinary is no longer on the menu.

Only one thing is.

I thought of the valley. I thought of the report I had sent ahead from Thornvale: *anomalous birth signature detected, vessel-grade, confirmation pending.* I had written it in my official hand, steady as scripture.

Confirmation pending. Confirmation pending. I had been sitting on that word like a man sitting on a lit fuse, telling himself it was patience.

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which is unremarkable; I rarely sleep in the capital. What was remarkable was where I went instead of sleeping. Downward. There are stairs in the Spire that the acolytes pretend do not exist, maintenance ways from the era when the machine was tended by engineers rather than priests, and I have known about them since I was nineteen and curious, which was the last year I was permitted curiosity.

I went down to the machine's root and stood close enough to touch the iron, which is forbidden, which is excommunication, which is death, and I put my palm flat against it because I wanted to know — needed to know, the way you need to press a bruise — whether it would answer me.

It answered.

Not with the Assent. Not with light. With a sound I can only describe as a stomach. A long, low, grinding emptiness, the sound of a thing so hungry it has passed through hunger into something past it. Under my hand the iron was warm the way a fevered brow is warm, and somewhere deep inside, something turned over and whispered — not words, not yet, but the shape of words —

*more.*

I took my hand back. I stood in the dark under the empire's god and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do across four months of road and twenty years of service:

A machine that eats souls. An order that feeds it. An empire that runs on it — the ward-roads, the truth-lamps, the healing fonts, all of it drinking from the same reservoir. And a reservoir running dry, so that fourteen prisoners produce nothing, so that the Voice-Channels die one by one, so that the hunters grow thin and desperate and begin tearing apart valleys looking for—

For a spark. For Vethra's spark. For the last living piece of a god we ourselves killed, dragged back to be poured into the furnace we built out of its corpse, so the furnace can keep burning so the empire can keep pretending it is blessed rather than merely *fueled*.

We do not serve a god. We serve a stove. And I have spent my life as the man who carries wood.

* * *

Here is the crack, then. Let me set it down plainly, because I suspect cracks are the only honest architecture left to me:

If the machine is a furnace, then everything is fuel, and I am a stoker, and the question is only efficiency. Fine. I could live — have lived — with efficiency. But if the machine is a god, then a god that must devour its own worshippers to survive is not a god; it is a wound wearing a crown. And if it is neither — if it is just hunger, ancient and mechanical and blind, hunger that will eat the empire's enemies and then the empire's faithful and then the empire itself, and we have built our roads and laws and children's blessings on top of it like a city built over a fire —

Then Inquisitor Corvane has burned forty-one years of lives for a boiler.

And yet.

And yet the lights still burn. The truth-lamps still shine in the courts. The ward-roads still keep the borderlands safe. Whatever the machine is, it is *ours* now, and it is dying, and when it dies the lamps go out and the roads open and every god we ever swallowed comes home to collect. I have read the contingency scrolls. I helped burn the last order that tried to let it happen. There are perhaps six people alive who understand what follows machine-death, and I am one of them, and I will tell you honestly: I do not know if I am trying to save the empire or merely postpone the funeral.

So I need the spark. You see? Even now, even having pressed my hand to the iron and heard it whisper *more*, I need the spark. That is the trap entire. Doubt changes nothing, because there is no version of tomorrow in which I stop needing it. Faith cracked clean through, and the crack lets in exactly enough light to see the cage.

All that remains is to decide what kind of man carries the spark to the furnace. One who believes. Or one who knows better and does it anyway, with his eyes open, and calls the difference mercy.

* * *

On the fourth morning a courier reached me from the west, mud-spattered, riding hard. Two dispatches.

The first was from my outriders at Thornvale's border: the valley wards had fallen on the night the stars vanished — *the night the stars vanished*, mark that, the sky itself going dark over that one fold of mountains, and no astronomer in the capital willing to speculate on the record. The garrison had not surrendered the anomaly. Negotiations ongoing. Reinforcements requested.

The second dispatch was smaller, sealed with the hunter's cipher, from a source I pay in gold and never name even to myself. One line only:

*The vessel is not what the files say. Neither is your tracker.*

I read it three times by the window light. Sable. My Sable, my quiet shadow, my best instrument, the child the order took from nowhere and made into a compass that points at magic. Twelve years I have aimed that compass, and never once wondered what it cost to make metal out of a person, and never once — this is the part that sat coldest — never once asked myself why the compass was so very good at pointing *away*.

Reinforcements leave within the week. Corvane descends on Thornvale with two hundred hunters and a portable reliquary-cage, and whatever Sable is hiding, and whatever the vessel turns out to be, they will both be standing in a valley with no walls when I arrive.

I summoned my lieutenant and gave the orders. Horses, provisions, the cage, the rites. My voice did not shake. I have been many things in this life, but steady is the one I do best.

Then I sat alone with the second dispatch burning in a dish, and watched the paper curl, and understood at last what I had felt under the machine's iron in the dark:

It wasn't horror.

It was recognition. Hunger knows hunger. And some cold practical corner of me had already begun calculating — not whether I serve a god or a furnace, but how long a starving thing can be pointed at someone else before it turns around.

The reinforcements ride at dawn. I will ride with them, carrying my doubt like a stone under the tongue, and I will look my tracker in the eye across a parley table, and I will know, one way or another, by morning.

Whether Sable lies well enough to fool a man who has finally learned to recognize lying — his own.
