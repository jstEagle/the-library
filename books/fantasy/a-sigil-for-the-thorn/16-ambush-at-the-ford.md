## 16. Ambush at the Ford

I've been in command long enough to know the smell of a bad decision. It smells like wet horse and pine resin and Fen trying very hard not to look frightened. It smells, on this particular gray morning, like the river Vell.

The Vell wasn't on Odile's maps. That should have been my first warning. Rivers that aren't on maps are either too small to matter or too important to share, and this one was running high with snowmelt, wide as a street and loud as a congregation. The ford was the only crossing for twenty miles in any direction. Corvane didn't need to chase us. He only needed to stand where we were going.

"You're smiling," Sable said, falling into step beside me. "That's a bad sign. Marguerite smiles when it's going badly too. Is it something you people are taught?"

"We're taught that if you can't fix a thing, you may as well enjoy watching it break."

"That's a terrible philosophy."

"It's kept me alive thirty years longer than the good ones."

Marguerite came up on my other side, quiet the way they'd been quiet since the pass — since Bram went down under hunter steel with his arms wrapped around Fen like a man trying to hold shut a door against a flood. Bram was riding in the supply cart now, propped upright between sacks of oats, gray-faced and furious about it. He'd lost enough blood to make a strong argument for resting a week. We did not have a week. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it. Thornvale garrison had always communicated its worst news through silence and second helpings.

"The ford," Marguerite said.

"The ford," I agreed.

"How many?"

"Odile counted banners at first light. Sixty outriders visible. There'll be more in the trees." I shrugged. "Call it a hundred and change, plus whatever Corvane brought up from the capital. Against nine of us, one of whom is bleeding, one of whom is fifteen, and one of whom is a defector witch-hunter whose former colleagues would very much like to discuss her career choices."

"Fen isn't fifteen," Marguerite said. "Fen's sixteen. They'll be insufferable about it later."

"Then we'd better make sure there is a later."

Sable looked between us. "You two have a plan. You've had a plan since last night, and neither of you told me."

"Ilsa has a plan," Marguerite said carefully.

"Marguerite knows about the plan," I said, "because Marguerite can guess what I'm thinking, which is a compliment I intend to bill them for."

"And the plan is?" Sable asked.

I watched the river come closer through the trees, white water over dark stone, and I thought: thirty years ago I stood on a different bank and made a different trade, and every day since then has been interest paid on that debt.

"The plan," I said, "is mine alone. The rest of you cross."

* * *

Let me tell you about the oath, because nobody else will, and because a woman marching to her own surrender deserves to have the terms read aloud.

Thirty years ago I was a young officer in the border companies, back when the empire still pretended the valley wards were an act of nature. I was posted to Thornvale because I'd annoyed the wrong colonel, and I stayed because the valley got under my skin the way a splinter does — small, irritating, impossible to ignore. I met the midwife Maren Quintana. I met the dying god, or what was left of him, in a cellar shrine that smelled of candle smoke and iron. And I held the swaddled newborn while Maren spoke the binding words, and I felt the spark go into the child like a coal dropped into snow — hiss, glow, gone.

Vessel. Vethra's last living spark, sewn into a baby who would grow up believing their god simply never answered anyone.

We did it to save the valley. That part was true. The wards needed a heart to anchor them, and the machine in the capital needed feeding or the empire would tear itself apart looking for miracles, and a hidden child in a forgotten valley seemed a kinder arithmetic than open war. I told myself it was kindness. I told myself that for thirty years, which is exactly how long it takes to wear a groove in a lie deep enough to walk in without looking down.

Maren made me swear. Not to protect the child — she knew I'd do that. She made me swear that if the debt ever came due, if the hunters ever came and the choice was the spark or the valley, I would pay it myself and not spend the child to save the rest. *You lit the candle, Captain. You carry it.*

I've carried it thirty years. This morning I mean to set it down.

* * *

The ford was worse than Odile's description, which for Odile is saying something; she describes things the way she shoots, economically and with malice.

The water ran thigh-deep and fast over slick stones, maybe forty paces across, with a shingle bar in the middle that would let a rider catch breath halfway. On the far bank the ground rose into a shelf of rock and scree, and on the shelf, arranged with the neatness of men who wanted to be seen, stood the hunters. Black-and-silver livery, pale banners with the sigil of the god-machine stitched in thread that caught the light wrong. At their center, on a gray horse that matched his mood, Inquisitor Corvane sat like a man attending a lecture he had already read.

"There he is," Odile said from the treeline, glass to her eye. "And he's put archers on both flanks upstream. Clever bastard. Anyone tries to swim it, he pins them in the water."

"He doesn't want a fight," Sable murmured. "Look at the formation. It's theater. He wants us to see that fighting is stupid, so we'll talk."

"Corvane loves talking," Sable added, and something crossed her face that I recognized: the particular exhaustion of a person remembering why they left.

Fen crouched beside me, bow across their knees, trying to look older than sixteen and failing beautifully. "Captain, if we rush the near bank before—"

"No."

"But if—"

"Fen." I put my hand on their head, ruffled their hair the way I've done since they arrived at the garrison aged eleven, all elbows and stolen bread. "When I give the word, you get Marguerite across that river if you have to carry them. Do you hear me? Marguerite goes east. That is the whole of your duty today. Everything else is decoration."

They stared at me. Kids always know. That's the cruelty of it — you can fool a general, fool an inquisitor, fool yourself for three decades, but a sixteen-year-old looks at your face and reads the whole ledger.

"You're doing something stupid," Fen said.

"I am a career soldier. Stupidity is my native language."

Bram's voice drifted weakly from the cart. "If she dies, I want her boots. I've always liked her boots."

"You can't fit in her boots," Odile said.

"I'll stretch them."

And that — gods help me — that was almost enough to make me laugh. That ragged, ridiculous family, arguing about footwear on the edge of a slaughter. I looked at each of them: Odile with her glass and her flat gray eyes; Bram propped among the oat sacks like a disgruntled scarecrow; Fen with their knuckles white on the bow; Sable standing slightly apart, the way defectors always do, half in and half out; and Marguerite, who had guessed, who always guessed, and who was looking at me now with an expression I hadn't seen since Maren died.

"Don't," Marguerite said quietly.

"You don't know what I'm—"

"Ilsa. Don't."

* * *

I rode out alone.

It's a strange thing, how calm the body stays when the mind has already signed the papers. My hands didn't shake on the reins. The morning was cold and clean, mist lifting off the Vell, and I remember thinking the valley had never looked so much like itself — green and stubborn and entirely unaware it was worth anything at all. Maybe that's the trick of hiding places. They work best when even the hidden don't know what they're hiding.

Corvane came down to the waterline alone too. His honor guard stopped at fifty paces, which meant he trusted me, which meant he understood exactly what I was offering before I offered it. Smart men are easy to negotiate with. It's the stupid ones who get you killed.

"Captain Reyes." His voice was mild, educated, faintly tired. "You've led us a long way through ugly country."

"You could have written ahead. I'd have baked something."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. "Is it true what they say about you? That you helped found the warding? That you were there at the binding?"

"Whoever told you that talks too much."

"I've read the old dispatches. A young captain, decorated twice, requesting permanent posting to a valley with no strategic value. Officers request such postings for two reasons: cowardice, or guilt." He tilted his head. "You don't strike me as a coward."

"And you strike me as a man who already knows the answer to every question he asks, and asks them anyway because the asking is the point."

Now he did smile. Small, genuine, terrible. "Guilty parties always recognize me fastest."

Downstream, the mist hung low. Upstream, sixty archers pretended to be scenery. Somewhere behind me, eight idiots I loved more than my own name were holding their breath in the trees.

"Terms," I said. "You want the vessel. The vessel is not here. The vessel went north over the pass three days ago while we made noise in the south — check your scouts' reports, the ones from the ford below the falls, and you'll find cold campfires and a lie I fed a goatherd who talks too much. What's here is the garrison of Thornvale. Nine soldiers, one wounded, no spark, nothing your machine can drink."

"And you expect me to believe the most valuable fugitive in the empire slipped past me?"

"I expect you to believe it because it flatters your cleverness. You didn't fail to catch them. They were never here to catch. Very different entry in the report."

He studied me for a long moment. Wind moved the banner above his men; the machine's sigil writhed on the silk. "And what do you offer instead, Captain Reyes, if your cargo is a ghost?"

"Myself." I said it plainly, the way you'd state a price at market, and heard thirty years click shut behind the word like a door. "Commander of the Thornvale garrison. Keeper of the valley's founding. The woman who bound the spark into flesh with her own hands and has spent three decades keeping it hidden. You want answers — where the vessel was born, how the binding works, what the wards were truly built to conceal, why your god-machine has been starving for a century. I am the last living soul who knows all of it. Kill these soldiers and you get corpses. Take me, and you get the history of your god, written by its jailer."

Somewhere in the trees behind me, I knew, Marguerite was cursing me in every language the garrison had ever picked up, including the ones Odile invented.

Corvane was silent for the length of ten heartbeats. When he spoke again, the mildness had thinned, and underneath it I saw the thing Sable must have seen for years: not cruelty, but hunger wearing cruelty's uniform. A man serving a furnace and calling it a god.

"You'd trade yourself for a garrison of mercenaries."

"I'd trade myself for breakfast. I'm cheap. Ask anyone."

"Why?" And there it was — the real question, the one under all his questions. "Why would a soldier of thirty years' service throw herself on the machine's mercy for other people's children?"

Because I lit the candle. Because a midwife with blood on her sleeves made me swear. Because I watched a newborn be made into a lock and called it mercy, and I have owed a debt since before you took your first vow, Inquisitor, and debts of that size don't accrue interest forever — eventually someone comes to collect the principal.

What I said was: "Because they're mine."

Something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Faith, cracking along a hairline fracture he probably hadn't noticed yet. Men like Corvane don't break all at once. They crack, and keep marching, and the crack widens a little more each time the furnace whispers to them instead of singing.

"The garrison disarms and disperses," he said. "They walk free. You come with me to the capital, and you tell me everything, and you do not die quickly. Those are my terms. I will not improve them, and my patience has a floor."

"They keep their weapons."

"Captain—"

"They keep their weapons, or I start reciting the binding ritual right here in the shallows, loudly, incorrectly, and poison whatever scraps of lore your scholars think they have. I'm old, Inquisitor. I've earned a spiteful death if I want one."

The pause stretched. Mist crawled off the water between us like something eavesdropping.

"Their weapons," he said at last. "But they leave the valley lands within the season. If I find any of them harboring the vessel after today, the bargain burns, and I burn it slowly."

"Bargain," I said, and spat in my palm, because some ceremonies deserve the indignity.

His hand was dry and cool. Somewhere far away, I imagined, a god-machine turned its blind face toward me and began, patiently, to wait.

* * *

They argued, of course. Gods, how they argued.

Marguerite loudest, though they started quiet, which is always worse. "This isn't your debt. Whatever you did thirty years ago, I forgave it in a cellar in Thornvale, I told you that—"

"You forgave the binding. You didn't forgive me. Forgiving takes longer, and I'd rather you owe me the time than waste it visiting a cell in the capital."

"That's not—" Their voice broke, and they hated it, jaw tight, hands fisted. "You don't get to buy our lives with yours and call it arithmetic. That's what they did to *me*, Ilsa. That's the whole crime. Someone decided one person was currency."

"And I'm telling you it's different, because I'm choosing it. That's the entire difference, Marguerite. The whole of it. A chain and a rope are the same cord until someone picks up their own end." I gripped their shoulder. "You gave half your god away to save everyone. Don't begrudge me the smaller version."

Odile shook my hand without a word, which from Odile is a eulogy. Bram, wheezing from the cart, informed me that if I died he would haunt my boots personally. Fen cried and pretended it was the wind and I let them pretend, because that's also command.

Sable was last. She stood apart, arms folded, and for once the defector's distance was gone — she looked at me like a soldier looks at a flag going down.

"He'll try to break you," she said. "Not torture, at least not first. He'll use the machine. It gets inside your head. Whatever it shows you, whatever it promises — remember it's starving. Starving things lie."

"Noted."

"Ilsa." Her voice dropped. "There's a cell in the lower archives of the citadel. Third level, east stair, behind a door marked with a broken wheel. I hid supplies there two years ago when I planned my own defection. Rope, blade, a key that opens half the interior locks. No one checks it. The order thinks that wing is haunted."

"Sable Reyes," I said, "did you build me an escape route into your own surrender?"

"I built it into mine. Consider it inherited." She hesitated, then did something I'll carry out of this chapter of my life if nothing else: she saluted. Full, formal, hunter's salute, fist to the sigil-less breast — the salute of an order she'd abandoned, given to a woman she'd known six weeks. "Walk in tall, Captain. Let him wonder."

I mounted. I gathered the reins. Behind me, my ridiculous, beloved, insubordinate garrison stood along the treeline with their weapons still on — per the bargain — and their faces all pointed at me like compass needles, and Fen shouted, cracked-voiced, "Sixteen! I'm sixteen, you still owe me a birthday!"

"So noted," I called back. "Invoice the empire."

And I turned my horse toward the far bank, toward the black-and-silver ranks and the pale banners and the gray-eyed man on the gray horse, and I rode down into the cold bright water of the Vell. It rose over my boots, my knees, my thighs, pushing hard, the current of a river that wasn't on any map because some crossings matter too much to share. Halfway across, on the shingle bar, I paused and looked back once — just once — at the green wall of Thornvale's hills, at the smudge of the garrison still standing watch, at the small figure that was Marguerite with one hand raised, not waving, just raised. Held up. Like a promise, or a sigil.

Thirty years ago I carried a coal into the snow and called it mercy.

Today I walked into the furnace myself, and finally, finally, the books balanced.

Corvane fell in beside me on the far bank, matching his horse to mine, courteous as a funeral. "You understand," he said, almost gently, "that if the vessel reaches the mountain shrines, everything changes. War. Real war. The kind the histories skip."

"Then you'd best hope," I said, "that your machine likes my storytelling better than theirs."

Ahead, the road bent north toward the capital, toward the furnace and the broken-wheel door and whatever waited in the dark of the third archive level. Behind me, eight voices I loved faded into the river noise, arguing about boots.

I sat straight in the saddle, an old woman riding into captivity with her oath paid in full, and I smiled — because the last thing Corvane needed to learn was that the jailer he'd just captured had spent thirty years teaching her prisoner exactly how to run.
