## 17. Vethra's Summit

The mountain did not want us there. I could tell because it kept trying to kill us.

Three days of climbing after the ford — three days of Odile setting the pace like a woman personally offended by gravity, of Fen's chapped lips counting switchbacks out loud because silence made the fear louder, of Sable walking beside me without touching me, which was its own kind of conversation. Bram rode slung across a travois we'd built from a broken shield and two spears, fevered but stubbornly alive, complaining about the altitude in a voice gone thin as old paper. "In my day," he croaked somewhere above the treeline, "mountains had the decency to be scenery."

"You're from a river town," Odile said.

"Exactly. Rivers have sense. They go *down*."

I laughed, and the laugh hurt my ribs, and I was glad of both. You take what warmth the road offers.

We crossed the last ridge at dusk on the third day, and that was when I understood why Sable had stopped talking about an hour ago and simply watched me with that careful hunter stillness, like a woman holding a door shut against something.

The shrine stood in a bowl of stone below the summit, half-swallowed by snow, older than any empire, older maybe than the idea of empires. It wasn't a building so much as a wound the mountain had agreed to keep open — walls of black rock fitted without mortar, roofless to the sky, and everywhere carved with the sigil. My sigil now, I supposed, though nobody had asked my opinion. The thorn-and-circle, cut deep into every surface, worn smooth at the edges by centuries of pilgrims' hands. Pilgrims who had come here to die, if the small cairns ringing the bowl were any indication. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each stone stacked by someone who had climbed all this way carrying a piece of a god.

"Vethra died here," I said. Not a question. The place hummed it into my bones before I finished speaking.

"The records say so," Sable said quietly. "The order called it the Godfall. The founding grief." She looked around at the cairns, and something moved behind her eyes that she'd spent twenty years learning not to show. "They never told us how many vessels came here first. Before you."

"Before me," I echoed. Because that was the truth we'd dug up in the shrine-ruin lower down the pass, the truth Ilsa had confessed in pieces over a cookfire: every vessel the empire had ever found, they'd fed to their machine. Every one. And the ones the hunters missed — those had heard this same call I was hearing now, faint and patient under the wind, and come here on their own feet, and climbed these stones, and poured themselves out, and been *consumed*. That was the word Vethra used. Not sacrificed. Not returned. Consumed.

The god had been honest about that much, at least. Gods, in my limited experience, are honest the way avalanches are honest.

"We should camp," Odile said, practical to the marrow. "Nobody climbs to a god on empty legs."

But I was already walking down into the bowl, and the snow didn't crunch under my boots so much as *hush*, and behind me I heard Fen start to follow and Sable's hand catch their shoulder. Gentle. *Let them.* This part was mine, and everyone knew it, and knowing it sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

The shrine floor was bare black stone except for a single depression at its center, man-sized, shaped like someone lying down. A bed. A god's deathbed, or a vessel's. Around it, the sigil was carved deepest, and when I knelt beside it and pressed my palm flat against the thorn-and-circle, the spark in my blood answered.

You have to understand what the spark felt like by then. For fifteen years it had been nothing — a warmth I mistook for health, a silence I mistook for a god's absence. Since the wards fell it had been growing, waking, and since the ford, where Ilsa had walked into Corvane's lines with her spine straight and her old oath finally paid, it had been *loud*. A second heartbeat. A guest in my chest who was slowly remembering they used to live here.

Now, kneeling on the stone where Vethra had died, it spoke clearly for the first time.

*Little thorn.*

Fifteen years ago, that voice had been my whole childhood. The silent god of Thornvale hadn't been silent to me; he'd murmured to me through the wards like a father talking through a wall, fragments and lullabies and the smell of rain. Then the wall went quiet, and I'd grieved without knowing what I was grieving, and joined a garrison, and learned to sharpen things. Now the wall was open and the voice came through whole, and it was smaller than I remembered. Thinner. A fire down to its coals.

"I'm here," I said aloud, because some part of me is still seven years old and talks to gods face to face.

*You climbed well,* Vethra said. There was amusement in it, faint and dry, which surprised me until it didn't. Of course the dying god of a mercenary valley would have a sense of humor. We breed for it. *Better than most. Most of them crawled the last stretch.*

"That's morbid."

*I am very old and currently residing in the chest cavity of a person who once stabbed a tax collector.* Wait, that wasn't me— *Morbid is a comfort to me.*

I sat down properly, cross-legged on the stone, because this was going to be long and my knees had opinions. "Then let's not dance around it. I know why I'm here. Ilsa told me what the binding was. The pilgrims tell me the rest." I looked at the deathbed depression, at the hundreds of cairns outside. "You want me to pour the spark back. All of it. And be consumed."

Silence. Wind. Somewhere above, a raven commented rudely on the weather.

*Yes,* Vethra said at last.

"And the other option?"

*There is no other option.*

"There's always another option," I said. "I've made a career of finding them. Usually for people who insisted there weren't."

*Little thorn—*

"No, listen." My voice cracked and I let it. "Every vessel who came here chose this. I get that. I've read enough of the order's records off Sable to know you didn't compel anyone — you *asked*, and they said yes, because you're a god and asking nicely is your whole terrible trick. But every single one of them believed there were only two roads. Give you back everything and die. Or keep you hidden and watch the world starve of whatever it is you do. Nobody ever asked whether those were actually the only roads. Nobody with a mercenary's brain and a bad attitude and a garrison full of people who'd riot on their behalf."

*And what would you ask?*

The question landed soft as snowfall, and underneath it, unmistakably, was hope. This god — this ancient, exhausted, half-eaten thing — had been waiting centuries for someone to argue. That undid me a little. I pressed my palm harder against the sigil.

"Tell me what you actually are," I said. "Not what the hymns say. Not what the machine-cult says. What happens if you burn again? What are you *for*?"

Vethra was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I counted my breaths, and got bored, and started counting ravens instead. When the answer came, it came slow, like something being unpacked from storage after centuries.

*I am the god of the unguarded hour,* he said. *Of the sentry who looks away. Of the door left unlatched between friends. The empire's machine eats devotion — ordered, compelled, harvested belief, faith as fuel, souls as cordwood. I was never its opposite in power. I was its opposite in* kind. *I run on what is given freely, in the gaps, unwitnessed. The supper no one counts. The watch someone takes so another can sleep. Your little valley, Marguerite. Do you think the wards held it closed? The wards were a* courtesy. *Thornvale held itself together with such a density of freely given love that I could rest inside it like a coal in ash. The wards just kept the empire from noticing the warmth.*

My throat tightened. Twenty-nine years in that valley, thinking myself ordinary. Thinking the garrison's noise and Bram's stew and Odile's gruff kindnesses and Fen's terrible jokes were just — life. Small life. Unimportant life.

"It was a hearth," I said. "You were sleeping in our hearth."

*You were keeping me,* he corrected. *There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Which is why I must tell you the rest, and why you will hate it.* A pause. The coals shifted. *If you pour the whole spark into me, I wake entire. And an awakened Vethra is a bonfire in a century of famine. Every frightened soul within a thousand miles will feel the pull of something warm and free, and the empire's machine will feel it too, and the war you have been running from will arrive at a sprint. I will be glorious, little thorn. I will be a beacon. And beacons are for burning.*

"And the machine starves either way," I said. "Corvane showed us that much truth. Feed it prisoners, feed it zealots, it spits them back hollow. Without my spark it dies within the year, and the empire comes apart, and however many millions live under it come apart with it. That's the prophecy math. Whole spark: god rises, war comes, I'm dead. No spark: machine fails, empire collapses, millions suffer, I live with it."

*You summarize cruelly.*

"I had a good teacher. Her name's Captain Reyes and she's currently in an imperial cell because of choices I helped make." I breathed out. "So. Those are the two roads. Pour it all, or keep it all. Die a martyr or live a fugitive. Everyone who's touched this decision has framed it that way — Ilsa thirty years ago, you for centuries, Corvane since the day he first read a ledger about anomalous births. Whole or nothing. It's always whole or nothing with you holy types."

*It has always seemed sufficient,* Vethra said, and there was something almost sheepish in it. A god embarrassed. If I survived the week, I decided, I was having that engraved on a mug for Bram.

"Well, it isn't." I stood up. My legs shook and I ignored them. "Because you just told me what you're made of. Freely given portions. Unwitnessed gifts. The unguarded hour. So answer me this, old man: has anyone ever given you *half*?"

The silence this time was different. Not reluctance. Genuine bewilderment, vast and ancient, a god confronted with arithmetic.

*Half.*

"Half. Here's what I see, standing on your grave with your heart in my chest. The machine doesn't need me whole. It needs *fuel* — real fuel, freely given, which is why it's been starving on conscripted faith. Half a spark, given honestly, keeps it alive. Keeps the empire stable. Buys peace. Not forever — but years. Decades, maybe. Time."

*And the other half?*

"Wakes you. Not whole. Not a beacon. A coal, not a bonfire — small, warm, *hidden*, the way you lived in Thornvale for thirty years. Enough to tend. Enough to grow, slowly, in places the empire doesn't look. In valleys. In garrisons. In suppers and night-watches and all the small free things you actually eat." I was pacing now, boots ringing on the black stone, gesturing at the sky like a lunatic, and I didn't care. "You don't need to be resurrected all at once. Gods aren't bread. You can rise the way spring does — from a hundred directions, quietly, and by the time anyone notices, it's everywhere and it's too late to stop it."

*No vessel has ever—* Vethra began, and stopped. Started again, slower. *The binding rites assume totality. The prophecies assume totality. The machine's hunger assumes—*

"Assumptions," I said. "That's all any of it is. Ilsa assumed hiding you whole was mercy. The pilgrims assumed dying was duty. Corvane assumes the machine is a god and not a furnace with good publicity. Everyone took the shape they were handed and called it fate." I knelt again, put my hand back on the sigil, and let him feel what I felt — deliberately, openly, the way Sable had taught me to lower a guard. "I'm not doing that. Not tonight. I've spent my whole life being told what I am: ward-born, spark-bearer, vessel, weapon, sacrifice. Tonight I decide. And what I decide is that prophecy is a suggestion."

Wind moved through the roofless shrine. Snow drifted down through the open crown of it, catching light that had no source, and for a moment the whole bowl of stone filled with a glow like banked embers breathing. In the center of the floor, the deathbed depression filled with pale radiance, the outline of something that had lain there a very long time ago, waiting.

*It will hurt,* Vethra said. *Splitting. Nothing that lives enjoys being divided.*

"Everything that lives is divided," I said. "Ask anyone with a family."

A sound came out of the stone then, and it took me a moment to recognize it, because I had never heard it before and neither, probably, had the mountain. It was laughter. Thin and crackling, a fire catching after wet years, but unmistakably laughter.

*You are certain you won't give me all of you?* he asked, and it was a formality now and we both knew it. He already knew the answer. He'd known it since the wards fell, maybe. Maybe since the day Ilsa and the others had bound him into a squalling newborn, he'd known what kind of person he was being handed to.

"I'm certain I'm keeping enough of you to introduce you around," I said. "There are people you need to meet. A cook who feeds strangers. A scout who never sleeps so others can. A kid who lied to an inquisitor's scouts with his heart hammering and his story straight. You'll like them. They're your kind of congregation — the kind that never kneels."

*Marguerite Quintana,* said the dying god of the unguarded hour, from inside my chest, with something that in a lesser being I'd have called love, *when you carve the sigil, carve it shallow. I want to feel your pulse around it.*

* * *

They were waiting at the bowl's rim when I climbed out — Sable in front, of course, because she'd have fought her way down to me if I'd stayed another hour, and behind her Odile with her arms crossed, Fen bouncing on their heels, and even Bram propped upright against the travois with a blanket around his shoulders and his fever-bright eyes fixed on the light leaking out of my shirt.

Because I was glowing. Faintly, along the line of my sternum, where the knife was already in my hand and the sigil already half-drawn in my mind. The thorn-and-circle, cut shallow, exactly where he'd asked. My blood on old stone. My choice on older prophecy.

"You're lit up like a festival lantern," Odile said flatly. "Explain."

"Compromise," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "He gets half. The machine gets half. Nobody gets all of anything, and everybody gets to keep living, and the war — if it comes — comes later, and finds us ready, and finds us *together*, which is the only tactical advantage I've ever trusted."

Sable stepped closer. Close enough that the cold coming off her leathers mixed with the warmth coming off my skin. Her eyes tracked the glow, then lifted to my face, and what was in them wasn't hunter's assessment anymore and hadn't been for a while. "The machine-city," she said. "To deliver the half he's owed, we walk into Corvane's capital. Into the Furnace itself."

"Yeah." I looked west, where the mountains dropped toward the lowlands, toward the smoke-smudge on the horizon that was an empire built on a starving god. "Any chance you know a back way into a fortress city?"

Something that was almost a smile crossed Sable's face — the first one I'd seen since Ilsa marched into captivity, and worth more than the stars coming back.

"As it happens," she said, "I helped build the maps they don't keep."

Behind us, in the shrine, the old stone gave one long low hum — a god settling into half a heart, making himself comfortable for the long climb back toward morning — and Fen, watching the light spread under my collarbones, whispered the words we were all thinking:

"So which end of the war do we start with?"
