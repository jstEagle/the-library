## 18. Storming the Furnace

### Fen

The thing nobody tells you about storming a city is how much waiting comes before it. You'd think it's all shouting and running and stabbing. Mostly it's crouched behind a dead cart at midnight, smelling like a goat, while Odile counts her arrows for the ninth time.

"You're breathing too loud," she said.

"I'm always breathing."

"Then do it quieter."

I love Odile the way you love a whetstone. She makes you sharper and she doesn't apologize for the sparks.

We lay in the shadow of the machine-city's outer wall, all forty-one of us — the garrison, plus Sable's deserters, who were a scruffy lot of hunters-turned-runaways with the haunted look of dogs rehomed from a bad kennel. Their leader was a woman called Ash with burn scars up one arm and a laugh like a door hinge. She'd looked at our little company when we met on the road — one cook with a bandaged chest, one captain-shaped hole where Captain Reyes used to be, one mercenary carrying half a god under their ribs — and said, "Well. We're all going to die somewhere. Might as well be somewhere warm."

The furnace-city was warm. You could feel it from the wall: a low thrum underfoot, like the ground itself was purring, and a smell of hot iron and something sweeter underneath, something that made my teeth ache. That was the god-machine. Or the furnace. Depending on who you asked.

Marguerite asked no one. They just stood at the front of our hiding place with their eyes half-closed and their hand pressed flat against the earth, listening to it the way you listen to a sick friend breathing.

"It knows we're here," Marguerite said quietly. "It's been hungry so long it can't tell food from rescue."

"That's cheerful," Bram rumbled beside me. He should have been lying down. He was going to be insufferable about that scar for the rest of his life; I could already tell. But he'd insisted on coming, because he said somebody had to cook for an army of forty, and because — though he'd never say it — Ilsa Reyes was in there, and Bram had loved her longer than I've been alive.

"The gate," Marguerite said, opening their eyes. "Fen. Odile. You hold the gate."

"Hold it," Odile repeated flatly. "Against a garrison."

"Against whatever Corvane sends out after us," Marguerite corrected. "And more importantly — against whoever tries to follow us *in*. Sable says the lower cells are where they keep prisoners. If the fighting goes badly, someone might run for the cells to use them as hostages. Don't let them."

It was the least glorious job in the whole plan and Marguerite gave it to us without apology, which is how I knew it was the most important one. Marguerite never wasted glory. They spent it like a miser spends coin.

So that was my war: a gate, a lever, two people, and the longest night of my life.

* * *

### Odile

I have held a river crossing against cavalry with six men and a prayer. I have held a treeline against wolves. I have held my tongue for eleven years in a garrison full of people who talk through their feelings like water through a sieve.

The gate of the machine-city was the worst holding of my life, because for the first hour nothing happened at all.

Above us the city roared — actual fire now, orange light climbing the towers where Marguerite and Sable had gone with their deserters to raise hell and draw every eye upward. The plan was simple enough to fit on a knife blade: make noise above, break the prisoners out below, put Marguerite in front of Corvane before the man could feed anyone else into his starving god. Simple plans are the only kind that survive contact with reality, and even then it's a near thing.

"They're not coming," Fen whispered. My young shadow, pressed against the gatehouse wall, crossbow held wrong again. "Odile, why aren't they coming?"

"Because the smart ones are busy upstairs," I said. "And because the stupid ones are being saved for us. Patience."

She was right, of course. The stupid ones came at the second hour, a dozen hunter-soldiers in grey, sent around by some sergeant with a brain, to retake the gate and seal the city. I put three arrows into them before they reached the courtyard and Fen — bless her terrified heart — remembered everything I'd ever screamed at her and took the high wall of the gatehouse, dropping bolts down onto their flank until they broke.

Then it stopped being archery and became the other thing, the close ugly thing, and I learned that Fen had grown a spine sometime between the pass and here, because she stood next me in the gate mouth with a borrowed sword and did not run.

A hunter officer got inside my reach. Old habit saved me — elbow, knee, throat — and I put him down and turned to check on her and found her standing over a soldier twice her size, shaking, sword bloody, eyes enormous.

"I didn't kill him," she said. "I hit his arm. Is that — Odile, is that allowed?"

"He'll live," I said, checking. "Then yes. Allowed."

Something like a laugh came out of her, high and cracked. "We're holding the gate."

"We're holding the gate."

And then the doors below the gatehouse — the cell block, the reason Marguerite had posted us here — banged open from the inside, and I understood what we were actually holding it against.

They brought Captain Ilsa Reyes out first. Because of course they would. A hostage worth more than the whole city, marched into our courtyard with blades at her back, grey-faced and limping but walking upright, because that woman has never once in thirty years done anything slouched.

"Well," said Captain Reyes, surveying the burning skyline, the broken gate, and her two youngest soldiers standing in it. "You look terrible. Both of you."

"Ma'am," Fen managed, and burst into tears.

* * *

### Sable

I want it written down somewhere, in some honest ledger, that I fought my way into the heart of the order that raised me, and that the hardest part was not the killing.

The hard part was Halloran.

He found me on the bell-tower stairs, because Halloran always found me. Twelve years he'd been my senior, my instructor, the closest thing the Order ever let me have to family. He stood on the landing with his white cloak singed at the hem and his sword drawn and his face doing the terrible arithmetic of a man deciding whether I was a traitor or a prisoner.

"Sable." My old name in his mouth. The name they gave me when they took the valley out of me and wrote the Order in its place. "Put it down. Corvane will be merciful. He's always valued you."

"Corvane fed a child to the furnace last month," I said. "I read the ledger. Did you?"

"The vessel's spark sustains thousands—"

"A *child*, Halloran."

He flinched. Good. Somewhere under all that training there was still a person who flinched.

Below us the tower stairwell rang with fighting — Ash's deserters holding the landing below, giving me exactly this much time and not a second more. Above, through the smoke, I could see Marguerite climbing toward the spire where the machine's heart beat loudest, firelight catching on them like they were made of it, and I had a job to do and it was this: keep the stairs clear. Keep everyone off Marguerite's back. Keep my own past off it too, if I could manage.

"Halloran," I said, and lowered my voice, because this part wasn't for anyone else. "You taught me that the Order hunts what threatens people. You made me believe it. So tell me — honestly, the way you taught me — what does the machine threaten? Look at what we've become to feed it. Are *we* still protecting anyone?"

He didn't answer. His sword hand shook. That was answer enough, and it broke something in me anyway, because I'd wanted him to fight me clean. Hatred is easy to carry. Doubt is heavy as wet sand.

He came at me anyway. Duty does that to people; I'd know.

We fought the length of the landing and I fought him the way he taught me — which is to say I fought him fairly, and fairly nearly killed me, and in the end I didn't beat him with skill at all. I beat him because Marguerite's voice came down the stairwell, raw and blazing, speaking a name I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was six years old — *Vethra* — and the whole tower shuddered, and Halloran glanced up.

One glance. One heartbeat of doubt, aimed at the sky instead of at me.

I put him down with the flat, then bound his hands, then sat him against the wall where the smoke wouldn't reach.

"When this is over," I told him, "ask yourself why you looked up."

Then I ran for the spire, because Marguerite was alone up there with Corvane, and I had promised myself, somewhere over the mountains, that I was done letting the people I love walk into furnaces without me.

* * *

### Fen

Afterward, when people ask me about the night we stormed the Furnace — and people ask constantly, because apparently I'm famous now, which is ridiculous — I always tell them about the moment the stars started coming back.

But the truth is the moment I remember best is smaller and stupider. It's Bram.

When the fighting moved inward and Odile went to escort Captain Reyes to safety, someone had to hold the courtyard, and Bram appeared out of nowhere with a meat cleaver and a pot lid, bleeding through his bandages, planting himself next to me like a wall that had decided to grow opinions.

"You're supposed to be resting," I said.

"And you're supposed to be a recruit," he said, "and yet here we both are, exceeding expectations." Then, gruffly, watching the fires climb the spire: "Is Marguerite up there?"

"At the top. With Sable."

Bram nodded slowly, the way he nods when bread is proving properly. "Then it'll hold," he said. "Those two. Whatever happens, it'll hold." And he put his huge scarred hand on my shoulder and we held the courtyard together, a cook and a kid with a pot lid and a cleaver, while half a god went up the tower to meet its maker.

That's when the sky changed.

I felt it before I saw it — a pressure lifting off the world, like a hand unclenching — and then, above the burning city, between the smoke columns, the stars began to come back. Not all at once. One, then three, then a scatter of them, like embers breathed alight. The whole battlefield slowed. Hunters and deserters alike stopped swinging and stared up, faces lit silver, and I heard a woman near me start to sob and couldn't tell you if she was one of ours or theirs.

Because here is the thing I understood, standing in that courtyard with my heart hammering: those stars had been gone from every sky for years, and every soul in that city — hunter, deserter, soldier, all of us — had been living under a lid without knowing it. And Marguerite Quintana, who grew up herding goats and mending fences and complaining about Bram's stew, had just taken the lid off the world.

Then the tower door burst open, and Corvane came out ahead of them, and everything after that happened very fast and I mostly watched it from the ground, because Odile tackled me behind a barricade, which — fine. Fair.

* * *

### Odile

I saw the end of it from the courtyard, through the tower doorway, and I will carry what I saw until they put me in the ground.

Marguerite stood before the machine with their shirt open and Vethra's sigil carved shining into their chest, and the light coming off them was not firelight. It was older than firelight. It was the color of the sky remembering itself. Corvane knelt — the Inquisitor of the Empire, the man who burned valleys, kneeling on his own scorched floor with his face wrecked open like a boy's — and the great machine, the furnace that had eaten children and gods and centuries, was *singing*. Low and vast, a note you felt in your back teeth, a note that sounded — and I know how this sounds — grateful.

Half the light poured out of Marguerite into the machine. Half stayed. I watched them choose it. Nobody forced them. That's the part that matters. The empire offered them prophecy, the god offered them martyrdom, and Marguerite looked at both and said, essentially, *we'll split the bill.*

Then the stars came back, and Marguerite swayed, and Sable caught them — caught them like it was the single purpose Sable had been built for, arms closing around them, forehead pressed to theirs — and the last thing I heard from the spire before the singing faded was Marguerite laughing. Actually laughing. Wrecked and bleeding and laughing, saying something to Sable too quiet for the rest of us, something that made Sable — Sable, who I once watched eat a boiled boot without comment — press their face against Marguerite's shoulder and shake.

Fen surfaced beside me, soot-streaked, alive, gripping my sleeve.

"Did we win?" she asked.

I looked at the courtyard: deserters and hunters standing down together, weapons forgotten; Bram weeping openly into his apron; Captain Reyes limping toward the tower with her chin up and her eyes wet; the stars thick overhead for the first time in years.

"No," I said, because somewhere beyond the mountains an empire had just lost its excuse and its faith in one night, and empires do not take that gracefully. "But tonight, we did."

Down in the courtyard, Corvane rose to his feet, unsteady as a newborn foal, and gave an order in a voice scraped raw: *"Stand down. All of you. Stand down."*

His hunters looked at him. Then at the sky.

And far away, past the passes, past the borders, in the dark capital where the machine's masters sat — I felt it in my bones, the way you feel weather coming — they would already have seen the stars return.

And they would be asking who to blame.
