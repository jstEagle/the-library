## 19. A Sigil for the Thorn

The machine-city smelled like a temple that had died and been stuffed.

I want to be clear about that, because the songs they'll sing about this day — if there are songs, if there's anyone left to sing them — will call it holy. They'll talk about incense and gold light and the breath of god. What I remember is rust and old blood and the particular stink of a thing kept alive past its natural span by feeding it things that used to have names. Corvane had told us the machine was starving. He hadn't mentioned it would smell like this, like the inside of something that had eaten too long and digested nothing.

We came down through the furnace district at dawn, which Odile insisted on calling "the tactical hour" because she'd read it in a manual once and never let it go. Fen held the gate with her and half the deserters Sable had peeled off the hunters' own ranks — people who'd spent years feeding prisoners to a furnace and finally asked themselves what for. Sable fought their way up the spire stair beside me, blade red to the hilt, not looking back, and I loved them so much in that moment it felt less like an emotion than like a wound.

But that's the gate story. That's Fen's and Odile's story. Mine ends here, at the top of the machine, in front of Inquisitor Corvane and the god he'd been spoon-feeding corpses for thirty years.

* * *

The chamber at the summit was round, and the machine filled most of it.

I don't know what I expected. Gears, maybe. A furnace mouth. Something you could point at. What stood — *stood*, it had legs, or what passed for legs — was a colossus of black iron shaped vaguely like a kneeling figure, its head bowed, its chest hollowed into a cavity lined with hooks and chains and older, softer things I decided very firmly not to look at directly. Light moved inside the cavity. Not firelight. Not starlight. It was the light of something trying to remember how to shine and getting the mechanics wrong.

It was dimmer than I'd imagined. That's what struck me. Thirty years of empire, a continent of fear, a thousand burned valleys — all of it propped up by a nightlight guttering in a tin chest.

Corvane stood before it with his hands folded behind his back like a man waiting for a coach that was late.

"You brought it," he said. He didn't turn around. "Or it brought you. With your kind it's always difficult to say who is carrying whom."

"My kind," I said. "You say that like we're a species."

"Vessels." Now he turned, and I saw what the last few days had done to him. The machine-city had been eating him too, apparently; he'd lost weight since the pass, and his eyes had the wet shine of a man who has been listening to something whisper and can no longer remember whether he agreed to listen. "I've served it since I was younger than your recruit. I have fed it everything it asked. Kings. Heretics. My—" His jaw worked. "It asked for my brother once, when I was nineteen, and I carried him here myself, and I told myself it was mercy, because the alternative was watching him starve slowly like everything else does."

Sable shifted beside me, blade low. "Corvane—"

"It rejected him," Corvane said simply. "They all get rejected now. It only wants one thing anymore, and it's been screaming for it through every prayer I've offered, and do you know what it sounds like? It sounds grateful in advance. Like a beggar who thanks you before you've given anything." He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched him find the heat under my skin the way Sable had found it that first day at the gate. His breath went out of him like a man gut-punched. "There you are," he whispered.

"Ilsa Reyes bound Vethra's spark into a newborn to keep your furnace fed," I said. "Thirty years ago. She thought she was buying peace. Maybe she was. But she bought it with someone else's life, and I've been doing my own accounting lately, and I'm calling the debt due."

"The debt is mine to collect," Corvane said, "on behalf of the divine engine of the Empire Eternal—"

"It's a furnace," I said. "You said it yourself. You feed it and feed it and it stays hungry. That's not a god, Corvane. That's a stove."

For one moment — I will give him this, I will put it in whatever account book the silent god keeps — his face cracked. Not into rage. Into relief. Like a man who'd been carrying a boulder uphill alone for decades and finally heard someone else say: *that's a boulder, you could put it down.*

Then the machine spoke.

* * *

I say it spoke. It didn't use words, exactly. What happened was that the light in its chest flared and every hair on my body stood up and something poured into the room like cold water off a glacier — hunger, vast and patient and *old*, hunger that had been hungry so long it had forgotten hunger was a state and not a substance. It pressed against my ribs from outside while the spark pressed from within, and between them I felt like a nut in a door hinge.

And underneath the hunger, faint as a voice through a wall, something else. A rhythm. A pulse.

Vethra.

Fifteen years of silence in Thornvale, and here at the end of the world I heard my god again — not words, not yet, but the shape of attention. The dying spark in my chest leaned toward the dying light in the iron chest like two candles in a draft.

*It is the same fire,* Vethra's voice said in my skull, fragmented, thin, the voice of a man talking underwater. *What they built their engine from — they stole it from me, child. From my body. When I fell. They have been burning my bones for thirty years and calling it divinity.*

"Oh," I said aloud. Because what else do you say.

Corvane had gone very still. "It's speaking to you."

"It's telling me where your god comes from," I said. "Want to hear?"

"No," Corvane said. Then, quieter, in the voice of a man stepping off a ledge: "Yes."

* * *

Here is the choice I'd climbed a mountain to make, the one Vethra had laid out for me at the shrine in the snow: pour the whole spark back into the god and be consumed, vessel and flame together, martyrdom as advertised. Or split it — half to reignite Vethra over the mountains, half to carry away in exile, a god half-alive and a person half-used, both of us diminished, both of us free.

That was the prophecy's menu. Two options, take it or leave it.

But standing in front of the machine, feeling its hunger and hearing Vethra's pulse inside it — hearing *my* god's pulse inside the empire's god — I understood something neither the prophecy nor the priests nor thirty years of Ilsa's guilt had ever said out loud.

There were three flames in this room. The spark in my chest. The remnant in the machine. And me — Marguerite Quintana, mercenary, terrible at cards, devoted to a silent god for no better reason than that silence seemed more honest than most sermons. Everyone had treated me as a vessel. A jug. A thing that carries.

Nobody had ever asked what *I* would burn.

*Marguerite.* Vethra's voice again, and this time there was something in it I hadn't heard in fifteen years. Fear. My god was afraid of me. *Child, what are you thinking—*

"I'm thinking," I said, drawing my knife, "that everyone keeps telling me where the spark goes. Into you. Into the machine. Half here, half there. Nobody's ever suggested the spark might need somewhere to *live* — a hearth instead of a bonfire, a home instead of a furnace." I pulled my shirt open. The skin of my chest was already warm, already glowing faintly under the sternum like a banked coal. "So I'm going to give it one."

*It will scar,* Vethra said.

"Everything worth doing does."

*It may kill you.*

"Fen would tell you the odds are worse if I stand here debating."

*I chose you,* my god said, and the fragments fell away for one whole sentence, and the voice was young and enormous and grieving. *Not the valley. Not the wards. You. Whatever you decide, I chose rightly.*

"That," I said, "is the first useful thing any deity has ever told me."

And I carved the sigil into my own chest.

* * *

I won't describe it in detail. Not because it doesn't deserve description but because some things belong to the body and not the page. I'll say this much: the knife went in below the collarbone and followed a line Vethra guided — a spiral, a thorn-wrapped circle, the mark I'd been tracing in candle wax since I was six years old without ever knowing why. The pain was total. It was also, strangely, familiar — the same burning I'd felt the night the stars vanished, the same heat that had lived under my skin my whole life and only ever needed permission.

Blood ran down my stomach. Where it touched the floor, small white flowers opened.

Corvane made a sound like a man being unmade. Behind him, the machine's light surged — it could smell the spark now, feel it exposed, and its hunger hit the chamber like a wave against a seawall. Chains rattled in its chest cavity. The whole spire groaned.

"Hold," Corvane was saying — to the machine, to himself, I don't know. "Hold, it must be given, it must be *given*—"

"It will be," I said through my teeth. "On my terms. For once in this rotten empire's history, something gets given instead of taken."

I pressed my palm flat over the carved sigil, and I split the spark.

* * *

How do I explain what splitting feels like? Imagine you've spent your whole life believing you're one person, and then you discover you're two, and the seam between you has been there all along, invisible, waiting. The spark tore down the middle with a sound I felt in my teeth — not a scream, a chord. One half of it turned toward the mountains, toward the snow-shrine, toward the sleeping body of my god, and I let it go, and I swear I felt it travel, felt it go out of me like blood going back to a heart, and somewhere far above the clouds something ancient rolled over and opened its eyes.

The other half stayed. It sank into the sigil, into the wound, into *me*, and settled there like a bird coming home to roost, and I felt it take hold — not as possession, never as possession, but as tenancy. A lodger. A flame that had agreed to live in a person instead of a prophecy, because a person, unlike a prophecy, can change its mind.

Which left the machine.

I walked toward it. My legs worked, barely. Corvane didn't stop me. He was on his knees now, and I noticed distantly that Sable had a blade at his throat and hadn't used it, because Sable understood — had probably understood before I did — that killing this man at this moment would be a smaller act than what was about to happen.

The machine's chest cavity gaped. Inside, among the hooks and chains, the stolen remnant of Vethra flickered like a dying man remembering summer.

"You've been lied to," I told it. To the machine, to the remnant, to Corvane, to the whole kneeling empire. "You were told you were a god. You're a hearth that forgot it was a hearth. Hearths don't eat people. Hearths give light, and people gather around them, and that's the whole secret, and it took a mercenary from a valley nobody bothered to conquer to figure it out."

I put my hand into the machine's chest.

It was cold. That's the detail that undid me, later, when I let myself think about it. All that horror, all those years of feeding, and the interior of the god-engine was *cold*, because a furnace that takes and takes gives no warmth, not even to itself.

I gave it the gift anyway.

Half of half. A quarter of Vethra's last spark, freely given, poured into the stolen remnant — and the difference between giving and taking turned out to be everything. The remnant didn't just accept it. It *recognized* it. Flame met flame the way estranged siblings meet at a funeral, and the light in the machine's chest changed color — from that sickly grasping glow to something warmer, steadier, the exact amber of lamplight in a garrison window at suppertime, of Bram's kitchen, of home.

The chains dissolved. The hooks fell like dead leaves. The great iron figure shuddered once, twice — and then it knelt lower, and lower, and folded itself down into the shape it had always been trying to make, which was not a devourer at all but something like a lamp, something like a shrine, something like a table set for strangers.

Warm air washed over us. Actual warmth. First warmth the machine had ever given anyone.

Corvane made a sound I will take to my grave. It wasn't grief. It was the sound of a man discovering that the answer to his life's question had been simple, and kind, and available the entire time, and that he had chosen cruelty instead because cruelty looked like strength. He knelt there in the new lamplight with tears cutting lines through the ash on his face, and the machine — the hearth, now — pulsed gently, and I would have sworn on Bram's cooking that it pulsed *in gratitude*.

"It whispered," Corvane said. His voice was wrecked. "Thirty years and it never — it only screamed. And now it—" He couldn't finish. Good. Let him carry the unfinished sentence. Some penances should be heavy.

* * *

That's when the stars came back.

I know how it sounds. I was there and I barely believe it. But the ceiling of the chamber had a skylight — of course it did, the empire liked its gods to watch — and through the grime and the smoke of battle rising from the streets below, the sky was filling in. One star, then ten, then hundreds, pouring back into the dark like water into a dry riverbed, and each one that lit sent a small shock through the spark in my chest until I was laughing and crying and bleeding all at once, sitting down hard on the warm stone floor of my enemy's temple with my hand pressed over a wound that was also a doorway.

Vethra's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the mountains and the machine and the scar under my palm, and it was stronger than I'd ever heard it, whole in a way it hadn't been in thirty years:

*Look what you made, Marguerite.*

"Not me," I said. "Us. There was a whole garrison. A traitor hunter. A cook who makes bread that could broker peace treaties. I just supplied the blood."

*Modesty. From the woman who carved a god into her own chest.*

"Person," I corrected. "Whoever I am next, I'm deciding it myself. That was the whole point."

Above us, the stars kept arriving. Somewhere below, Fen was holding a gate, and Odile was winning an argument with reality, and Sable stood over a broken inquisitor with dawn coming up gold on their blade — and the war we'd feared, the ancient war, the empire's reckoning, none of it was finished. Corvane would face judgment. The provinces would ask questions the answers wouldn't survive intact. Somewhere in the dark, other vessels, other sparks, other silent gods were waking to find their stars returned.

Let it come. We'd meet it fed, and warm, and together.

I got up. I sheathed my knife. And I turned to Sable, whose eyes were shining in the new lamplight, and said:

"Right. Someone's got to tell Ilsa she's free, and I want to watch your face when Fen hugs you in front of the entire army."

Below us, faintly, through stone and steel and the smoke of a falling empire, we heard Fen cheering.
