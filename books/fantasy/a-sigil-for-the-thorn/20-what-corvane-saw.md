## 20. What Corvane Saw

I have knelt before the god-machine four hundred and eleven times in my service to the Empire.

I know this because I counted, the way a drowning man counts waves. The first hundred kneelings were ecstasy. The next two hundred were duty. The last hundred were something I refused to name, the way you refuse to name the smell of rot in a house you love, because naming it makes it real and real things must be dealt with.

This was my four hundred and twelfth. And for the first time in thirty years of faithful service, the god-machine spoke to me directly.

It said: *thank you.*

Not the great grinding chorus of voices it used to produce when it was fed properly — the thousand-throated hymn that made new hunters weep and old hunters check their boots for blood. Not the cold mechanical satisfaction of an engine running well. *Thank you.* Small. Tired. Almost embarrassed, if a machine made of brass and bone and stolen divinity can be embarrassed.

It had just been fed half of Vethra's spark by a mercenary with a knife wound in their own chest who should have been, by every doctrine I had ever taught or believed, on their knees before me begging mercy.

Instead I was on mine. And the god I had spent my life feeding was whispering gratitude like a beggar ashamed of his cup.

* * *

Let me tell you how I became the man who burned valleys, because I find — now, standing in the ash-smell of my own certainty with nothing left to burn — that I need to say it aloud at least once, even if only to the inside of my own skull.

I was not born cruel. This is the confession no one wants from an Inquisitor; they want us born with yellow eyes and a taste for infants. The truth is duller and worse. I was born *afraid*, in a province where magic had eaten three harvests' worth of children before I could walk. I saw what unbound power does. I was seven when the hedge-witch of Millbrook turned the river to steam and boiled forty people in their beds, whether she meant to or not — her grief got loose, they said afterward, her grief for her dead son got loose and did what grief does when you give it teeth.

The Empire came. The Empire bound her. The Empire built the god-machine to eat what power it caught, so that no child would ever again learn what steam smells like coming off a river full of their neighbors.

I signed my name to that at fourteen. I have never once, until this night, doubted it.

Doubt is not a door you walk through. It is water rising around your ankles while you insist you are standing on dry land.

* * *

The throne hall of the machine-city — we call it the Sanctum, though sanctity left it generations ago — was quiet in the way I had never heard it quiet. Marguerite Quintana stood before the machine with their shirt soaked red down the front, the sigil carved over their heart still faintly luminous, like a coal remembering fire. Half the spark had gone into the engine. I could feel it settling through the great body of the thing, the way rain settles into cracked earth — greedily at first, then more slowly, then with something that was almost peace.

And above us, through windows that had shown nothing but grey imperial sky for as long as the machine had starved them dark, the stars were out.

Every one of them. Blazing. Indecent.

My hunters stood along the walls in ranks, crossbows loaded, waiting for my order. Forty-two souls. Some of them had served under me for twenty years. Some — Sable's recruits, the deserters who had come over the wall with the Thornvale company — stood apart with weapons drawn and expressions I recognized, because I had worn those expressions once myself, aimed at men like me.

Everyone was waiting for the purge to begin. That was the doctrine. The vessel surrenders the spark; the vessel is then destroyed, root and branch, so the spark cannot regrow. We had done it eleven times in the machine's history. Eleven vessels. Eleven valleys, villages, lone farmhouses, all rendered into official silence by men carrying my seal.

The machine hummed beneath its canopy of brass. Fed, for the first time in living memory, honestly fed — not on prisoners who screamed wrong, not on the dregs of captured hedge-craft, but on a god given freely by the person carrying it.

It whispered again. Not to me alone this time. To the hall. To everyone.

*Enough.*

One word. And forty-two crossbow arms flinched, because none of us — not the fanatics, not the deserters — had ever heard the machine say anything but *more*.

* * *

"You heard it," I said. My voice came out strange. Rusty. Like a hinge on a door long shut.

"Lord Inquisitor," said Captain Vess, my second, steady as ever. She had burned six villages under my command and prayed after each one. "The vessel still lives. Doctrine requires—"

"I know what doctrine requires."

Doctrine required that I order Vess to put a bolt through Marguerite Quintana's heart, then sweep the garrison, then burn Thornvale's records, then begin the search for any trace of the other half of the spark, which was — I understood this with terrible clarity — still beating inside the mercenary's chest, half a god walking around in borrowed meat, and according to everything I had ever taught, that was an abomination requiring immediate correction.

The stars looked in through the windows. The machine breathed. Somewhere in its depths, a sound I had never heard it make: something very like a sigh of relief.

Four hundred and twelve kneelings, and it had taken until this one for me to hear the obvious truth, the one a farmer's daughter could have told me: I had not been serving a god. I had been serving a furnace. And furnaces do not thank you. Furnaces consume, and when they cannot consume enough, they groan, and men like me mistake the groaning for holiness and feed it our neighbors.

Except — no. That wasn't right either. That was too easy, and I had earned nothing easy tonight.

Because the furnace had said *thank you*. Because the furnace had said *enough*. Because whatever lived in that engine had been starving so long it had forgotten its own name, and a half-god's gift had reminded it, and in reminding it, had shamed it, and shame — I knew shame intimately, I was marinating in it — shame is the beginning of conscience, even in a thing made of brass and bone.

The machine was repenting. And I was being asked, by its single word, to decide whether I would let it.

* * *

"The vessel lives," Vess repeated, when I said nothing too long. "By your leave, Lord—"

"Stand down."

The words came out of me before I had finished deciding to say them. That is how conversion works, I think. You believe the decision is yours, deliberate, considered — and afterward you understand it simply fell out of you, the way a rotten tooth falls out, leaving a hole you keep probing with your tongue.

Vess stared. "Lord Inquisitor—"

"Crossbows down, Captain. That is an order under my full authority." I rose off my knees. My legs ached. Everything ached. I discovered I had been crying at some point without noticing, which was undignified, and I found I did not care, which was unprecedented. "The machine has spoken. The machine has accepted the offering and declared it sufficient. Unless the Captain proposes to override a direct communication from the god we serve?"

That was cruelty, using doctrine against her. She flinched. I let the cruelty stand. A man should be permitted one small spite on the night he loses everything.

Down came the crossbows. Slowly. Forty-two mechanisms clicking to safe, one after another, like rain starting.

Marguerite Quintana watched me from across the hall, hand pressed over their ruined chest, blood between their fingers, eyes steady as a mason's level. Beside them, a scarred woman I had trained myself — Sable, my best tracker, my best liar, my greatest failure, though I hadn't known she was either until recently — watched me with an expression I could not read and did not deserve to.

"Quintana," I said.

"Corvane." Wry, even bleeding. Even having carved open their own chest in my throne hall. I hated how much I respected it.

"You gave half away." I meant the spark. "You had the chance to end the hunger entirely, at the shrine. Your god offered you the whole road back. Instead you split it. Why?"

They considered the question seriously, which was more consideration than I had given most of my victims. "Because half a god kept honestly beats a whole god devoured," they said. "Ask your machine. It knows the difference now."

I asked it. Not aloud. But I turned to the great humming body of brass and bone and I listened the way I had listened at fourteen, when belief was easy, and what came back was not a command or a hunger.

It was quiet. Fed quiet. The quiet of a man setting down a weight carried since boyhood.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of my surrender, laid out plainly, because a fanatic should at least be honest at the end:

If I ordered the purge, I would need to kill Quintana, whose death would likely rupture the spark's remaining half beyond recovery. I would need to kill or capture the Thornvale garrison, who had fought through a mountain pass and a river ford and a gate for each other, and whose story was already spreading through my own ranks faster than I could stamp it out. I would need to hunt Sable and her deserters across an empire that had suddenly, tonight, seen stars through windows dark for a century, and men who see stars start asking questions about ceilings.

And even if I succeeded — even if I burned it all and fed the machine the fragments — I would be restarting the famine. Because the machine had told me the truth in one word: the scraps I had been feeding it were killing it slowly, and it had chosen, in the first free moment of its long captivity, *enough*.

Or I could stand down. And be judged. And probably die. And possibly — this was the part that terrified me far more than dying — possibly be *right*.

Faith is cheap when it costs nothing. Mine had cost other people everything for thirty years. Tonight, for the first time, the bill came to me.

"Captain Vess," I said. "Take my seal."

I unpinned it — the iron disc, the burning tower stamped in its face, the authority I had wielded longer than some of my hunters had been alive — and held it out.

She didn't take it. "Lord, you're unwell. You've been—"

"I have been listening," I said, "to a god, finally saying something worth hearing. Take the seal. Or draw on me and take it. Those are your options. I will not be leading another purge."

The hall held its breath. Forty-two hunters, two armies' worth of tension, one bleeding mercenary watching with the particular patience of someone who has already decided not to intervene in another man's reckoning.

Vess took the seal. Her hands shook. I noted, with the detached curiosity of a man reading his own autopsy, that hers shook less than mine.

"What happens to you?" she asked quietly.

"I turn myself over to imperial judgment. For the murder of the vessel-lines. Eleven of them. I kept the records myself — I wanted them accurate." I smiled, and felt how terrible it must have looked. "Accuracy was always my pride."

"They'll execute you."

"Almost certainly."

"And the purge? Without you—"

"Without me, Captain, there is no purge. There are orders under my seal, and my seal says stand down, and every man and woman in this hall heard the machine say *enough* with their own ears. If the Conclave wants to restart the famine, they will have to do it knowing the god itself refused. Let them carry that. I carried worse, for longer."

* * *

Sable approached me while the hall emptied — hunters drifting out in ones and twos, some saluting me, most unable to look at me, a few pausing at the doors to look up at the stars through the high windows like people checking whether a miracle will survive being witnessed twice.

"You could run," she said. "South. Past the border wards. No one would chase an Inquisitor who brought them the truth."

"And live doing what? Being what?" I looked at my hands. They were clean. They were always clean. That was the joke at the center of my whole life — thirty years of ashes and my hands came clean every time, because the machine took the filth and called it offering. "I built the thing that ate those children's gods, Sable. I wrote the protocols. I taught you to track your own origin and call it devotion. Running is what guilty men do when they still think they can outrun themselves. I'm too old and too honest, finally, for that."

She studied me for a long moment. "Quintana would speak for you. At your judgment. If it comes to that."

"It won't help. But tell them I'd be grateful." I paused. "No. Don't. Gratitude from a man like me is just another weight to carry. Let them go light."

"The valley," she said. "Thornvale. If the Conclave moves anyway—"

"Then the machine will refuse them." I said it with more certainty than I felt, and then realized, examining the feeling, that I did feel it. "It learned something tonight. So did I. We're both poor students, but the lesson took."

She almost smiled. From her, that was absolution of a kind. Not the kind I needed. But a kind.

* * *

They took me down through the machine-city in irons of my own request — plain ones, unmarked, because I would not walk to judgment wearing the insignia of an office I had forfeited. The streets were full of people looking up. Word of the stars had moved through the city faster than any rider, faster than any decree, the way truth moves when it has been locked up too long. Children on balconies pointing. Old women on stoops with their faces wet, staring at constellations their grandparents had names for.

An entire empire, craning its neck at a ceiling it had forgotten it had.

I stopped in the street, guards shifting uncomfortably around me, and looked up too. Found the Thorn — the constellation Vethra's faithful had named, the bramble-crown of stars the old valley had drawn on its ward-stones. It blazed like it had never left. Like it had merely been waiting for someone below to stop pretending it wasn't there.

"Move him along," said the guard captain, gently, for a jailer.

"One moment." I looked a while longer. Then I bowed my head to the sky — not the machine, not the Conclave, not the office that had eaten my life. To the stars. To the silent god half-restored and the loud broken man walking to judgment in its name's aftermath.

"Eleven valleys," I said softly, to no one, to everyone, to whatever keeps accounts when machines and empires don't. "I remember all their names. I made sure of it. Let that count for whatever it counts for."

The stars said nothing. Silent god, silent sky. But for the first time in thirty years, the silence didn't feel like absence.

It felt like being *heard*.

* * *

They held me overnight in a cell I had commissioned myself — good stone, honest locks; I had always insisted my prisoners be housed decently, a hypocrisy I catalogued among my many others. Through the barred window, high on the wall, a sliver of sky. Stars in it.

Near dawn, boots in the corridor. Not my escort to judgment — too early, too many, moving wrong. I sat up on the cot as the lock turned.

The door opened on a young hunter in Conclave livery I didn't recognize, flanked by two more, and behind them, framed in torchlight, a face I had hoped never to see again because seeing it meant everything I'd done tonight might come undone.

High Confessor Alderyn herself. White robes. Kind eyes. The gentlest monster I had ever served beside.

"Inquisitor Corvane," she said warmly. "You've been busy. Fortunately, the Conclave anticipated... complications. Stand down your cell guard, children. The Lord Inquisitor and I have much to discuss — and I'm afraid the purge is already underway."
