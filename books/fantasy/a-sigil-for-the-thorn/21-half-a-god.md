## 21. Half a God

The first thing I learned about peace was that it itched.

Three weeks after we burned our way into the imperial capital and walked back out again — most of us, anyway, and Ilsa carried on Odile's shoulders like the world's most profane war trophy — Thornvale had settled into a rhythm that felt suspiciously like happiness. And I didn't trust it. Fifteen years of serving a silent god had taught me to be suspicious of anything quiet. Silence used to mean absence. Now it meant something listening.

I stood at the edge of the valley in the grey-gold light of early autumn, watching Odile's crew work on the new wardstones.

The old wards had been a wall. That was the thing nobody had told me my whole life — not the garrison, not Ilsa, not the god sleeping in my blood. The wards hadn't been built to keep magic *out*. They'd been built to keep *me* hidden. A cage dressed up as a shield, and every soul in Thornvale had lived inside it without knowing they were the bars.

The new ones were different. Fen had drawn the design — the kid had a surprising talent for sigilwork once you got past the fact that their handwriting looked like a spider drowning in ink. Instead of a sealed ring, the new stones formed an open lattice, a web of faint silver light that let magic move through the valley like weather instead of locking it out like a siege. Vethra's half-spark hummed under my sternum whenever I walked the line of them, a low warm pulse, like a cat settling against your ribs.

"Marguerite!" Odile waved from the far stone, her voice carrying across the field the way it always did, loud enough to startle crows three counties over. "Your turn to hold the anchor!"

I held up a hand to show I'd heard and trudged out through the wet grass. Holding the anchor meant standing at the master stone with your palm flat against it while the lattice drank a thread of whatever the spark offered and spun it through the web. It didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. For fifteen years the only thing my blood had ever given anyone was silence, and now it gave light, freely, daily, and the giving cost nothing but warmth.

"Any dizziness?" Odile asked as I pressed my hand to the stone. She'd appointed herself my personal physician after the battle, mostly because Bram was still horizontal and someone had to nag.

"A little," I admitted. "Like standing up too fast."

"That's normal." She said it with such confidence that I almost believed she knew what she was talking about. Odile had never treated anything more serious than a hangover before this year. "Bram says the same."

"How is he?"

She hesitated, which told me more than the answer would have. "Better. He ate half a loaf this morning and complained about the crust. That's practically a full recovery, by Bram standards."

I smiled, and the stone drank its thread of light, and the web flared silver down the whole length of the valley — visible now, honest, beautiful, nothing hidden. Farmers in the fields below paused to watch it. Some of them still crossed themselves when it did that. Old habits. You don't spend a lifetime being told the border is sacred and then shrug when it starts glowing.

*You are thinking about the machine,* said the voice in my blood.

Vethra spoke more often now. Not words, exactly — more like weather fronts moving through my mind, impressions with edges sharp enough to cut. But since the splitting, since I carved the sigil into my own chest and poured half of myself — half of *it*, half of *us* — into that starving iron furnace in the capital, the fragments had been knitting themselves into sentences.

"I'm always thinking about the machine," I muttered.

*It dreams,* the spark said, which was not an answer to anything and also somehow answered everything. *It dreams of stars.*

I pulled my hand off the stone and shook out my fingers. "Later," I told it. "We're busy."

Odile raised an eyebrow. "Talking to yourself again?"

"Talking to my god, thank you very much. There's a difference. Slightly."

"Uh-huh." She clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to rattle teeth. "Go see Bram. He's been asking for you all morning and pretending he hasn't."

* *

Bram had been installed in Ilsa's old quarters because it had the biggest windows, and he hated it, and complained about it, and would have fought anyone who suggested moving him somewhere smaller.

He lay propped against about nine pillows, one arm splinted and bound across his chest where the hunter's blade had gone through shielding Fen, his big grey head bare for once — no cook's kerchief, which somehow made him look naked — and a book open on his lap that he was very obviously not reading.

"You look terrible," I said, from the doorway.

"And you look like a man who's been talked at by a stone," he rasped. His voice still hadn't fully come back; the wound had been deep enough to touch something important in the throat. He said the healers thought it would mend. He said it the way people say things they need other people to believe so they can believe them too. "Come in or stand there judging me, but pick one. The suspense is ruining my convalescence."

I came in and sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the splinted arm. Up close he smelled like himself still — woodsmoke and rosemary and thirty years of garrison kitchens — which undid me a little, the way small familiar things do when everything large has been unfamiliar for months.

"Fen says you're teaching them bread."

"Fen says a lot of things." But his eyes crinkled. "Kid's got hands for dough. Better than they've got hands for lying to scouts, apparently, though I hear that worked out too." He shifted, winced, kept going the way Bram always did, barreling through pain like it was a slow line at market. "Ilsa came by."

"I know. I saw her leave."

"She told you, then?"

"She told me she's stepping down. Effective when she can walk unassisted, which knowing Ilsa means sometime tomorrow out of sheer spite." I picked at a loose thread on the blanket rather than look at him. "She wants Odile to take the garrison. Odile wants Fen trained up first. Fen wants everyone to stop deciding things about them without asking. Which, frankly, fair."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"Marguerite." He said my name the way he said it over supper, that patient gruffness that could make even a name sound like a meal being set in front of you. "Everyone in this valley knows you're leaving. The kid knows. The horses probably know. Only person pretending not to notice is you, and you've never been good at pretending, love. You once tried to bluff Odile at cards and sweated through your shirt."

I opened my mouth to object to *love* — Bram called everyone love, it meant nothing, it meant everything — and closed it again.

"The spark's half mine," I said finally. "And half of it went into that machine to buy the empire time. Corvane's letter came yesterday. Did you read it?"

"I read it. Read it twice."

"Then you know the machine *accepted* it. It hasn't accepted anything in two hundred years. It whispered *gratitude* to a witch-hunter and broke him like kindling. Whatever we fed it, it wasn't just fuel. It was a seed. And seeds grow, Bram. And when it grows—" I made myself look at him. "There are other silent gods. Vethra remembers them. Sleeping under mountains, drowned in lakes, scattered when the empire started feeding its furnace. If any of them have vessels left, if any of them have sparks left, then—"

"Then somebody has to go find out whether the next war gets born or gets prevented." He nodded slowly. "And that somebody has a god living in their chest and a bounty hunter turned sweetheart who knows every hunter road over those mountains."

"They're not my sweetheart."

"No?" Bram grinned, and it split his cracked lips, and he didn't care. "Funny. Because I heard you two last night on the wall, arguing about route maps, and there's a particular tone you get, Quintana, when you're arguing with someone you intend to follow anywhere. Used to have it about Ilsa. Now you've got it worse."

"Bram—"

"Heard you say 'we'll take the southern pass' and 'we'll' this and 'we'll' that, and I've known you twenty years and you have never once said 'we'll' about a patrol unless you trusted the person next to you with your whole miserable life." He reached over with his good hand and gripped my wrist, calluses against scars. "So stop coming in here to ask permission by way of goodbye. Nobody here is letting you go alone, and nobody here is making you stay. Both of those can be true. That's what family is, kid. It's the only thing I ever cooked that nobody taught me."

My throat did something embarrassing. I blamed the smoke damage from the capital.

"Eat your soup," I managed.

"There's no soup."

"There will be by the time I'm gone."

* *

Sable found me at dusk on the wall, which was where I spent most evenings now, watching the new lattice glow and trying to imagine what travelers would think when they crossed into Thornvale and saw a valley lit from within like a lamp.

They came up the ladder quietly — Sable moved quietly the way some people breathed, without deciding to — and leaned beside me on the parapet, close enough that our shoulders touched. After the shrine, after the pass, after everything, we had developed this language of proximity. Shoulder contact meant *I'm here*. Hand contact meant *I'm yours*. Anything further was still being negotiated, mostly by blushing, which neither of us would ever admit to.

"Route maps," I said.

"Route maps," Sable agreed solemnly. "The southern pass is wrong, by the way. Corvane's outriders will sweep it come spring regardless of the truce. Truces end. Outriders don't read letters."

"So the northern crossing."

"Through the old shrine road. It adds four days." They glanced sideways at me. "Four days near the summit shrine. Where you communed with a dying god and came back with your eyes doing *that*."

"Doing what?"

"Glowing. Faintly. When you're happy." They said it flatly, deadpan, but the corner of their mouth betrayed them. "It's very inconvenient for covert travel. You'll need a hood."

"I do not glow."

"Sable's First Rule of Hunting: deny the glow, admit the glow, become the glow."

"That is not a real rule."

"It's rule seven." Their hand found mine on the parapet, deliberate this time, fingers lacing through. Below us the lattice pulsed its slow silver heartbeat, and the valley exhaled toward evening — cook-smoke from the garrison kitchens where Fen was attempting Bram's recipes and producing, by all accounts, things that were edible-adjacent, and lamplight kindling window by window in the town, and somewhere a fiddle starting up badly, which meant Odile was drunk already or celebrating, which with Odile were the same thing.

For a while neither of us spoke. With Sable, silence had stopped being a thing to fill. It had become a place to sit.

"Ilsa asked me today," I said eventually, "if I was afraid."

"Were you?"

"I said no. Then I lied to a captain, which felt strange after twenty years." I turned our joined hands over, studying Sable's knuckles, the old burn scar across the palm they'd never explained and I'd stopped needing explained. "Here's what I actually am. I'm not afraid of the mountains, or the hunters, or even the machine dreaming its star-dreams in the dark. I'm afraid this ends. This exact thing. Standing on a wall holding your hand. Because everything I've ever loved either got taken or got spent, and I spent half my own god buying time for people who'll never know my name, and I don't regret it, and that's the terrifying part — I'd do it again — and someday the ledger comes due, doesn't it? Someday the empire remembers what we took from it."

Sable was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had the roughness it got when they weren't performing steadiness.

"When I was eleven years old," they said, "the hunters took me out of a valley very much like this one. I spent nineteen years learning that loving a thing was how you marked it for burning. So I stopped. Loved nothing. Trusted nothing. Hunted children like the child I'd been, and called it duty." Their grip tightened on my hand. "Then I walked through a gate under a parley banner, sensed a spark in a mercenary's blood, and for the first time in my life lied to my order. And it didn't end the world. It ended *my* world and started a better one. So no, Marguerite. I don't believe the ledger comes due. I believe ledgers are what frightened people write so they have permission to expect the worst." They turned to face me, and in the lattice-light their eyes were steady and dark and absolutely certain. "Choose the worst if you want. I've lived there. It's overrated. Or choose this."

"This," I said. "Definitely this."

"Yes?"

"*Yes.* Openly. Loudly. In front of the entire garrison, if necessary, though I'd rather not, because Odile will compose a ballad and Fen will draw pictures."

Sable laughed — actually laughed, that rare unguarded sound I'd only heard maybe five times, each one hoarded like treasure — and then kissed me, right there on the wall, in front of the glowing wards and the bad fiddle and the whole unhiding valley, and I glowed, faintly, inconveniently, and did not care one bit.

Below us, predictably, Odile began composing a ballad.

* *

We left at the end of the month, when Bram could sit a horse in a sling and insisted on riding to the gate anyway, and Ilsa stood straight-backed in the yard she'd commanded for thirty years and saluted us out like soldiers instead of family, which from Ilsa was the tenderest thing she knew how to do.

Fen hugged me hard enough to crack ribs and pressed a paper into my hand — a map, beautifully drawn despite the drowning-spider handwriting, of every shrine-road over the northern passes, annotated in their careful scrawl: *places the hunters don't watch. places with old gods maybe. come home.*

Home. The word sat in my chest next to half a god and warmed everything around it.

We rode north as the lattice flared silver behind us, the whole valley lighting our road out like hands waving, and ahead the mountains waited with their buried sleepers and their unanswered questions.

And far to the south, past the passes, in the iron dark beneath the capital, something ancient dreamed of stars — and in its dreaming, I felt it turn, faintly, curiously, toward *us*.

Sable rode close, knee brushing mine.

"We'll meet it together," they said.

"Yeah," I said. "We will."
