## 22. The Road Over the Mountains

The morning we left, Bram tried to send us off with a breakfast that could have provisioned a small army, and I let him.

"You'll want the hardtack for the pass," he said, pressing a wrapped parcel into my arms with the tenderness of a man handing over his firstborn. "And this is for after the pass. And this one's for when you're sad. It's honey cake. Sadness responds well to honey cake."

"Bram," I said, "we're crossing mountains, not colonizing them."

"Mountains are exactly the kind of place a person underestimates." He jabbed a flour-dusted finger at me. "I've been cooking for soldiers thirty years. Soldiers always underestimate two things: mountains and feelings."

"He's not wrong," Sable said beside me, already shouldering their pack with the easy economy of someone who had crossed worse country in worse weather with worse company. They'd taken to garrison rations like a heretic takes to scripture — hungrily, and with a faint air of disbelief that it was allowed. "The first time I crossed the Teeth, I ate my boot leather on day four. The second time I packed better."

"The second time you were hunting people," I said.

"Which is why I packed *well*. Guilt makes you efficient."

Bram laughed, then caught himself, because it was still early enough in the day for laughing to feel like something that needed permission. Six weeks since the furnace city. Four since we'd carried Ilsa home through the rebuilt gate, thinner and grayer and grinning like a woman who'd argued with an empire and gotten the last word. Some mornings the whole valley still woke up touching its own ribs, checking the wound was real and closed.

"Come here," Bram said, and hugged me before I could invent a reason not to. He smelled of woodsmoke and yeast and thirty years of other people's suppers. "You come back," he said into my hair. "Not someday. You come back."

"That's the plan."

"Plans are what people make when they're scared to say promises." He pulled back and looked at me with those red-rimmed cook's eyes that missed nothing. "Promise."

I looked past him at the valley — the terraced orchards going gold, the river running bright and ordinary, the new wards humming along the ridgeline like a held breath that had finally learned how to exhale — and I said, "I promise," and found, to my considerable irritation, that I meant it.

* * *

The old wards had hidden. The new ones, if you could call them wards — Odile insisted they were more of a *statement* — did the opposite. Vethra's half-spark, poured thin through sigils carved into standing stones at every approach, made Thornvale legible from miles away. Any witch-hunter with the sense to look would see a valley glowing softly with a god's presence, guarded by a company of mercenaries who had stormed a machine-city and lived.

It was Ilsa's idea. "Let them see us coming," she'd said, the first morning she was well enough to sit in council and be insufferable about it. "An empire fears what it cannot find. But it *respects* what it can't afford." She'd tapped the map where the capital sat, fat and far away. "We are no longer cheap to take. That's the only ward that's ever worked."

So Thornvale stood open now, and strange things came through the gate because of it. Pilgrims, mostly — thin, wary folk who'd heard rumors of a valley where the silent god still breathed, carrying offerings of bread and buttons and, in one memorable case, a live goose that Fen had adopted within the hour and named Sergeant. A few deserters from the hunter orders, who Sable interviewed alone in the guardhouse and vouched for or didn't. And once, a woman who claimed to hear another silent god sleeping under the eastern fens, which was the rumor that had set our feet on this road in the first place.

Odile found us at the gate, as arranged, ostensibly to inspect our gear and actually to delay us.

"Your rope's rotten," she said, tugging the coil off Sable's pack and replacing it with her own without asking. "Your flint's cracked. You've packed salt but no fishhooks, which is a crime against intelligence. And Marguerite—" she turned on me, scout's squint fully deployed, "—you've got your knife on the wrong hip."

"It's always been on that hip."

"And look how you turned out." She thrust a bundle at my chest. Fishhooks, line, dried apples, and a letter sealed with wax that smelled of pine. "For a man in Highmoor named Talloway. He owes me money and an apology, in whichever order survival permits. If he gives you trouble, tell him Odile knows about the goat."

"I don't want to know about the goat."

"No one does. That's what makes it leverage." She hugged me then, quick and fierce, the way she did everything — as though affection were a scouting maneuver best completed before the enemy noticed. When she pulled back her eyes were wet and furious about it. "Sixty leagues to the fens. Then whatever's east of the fens. Send word by bird if there are birds. Send word by rumor if there aren't. Just *send word*, or I will come find you, and I will be insufferable, and you will deserve it."

"Yes, Captain Scout."

"Not captain. Not yet." She glanced toward the guardhouse, where Ilsa stood watching us with her arms folded and her weight off her bad leg. "Give it a year. She says I rush."

"Do you?"

"*Constantly.*" She grinned, wiped her face with her sleeve like a child, and shoved us both toward the road. "Go. Before I say something sincere."

Too late, Odie. Too late by about six weeks.

* * *

Fen ran the last quarter mile to catch up, which told me everything, because Fen had been assigned to the wall that morning and had clearly traded shifts the moment my back was turned.

"I'm not coming," Fen announced, falling into step, breathless and defiant about a thing nobody had suggested.

"We know," I said.

"I *could* come."

"You could," Sable agreed mildly. "And then who teaches Sergeant the goose to stand sentry? That animal has potential."

Fen scowled, sixteen going on forty, all elbows and conviction. "I just wanted to walk you to the boundary stone. Is that a crime?"

"It's a kindness," I said. "Those are rarer. Walk on."

We walked. The road climbed gently out of the valley floor, past the orchards where pickers straightened to watch us pass, past children who had stopped being afraid of Sable's hunter grays sometime in the third week and now simply demanded tricks, because Sable could palm a coin like a con artist and denied it poorly. One of them — little Maren from the mill, missing teeth, fearless — detached from her gang long enough to press something into my hand and flee.

A river stone, painted with the thorn-and-circle. Vethra's sigil, done in childish blue, slightly wrong in the way that meant it had been copied from watching me carve it rather than from any book.

"She asked me what it meant," Fen said, embarrassed on behalf of the entire valley. "I told her it means somebody's listening even when nobody's talking."

I closed my hand around the stone. Under my skin, the half-spark warmed — it did that lately, whenever anything true happened nearby, like a dog hearing its name — and for a moment I felt the faint pressure at the edge of perception that had become my constant companion: Vethra, or what remained of Vethra, leaning close to the walls of me and listening too.

*Somebody,* the god murmured, in that voice like wind through a burned forest. *Always somebody.*

Fifteen years of silence, and now I couldn't shut the door all the way. I wouldn't have, even if I could. But there were days I missed the quiet the way you miss a childhood house — knowing full well it was small and drafty and that you outgrew it.

At the boundary stone — the same squat granite marker where, a lifetime ago, the wards had shattered and the stars had gone out — Fen stopped and squared their shoulders like a soldier at review.

"When you find the others," Fen said, "the sleeping gods. What happens if they wake up angry?"

Nobody had asked me that plainly yet. Ilsa hadn't, because Ilsa already knew the answer and hated it. Corvane hadn't, because Corvane had stopped believing questions deserved answers somewhere around the time he started feeding people to a furnace.

"They might," I said. "Angry seems reasonable, honestly. We'd be angry, if we'd watched everyone we love get murdered for centuries and slept through it." I turned the painted stone over in my fingers. "But anger isn't the whole of anybody. Not even a god's. Vethra chose to hide instead of fight. Chose a baby in a valley instead of a war. Whatever wakes up out there, it woke up *wanting* something besides revenge once, and I'm betting that part's still in there."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll have very good company being wrong," I said, nodding at Sable. "They're wrong professionally."

Sable, to their credit, saluted.

Fen laughed, and then cried a little, and pretended not to, and I hugged them hard enough to creak ribs and whispered the thing I'd been saving: "You held the gate. There were grown killers on either side of you and you held it. Whatever comes next for you, remember you've already been brave once when it counted. Everything after is just repetition."

Then I let go before either of us could make it worse, and Fen stood at the stone getting smaller as we climbed, until the road bent and took them away.

* * *

We camped that first night on the shoulder of the world, above the tree line, where the Teeth showed us their teeth. Sable built a fire with the competence of long habit and I fed it with a fingertip, a habit of my own — the spark leaping gladly from my skin into the wood, warm as a cat settling in.

"You don't have to do that anymore," Sable said. "Flint works fine."

"So does flint. This is faster." I sat back against my pack and looked at the sky. The stars were all there — had been since the night I split the spark, since the machine drank its honest half and hummed gratitude into Corvane's skull, since the whole broken sky lit back up like a lamp relit in a window. But I checked anyway, most nights. Old habits. New heavens.

Sable settled beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was still new enough to feel deliberate every single time. Six weeks of sharing a bedroll and I still noticed. I intended to keep noticing.

"Tell me what you felt today," they said. It was becoming our evening liturgy. Not prayers — neither of us trusted prayer much — but inventory. What the spark did. What the silence did. What we each did about either.

"Warmth when Fen hugged me. Pressure when Maren gave me the stone. And just now—" I nodded at the fire, at the dark beyond it. "Something east of here. Faint. Like hearing a conversation through three walls. Could be the fens god. Could be wind. Could be me wanting it so badly I'm inventing it."

"Could be," Sable agreed. "Wanting things badly is how I found every vessel I ever tracked. It's a good compass, if you aim it carefully." They were quiet a moment. "My order taught us that wanting was corruption. Desire was the door magic walked through. So we amputated it, piece by piece, and called the stump holiness." Their hand found mine in the dark. "Took me twenty years and one stubborn mercenary to learn the amputation was the corruption."

"Wise words from the person who ate their boot."

"I contain multitudes and poor planning."

I laughed, and the spark flared gold in the fire, and far to the east — so faint I might have dreamed it, so real that Sable went still beside me — something answered. Not a voice. A *turning-over*, like a sleeper shifting toward dawn. A god, or the grave-shaped absence where one had been, registering that someone was walking toward it with a lantern made of half a heart.

Sable's grip tightened. "You felt that."

"Yeah."

"Good feeling or bad feeling?"

"Honestly?" I watched the sparks climb. "Like knocking on a door and hearing footsteps."

Neither of us said the next part, because we'd already said it in the council tent and on the road and once, memorably, in the middle of the night sitting bolt upright at the same moment: that footsteps could mean welcome or they could mean war. That the empire had murdered every vessel for three hundred years and called it stewardship, and the gods had watched, and sleeping things remember. That we might spend the rest of our lives apologizing for crimes we didn't commit, to powers we couldn't comprehend, armed with nothing but a painted stone and some honey cake.

That we were going anyway.

* * *

On the third morning, the road crested a ridge and gave us Thornvale one last time — the whole valley laid out below like a secret finally told, warded in light instead of shadow, smoke rising from Bram's chimney in a column that managed to look defiant. Tiny figures moved along the terraces. Someone, and I'd have staked my knife it was Odile, was standing at the gate with a spyglass, watching us watch them.

I raised a hand. After a moment, so did the tiny figure.

"They'll be fine," Sable said.

"They'll be better than fine. They'll be *insufferable*. 'Oh, you survived the fens, did you? Did you check your rope knots?'" I slung my pack higher. "Let's go before I talk myself into staying."

"You won't. You promised Bram."

"Promises are what brave people make when they're scared," I said, and stepped onto the road east, and the spark under my skin leaned toward the horizon like a hound scenting rain.

Behind us the valley held its light. Ahead, past the passes and the fens and whatever waited there — sleeping gods, waking wars, an empire learning to be afraid of mercy — the road went on, worn smooth by feet that had never once imagined carrying what I carried.

Let the war come, then, if it must. Let it find us mid-stride, mid-meal, mid-argument about fishhooks, with honey cake in our packs and a god's heartbeat under my ribs and Sable's hand in mine.

We would meet it together.

We were already meeting it together.

The stars stayed lit the whole way down.
