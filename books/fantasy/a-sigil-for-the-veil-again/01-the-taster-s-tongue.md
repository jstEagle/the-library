## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The bell rang at half past the fifth hour, as it always did, and Astrid Ishikawa was already awake, already dressed, already standing in the cold stone corridor that connected the servants' wing to the royal kitchens. They had learned long ago that being early was a kind of armor. The taster who arrived late was watched. The taster who arrived early was furniture.

Furniture was safe.

The kitchens of Vell's palace were already roaring when Astrid slipped through the door — forty fires lit, sixty cooks sweating over copper pots, the air so thick with steam and rendered fat and roasting chestnuts that it lay on the tongue like a second breakfast. Nobody looked up. That was the point. In ten years, Astrid had perfected the art of walking through this room without disturbing a single particle of its attention. Head down, shoulders soft, hands folded like a servant carrying nothing worth stealing. A ghost with a payroll.

*Good morning, Ishikawa,* said no one, because no one said anything to the taster before the queen had eaten. It was not rudeness, exactly. It was superstition wearing politeness's clothes. To speak to the taster was to think about poison, and to think about poison was to invite it, and in Vell you did not invite what you could not afford.

Astrid took their station at the long oak table nearest the servery door, the one with the worn depression in its surface where ten years of trays had rested. The under-cook, a broad woman named Perrin whose forearms were scarred from oven racks, set down the first tray without a word: warm bread from the morning bake, butter whipped with honey, a compote of preserved plums, tea of dried blackberry leaf, and a small dish of clotted cream that smelled faintly of the cowshed, which meant it was fresh, which meant it was honest.

Astrid tasted everything. Not nibbled — *tasted*, properly, letting each element sit against the tongue long enough to make acquaintance with it. Bread first, the crust and then the crumb, pressing a piece against the roof of the mouth where the bitter almonds of cyanide announced themselves earliest. Then the butter, which today was clean, sweet, unremarkable. The plums, which carried the particular sharpness of fruit preserved in a bad year, but no metallic edge, no numbness blooming at the back of the throat. The tea they held longest, swishing it against the gums, because the palace's enemies had twice tried to deliver poison through an infusion — once arsenic, once something stranger, a toxin that had made Astrid's fingertips go cold for an hour while their heart raced like a rabbit's. They remembered that one fondly. It had been educational.

Nothing today. Nothing ever, lately. Three months since the last attempt, and even that had been amateurish — powdered foxglove in the cream, dosed for a grown man, which would have given the queen a sickening afternoon and Astrid a racing pulse and a headache behind the left eye. Astrid had reported it anyway, calmly, precisely, describing the taste the way a jeweler describes a flaw, and the steward had thanked them with a nod and a raise of two coppers a month, and the cook responsible had been dismissed into the snow, and the whole thing had passed through the kitchens like weather.

That was the work. That was all the work was. Ten years of mornings, each one identical, each one ending with Astrid raising a small white flag of a napkin and saying, in a voice trained to be neither eager nor reluctant: *The tray is clean.*

And then the queen ate, and Astrid went back to their narrow room above the coal store, and the day folded itself away like linen.

It should have been a lonely life. By every measure Astrid understood, it *was* a lonely life. They had no family — a dead mother in some border village, a father never spoken of, a surname kept out of stubbornness rather than love. They had no friends, because friends asked questions, and questions were holes in a wall, and walls were the only thing keeping Astrid alive. What they had instead was routine, and routine, Astrid had come to believe, was a form of love. The bell at half past five. The tray. The tongue. The flag. The walk home past the chandlery and the fountain with the cracked saint. The same soup, the same candle guttering at the same hour. If you built your days strong enough, nothing could get in.

Nothing except, of course, the things already inside.

Astrid's tongue was famous, or would have been if anyone could talk about it. It was not merely that they survived the poisons — plenty of tasters survived by spitting fast and praying faster. It was that Astrid *knew* them. Arsenic tasted of garlic and regret. Belladonna was sweet, treacherously so, like a lover who flatters. Hemlock had a greenness to it, the flavor of a stem snapped in spring. Antimony sat on the tongue like a coin left too long in a pocket. There were thirty-one poisons Astrid could name blindfolded, and eleven more they knew only by symptom, and four they suspected existed but had never met, and those four lived in Astrid's head like monsters under a child's bed, named and unnamed at once.

They had built the tolerance deliberately, the way other people built muscle. Small doses, carefully measured, taken alone in their room with a bucket by the bed and a written account of every symptom in a ledger disguised as a book of recipes. *Bitterness at the root of the tongue, hour one. Cold in the extremities, hour two. Do not sleep. Count breaths.* Ten years of that ledger. Ten years of swallowing small deaths so that the large ones would find nothing to kill.

The palace believed Astrid was simply lucky, or blessed, or constitutionally robust. The steward had once suggested, kindly, that Astrid must have been born with a strong stomach, some peasant inheritance from hardy border stock. Astrid had smiled the smile they used for such moments — warm, brief, entirely hollow — and agreed, and said nothing about the bucket, or the ledger, or the night three years ago when they had drunk a quarter-dose of aconite and lain on the cold floor watching their own heartbeat slow and deciding, with the strange clarity of the poisoned, that if they died, no one would know where the ledger was, and that thought had frightened them more than dying did.

Because the ledger was not really about poison. The ledger was proof. Proof that Astrid existed, even if no one else ever read it. A life built on being no one still needed, somewhere, a single page that said *I was here, and I felt this, and I counted my own breaths so someone would know I had any.*

* * *

The second tray came at the seventh hour, and with it came Tam.

Tam was twelve, maybe thirteen — scullery boys aged quickly here, steam and lye aging skin the way grief ages eyes — and he was the lowest of the low, the boy who hauled the slop buckets and scrubbed the hearthstones and got shouted at by everyone including, occasionally, the cats. He was also, in Astrid's private and never-spoken taxonomy, the only person in the palace who had ever been kind to them without wanting anything. Two winters ago, when Astrid had been ill — genuinely ill, a fever, not a poisoning — Tam had left a cup of broth outside their door with a note scratched on birchbark: *for the taster, get well.* No signature. No expectation. Just broth.

Astrid had never forgotten it. You do not forget the first kindness in ten years; it goes through you like a nail through wet wood, and stays.

So there was an arrangement, unwritten, unspoken, invisible to everyone, and it worked like this: when Tam brought a tray, Astrid tasted it, pronounced it clean, and then, in the act of handing back the empty tasting spoons, let something small fall from their sleeve into the boy's palm. Today it was a luck-charm. A little thing, no bigger than a thumbnail — a disc of pale river-clay, fired in Astrid's own hearth, stamped with a spiral pattern pressed there using the head of a pin, and threaded on a cord of waxed linen. It looked like nothing. It cost nothing. It was, according to every law of the Crown of Vell, contraband.

Magic was outlawed in Vell. Had been for a hundred years, since the Purging, when the old gods' names were scraped off the temples and the temples themselves were pulled down and their stones used to pave the streets, so that the city walked daily, unknowingly, upon the faces of its own forgotten protectors. Charms, sigils, blessings, prayers to anything but the Crown's sanctioned church — all of it forbidden, all of it punished. First offense, whipping. Second, the stocks. Third, burning, though the burnings were rare now, mostly because nobody was fool enough to be caught twice.

Nobody, that is, except people like Astrid, who were fool enough in a quieter way — fool enough to believe that a boy hauling slop buckets deserved one small thing in the world that was his, that worked, that was *his*.

The charms were harmless. That was the phrase Astrid used, to Odessa, to themselves, to the dark ceiling of their room at night: *harmless.* Luck-charms for gamblers and grandmothers. Sleep-knots for the insomnia of midwives. A charm against chilblains, because the winter was coming and the wash-women's hands split like overripe fruit. Little clay discs and knotted cords and bundles of dried herbs wrapped in cloth, none of them — Astrid had always believed — containing anything more potent than suggestion and the comfort of ritual. Placebos with pretty faces. The magic had been burned out of the world a century ago; whatever Astrid sold was just the shape of magic, the memory of it, kindness wearing magic's clothes.

At least, that was what Astrid had believed until very recently. But belief is a house you live in, and houses settle, and cracks appear, and lately — though Astrid could not have said when it started, could not have pointed to the morning — the charms had begun to feel different in the making. The clay hummed under the pin. The knots wanted to tie themselves in patterns Astrid had never taught them. When Astrid stamped the spiral, the line sometimes curved where the pin had gone straight, as if the clay remembered a older hand guiding it. Astrid had told themselves these were tiredness, imagination, the small madness of a solitary life. One does not look too closely at cracks in the floor of a house one cannot afford to leave.

So: the arrangement. Tam stood at the table's end, waiting for the tray to be pronounced clean, eyes down, ears red from the cold of the yard. Astrid tasted, nodded, spoke the words — *clean, take it up* — and then, in the pass-back of the spoons, slid the little clay disc along the wood, under the flat of their own palm, into the curl of the boy's fingers.

"Mind the cord," Astrid said, softly, the way one says anything deniable. "Wear it inside your shirt."

Tam's eyes came up, just briefly — quick, bright, terrified and delighted at once, the exact expression of a boy holding a stolen apple in a garden full of dogs. "For luck?" he whispered.

"For the hearth-work," Astrid said, which meant nothing and covered everything. "It likes ash."

And Tam grinned, and pocketed it, and hauled his tray away toward the queen's apartments, and Astrid washed the tasting spoons in water hot enough to scald, and stood afterward with their hands in the steam, thinking about nothing, which was the safest thing to think about, and therefore the hardest.

* * *

The walk home began at dusk, which in the tenth month came early, the light going thin and gray-gold over the rooftops by the fourth bell after noon. Astrid left the palace by the servants' gate, showed the gatekeeper a face he had seen ten thousand times and never once studied, and descended into the city proper — down, always down, because the palace sat on its hill like a rich man on a chair, and the rest of Vell spilled downhill from it in crooked tiers, street stacked on street, chimney stacked on chimney, until at the bottom the houses leaned together like drunks holding each other upright.

Astrid's route wound through the fish market, already shuttered, its stalls smelling of brine and old ice; past the chandlery where the wick-maker's apprentice was bringing in the drying racks; across the fountain square with the cracked saint, whose face had been defaced a century ago and whose remaining eye, worn smooth by a hundred years of touched-and-prayed-at, gleamed like a coin in the fading light. People still touched it. Officially, touching the saint was nothing — he wasn't a god, just a decoration, just stone. Unofficially, every washerwoman in the quarter touched that eye on her way past, quick, furtive, a thumb pressed flat for luck, for health, for the son at sea, and the stone stayed smooth because hunger outlasts law.

Astrid touched it too. Every evening, the same spot, the same pressure, the same half-second. They had never told anyone. It was the single most dangerous habit of their life, more dangerous than the ledger, more dangerous than the poisons, because it was done in public, in daylight, among witnesses — and yet so small, so habitual, so folded into the ordinary choreography of walking home that no one had ever marked it. A woman brushing past a statue. Who notices? Who *could* notice?

But Astrid noticed. Every evening, thumb to stone, Astrid felt the small transaction occur — not prayer exactly, Astrid did not pray, praying was for people who believed they'd be heard — but an acknowledgment. *You're still there. Under everything. I see you.* And the stone, being stone, said nothing, and Astrid, being careful, expected nothing, and between the two of them a century of silence stood like a wall both leaned against.

Tonight, though — tonight the wall felt thinner.

Astrid could not have explained it. The air was the same gray air. The light the same dying light. The fishwives the same fishwives, the wick-smell the same wick-smell. And yet somewhere between the chandlery and the fountain, a feeling rose in Astrid's chest like water rising in a flooded cellar — slow, silent, level, unstoppable — a feeling with no name except perhaps *attention*. As if the city itself had turned, very slightly, to look at them. As if something under the paving stones, under the cracked saint, under the palace hill, under a hundred years of silence, had opened one eye.

Astrid stopped in the middle of the square. Stood still. Listened — to what, they could not say. The wind in the eaves. The distant bells marking the hour. A dog barking three streets away. All normal. All wrong.

Then the feeling passed, the way a shadow passes over water, and the square was only a square again, and Astrid shook their head at themselves — tired, Ishikawa, you're tired, eat your soup — and walked on, and climbed the stairs to their room above the coal store, and lit the candle, and ate the soup, and opened the ledger, and wrote the day's entry in a hand so small it might have been printed by an insect:

*Tenth month, ninth day. Tray clean. No attempts. Gave T. a luck-charm, spiral-stamp, river-clay. Clay behaved strangely in the stamping — line curved left of the pin's path, third time this month. Note it. Watch it.*

*Watch it.*

Astrid blew out the candle and lay down in the dark and did not sleep, because sleep required believing tomorrow would be the same as today, and tonight, for the first time in ten years, Astrid Ishikawa was not sure.

Somewhere below, in the sleeping city, a beggar was dying. Astrid did not know him. Would not know him until tomorrow, or the day after, when the story reached Charm Row the way stories did — carried in baskets, in gossip, in the wet footprints of rumor. Did not know that the man was praying. Did not know to whom. Did not know that one of Astrid's little harmless charms — sold for two coppers to a fishwife with bad knees, a sleep-knot, nothing, a nothing, a kindness wearing magic's clothes —

— was about to answer.
