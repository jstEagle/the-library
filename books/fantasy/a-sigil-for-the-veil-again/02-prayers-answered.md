## 2. Prayers, Answered

Astrid Ishikawa woke before the bells, as always, and lay still in the dark of their narrow room above the tannery, listening to the city breathe.

Vell breathed badly. It was a wet, rattling breath, the breath of a city built on top of itself — on top of its own older cities, its own buried mistakes. The walls sweated in the night and dried by day, and somewhere beneath all the stone and mortar there was a sound Astrid had heard only once, years ago, lying drunk on a cellar floor after a friend's funeral: a low hum, like a held note that no throat had ever sung.

They had decided, that morning and every morning since, not to hear it again.

Routine was a kind of armor. Astrid dressed in the dark by touch — wool shirt, the grey apron with the burn-spots, the plain boots — and became, piece by piece, the person Vell expected them to see: a food-taster of middling rank, unremarkable face, unremarkable voice, a body that moved through the palace kitchens like water through cracks, noticed by no one. Ten years they had practiced this. Ten years of being no one so carefully that even Astrid sometimes had to stop and remember what was underneath.

There wasn't much underneath. That was the price, and they had paid it willingly.

* * *

The queen's breakfast arrived at the tasting table just past dawn: barley porridge with honey and crushed hazelnut, small beer, a pear cut into crescents, bread still warm enough to steam. Astrid stood over it in the cold stone antechamber off the royal kitchens and did the work their tongue had been trained for since childhood — the work, though the Crown would flay them for saying it, that their blood had been doing since before they could read.

Porridge first. A spoonful, held on the tongue, counted under the breath. Bitterness at the edges? No — honey, fat, the green ghost of the grain itself. Clean. Small beer next, then the pear, then the bread's soft heart. Astrid catalogued each taste the way a clerk catalogues coin: arsenic would whisper sweet-almond; hemlock spoke with a numbing frost; wolfsbane announced itself like a struck bell at the back of the throat. Nothing spoke this morning. The kitchen had outdone itself, and somewhere old Marta the undercook was probably preening about the hazelnuts.

Astrid ate half the pear, waited out the count, felt nothing climb up their spine, and signed the slate: **Fit.**

Then, because routine was armor but it was also a cage, they palmed the little bundle from their apron pocket and made their way through the steam and shouting of the kitchens toward the scullery yard.

Pip was there, as he always was, scrubbing the great cauldrons with sand until his knuckles were raw. Sixteen maybe, all elbows and chilblains, an orphan the kitchens had chewed up and mostly forgotten. He'd been coughing for a week. Astrid had watched him hunch over the wash-water yesterday and hide the blood in his sleeve, quick as any thief, and something in Astrid's chest had gone tight and unbearable.

"Scullery boy." They didn't look at him. You never looked. "You dropped this."

"I didn't drop anything," Pip said, bewildered, hands dripping.

"Then it's yours now." Astrid pressed it into his wet palm and walked on without breaking stride: a knotted thread charm, red wool, three knots, worth two coppers at most. A luck-knot. A nothing. A kindness wearing magic's clothes.

Except — and here was the thing Astrid did not let themselves think about, the thing they kept locked in the same cellar-room as the humming note under the city — except that when Astrid tied those knots, they *wished*. Properly wished, with the whole of them, some wordless pressure behind the breastbone, some leaning of the soul against the world. And sometimes — rarely, once in a hundred — the wish seemed to lean back.

The knots were harmless. Astrid told themselves this nightly, like a prayer of their own: sleep-knots and luck-threads and charms against chilblains, small mercies, nothing that could burn anyone. In a city where the old gods were outlawed and magic was a pyre waiting for kindling, harmlessness was survival. Harmlessness was the whole architecture of Astrid's life.

They went back to the tasting table. They washed the slate. They were no one again.

* * *

It happened that evening, and it happened in the worst possible place: the gutter.

Astrid had taken the long way home from the palace — down through Tanner's Walk, past the fish-market stinking of ice, along Charm Row where Odessa's shop glowed amber in the blue dusk and strays of every age clustered at her door like moths who'd found a lamp they trusted. Astrid meant only to pass. Meant only to leave a wrapped parcel of six sleep-knots on Odessa's step, as they'd done a hundred times, and vanish home to their narrow room and their careful, invisible life.

The beggar was slumped in the alcove of the shuttered candlemaker's shop, three doors down from Odessa's. Astrid had seen him before — a big man gone hollow, once a porter perhaps, his legs ruined, his cough the same wet terrible cough that Pip had been hiding in his sleeve. He was shivering so hard his teeth clicked. His eyes were fixed on nothing.

And he was praying.

Not the way folk prayed in Vell anymore — not the stiff, frightened formulas to the Crown's sanctioned god of Order, whose name was said aloud only in churches with guards at the doors. This was older. Rougher. The man's cracked lips moved around words that Astrid had never been taught and yet somehow knew, the way you know the shape of your own hand: names like riverbeds worn smooth, a god of thresholds and small mercies, a doorkeeper god, a god whose whole dominion was the moment between one place and another.

*Let me cross,* the beggar whispered. *Let me get warm. Let me get through. Door-Keeper, I have nothing to give you but the asking. Let me get through.*

He had a thread around his wrist. Red wool. Three knots.

Astrid's own knots. Sold — or given, more likely; Astrid gave away as many as they sold — through Odessa's hands, months ago, to a man who'd wanted luck finding work. Two coppers' worth of nothing.

The beggar prayed. And the knot answered.

There was no other way to describe it. The red thread *lit* — not burning, blazing, a light with weight to it, gold-white and warm as a hearth seen through a window on a freezing night. The light poured down the man's arm like water finding a channel. His shivering stopped. His breathing eased, deepened, steadied. The ruinous rattle in his chest went quiet, and his ruined legs — Astrid saw it plainly, saw the flesh fill and the color return — his legs straightened, flexed, drew up under him, and the man stood. Stood. Weeping, laughing, staring at his own hands in the glow of a two-copper charm that should have been nothing, that had always been nothing —

— that had never, not once in ten years, been nothing at all.

Astrid stood frozen in the mouth of the alley with the parcel of sleep-knots dangling forgotten from their fingers, and felt the truth arrive in their body before their mind: a warmth behind the breastbone, the exact leaning-pressure of every wish they had ever tied into wool and thread, all of it *real*, all of it *answered*, all of it sitting up inside them now like something waking after a decade of pretended sleep.

Every knot. Every charm. Every kindness wearing magic's clothes.

The beggar turned, glowing faintly, healed, alive, and looked directly at Astrid — looked *through* the carefully built nothing, the middling face, the invisible life — and said, in a voice gone clear as bell-bronze:

"It heard me. Through you."

Then the light faded, and he was only a man again, whole and astonished, stumbling off toward the tannery district on legs that worked, and Astrid was alone in the cold with a parcel of charms that suddenly weighed as much as a pyre.

Terrified. Exhilarated. Both at once, tangled so tightly they couldn't be told apart — the feeling of a lock opening in your own chest, and the knowledge of what happens in Vell to people whose chests open.

* * *

Astrid made it four steps toward Odessa's door before the bells began.

Not the hour-bells. Not the alarm. The *finding*-bells — the deep bronze triple-strike from the Citadel that Astrid had heard perhaps five times in ten years and had learned, each time, to be elsewhere for. The sound rolled across the rooftops and every window on Charm Row lit up with anxious faces, and shutters began to close like eyes, and somewhere down the street a child started crying.

A crier's voice rose near the market-cross, thin and official in the freezing air:

*"By decree of the Crown: a witch-finding is declared for the wards of Lower Vell, effective this night. Any soul practicing the forbidden arts, harboring charms, or trafficking with outlawed powers shall be brought before the witch-finder Halvard. Doors will be searched. Names will be taken. The faithful are commanded to report their neighbors."*

*Report your neighbors.* Astrid's hands had gone numb around the parcel. Down the row, Odessa's door opened and her broad silhouette filled it, and she looked straight down the street at Astrid — at the frozen figure, the telltale parcel, the face that was supposed to show nothing and was showing everything — and jerked her chin once: *inside, now.*

Astrid had spent ten years making sure no one in Vell knew what they were.

Tonight, one healed beggar knew. One glowing thread knew. One crier's bell had told the whole ward that knowing was dangerous.

And Odessa — Odessa was standing in her doorway with her arms crossed and her ledger of buyers surely burning a hole in her apron pocket, holding the door open, waiting, *waiting*, for Astrid Ishikawa to decide whether invisibility was still worth the price.

The bells rang again. Astrid ran for the light.
