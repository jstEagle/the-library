## 3. The Charm-Row Family

Odessa Vane kept her ledger the way other women kept their prayers: close to the heart, written small, and never spoken aloud.

It was a slim book, bound in cracked green leather that had once been someone's good coat, and it lived behind the loose brick beside her hearth, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and fire both. Every name in it had come to her frightened or broke or both, and every name in it she intended to die knowing—not because she was sentimental, though the neighborhood called her sentimental behind her back and sometimes to her face, but because a fence who forgot her buyers was a fence who deserved the rope.

She sat at her kitchen table with the ledger open before her, doing the night's arithmetic by tallow-light: three luck-knots from Astrid Ishikawa at two coppers apiece, one fever-charm at six, and a birthing-bell she'd sold to the midwife on Tallow Street for more than it was worth because the midwife's husband had lost his legs to the quarry and pride was a currency too. Odessa tallied it all in pencil so fine it was nearly a whisper. Profit, this month: almost nothing. Enough, though. Enough was a word she'd learned to live inside like a well-tailored coat.

The kettle muttered on the stove. Upstairs, four of her strays slept in the two rooms above the shop—Tam with his burned hands from the rope-works, the sisters Pell who'd run from a father whose fists had opinions, old Marta who wasn't old enough to be old but had been used up early by other people's kitchens. Odessa fed them all out of the same pot and charged none of them rent they couldn't pay, which was to say most of them paid nothing, which was to say Charm Row held together the way a net holds: not by any single thread, but by the stubborn crossing of many.

She heard the bells at half past eleven.

Not the hour-bells. Not the alarm from the harbor that meant fire or flood. This was a new sound—three bells, a silence, three bells again—and Odessa's hands went still over the ledger before her mind even caught up to what her body already knew. Three-and-silence. She'd heard it once before, twenty years ago, when the witch-finders came through the Weavers' Quarter and took a dyer's widow away in a cart with her mouth sewn shut for cursing a loom.

Three-and-silence. A witch-finding.

"Up," she said, already moving, taking the stairs two at a time. "All of you, up. Now."

* * *

Astrid Ishikawa woke in their narrow room above the chandlery with the taste of copper in their mouth and no memory of falling asleep.

That was wrong. Everything about waking was wrong tonight—the bells ringing their strange triplet, the candle they didn't remember lighting still guttering on the sill, and beneath it all a feeling like a second heartbeat, low and rhythmic, coming from somewhere under the floor. From somewhere under the *city*. Astrid pressed a palm flat to the boards and felt it there, faint as a pulse through wet cloth: a slow, patient thrumming, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

They had felt it first three nights ago, in the beggar's alley, when the dying man clutched Astrid's cheap tin medallion and prayed to a god with a name out of the oldest songs—and the medallion had answered. It had blazed blue-white and warm as a living thing, and the beggar's lungs had opened, and he had wept and lived, and Astrid had walked home shaking with terror and something worse than terror. Hope. They hadn't dared light a candle since. Their charms were real. Ten years of harmless little trinkets, of carefully dead magic, of being no one—and the gods had come back and made every knot and sigil in Astrid's stock a confession.

Now the bells were saying so to the whole city.

Astrid dressed in the dark, pulled on the gray coat that made them furniture, and did the thing they'd trained themselves to do for a decade: became unremarkable. Shoulders down. Face smooth. A tired worker going out into the cold because work doesn't wait. But their hands, buttoning the coat, would not stop trembling, and the thrumming under the floorboards followed them down the stairs and into the street like a dog that knew its name.

Charm Row was already waking badly. Shutters banging. Voices pitched low and fast. At the corner by the pie-shop, a boy was nailing up a notice by lantern-light, and Astrid read it upside down as they passed, keeping their gait bored:

BY ORDER OF THE CROWN. GATHERING OF THE FAITHFUL FORBIDDEN. POSSESSION OF CHARM, SIGIL, OR HEATHEN TOKEN IS EVIDENCE OF WITCHCRAFT. INFORMERS WILL BE PAID. — HALVARD, WITCH-FINDER.

"Informers will be paid." Astrid turned the words over as they walked, quick and cold. There were perhaps forty people in Vell who knew where Astrid's charms came from. Perhaps ten who knew it outright. And every one of them was poor, and hungry winter was coming early this year—the frost had bitten the orchards in the First Month, weeks before its season—and paid informers were how hungry winters ate people.

The thought arrived whole and terrible: *I could be no one, or I could warn them. I cannot be both.*

Astrid turned toward Charm Row and walked faster.

* * *

Odessa got the strays out the back while the front door was still holding.

She'd spent the last quarter-hour turning her shop inside out with practiced calm: charms into the flour bin, charms into the false bottom of the salt box, charms wrapped in rag and dropped into the rain barrel where the water would hide them from a torch. Tam carried the strongbox. The Pell sisters carried each other, white-faced, arms linked like girls skipping rope. Old Marta refused to go until Odessa put the ledger question to bed, and Odessa lied to her about it smoothly, the way you lie to children and the dying.

"Go through the tannery yard," Odessa told them, pressing coins into Tam's burned hand and ignoring his flinch. "Then the cellar under the chapel of Saint Ferren—Father Anselm owes me. Knock twice and say the bread's risen. Don't knock any other way."

"And you?" Tam said. His voice cracked in the middle. Sixteen years old and already he knew the shape of what happened to women who stayed behind.

"I'm right behind you," Odessa said, which was true, in the way that a promise to meet someone in the next life is true. "Go."

She watched them vanish into the dark between the tanning sheds, and then she went back inside, knelt at the hearth, and pulled loose the brick.

The ledger lay in its oilcloth, green leather worn soft as skin. Forty names, give or take. The midwife on Tallow Street. The ferryman who wore a storm-knot under his collar. The guard captain's wife, gods help her, who bought a fidelity charm for a husband who didn't deserve one. Astrid Ishikawa's name, entered the first week of their acquaintance in Odessa's smallest, most careful hand. Every name a person who had been afraid and had reached for a little hope, and hope was now, by royal decree, evidence.

Boots in the street outside. Many boots. The front door shuddered under a shoulder.

Odessa did not hesitate, because hesitation was a luxury of people with futures. She fed the ledger to the coals.

It caught slowly at first, the old leather curling and blackening, and she fed it page by page, tearing them free so the fire could get at the ink underneath, watching names become smoke. The midwife. The ferryman. The captain's wife. Astrid. Her own name at the top of the first page, written twenty years ago by a girl who'd thought fencing lucky ribbons was a lark. The fire took it all without ceremony, because fire is the only honest priest left in Vell.

The door burst open as the last page curled.

There were six of them, gray-cloaked, and in front of them a man Odessa knew by reputation the way you know a disease: Halvard, the Crown's witch-finder. He was younger than his reputation—a lean, ordinary face, the kind of face that could stand at a market stall unnoticed—and his eyes moved across her kitchen with a terrible patience. They stopped at the hearth.

"Burning correspondence," he said. Not a question.

"Household accounts," Odessa said. "You're welcome to the ashes."

He crossed the room in four steps and crouched at the grate, and for a moment Odessa thought—hoped—he would find nothing, because paper burns thoroughly and arithmetic leaves no residue. But his gloved hand hovered over the embers, and he tilted his head, listening to something Odessa couldn't hear, and his expression changed. Not triumph. Something sadder and far more dangerous: recognition.

"There's ash here that smells of the old rites," he said quietly. "Marjoram and iron and grave-dirt. Someone has been burning charms in this house within the hour."

"My grandmother's recipe for keeping moths out."

Halvard looked up at her then, fully, and Odessa saw that his eyes were the eyes of a man standing on a bridge between two countries, deciding which one to burn. "Madam Vane," he said. "Your neighbors speak highly of you. Your ledgers, I'm told, are famous on this street. Everyone you've ever sold to, everyone you've ever sheltered—one honest list, and you sleep in your own bed tonight."

"The chimney draws badly," Odessa said. "Half this street's ledgers have gone up my flue."

Something flickered behind his eyes—frustration, or respect, or grief; with men like him the three wore the same face. He rose. "Search the house," he said to the others. "Take her."

They found the rain-barrel charms in less than a minute. Of course they did; Odessa had known they would when she dropped them in. That was the arithmetic she'd done at the table while the kettle muttered: the strays needed the search slowed, needed every gray cloak in this kitchen counting tin knots instead of chasing footsteps toward the tannery yard. Her freedom had been spent buying theirs. It was, she reflected as they cinched her wrists with cord, the best bargain she'd ever struck, and she'd struck it with herself.

"Well?" said one of the hunters, hauling the dripping charms onto the floor between them.

Halvard crouched and picked one up—a simple luck-knot, red thread, the kind Astrid sold for two coppers. He held it a long moment. Then, so softly Odessa nearly missed it, he said a word. One word, in a language the Crown had outlawed before Odessa's mother was born, a word shaped like an opening door.

The knot glowed. Briefly, gently, blue-white as a struck match held to the dark, and then it died—but the look on Halvard's face did not. He stared at his own palm as if it belonged to someone else.

"They're lighting up," whispered one of his hunters, delighted and horrified. "Sir, they're *all* lighting up—" And indeed the charms on the floor had begun to shimmer, faintly, one after another, like a street of windows going warm at dusk, and beneath the floorboards Odessa felt it too now, unmistakable: that slow, vast pulse, rising.

"Bag them," Halvard said hoarsely. "Bag all of it. And send word to the palace." He looked at Odessa, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, private as a prayer. "Where do these come from?"

"You tell me," Odessa said. "They seem to know your hand better than mine."

* * *

Astrid reached the mouth of Charm Row as they brought Odessa out.

Two hunters flanked her, cords at her wrists, and she walked between them the way she did everything—unhurried, chin level, a woman being escorted rather than taken. Her eyes swept the crowded doorways, the pale faces in the windows, and when they crossed Astrid's hiding place in the shadow of the pie-shop awning, she did not look away and she did not look *at* them. She simply let her gaze pass through, the way you greet someone across a crowded church: seen, acknowledged, protected by the pretense of not seeing.

But her lips moved, just slightly, around words pitched for no ears but the wind's—and Astrid, who had spent ten years learning to read mouths across crowded rooms, caught it:

*Tannery. Chapel. Twice. Bread.*

Then Odessa Vane was bundled into the cart with the witch-finder's gray cloaks, and the cart rolled north toward the palace, and the thrumming beneath the streets grew louder, and Astrid stood alone in the freezing dark with a message, a family to gather, and—for the first time in ten years—no idea how to be no one.
