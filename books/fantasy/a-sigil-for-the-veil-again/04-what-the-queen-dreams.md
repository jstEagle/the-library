## 4. What the Queen Dreams

The queen of Vell had learned to sleep in pieces.

It was a skill, like any other. She had been taught it young, by a governess with cold hands and colder doctrine: *a queen who sleeps deeply is a queen who can be surprised.* So Serevanne slept in fragments—twenty minutes here, an hour there—waking always to the sound of her own breathing, cataloguing the room before her eyes opened. The weight of the canopy. The tick of the clock in the antechamber. The guard's boots passing the door on the quarter-hour, unhurried, which meant all was well.

For thirty years this had served her. For thirty years she had woken from her shallow sleeps into a world that was exactly as she had left it: the palace above its own foundations like a ship riding at anchor, the city below it glittering and obedient, her husband's ministers filing in each morning with their ledgers and their lies, none of them larger than she could manage.

Then, eleven nights ago, the dreams began.

They did not come like dreams. They came like weather. She would be drifting in her first shallow sleep, the candle still burning, and the room would grow cold—not the ordinary cold of Vell's stone corridors but a cold with intention to it, a cold that pressed against the windows from outside as though something vast had laid its face against the glass. And beneath her, beneath the featherbed and the floorboards and the forty feet of dressed granite her architects had sworn were solid, she would hear it.

A door.

Not opening. Not yet. But straining. A sound like a tree groaning in wind, like ice on a lake at dusk, like the long complaint of something that has held one position for so long that movement itself has become agony. It came up through the bedframe, through the bones of the palace, through the bones of her, and every night it was louder, and every night she woke with salt on her lips and could not have said whether she had been weeping or whether the sea itself was trying to reach her.

On the fourth night she had dreamed the door itself: black wood gone silver with age, bound in iron that was not iron, carved over every inch with sigils worn nearly smooth. Someone had sealed it. Someone had stood where she stood in the dream, in the dark beneath the palace, and had poured their whole life into the closing of it—their name, their years, their breath—and the door had accepted the gift and shut, and the shutting had sounded like the last word of a very long argument.

And behind the door, patient as winter, something had said: *not forever.*

She woke then, truly woke, and rang for her ladies, and drank the posset they brought her, and did not taste it, because for the first time in her reign Queen Serevanne was afraid of her own house.

* * *

By day she governed. That was the arrangement she had made with herself, the covenant that held her life together: whatever moved beneath the stones by night, by day there were petitions to hear and grain tariffs to argue and a husband who smiled too easily and ministers who smiled not at all. She sat the ivory chair in the long gallery and listened to the city report itself to her, and if her hands were colder than usual on the armrests, no one remarked upon it. Queens are permitted cold hands.

But the reports had begun to rhyme with the dreams, and that she could not ignore.

The frost in the low orchards, weeks early. The well in the Tanners' Quarter running bitter. And now—this was the item that made her set down her stylus mid-word—a tinker hanged in Ironmarket Square for selling charms, and the bells rung backward afterward, and the witch-finders given license to enter any house in the city without warrant. Halvard's men. She knew the name the way one knows the name of a disease going around: distantly, thankfully, until it isn't distant anymore.

"Charm-sellers," Chancellor Voss said, in his voice like dry paper. "Superstition, Majesty. The old gods are dead; what remains is fraud and fever. The witch-finders will have it scoured from the city within the month."

"And the frost?" she asked.

"A hard season, Majesty. Nothing more."

She looked at him for a long moment. He looked back with the flat patience of a man who had told four monarchs comforting lies and expected to tell a fifth. She let it go. There was no use asking Voss about doors beneath palaces; he would have the masons pour concrete over the question and call it maintenance.

That evening she dismissed her ladies early. She sat alone with the lamp and thought about the dream-door, and about how, in the dream, she had known the person who sealed it. Not their face—the dream kept that turned away—but their *stance*, the particular way they had braced themselves against the closing, shoulders set, feet planted, the posture of someone doing a thing they believed must be done and did not expect to be thanked for. She had felt, waking, that she ought to know that stance. That she had seen it somewhere, recently, in waking life, bent over some small domestic task.

The thought would not leave her. It sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

There was one person in the palace whose stance she watched more closely than anyone's. One person whose body she studied daily, professionally, with her life depending on the reading of it.

Her food-taster.

* * *

She summoned Astrid Ishikawa to the morning room, informally, without ceremony—which was itself a small strangeness, and Astrid, arriving with the covered tray and the little silver spoons, registered it at once. Queens did not summon tasters to morning rooms. They sent for them in kitchens, in corridors, in the gray half-hours before state breakfasts. A summons to a private room meant either favor or suspicion, and Astrid's whole art lay in ensuring that no one ever had reason to feel either toward them.

Serevanne watched the taster cross the room and thought: *there. That is the stance.*

It was nothing dramatic. It was the way Astrid set down the tray—both hands, deliberate, weight tested before release. The way they stood afterward: feet planted, shoulders level, head slightly bowed but not servile, a body arranged to occupy the smallest possible amount of the world while remaining entirely ready. It was the posture of a person who had decided, long ago, exactly how much space they were allowed, and defended that decision with everything they had.

It was the posture of someone holding a door shut.

"Eat," the queen said, when the tasting was done—porridge, honeyed pears, the small bitter cup of almond water that was ritual more than risk these days. Astrid ate. Serevanne watched the throat move, watched for the flicker of anything—hesitation, pallor, the fine tremor she had trained herself to see. There was nothing. There never was. Ten years, and the taster had never so much as flushed.

"You never sicken," the queen said. It was not a question, and she had not planned to say it aloud, and she heard her own voice come out quieter than she intended. "Ten years. Poisons that killed two of my husband's cousins. You take them like bread."

"The tongue learns, Majesty." Astrid's voice was even, pleasant, forgettable. "As the hand learns the lute."

"The tongue does not learn aconite." Serevanne rose and crossed to the window. Below, the gardens lay under a rime of frost that should not have existed in the First Month; the fountain had frozen mid-arc, a white sculpture of water caught fleeing. "I am going to ask you something strange, taster, and I ask you to remember that I am asking it as a woman who has not slept properly in eleven nights."

Astrid said nothing. Waited. The silence of a person practiced at being furniture.

"I dream of a door," the queen said. "Beneath this palace. Under the foundations, in the dark, in water. It is sealed—someone sealed it, long ago, at great cost—and every night it strains harder against the seal, and every night I wake certain that when it opens, it will not be a disaster. It will be a *homecoming*. Do you understand how frightening that is? To dream of your own ruin and grieve that it hasn't come?"

She turned. And she saw it—saw the thing she had summoned Astrid here to see, the thing no one else in the palace would ever have caught because no one else was watching for it.

The taster's composure cracked. Not much. A hairline fracture, a seam of light. But Astrid Ishikawa's eyes went wide and dark and *recognized*, and for one unguarded instant the forgettable face was neither forgettable nor pleasant, and Serevanne understood that she was looking at another woman pretending to be smaller than she was.

"You've dreamed it," the queen said softly. "Haven't you. Or something like it."

Astrid's hands, folded at their waist, tightened. When they spoke, the pleasant voice had roughened at the edges. "Majesty should not ask me this."

"Majesty is asking anyway." She came closer. Up close, the taster smelled faintly of herbs and candle smoke and, beneath it, something older—woodsmoke and river water, the smell of the dream-dark under the palace. "Three nights ago I dreamed the door spoke. It used my grandmother's voice. It said the country was already gone—that Vell was a name written over a deeper name, and the deeper name was waking. Tell me I am mad, taster. Tell me the winter is only weather. Give me one comfortable lie and I will let you go back to your kitchen and we will never speak of it again."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, so faint it might have been the settling of the building, might have been the pipes, might have been nothing at all, came a long, low groan—stone against stone, or wood against iron, deep in the roots of the palace.

Both of them heard it. Both of them pretended, for a moment, that they hadn't.

Then Astrid Ishikawa lifted their head, and the mask did not come back all the way, and what looked out at Serevanne from behind the servant's face was tired, and frightened, and fiercely awake.

"There's a charm-seller on Charm Row," Astrid said quietly. "Odessa. If Majesty wants the truth instead of a comfortable lie—" the taster hesitated, glancing once at the frozen garden, the dead fountain, the winter that had come three months early and shown no sign of meaning to leave, "—then Majesty should know that yesterday, one of my trinkets answered a dying man's prayer. And I never made it to do that. I don't think I made it at all. I think something made *me*, and it's under our feet, and it's waking up."

The queen stood very still. Outside, the first snow of the false winter began to fall—fat, slow flakes, falling straight down, as though the sky had stopped caring where things landed.

"Send for this Odessa," Serevanne said at last. "Discreetly. Through channels no one watches."

"Majesty—" Astrid's voice caught. "If the witch-finders learn you've asked—"

"Then the witch-finders will learn what happens to people who frighten the queen of Vell." But her hand, resting on the windowsill, was not steady, and both of them saw it, and neither of them mentioned it. Two women, pretending to be smaller than they were, standing in a palace that was groaning at its foundations like a ship taking water.

"Go," the queen said gently. "Before someone notices how long I've kept you."

Astrid bowed—the full bow, the one servants gave, but slower than usual, and at the bottom of it their eyes came up and held hers for a heartbeat: kinship, conspiracy, fear shared out between them like bread. Then the door closed, and Serevanne was alone with the snow, and beneath her feet, faint and patient and unmistakable now, the door began—very softly—to knock.
