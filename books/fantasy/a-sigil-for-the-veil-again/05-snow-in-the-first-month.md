## 5. Snow in the First Month

The snow came in the first month, when the lindens along the Processional still had their leaves, and it did not stop.

Astrid stood at the kitchen window on the morning of the ninth day and watched it bury the herb garden they had tended for six years. The rosemary was a white mound. The sage had vanished entirely. Somewhere beneath that smooth blank slope were the thyme beds the under-cooks called Astrid's garden, because Astrid was the only one who could tell thyme from creeping savory by leaf alone—and now there was nothing to tell. The snow made everything honest in the worst way: it erased distinctions, smoothed the world into one unbroken surface, and underneath it things went on growing or dying without anyone knowing which.

Nine days of snow, in a city where the old almanacs promised perhaps two falls a winter, light as flour dusted over pastry.

"Third time I've swept the yard," said Bren, the scullery boy—the one to whom, a season ago, Astrid had slipped a little luck-charm shaped like a wren. He had not taken it off since. It hung against his chest on a leather cord gone dark with grease and sweat, and every morning he touched it before he touched his work, an unconscious little gesture that made something twist behind Astrid's ribs. "Master Quillon says if it keeps up he'll let us melt snow for the wash-water. Says the cisterns'll freeze."

"The cisterns won't freeze," Astrid said. "They're under the kitchen. Kitchen heat rises through the stone."

"Everything's cold," Bren said, with the flat certainty of the young. "Even the ovens are working harder than they should. Cook says the bread's coming out wrong. Dense. Like the flour's tired."

*Like the country's tired,* Astrid thought, and did not say it.

Because Astrid knew something the kitchens did not, something gathered in fragments from Odessa's back room and from the hushed talk of charm-buyers who came to Charm Row with snow still in their hems: the winter was not weather. The winter was a symptom. The priest—half-mad, frost-bitten at the fingertips, smelling of tallow and old incense—had said words that Odessa repeated to Astrid in the dark of her shop with her hand gripping Astrid's wrist hard enough to bruise: *The seal is failing. What's leaking through isn't cold. It's absence.*

Astrid had swallowed that sentence the way they swallowed everything: completely, without flinching, and with no one the wiser.

That was the discipline of the taster's life. Ten years of swallowing.

* * *

The queen's breakfast that morning was barley porridge with honey, poached pears, small beer, and a plate of candied ginger because the queen had been dreaming badly again—everyone in the palace knew Her Majesty slept poorly, though no one knew why, and no one knew that she had summoned her food-taster three weeks past and asked, in a voice like a struck bell heard through water, whether Astrid dreamed of doors.

Astrid tasted each dish in order, as ritual demanded, holding each mouthful long enough to count its lies. Barley—clean, faintly of the field it came from, no bitterness. Honey—honey, and something underneath, almost imperceptible, a green edge like crushed stems. Astrid held it on the tongue, closed their eyes, walked down the corridor of flavors the way they'd been taught by the old taster Vess, dead now these four years: *every poison has a neighbor-flavor, girl. Bitter hides beside sweet, metal beside fruit. Your tongue is a dog; make it hunt.* But this wasn't bitter, and it wasn't metal. It was green. Growing. Like the taste of the charms when they had begun, that terrible wonderful night on the beggar's corner, to blaze with actual power.

Astrid swallowed the honey.

Nothing happened. Nothing ever happened. That was the whole architecture of Astrid's life—a body so steeped in poison that poison had given up on it, a face so forgettable that forgettability had become a kind of armor, a magic so carefully small that it had passed for ten years as craft, as knack, as nothing at all.

But lately the nothing had been getting heavier. The charms Astrid sold through Odessa did more than they should. A fever-charm that had been good for taking the edge off a child's chills had, last week, broken a fever that should have killed. A warding knot Astrid tied out of habit had turned away a cutpurse so thoroughly that the man had walked into the canal. Small magics, going loud. And Astrid could feel, under the skin, under the discipline, something waking up the way a limb wakes from numbness—all pins and needles and terrible returning feeling.

"You're frowning at my breakfast," the queen said.

Astrid bowed. "Only admiring the pears, Majesty."

"Liar." But the queen said it gently, almost fondly, and looked out the window at the falling snow. She was younger than her portraits, thinner than her gowns allowed anyone to see, and she had shadows under her eyes the color of storm-water. "I dreamed of it again last night. The door." She said the word the way one says a name one has been warned against saying. "It was open a crack this time. There was light behind it. Not firelight. Not sunlight." Her hands were folded on the table, and her knuckles were white. "Green light. Like spring seen through a keyhole."

Astrid said nothing. Saying nothing was the first rule of surviving Vell. But their heart was hammering against their ribs like a bird against glass, because Astrid had dreamed the same dream, had stood before that same door in sleep, had felt the green light on their face like a hand.

"Eat the pear, Ishikawa," the queen said quietly. "You've stopped tasting."

Astrid ate the pear.

* * *

It happened at the Feast of the Founding—or rather, it happened in the hour after, in the corridor outside the banquet hall, when the palace was all candle-glow and wine-loosened laughter and the snow pressed against the windows like a crowd trying to look in.

Astrid saw it happen, which was the problem. Saw the second cup-bearer—young, nervous, new since the autumn—stumble against the queen's chair, saw his sleeve brush the queen's goblet, saw the tiny glass vial that dropped from his cuff and shattered on the flagstones, saw him go white as the view beyond the window and bolt.

And then everyone looked at the queen, and the queen looked at her cup, and the Master of Ceremonies began shouting for the witch-finders before anyone had even decided what was wrong.

What was wrong was that the wine was poisoned. Astrid knew it the way they knew their own heartbeat. They could smell it from three feet away—hemlock and something else, something newer, alchemical, the sort of thing the Crown's own laboratories brewed and then pretended other people brewed. The queen had not drunk. The queen had been distracted, laughing at something her fool had said, and the poisoned cup sat there gleaming like an accusation.

"Seal the hall!" Halvard's voice, from the doorway. The witch-finder himself, gray-cloaked, gray-eyed, with the ash-gray brand of the Office of Vigilance on his jaw. He filled a doorway the way winter fills a valley. His eyes went to the spilled vial, to the cup, to the queen—and then, with the terrible economy of a man who wasted no motion, to Astrid.

"Taster," he said. "Prove the cup."

It was procedure. Of course it was procedure. The taster proves the poison; the taster's body is the instrument the Crown trusts above all instruments, because the taster cannot lie. Poison does not negotiate.

Astrid lifted the goblet. The hall had gone silent except for the wind worrying the shutters. The queen watched with her folded hands and her storm-water eyes, and there was something in her face—not hope, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman watching someone else stand where she herself had been standing in her dreams.

Astrid drank.

Hemlock first, climbing the throat like cold feet climbing stairs. Then the other thing, the laboratory thing, which had no name Astrid knew but had a flavor—oily, mineral, wrong, like licking a coin that had been buried in a grave. Astrid's body received both the way sand receives rain. Ten years of swallowing. Ten years of measured doses, escalating by grains, by drops, by the width of a hair, until poison looked at Astrid's blood and found nothing to hold onto. Vess's training. Astrid's patience. Astrid's entire invisible life, distilled into this single moment of not dying.

Astrid set down the cup.

"Hemlock," they said, voice steady, "and something compounded. I couldn't name it. The dose would kill any person in this hall within the quarter-hour."

Silence.

Then Halvard crossed the floor, slow as a glacier, and took Astrid's chin in fingers cold as the window-glass, and turned their face up to the light, and studied them the way a man studies a horse's teeth or a coin's edge.

"The dose would kill any person in this hall," he repeated softly.

"I have a trained constitution," Astrid said. "Ten years of service. My records are open to the Crown."

"They are. I've read them. Impeccable." His thumb pressed, briefly, painfully, beneath Astrid's jaw, against the pulse. "Impeccable records are how the clever ones hide. You know what I found in the Charm Row ashes, taster? A ledger, burned, but the iron fittings survived. And stamped into the fittings, the maker's mark of a sigil-cutter whose work I have been collecting for two years." His eyes were very pale and very patient. "Every trinket in that ledger bore the same mark."

The hall tilted. Or Astrid did. The candles swam. Behind Halvard, the queen had risen from her chair, and her face was unreadable, and her hands were still folded, and her eyes said—I don't want them to take you—though her mouth said nothing at all, because queens do not say anything at all when witch-finders are listening.

"A luck-charm here and there," Astrid said. "Trinkets. Knack-work. Every kitchen has—"

"Yes," Halvard said. "Every kitchen has. And every kitchen's knack doesn't drink a grave's worth of poison and ask for more." He released Astrid's chin and stepped back, and his voice did not rise, which was worse than rising; it carried anyway, gentle and absolute, into every corner of the silent hall. "Take the taster to the Vigilance cells. Quietly. Her Majesty will be told the assassin's accomplice is in custody pending examination."

Two guards took Astrid's arms. They were not rough. That was somehow the most frightening part—the gentleness, the courtesy, the sense that Astrid had already stopped being a person to them and become a thing to be moved.

As they led Astrid past the high table, the queen finally unfolded her hands. She did not speak. She could not. But as Astrid was drawn by her, close enough to smell the honey still on her breath, her lips moved once, shaping words with no sound behind them, words meant for no ears but Astrid's:

*Find the door.*

Then the corridor, and the cold, and the sound of the guards' boots, and behind them the feast resuming its laughter too quickly, the way people laugh when they are deciding not to have seen what they saw.

Astrid had spent ten years learning how to be unseen. Walking between the guards through the frozen corridors of the palace, tasting hemlock's ghost on their tongue, Astrid understood, with the clarity of a blade laid flat against skin, that the hiding was finished.

Halvard had seen them. The queen had seen them. And being seen, in the country of Vell, in the winter that would not end, was the same as being doomed.

Unless the door was real. Unless the dreams were real. Unless the green light behind the keyhole was waiting, patient as spring, for someone with nothing left to hide behind to come and open it.

The cell, when they reached it, was cold as everywhere else. Astrid sat down on the stone shelf that served for a bed, folded their hands the way the queen folded hers, and began, silently, to pray—to gods whose names they had never been permitted to learn—for the first time in ten years, with no care at all who might be listening.
