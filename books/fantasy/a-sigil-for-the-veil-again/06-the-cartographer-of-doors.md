## 6. The Cartographer of Doors

The snow had been falling for nine days when the mad priest came scratching at Odessa's back door like a cat that had once been fed and never forgot it.

Odessa heard him before she saw him—a thin, papery sound against the shutter, almost lost beneath the groan of the wind off the Veilwater. She was in her counting room, which is to say her kitchen, which is to say the warmest and most crowded room in the crooked house at the end of Charm Row, where six of the neighborhood's strays were packed around her stove like spoons in a drawer. Tam was mending nets by candlelight. The twins, Pip and Perrin, were arguing over a game of knucklebones with the solemn fury of boys who had lost their school and their street and their certainty all in one winter. Little Maren slept in a nest of blankets near the hearth, her fever finally broken after four days that had aged Odessa ten years.

She knew every one of those sounds. She knew the house the way she knew her own hands. Which was why the scratching bothered her like a wrong note in a familiar song.

She opened the door with the iron poker in one hand and found an old man half-buried in drift, his robe crusted white, his beard frozen into a shape like a breaking wave. His eyes were the pale washed-out blue of very old glass, and they were looking at her door as if it were something he had drawn once, long ago, from memory.

"You're the fence," he said. "Odessa. They said on the Row you take in what nobody else will hold."

"I take in goods," Odessa said. "And children. And occasionally trouble, when it's small enough to fit through the door." She looked him over—the frostbitten knuckles, the way his weight leaned left, favoring a bad hip, the ink stains gone gray at the creases of his fingers. A scribe's stains. A mapmaker's stains. "You're not any of those things."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I'm a dead man. Brother Anselm, formerly of the Order of the Threshold, which the Crown dissolved forty years ago and has been pretending not to remember ever since. I've come to die somewhere warm, and to draw you a picture before I do."

* * *

They nearly didn't let him in. That should be recorded, Odessa thought later, because everyone always assumed the family on Charm Row was soft-hearted, and they weren't—they were careful, which is harder and worth more.

It was Astrid who tipped the balance. Astrid arrived past midnight, coming through the cellar hatch with snow in their hair and a look on their face that Odessa had learned to read the way sailors read weather. Astrid crouched in front of the old man where he sat steaming by the stove, took his frozen hands without asking, and said, "Tell me about the door."

Anselm's eyes came into focus for the first time since he'd walked in. "So you're the taster," he said. "The one whose charms caught fire."

"They told you that?"

"The snow told me. The snow talks now, did you know that? It's been trying to tell me for thirty years and lately it won't stop." He laughed, a dry rustling sound, and then stopped laughing as though he'd forgotten how he'd been doing it. "I mapped it, you know. Under the palace. Forty-one years ago, when the Order still had candles and permission. There is a door beneath Vell, girl—beneath the foundations, beneath the cisterns, down where the water remembers being sky. My order sealed nothing. We only measured the seal the founders left. And I am the last living soul who drew it true."

"Show me," Astrid said.

And there it was, Odessa thought, watching them: the thing about Astrid that made her keep them close all these years. Not the charms, not even the magic that had come roaring up out of them like spring water through cracked stone. It was this—that when everyone else in the room saw a madman, Astrid saw a map.

* * *

He worked for three days.

That was the strange mercy of it. Whatever had broken Anselm's mind had left his hands whole; set before him paper and ink and a candle stub, he became someone else entirely, someone precise and calm, and the lines that came out of him were beautiful in the way that engineering is beautiful—all load and measure, no ornament. He drew the palace foundations from memory: the cistern levels, the drowned stairways, the old well-shafts from before the founders poured their stones over everything. And below all of it, at the bottom of the page, a single shape.

A door. Drawn in a hand that shook, though every other line was steady.

"It isn't a door the way you mean doors," Anselm said, tapping it. "It doesn't open onto another room. It opens onto an absence. When your founders built Vell, they built it over a wound in the world, and they sewed it shut with oaths and blood and names nobody speaks anymore. They called it the Veil. Everyone thinks the Veil is between us and the gods." He smiled, and there was no comfort in it. "The Veil is between us and the rest of creation. We are inside the sewing, children. All of us. Every wall in this city is a stitch."

The kitchen had gone quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in at the shutter to listen.

"And now?" Odessa asked. She kept her voice level because three children were watching her face to learn how afraid to be. "The winter. Say it plainly, old man. I deal in plain things."

"Plainly, then." Anselm set down his pen. "The founders are dust. Their names are forgotten, and a seal made of names cannot hold when the names rot out of it. Your winter isn't weather. Weather comes from somewhere and goes somewhere else. This winter is the outside leaking in—the cold of everywhere-else, pouring through a seam that's coming undone stitch by stitch. The gods aren't returning because they forgave us. They're returning because they can finally see us again. Through the holes." He looked around the kitchen, at each face in turn, gentle and terrible. "In the eyes of everything beyond that door, this country already ended. You are all ghosts who haven't been told."

Maren began to cry. Tam picked her up and held her against his shoulder, rocking, glaring at the old man over her head as if he could burn him with indignation alone.

"That's a fine sermon," Odessa said, "for a man who wants soup and a bed. But sermons don't feed anyone, and neither does doom. So here's my plain question, and I'll thank you for a plain answer." She planted both fists on the table. "Is there a way through? Yes or no."

"Yes or no," Anselm repeated, delighted, as if she'd offered him a game. "Yes or no. Fence-woman, do you know what a cartographer says when asked if a road exists? He says: I have drawn it. Whether it holds is between the walker and the ground." He pulled the finished map toward himself and turned it so they could all see the door-shape at its heart, and beneath it, in cramped and ancient script, a line of text Odessa couldn't read but which made Astrid go very still, the way a deer goes still when it scents something it has smelled once before, in a dream.

"What does it say?" Odessa asked.

"It says," Anselm read aloud, "'The door answers to a name freely given.'" He looked up at Astrid. "Not taken. Not bought. Given. Whoever wrote that knew more than my whole order did, God rest their cowardly bones."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned to pools.

Tam wanted to run—north, over the mountains, ahead of the winter, take the children and the strong ones and damn the rest. "There's no door," he said, jabbing a finger at the map. "There's a story a mad priest tells to get warm. And even if it's real, we'd be going *down*, into the flooded dark, under the palace, where the witch-finders are crawling thicker than rats since the queen's taster survived poison meant to kill a horse. You want to walk toward Halvard?"

Perrin wanted to stay and fight, which was easy for a boy of fourteen to want. Pip said nothing and sharpened a knife that didn't need it. The twins' aunt Sera, who had come in from the dyer's shop with frost in her lashes, said quietly that she'd buried two husbands in Vell and would not bury her sister's sons in some foreign spring, and besides, what god wanted worshipers who'd abandoned their neighbors?

Through all of it Odessa listened, and watched Astrid, who said almost nothing, who sat with Anselm's map spread flat under their palms like a person warming themselves on a stove.

Because that was the arithmetic she couldn't get out of her head, and wouldn't say aloud in front of the children: running was possible. Barely. The roads north were closing but not closed; her contacts in the carters' guild owed her enough favors to move seven people and a chest of goods past the tolls. Flee and live—that was on the table. Stay and seek a door that might be a madman's doodle, beneath a palace full of witch-hunters, and likely die in the dark with the whole city watching the snow bury everything.

Any fence worth her salt took the certain profit over the gamble. Any mother worth hers did the same.

But she looked at Astrid's hands on that map, and she thought about the luck-charms she'd sold for copper coins for eleven years, harmless little things, she'd sworn to herself they were harmless—and she thought about Maren's fever breaking the night one of those little things hung over the child's bed, blazing faintly blue when no one but Odessa was awake to see it.

"There's no choosing yet," she said at last, and the room went quiet, because when Odessa spoke in that voice, the room went quiet. "We don't decide tonight whether to run or dig. We decide tonight whether we decide together or scatter. That's the only vote that matters." She looked around at them, at her strays, her ledger that wasn't written in any book. "Whatever we do, we do as a family or not at all. Agreed?"

They agreed. Of course they agreed. That was the whole point of her, and they knew it.

* * *

Later—well past midnight, the children asleep, the snow ticking softly against the shutters like a patient finger—Anselm found her in the doorway of the counting room, standing watch out of habit.

"You didn't ask me the real question," he said.

"I asked whether there's a way through."

"No. The real question. Whether you believe me." He settled beside her, old bones cracking, and gazed down the dark length of Charm Row, where the snow lay unbroken and blue-white under the moon, deeper than any winter in living memory. "I'll tell you why I know the door is real, fence-woman. Not because of my maps. Because for thirty years I prayed to a silent heaven and got silence, and silence is a kind of answer—it means *no one is listening*. And three weeks ago, the snow began talking back." His breath fogged and hung between them. "The gods are at the walls of the world, Odessa. Knocking. Something on the other side of that door knows your taster's name."

"Then it knows more than I do," Odessa said. "I've known the child eleven years and I still only know the shape they leave behind."

"Mm," said Anselm. "Doors are like that."

Before dawn, one of Tam's runners came pounding up the Row with ice in his hair and terror all down his front, and hammered on the door hard enough to wake the dead: word from the palace district, passed mouth to mouth down the chain of people who owed Odessa favors.

The witch-finder Halvard had questioned the royal kitchens. And he had asked for one name.

Hers.
