## 7. Beneath the Palace Stones

The priest's map was drawn on the back of a funeral shroud, which Odessa said told you everything you needed to know about the man who made it.

Astrid held it close to their one candle and studied the lines again. Ink gone brown with age, but sure-handed: the palace above, rendered small and almost contemptuous, like a child's drawing of a house; and beneath it, spreading wider than the palace itself, a country of corridors and cisterns and stairways that no mason's record admitted existed. At the center, circled twice, marked with a sigil Astrid had never seen before this winter and now saw everywhere—burned into doorframes, traced in frost on windows, dreamed by queens—a simple shape like an eye crossed by a river.

*The door,* Father Anselm had said, in the voice of a man describing his own grave. *I mapped it when I still believed mapping was the same as understanding. It is below the oldest stones. It is below the water. And it is awake.*

Now the water was the problem.

"You're certain about the tide," Odessa whispered. She stood at the top of the stair that spiraled down behind the palace's old well-house, her coat pockets sagging with oilcloth bundles, flint, rope, and every kind of small useful violence a fence could carry. Her breath fogged. Everything breathed fog now; the cold had stopped being weather and become a fact, like stone.

"The cisterns drain at the third hour," Astrid said. "The queen's dreams said so." They had learned, over the last weeks, to stop apologizing for sentences like that.

"Her majesty dreams us a plumbing schedule."

"She dreams true. That's more than most of us can say."

Odessa grunted, unconvinced but loyal, which was the shape of all her faith. Then she pressed something into Astrid's hand: a charm, small and ugly, knotted twine around a river pebble. One of Astrid's own, from years ago, sold for two coppers to a girl who'd wanted luck in love.

"It came back to me," Odessa said. "Everything you ever sold is coming back. Half my stock glows now. I figured—" she shrugged, embarrassed, "—you should have it. For the dark."

Astrid closed their fist around it. The pebble was warm. It had always been warm, they realized; they had simply spent ten years perfecting the art of not noticing.

They went down.

* * *

The stair was older than the palace above it. Astrid knew stone the way other people knew bread—the grain of it, the age of it, what had been cut by ambition and what by fear—and these steps were cut by fear. The walls sweated. Somewhere below, water moved in long slow breaths, filling and emptying, like the foundations were sleeping and dreaming badly.

The queen's dreams had come to Astrid too, now, three nights running. In them they stood in a corridor exactly like this one, and something on the other side of a door knew their name—not the name they used, not *Astrid Ishikawa, royal taster, unremarkable, harmless*, but another name, a name like a key turning. They woke each time with their mouth full of the taste of iron and honey.

Poison and sweetness. Their whole life in two flavors.

"Talk to me," Odessa murmured. The dark down here ate sound, and she hated things that ate sound. "Tell me about the first poison you ever took. You never told me."

"Bitterleaf extract. I was nine. My mother fed it to me in honey wine, a drop at a time, until my body learned to lie about dying." Astrid kept their voice level because Odessa needed it level. "She wanted me employable. A taster who can't die is worth three who can."

"And you've been swallowing the city's sins ever since."

"Someone has to digest them."

The stair ended at a landing where the air changed—colder, wetter, tasting of minerals and something older underneath, something like the smell of a forest floor after snowmelt. Anselm's map showed a gallery here, then a flooded hall, then the door beyond it, dry only during the third-hour drain.

The gallery was there. The map was true. That frightened Astrid more than anything else so far.

They waded out into the flooded hall when the water began its withdrawal, black sheets of it peeling back from pillars to reveal a floor worked with tiles, each tile bearing the eye-and-river sigil, thousands of them, worn nearly smooth by feet that had walked here before the founders poured their city on top like wax over a seal. The water rose to Astrid's knees, then thighs, then began to fall as they walked, draining away through channels in the floor with a sound like a long exhale.

"This place was a temple," Odessa said softly. "Before it was a cellar. Before it was nothing."

"It was never nothing. Nothing doesn't hold a shape this long."

At the far end of the hall stood the door.

Not grand. That was the shock of it—it was barely taller than a man, plain oak gone grey as driftwood, set into a wall of living rock with no frame and no arch, as if the mountain had grown around it rather than the reverse. The sigil was carved at its center, deep enough to hold shadow. And it was *breathing*. Slowly, subtly, the wood swelled and settled, swelled and settled, in time with the water, in time, Astrid realized with a lurch, with their own pulse.

Behind them, far above, a bell began to ring.

Then a second bell. Then all of them, the witch-finding pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, spilling down through the stones.

"They know we're here," Odessa breathed.

"Halvard knows we're here. The bells are for the city." Astrid turned. Far up the gallery, torchlight bloomed—two flames, then four, then eight, descending in disciplined silence. Witch-hunters did not shout. Shouting was for people who might be wrong.

"Run for the door—"

"No." Halvard's voice reached them before he did, carried strangely clean across the water. He stepped into the torchlight alone, his hunters fanning out behind him along the gallery rim, crossbows leveled downward. He looked tired, Astrid thought. He looked like a man who had been awake for a decade. "You'll want to hear what I have to say before you touch that."

* * *

But the gods got there first.

It happened the way the first answered prayer had happened—one moment ordinary, the next moment the world turned inside out. The torches guttered though there was no wind. The water, ankle-deep now on the tiles, began to glow faintly, sigil by sigil, each tile lighting like a coal, and the light ran inward toward the door in converging rivers until the whole hall was a single opened eye.

And Astrid heard their own name.

Not *Astrid*. Not the name their mother gave them to be forgotten under. Something beneath it, the way the temple lay beneath the palace—a name spoken in a voice like many waters, like the cisterns breathing, like the long winter wind finding the one gap in a shuttered house:

***We kept it for you. All these silent years, we kept it safe, as you kept our charms. Come and take it back.***

Astrid's knees nearly went. It wasn't sound, exactly; it arrived through the bones, through the tongue, through the trained organ of them that had spent a lifetime cataloguing what entered their body. This entered their body. It tasted of iron and honey.

*You know what we are,* the voice said—or didn't say, offered, like a hand extended. *Taster. Swallower of deaths. You have taken into yourself every poison this crown could brew, and you are still standing, because you were never building tolerance, little one. You were building a vessel. We watched. We could not speak, but we watched, and we loved you for it, and your name is—*

"No," Astrid said aloud, and their voice cracked in half. "Don't. Not yet."

Because it was sweet. That was the terror of it. Ten years of being no one, and here at the bottom of the world was someone offering to make them *everybody*—known to the roots, named by gods, seen so completely that seeing could never be taken back. And some starving part of Astrid, the part that slipped luck-charms to scullery boys just to be noticed for a moment, leaned toward that voice like a plant toward a window.

Beside them, Odessa had gone white and steady, one hand fisted in Astrid's sleeve. "Whatever it's saying—don't take anything from a god for free. Fencing rule."

Above, Halvard was speaking, his voice fraying against the light: "...renounce it, taster. Say the words, walk away, and your family lives—I swear it on whatever I have left to swear on—"

"Astrid." Odessa shook their arm. "The water's coming back."

She was right. The exhale had ended. Down the far channels the flood was returning, black and fast, and the door's carved sigil had begun to seep a thin gold light, and the gods' voice rose with it, patient and vast:

***Choose. Be known, or be nothing. But know that we will wait for you past the end of this winter, and the winter has no end unless you open it.***

Halvard was descending the gallery steps now, torch in one hand, something in the other that caught the light—iron, old iron, shaped like a brand. His face, as it came into the glow of the rising water, was the face of a man looking at a door he himself had once been promised.

Astrid looked at the door. Looked at Odessa. Looked at the name hanging unspoken in the flooded air, waiting, warm as a pebble in a closed fist.

They stepped forward.
