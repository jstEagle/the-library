## 8. The Price of the Threshold

The witch-finder Halvard had spent thirty years learning to read a city the way other men read scripture, and so he knew before his boots touched the first stair that tonight he would find what he had been sent to find. The signs were all there, written in the language he alone still bothered to study: the frost climbing the palace walls in patterns no ice should make—spirals, deliberate spirals, like handwriting; the way the rats had fled the lower cellars in a single mass migration three nights past, as though something below had spoken to them in their own tongue; and above all the dreams. His informants reported that half the palace servants woke weeping from dreams of a door. Halvard did not need informants for his own dreams.

He dreamed of it every night now. He had stopped fighting them weeks ago.

He came down into the flooded foundations alone. This was policy—the witch-finders worked in pairs by law—but Halvard had learned long ago that witnesses were a kindness he could not afford. What happened at the bottom of Vell was between him and whatever gods still bothered to listen, and he did not want anyone else to hear what he might say down there, in the dark, with the water rising around his knees and his lantern guttering in air that tasted of iron and old incense.

He heard them before he saw them: voices echoing off wet stone, low and urgent. A woman's voice he recognized from a hundred confiscated ledgers—Odessa Vail, fence, receiver of stolen goods, mother of strays—and beneath hers, another voice, quieter, one he had been hunting since the night a food-taster drank enough poison to kill a regiment and stood up afterward asking if there was more bread.

Astrid Ishikawa.

Halvard rounded the last pillar and raised his lantern, and there they were: the taster and the fence, soaked to the waist, standing before a wall that was not a wall. The old priest's map lay open in Odessa's hands despite the water soaking its edges, and on the stone behind them—impossibly, beautifully, horribly—a sigil burned. It burned without heat, without smoke, gold light welling up out of the mortar like blood from under a bandage, and Halvard felt it in his teeth, in his scars, in the place behind his sternum where he kept everything he refused to think about.

"There you are," he said, and was surprised at how gentle his own voice sounded.

Odessa moved first, as he'd known she would—she put herself between him and the taster, arms spread, an old woman in a flooded cellar squaring up against the Crown's witch-finder as if her body were any kind of argument. "There's nothing here," she said. "You're seeing things, finder. Old man's eyes."

"I see very clearly, madam." He set the lantern on a dry shelf of rock and unslung his crossbow, but he did not raise it. Not yet. "I've seen clearly for thirty years. That is my whole profession, and my whole curse."

Behind Odessa, Astrid Ishikawa stepped forward, and Halvard got his first true look at the face he had studied in reports and sketches for months. Unremarkable. That was the word every informant used, the word the taster had built a life upon, and Halvard understood the craft of it better than they knew—you could not build a face like that by accident. It was architecture. It was a wall painted to look like weather.

"You're the witch-finder," Astrid said. Not a question. Their voice was steady, which cost them something; Halvard could see the price of it in their eyes.

"Halvard. Yes." He looked at the burning sigil, then back at them. "You found it. I confess I wondered if anyone would. I wondered if it *could* be found."

"Then let us walk through it," Odessa said, "and you can report we drowned in the cellars. Everyone wins."

"No," Halvard said, and drew the crossbow after all—not aimed, just held, the way a judge holds a gavel. "No, madam, I'm afraid drowning is not among your options. Nothing is among your options anymore. You are the most important people in this country, and this country is dying, and I—" He stopped. The words he had rehearsed on the stairs, the official words, the words of writs and warrants, sat in his mouth like stones. He spat them out. What came instead was older.

"I was a charm-seller," he said.

The silence afterward was total except for the water dripping somewhere in the dark and the sigil's light pulsing slow as a heartbeat.

"Row Nine," he went on, because now that the door of it had opened he could not make himself stop, thirty years of sealed silence coming up like floodwater. "Before your time, girl. Before yours too, madam. Little stall, little trinkets—knots against nightmares, salt for safe roads. My mother taught me the knots. They didn't work, of course. None of it worked. We all knew it didn't work, and we sold it anyway, because hope is a commodity and someone has to move inventory." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going. "I sold a knot to a woman whose daughter had fever. I told her it would hold. She believed me because she needed to believe me, and the daughter died, and when I went to the mother she looked at me and said, *You knew. You always knew.* And she was right. I always knew."

He picked up the lantern again, held it high so the light caught all three of them.

"The gods were silent. Do you understand? Silent for two hundred years. Every prayer fell into a hole. Every charm was a lie we told each other to keep from going mad in the dark. And I decided—if the heavens will not answer, then I will spend my life making sure no one else lies about hearing them. I became what I am because I could not bear one more hopeful face. The hunt is not cruelty, taster. The hunt is grief with a warrant."

Astrid had gone very pale, but they did not step back. "You're wrong," they said quietly. "It wasn't a lie. Not all of it."

"No?"

"The knots worked. The charms worked. They were working the whole time—they just weren't working *yet*. The gods weren't silent. They were sealed away. There's a door under this city, and your founders locked it, and everything your whole life has been built over the grave of the thing you were grieving." Astrid gestured at the burning wall. "You hunted us for hearing what was real. The silence wasn't heaven's answer, Halvard. It was a wall's."

Halvard stood very still. The water lapped at his thighs. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of stone, the bells of Vell rang the hour, and he thought: *I have given this city thirty years of my life, and it has been standing on a corpse the entire time.*

"That," he said at last, "changes nothing about what happens next. You understand that. It changes everything about what I believe, and nothing about what I must do."

"It doesn't have to be that way," Odessa said.

"But it does, madam, because I am not the only one who followed the signs tonight. My colleagues are in the upper cellars. Twelve of them, armed, obedient, and certain. When I do not return within the hour, they come down. So." He leveled the crossbow at last—at Astrid, because it could only ever be at Astrid. "Here is the offer. The only offer I have the authority or the heart to make."

"Make it," Astrid said.

"You renounce it. Publicly, tomorrow, in the square, before the crowd and the Crown. You say the words—I will write them for you myself: *I confess to sorcery, I abjure the old gods, I renounce all charms and workings, and I submit to the mercy of the throne.* Your immunity becomes a matter of record—a constitution, an anomaly, a medical curiosity. No trial. No pyre. The fence and the priest and the children go free, pardoned, their names struck from every ledger I control." He paused, and his voice dropped. "And the door stays shut. You seal that sigil, you turn your back on it, and you live. All of you. In the country that exists."

"And the winter?" Astrid asked softly. "The crops failing? The country dying? You know what's happening up there. You've seen the frost-spirals same as I have. If the seal fails anyway—if the door breaks on its own—then everyone dies believing your lie, and no one is ready."

"Then they die warm in their beds instead of screaming on a pyre," Halvard said. "That is not nothing, taster. I have arranged gentler deaths for this city than starvation. I can arrange this one."

"And you?" Odessa's voice was rough. "What do you get out of it, finder? Thirty years of hunting, and you end it with a pardon?"

Halvard almost laughed. "What do I get? I get to stop dreaming of doors. I get one morning where I don't hate myself. I get to believe, for exactly as long as it takes you to speak the words, that the silence was honest after all—that the world really is empty and cold and rule-bound, and everything I burned, I burned for a good reason." He tightened his grip on the stock. "That is the price, taster. Your truth for their lives. Lie once, beautifully, publicly, and live. Or tell the truth down here in the dark where no one will ever hear it, and die, and take them with you."

The sigil pulsed. And in the pulse, Halvard felt—or imagined he felt—something attend. Something vast and patient leaning close through forty feet of stone and two hundred years of seal, listening to the terms of the bargain being struck over its grave.

Astrid looked at Odessa. Odessa shook her head slowly—*don't*—and Halvard saw it then, the thing that made this family what it was: the old woman would rather burn than be purchased with a lie.

"Don't answer yet," Halvard said. He lowered the crossbow a fraction. "Think. That is not mercy; it is strategy. Because I want you to know, before you choose, that I mean it. Every pardon. Every name struck. I will stand in that square and swear it on my office, and my office is the only god I have left." His jaw worked. "But I want you to know something else too. The night my mother died, I knelt at her bed and prayed to the old gods anyway. After everything. After all my certainty. I knelt in the dark like a child and I begged them to take me instead of her, and nothing answered, and I told myself that proved they were dead." He looked at the burning door, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them. "If I had known there was a wall between us—if I had known they never even *heard* me—"

He could not finish it. He turned his face away, this man who had watched a hundred executions without blinking, and stared at the dark water as though it might show him the boy he had been on Row Nine, selling knots that worked.

"So think carefully, taster," he said hoarsely. "Lie and live. Truth and die. Those are the doors, and mine closes in less than an hour."

And Astrid Ishikawa, who had spent ten years being no one, who had drunk poison daily to become invisible, who stood now knee-deep in a drowned foundation with their family behind them and their true name burning on the wall before them—Astrid lifted their chin, and reached into their coat, and drew out a knife.

"Halvard," they said, "there's a third door. Watch."

* * *

He watched. He would watch until the day he died, some part of him understood that already—watched as the taster pressed the blade's point against the heel of their own palm and began, deliberately, steadily, to carve.

Not flesh-marks. Letters. A word, cut into their own skin in the old script, the script from the confiscated books Halvard had burned by the cartload, the script his mother had whispered over her knots on Row Nine. Blood welled black in the lantern-light, and the sigil on the wall flared in answer, and the water around their ankles began to glow.

"What are you doing," Halvard breathed. It was not a question. It was a plea.

"Astrid Ishikawa is the name the city gave me," the taster said, and their voice had changed—it resonated now, off the stone, off the water, off the inside of Halvard's skull, as though several voices spoke in unison and only one of them was human. "It's a fine name. It kept me alive. But it isn't the key." They raised their bleeding hand toward the burning wall. "This is."

And the letters on their palm opened like an eye.

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the bells of Vell began to ring out of order—wild, clashing, all eleven towers at once—and Halvard knew the witch-finders in the upper cellars had heard it too, and were coming, running, down.

He had perhaps three hundred heartbeats left to decide which side of a door he would be standing on when it opened.
