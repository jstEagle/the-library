## 9. A Sigil for the Veil

The door was smaller than Astrid had imagined.

That was the cruelty of it, perhaps. A decade of nightmares had built it vast—iron-banded, taller than the palace, breathing frost. But here, at the bottom of the flooded stair, in a chamber where the water hung suspended like a held breath, it was barely taller than a man. Grey stone. No hinges. No handle. Only a surface of dark basalt, slick and gleaming, carved over every inch with names so worn they were less than whispers.

Astrid stood before it with water dripping from their hem, and felt the whole country lean against the other side.

*We are here,* said the gods, and their voice was not one voice but many, layered like bells under ice. *We have always been here. Open us.*

Behind them, Odessa held the lantern high, its light trembling across the flooded vault. The old priest, Brother Cassian, knelt in the shallows with his ruined map pressed to his chest, mouthing prayers he had not been permitted to say in forty years. And beyond them all, up the stair, boots were coming—dozens of them, the measured cadence of witch-hunters who had found what witch-hunters always dreamed of finding: real magic, real witches, a real door.

And Halvard.

Astrid could feel him the way they felt poison on their tongue—not a taste exactly, but a knowing. He was close. He was always close now.

"Astrid." Odessa's voice was low, rough as banked coals. She'd set down her ledger-burning hands at last; they hung open at her sides, empty and ready. "Whatever you're going to do, do it fast."

"The name." Astrid's own voice sounded strange to them, unhidden for the first time in ten years. "I need my true name. You offered it. In the dark, on the stairs—you offered to give it back."

The gods stirred beneath the stone like fish under winter ice. *We did. But a name given is a gift. A name taken back must be chosen. Speak it yourself, or remain nameless, and the door stays shut, and the winter wins.*

"That's the trick of it, isn't it," Astrid whispered. "There's always a price."

*There is always a truth,* the gods corrected, gently, terribly. *You have spent your life paying in lies. Pay once in truth instead.*

Boots above. Louder. Torches bleeding orange down the spiral of the stair. Somewhere up there, Halvard was shouting orders in that flat, dead voice he used, the voice of a man who had sawed out his own heart years ago and filled the hole with procedure.

Odessa caught Astrid's shoulder. "Look at me." Her face was wet—it might have been the floodwater, it might not. "Ten years I've sold your charms. Ten years I've watched scared people come to my counter shaking, and leave holding something you made, something warm, and stand straighter. You think that was nothing? You think hiding was all you were?" She shook her head hard. "I don't care what your name is. But you should. *You* should. That's the only thing they can't burn."

The lantern light caught the basalt door, and in its sheen Astrid saw themselves reflected—thin, waterlogged, ordinary. The taster's face. The no-one's face. Built stitch by stitch over a decade, mortar over brick over—

Over a name.

They closed their eyes.

It came slowly at first, the way a taste returns after fever. Not *Astrid Ishikawa*, the careful fiction, the papers bought from a forger in the Salt Quarter, the surname chosen because it was common enough to be invisible. Underneath that: a smaller child, standing in a kitchen that smelled of yarrow and smoke, being told by a grandmother whose hands shook, *Never say this aloud, little one. Never write it. Names are hooks, and there are those who fish with them.*

But the grandmother was long dead, and the city was dying, and the winter did not care about caution.

"*Vessandra,*" Astrid said.

The word left them like a tooth leaving the gum—a small bright pain, an absence that ached more than the wound. The water in the chamber shivered. Every worn carving on the door seemed to lean inward, listening.

"*My name is Vessandra.* My mother named me for the thaw. My grandmother taught me the nine herbs and the nine words and the patience of things that grow in the dark. I swallowed poison for a queen because it was the only work a hidden thing could do, and I survived it because—" their voice cracked, steadied— "because I was never nothing. I was never harmless. I was a charm-seller and a liar and a coward and a keeper, and every drop I drank to stay alive, I drank *alive*, and it is mine. All of it is mine. Take it. Take every poison I ever swallowed—the ratbane in my first week, the queen's breakfasts, the arsenic I built into my blood grain by grain like a miser building a fortune. It was my armor. Let it be the key."

They opened their eyes and drew the taster's knife—the little blade they had used for ten years to portion death in careful half-teaspoons—and set its point against the basalt.

And carved.

Not letters. Deeper than letters. The knife moved as if the stone guided it, and each stroke burned cold, and with every line Astrid felt something leave them—not strength, not memory, but the *hiding*. Ten years of practiced smallness peeling away like paint stripped from wood. It hurt the way honesty hurts: not the body, but everything the body had been braced to protect.

*VES SAND RA,* the door read, in strokes that glowed faint blue, like foxfire, like frost-flower, like the first green under snow.

The chamber went silent. Even the pursuit above seemed to hold its breath.

Then the door exhaled.

Warm air came through the seams—actual warmth, grass-smelling, impossibly spring—and the suspended water fell at last, all at once, a curtain of black drops that turned to light as they fell, and the seal that had held for three hundred years came apart not with thunder but with a sound like a great sigh, like a patient finally allowed to sleep.

Above them, through stone and earth and the bones of the palace, the winter paused.

They would learn it later, all of them: how the snow stopped mid-fall over Vell, every flake hanging in the grey air like stars refusing to fall; how the dying crops in the royal glasshouses lifted their heads; how the river, frozen to its bed for two months, shivered and began again. A country holding its breath between one season and another, between one age and another.

But Astrid knew it immediately, in their body, the way they knew poison: the pressure behind the door had eased. The land was no longer dying. It was deciding.

Footsteps on the stair. Many. Then few. Then one.

Halvard came down into the flooded chamber alone, and his witch-hunters waited above—he had ordered them to wait, Astrid realized, ordered them with some pretext, iron and rope and prayer-books idle in their hands. He descended slowly, torchlight sliding off his grey face, and stopped at the edge of the light with his hand resting on the counterweight of his crossbow, though he did not raise it.

He looked at the glowing name on the door. He looked at Astrid, standing before it, knife still in hand, lit from within by their own signature.

"Vessandra," he read aloud, and his voice broke on it, quietly, like ice cracking.

"You knew," Astrid said. "On the stair. You asked me to renounce it. Publicly. You'd let the family live." They turned to face him fully. "You wanted me to do what was done to you."

Halvard's jaw worked. Up close, without his hunters behind him, without his office, he looked less like a witch-finder than like what he was: a charm-seller whose gods had gone silent, who had prayed until praying felt like madness, who had concluded that anything which did not answer deserved to be hunted. Forty years of that arithmetic, wearing him smooth.

"They never answered me," he said. "Thirty years I sold charms. Real ones—I know that now, God forgive me, I know it *now*—and they never once answered. So I decided they were lies. It was easier." His eyes were wet. He did not wipe them. "And then you. A food-taster. A *taster*. And the gods come back the moment you sneeze."

"They didn't come back for me," Astrid said gently. "They came back because the door was failing, and they were dying, and they reached for anyone who would listen. They reached for a beggar. They reached for the queen in her sleep. They would have reached for you, Halvard, if you hadn't spent thirty years shooting at every hand extended toward you."

The crossbow trembled in his grip. Above, voices—his hunters, impatient, calling down questions. He raised one hand without looking, and they quieted.

"Renounce it," he whispered, one last time, and it was not a command anymore. It was a plea. It was a man begging someone else to make the choice he had been too afraid to make. "Say it was trickery. Say it was poison-craft and superstition. They'll let you walk. They'll let all of you walk. I'll see to it. Just—say it isn't real. Say I haven't wasted my whole—"

"No," Astrid said.

Simple. Quiet. The same tone they'd used a thousand times over a thousand breakfasts: *this dish is clean; this dish is death.*

"I spent ten years learning to say 'no, it's nothing' about myself," they said. "About my charms, my name, my grandmother's kitchen, everything I am. It kept me safe and it hollowed me out, and I will not carve that lie into this door on the last day of the country that demanded it." They laid a hand flat against the warm stone, against the glowing syllables of their own self. "I am Vessandra. I am a witch. I am going through this door with my family, and I am not renouncing one hair of it. Not for you. Not for the Crown. Not even for pity—which is what you're actually asking for, Halvard. You want me to make your lie true by joining it. I won't."

For a long moment the witch-finder stood in the falling light of his torch, and the water dripped, and the door breathed spring.

Then Halvard sank to his knees in the floodwater—witch-finder-general of Vell, kneeling before a charm-seller—and wept. Ugly, silent, thirty-years-deep weeping, the grief of a man meeting the god he'd murdered in himself, arriving too late.

"Go," he said at last, hoarse. "Take them. All of them—the strays, the fence, the priest, whoever's down here. Go through." He dragged a sleeve across his face and stood, and when he straightened, something had gone out of him and something else had come in, raw and new and terrible. "I'll hold the stair. I'm good at holding doors." A broken laugh. "Seems fitting."

"Halvard—"

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Don't forgive me yet. Maybe not ever. Just—go. Before I remember who I'm supposed to be."

Astrid crossed to Odessa, took the lantern from her steady hands, and looked back once.

The door stood open.

Beyond the threshold: not darkness. Green hills under a sky the color of new leaves, and figures waiting in the long grass—many-formed, ancient, patient—old gods with open hands and faces like weather, and among them, unmistakably, a woman with shaking hands and the smell of yarrow and smoke, who had been waiting thirty years for her grandchild to come home.

Astrid took Odessa's hand.

"Come on," they said—*Vessandra* said, and meant it, and the name fit like a key fits a lock at last, at last turning. "Everyone through. Spring's on the other side, and it's not waiting forever."

Brother Cassian went first, sobbing prayers into the warm wind. Then Odessa's strays, one by one, blinking, disbelieving. Behind them, up the stair, Halvard's voice rose—flat and iron and absolute: *"None pass. Witch-finder's order. NONE PASS."*—buying them minutes with the only authority he had left.

Astrid stepped onto the threshold, one foot in the drowned dark of Vell and one foot in the impossible green, and paused, looking back at the city that had taught them to be no one—the towers, the bells, the snow hanging frozen mid-air like a held breath, like a country deciding whether to end or begin.

Then they stepped through, and the door began, softly, to close behind them.
