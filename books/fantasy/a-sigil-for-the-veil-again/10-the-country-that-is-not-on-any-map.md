## 10. The Country That Is Not on Any Map

The first thing Astrid felt was grass.

Not the pale, defeated moss of Vell's gutters, not the frost-stiffened stubble of the palace lawns, but true grass, deep-rooted and warm from sun, bending beneath their boots with a sound like a whispered welcome. They stood at the top of a long green slope, and the air—*the air*—smelled of thyme and turned earth and something sweeter underneath, like honey left in an open jar. Above them the sky was a blue so unguarded it made Astrid's chest ache. No smoke-haze. No bell-towers. Just light.

Behind them, the door stood open in the middle of the meadow, which was impossible, but everything here was impossible, and Astrid found they had stopped minding. It was a door built of no stone Astrid knew, veined with gold like the sigils they had carved, and through its frame they could see it still: the flooded dark beneath the palace, the torchlight on black water, Halvard's upturned face as he held the witch-hunters back with his own body, his voice cracking on the words *let them go, let them go, let them go.*

"Close your mouth, taster," Odessa said beside her. "You'll catch flies. Or gods."

Astrid laughed—a rough, disused sound, like a hinge that had never been oiled. "Is that a warning or an invitation?"

"Here?" Odessa spread her arms. Her ledger-scarred hands were bare, her sleeves rolled, and where the winter had gnawed her knuckles raw in Vell, the skin was whole. "Here it's an invitation."

Astrid turned to count them, because counting was what a taster did, because for ten years their whole art had been noticing what might harm the people they served. Odessa, solid and grinning. Tam the scullery boy, sixteen now and weeping openly, his luck-charm—the very first one Astrid had ever slipped him, a bent copper coin stamped with a wren—clutched in his fist and glowing softly, patiently, like an ember that had waited a decade to be acknowledged. The widow Sarei and her two daughters. Old Master Quill from the bindery. The lamplighter Brannoc, who had carried the priest on his back through three flooded galleries without complaint. Twenty-three souls from Charm Row and the palace undercity, twenty-three who had looked at a wanted taster in the market square and chosen not to see a bounty.

And the queen. Isolde stood a little apart, still in her mud-hemmed gown, her crown left somewhere in the dark below Vell. She was looking at the sky the way a prisoner looks at the sea.

"It's spring," she said, to no one. To everyone. "It was the first month of winter, and it's spring."

*It was always spring here,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived the way warmth arrives through a window: without effort, without permission, without weight. *Winter was only the shadow your founders cast over our door.*

Astrid felt them before Astrid saw them—at the bottom of the slope, where the meadow met a river running silver over pale stones. Figures stood in the shallows and along the bank, more figures than the eye could settle on, shapes that shifted like sunlight through leaves. The old gods. Not the grim idols of Vell's forbidden chapels, not the stern faces carved on smuggled charms, but something larger and gentler, the way a mountain is gentle when it holds up the sky.

One of them came up the slope. It wore, for courtesy's sake, the shape of a tall woman with river-reeds in her hair, and its face was kind in a way that hurt to look at, the way home hurts when you have been gone too long.

*Astrid Ishikawa,* it said. *Taster. Charm-seller. Swallower of ten years of poison so that others would not die.*

"You know me," Astrid said. Their voice shook. In Vell, being known had been death; even now, half of them flinched at the words.

*We knew you before you had a name to hide. We watched you make your little charms and call them tricks. We watched you give away luck you did not believe you had.* The god knelt—not in deference, Astrid realized, but simply to be level with them, the way one kneels to a child or a wounded bird. *Every poison you swallowed, we tasted with you. Every charm you sold was a prayer you made without knowing it.*

"I have a name now," Astrid said quietly. "I carved it into the door."

*Yes. And it is yours. We do not take names back, little taster. We never did. That was the lie the founders told—to seal us out, they first had to convince you that being known would destroy you.* The god's eyes were the color of the river. *Being known is the only thing that has ever saved anyone.*

Tam had come up behind them, sniffling, still holding the coin. "It's real," he kept saying. "Miss Astrid, it's really real, it's been real this whole time—" And the god turned its head to him and smiled, and Tam sat down abruptly in the grass, laughing and crying at once, and around the meadow the other gods began, gently, to move among the people of Charm Row like hosts among guests at a wedding.

* * *

Odessa had always measured wealth in obligations. A fence learned early that coin was just metal until someone owed you—or you owed them—and so she had spent forty years weaving a web of small debts across Vell's underbelly: favors banked, names remembered, children fed who would one day hold a gate open. Her ledger, burned in the raid, had held thirty years of those debts, and she had grieved it like a limb.

Now she stood in an impossible meadow watching a god teach Sarei's youngest daughter to skip stones in triple ripples, and understood that the ledger had never been the wealth. The debts had. And every debtor was here.

She found Astrid sitting alone on the slope above the river, knees drawn up, watching the water with the wariness of a person waiting for the bill.

"Funny thing," Odessa said, settling down beside them with the grunt of a woman whose knees were honest about her age. "I spent thirty years selling your work and telling buyers it was rubbish. Snake oil. Novelties." She plucked a stem of grass and chewed it. "Turns out I was underselling the finest craftswoman in Vell. I want a commission renegotiation."

Astrid huffed. "Everything's free here, apparently."

"Nothing's ever free. Someone paid." Odessa's voice went soft. "That boy in there, Halvard. Standing in the dark holding the door for us." She was quiet a moment. "He could have come."

"He wouldn't have fit," Astrid said. "Not yet. Maybe someday, if the door stays—"

"The door stays." Odessa said it with the flat certainty she'd once used to set prices. "I've watched doors my whole life, taster. Locked ones, bribed ones, ones you go through at night with the goods wrapped in sacking. That one doesn't lock. It never locked from this side. That's the whole trick, isn't it? Your founders didn't shut us out of the gods' country. They shut themselves into a cellar and called it a kingdom."

They sat with that. Below, Brannoc the lamplighter was showing a god—which one, Odessa couldn't tell, they all blurred when she wasn't looking directly—how Vell's streetlamps worked, gesturing with both hands, and the god was listening as though lamp-lighting were a holy mystery. Perhaps here it was.

"Astrid." She waited until the taster looked at her. Odessa had one gift she'd never sold, never traded, never put in any ledger: she could say a true thing plainly when it mattered. "Ten years I watched you walk through my shop like a ghost trying to apologize for haunting it. Smaller than small. Invisible by choice." She took Astrid's hand—scarred palm against scarred palm. "Look around you. Every soul on this hill stayed because they saw you. Not the taster. Not the charm-witch. You. Home isn't a city, girl. Home is the people who saw you and stayed."

Astrid's jaw worked. Their eyes were bright and furious with the effort of not weeping, and Odessa, mercifully, looked back at the river and let them.

"I keep waiting," Astrid said at last, hoarse, "to wake up in the kitchen. Waiting for the queen's breakfast, cataloguing the poisons, being no one. It kept me alive for ten years, Odessa. Being no one. I don't know how to stop."

"Well." Odessa stood, brushing off her skirts, and hauled Astrid up by the hand. "Lucky for you, you've got family who know how. Come on. There's a god down there asking after you specifically, and I've never known you to leave a meal uneaten."

* * *

Queen Isolde of Vell walked the length of the meadow alone, because she had spent her whole life learning how to be near people while remaining unreachable, and old habits walked with her.

She had dreamed of this country since childhood. A door beneath the palace; a green light seeping up through the foundation stones; her mother shaking her awake, saying *queens do not dream of doors.* She had ruled for eleven years over a kingdom of ash-weather and fear, signing the witch-hunter commissions because the alternative was losing the throne to men crueler than herself, telling herself every night that she was keeping the country safe—and all the while dreaming, dreaming, of a door.

Now she stood at the riverbank where the water ran clear over gold-flecked stone, and a shape rose out of the shallows wearing the likeness of her dead mother, young, unarmored, hair loose, and Isolde did not flinch, because she understood at last that the gods wore whatever truth a heart needed, and hers needed this: her mother, unafraid.

*You gave up the crown to cross a threshold,* the shape said. *Most rulers cannot.*

"I didn't give it up. I abandoned it. There's a difference, and historians will be unkind about it."

*Historians are not here.* The river-mother smiled. *Your city is dying, Isolde. It was dying before you were born. You could not have saved it. But the people who came through that door—you saved those. One signature forged. One hunt called off at the last hour. One taster warned.*

"One taster I nearly burned," Isolde said. Her voice caught. "She stood in my kitchen tasting my food for ten years, and I never saw her. I summoned her to ask about dreams, and she stood there so carefully empty, and I let her go back downstairs to the dark. If Halvard hadn't exposed her—"

*Then she would have come to you anyway. Some doors take longer.* The shape reached out and touched Isolde's brow, and eleven years of sleepless nights poured out of her like water from a tipped cup. She sat down hard on the riverbank, a queen with no crown and no kingdom, and found, to her astonishment, that she was lighter than she had ever been.

Up the slope, Astrid was walking toward her, slow, careful—the same careful emptiness gone from their face at last, replaced by something Isolde could only call openness, terrifying and radiant.

"Your Majesty," Astrid said.

"I'm not," Isolde said. "Not here. Here I'm only Isolde, who dreamed of a door." She hesitated, then offered the truth like a hand: "I saw you, that day in my chambers. I saw you and I was afraid, because if you were real, then everything I'd signed was murder. So I let you walk away and I burned the summons record. That was my cowardice. I want you to know I've counted it among my poisons."

Astrid studied her—the way Astrid studied everything, cataloguing, weighing, testing for harm. Then: "My name is Astrid Ishikawa. Not 'the taster.' If we're going to build anything here, I'd rather start with names."

"Isolde," the queen said, and heard, in her own ears, the sound of a second door opening.

* * *

Evening came to the meadow the way evening must come in a country with no maps: slowly, generously, the sky going amber then rose then a violet deep enough to hold stars—more stars than Vell had ever permitted, ten thousand cold campfires of the dead gods' kin. The people of Charm Row built fires on the hillside because building fires is the oldest thing grieving, exhausted people do, and Tam had found a stream full of fish too trusting to be believed, and soon the smell of roasting fish and the sound of Sarei's daughters arguing over the last of the bread rolled up the slope like incense.

Astrid sat at the largest fire, Odessa on one side, Isolde on the other, Tam asleep against Astrid's shoulder with his wren-charm still glowing faintly in his fist, and understood, with the calm of a taster who has finally tasted something clean, that this was not an ending. The priest had said it in the dark below the palace, wild-eyed and certain: *a door opens both ways, child, and what comes through depends on which side is hungrier.*

Across the fire, the reed-haired god raised its head from its conversation with Brannoc and looked at Astrid, and its smile was patient and old and full of weather on the far horizon.

*Rest tonight, Astrid Ishikawa,* it said, so only Astrid heard. *Eat. Be seen. Tomorrow we will talk about the other doors—there are sealed countries beyond yours, sleeping cities, founders' lies stacked like stones—and someone with a tongue that knows poison and hands that make charms should be there when they open.*

Astrid looked down at Tam's sleeping face, at Odessa's scarred hands turning a fish-spit, at Isolde laughing at something for the first time in eleven years.

"All right," Astrid said to the god, and meant yes, and meant home, and meant, for the first time in ten years, *me.* "Tomorrow, then."

The stars came out over the country that was not on any map, and the fires on the hillside burned until morning.
