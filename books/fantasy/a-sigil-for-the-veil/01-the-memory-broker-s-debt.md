## 1. The Memory Broker's Debt

The snow in the Thornfell passes did not fall so much as it *remembered* falling — great slow curtains of it, drifting down through a sky the color of an old bruise, settling on the pines like ash on a hearth that had gone cold years ago. Cyrus Brightwater had crossed these mountains eleven times, and each time he told himself the silence up here was only the silence of altitude, thin air and thinner company. But there was a quality to it that no other range possessed. It was the silence of something holding its breath.

He crouched at the mouth of a shrine — one of hundreds scattered along the old pilgrim road, their lintels carved with names no living tongue could pronounce — and listened to a man die.

The pilgrim was young, or had been young once; it was hard to tell beneath the frostbite blooming black across his face like ink dropped in milk. He had come down off the high trail alone, which no sane traveler did after the first snows, and he had been walking for days on legs that no longer answered him properly. When Cyrus found him, propped against the shrine's weathered stone with his pack still strapped to his back as though he'd meant to keep going, the man was trying to speak. His lips moved. The words came out as clicks and wet breath.

"Slowly," Cyrus said, kneeling beside him. "I have time."

Everyone said that to dying men. It was almost never true. But Cyrus Brightwater dealt in time the way other men dealt in coin — he bought it, sold it, cut it into pieces small enough to fit inside another person's skull. A memory broker. There were perhaps a dozen of them left in the borderlands since the wars, men and women who could slip a blade of intention between the folds of a mind and lift out what lay there: a childhood, a grief, a first love, a crime. The desperate paid him to take things away. Collectors paid him better to give those things somewhere else to live.

The pilgrim gripped his own throat with both hands, as if trying to hold himself together from the inside. Then, in a voice that arrived all at once, whole, like a door swinging open:

"You'll want what I'm carrying."

Cyrus had heard that before too. Dying smugglers, dying thieves, dying priests — they all believed their last possession was worth a fortune, and it almost never was. A memory of a wedding. A memory of a harvest. Grief, mostly. Grief was the commonest cargo in the world.

But the pilgrim's eyes were strange. They had gone pale, not with death but *before* it, the irises washing out like dye in rainwater, and behind them something moved that was not quite reflection. Not quite light.

"What are you carrying?" Cyrus asked.

"A name." The man's breath fogged and hung in the air between them far longer than breath should. "A sealed name. You will know the seal when you see it. Everyone who was ever meant to see it knows it."

Cyrus's hands went cold inside his gloves, and it had nothing to do with the mountain.

* * *

The extraction took less than a minute. That was the part he would think about later, in the dark, turning it over like a stone with something alive underneath: how *easy* it was. Eleven years of practice, and he knew within the first touch whether a mind would resist him — whether the memory would cling like a burr or come away clean. This one came away clean. Too clean. It rose out of the dying man as if it had been waiting for the invitation, sliding up the silver filament of Cyrus's craft into the lacquered vial he held against the man's temple, and when it settled there the vial did not glint the way memory-glass was supposed to glint. It drank the light. It held the dark the way a well holds water — deep, patient, with a surface that seemed to watch you back.

Inside the seal, etched so fine he needed the lens screwed into his eyepiece to read it, was a sigil. Interlocking lines, seven of them, drawn in a hand that no human fingers could have steadied well enough to make. And beneath the sigil, in the old script of Thornfell — the script on every ruined shrine, every boundary stone, every door lintel in villages that had stopped saying the words aloud three generations ago — a single word.

Cyrus knew the shape of it. He had known the shape of it his whole life, the way you know the shape of your own name without ever having been taught it. He had never been able to read Thornfell's dead language. No one could. The priests who remembered it had burned or scattered decades back, and the grammar had gone with them, unmoored, orphaned, sleeping.

And yet he looked at the word and his mouth moved around it before his mind gave permission, syllables arriving in his throat like birds coming home to roost.

"*Vaelith.*"

The vial warmed in his palm.

Not the way glass warms near a fire. It warmed from the inside, evenly, deliberately, the way skin warms under a hand. And then — faint, distant, threaded through the whistling wind of the pass like a voice carried up from a valley floor — something answered.

Not in words. In *recognition*. A pressure against the inside of the seal, gentle as a knuckle tapping glass from the other side of a window. Once. Twice. As if to say: *I know you. Do you know me?*

Cyrus sat back on his heels in the snow and did not move for a long moment. The pilgrim died while he sat there, quietly, the way the mountain let people die when it was feeling merciful, and Cyrus closed the man's eyes because someone ought to, and said the rites his mother had taught him — except he had no mother, he had never had a mother, and the rites were ones he could not remember learning, spilling out of him in the old script, perfect and fluent, addressing a god by name.

He noticed that only afterward. He noticed it the way you notice blood on your sleeve — late, and with a lurch.

* * *

The crew found him two hours later at the waypoint hut above Treven's Gap, sitting too still, staring at nothing, the vial wrapped in three layers of wool and tucked against his chest like a second heart.

There were five of them, counting Sera. Sera Voss, the captain, who had pulled him half-frozen out of a canal in Lowmarsh nine years ago and never once asked where he'd come from before that — a courtesy Cyrus understood, because he had no answer that wasn't a blank page. Danneth, who cooked and complained in equal measure and considered both sacred duties. The twins, Osk and Ilva, who could move cargo across a frozen lake at night without leaving a track. And Maren, the youngest, who had named herself his apprentice two winters past and followed him around with the fierce, unembarrassed devotion of a dog that has decided a thing is true.

They were smugglers. That was the honest word for it. They ran goods through the Thornfell passes that the toll-wardens had opinions about — medicine, mostly, and salt, and sometimes people who needed to be somewhere else very badly. They were not good people, most nights. But they were *his*, and he was theirs, and in a country that had forgotten its gods and buried its dead in unmarked rows, that counted for more than goodness ever had.

"Cy." Maren sat down across from him, close, the way she did when she wanted to read his face. "You've got your ghost-look on."

"I lifted a memory today," Cyrus said. "Off a dead pilgrim."

Danneth snorted from the stove. "Was it worth the frostbite?"

"It's a name," Cyrus said. "It's a god's name."

The hut went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has said a thing that everyone has agreed for years not to say. The old gods of Thornfell. Every child in the borderlands grew up on the stories — Vaelith of the Veil, who stood at the border between the quick and the quiet, whose name was spoken only in whispers if at all, who had gone silent a lifetime ago along with all the others, leaving behind shrines and questions and a veil that everyone assumed was permanent because no one dared test it.

"They're stories," Osk said, but his voice had no conviction in it, and everyone heard that.

"I said the name aloud," Cyrus said. "Out loud, in the pass, with the vial open in my hand. And something answered me."

Ilva set down her cup. "Answered you *how*?"

"I don't know how to describe it. Like knocking. From the other side of something." He turned the vial over in his fingers, and even through the wool the warmth of it startled him. "Like something that's been asleep for sixty years, waking up, and the first thing it does is check whether I'm still here."

Maren leaned in until her eyes nearly touched the glass. "What do you see?" she asked. "Inside it."

"I see a door," Cyrus said slowly. "I see a country I've never—" and then he stopped, because the sentence broke apart in his mouth, because the truth arrived sideways and refused to go in straight: *he had seen this door before.* Not one like it. *This one.* The same lintel, the same seven-line sigil cut above the frame, the same crooked birch growing out of the foundation stone on the left side, leaning east. He had never been to the vanished country. No one living had. The maps showed white space and a warning.

And yet he knew the door the way you know the hallway of a house you grew up in. Knew which hinge stuck. Knew the sound the latch made.

Sera watched him from the doorway, arms folded, and said nothing for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and careful, the voice she used when she was deciding something. "Cy. Where did you learn the rites you said over that pilgrim?"

"I don't know," Cyrus said.

"When did you learn to read the old script?"

"I can't read the old script."

"But you said the name perfectly."

"Yes."

Sera looked at him the way a person looks at a lock they suddenly suspect they've had the wrong key for all along. "Then we have a problem," she said quietly, "and it isn't the kind we can run from."

That night the wind came down off the icefield hard enough to shake the hut on its stones, and Cyrus lay awake listening to it, and underneath the wind — so faint he could pretend he hadn't heard it, and did pretend, hour after hour — he could hear it again. The knocking. Patient. Rhythmic. *Still here? Still here? Still here?*

Toward dawn he stopped pretending.

He took the vial out, unwrapped it, and held it up to the grey light coming through the shutter-crack. Inside the seal, beneath the sigil, beneath the name, there was more than he'd seen before. The memory was not empty the way purchased memories were empty — a scene, a feeling, a length of somebody's life rolled up and shelved. This one had *depth*. He tilted it, and deep in the dark, layers down, he caught the flicker of faces. A crowd. A procession. People in the old dress of the vanished country, carrying lanterns toward —

— toward a door he recognized.

And in the front rank of that procession, clear as a struck bell, unmistakable, impossible: a face he knew.

His own.

Younger. Unscarred. Eyes he didn't remember ever having, bright and unafraid, looking back at him out of a memory that belonged to a dead man on a mountain, in a country that had been erased from every map in the world.

The vial knocked again. Three times now. Urgent.

*Cyrus Brightwater stood very still in the cold blue dark of the waypoint hut, and understood, with the calm of a man watching floodwater reach the doorstep, that whatever he had stolen off a dying pilgrim had not been stolen at all.*

*It had been delivered.*

Outside, far below in the valleys, the first fires of the zealots were being lit.
