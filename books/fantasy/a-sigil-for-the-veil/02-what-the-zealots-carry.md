## 2. What the Zealots Carry

The vial knocked a fourth time, and Cyrus set it down on the waypoint table as if it were a sleeping animal he did not want to wake.

Outside the shuttered window, the valleys of Thornfell had begun to glow. Not with dawn — dawn was hours off — but with the orange stutter of burning thatch, one village and then another, like embers dropped down the spine of the mountains. He could smell it even here, even at this height: pine resin and wet stone and something sweeter underneath, the smell of burned wool, burned hair. The smell of people.

*Delivered.* The word would not leave him. Twenty years cutting memories out of desperate skulls had taught him every texture a memory could have — grief like wet sand, love like honeycomb, guilt with teeth. And this thing in the vial had none of those textures. It had *intention*. It had wanted to be found by him specifically, the way a letter wants its addressee, and now that he had opened it, it would not stop knocking.

He lifted it again, carefully, between two fingers. Inside, the stolen light of the pilgrim's memory swirled, and there — unmistakable now, undeniable — stood his own face. Younger. Unscarred. Standing before a door.

Not any door. A door of black wood carved with spirals, set into a hillside that wore its trees like fur, under a sky the color of hammered tin. Behind him in the memory, the land rolled away in terraced fields and slate-roofed villages — a country Cyrus had never seen and yet knew, the way you know the layout of a house from your childhood, the way your feet know stairs in the dark.

Vellamere. The vanished country. Every map in Thornfell showed only mountains where it had been, and every priest said it had never existed at all. But his feet remembered those stairs. His mouth remembered the taste of the water there, cold and mineral-bright, tasting faintly of iron and glacier.

"Who are you," he whispered to the face in the vial, and the face looked back at him with an expression he recognized because he saw it every morning: the careful blankness of a man deciding what to show the world.

Then the voice came again, deeper than the first time, no longer content to be felt through glass. It moved through the hut like cold air under a door.

**YOU ARE THE LETTER.**

"I'm not a letter." His voice cracked on it. "I'm a man. I cut memories for coin. I drink too much. I owe money in three towns."

**YOU WERE WRITTEN TO CARRY ME HOME.**

The vial went warm in his hand. Warm as a living thing. And beneath his ribs, in some place he had never thought to inventory, Cyrus felt an answering warmth — a second heartbeat, patient and vast, keeping time slightly behind his own.

He put the vial in his coat, over his heart, because nowhere else felt safe, and that frightened him more than the fires below.

* * *

They came down out of the passes at dusk, and the zealots were already waiting.

Cyrus saw them first as a procession of lights descending the switchbacks — dozens of torches moving in perfect unison, which no human procession does, and that was how he knew what they were before he heard the singing. The Ashen Choir, the old women in the valley taverns called them, when they spoke of them at all, in the lowered voices reserved for plague and weather. Worshippers of the Sleeper Under Stone, they called their god. The god whose name Cyrus now carried against his chest like contraband.

Their song reached him across the darkening slopes: wordless, harmonized in fifths, beautiful the way an avalanche is beautiful. Each torchbearer's face was smeared with ash. None of them looked up at the stars. They walked as if the ground were telling them where to step, and perhaps it was.

Behind them, the villages burned methodically. Not looted — *purified*. He watched three figures move through a farmhouse with unhurried grace, and when they emerged, they carried nothing but handfuls of ash, which they scattered behind them like seed.

And in the lead, walking barefoot on frozen scree without leaving prints, was a tall figure in a robe of stitched grey, faceless beneath its hood except for a mouth.

Only a mouth.

It was singing too, and when its lips parted, the whole choir's voices bent toward whatever note it chose, obedient as iron filings.

Cyrus pressed himself flat against the rocks above the trail and did not breathe until the procession passed below him, close enough that he could hear individual words surfacing in the wordless song:

*Return. Return. The letter comes home. The seal is broken. Return.*

His heart slammed against the vial, and he swore he felt the vial slam back.

* * *

Sera Voss had built her crew the way masons build arches — each person a stone leaning on the others, nothing standing alone. She found Cyrus half-dead of fever in a Saltmarsh gutter eleven years ago, paid his debts without asking what they were, and gave him the two things no memory-broker ever gets: a bed that was his, and people who expected him home for supper. Tam with his endless card tricks. Old Maren who navigated by smells. Juno who never spoke but laughed like breaking ice.

Which is why, when Sera met him at the rendezvous cave above Hollowmere with her hand resting openly on her knife and her eyes refusing his, Cyrus understood the shape of what was coming before she said a word.

"You brought it down the mountain," she said.

"Sera—"

"The Choir hit Kettlebridge yesterday. Burned it. Forty souls, Cyrus. They asked one question in every house." Her jaw worked. "*Where is the broker who walked the high pass?*"

"They're not asking because of me—"

"Aren't they?" For the first time in eleven years she raised her voice at him, and it echoed wrong in the cave, flattened, as though the mountain itself were listening. "You don't know what you took. Maren read the ash-lines. Juno won't sleep. Whatever's riding in that little glass heart of yours, it's calling to things older than my grandmother's grandmother, and it's using *you* as a bell."

Cyrus touched his coat. "I can give it back. Bury it. There are places in the deep fells—"

"It's too late for burying." Something moved behind her eyes then — fast, swallowed, gone. Guilt, or grief, or both wearing the same mask. "There are buyers, Cyrus. Very old buyers who pay in safety instead of coin. They want the sigil. I told them where you'd be."

The words landed like a blade slid gently between ribs, so clean it took a moment to hurt.

"You sold me."

"I saved everyone I could." Her voice finally broke on the last word, and for one heartbeat she was just a tired woman in a cold cave, and Cyrus almost reached for her. Then the mask came back down, and Captain Voss stood where Sera had been. "Walk east. Don't run — running leaves sign. If you make it past the Saltmarsh, you were never one of us."

"That's not how family works, Sera."

"No," she agreed quietly. "Family is how I know exactly what this will cost me." She turned her face away. "Go. Before I have to watch them take you."

He walked. He did not look back, because looking back is how men die in stories, and because if he had seen her face he might have stayed, and staying meant the Choir finding forty more Kettlebridges.

* * *

He fled east all night, and the sigil sang against his chest.

Not the god's deep voice now — something smaller, slyer, almost tender. Fragments kept rising in him unbidden: the smell of bread from a bakery that stood in a country that no longer existed. A woman's hands kneading dough, a scar across one knuckle. A child laughing on slate steps. His mother's hands? His own childhood? He had no childhood. He had Saltmarsh, fever, Sera. Everything before that was fog, and he had always assumed the fog was ordinary forgetting.

Now the fog was full of architecture.

Near dawn he stopped in a hollow of dead bracken, shook the vial loose, and held it up against the greying sky. And this time he did not merely look — he *entered*, the way he entered other people's memories for a living, sliding his awareness into the amber light.

The door again. Black wood, spiral-carved, humming. His younger self standing before it, key raised — and now, because he was inside the memory rather than outside it, Cyrus could see what the dying pilgrim's perspective had hidden.

There were others in the scene. Figures at the edge of the frame, watching the young man at the door with reverence: a woman with ash-smeared cheeks, a broad-shouldered man carrying a lantern that burned without fuel, a child holding someone's hand.

Faces he knew.

Maren's face. Tam's. Juno's — young, speaking aloud, laughing.

And Sera's, unlined and fierce, standing closest of all.

The memory tilted. The young man at the door turned, and his eyes found the point of view itself, found *him*, and smiled with terrible kindness.

"We made you well," his own mouth said, in a voice like the god's cold depths filtered through a human throat. "We made you from everything we loved. Now come home, and give us back our name."

The vial shattered with cold in his fist.

No — not shattered. *Opened*. The memory poured out of the glass and into him like water into cracks, and the last thing Cyrus saw before the world whited out was the eastern horizon, where the vanished country's mountains stood up out of the morning mist like the backs of sleeping animals —

— and above them, impossibly, smoke rising from a single chimney. Someone was home.

Someone was lighting a fire for him.

Behind him, far to the west, the Choir's song changed key, and began to follow.
