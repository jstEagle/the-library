## 4. The Sigil and the Veil

The door stood where the country had been.

Cyrus had expected ruin — a shattered arch, a burned lintel, something to mark the wound the world had made when it erased a nation from its own maps. Instead there was only a door, freestanding in the snow of the pass, oak-dark and iron-banded, with no wall behind it and no house around it. Just a door, patient as a held breath. Beyond it, where a threshold should have opened onto rooms, there was a color that had no name: the pale gold of memory before it is cut, before it is bled into glass and sold by weight.

The snow did not fall on it. The wind went around it. Even the light of the dying afternoon bent slightly, as if ashamed to touch it.

Behind him, he heard them coming up the trail — boots in crusty snow, the jingle of Sera's harness buckles, Tam's wheezing breath, the soft clink of the crew's few shared possessions in their packs. They had refused to stay below at the treeline. He had argued until his voice cracked, and then Sera had looked at him the way she looked at storms she intended to sail through anyway, and he had stopped arguing.

"You could have left me in that cellar," he had told her on the climb, when the guilt finally broke him open. "When you sold me out."

"I know," she'd said.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you're mine," she said, as simply as reporting the weather. "I don't leave what's mine in cellars."

Now they stood in a ragged line behind him — Sera, Tam, old Wren with her hands wrapped in wool against the cold, the twins Ferro and Lys with their matched knives drawn for no reason either of them could name. Five people who had chosen a man who was not a man, standing in the shadow of a door that led to the truth of him.

*Come,* said the voice inside the sigil, which had lived behind his ribs since the night he cut it from a dying pilgrim's skull. *You carried me faithfully. Come through.*

Cyrus stepped forward. The cold fell away from him like a coat slipping from his shoulders, and he understood that this was the border, drawn not across mountains but across meaning, and that he had been walking along its edge his whole fabricated life without knowing it.

* * *

The god was waiting on the other side, and it wore Cyrus's face.

Not exactly. It wore the idea of him — smoothed, idealized, the way a sculptor imagines a face from a lover's description. It stood in a hall made of everything Thornfell used to be: hearth-smoke and harvest songs, the smell of bread and river-mud, a hundred thousand small remembered mornings stacked like masonry into walls that reached toward a sky stitched shut with gold thread. The veil. He could see it now, close up — not a curtain but a scar, a seam of divine needlework holding two worlds apart, fraying at every stitch.

"You are my finest theft," the god said, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "I gathered the memories of a nation and braided them into flesh, and I set you loose among the living so that you would find your way home carrying my name. And you have. Kneel, little vessel, and I will show you what you were for."

"I remember," Cyrus said.

And he did — all of it arriving at once now that he stood inside the country that had made him. The workshop of stolen recollections. The hands — whose hands? — pressing memories into clay and singing them awake. The purpose written into his marrow: cross the border, reach the door, tear the seam. His twenty-nine years of smuggling and card games and bad wine and Sera's terrible cooking were scaffolding, camouflage, a disguise he hadn't known he was wearing.

It should have unmade him. It nearly did. He felt the grief come up like floodwater, felt the vertigo of being a story someone else had written.

Then he heard Tam shouting his name from the other side of the door, and the flood found its banks.

"No," Cyrus said.

The god tilted its borrowed head. "No?"

"You gave me two roads, I know. I've heard them both ringing in my skull since the passes." He held up his hand, and the sigil rose from his palm — a knot of light and geometry, the god's true name folded into a shape small enough to hold. "Erase myself, restore the veil, and my crew walks free. Or open it, keep everything, and let the world pay the bill. But you made a mistake when you built me, old one."

"Tell me my mistake."

"You built me out of *their* memories too." His voice shook, and he let it. "Sera teaching me knots on the deck of the *Kestrel*. Wren stitching my arm after the Marrow Road job. Tam laughing so hard he snorted wine through his nose. You wanted a vessel full of a dead nation, but vessels fill up with whatever pours into them, and I have been pouring these people into myself for nine years. So no. I'm not your memory anymore. I'm mine."

He turned the sigil over in his hand, and saw, for the first time, the flaw running through it like a vein in marble — the hairline crack where a dying pilgrim's terror had fractured the god's perfect name. Everything since had poured through that crack. Every voice that answered back. Every moment the memory had felt *familiar*, because it wasn't only the god's. It had been broken open, and broken things can be shared.

"You cannot split a name," the god said, and for the first time it sounded afraid.

"You can if it's already broken," Cyrus said. "Watch."

* * *

He called them through.

Later, none of them could fully describe what the ghost-country was like; Wren said it was like walking through a house after a funeral, everything present and nothing inhabited. But they came — Sera first, because Sera would follow him anywhere she couldn't talk him out of going, and then the others, crowding through the impossible door into a hall of remembered light.

"Hold out your hands," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus—" Sera started.

"It's going to hurt. Maybe a lot. I won't order you. I've never had the right to order any of you, and that's the point."

Sera looked at the sigil burning in his palm. She looked at the god wearing his face. Then she pulled off her gloves, took his hand, and laced her fingers through his, and the others followed — Tam gripping his shoulder, Wren's wool-wrapped hands closing over his wrist, Ferro and Lys each taking a finger like children holding onto something in a storm.

"Now what?" Tam whispered.

"Now we share."

He broke the sigil.

It came apart the way a chord comes apart into notes — six threads of light leaping from his hand into theirs, one for each of them, and one remaining knotted in his own chest where the voice had lived. Six people carrying one god's name between them, and no single bearer enough to open or seal anything. The god screamed — a sound like a bell cracking — and the golden seam overhead shuddered, strained, and then settled into something new: not whole, not torn, but *mended badly*, the way sailors mend a sail, strong enough to hold and ugly about it.

The hall of memories began to fade around them, the dead country exhaling at last. The god's face — his face — grew dim and distant, and the last thing it said was not a threat but a question, almost wondering:

"What *are* you?"

Cyrus smiled through the tears freezing on his cheeks. "Free."

* * *

They walked out of the mountains three days later, moving slow because Wren's knees were bad and nobody minded. The world on the far side of the pass looked the same and was not: the light fell differently, faintly gold-tinged, and sometimes at dusk Cyrus caught a smell of bread and river-mud from nowhere, a leak in the imperfect seam, the dead country breathing through the mend. Small hauntings. Bearable ones.

His own memories had changed. He could feel it — the god's braided past thinning out of him, and in its place, settling in like sediment in still water, the ordinary treasure of his own life: the *Kestrel*'s deck under his bare feet, the taste of Tam's terrible stew, the sound of Sera humming off-key in the dark. Memories he had chosen. Memories that had chosen him back.

On the last morning, at the treeline, Sera fell into step beside him and handed him a cup of tea, and said, casually, the way she said enormous things:

"The sigil in your chest. Can you still hear it?"

He listened. Far away, behind the mended veil, something old and patient turned over in its sleep — and murmured, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, a word that was almost his name.

"Yeah," Cyrus said, and drank his tea. "It says thank you."

But that night, camping under a sky threaded faintly with gold, he dreamed of a door standing alone in the snow — and this time, when he looked closer, the door was opening from the other side.
