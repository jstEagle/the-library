## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Wendell had stood the night watch at the north face of the valley for six years, and in all that time the most remarkable thing that had ever happened was a fox.

A bold one, in the third winter, that had come down to the wardline to dig at frozen ground and given them something to do for an hour. Wendell had thrown a stone at it. The fox had left. That was the sum of the valley's wars, and Wendell had told themselves, night after night, that they were grateful for it.

Tonight the fox was gone. Tonight everything was gone — the rabbits, the nightbirds, even the wind, which had died an hour past sundown like a candle pinched between wet fingers. The air sat on the fields without moving. Wendell's breath made no cloud, though the cold had teeth.

And the hum under the wardstone was wrong.

They had learned its rhythm the way a shepherd learns a flock — by feel, by years, without ever naming it. A steady pulse, deep and patient, the sound of a promise being kept. Wendell had pressed their palm to the stone every night of their service and felt it answer, faint and faithful as a sleeping animal's heart.

Now it stuttered. Now it *skipped*. And between the skips, Wendell could feel — or imagined they could feel, and the imagining was worse — something on the other side of the stone. Not pressing. *Listening.*

"Say something," Wendell muttered to it. To the stone, to Vael, to the whole mute architecture of the valley's safety. "You've never said anything in a hundred years, but say something now."

The stone said nothing. The stone had never said anything. That was the covenant's great joke, the one the elders never laughed at: a god who had gone silent, and a valley so quiet even the god's silence had gone quiet.

Wendell pulled their cloak tighter and began the patrol circuit anyway, because the circuit was the one thing in the world that always behaved. North face to the standing stone at Wolf's Rest, Wolf's Rest down to the ford, the ford along the low wall to the eastern marker. Twelve hundred paces. They had walked it in rain, in snow, in the fever-dream of a broken ankle. Their boots knew it better than their mother's voice.

They were four hundred paces in when the bell rang again.

Not the valley bell — they'd left that behind, down in Hush proper, its sound arriving late and softened by distance. This was something else. A sound that came up through the soles of their boots and out of the ground itself, a single deep note with no source, as if the whole valley were a bell and something had struck it.

Wendell stopped. The fields around them lay silverless under a cloudless sky, every star burning in its ancient place — and Wendell counted them without meaning to, the way you count the exits of a room you're starting not to trust. The Shepherd's Yoke. The Seven Sisters. The pale smear of the River of Heaven.

The note faded. The hum under the earth resumed its broken rhythm.

*Skip. Skip. Beat.*

Wendell walked faster.

At the ford, the water had gone still — not slow, not calm, but *still*, the way water is still in a cup. Every stone on the riverbed visible and sharp, the surface holding the starlight like a held mirror. Wendell crouched at the bank and looked at their own reflection and thought, with a clarity that arrived like cold water down the collar: *this is what it looks like when something is about to end.*

They had grown up on the elders' stories, the same as everyone. The Silence of Vael. The Wards of the Founding. The covenant that kept the dark beyond the ridge and the magic beyond the dark, so that the Valley of Hush could be a place where nothing ever happened, forever. Wendell had never believed in the dark the way the elders wanted — had never believed in *anything* the way the elders wanted — but they had believed in the wards the way you believe in the floor beneath your feet. Not faith. Physics. The ground holds. You build your life on that.

The ground was not holding.

Wendell stood, and that was when they saw the stranger.

* * *

The figure came out of the eastern dark like a wound opening — no, like a *stitch* being pulled, the night's fabric puckering and tearing around a shape that should not have been able to cross the wardline at all. Wendell's hand went to their sword before their mind caught up. Nobody crossed the border. Nobody had crossed the border in living memory. The wards did not permit it; the wards were the whole point of everything.

The figure staggered. Fell to one knee on the near bank of the ford, twenty paces off. Wendell saw then that it was a woman, or shaped like one, dressed in travel-stained leathers the color of ink, and that her hands were pressed flat against the ground the way Wendell's were always pressed against the wardstone — as if she were listening too. As if she were *holding* something.

"You can't be here," Wendell said, and their voice came out smaller than they wanted. "The wards—"

"The wards are dying," the woman said. Her voice was low and rough, like a blade drawn slowly. She lifted her head, and Wendell saw her eyes — dark, exhausted, and lit from within by a faint moving light, as if someone had written small glowing letters on the inside of her irises. "I need a word with whoever maintains this line, and I needed it three days ago."

"I maintain this line."

"Then you're three days late." She tried to stand and failed, and Wendell saw the reason: sigils. Marks inked or scarred or *burned* into her forearms, dozens of them, intricate as frost on glass — and they were fraying. Even across the distance Wendell could see it, the lines unraveling like thread pulled from a hem, and with each line that failed she seemed to lose a little more of whatever was holding her upright. "The covenant is broken. It broke a century ago and everyone in this blessed, buried valley has been sleeping inside a corpse ever since, and the corpse is finally—"

The wards shattered.

Wendell would spend the rest of their life failing to describe the sound. It was not an explosion. It was the *opposite* of an explosion — a sound like the whole world inhaling at once, every hum and whisper and heartbeat in the valley sucked into a single point above the north ridge. Wendell felt it take their breath, felt it pull the river flat, felt it drag the stranger's head back like a hand in her hair. For one impossible instant the entire border stood visible — a lattice of pale light arching over the valley, ridge to ridge, beautiful as the inside of a geode, the thing that had held their whole life together revealed at the moment of its death.

Then it came down.

The light did not shatter so much as *go out*, section by section, north to south, like windows going dark in a house at night. And with each section that died, the stars above it went out too.

Wendell did not understand that. They would never understand that. The stars were a hundred thousand years away and the wards were a hundred feet up, and when the wardline fell the sky fell with it, darkness sweeping across heaven in the same pattern as the light dying below, star after star after star winking out like a city going black behind a marching storm. The Shepherd's Yoke, gone. The Seven Sisters, gone. The River of Heaven, gone — and not covered, not clouded, *gone*, erased, so that Wendell looked up into a sky with nothing in it at all, a black like the inside of a closed eye, a black with a texture, a black that was *looking back*.

They heard themselves scream. They didn't remember deciding to.

The dark came down off the ridge like water finding a crack. Wendell felt it before they saw it — a cold that was not cold, a pressure that was not weight, the specific sensation of the valley's long stillness *dying*, the held breath finally, catastrophically released. Every wardstone they had ever touched was screaming now, a frequency below sound, and the magic-less silence they had hated their whole life tore open like a scab, and underneath it the world was *loud*, full of forces and pressures and hungers that had been waiting at the border for a hundred years with their faces pressed to the glass.

The stranger was still on her knees at the ford. The dark was coming straight down the slope toward her, toward the place where the wardline had failed first, and Wendell understood with a soldier's flat clarity that she could not run, that whatever she was and whatever she'd done, the dark would reach her in ten heartbeats and there was no one in the valley who even knew she was here except Wendell.

Wendell did not think. That was the thing they would return to, later, in the long gray country beyond the ridge — that for once in their careful, restless, rule-keeping life, they had not thought at all.

They ran.

The ground was shaking. The ford's still water had begun to move again, all at once, violently, as if the river had woken from a nightmare, and Wendell hit it at a dead sprint and the cold went through their boots like knives. Ten paces. Five. The stranger's head came up, those ink-lit eyes going wide.

"What are you—" she started.

Wendell got a fist in her collar and hauled.

She was lighter than she looked and fighting them, which was the problem — twisting, snarling, *still pressing her palms to the ground*, still trying to hold a line that no longer existed. "The anchor—there's an anchor stone, I can stabilize—"

"It's gone!" Wendell shouted, and dragged her up, and the dark came down the bank behind them like a tide with a face in it — not a face, not really, but a suggestion of one, the way a crowd at distance suggests a single body, a vast slow pressure of *attention* that made Wendell's knees try to fold. "There's nothing to anchor to! *Move!*"

They moved. Wendell half-carried, half-dragged her off the ford and up the near slope, and the dark poured into the space they'd left and *stopped* — halted at the waterline as if the river were a wall, churning there, pressing, the face-shape leaning over the water like a drunk over a fence. Wendell did not stop to wonder why it stopped. Later they would learn the stranger had thrown something, a sigil, a last coin of her own power spent like a life's savings to buy them thirty feet of river. Later.

Right then there was only the running, and the black sky, and the valley screaming in a register beneath hearing, and the woman in their grip who weighed less than she should and burned cold as a banked fire.

They made the tree line at Wolf's Rest before Wendell's legs gave out. They went down together behind the standing stone, in the dark, in the noise, and for a long moment neither of them could do anything but breathe.

* * *

The valley was still screaming when Wendell found their voice.

"What did you do?"

The stranger sat against the standing stone with her head tipped back, her frayed arms limp in her lap. In the starless dark her sigil-lit eyes were the only light in the world, two small moving constellations in a face gray with exhaustion. "What did *I*—"

"The wards. The sky. Everything." Wendell's sword was out and shaking, and they hated it, hated the shaking, hated how young it made them sound. "You crossed the line. Nobody crosses the line. And the same hour you cross it, a hundred years of silence comes down on our heads. So I'll ask you once, plainly. What did you do?"

She looked at them for a long time. Wendell had faced down drunk mercenaries and a bull elk in rut and their own captain's disappointment, and none of it had prepared them for being *looked at* like that — like they were a page being read, closely, by someone who already suspected how the story ended.

"My name is Ilyra," she said. "I am — I *was* — a sigil-smith of the Order of the Written Vow. And I did not break your wards, mercenary. I came to warn you they were breaking. There's a difference, and it happens to be the only thing I have left." She lifted one ruined arm; the fraying sigils guttered. "The covenant died a hundred years ago, the night your god went silent. Everything since has been the echo. And tonight—"

She stopped. Her eyes went past Wendell, up, to the starless sky, and Wendell watched something cross her face that they had never seen on an elder's face and had never once felt in their own chest, in all their years of restless, aching, unmagical life.

Wendell recognized it anyway. It was grief. The specific, personal grief of someone standing in the ruins of their own faith.

"Tonight," Ilyra said quietly, "the echo ran out."

Above the ridge, where the north face of the border had stood since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, the darkness was gathering. Not passing over. Not moving on. *Settling* — the way snow settles, the way dust settles in a house where no one lives anymore. And down in the valley proper, Wendell could see lights moving, lanterns, torches, the whole of Hush spilling out of its doors into the streets to look up at a sky with nothing in it. They could imagine Brannoc's face. They could imagine the elders already reaching for the old scrolls, the old reassurances, the old lie that the silence had always held and would hold still.

The bell began to ring. Wild and unbroken and pulled by no rope, tolling and tolling over the valley like a funeral for the world, and Wendell sat down in the dead grass beside a stranger with dying sigils on her arms and a god's silence in her voice, and put their head back against the standing stone, and looked up into the black.

"Vael," they said. It was not a prayer. It had never been a prayer. It was the thing you said when the ground gave way — the name of the floor that wasn't there.

For the first time in a hundred years, something answered.

It was not a voice. It was a *pull* — faint, distant, and warm, like a single coal breathing in a cold hearth, rising up from the direction of the valley's heart, from the old shrine on its hill. Wendell felt it in their sternum, in the place where their restless ache had lived their whole life, and the ache turned over like a sleeper, like something waking, like something that had been waiting for exactly this night to be needed.

Ilyra's head snapped around. Her ink-lit eyes went wide.

"You felt that," she said. It was not a question. Her hand closed on Wendell's wrist, hard, her frayed sigils flaring. "Mercenary — *what are you?*"
