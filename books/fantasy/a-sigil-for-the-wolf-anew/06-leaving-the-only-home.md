## 6. Leaving the Only Home

The censer was heavier than it looked.

Ilyra had forged it overnight in the cold of the ruined shrine, hammering lead around a core of ash and old covenant-iron, and when she placed it in my hands at dawn she held on a moment longer than she needed to, as if testing whether I would flinch.

"Lead keeps secrets," she said. "Even from the dark. Even from gods. What's inside will stay inside until you choose to open it."

Inside was Vael. Or what remained of Vael — an ember no bigger than a moth's egg, warm as a sleeping bird against the stone where it had waited out three generations of silence. I had pressed my palm to that stone the night before and felt it beat, faint and stubborn, like a pulse heard through too many layers of cloth. My god, alive after all. Small enough to hold. Small enough to lose.

I had spent my whole life praying into that silence. Now the silence had handed me something back, and I did not know whether it was a gift or a debt or simply the last breath of a dying thing asking to be carried somewhere it could matter.

Brannoc stood at the shrine door when we left it, his gray beard wet with morning fog, his sword — the one he'd sworn never to draw against a valley-man — sharpened to a brightness I didn't like. He had stopped burning letters two days ago. Now he was writing them.

"You'll walk out that gate," he said, not looking at me but at the ridge road beyond, "and you won't look back. Looking back is how people die on roads. They see where they've been and forget where they're going."

He was lying, of course. Brannoc lied the way old soldiers breathe — constantly, efficiently, without malice. He wanted me to look back. He wanted me to look back and turn around and put the censer down and pick up a spear and stand on the wall with him when whatever was coming finally came over the ridge. He had buried a wife and a son in this valley's soil. He would die in it gladly. He could not understand why I wouldn't.

I understood him perfectly. That was the problem.

* * *

We went up through the terraces while the fog still clung to the barley like a second crop. The valley was quiet in the way it had always been quiet — not peaceful quiet, but held quiet, the hush of a room where someone has put a finger to their lips for so long that everyone has forgotten why. No birds sang past the second terrace. The wind came down off the peaks and died at the wardline like a traveler refused at a door.

People watched us pass. They tried not to. Old Maren stood in her doorway with her hands full of wool she wasn't spinning, and when I nodded to her she looked through me as if I were already gone. The cooper's boys were stacking staves by the millrace and one of them — Tam, who used to steal apples from our orchard when we were both small enough to fit in the same tree — straightened up and stared at the censer swinging at my hip, at the leaden dullness of it, at me, and then went back to his work very fast, as if staring too long might make him next.

Word travels fast in a valley with nowhere else to go. By the time we reached the upper pasture everyone knew: the Joubert mercenary, the one who'd pulled a stranger out of the collapsing wardline, was leaving with the stranger, carrying something in a box that no one was allowed to ask about.

They didn't ask. That was its own kind of answer. In the Valley of Hush we had been taught for a hundred years that questions about the outside were how you got hurt. We had perfected the art of not asking. It was the closest thing we had to a virtue, and watching them practice it on me now — watching them let me go without a single word of protest, without a single hand raised to stop me — I felt the full weight of what that perfection cost. A valley that cannot ask questions also cannot say *stay*.

Except one person said it.

She caught us at the sheep gate above the high pasture, breathing hard from the climb, her gray braid half-undone, her good shawl pinned with the copper brooch she'd worn to every harvest festival since before I was born. Aunt Sef. Not blood-aunt — valley-aunt, which in Hush meant more, because valley-kin was chosen kin, the family you were given instead of born to. She had fed me through the winter my father died. She had taught me to read using a psalter whose god had stopped listening before either of us was born. She was seventy-one years old and she had walked up a mountain at dawn to tell me a thing nobody else in that valley would say aloud.

"Come back," she said. "Whatever you're doing out there — come back."

"I can't promise that," I said, and hated myself for saying it, and hated her a little for making me say it.

"No," she agreed, fierce and gentle at once. "You can't. Nobody can. That's what's different now." She touched the censer once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you'd been told not to wake. "My grandmother remembered when the bell rang true. She said the whole valley wept for a day and then went back to work, because that's what we do here. We grieve quickly and work long." Her eyes were wet and her voice was dry. "Grieve quickly, Wendell. Work long. And if there is anything left of that silent god of ours worth saving — save it. Don't come home to a graveyard and call that faith."

Then she turned around and walked back down the mountain, and she did not look back either, and I understood that she was giving me permission by refusing to need it.

* * *

The wardline crossed the road at the saddle below Wolfjaw Peak, marked by twelve stones that had stood longer than any name anyone could remember. As children we'd dared each other to touch them. Touching was allowed. Crossing was not. There was a story about a shepherd boy who'd stepped past the stones on a dare and come back wrong — come back *quiet*, speaking in a voice that wasn't his, until the elders had walked him back across the line backward, blindfolded, reciting words from a book none of them could read anymore. I had believed that story completely for eleven years of my life. I had believed all the stories completely. Believing them was the price of living inside them.

Now I stood at the stones with a leaden god on my shoulder strap and an exile at my side, and the wards were dead, and the stories were just stories, and the only thing between me and the rest of the world was a step.

Ilyra waited three paces ahead, on the far side, and I noticed — I couldn't help noticing — that she had crossed without ceremony, without even seeming to notice the line existed. To her it was nothing. A boundary like any other. To me it was the edge of everything I had ever been.

"The stars," I said. "Beyond the line. Are they really there?"

She looked up. It was midmorning, gray cloud, no stars visible anywhere, and yet she answered as if the question made perfect sense, because to her it did.

"They're there. They've always been there. Your sky isn't missing stars, Wendell. It's missing *everything*. This valley has been looking at a painted ceiling for a hundred years and calling it heaven." She paused, and something gentler moved under her voice. "It's a beautiful painting. I'll give you that. Whoever made your wards loved this place. You can feel it in the work — every line drawn like a vow. But a painting is not a sky."

I knew she was right. I had known it since the night the black came down, since the moment the stillness died and I heard wind move through the valley for the first time in my life and realized that what I had always taken for peace was actually a held breath. Knowing didn't make the step smaller.

Here is what no one tells you about thresholds: they are not lines. They are moments. The stone markers were just geography. The real threshold was the half-second between lifting my foot and setting it down, and in that half-second every version of my life that would never happen now passed through me at once — the version where I took over Maren's flock when her knees gave out, the version where I made captain when Brannoc's hip finally failed him, the version where I grew old in the house I'd been born in and was buried under the rowan tree beside my parents with a carved wolf on the headstone because we are all wolves in Hush, we just live like sheep.

All of those versions of me stood on the near side of the line and watched me lift my foot.

I set it down on the far side.

Nothing happened. That was the cruelest part. No thunder, no tearing, no voice crying out that I had abandoned my post. The world simply continued, indifferent, and the line behind me became just a line, and I became just a person standing in a place I had never stood, and the vertigo of that — the sheer unremarkable enormity of it — nearly buckled my knees.

"Wendell." Ilyra's hand on my arm. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're gasping in rhythm. It's different." She steered me onto the switchback trail, downhill, away. "Everyone crosses twice. Once with the body, once with everything else. The second crossing takes longer."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a granite shoulder maybe four miles out — four miles, and I had never in my life been four miles from my own door — and it was there, eating hard bread with the censer between us like a third guest, that I turned around and saw it.

The valley was graying.

Not darkening. Graying. Color draining out of it the way warmth drains out of a body, starting at the edges and working inward. The barley terraces that had been gold-green at noon were now the color of old bone. The river that ran silver through the middle of it ran pewter. The roofs of Hush-town, red-brown clay my whole life, were fading toward the gray of ash, and the smoke rising from the evening fires rose gray too, and the mountain wall that had always worn its forest like a green cloak wore it now like a shroud.

And it was crumbling. Not fast — not dramatically, not the way things fall in ballads. Slowly, softly, the way snow loses its shape. The watchtower on the eastern ridge had lost a corner sometime in the last hour; I could see the gap where stone had simply ceased, quietly, without falling, without rubble, as if it had been subtracted rather than broken. A barn roof sagged in a way roofs don't sag. The bell tower still stood, but it stood slightly wrong, slightly less, a thing being erased by degrees by an artist with infinite patience and no mercy.

"It's the afterlife of the wards," Ilyra said, watching it with me, her voice flat with a grief that wasn't hers. "A ward is a promise, and promises have momentum. Yours kept holding for three days after the power behind them died, the way a wheel spins after you stop pushing. Now the spin is running out." She glanced at the censer. "Everything the wards were holding up is coming down. Everything they were keeping out—" she pointed at the gray creeping along the ridgelines, at the colorless haze pooling in the high passes like fog with no moisture in it, "—is coming in."

"And the spark?" I asked. "Is it the wards' death? Is carrying it out what's killing the valley?"

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I asked again, harder.

"Don't," she finally said. "Don't build that story. It will eat you alive, and you'll deserve better than it. The valley was already dying — the night the stars went out, the night the wards shattered, before you ever touched that stone. The ember in the censer isn't the cause. If anything, it's the opposite. It's the one piece of this place that refuses to die. Everything else is letting go. That—" she laid two fingers on the lead, and beneath them I swear I felt a warmth answer, faint, insistent, "—that is still trying."

I wanted to believe her. I needed to believe her. And I noticed, with a clarity that frightened me, that wanting and needing had started to blur — that I was beginning to take her word for things because her word was all I had, out here, where my own knowledge ended at a wardline and the world began. She could be lying. She had admitted to being exiled, disgraced, implicated in something terrible. Every hour I spent beyond the line was another hour in which my survival depended on the honesty of a woman my valley would have hanged.

But she had pulled me out of a collapsing wardline at risk to herself. She had slept in the open rather than let me travel alone at night. She had not once, in five days, asked me for the censer. And watching her watch my valley die — watching this outsider grieve a home that was never hers, grieve it honestly, helplessly, with tears she didn't bother to hide because hiding them would have been a lie and she had promised me, the night before we left, that she was done lying to me — I decided, provisionally, the way you decide anything when the ground has fallen away beneath you, to trust her. Provisionally. One day at a time. Trust as a practice rather than a possession, because possessions could be stolen and practices had to be chosen, daily, again.

That was the first thing the outside world taught me. Before the stars. Before the sigils. Before any of it: trust is not a wall you live behind. It's a bridge you rebuild every morning.

* * *

I did not sleep much. Around the coldest hour, when the fire had burned down to orange eyes in the embers, I got up and walked twenty paces from camp to a bare knob of granite, and sat down, and looked back one last time.

Brannoc had told me not to. Ilyra hadn't said anything, which from her was its own counsel. But I am a sworn soldier of the Valley of Hush and I had decided, somewhere on the switchbacks, that if I was going to carry my god into the dark, I would do it with my eyes open, and eyes open means looking at what you're losing.

The valley lay below in its gray shroud, and it was still beautiful. That undid me more than ruin would have. Ruin you can rage against. But beauty going quietly — beauty fading the way a face fades in an old photograph, all the structure still there, all the life leaking out — beauty going quietly asks nothing of you except that you witness it, and witnessing is the hardest work there is, because you cannot fix it and you cannot look away and you cannot, must not, pretend it isn't happening.

Hush-town was a skeleton of itself now, streets legible, buildings hollowed, the great covenant bell hanging in its tower like a heart in an opened chest. I thought of the bell ringing, that first impossible ring, the rope moving with no hand on it. I thought of Brannoc burning his letters, and then writing new ones. I thought of Aunt Sef walking down the mountain with her back straight, and of Tam the cooper's boy stacking staves by a millrace that would soon be gray water going nowhere, and of old Maren with her unspun wool, and of every person in that valley practicing their perfected silence one final season, not asking the only question that mattered, which was *what do we do now*, because asking it would have meant admitting that the answer was *nothing*, that there had never been a plan beyond the walls, that a hundred years of safety had been a hundred years of postponement.

The gray reached the bell tower while I watched. I saw the moment it happened — a slow bleaching, bronze to bone, and then the tower itself softened at the edges like chalk in rain, and a corner of the roofline simply was not there anymore, and no dust fell, and no sound came up the mountainside, because sound required change and change required time and the valley was running out of both.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, even if only in my own memory: I did not cry. I sat on the granite in the dark with a dying god warming my hip through lead, and I witnessed it, all of it, to the end of what could be seen, and then I stood up and walked back to the fire, and Ilyra was awake, sitting up with her knees drawn in, watching me come.

"You looked," she said.

"I looked."

"Good." She fed a stick to the embers. "Most people can't. Most people look away and spend the rest of their lives thinking they left something behind. You didn't leave anything behind, Wendell. You took the only part that was ever truly alive. The rest was architecture."

Architecture. Walls. Wards. A painted ceiling where the sky should be. I lay down next to the fire with the censer against my chest and felt the ember beat against my sternum, twice, three times, patient as a heartbeat that had learned to wait out centuries, and I understood at last what I was carrying. Not a relic. Not a hostage. Not a burden, exactly, though it weighed like one. A question. The oldest question my valley had ever failed to ask: *what is a god, if not the thing you choose to carry when everything else is taken away?*

In the morning we would go down out of the hills into country I had never seen, following a road on a map Ilyra drew from memory, toward a city called Corvane where mages sat in a court and argued about covenants, and where — Ilyra warned me, and I believed her provisionally, one day at a time — no one would believe a word we said.

But that was morning. For now there was the fire, and the gray valley behind us letting go of itself piece by piece in the dark, and the ember beating its small stubborn rhythm against my ribs like a second heart that had waited a hundred years for someone to listen.

Somewhere down in that dissolving town, Brannoc was awake too. I was sure of it. Old soldiers sleep badly, and he would be sitting at his table with his letters, writing the ones he'd never send, sealing the ones he would, and somewhere in the stack there would be one with my name on it, written in his careful soldier's hand, saying the thing he'd really meant on the shrine steps when he told me not to look back:

*Come home, if there's ever a home to come to. And if there isn't — make one.*

I fell asleep to the sound of the ember breathing, and dreamed — for the first time since the stars went out — of stars.
