## 8. Wolves of the Border

They came at dusk on the third day out of the valley, and Wendell knew them before Ilyra did.

Not by sight — the light was failing and the gray country gave nothing back, no color, no shadow worth trusting. Not by sound either, because these things made none. Wendell knew them the way a shepherd knows a storm: a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the air that had nothing to do with weather. The censer at their hip had grown heavier all afternoon, Vael's spark inside it pulsing like a second heart, and now it beat faster.

"They're here," Wendell said.

Ilyra looked up from the sigil she'd been scratching into the dirt with a stick — a ward-circle she redrew every night at camp, her chalk long since worn to nothing. "How many?"

"More than we can fight."

"You don't know how to fight them."

"I know how to count." Wendell drew their knife and turned in a slow circle, reading the treeline. The trees of the gray country were wrong — bark bleached like old bone, leaves the color of ash, branches that didn't move even when wind should have found them. Anything could stand among those trunks and be invisible until it chose not to be. "Ilyra. How many?"

She hesitated. That hesitation told Wendell more than any number would have. "Seven," she said finally. "Maybe eight. They're circling. They've been circling since noon."

"Since noon?" Wendell's voice came out flatter than they intended. "You knew since *noon*?"

"I wanted to find a place with good ground. A riverbed, a rock face — something to put at our backs. You can't outrun shadow-wolves, Wendell. You can only choose where they take you."

The censer swung against Wendell's thigh as they turned. Inside the leaden shell, the spark flared once, twice — like a lantern guttering, like something frightened. Wendell had carried it three days now, had slept with it against their chest, had spoken to it in the dark without meaning to, the way you talk to a horse or a child or a god who cannot answer. It had never answered back. But it moved. And right now it moved like prey.

"Then choose," Wendell said. "Where do they take us?"

Ilyra pointed north, where the land dropped away into a shallow gorge choked with dead bracken. "There's an overhang half a mile down. Stone above us, stone behind. They'll have to come at us from the front, two at most abreast."

"And then what?"

"Then you do what you did at the wardline." She met Wendell's eyes, and for once there was no irony in her face, no smith's coolness. Only tired honesty. "You hold anyway."

* * *

The overhang was exactly what she'd promised: a shelf of gray stone jutting from the gorge wall, low enough that Wendell had to stoop beneath its lip, deep enough for two people and no more. The dead bracken below crunched underfoot like frost. Wendell set the censer in the deepest corner and stood at the edge with their knife, feeling absurd — a valley mercenary with a belt blade and a lifetime of drills against men, facing things made of the space where light forgot to go.

Ilyra knelt beside them, drawing.

Her hands shook. Wendell had watched her work for three days and her hands had never shaken — steady as a mason's, sure as a surgeon's. Now the lines of the ward wavered, and when she reached the outer ring her chalk-stick snapped in her fingers.

"Ilyra."

"Don't," she said. "Don't say it's going to be fine. Don't say we'll manage. Just let me draw."

Wendell said nothing. They watched the treeline instead, and the sun went down behind hills that cast no shadows, because in the gray country shadows had been eaten already, and the dark that came was not the ordinary kind.

It arrived first as absence. The last gray light simply stopped, cut off along a line in the bracken, as if a door had been drawn across the world. Then came the smell — wet stone and old blood and something underneath both, something like a shrine left shut for a hundred years. Then the eyes.

That was when Wendell understood, and understanding was worse than fear.

Six pairs of eyes floated in the black between the trees, and they were not the flat silver shine of animal eyeshine. They caught the censer's faint glow and held it, and they were *brown*. Warm brown. River-stone brown. The exact brown of eyes Wendell had grown up looking across cookfires and drill yards and the long tables of the mess hall.

"No," Wendell breathed.

"What?" Ilyra didn't look up from her ward. "What is it?"

"The scouts." Wendell's mouth had gone dry. "The valley sent scouts past the ridge. Fourteen of them, over the years, before the wards sealed. My year-mates. Tam. Sera. The Corver brothers. They went out to map the borderlands and they never came back, and the elders said the world beyond took them, and everyone pretended that meant bandits or winter or wolves—"

"Wendell."

"*Look at their eyes.*"

Ilyra looked. Wendell watched her see it — watched her whole body go rigid, the way a person does when the floor turns out to be thinner than they thought. One of the shapes stepped forward into the edge of the censer-light, and it was almost a wolf. Almost. The proportions were right, the lean shoulders and the low head, but it was built from darkness the way a man is built from meat, and where fur should have caught the light it drank it instead, so that the creature seemed less a thing standing there than a hole shaped like a thing. Its ribs showed through the dark like lines of script.

But the eyes were Tam's eyes. Tam, who used to cheat at dice and laugh about it. Tam, who had kissed Wendell behind the granary the summer they were seventeen and then never spoken of it, and Wendell had spent years deciding whether that silence was kindness or cowardice.

"It knows me," Wendell whispered.

The wolf-shape's head tilted. Its nostrils worked, drawing in scent — and then it turned its head toward the censer, toward the corner where Vael's spark pulsed in its leaden shell, and every line of its body changed. Hunger poured off it like heat off a forge.

"It doesn't want me," Wendell said. "It wants him."

"They all do." Ilyra finished the outer ring and sat back on her heels, breathing hard. "Everything out here wants the spark. The dark has been starving for it since Vael went silent. These—" She gestured at the circle of watching shapes, and her voice caught. "These are what's left when the hunger wears a person all the way through."

"You're saying they were people."

"You're saying it first. I'm just agreeing."

The first one came without warning. No growl — these things didn't growl, didn't warn, because warning was something living things did — just a sudden collapse of distance, six feet of bracken flattened in a heartbeat, and Wendell's body did what ten years of drill had taught it: step inside the lunge, turn the hip, put the knife where the throat would be on anything made of flesh.

The blade sank into the shadow and stopped. Not resistance — *absence*, the sensation of cutting into cold water, into night itself. The wolf-shape screamed, and the scream was worse than anything: it had a human register, a human crack in the middle of it, Tam's voice broken into pieces and reassembled wrong.

It fell back. Black spilled from the wound like ink from a cut page, hissing where it struck the stones, and the shape reeled away into the dark and did not rise again.

But the others had seen the ward-line. Two more came together, low and fast, and hit Ilyra's circle — and the circle *held*, flaring up in thin white fire, lines of light Wendell had watched her scratch into dirt suddenly blazing like the border wards themselves. The wolves recoiled, smoking, circling for another angle.

"They can't cross while it holds," Ilyra panted. "But I can't hold it long. The lines fray. Everything frays out here."

"How long?"

"A minute. Maybe two."

Wendell looked at the knife. Shadow-blood still smoked along the blade, eating at the steel, pitting it like acid. They looked at the eyes ringing the overhang — five pairs now, patient, hungry, and every pair wearing a face Wendell knew.

"We can't kill our way out of this," they said. "There are five of them and one knife and they don't die right."

"No. But they can be driven. The light wounds them; the ward repels them. If we can make this cost more than the prize is worth—" Ilyra stopped. Her eyes had gone to the censer, and something terrible crossed her face. "Or they stop caring about cost. When the spark burns low, they stop caring."

The spark *was* burning low. Wendell felt it — the pulse at their hip had gone faint, fluttering, a candle in a draft. Three days of travel, three nights of cold, and the ember that was all that remained of a god was guttering.

And the wolves knew it. Wendell saw it happen: the patience draining out of those brown eyes, replaced by something older and simpler, and the five shapes began to move at once — not lunging, not testing, but converging, flowing around the edges of Ilyra's failing circle like water finding every crack in a dam.

"Hold it," Wendell said. "Hold it as long as you can. Then drop flat."

"What are you doing?"

"Something stupid."

They pulled the censer from the corner, and the moment the leaden shell left the shelter of the stone, every wolf-head turned toward them with the smooth synchronization of a single mind wearing five bodies. Wendell climbed onto the lip of the overhang, held the censer high, and did the thing that ten years of guarding a silent god had taught them without their ever knowing they'd learned it.

They spoke the evening office. The words the elders mumbled without meaning, the litany every child in the Valley of Hush recited before bed, addressed to a god who hadn't answered in a hundred years. *"Vael who walks the ridge-lines. Vael who keeps the hearth. Keeper of the border, holder of the door—"*

The spark flared.

It wasn't much. A breath of gold through the seams of the censer's lid, a warmth like sunrise on Wendell's knuckles — but in the gray country, where nothing had burned bright in longer than anyone could remember, it was a shout in a tomb. The wolves recoiled as one, bracken charring where they stood, and for one suspended moment the whole gorge was lit gold at the center and black at the edges and utterly silent.

*"—keep your servant, keep your spark, keep the covenant whole."*

The light died. Of course it did. The spark was nearly spent, and gods did not come when called, not anymore. But the minute had been enough. Two of the wolf-shapes broke and fled into the trees, yammering that broken-human sound, and three more circled wide, wary now, hurt.

Three. Wendell could count to three.

The first came from the left, fast and low, and Wendell met it with the knife and the weight of their whole body behind the blade, driving it down through the shadow-skull into the earth beneath. The shape convulsed and came apart like smoke in wind, and the last sound it made was almost — almost — a word. Wendell would spend the rest of their life not deciding whether it had been *run* or a name.

The second got past them. It flowed over the ward-line as Ilyra's circle finally failed, white light dying in a scatter of sparks, and it came for the censer with its jaws open on darkness, and Ilyra threw herself bodily into its path. Wendell heard her cry out — heard fabric tear, heard something wet — and then they were moving, driving their shoulder into the thing's flank, hauling it off her, putting the knife in again and again, four times, six, until it unraveled around the blade like a knot coming loose.

The third was gone. Fled with the others, or waiting, or simply done — the dark held no answers and gave none.

Silence came back to the gorge, which was not the same as peace. It was the silence of a room after a fight, ringing and unreal. Wendell knelt in the dead bracken, knife hanging from fingers they couldn't quite feel, and looked at the place where the first wolf had fallen. Nothing remained. No body, no blood — only a scorch-mark on the stones in the rough shape of a curled-up animal, small, like a dog sleeping.

Like a scout resting after a long walk home.

* * *

Ilyra had taken the wound across her forearm — a raking gash where shadow-teeth had opened her sleeve and the skin beneath, not deep, but the edges of it were gray, colorless, as if the injury had drained the life out of the surrounding flesh. Wendell cleaned it with the last of their water and bound it with strips of shirt, working in silence, and Ilyra let them, which was its own kind of admission that she was more shaken than she'd ever allow in words.

"That won't heal right," she said quietly, flexing her fingers. "Shadow-wounds don't. I'll need a proper sigil-worked salve. In Corvane, maybe." She paused. "If the Court doesn't hang us first."

"Corvane's five days' walk."

"Four, if we push."

Wendell tied off the bandage and sat back on their heels. The censer lay between them, and the spark inside had gone very quiet — not guttering now, but dimmed, banked, a coal rather than a flame. Wendell picked it up and held it, and hated how natural the gesture had become.

"Tam Verrin," they said.

Ilyra said nothing.

"He was a scout. Second patrol past the ridge, nine years ago. He cheated at dice and he laughed like a drain unclogging and he—" Wendell stopped. Started again, flatter. "There were fourteen scouts over thirty years. Fourteen names on the memorial stone by the bell tower, and every year the elders laid flowers and said the world beyond had taken them, and everyone knew it was a lie of politeness but nobody asked what the truth was, because asking might mean going to look."

"Now you know."

"Now I know." Wendell's hand closed around the censer until the metal bit. "The dark didn't eat them. It kept them. It wore them hollow and hung hunger where they used to be, and it sent them home tonight wearing their own eyes so that—" Their voice cracked, and they let it, too tired to fight it. "So that whoever came out here would hesitate. That's what the eyes are for. That's the trap. You see Tam's face in the thing's skull and you freeze, and freezing is death, and if you don't freeze then you're the one who killed him twice."

"Yes," Ilyra said. No softening, no comfort. She had learned, in three days, that Wendell did not want softening. "That's how it works. That's why my order failed, in the end. Not because we weren't strong enough. Because the dark learns what you love, and it builds its traps out of it."

Wendell stared at her. Something cold slid down their spine, colder than the gorge air.

"Ilyra. The herald stories. The thing that speaks for the dark."

"What about it?"

"It speaks in stolen voices. You said so, the first night, when I asked what waited beyond the wards." Wendell turned to face her fully. "Whose voice does it use on a sigil-smith? On *you*?"

Ilyra was quiet for a long time. Above them, through the haze of the overhang's edge, the sky hung starless and absolute, a blackness so complete it seemed less like night than like a lid.

"My teacher's," she said at last. "Master Corvess. He led the order when they broke the covenant. He taught me everything I know about drawing power from silence." Her jaw tightened. "And when I ran, he was the one they sent to bring me back. So yes. I know whose face the dark will wear when it comes for us. I've known since the day I left."

She rose, wincing at her arm, and looked north along the gorge, where the land climbed toward distant hills and, somewhere beyond them, the towers of Corvane.

"Four days," she said. "We walk at first light. And Wendell—" She glanced back, and in the faint glow of the banked spark her eyes were very clear and very tired. "Whatever we meet on that road. Whatever voices it uses. Promise me you'll remember tonight. Remember that seeing a face you love is the trap, not the person. Because it *will* use mine eventually, and I need to know you'll drive the knife anyway."

Wendell looked at the scorch-mark on the stones, small as a sleeping dog, and made a promise they were not at all certain they could keep.

"First light," they said.

But sleep, when it finally came, brought no peace — only the memory of brown eyes in a dark skull, and the sound a name makes when it comes apart.
