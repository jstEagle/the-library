## 9. The Sigil Court

The road to Corvane climbed for two days through country that remembered being green.

Ilyra rode ahead of Wendell, reading the land the way her order had taught her to read it — not by what grew there but by what refused to. The gray had passed over these hills like a hand dragged across wet ink, smearing everything it touched. Trees stood in rows like witnesses who had seen too much and been forbidden to speak. Their bark was bleached the color of old bone. A river ran beside them for a mile, and its water was clear and moving and utterly dead; no insect touched its surface, no bird dipped toward it. Even the stones seemed to have forgotten their color.

Wendell noticed none of this at first. They had grown up in a valley where nothing ever changed, where the wards held so absolutely that even the seasons arrived on schedule like servants. To them, a dead tree was just a tree in winter. But they were learning. By the second day, Wendell was pointing things out before Ilyra saw them — a fence post with no lichen, a field where the grass grew only in stripes, as if the dark had swept through in long strokes and missed the places where a farmer's shadow had fallen at the wrong hour.

"You're getting good at this," Ilyra said, and meant it as praise, and watched Wendell flinch from it anyway.

"I'd rather be good at something that isn't noticing how everything dies."

"Everything doesn't die." She reined her horse closer. "That's the point of what we're doing. The gray is a wound, not a conquest. Wounds can close."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment with an expression she was beginning to know well — the look of someone deciding whether to believe her, weighing her words against the evidence of their own eyes, and finding the evidence heavier every time. Then they turned back to the road and said nothing, and Ilyra understood that silence for what it was: not agreement, not refusal, but the sound of a person holding two truths in their hands and waiting to see which one would break first.

She knew that feeling intimately. She had carried it for eleven years.

* * *

Corvane announced itself before they could see it.

They crested a ridge in the late afternoon and heard it first — a hum, low and constant, like a hive the size of a mountain. Then the light: a shimmer above the horizon, gold shot through with threads of blue and violet, the glow of ten thousand active sigils burning in the city's walls and towers and streets. Against all that gray country, Corvane burned like a coal that refused to go out.

"It's beautiful," Wendell said quietly.

Ilyra's chest tightened. Because it was. And because she knew what it cost, and who paid, and how much of that light was borrowed against a debt the city had never once acknowledged. She had spent half her life inside those walls. She knew the smell of the sigil-smiths' quarter at dawn — hot metal and ozone and tallow — and the sound the great ward-bells made when they rang the hours, each one tuned to a different covenant-line. She had been a child prodigy there. She had drawn her first living sigil at nine years old, inked into air instead of skin, and the whole hall had applauded, and her mother had wept, and none of them had known — none of them had wanted to know — what the applause was actually for.

"They'll have seen us coming," she said. "There are watch-sigils on every approach. We should decide now what story we're telling."

"The truth," Wendell said immediately.

Ilyra almost laughed. Almost. "The truth is that I'm an exile of the Order of the Sundered Quill, that my order broke the covenant, that I'm carrying a fugitive from a dead valley with a god's last spark in a leaden censer, and that the sky has gone out. If we tell them the truth, they will take the censer, hang me, and thank you politely for your service."

"Then they don't get the truth."

"No. They get a version of it. There's a difference, and the difference is survival." She pulled up short at the top of the ridge, looking down at the glowing city, and made herself say the rest plainly, because Wendell deserved plainness even when it was ugly. "I'm going to tell them the wards failed. I'm going to tell them the stars went out. Those things are undeniable — their own watch-sigils have been screaming about it for weeks. What I'm not going to do is walk into that city and announce myself. Not yet. Not until we've spoken to someone who might listen."

"And if no one listens?"

Ilyra didn't answer right away. Below them, the road descended in long switchbacks toward the western gate, and already she could see travelers on it — carts, pilgrims, a column of soldiers whose armor caught the city-glow and threw it back in shards. Life. Ordinary, oblivious life, flowing toward the light because that was what people did.

"Then we steal what we need and keep moving east," she said finally. "There are other archives. Other libraries. The Court doesn't hold all the knowledge in the realm, whatever it believes."

Wendell studied her. "You're scared."

"Yes."

"You weren't scared of the wolves."

"Wolves are honest." She nudged her horse forward, down onto the switchbacks, and felt the city's hum grow louder beneath her, vibrating up through the saddle, through her ribs, into her teeth. It sounded like home. That was the worst part. It sounded exactly like home.

* * *

The western gate took three hours.

Not because of any line — though there was one, a mile of carts and cattle and refugees from the eastern reaches, people who had seen the sky go black and walked until their shoes split. It took three hours because of the inspection. Every traveler passing into Corvane was scanned by a lintel-sigil older than the dynasty, a great carved eye above the gate that read each body for active marks. Most people passed under it without a flicker. Wendell passed under it and the eye did nothing at all, which told Ilyra something interesting: the censer's lead lining held, even here, even against a reading-sigil of that age and power. Vael's spark was invisible to the Court. For now.

But when Ilyra passed beneath it, the eye woke.

It found her in half a heartbeat — the old guild-mark burned into the skin of her left forearm, the mark of the Sundered Quill, half-erased by her own hand the night she fled and half-erased was worse than whole, because a broken mark read as a tampered mark, and tampered marks meant heresy. A bell tolled somewhere inside the gatehouse. Guards converged. And Ilyra stood very still in the middle of the road with a hundred refugees pressing around her and did the only thing she could think to do: she reached out and took Wendell's hand.

"What are you—" Wendell started.

"Don't let go," she said, low and fast. "Whatever happens. Don't let go."

The guards came through the crowd like a wedge. Four of them, then six, in the gray-and-gold of the City Wardens, sigils stitched along their hems that glimmered faintly with each step. Their sergeant was a broad woman with a scarred jaw and the flat patience of someone who had done this a thousand times.

"You. Arm." She pointed at Ilyra's marked forearm. "Show it."

Ilyra showed it. There was no point pretending; the eye had already sung her name to the gatehouse rolls, and somewhere a clerk was even now pulling her file, and somewhere else a message was being drafted to the Court. Eleven years of hiding, undone by a doorway.

"Sundered Quill," the sergeant read off a slate a runner handed her. Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Exiled order. You're supposed to be dead or gone, smith."

"Gone," Ilyra said. "Clearly not far enough."

The sergeant looked at her, then at Wendell, still gripping her hand, standing there in valley-woven clothes with a mercenary's sword and the stubborn set of someone who had decided, on insufficient evidence, that this stranger was worth standing next to. "And this one?"

"My sworn companion," Ilyra said. "From the Valley of Hush."

Something moved behind the sergeant's eyes. Hush. Everyone past forty knew the name; it was a story told to children, the hidden valley where the silent god slept, the place the maps drew as blank parchment. Nobody believed it was real. Nobody except the clerks, and the clerks had files.

"Hush is a fable," the sergeant said, but she said it carefully, the way people repeat official lines while checking whether the ground has shifted under them.

"The stars went out three weeks ago," Wendell said. "You can check that yourself tonight. Hush is real, and it's dying, and we've come to warn you."

The sergeant's slate-hand tightened. For a moment Ilyra thought it would go badly — thought they would be taken into the gatehouse, separated, questioned in rooms with listening-sigils carved into the ceilings. Instead the sergeant exhaled through her nose, glanced once more at the joined hands, and said, "Quill-smiths enter Corvane under escort or not at all. You'll come with me to the Hall of Audiences, and you'll speak to whoever the Council sends, and you'll count yourself lucky it's me walking you in and not the Inquisitors. Move."

They moved. Through the gate, into the city, into the light.

* * *

Corvane at dusk was a cathedral built out of fire.

Sigils burned everywhere — on the walls, in the gutters, woven into the awnings of market stalls, tattooed crawling and alive across the arms of half the population. Street-lamps were not lamps but bound motes of covenant-light, each one humming its single note, and together the notes made the chord Ilyra had grown up inside, the chord that meant safe, the chord that meant home. She heard it and her throat closed. Wendell walked beside her staring upward, mouth slightly open, a person from a world without magic seeing magic for the first time at scale, and Ilyra watched their face and grieved in advance for what the city was going to do to that wonder.

Because the city was lying to itself. She could see it now, from outside, after eleven years away. The chord was thinner than it used to be. The great ward-lines that ringed the city — she counted them as they crossed each one, feeling the faint pressure-change against her skin like stepping through curtains of warm water — there should have been seven strong lines. She counted four. Two of the remaining three flickered when she passed, stuttering like a candle in wind, and no one on the street seemed to notice. No one wanted to notice. The whole city was a household singing loudly in the parlor while the roof beams groaned upstairs.

The Hall of Audiences occupied the heart of the Sigil Court: a domed chamber of white stone veined with silver, where the Nine Chairs sat in a crescent beneath a ceiling painted with the night sky as it had been before the schism — every star in its place, every constellation named, the heavens rendered as they were when the covenant was whole. Ilyra had stood in this room at nineteen, presenting her masterwork, and the painted stars had seemed to approve of her.

Now she stood in it again, eleven years later, in travel-stained clothes, under escort, accused, and the painted stars hung overhead like a taunt. Because outside the dome, the real sky was black. Inside, the lie was still gorgeous.

Three of the Nine Chairs were filled. The others stood empty, draped in cloth-of-gold, which Ilyra knew meant the full Council had not been convened — this was an informal hearing, which meant either they considered her unimportant or they considered her dangerous enough to handle quietly. With a Sundered Quill smith, it would be the latter.

The center chair belonged to First Councillor Mavelet, a narrow woman of perhaps seventy with iron-gray hair coiled tight and eyes like awls. To her left sat Ondrell.

Ilyra's stomach dropped. Of course. Of course it was Ondrell — Court Mage of the Outer Wards, the rising star, the man whose theories about covenant-restoration had been laughed out of the academy a decade ago and who had spent every year since building power instead of arguments. He was younger than the others, handsome in the deliberate way of men who had chosen their faces, and his gaze found Ilyra the moment she entered and stayed on her with an attention that felt less like recognition than like appetite.

"So," Mavelet said, when the escort withdrew. "A ghost. Ilyra Vess, of the Sundered Quill. Reported dead in the purges. And yet here you stand, in my hall, with a tale I am told involves the Valley of Hush."

Here it was, then. Ilyra had rehearsed versions of this speech for eleven years, in cells and ditches and the gray country, and every version had assumed she would be alone. She was not alone. Wendell stood at her shoulder, and when she glanced sideways, they gave her the smallest nod — the nod of someone saying *say your piece, I'm here* — and something in her chest unlocked that had been locked since the night she ran.

"The covenant is broken," Ilyra said. Her voice carried in the dome better than she intended; the acoustics had always favored confession. "Not fraying. Broken. The border wards of the Valley of Hush collapsed twenty-two days ago. On the same night, every star visible from the valley went out. Not clouded. Not occluded. Gone. The sky is a closed eye, and the dark beyond it is not empty — it moves, it hunts, and it is spreading westward through the borderlands. You have four ward-lines left around this city. I counted them on my way in. Two of them are failing."

Murmurs ran the crescent of chairs. Mavelet let them die.

"You bring remarkable news," the First Councillor said dryly, "from a valley our records describe as myth. From an order that was exiled for—"

"For breaking the covenant," Ilyra said. "Yes. Say it plainly, Councillor. I won't pretend otherwise. My order committed a crime against the realms, and I have spent eleven years paying for a crime I helped commit. I am not here to be forgiven. I am here because I am the only person in this hall who knows what broke, and how, and what is coming through the gap."

"And what is coming?"

"Vael's grief."

The word landed in the dome and rolled around it. Grief. Not darkness, not entropy, not the cold rational force the academy had theorized — grief, given form, a god's mourning walking abroad in the world wearing the shape of hunger. She watched Ondrell lean forward slightly, the way a scholar leans toward an error he intends to dissect.

"You claim," Ondrell said, and his voice was smooth as poured wax, "that the entity consuming the borderlands is the emotional residue of a god whom, I remind the hall, no credible source has confirmed existed at all. The Valley of Hush appears in no survey, no census, no ledger. Its 'god' is folklore. And you, Vess, are a convicted heretic asking us to dismantle four centuries of doctrine on your testimony." He spread his hands, gracious, reasonable, monstrous. "I admire the audacity."

"Ask her companion," Ilyra said.

Every head turned. Wendell stepped forward — and Ilyra saw them plant themselves the way they planted themselves on patrol, feet shoulder-width, weight even, the stance of a body that had learned to hold ground. They were afraid. She could see it in their hands. They spoke anyway.

"My name is Wendell Joubert. I was born in the Valley of Hush, and I lived there thirty-one years, and I swear to you by whatever you hold sacred — by your painted sky up there, by the four ward-lines still standing around this city — that every word she has said is true. I stood on the wardline the night it fell. I felt it go. I watched the stars vanish like snuffed candles, all of them, at once, in a single breath. There is no doctrine that explains what I saw. There is only what I saw." Wendell's voice cracked and steadied. "You want to argue about whether the valley exists. Fine. Argue. But the sky went out, and the gray is eating your borderlands, and while you sit here deciding whether to believe us, it is walking west."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then Ondrell smiled, and Ilyra understood, with a cold sliding certainty, that she had misjudged the shape of the trap. He wasn't dismissing them. He was savoring them.

"A passionate advocate," he said to the hall, warmly. "And clearly devoted to you, Vess. Devotion is such useful material." He turned back to Wendell, and his voice gentled into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register he owned. "Tell me, Joubert of Hush. When the wards fell — did anything survive? Anything... carried out?"

Beside her, she felt Wendell go still. Felt the lie assemble itself behind their eyes, quick and clumsy and loyal.

"There was nothing left to carry," Wendell said.

Ondrell's smile widened by a single degree. "How strange. Our instruments registered a resonance event in the eastern reaches the night the valley fell. Small. Brief. The signature of a living ember passing through dead wards." He let the words sit. "Lead-lined, I'd guess. Censer-grade shielding. Very old work." His gaze drifted down, unhurried, to the satchel at Wendell's hip, and stopped there, and stayed.

The painted stars burned overhead, every one of them false, every one of them bright.

"You have until morning," Mavelet said quietly, "to convince this Council that you are a witness and not a thief. Guard them to the guest chambers. Sealed quarters. No one enters, no one leaves."

As the wardens closed around them, Ilyra leaned close to Wendell, close enough that only they could hear, and whispered the truth she had been carrying since the gate:

"Now you understand why I never wanted to come home."
