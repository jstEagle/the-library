## 10. What Power Wants

The guest chambers of the Sigil Court were the finest room Wendell Joubert had ever stood inside, and they hated every inch of it.

The floor was pale marble veined with silver, warm underfoot though no fire burned. The walls held niches where soft light pooled around objects that might have been art or might have been weapons — a glass sphere full of slow-turning mist, a knife on a silk cushion, a single dried flower that had not faded in what was clearly a very long time. And overhead, painted across the whole of the ceiling with obsessive, loving precision, the stars: constellations Wendell had never seen and could not name, burning false and bright above a bed wide enough for four people.

A cage, Wendell thought. A very beautiful cage, with a painted sky.

They had counted the doors twice. The one they'd come through — warded, the wardens had said, sealed until morning. The one beside the washroom, which opened onto a broom closet stacked with folded linens that smelled of lavender and something older, something mineral, like wet stone. And a third, narrow door set into the far wall, unmarked, its handle worn smooth by hands.

Wendell did not try it. Not yet. Instead they sat on the edge of the too-soft bed, boots still on, elbows on their knees, and watched Ilyra.

She stood at the window — a real window, glazed with glass so clear it made the valley's mica windows look like smeared wax — looking down into the night city of Corvane. Lamps strung along the canals. Towers crowned with sigil-lanterns whose light moved, slowly, like breathing. She had not spoken since her whisper at the Council door. *Now you understand why I never wanted to come home.*

"The bed could sleep your whole patrol," Wendell said, to break the silence, because silence had started to feel like water rising.

Ilyra didn't turn. "I'm not tired."

"I didn't ask if you were tired."

That earned him — no. Wendell corrected themself, as they had been correcting themself for days now. Earned *them* a glance over her shoulder. One corner of Ilyra's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "No," she said. "You didn't."

Wendell leaned back until their shoulders found the wall. The plaster was warm too. Everything here was warm, and soft, and lit, and none of it was theirs, and all of it was watching. They could feel the wards in the walls the way you feel a stare between your shoulder blades. After twenty-some years in a valley where magic could not follow, living inside a building that hummed with it was like sleeping in a den of sleeping animals.

Somewhere below, wrapped in lead and linen in their pack, the spark sat in its censer like an ember that refused to die. Wendell could not feel it from here. They felt it anyway. They suspected they always would, now.

*Only a sworn servant of Vael can carry it,* Ilyra had said, in the empty shrine, in another life. Wendell had spent their whole existence serving a god who never answered, and now they knew why: there had been almost nothing left of the god to answer *with*. One ember. Lodged in stone. Given to the only person in generations stubborn enough to keep kneeling anyway.

Footsteps in the corridor. Two sets, then one. Then a knock — courteous, unhurried, three soft raps — and a voice through the wood:

"Joubert of Hush. May I have a moment of your evening?"

Ilyra crossed the room in three silent strides and put herself between the door and Wendell, which would have been touching if it weren't also absurd; whatever came through that door, a former exile standing in front of Wendell would stop it for exactly as long as it took to be amused.

"Who is it?" Wendell called.

"Ondrell. First Chair of the College of Wards." A pause, dry as paper. "I believe Mavelet mentioned me. Unflatteringly."

"He mentioned everyone unflatteringly," Ilyra murmured.

"The door's unlocked," Wendell said, and stood up, because whatever this was, they would not take it sitting down.

* * *

Ondrell was not what Wendell expected, which was foolish, because Wendell hadn't known what to expect. They had imagined — based on nothing but the Council chamber's cold grandeur — an old man like a drawn blade. Instead the mage who stepped in was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, dressed plainly in charcoal wool with a single sigil stitched at the collar in thread so fine it looked like a scar. Their hands were a working smith's hands: scarred, burn-speckled, clean nails. Wendell knew those hands. They had seen them on Brannoc, on the valley's farriers, on every honest tradesperson who had ever fixed something with their fingers while their mouth did the talking.

"You're the ward-smith," Wendell said.

Ondrell's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "I'm a great many things. But yes. Twenty years on the borderlines before they gave me a chair and told me to sit in it." They glanced past Wendell at Ilyra, and something complicated moved behind their face — recognition, history, a door closing politely. "Vashtane."

"Ondrell." Ilyra's voice was flat as slate.

"I argued against your exile," Ondrell said. "For whatever that's worth now. Which is possibly nothing." They turned back to Wendell before Ilyra could answer, and Wendell noted the maneuver for what it was: deliberate, practiced, kind-seeming. This person was dangerous in exactly the way Brannoc had always warned about — not the ones who threaten you, but the ones who make you want to please them.

"I'll be brief," Ondrell said. "Mavelet has the votes to have you both in cells by noon unless something changes overnight. I'd rather something changed overnight." They walked to the window, unhurried, and looked out at their city the way a shepherd looks at a hillside. "May I ask you a question, Joubert? Answer or don't."

Wendell shrugged. "People keep asking. Might as well keep answering."

"What did it feel like, the day the wards fell?"

It was not the question Wendell had braced for, and it landed somewhere under their ribs before they could decide how to receive it.

"Loud," they said finally. "Which is strange, because it was mostly quiet. The bells, the shouting — none of that was it. It was like—" They searched. "Like a tooth you've had all your life coming out. All that ache, gone, and in its place just this wrong open space. Like part of my skull was missing."

Ondrell nodded slowly, and their nod had the quality of someone confirming a diagnosis. "That's what I thought. That's what the old accounts said, from the founding. The wards don't just protect a place. They *define* it. When they fall, the place falls with them, whether the walls do or not." They turned from the window. "Your valley is dying, Joubert. You know that. You watched it start to gray behind you."

"Don't," Wendell said quietly.

"I'm not being cruel. I'm being precise." Ondrell crossed to the table where the pack sat and stopped short of it, respectful of the censer's leaden shape as if it were a sleeping animal. "The Court's diviners read the residue on your clothing when you passed the gate. They think it's interesting. I think it's terminal. Whatever carried the Valley of Hush for three hundred years is failing, and everything inside its borders will follow — soil, water, memory, people. Unless the covenant is reforged."

"Then let us do our work," Ilyra said from across the room, "and you won't need to reforge anything."

"Your work." Ondrell didn't laugh, but the word bent slightly in their mouth. "You come into my city carrying the last living piece of a god, smuggled in lead, defended before my Council by a mercenary who drew a blade in chambers—"

"They drew it to stop an arrest," Ilyra said. "It stayed sheathed. Ask the wardens."

"I did ask the wardens." Ondrell's gaze slid back to Wendell, and Wendell saw the trap being laid out with such care that they almost admired it. "That's rather my point. Everyone in that chamber tonight saw the same thing: a stranger with no training, no sigil-craft, no lineage, standing between the Sigil Court and something priceless — and the Court *blinked*." They spread their scarred hands. "Do you know what happens to institutions that blink, Joubert? They get eaten. If not by the dark at your heels, then by the ambitious in their own halls. Mavelet wants the spark secured in the Reliquary Vault by winter. Others want worse."

"And you?" Wendell asked. "What do you want?"

For the first time, Ondrell smiled, and the smile was startlingly young. "I want to teach you."

* * *

They talked for two hours.

Later, lying awake under the painted stars, Wendell would try to find the exact seam where the offer had become a temptation and would fail, because there wasn't one. That was the craft of it. Ondrell never once said *give me the spark*. They built a cathedral out of everything else and left the price standing modestly in the corner, waiting to be picked up.

They said: *You stood in front of the Council without shaking. Do you know how rare that is? Raw talent is common. Nerve isn't.*

They said: *Sigil-work isn't bloodline, whatever the houses tell themselves. It's grammar. It's language. You've been speaking it your whole life without knowing — why do you think the spark chose to lodge itself where a servant of Vael could reach it? Why do you think it holds for you when it frays for trained smiths?*

They said: *I can teach you wards that would make your valley's borders look like chalk lines. I can teach you to draw protection in the air, in stone, in skin. In six months you'd be beyond half my college. In two years—*

And then, softly, folding the last fold:

*In two years you could have a place here. Rooms like these. Work that matters. A seat eventually, if you wanted it, and you'd earn it, no one would hand it to you. People who'd stand beside you instead of a rope's length behind.* Ondrell had glanced once, briefly, at Ilyra — who had gone very still by the window — and then back. *Belonging, Joubert. You've been outside every wall your whole life. I'm offering you the inside of the most important one there is. All the covenant requires is that the spark rest where the realm can guard it. In the Vault. Under seal. Not taken. Protected. What is faithfulness to a silent god, weighed against keeping the last of that god safe?*

And Wendell had sat there with their heart hammering like a fist on a door, because every word of it was aimed true.

They thought of the valley. Not the dying gray version behind them but the real one, the green one, the one that lived behind their eyes: dawn patrols along the ridge with frost in their collar, Brannoc's gruff nod that meant *well done*, the bell that never rang, the shrine where they had knelt ten thousand times to a god who never spoke, and the ache underneath all of it, the ache that was almost the whole of them — *there has to be more than this, there has to be a world past the ridge, there has to be a life I haven't had yet.*

Ondrell was offering the world past the ridge. With walls, yes. With seals and vaults and committees, yes. But warm. Lit. Real.

*You could belong somewhere,* said the voice under Ondrell's voice, which was Wendell's own, which had been talking since they were seven years old and first climbed the lookout tree and saw the haze where the world supposedly went on.

And the spark sat in its censer, and said nothing, the way Vael had said nothing for three hundred years, and Wendell realized, with a coldness that started in their stomach and climbed, that they had nearly answered. That some part of them had already handed it over — gratefully, even. Here, take it. Take the burden, give me the bed and the warm floor and the name on the door.

Here was the thing Brannoc had tried to teach them on the practice yard a hundred times and Wendell had only ever half learned: *the dangerous offer is never the one you don't want.*

"It's late," Wendell said at last, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "I need to think."

"Of course." Ondrell rose, buttoned their coat, and paused at the door with the courtesy of someone who knows exactly when to leave a hook in the water. "One more thing, and then I'll go. The Reliquary Vault is not a prison for the spark, whatever Vashtane tells you. It is the most heavily warded space in the realm. Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out. Nothing gets *near*." A beat. "Including the dark. Sleep on that, at least."

The door closed behind them. The painted stars burned on.

* * *

Silence again. Worse than before, because now it had a shape.

Wendell stood in the middle of the beautiful room and hated how well they understood Ondrell, because it was the same understanding they'd had of Brannoc, of the elders, of every adult in the valley who had ever told them the walls were for their own good. Ondrell was not lying. That was the horror of it. Every word had been true — the valley was dying, the Court was treacherous, the vault was impregnable. Truth, laid brick by brick into a road that led exactly where Ondrell wanted.

*I can teach you.*

Wendell's hands curled at their sides. They looked down at their palms — sword-callused, bowstring-roughened, ink-stained only lately, only since Ilyra had put a stylus in their grip in the gray country and shown them the first shapes. The little ward that had saved them from the shade was still the proudest thing they had ever done, and Ondrell had seen that hunger in five minutes flat and set a feast in front of it.

*Six months and you'd be beyond half my college.*

But the spark wouldn't be theirs anymore. It would be the Court's, under glass, in the dark, guarded so thoroughly that no one — not a sworn servant, not a friend, not anyone who loved it — could ever reach it again. And the covenant would remain broken, and the Hollow Throne would remain empty, and the dark would keep eating the world at its patient pace, and Wendell would live out their life in warm rooms with a painted sky, telling themself they had saved the last of Vael by burying it.

"No," Wendell said aloud, to the room, to themself, to the seven-year-old in the lookout tree. "That's not home. That's just a nicer cage."

From the window, very quietly, Ilyra said: "I wasn't sure you'd say that."

Wendell turned. She hadn't gone to the window to look at the city at all, they realized. She had positioned herself there so the light wouldn't touch her face — so that neither of them could see what the other showed while Ondrell talked. All that stillness hadn't been indifference. It had been white-knuckled.

"You weren't sure I'd refuse?"

"I wasn't sure you'd refuse *yet*." Ilyra came away from the window, and in the low lamplight her face was bare in a way Wendell had never seen — no irony, no exile's armor. "Ondrell doesn't make offers. They make mirrors. Whatever you most want, that's what you see in the glass. I saw mine, twenty years ago. I said yes. It cost me my order, my name, and everything I—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Wendell. Before morning comes, before Mavelet's vote, before any of it — there's something you have to know about the spark. About what my order actually did to Vael. I've been carrying it since the shrine, and I can't—"

She got no further, because that was when every lamp in the room guttered at once, and the third door — the narrow one, unmarked, worn smooth by hands — swung slowly open onto darkness that did not belong to any hallway.

And out of that darkness came a smell Wendell knew from the border, from the dead scouts' eyes, from the gray country: cold ash and old grief, and beneath it, faint and terrible, a voice like a god remembering how to speak.

"Wendell Joubert," it said. "We should talk about your valley."
