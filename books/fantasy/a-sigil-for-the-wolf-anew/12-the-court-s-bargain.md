## 12. The Court's Bargain

They came for the spark at dawn, and they came politely.

That was the thing about Corvane Wendell could never forgive later — not the cruelty, but the courtesy. Ondrell did not send soldiers with drawn blades. He sent a page in livery soft as moth wings, carrying an invitation on card stock heavy enough to stop a door: *The Court requests the presence of the bearer of Vael's ember, at the hour of first light, in the Hall of Nine Voices.*

*Requests.* As if the word meant anything. As if the whole city hadn't been tightening around them for two days like a fist remembering it had fingers.

Wendell stood at the window of their borrowed rooms — gilded, comfortable, and locked from the outside since the second night — and watched the sun come up over spires that had never once been dark. In Corvane, the stars were ornaments. Here, people hung lanterns shaped like constellations and forgot what they were imitating. Wendell had spent twenty-six years under a sky with no magic in it at all, and even they knew that was a kind of blasphemy.

Behind them, Ilyra was packing. Not clothes — there'd be no time for clothes. She packed sigils: rolled vellum, chalk sticks wrapped in waxed linen, a bone stylus worn smooth by thirty years of other hands before hers. Each item went into her satchel with the economy of someone who had fled places before and expected to again.

"You're going to say we shouldn't go," she said.

"I'm going to say we shouldn't have stayed this long." Wendell turned. "He asked to *see* the spark yesterday. Today he asks us to bring it to him. Tomorrow he won't ask."

"Then today is our last chance to walk out the front door instead of over a wall." Ilyra tied the satchel shut. Her hands were steady; her eyes weren't. "Ondrell believes the covenant is a legal instrument, Wendell. A deed of realm. He thinks if the Court holds the spark, holds *you*, it can amend the terms. He'll want witnesses. Procedure. He wants this done so cleanly that history will call it stewardship."

"And you know that how?"

"Because it's what my order told itself, the night we broke the world." She said it flat, without flinching, and Wendell still hadn't learned how to hear it without flinching for her. "I was raised in those halls. I know every mercy they're capable of, because I've watched them perform each one."

A knock at the door. Two knocks, then a pause, then one more — the pattern Brannoc had used on patrol gates for as long as Wendell had carried a spear beside him.

Wendell crossed the room in four strides and pulled the bolt, and there he was: Brannoc Hale, captain of the Valley of Hush watch, standing in a corridor full of Corvane marble looking like a fence post someone had planted in a ballroom. He was gray-faced and road-worn, his cloak stiff with dried river mud, and behind him the two guards assigned to their floor lay slumped against the wall, breathing, unharmed, embarrassed.

"How," Wendell said.

"The valley's dying, lad. Gray all the way to the ridge now. The elders finally stopped arguing about whose fault it was and started arguing about who to send." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I volunteered. Someone had to tell you your mother's alive, and someone had to tell you the rest."

"The rest?"

Brannoc looked past Wendell's shoulder at Ilyra, and something old and tired moved through his face. "Maybe let me say it inside, where the walls aren't wearing Court colors."

But there was no time for inside, and no time for the rest, because that was the moment the bells began.

Not the covenant bell — Wendell would have known that sound through stone and soil and death itself. These were Corvane's own bells, nine towers ringing at once, and they were not ringing the hour or the alarm or any pattern Wendell knew. They were ringing *wrong*, arrhythmic, like a heart stumbling, and down in the streets people were pouring out of doors to look up at a sky that had begun, at its eastern edge, to bruise.

Ilyra went white. "It's not waiting for the throne," she breathed. "It's coming *here*. It's coming for the spark while it's still in reach—"

"Then Ondrell's invitation just became a cage with a schedule," Wendell said. "We go now. Front door, back door, I don't care. Brannoc — is there another way out of this city that doesn't go past the Hall of Nine Voices?"

"There's the Undercroft. Old aqueduct. Runs west under the Scriptorium quarter." He was already moving, herding them both into the corridor with the reflexes of twenty years of getting slower people to safety. "I came in through it. We go out through it."

They made it six steps.

The corridor's far end filled with light — cold, orderly, silver-white — and Ondrell stepped into it with eight Court wardens behind him, their sigils already lit, their faces already sorry.

"Captain Hale," Ondrell said mildly, as if greeting a colleague. "You've had a long journey. Perhaps you'd like to rest."

"I'd like you to step aside," Brannoc said.

"I imagine you would." Ondrell's gaze moved to Wendell, and it was the gaze of a man appraising a bridge he intended to cross and did not intend to thank. "Wendell Joubert. The situation has changed. You've seen the sky. The dark is coming here, to my city, and I have ninety thousand souls to keep breathing. What I need is in that censer, and what I need from you is cooperation. Give it to the Court freely, and I swear to you on the Nine Voices that you will want for nothing. Training. Standing. A place." His voice softened into something almost kind. "You've spent your whole life behind wards that kept nothing in but you. I am offering you a country."

For one terrible half-second, Wendell felt it land. That was the shame they would carry longest — not the fear, not even the grief, but the half-second when *a place* sounded like a name for everything they'd ever wanted.

Then they thought of the valley graying behind them. Of Brannoc burning letters by candlelight while the elders called it prudence. Of a god who had gone silent rather than be owned, and a woman who had confessed to helping silence him and had hated herself honestly ever since, which was more honesty than any institution in this shining city had shown in a hundred years.

"No," Wendell said.

Ondrell sighed, genuinely regretful. "I was afraid of that. Wardens — take the censer. Take the bearer too, gently. Gently," he repeated, to the warden whose sigil was already flaring, "or the ember may gutter, and then none of this will matter."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

The first warden reached for the censer at Wendell's hip, and Ilyra moved — not toward Wendell but toward the wall, dragging two fingers through the air, chalk flashing up from nowhere, and the corridor's torchlight bent sideways and went out. Darkness, total, except for the glow of eight warden-sigils and the thin gold seam of the censer against Wendell's ribs.

"Left!" Brannoc bellowed, meaning the Undercroft stair, and then he did the thing Wendell would spend the rest of their life not forgiving him for.

He stepped backward. Into the gap between them and the wardens. Spear — his old patrol spear, ash-shafted, valley-cut, the one weapon in Corvane that had never held a drop of magic — leveled at eight lit sigils like a dare.

"Brannoc—" Wendell's voice cracked on it.

"Go." Calm. Patrol-calm. The voice he used for flooded fords and panicking recruits. "Stair's ten paces left. Count them and don't look back, because if you look back I'll know you're counting wrong." A warden's sigil flared; Brannoc's spear took the man's wrist, precise as bookkeeping. "Twenty years I watched that valley eat everyone's future and call it peace. I burned letters because I was a coward with a rank. I'm not being one twice in one life."

"Captain—" Ondrell, sharp now, the courtesy cracking. "Stand down. This is treason against the—"

"It's not treason," Brannoc said, and swung the spear in a wide, patient arc, holding the whole corridor the way a dam holds a river. "Treason needs a country. I've got a valley, and it's dying, and these two are the only ones walking out of here trying to save anything bigger than themselves. That's not treason, mage. That's the *job*."

Wendell ran. Ten paces, counted, hating every one. At the seventh, light burst behind them — white, then red, then a sound like a tree falling that was not a tree — and Ilyra caught their arm and hauled them down the stair into the dark, and Wendell fought her the whole way, fought her like drowning, because looking back was the only thing left that mattered and she wouldn't let them do it.

At the bottom of the stair, in the black throat of the aqueduct, the fighting sounds above them stopped.

All of them. At once.

And then, faint through the stone, one last sound: Brannoc's voice, not shouting anymore, almost conversational, the way he talked to green recruits on their first night watch.

*"S'fine, lad. S'fine. Go on."*

Then nothing, and the bells overhead rang on, wrong, arrhythmic, and Wendell stood in the dark with the spark pressed cold against their ribs and understood that the last piece of home had just spent itself buying them ten paces.

* * *

They walked the aqueduct for hours, and Wendell remembered none of it.

Later, piecing it together from Ilyra's account, they learned they had walked upright, answered questions, and once stopped to drink from a condensation pipe without breaking stride — a body running on rails while everything that steered it sat somewhere far away, replaying seven paces of a corridor. Seven paces they'd counted. Seven paces they'd bought with a man who'd taught them to count them in the first place, on night patrols, saying *count your steps and the fear can't catch up*.

The aqueduct ended at a drainage grate in the western hills, opening onto gray scrubland under a bruised sky. The dark was visible now even in daylight — a slow tide far off to the east, swallowing the horizon in silence, and Corvane's nine towers stood against it glittering and proud and doomed, still ringing their broken pattern.

Wendell sat down in the dead grass and finally shook.

It wasn't quiet sobbing. It was rage wearing grief's face — rage at Ondrell, who offered a country with one hand and chains with the other; rage at the Court, whose Nine Voices had sung about the covenant for centuries without once asking whether the god in it was still breathing; rage at the elders who'd argued while the valley died, at the order that had devoured a god, at the wards that had caged a child for twenty-six years and called it protection. Every institution Wendell had ever trusted had failed in the same way: they had all loved the *idea* of keeping things safe more than the things themselves.

"They'll write him down as a traitor," Wendell said at last, voice scraped raw. "Brannoc. Some clerk in a clean robe will write *died resisting lawful authority* and file it, and in fifty years some student will read it and feel nothing."

"Yes," Ilyra said. She didn't soften it. That was the strange mercy of her — she never lied to make a wound smaller. "That's what institutions do. It's why we're not bringing the spark to any of them."

Wendell looked at her — ink-stained, exiled, kneeling in the gray grass with her satchel of chalk and vellum, the only person left in the world who belonged to no institution at all. Who had burned hers down herself, in a way, and had carried the ashes honestly ever since.

"The Hollow Throne," Wendell said. "How far?"

"Too far. And closer every hour, because the dark is moving toward it too, and it doesn't tire." She rose, and offered a hand, and after a moment Wendell took it. "We walk tonight. We walk until the spark tells us to stop."

The spark. Wendell touched the censer at their hip and felt it answer — faint, stubborn, warm as a coal banked for the night. Vael's last living breath. Brannoc had died for it without ever once asking what it was worth. Maybe that was the only eulogy that mattered: he'd known exactly what it cost and never once what it was for, and he'd held the door anyway.

Wendell stood, wiped their face with a sleeve, and turned west, away from the burning bells and the filing clerks and the shining, sinking city.

Behind them, far off, the nine towers of Corvane went dark — not all at once, but one by one, like stars going out in reverse.

Ahead, the road to the Hollow Throne waited, and the first shadows of evening were already learning their names.
