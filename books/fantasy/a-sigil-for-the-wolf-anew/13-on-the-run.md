## 13. On the Run

Trust, I had learned long ago, was not a wall that broke. It was a bone. When it snapped it did not shatter cleanly; it splintered, and the fragments stayed in you, and every step ground them a little deeper until either the flesh grew around them or the flesh gave out.

Wendell Joubert had not yet decided which of us was which.

Three days since Corvane. Three days since Brannoc died holding a door with his body and his oath and nothing else, because the Court had taken his sword at the gate and he had never once in his life needed a weapon to be dangerous. Three days since I had watched Wendell look at me across a burning courtyard and see — for one unguarded heartbeat — the woman whose order had eaten a god's voice, and then turn away from that sight and run *with* me anyway.

I did not know what to do with that. My whole life had been built on the assumption that people were legible, that sigils and oaths and bargains made the world readable if only you knew the script. Wendell was a page written in a hand I could not parse. They had every reason to hate me. They had said as much, once, on the gray road: *You took Vael's voice. You took my god. And now I'm supposed to walk into the dark holding your hand?*

And yet they walked beside me. That was the splinter working deeper with every mile.

We moved north through the wilds where the Gray Country thinned into something older — forest that the dark had passed over but not through, so that it stood in a strange half-life, colorless at the crown of each tree and green-black at the root, like a thing remembering itself from the bottom up. We traveled by day and hid by dusk, because the herald's mark on Wendell's shoulder blade drew the shades the way an open wound draws flies. I had bound it twice with counter-sigils inked in my own blood, and twice the binding had frayed by morning, and both times Wendell had sat very still while I worked, saying nothing, offering their back to me without being asked.

That silence was louder than any accusation. I would have preferred the accusation.

* * *

The rain found us on the fourth evening, a thin cold drizzle that the canopy turned into a hundred small betrayals dripping down our collars. We sheltered beneath a fallen oak whose roots had torn loose a wall of earth, and there, in the hollow, we made our first fire in three days — small, screened, fed with dead wood that still remembered how to burn. Wendell crouched over it like a man guarding a prisoner, and I understood why. Fire meant warmth, and warmth meant stopping, and stopping meant talking, and talking was the one country neither of us had mapped.

I broke the silence anyway. It was becoming a habit I couldn't explain.

"Your hands are shaking," I said.

"They're always shaking." Wendell held them toward the fire as if presenting evidence. "Since the wards fell. Since the sky went black. The healers in Corvane called it ward-sickness — the body remembering the hum of the valley and grieving it."

"The healers in Corvane," I said carefully, "also wanted to cut the spark out of you and study it. So perhaps weigh their diagnoses accordingly."

A muscle moved along Wendell's jaw. But they didn't flinch away, and after a moment they said, "Brannoc used to say something like that. About institutions." Their voice went rough on the name. "He'd have hated this. Hiding in a hole with a sigil-smith, eating hardtack and stolen apples."

"Did you love him?"

"I don't know how to answer that." Wendell turned an apple over in their shaking hands — one of the ones we'd taken from an orchard two valleys back, the trees there still stubbornly bearing fruit no one would come to pick. "In the valley you don't get words for things like that. You get duty. He was my captain for nine years. He taught me how to read the ridge-line, how to hold a patrol together when the fog came down, how to be quiet inside your own head. When the bell rang and he started burning letters, I thought he'd gone mad. Now I think he was the only sane person in Hush, and sanity looked exactly like grief."

"He burned letters," I repeated. I had heard the phrase before, in Wendell's account of those last days, but I had never asked what it meant.

"His correspondence. Thirty years of it. Reports to the Court, requests, petitions — everything the valley ever sent out asking for help that never came." Wendell's thumb dented the apple's skin. "He burned it all the night before the wards fell. I asked him why. He said, *Because when they write the history of what happens next, I don't want them reading my handwriting in it.*"

I sat with that for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us, water gathered and fell.

"Ilyra." Wendell said my name the way they'd begun to lately — not as an accusation, not yet as anything warmer, but as a door left deliberately unlocked. "Tell me something true. Not about the covenant. Not about the order. Something true about you."

"That's a strange request from someone who has every reason to want nothing from me but distance."

"It's the only request I can afford to make." They met my eyes across the fire. "I can't trust what you know. I can't trust what you did. But maybe I can start trusting whether you lie to me about small things. So. Small thing. Go."

I thought about refusing. Pride is a comfortable coat; I had worn mine for twenty years and it fit perfectly. But pride was also what had brought my order to the Hollow Throne in the first place, what had made forty sigil-smiths decide that a god's voice was a resource and a covenant was a leash, and I had sworn — in the ashes of my exile, alone in a border town learning to draw wards again from memory and shame — that I would never wear that coat again.

"My mother was a cook," I said. "Not a smith. She worked in a wayhouse on the Salt Road, and she could make a stew out of anything — bark, old mutton, river weed — and she used to tell me that magic was just cooking with different ingredients. Heat, patience, knowing when to stop stirring." I pulled my knees up. "She died when I was eleven. Fever. The order took me in afterward because I'd been drawing shapes in the flour dust since I could stand, and they saw a tool. I let them. For thirty years I let them use me, because being useful felt like being loved, and I didn't know the difference until the night they put Vael's voice in a jar and told me it was necessary."

Wendell didn't speak. They didn't need to. The firelight did something kind to their face, or perhaps it was only that listening is its own kind of light.

"Necessary," I said again, tasting the word. "That was the order's favorite word. Every cruelty came dressed as necessity. And I believed them, Wendell. I want you to understand that — I'm not asking forgiveness, I'm asking you to believe it. I believed that breaking the covenant would save more than it killed. I believed the dark was a disease and we were surgeons. It took me fifteen years of exile to understand that we weren't surgeons. We were thieves who'd broken into a house and couldn't understand why the family kept bleeding."

The apple rolled from Wendell's hands into the dirt between us. Neither of us reached for it.

"Why tell me that?" they asked finally. Quietly. "Why now?"

"Because you're carrying a god's last ember in your chest and walking toward the place that ate its voice, and you deserve to know exactly what kind of guide you have." I looked into the fire rather than at them. "And because you asked for something small, and that was the smallest true thing I own."

* * *

We walked north for six more days, and somewhere in those days the splinter began, slowly, against all my expectations, to knit.

It happened in increments too small to name at the time. Wendell started handing me the first watch without being asked. I started making tea the way their valley made it — bitter, over-steeped, an abomination I drank without complaint and secretly came to prefer. Wendell laughed once, a short startled sound, when I tripped on a root and swore in three dead languages, and the laugh seemed to surprise them more than the fall surprised me. Small honesties. A shared ration split unevenly in each other's favor. The word *we*, appearing in sentences where neither of us had put it on purpose.

On the ninth night, camped in the ruin of a shepherd's cot with a roof like a colander, Wendell asked me the question they had been circling since the valley.

"How does it end? If we do everything right. If we reach wherever we're going. What actually happens?"

I should have answered immediately. Instead I hesitated, because the honest answer required me to admit how little I knew, and honesty — I was discovering — is a discipline, not a virtue. It costs something every single time, even when it's free.

So I told them the truth slowly, building it piece by piece, the way I'd build a sigil.

"The covenant wasn't a contract," I began. "Everyone thinks it was — mortals serve, gods protect, terms and conditions. That's what the Court teaches. That's what my order taught. But I've spent years reading the oldest fragments, the pre-schism texts, and the covenant was never a bargain struck between unequal parties. It was a *promise*. Made between equals. Vael spoke the first word at the place where the world is thinnest, and the people who heard it answered with a word of their own, and the two words together wove the border between light and dark — not a wall, Wendell, a *seam*. Like the seam of a garment. Something that joins two pieces of cloth while letting them remain two pieces of cloth."

"And the dark?"

"The dark is what the seam was made to hold apart — but it's also, I think, what the seam was made to hold *together*. Grief given form. Vael's grief, specifically. When my order devoured the voice, they didn't kill the god. Gods don't die the way we die. They go silent, and the silence has weight, and the weight has hunger, and the hunger walks." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "Every shade you've seen. Every wolf with a scout's eyes. That's not an invasion, exactly. It's a wound, walking around looking for the knife that made it."

Wendell was silent for a long moment. Then: "Where Vael first spoke. That's where we're going."

"You've been reading my maps."

"I've been watching you stare at them." A ghost of their old dryness. "You trace the same route every night. North, past the drowned river, into the basin the old texts call the Hollow. There's a mark there you touch with your finger like it might bite you."

"It might." I pulled the map from my pack and spread it between us, weighting the corners with stones. The mark was a ring within a ring, drawn in faded ink by a cartographer four hundred years dead. "The Hollow Throne. Beneath the dark — literally. The basin sits lower than everything around it, and the dark pools there like water in a cup's bottom. Vael's first temple stood on its rim. The throne itself is below — the seat where the first word was spoken, carved from the bedrock of the world before the world learned any other words. The covenant can't be reforged anywhere else. A promise can only be kept where it was made."

"And the spark." Wendell touched their own chest, unconsciously, the way they always did. "It has to go back there."

"Yes."

"And what happens to me?"

There it was. The question underneath every other question, the one they'd carried since the shrine, since the censer, since the morning they crossed the valley's edge and watched their home begin to gray behind them. I had dodged it in Corvane. I had dodged it on the road. Every dodge had cost us a little trust, and I could feel the ledger thinning.

So I didn't dodge.

"I don't know," I said. "I've read every fragment. I've talked to every exiled scholar who would still speak to me. No text says what becomes of the bearer when the spark is returned. Some say the god takes the bearer's voice in exchange — a mirror of what my order did. Some say the bearer simply walks out, empty and unchanged. One fragment, the oldest one, says only: *the giver decides what the gift weighs.*" I let that sit between us. "Which means, if it means anything, that the ending isn't fixed. That some part of it is yours to shape. And I know how that sounds — hope dressed in scholarship — but it's the truth, and you asked for the truth, and I am done giving you anything else."

Wendell studied me across the dying fire, and for the first time since Corvane I felt seen rather than examined. Whatever calculation they were running, it came back differently this time.

"All right," they said. "Then here's my truth, since we're trading." They leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the gray in their eyes. "I'm not doing this for Vael. I tried to make myself do it for Vael — the god who went silent on my valley, the faith I was supposed to feel and mostly didn't. But sitting in that shrine, holding the stone with the ember in it, what I felt wasn't worship. It was—" they searched, and found it— "*recognition*. Like meeting something that had been waiting for me my whole life. And I think — I think the spark chose me not because I'm faithful but because I'm *stubborn*. Because I'll carry it all the way in and refuse to set it down until someone tells me honestly what it's for."

"That sounds like faith to me," I said. "The old kind. The kind the covenant was actually made of."

"Don't flatter me. I'm still deciding about you." But the edge had gone soft, and we both heard it.

* * *

The eleventh day brought us to the drowned river.

The maps called it the Sere, though no water had run in its bed since the dark first passed this way — only a residue of wet silver that moved without flowing, pooling in the low places and vanishing by noon. Crossing it meant a full day exposed on the open gravel, under a sky that had shown no stars since the night the wards fell, and I argued for going around, upriver, through the hills. Wendell overruled me. Gently, but firmly, the way Brannoc must have taught them.

"The herald's mark pulls shades to us," they said. "Open ground means we see them coming. Hills mean ambush. I've done patrols in worse than this." A pause, and then, quieter: "Trust me on terrain. It's the one thing I know better than you."

So we crossed the Sere at dawn, when the silver residue lay flattest, and for five hours we walked a riverbed that had forgotten its purpose, our boots crunching on gravel that glittered like crushed mirrors. Halfway across, Wendell stopped and crouched and picked something out of the silt: a child's toy, a wooden wolf on wheels, its paint gone gray but its shape intact. They turned it over in their hands for a long time.

"People lived here," they said. "Before. Whole lives. Someone loved this enough to carve it."

"The dark doesn't kill quickly," I said. "It unmakes slowly. Everything it passes stays standing. That's the horror of it — the Gray Country looks like a place where everyone just stepped out for a moment and forgot to come back."

Wendell set the toy down gently, facing north, as if it might walk home on its own. We crossed the rest of the riverbed without speaking, and when we climbed the far bank and looked back, the Sere lay behind us like a vein of mercury through the gray land, and ahead the land began — barely, tentatively — to remember color. A thread of rust in the bracken. A moss the color of old bronze. The world healing itself in patches, the way a body heals, from the edges inward.

"We're close," I said, and my voice came out strange, because I had been telling myself *we're close* for nine days as a way of not thinking about what closeness meant.

Wendell heard it too. "How close?"

"Two days to the rim of the Hollow. Maybe three, if the weather turns." I looked at the sky, which offered no opinion. "After that, down. The texts say the descent takes a night and a day. The dark thickens as you go — not shade-thick, not wolf-thick. Thick like deep water. Like pressure. Things live down there that the surface versions of the dark are only shadows of. And at the bottom—" I stopped myself. I had recited this list a dozen times in my head, and every item on it was true, and none of it was the thing I actually needed to say.

"At the bottom," Wendell prompted, "what?"

"At the bottom, we give the spark back. And I don't know what that costs you, and I've lied to myself for nine days that I don't care, and I'm telling you now that I do." The words came out faster than I intended, tumbling, undignified. "I've spent twenty years being precise about everything except the things that mattered most. I can draw a ward that will hold a mountain pass for a century, and I cannot say a plain sentence to save my life. But you asked me for small truths, and this one isn't small anymore, so: I don't want to reach the bottom of that place. I want to stop halfway and build a house and never go down. And I'm going to keep walking anyway, because you're walking, and because the alternative is letting the dark eat everything including the valley you came from and the road we slept on and the bitter tea you make, and I find that I mind now. I mind losing things. I didn't used to. That's what you've done to me, Wendell Joubert, and I'm not apologizing for it, I'm just — telling you. Because you asked."

Wendell did not answer right away. They stood on the far bank of a dead river with the first real color of the world at their back, and they listened the way they did everything — completely, stubbornly, as if listening were a form of combat they intended to win.

Then they reached out and took my hand. Not dramatically. Not the way lovers do in stories. They took it the way you take a rope in the dark: testing the weight of it, finding it holds.

"Small truth," they said. "I'm glad it's you walking down there with me. I couldn't do this with anyone I trusted completely. Trust like that doesn't leave room for choosing. This—" they squeezed, once— "this leaves room."

We stood like that while the silver light drained off the riverbed, and I thought: *so this is what the covenant was.* Not a wall. Not a contract. Two unequal, damaged, stubborn things, holding on in the dark because holding on was the only word either of them had left.

* * *

We made the rim of the Hollow at dusk on the second day, earlier than I'd dared hope, and stopped behind the last stand of trees to look down into it.

The basin was vast — miles wide, a bowl sunk into the bones of the world — and it was full of the dark the way a cup is full of wine. Not mist. Not shadow. A substance, black and slow, turning on currents too deep to see, and along its surface moved lights: pale, wandering, patient, hundreds of them, like lanterns carried by a procession that had been walking in circles for a thousand years. Far below, at the center, something vast caught the wandering light and threw it back in fragments. The throne. Or the hall above the throne. It was impossible to tell scale down there; the eye kept insisting the structure was both enormous and near.

And rising from the whole of it, faint as heat-shimmer, came a sound.

Not a voice. Not yet. A vibration in the throat of the world, felt in the sternum before it reached the ear — and I knew it, gods help me, I knew it instantly, because I had helped steal it. The voice of Vael, or the echo the dark had made of it, humming up out of the basin like a bell that had been struck once, ages ago, and never allowed to finish ringing.

Wendell heard it too. I watched their face change — watched the recognition move through them like weather.

"It's singing," they whispered.

"No," I said, and my mouth was dry, and every fragment I had ever read rearranged itself in that moment into a warning I should have seen weeks ago. "It's *calling*. There's a difference, and the difference is that a song wants to be heard, and a call wants an answer." I gripped their hand tighter. "Wendell — the spark in your chest. Tell me what it's doing."

They pressed their palm flat against their sternum. And I saw it happen — saw the glow bleed through their shirt, gold-white, brighter than it had been since the shrine, pulsing in rhythm with the hum rising from the dark. Answering. Call and response, across all that black distance, like two voices remembering each other.

"It's warm," Wendell said. "It's — Ilyra, it's pulling. Downward. Like a tide."

"Then we go down with the tide, tonight, before the pull gets strong enough to drag us whether we choose it or not." I shouldered my pack and checked my satchel of inks and salts, and my hands were steady, which surprised me, because everything in me was screaming. "But hear me now, and hold onto it down there where the dark will try to pry it loose: whatever calls to us from the bottom of that place, however much it sounds like Vael, however much it knows — *it is not the god*. It is the grief wearing the god's voice. The spark is the truest thing left. We follow the spark. Nothing else."

Wendell nodded. But their eyes had gone distant, fixed on the wandering lights below, and when they spoke again their voice was very quiet.

"One of those lights," they said, "is moving against the current. Coming up. Toward the rim. Toward us."

I followed their gaze, and found it, and felt the temperature of the world drop.

It was still miles off. It would not stay miles off for long.
