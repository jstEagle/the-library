## 16. Refusal

The herald wore Vael's voice like a stolen coat.

That was the thing Wendell could not stop circling, even as the dark pooled around the ruined temple's broken columns like spilled ink finding every crack in the world. The voice was *right*. It had the same low warmth their mother's prayers had been murmured into at night, the same grain the shrine-stone hummed with on feast days, back when the valley still bothered with feasts. Somewhere inside that shapeless vastness was a piece of their god, swallowed whole, and it was being used now to make an offer no honest mouth could make.

"Your valley," the herald said again, and the darkness shaped itself — reluctantly, the way water shapes around a stone — into something almost like a figure. Tall, robed, faceless where a face should be, because Vael had never shown a face and whatever wore Vael's voice could only borrow so much. "Whole. Unbroken. The wards restored, the bell ringing, your captain alive again if you wish it. The stars over the ridge as they were when you were a child. All of it, Wendell Joubert. All you must do is give me what you carry."

The censer hung heavy against Wendell's chest. Inside it, Vael's last spark pulsed like a second heartbeat, fainter than it had been a week ago, fainter every day, but still stubbornly, impossibly alive.

Behind them, Ilyra stood very still among the shattered tiles of the temple floor. Wendell didn't need to look to know her hands were moving — they always moved when she was afraid, tracing sigils in the air, on her thighs, against the seam of her coat. Little wardings that meant nothing against this. Little prayers she'd never admit were prayers.

"You can't have it," Wendell said.

"Consider—"

"I said you can't have it." Their own voice came out rougher than they meant, scraped raw by three days of hard travel since the Court's betrayal, since Brannoc's body went cold holding a door so they could run. "You're not him. You're wearing his voice and you think I can't tell, but I can. He never asked for anything. That was the whole point of him. You sit there in his silence asking for everything."

The dark rippled. For a moment — just a moment — the borrowed figure flickered, and beneath it Wendell saw something else: not malice, but hunger. A grief so old and so large it had curdled into appetite. The god's sorrow, given form, given teeth.

"The valley is gone," Wendell said, quieter now, and found the words came easier the quieter they went. "I know what you're offering because I've already mourned it twice. Once when the wards fell and once when I watched it gray out behind me from the ridge. What you're showing me isn't my home. It's a picture of my home painted by something that never lived in it. My mother's kitchen. Brannoc swearing at his boots. The smell of rain on the wardline stones. You don't have those. You couldn't build them if you tried, because they weren't things, they were people, and people don't come back when you hand them a sky full of painted stars."

"I can give you *everything*—"

"You can give me nothing," Wendell said. "You took his voice. Take mine too, if you want it. But the spark goes where I carry it, and I carry it freely, and that's the part you'll never understand. That's why he went silent and you went hungry. You think love is a price. It isn't. It never was."

* * *

For a long moment the temple held nothing but wind through broken rafters and the small sound of Ilyra breathing.

Then the herald stopped pretending to be a figure at all.

The dark came off the walls like a tide reversing. The columns vanished into it; the floor; the horizon. Wendell felt the temperature drop through their boots, felt the spark in the censer flare hot enough to sting through lead, and understood, with the calm of someone standing on the last plank of a burning bridge, exactly what they had done.

"You refuse," the herald said, and Vael's warm voice cracked down the middle into something with no warmth left in it at all. "Then be refused. Be unmade, little keeper. I will take your name out of the world so thoroughly that even she—" the dark coiled toward Ilyra, tasting her, "—will wake one morning unable to remember why her hands are empty."

"No," Ilyra said.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. She stepped forward — past Wendell, in front of them, which was absurd, which was suicidal, which was the most Ilyra thing Wendell had ever witnessed — and drew a sigil in the air with two fingers and the blood from a cut she'd opened on her own palm without Wendell seeing her do it.

"You don't get his name," she said. "You stole a god's voice and you think names are things you can take. They aren't. They're *given*. That's the whole covenant, you enormous grieving idiot, and you were there when it was written."

The sigil caught light. Not firelight — the spark's light, answering her from inside the censer, gold threading up through the seams of the dark like dawn under a door. The herald recoiled. Not far. But it recoiled.

"Run," Ilyra said, without turning her head. "Now. While it's remembering what pain is."

They ran.

* * *

They ran until the temple ruins were a smudge behind them and the gray country closed around them like a fist — drained fields, dead orchards, a road that wound through colorless hills toward the distant bruise of the Hollow Throne. Only when the light changed — truly changed, a thin real daylight breaking through clouds that weren't made of the dark — did Ilyra finally stumble to a halt beside a fallen wall and put her back against it and slide down it until she was sitting in the dust, shaking.

Wendell sat down next to her. Set the censer between them. The spark inside had gone quiet, spent-feeling, like a heart after a sprint.

"It marked me," Wendell said. "Didn't it. I felt it go in. Like a hook behind my sternum."

"Yes." Ilyra was staring at her own bloodstained fingers. "Unmaking isn't death. Death leaves a body, a grave, a name on a stone. Unmaking is—" She exhaled. "It takes longer. Days, maybe. You'll feel yourself thinning. Things will start sliding. Faces. Words. Then places. Then you." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "I've seen it done before. To a smith who broke an oath. It took four days and none of us could remember what we'd lost by the fifth."

"Cheerful."

"I'm not finished. There's a counter." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a smear of ash and blood across one cheekbone, and Wendell's chest did something complicated and painful that had nothing to do with hooks or gods. "There's always a counter. A mark of unmaking is a claim, and claims can be contested. But contesting it means standing in its path and saying *no* with your whole self — your name offered against theirs — and whoever does it shares the mark. Half the hook in each of us instead of all of it in one." She laughed, short and bitter. "Which buys time, not salvation. Maybe six days instead of four. Long enough to reach the throne. Possibly."

"So we split the difference," Wendell said. "Six days together instead of four apart."

"That's not—"

"Ilyra." Wendell turned to face her fully. The gray country stretched out ahead of them, the throne a smudge on the horizon, the armies of the Court somewhere behind and the dark somewhere everywhere, and none of it mattered even slightly compared to what they had to say, which they had been not-saying for weeks, through shade-attacks and court betrayals and confessions that shattered trust and rebuilt it by hand. "We're going to die. Maybe in six days, maybe tomorrow, maybe at the throne itself doing the one thing that might actually work. I am not spending whatever's left of me not saying it."

Ilyra went very still. Her fingers, traitorously, began tracing a sigil against her knee — and then stopped, deliberately, pressing flat.

"Say it, then," she whispered.

"I love you." The words came out steadier than Wendell expected, as though they'd been waiting so long they'd polished themselves smooth. "Not the way I loved the valley, which was easy, because the valley never asked anything of me except that I stay. I love you the way the covenant was written — the way the spark showed me at the first temple. Between equals. Freely. Knowing the cost. I loved you before you told me what your order did, and I hated you for a night and half a day after, and then I loved you again anyway, because it turns out the truth didn't change anything except making it heavier. I love you with a hook of unmaking in my chest and I would say it again with both of them in there. That's what I've got. It's not much. But it's all true."

Ilyra made a sound that was half laugh and half something broken loose.

"Do you know what I was doing," she said, "when the wards fell? Before I ever saw you?" She wasn't looking at Wendell; she was looking at her hands, turning them over as if reading old sigils scarred into the skin. "I was carving a new name into the covenant-stone at our order's sanctuary. Trying to fix what we broke. Alone, at night, because no one else in my order believed it could be fixed, and I thought — if I could just get the wording right, if I could find the exact phrase, some perfect arrangement of lines — then everything would be mended and no one would ever have to know how badly we'd failed." She looked up. "And then the sky went black and a mercenary from nowhere pulled me out of a collapsing wardline without knowing who I was or what I'd done, and complained about it the entire time."

"I did not complain the *entire* time."

"You complained constantly. It was the first honest thing anyone had said to me in ten years." Her mouth curved. "I love you too, Wendell Joubert. I loved you when you defended me before the Court with no idea what you were defending. I loved you after my confession, which should have been impossible, and I've decided to stop questioning it, because I'm a smith and I know craftsmanship when I see it, and you are the finest thing I never made." Her voice frayed at the edge. "So yes. Split the mark. Six days. And if we live past the throne — if any of this works, if I end up carrying a god's spark and standing at the border of the world for the rest of however long that is — I want you there. Not because the covenant needs a keeper. Because I do."

Wendell reached over and took her bloodstained hand. Held it. Outside the fallen wall, the gray country waited, and the dark waited beyond it, and somewhere behind them Ondrell's army ground forward toward the same destination like a wheel of knives. None of it touched the small circle of dust where they sat.

"How does it work," Wendell said. "Splitting it. Tell me what to do."

Ilyra drew a slow breath. Took out her knife.

"We stand facing each other," she said. "You speak my name, I speak yours, and we both say *no* to the mark — aloud, at the same moment, meaning it completely. Names are given, remember. We give each other ours, right into the dark's teeth, and dare it to argue." She managed a crooked smile. "It's either profound covenant-work or the most reckless thing either of us has ever done, and given the competition, that's genuinely saying something."

"Say it with me, then."

They knelt in the dust of the gray country, forehead nearly to forehead, the censer between them glowing faint gold, and spoke each other's names into the wind — and somewhere far off, in the folds of the unlit world, something with a stolen voice screamed.

The hook split. Wendell felt it happen: a tearing, a redistribution, half the cold lodged behind their sternum shifting sideways into Ilyra, and in exchange a strange lightness, a shared weight, the way a burden carried by two is somehow more than half as bearable. Ilyra gasped, gripped their hands harder, held on.

When it passed, they stayed kneeling a moment longer, foreheads touching, breathing the same thin air.

Then Ilyra lifted her head, and her eyes went past Wendell's shoulder to the horizon, and every muscle in her face went taut.

"Wendell," she said quietly. "Look at the road."

Wendell turned.

Far away, along the pale thread of the highway they'd abandoned days ago, dust was rising — not gray dust, not the country's dead pallor, but a long rolling cloud kicked up by thousands of marching feet, and above it, catching the thin light like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath, the banners of the Sigil Court.

Ondrell had found another way to the throne. And he was not marching alone.

Ahead of his army, scattered black across the hills like spilt ink running downhill, the dark was moving too — converging, hurrying, all of it flowing toward the same hollow point on the horizon.

Six days, Ilyra had said.

Looking at that horizon, Wendell privately doubted they'd be given one.
