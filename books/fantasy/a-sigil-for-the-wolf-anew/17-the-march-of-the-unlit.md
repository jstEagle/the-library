## 17. The March of the Unlit

We saw the army before we heard it.

Ilyra had been teaching Wendell to read the land's silences — which stillness was sleep, which was death, which was the dark simply holding its breath — when the horizon changed. Not darkened. *Changed.* The gray country east of us, the drained and colorless waste where nothing grew, grew a second skyline: a ridge of banners and spearpoints and lantern-glow that should not have existed in a place the light had abandoned.

"Torches," Wendell said. They stood beside me on the spine of a dead hill, their breath fogging in air that had no warmth left to give. "That many torches. That's not a raiding party."

"That's an argument," I said. "The Court has decided to stop debating."

Ondrell's host came on like a wound opening across the plain. I could pick out the shapes even at this distance because I had trained beside them once, before my exile, before my name became something mages spat rather than spoke: the ranked columns of the Sigil Court's war-mages, each one wrapped in wards stitched into their very skin; the siege-sigilists hauling their great brass frames on groaning wagons, lenses wide as cartwheels; the rank upon rank of hired steel beneath them, ten thousand men who had never seen the dark and did not believe in it.

Ten thousand men marching toward the Hollow Throne with the confidence of people who thought the covenant was a legal technicality.

"They're going for it," Wendell said. There was something awful in how flat their voice had gone. "Ondrell. She's not chasing us anymore. She's just going to take it first."

"She was always going to." I kept my eyes on the columns. "A spark like Vael's, unclaimed, sitting in a hole in the world? To the Court, that isn't a god's last ember. It's an asset. An heirloom without an owner. They'll put it in a vault and call it stewardship, and in a hundred years some archmage will be drawing power from it to light their bathwater."

"And the dark?"

I didn't answer right away, because the answer was in front of us, moving.

The army marched through the Gray Country as if the gray were weather. And the gray let them — at first. But I could see what they couldn't, from this height, with sigil-sight bleeding faint silver at the edges of my vision: every ward the war-mages cast, every protective circle they laid down behind their advancing line like a trail of breadcrumbs, *frayed* the moment they moved past it. The dark didn't attack the barriers. It drank them. Each ward they spent made the ground behind them a little softer, a little more open, a little more *theirs* — the dark's, I mean. The grief's.

"They think they're building a road," I said. "They're building a throat."

* * *

We ran ahead of them for two days.

It sounds impossible. It was nearly impossible. But an army moves at the speed of its slowest wagon, and we moved at the speed of desperation, cutting across country no column could follow — scree fields where the stones had gone light as bone, dry riverbeds whose water had been drunk by the dark years ago, forests of trees that still stood but had forgotten how to be anything but gray. Wendell learned the terrain faster than I did. Valley-born, ridge-walker, mercenary patrol: they read broken ground the way I read sigils, instinctively, hungrily, and more than once they hauled me down into a fold of earth seconds before a Court scout-wing passed overhead, their lanterns sweeping, their detection-wards humming like trapped flies.

The third night, we crouched in the ruin of a shepherd's cot — walls half-collapsed, roof open to a sky that still refused to give us stars — and Wendell finally asked the question I'd been dreading.

"If Ondrell reaches the throne first," they said, "what happens?"

I fed a twig into our tiny fire — small enough to hide, large enough to boil the moss-tea that was keeping us upright. "Best case? She seals the spark in a warded reliquary and spends the next decade studying it while the dark eats everything outside the Court's borders. Worst case—" I turned the twig over. "Worst case, she tries to *use* it. Draw on it. Bind it into the Court's great working and make Vael's last ember a battery."

"And the dark comes for it."

"The dark is already coming for it. It's been coming since the day Vael went silent. Every step that army takes toward the throne, every barrier they tear or drain, is one less thing between the grief and the ember." I looked up at them. Their face was firelit on one side, shadowed on the other, and I thought — not for the first time, and with a fresh ache each time — that I had done this to them. Dragged them out of a valley where nothing hurt, into a world where everything did. "We can't outrun them anymore, Wendell. Tomorrow they'll close the gap. We have to go under."

"Under?"

"There are ways into the Hollow Throne that don't run along the surface roads. My order used them, back when we still had an order." I said it evenly. I had told them what my order did — devoured Vael's voice, broke the covenant, birthed the dark out of a god's stolen grief — and Wendell had not forgiven me, but they had stayed, and staying was its own kind of sentence we served together. "The Underways. Old pilgrim tunnels from when the throne was a temple and not a wound. If we move tonight, we come up inside the throne-hall hours before Ondrell's vanguard breaches the doors."

"And if the dark gets there first anyway?"

"Then we lose," I said. "But we lose *there*, with the spark in reach, instead of here, watching from a hill."

Wendell was quiet a long moment. Then they reached into their coat and brought out the leaden censer, and the spark inside it answered the motion with a pulse of warmth I felt through three layers of cloth — a heartbeat that wasn't ours, patient and small and terribly alive.

"It's been quieter lately," Wendell said. "The spark. Like it knows."

"Gods know things," I said. "Even dying ones. Even *especially* dying ones."

They closed their hands around the censer, and I watched them do the thing they did now instead of praying: they listened. Whatever they heard, it settled them. When they looked up, the fear was still there, but it had been arranged into something usable.

"Then we go under," they said. "Tonight."

* * *

The Underway mouth was a crack in the side of a ravine, hidden behind a curtain of stone that only opened for a sigil my order had carved into the world before the world learned to hate us. I drew it with numb fingers, and the rock sighed apart, and the smell that came out was old incense and older dust — the smell of a faith mid-collapse, frozen at the moment of its failure.

We went down with one lantern and the spark.

The tunnels were grander than any ruin had a right to be. Pilgrim-halls with ceilings lost in darkness above our lamplight; colonnades where every pillar bore the same worn sigil — Vael's mark, the open hand — rubbed smooth by centuries of desperate fingers touching it for luck. Wendell trailed their hand along them as we walked, and I saw them flinch at the third pillar, then the seventh, then stop touching altogether.

"They're warm," they said quietly.

"Faintly. Residually. Stone remembers heat longer than it remembers love." I regretted the words the moment they left me. Bitterness was a habit I hadn't managed to break, and Wendell deserved better than my habits. "I'm sorry. What I meant is — yes. Something of him is still in the walls. The spark will be stronger down there. Strong enough to finish waking, maybe. Strong enough to—"

"To what?" Wendell stopped walking. In the lantern light their eyes found mine, and I understood that they had been carrying this question for days, politely, the way you carry a blade edge-down. "You've never actually said, Ilyra. We take the spark to the throne. Then what? You told me the covenant can be reforged where Vael first spoke. You've never told me *how*. Who speaks? Who pays?"

The tunnel waited around us, patient as all old holy places are patient.

"I don't know," I said. It was the truth, and it cost me more than any lie I'd ever told. "My order's records end the night we — the night it happened. Everything after the silencing is blank pages. Whatever rite was meant to restore a god, we destroyed it along with his voice. All I know is what the oldest fragment says, the one line we couldn't erase because it wasn't written in ink. *What was given freely cannot be taken. What is offered cannot be owned.*" I spread my empty hands. "I know the shape of it, Wendell. A gift, at the place of the first word. I don't know the mechanics. I don't know the price. I don't know if the giver survives the giving."

Wendell held my gaze for a long time. Above us, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, came a sound neither of us wanted to hear: a deep, rolling concussion, and another, and then a long grinding roar — the sound of an army's siege-sigils firing, far away and yet near enough to shiver dust from the ceiling.

"They're breaching the outer barriers," I breathed. "Ahead of schedule. Ondrell's spending her wards like water—"

The second roar was different. Deeper. And underneath it, rising through the stone, through the floor, through the soles of my boots, came a vibration I had felt only once before — the night the valley's wards shattered, the night the stars went out. The dark, answering. The grief, hearing the door open and beginning to pour.

Wendell grabbed my wrist. "The tunnels. Are they sealed against it?"

"They were built by believers." I was already running. "Believers are always the last to imagine betrayal."

We ran. The pilgrim-halls blurred past, the open-handed pillars streaming by like the ribs of some vast drowned beast, and behind us the vibrations grew from tremor to shudder to a rhythm — and it was the rhythm that froze my blood, because it wasn't random. It wasn't collapse. It was *footsteps*. Thousands of them. The dark had found the surface entrances, the ones Ondrell's own barrage had cracked open like eggshells, and it was coming down into the holy places on legs of shadow, drawn by the same scent we carried: the spark, the ember, the last living piece of the god everyone had failed.

And ahead of us — below us, deeper than the deepest hall — the spark felt it too. The censer at Wendell's chest flared so bright that light spilled through their coat seams, through the lantern's glass, through the grout-lines of ten-thousand-year-old tiles, until the tunnel around us glowed like dawn under a door.

"It's calling," Wendell gasped as we ran. "Why is it calling?"

"Because it's almost home," I said. "And because everything else is coming home too."

The tunnel forked, and forked again, and I chose by instinct and by the growing warmth underfoot, and then the passage opened all at once into a gallery so vast our lantern was a match-flame in a cathedral — and across that gallery, filling it floor to unseen ceiling, stood the dark.

Not flooding. *Standing.* Waiting, the way it had waited at the threshold of the throne-hall since the day of the silencing, a held breath a thousand years long. It had come down ahead of the army, ahead of the footsteps, ahead of everything, because it had never needed to march. It had only ever needed to be invited.

And Ondrell's siege-sigils, cracking the barriers above us, were doing exactly that.

At the gallery's far end, past the standing dark, past the wall of unlit that did not move and did not need to, I saw it: a seam of older, warmer light. The doors of the Hollow Throne. The first temple. The place of the first word.

Between us and it stood everything the world had ever grieved.

Wendell's hand found mine in the glowing dark. The spark pulsed between us like a shared heart.

"We go through," they said.

It was not a question, and there was no time to answer it anyway — because behind us, down the tunnel we had come by, the footsteps arrived.
