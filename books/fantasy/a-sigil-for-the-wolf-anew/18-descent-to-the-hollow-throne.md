## 18. Descent to the Hollow Throne

The armies made the mountains sound like the sea.

Wendell lay flat against a ridge of black stone above the valley of the Hollow Throne and listened to ten thousand boots, ten thousand banners, ten thousand small human noises — coughing, cursing, the creak of leather and the groan of wagon wheels — all of it rolling together into one vast gray roar that never quite ended. Ondrell's host filled the pass below like water filling a cup: ranks of sigil-bound soldiers in lacquered half-armor, siege-mages on palanquins with their hands wrapped in glowing bandages, supply trains stretching back along the road until they vanished into the haze where the world's color used to be.

Above them all, the sky was wrong. It had been wrong for days. The stars had not come back since the night they went out; what remained was a low, bruised ceiling of cloud that did not move and did not rain and sometimes seemed, when Wendell looked at it too long, to be looking back.

"They'll march at dawn," Ilyra said beside them. She was lying so close their shoulders touched, her ink-stained fingers sketching in the frost-dust of the stone without quite touching it — the descent route, drawn for the fourth time. "Ondrell will want to take the throne-hall before the dark can consolidate. He thinks it's a door he can hold."

"Is it?"

"It's a mouth." She wiped the sketch away with her palm. "And we're going to slide down its throat while it's busy chewing him."

Below the ridge, on the army's eastern flank, something answered her. A horn sounded — not one of the Court's bright silver horns but a deep, wet note that came up through the ground rather than the air, and every hair on Wendell's arms rose to meet it. Along the far side of the pass, where the land fell away into the country the dark had already eaten, the horizon shifted. Not clouds. Not shadow, either, not anymore. Lately the dark had been developing texture, depth, edges — a slow tide learning the shape of waves.

The held breath, Wendell thought. The whole world holding it.

They climbed down the back of the ridge in the last hour before light, Ilyra leading, Wendell counting their own heartbeats because counting anything else felt like tempting fate. The censer hung at their chest under their coat, chained to a baldric across both shoulders, and it had grown heavy in a way that had nothing to do with lead. Vael's spark inside it burned low. They could feel it the way you feel a fire dying in the next room — a warmth thinning, an ember-light flickering behind the ribs, smaller every day. Some mornings now it barely woke at all.

*Hold on*, Wendell told it, as they had told it every day since Corvane. *Just hold on.*

It gave no answer. It never had. But it pressed faintly against the inside of their sternum, the way a sleeping animal leans toward a hand, and that had to be enough.

* * *

The way down began in a dead river.

Ilyra had found it on their third day scouting the pass — a channel of smooth gray stone where water had once run down from the heights into the throne-valley, long since drained or drunk or unmade. It ran beneath the armies' camp in a vaulted tunnel of ancient masonry, and it ran in the right direction: down, and inward, toward the black seam in the mountain's flank where every map Ilyra had ever stolen or memorized agreed the Hollow Throne waited.

They walked it by touch. Light was a debt they couldn't afford; the dark noticed light the way spiders notice struggling flies. So Wendell followed the whisper of Ilyra's coat ahead of them, one hand trailing the damp stone wall, and tried not to think about how the silence here differed from the Valley of Hush. That silence had been a held shield, a wall of quiet. This silence had weight. It leaned. It listened.

Twice, in the first hours, they heard things moving in the walls.

Not in the tunnel — *in* the walls themselves, behind the stone, a soft slithering like sand pouring through an hourglass, always keeping pace, always just out of sight. The first time, Ilyra stopped dead and stood so still Wendell bumped into her back. Neither of them breathed for a count of fifty. The slithering passed on overhead, unhurried, and faded.

"It knows we're here," Wendell whispered.

"The dark has known where you are since the herald marked you." Ilyra's voice was flat and very quiet. "It doesn't matter. What's marked, it waits for. It wants you to arrive. We're not sneaking past it, love — we're being *allowed*. The trick is making sure we're allowed all the way to the throne before it decides dinner is served."

That was the plan in its entirety, and they both knew it. There was no second gate, no hidden road, no clever escape. They would walk down into the place where a god had been silenced, carrying the last living piece of that god, and everything between the entrance and the altar would be trying to stop them or eat them or — worse, far worse — *invite* them. The herald's offer still sat in Wendell's memory like a splinter under the skin: the valley restored, whole, green, their mother's kitchen smelling of bread, Brannoc laughing at the hearth with both his ears intact and his blood still in him.

*Come home*, the dark had said, with Vael's stolen voice. *All you have to do is hand it over.*

They walked on.

* * *

Near midday — if midday meant anything under a sky like this — the dead river opened into a chamber that stopped Wendell's breath.

It was a temple. Or it had been, once, before anyone thought to carve the word *temple* into history. The cavern was vast beyond sense, its ceiling lost in darkness, its floor worn glass-smooth by feet that had come here when the world was young. And everywhere — on floor, on pillars, on the curving walls — were sigils.

Not like Ilyra's. Not like anything the Sigil Court drew in their luminous inks with their careful geometries. These were older, vaster, cruder and more certain, cut into the living rock a finger's depth and more, and though they were dark and dead, something in them still hummed against Wendell's awareness like a struck bell gone silent but not finished ringing.

Ilyra sank to her knees in front of the nearest pillar.

Wendell had seen her weep exactly twice: once at Brannoc's grave-mound, scratched out in haste in the gray country, and once in the ruins of the first temple, when the spark had shown them the covenant made between equals. This was neither. Her face didn't move at all. She knelt there with her hand hovering a hair's breadth from the carved lines, trembling, and when she finally spoke her voice came out sanded flat.

"This is the source of everything my order ever wrote. Every sigil, every ward, every working — all of it is a copy of copies of copies of *this*. We've been tracing a letter we found, and this—" her hand shook, "—this is the hand that wrote it."

"Can you read it?"

"No." A pause. "Yes. A little. It's like hearing your own language spoken in a voice you dreamed once." She stood, and steadied herself against Wendell's arm, and Wendell pretended not to notice how much she needed the steadying. "There's a stair. Over there, past the third pillar. Down. All the way down."

The stair had been a waterfall once, maybe — a slot in the rock descending in wide shallow steps, big enough for ten abreast, sized for congregations. Their footsteps rang wrong on it. Too loud. Every step echoed up and up and kept going, and somewhere far below, Wendell could have sworn, each echo came back a half-beat late, like something enormous matching their pace in the deep.

The spark felt it too. Under Wendell's coat, the censer warmed — faintly, fitfully, a coal remembering fire — and pulsed once, hard, like a heartbeat stumbling over a step.

"I feel it," Ilyra said softly. She hadn't touched the censer; she didn't need to. "It's close to home, Wendell. Whatever's left of it remembers this place."

"That comforts me," Wendell said, "and horrifies me, and I'd like you to explain which one I should listen to."

"Both." Her teeth showed white in the dark, briefly. It wasn't quite a smile. "Both is correct."

* * *

They slept in shifts on the stairs, wedged into a niche where the wall curved away, sharing a strip of waxed cloth and half a loaf gone stale. Wendell took first watch and spent it watching Ilyra sleep instead, which she would have mocked them mercilessly for, which was why they didn't tell her.

Her sigils were fraying. She'd hidden it for two days, and she was good at hiding it, but watchfulness makes liars useless. The fine line-work on her forearms — the lattice of wards she'd redrawn every dawn since the border broke — had begun to blur at the edges, ink bleeding into skin, geometry sagging the way old rope sags. Each morning her redrawing took longer. Each morning a little more of it failed to take. The dark down here pressed against her work like deep water against a hull, patient, constant, finding every seam.

She woke near the end of his watch, instantly, completely, the way soldiers wake, and caught them looking.

"How bad?" she asked. No preamble. She'd learned Wendell's silences.

"You tell me."

She rolled her sleeve and held her forearm up to the faint phosphorescence of the fungus clinging to the stair-wall. In the pale light Wendell could see it clearly: the ward-lines wavering like reflections on disturbed water, three of the anchor-sigils already smeared to nothing, the rest holding by habit more than power.

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three, if I don't spend anything on offense. After that I'm just a woman with interesting tattoos."

"We'll be at the throne by then."

"Yes," she said, in a tone that agreed entirely and believed none of it, and pulled her coat back around her shoulders, and put her head against Wendell's shoulder because there was no one left alive to perform toughness for. "Sleep. I'll wake you if the walls start talking."

"Do they talk?"

"Not yet," she said. "Down here, yet is a very short distance away."

* * *

The last stretch of the descent was not stone.

They came around a bend in the stair and the steps simply ended, running out into a plain of smooth black glass that stretched away and downward into immensity — a frozen lake of shadow, or the surface of the dark itself, pinned here like a specimen under crystal. Far across it, at the exact center of that buried world, something rose: a shape that resolved slowly as they stared, and resolved wrong, because the eye kept trying to make it architecture when it wanted to be anatomy. Columns that might have been ribs. A dome that might have been a bowed skull. And at its heart, on a dais of fused night, a seat.

The Hollow Throne. Empty. It had been empty for four hundred years, and somehow emptiness had never looked so much like waiting.

"There should be guards," Wendell whispered. Their voice came out crushed small, swallowed by the glass plain. "Shades. Wolves. The herald. *Something.* Where's the army, Ilyra? Where's the war?"

"Gone ahead," she said quietly. "Or called off. Or—" she stopped, and Wendell watched her arrive at the same thought they were drowning in. "Or it doesn't need one anymore. Not here. This is the god's ground. The dark doesn't fight on the god's ground. It just... attends."

They stepped onto the glass.

Their boot made no sound at all. Neither did hers. The plain swallowed footfalls the way it swallowed light, and they crossed it side by side, close enough that their sleeves brushed, walking toward a throne where a god had been silenced and a covenant had broken and everything — the wards, the valley, the stars, the whole wounded shape of the world — had begun to die.

Halfway across, the spark woke fully for the first time in days.

It flared under Wendell's ribs like sunrise trapped in lead, sudden and searing, and they staggered, and Ilyra caught their elbow, and for one raw instant Wendell felt what the spark felt: recognition, vast and terrible, an ember sensing the hearth it had been torn from. The throne-hall ahead of them was not empty to the spark. It was *home*. It was the room where its voice had been taken, and it was pulling Wendell forward the way rivers pull fallen leaves.

"Wendell." Ilyra's grip tightened. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're lit. From inside." She turned them by the shoulder, made them look at her, and her face — gods, her face, careful and cracked open at once. "You have to carry it. It can't carry you. If it drags you onto that dais ahead of yourself, whatever's waiting will know exactly how hungry to set the table."

Wendell breathed. In, and out, the way she'd taught them for drawing wards — the way you breathe before any line you can't afford to smudge. The flare dimmed. The warmth settled back to an ember's stubborn pulse, and the lit feeling behind their eyes guttered out, and the throne-hall was only a ruin again, and the glass underfoot only cold.

They walked the rest of the way.

At the threshold — a doorway tall enough to swallow a cathedral, its frame carved with the oldest sigils yet, worn nearly smooth by centuries of wind that had no business existing this far underground — Ilyra stopped. She pressed her fraying forearm against the stone, and Wendell watched her read what was written there, and watched the color leave her face in real time, shade by shade, until she matched the gray country.

"What is it?" Wendell asked. "What does it say?"

She was quiet a long moment. Above them, impossibly, distantly, the armies' horns began to sound — Ondrell's silver calls, bright and arrogant even filtered through a mountain — and beneath them, answering, that deep wet note from the other side of the world, closer now, so much closer.

"It says *welcome home*," Ilyra said at last. "In Vael's handwriting. And underneath it, newer, cut by different hands—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to, because Wendell had already tipped their head back and read it themselves, the letters scored deep and recent into the threshold of the god's own house:

**THE GUEST OF HONOR IS EXPECTED. THE TABLE IS LAID FOR THREE.**

From the darkness inside the hall, patient as the grave, unhurried as the held breath of the world, something stood up from the Hollow Throne.
