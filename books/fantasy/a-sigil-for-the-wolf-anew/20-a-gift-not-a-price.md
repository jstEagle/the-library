## 20. A Gift, Not a Price

The spark was dying in my hands.

I felt it the way you feel a bird's heartbeat through your fingers — quick, frantic, smaller than it had been an hour ago, smaller than it had been when Ilyra first pried it loose from Vael's shrine stone and told me only a sworn servant could carry it. The leaden censer was gone now; somewhere in the descent I had stopped pretending the spark needed a vessel other than me. It sat against my palms like a coal wrapped in skin, and it was going out.

The Hollow Throne breathed around us.

That was the only word for it. The great hall beneath the world was not silent — silence would have been a mercy — it *breathed*, long and slow, a darkness drawing in and letting out, and every exhalation pressed against my skin like cold water. The walls were black stone veined with something that might once have been gold. Where sigils had been carved, they hung broken, their lines snapped mid-stroke, a language interrupted mid-sentence a thousand years ago and never allowed to finish.

Ilyra stood three paces from the throne itself, her hands raised, her last working sigils burning in the air around her like moths made of script. They were fraying. I could see it — the elegant lines she drew so effortlessly, the ink-and-iron beauty of her craft, unraveling thread by thread as the dark leaned against them. She was sweating. Her braid had come half-loose. She looked, for the first time since I'd dragged her out of a collapsing wardline what felt like a lifetime ago, afraid.

"It's not enough," she said quietly. "My name isn't enough. The wards need a god's weight behind them, or they're just marks on air."

Behind us, at the mouth of the hall, Ondrell's voice rang off the stone.

"Then give the Court its chance to provide one."

* * *

He came down the stair with six of his mages behind him, their sigils blazing white and arrogant, and for a moment the hall looked almost like it had in the visions — the first temple, the covenant of equals, light answering light. Then I saw that Ondrell's mages weren't lighting the dark. They were just standing in it, and their light was a small, frightened thing, a candle held up in a cathedral.

Ondrell himself looked terrible. He had marched an army across the Gray Country and torn the last barriers open doing it, and he knew it, and he had decided that knowing it changed nothing. That was the thing about Ondrell: he was not a fool. He was worse. He was a man who saw the cliff edge clearly and believed his will could pave it.

"Wendell." He spread his hands. Blood on his sleeves — Brannoc's blood, though he'd have said it wasn't his fault, and maybe it wasn't, maybe it was all our faults, every institution that ever decided a god was an asset. "You've carried it further than anyone could have asked. Give it to us now. The Court has a thousand sworn smiths. We can anchor the covenant from Corvane, properly, with structure, with—"

"With yourselves at the center," Ilyra said. Her voice cracked like ice underfoot. "That's how it broke the first time. You've read the same ruins I have, Ondrell. You know what happened here."

"I know your order happened here," Ondrell said, and there it was, the old wound, the old blame, passed hand to hand like a coin worn smooth. "I know the Sigil-Smiths devoured a god's voice and called it scholarship. I know everything downstream of that is ash. Which is why the spark should never again be trusted to an individual."

"And instead to a committee," I said.

I hadn't meant to say it aloud. But the dark laughed.

It didn't laugh with sound. It laughed the way a landslide laughs, the way winter laughs — a shifting, a settling, a vast attention turning toward us. At the far end of the hall, where the throne sat empty, the darkness gathered itself into something almost like a shape. Almost like a face. The herald's stolen voice, Vael's voice, poured out of it and filled the hollow of the hall the way water fills a cup.

*LITTLE KEEPER.* The words came gentle, which was the cruelest thing about them. *YOU HAVE WALKED SO FAR. GIVE IT HERE, AND YOUR VALLEY LIVES AGAIN. THE BELL RINGS. THE STARS COME BACK OVER THE RIDGE. YOUR CAPTAIN'S DEATH BECOMES A STORY WITH AN ENDING INSTEAD OF A HOLE.*

Brannoc. Even now, even dead these two days with his body left holding a door in Corvane, the dark knew exactly where to press.

And gods help me, some part of me wanted it. Some tired, grieving, valley-born part of me stood there in the breathing dark and thought: *one word, and home again. One word, and none of this ever happened.*

But I had learned things on the road that no herald could unteach. I had learned that the valley of my memory was already gone — that the place I missed had stopped existing the moment the stars went out, and what the dark offered was not restoration but taxidermy. A valley stuffed and posed behind new walls, with me inside it, forever, a keeper of nothing but a display case.

"You don't want the spark," I said. My voice sounded strange in the huge hall, small and stubborn. "You want it *spent*. Swallowed. Ended. Because if it's given — freely, the way the first covenant was made — then the grief that built you doesn't get to be the last word."

The face-shape rippled. For one instant, underneath the stolen voice, I heard something else — something raw and enormous and wounded, a sound like a god weeping without eyes.

*IT CANNOT BE GIVEN,* the dark said, and now the gentleness was gone, and what remained was hunger wearing patience like a mask. *ALL THINGS ARE TAKEN OR SPENT. THAT IS THE LESSON OF THIS HALL.*

"No," Ilyra whispered beside me. And then louder, her fraying sigils guttering around her: "No — Wendell — look at it —"

I was already looking. Not at the dark. At the spark.

It had flared.

Small still, dying still, but when the dark had spoken those words — *it cannot be given* — the ember against my palms had burned *brighter*, the way a truth makes a lamp stand up straighter. And understanding came over me the way dawn comes over a ridge: not all at once, but completely.

Vael had not been devoured because the spark was weak. Vael had gone silent because a god who is owned cannot speak — and the spark could not be spent because it had never been currency. Not to Ondrell's Court, not to the dark, not to any hand that reached to take it. Every attempt to seize it, hoard it, bargain for it, had only starved it further. It was not a treasure.

It was a gift. It had always only ever been a gift. That was the whole covenant, the first word, the promise between equals I'd seen in the ruined temple: *freely offered, freely received, or nothing at all.*

The dark couldn't take it. Ondrell couldn't take it. No one could take it, because taking was the one thing it was proof against.

Only giving worked.

And giving required someone willing to let go of it entirely — not to trade it away, not to spend it on a restored valley or a seat on a council, but to press it into another person's hands with nothing asked back. Nothing. Not safety, not return, not even a guarantee it would work.

I looked at Ilyra.

She was watching me with the expression she wore when she'd solved a sigil and hated the answer. Her fraying wards spun slower around her. Behind her, Ondrell was shouting orders to his mages, and the dark was leaning forward like a wave deciding, and none of it mattered, none of it touched the small bright space between her hands and mine.

"Ilyra," I said.

"Don't," she said. "Wendell, whatever you've just thought of, don't. You don't know what it does to a person. I watched my order try to hold a god's power and it unmade them — it took everything soft in them and—"

"They tried to *own* it," I said. "That's different."

"How is that different?"

"Because I'm not keeping any of it."

* * *

Here is what I knew, in the last clear moment before everything changed: I knew I loved her.

Not the way I'd loved the valley — that fierce, suffocating love of a thing I'd been bound to, walled into, told was the whole world. This was the other kind, the kind I'd crossed the Gray Country learning the name of. Love as a choice made daily. Love that walked a border to keep a promise. Love that did not wall anything in.

If the spark needed a giver, then it needed someone who gave the way the first covenant had been made — between equals, freely, with the whole self. And I could not think of a single other soul in creation I trusted with divinity. Not the Court. Not the dead. Not myself.

Her. Only ever her. The disgraced smith whose order broke the world, who had confessed her guilt with her chin up and her hands shaking, who had rebuilt trust with me one small honesty at a time because she understood that trust was also a thing that could only be given, never taken.

"Your name," I said, stepping toward her. "You said the wards need a name at their heart. Yours."

"My name is poison in every registry in Corvane—"

"Then it's time someone wrote a better registry." I was close enough now to see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the ink stains that never fully washed out, the woman and not the exile. "Ilyra. I can't carry it anymore. Not because it's heavy. Because carrying it means keeping it, and keeping is how gods go silent."

Her breath caught. She understood. Of course she understood — she was the finest sigil-smith of her generation; she had spent her whole life studying the grammar of binding, and I was handing her the one sentence no one had dared write in a thousand years.

"This will change you," she said. "It may end you. What's left of Wendell Joubert after this might not—"

"Might not want to patrol a quiet valley ever again?" I smiled, and found, to my astonishment, that it was real. "Good. I was getting bored anyway."

The dark surged. Ondrell screamed something about treason and heritage and the Court's sacred charge. His mages' white sigils flared and shattered like sugar glass. None of it reached us. We were inside the oldest ward in the world by then, the one made of a decision.

I took her hands. They were cold, ink-stained, trembling. I turned them palm-up, the way you hold something you intend to receive rather than seize, and I felt the spark rise against my skin one last time — quick, hopeful, a bird feeling the cage door swing.

"I was sworn to Vael," I said. The old oath, the one I'd spoken at seventeen in a shrine that never answered, the words that had felt like talking to a locked door my whole life. "In service and in keeping, until the silence breaks." My voice shook. I let it. "The silence is breaking. I release the keeping. I give what was kept — not spent, not sold, not surrendered. Given. To you. Freely."

*Ilyra Sigil-Smith,* said the spark, in a voice that was not Vael's and not mine and not the dark's but older than all of us, the first voice, the voice from before the schism, *do you receive?*

And Ilyra — proud, ruined, brilliant Ilyra, who had every reason to refuse, who knew better than anyone alive what divine power did to the hands that held it — closed her fingers around mine and around the ember between them, and said:

"Freely. With my own name at the heart of it. I receive."

* * *

Light did not describe it. Light is a thing that travels, that falls on things, that leaves shadows. What happened in the Hollow Throne was the opposite of shadow. It was the world remembering its own outline.

The spark went through our joined hands like water finding a channel, and Ilyra breathed in — one long, slow inhalation, the exact inverse of the dark's breathing — and the hall *stopped breathing*. The darkness recoiled from her like a tide from a lit shore. Where she stood, the broken sigils on the walls began, line by line, to finish their interrupted sentences. A thousand years of a language cut off mid-word, completing itself at last, and every completed stroke glowed with a color I had no name for because it existed nowhere in the Valley of Hush and everywhere beyond it.

She rose. Not far. An inch, maybe — enough. Her feet left the black stone and the black stone accepted it as correct. Her fraying moth-sigils knitted themselves whole and then multiplied, spreading out through the hall, up the stair, out into the world, and I understood that I was watching wards being remade — not the old walls, not the shutting-away, but a lattice, a covenant-web, her name written at every crossing point like a signature on a treaty.

Ilyra opened her eyes.

They were the same eyes. That was the detail that undid me — not stars, not fire, just her eyes, tired and sharp and looking at me like I was the only fixed point in a rearranging universe.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Wendell. It's so *loud*. Everything is praying and I can hear all of it."

"The dark," I managed. "What about—"

She turned her head, and the gathered shape at the end of the hall — the almost-face, the stolen voice, the grief given form — shuddered. And Ilyra, new-made god of the covenant, did not smite it. She did the one thing no one in a thousand years had thought to do with Vael's grief.

She spoke to it kindly.

"You were never the enemy," she said. "You were the wound. Come home. There's room in the wards for sorrow too — there always was. That was the whole point."

The darkness hesitated. Breathed. And began, slowly, like frost surrendering to morning, to thin.

Ondrell was on his knees in the wreckage of his mages' work, staring up at her, and his face was doing something complicated — awe and horror and the death of twenty years of ambition all at once. "The Court will never accept—" he started.

"Then the Court can walk the border and argue with me there," Ilyra said, "like everyone else."

And me? I sank down against the throne's cold steps, emptied in a way I had no words for yet, my hands still warm where hers had been. The spark was gone. Vael's last ember, entrusted to a mercenary who'd never been sure the god was listening, given away at the bottom of the world.

I felt no loss. That was the miracle of it. I felt like a courier who has finally set down the letter, and seen it opened, and watched the reader's face change.

Above us, faint through a thousand feet of stone, something flickered.

Ilyra tilted her head, listening to the sky the way she once listened to sigils. Then she smiled — tired, wondering, half-mortal still.

"Wendell," she said. "Look. The stars are coming back."
