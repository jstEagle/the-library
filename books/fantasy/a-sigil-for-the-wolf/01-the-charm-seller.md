## 1. The Charm Seller

The Nightmarket woke the way it always did: not all at once, but in layers, like sediment settling in reverse.

First came the smell—tallow and frying onions, wet wool from the canal side, the sweet rot of overripe pears that old Bessam never threw away because someone would always buy them cheap after dark. Then the sound: awning poles knocking into their sockets, the rattle of shutters going up along Copper Lane, a cart wheel complaining somewhere near the fish stalls. And last, when the light had gone from amber to that particular bruised violet Vharen wore like a bruise of its own, the people came—flowing down from the tenements and the tannery rows, out of the alleys where the sun never bothered to look, all of them hungry for something they couldn't name.

Mara Almeida was already at her stall by then. She was always already there. Twelve years of practice had taught her that the safest place to be invisible was exactly where everyone expected you to be.

She laid out her wares with the care of a woman arranging an argument she'd already won twice over. Luck-knots first, front and center, where the eye went naturally: cords of undyed hemp and river-reed, knotted in patterns that looked, to any passing eye, like nothing more than a fisherman's habit or a grandmother's superstition. A sailor's true-love knot here. A simple three-loop blessing there. Nothing that would raise an eyebrow. Nothing that would raise anything at all.

Behind them, the fever-charms: little pouches of linen stuffed with dried willowbark and meadowsweet, stitched shut with red thread, hung on wooden pegs carved into shapes so plain they were almost apologetic. Mothers bought them for colicky infants. Wives bought them for husbands who coughed through the winter months. They worked—not miracles, but honest work—and Mara priced them fairly, because a charm seller who gouged the poor made enemies, and enemies talked, and talk in Vharen had a way of reaching ears that wore gray gloves.

And beneath all of it—beneath the knots and the pouches and the pleasant, harmless commerce of it all—ran the other thing. The real thing. The thing she hadn't spoken aloud in twelve years, not even to herself, not in words, because words left traces and traces could be read.

She kept her hands moving as she thought about it, which was how she always thought about it. Hands moving, face mild, mind elsewhere.

*You are a shopkeeper,* she told herself, the way she told herself every evening, a small litany worn smooth as a river stone. *You sell trinkets. You are good at trinkets. You have always been good at trinkets.*

The lie fit her like an old coat. It had stopped chafing years ago. That was the worst part—that it had stopped chafing.

* * *

Vharen remembered the Purges the way a body remembers a burn: not in the mind, but in the flinch.

It had been twelve years since the Gray Decrees, twelve years since the Crown's inquisitors had gone street by street with their ledgers and their iron pokers and their polite, terrible questions. Twelve years since the guildhouses burned—not just the great ones on Sigil Row, but the modest ones, the back-alley workshops, the midwife-crafters and the luck-binders and the old women who could ease a fever with a word and a thread. Twelve years since the pyres in King's Square smoked for a season straight, until the stonework itself went black, and no amount of scrubbing ever brought the color back.

The city had learned its lesson well. These days, if you saw a woman muttering over a knot, you looked away. If your neighbor's child recovered from a sickness that should have taken him, you didn't ask how. If you found a sigil scratched under a windowsill—a ward against nightmares, a blessing on a doorway—you painted over it yourself, quickly, before anyone saw you looking, and you never mentioned it again, not even to your own reflection.

Fear, Mara had learned, was the most efficient magic of all. It required no sigilwork whatsoever. It bound an entire city without a single knot.

She watched it work every night from behind her little counter. She watched the dockhands buy luck-knots and joke loudly about how it was all nonsense, of course, nonsense, but their grandmothers had done it, so why not. She watched the young mother with the gray-eyed baby count out coppers for a fever-pouch and then hide it in her sleeve before crossing the market square, as though even the cobblestones might inform on her. She watched everyone perform their unbelief, and she performed hers right along with them, smiling her shopkeeper's smile, and some nights the performance was so seamless she couldn't find the seam herself.

"Evening, mistress Almeida."

Tam Voss, the rope-walker's boy, sixteen now and all elbows, leaned on her counter the way he'd been leaning on it since he was nine. He wanted a knot for his first solo walk tomorrow—a high line strung between the granary towers for the Harvest Vigil, forty feet up over cobblestones that showed no mercy.

"A faller's knot," he said. "The kind my da used to buy."

His father had died three winters ago, off the same line, in a wind that no one had predicted. Mara remembered selling the man his knots. She remembered the pattern she'd woven into them—the one that wasn't only hemp.

"This one," she said, and set a cord on the counter between them. It looked like every other cord in the basket. Three loops, a weaver's cross, a tail tucked neat. "Tie it at your belt. Left side."

"It's just rope, isn't it," Tam said, grinning, paying out his coins. "Just rope and a silly old custom."

"Just rope," Mara agreed. "And a silly old custom."

But her fingers had moved while they spoke, quick and quiet beneath the counter's edge, and the knot she'd tied held more than hemp. It held a turning—a small redirection of chance, a nudge given to the world's great indifferent shoulder. Not enough to save a fool from folly. Enough to catch a foot on a slicked plank. Enough to make the wind hesitate half a breath.

Enough, she prayed, to keep this boy's mother from learning what Tam's mother had learned.

He bounded off into the crowd, cord at his belt, laughing at his own superstition. Mara watched him go and felt the familiar ache open under her ribs, the one that never quite healed, the one shaped exactly like everything she used to be.

* * *

By full dark the market was roaring. Lanterns swayed on their hooks; the canal-side stalls threw long rippling reflections across the black water; somewhere a fiddler was murdering a reel and being paid for it anyway. Mara sold a wind-knot to a fisherman who'd lost two nets that week. Sold a sleep-pouch to a night-soil man whose hands shook. Sold nothing at all for a long stretch, and stood in the lantern-light letting the crowd flow around her like water around a stone.

Invisible. Safe. Dying inside, quietly, at the speed of one unremarkable evening at a time.

That was the arithmetic of her life now, and she knew it precisely. Every day she did not use the craft, the craft sat in her like a held breath. You could hold a breath a long time if you practiced. But the body keeps its accounts. Some mornings her fingertips went numb for no reason. Some nights she dreamed in colors she no longer had names for—guild-colors, workshop-colors—and woke with her hands already moving, tracing patterns on the blanket, patterns that unraveled the moment she looked at them, the way dreams do, the way everything did now.

Once, she had been able to write her name in light.

Now she tied knots and called them customs, and the city slept safer for her silence, or so she told herself, and some nights she even believed it.

A shadow crossed the stall. She looked up with her smile already in place.

But it was only Bessam, hauling his crate of dying pears toward the canal, pausing to spit philosophically. "Late one tonight," he said. "Watch yourself walking home. There's been a gray-glove patrol on Tanner's Row since sundown."

"They're always on Tanner's Row," Mara said.

"They're *extra* on Tanner's Row." He squinted at her. "Something's got them stirred. Courier went missing, I heard. Crown courier, no less. Carrying something important, if the wine-stall gossip is worth anything, and it never is."

Mara's hands, folding a length of cord, did not pause. This was a skill older than her craft: stillness. "Couriers go missing every week. The roads are bad."

"Crown roads aren't." Bessam shrugged and shuffled off into the crowd, and Mara stood very still among her harmless trinkets and felt the evening tilt, very slightly, on its axis.

A missing courier meant nothing. A missing Crown courier meant a search. A search meant checkpoints, door-to-door questions, inquisitors with their patient gray eyes asking everyone the same questions in the same soft voices, and everyone answering honestly because they had nothing to hide—

She had things to hide. She had spent twelve years building a life that was mostly hiding, a shop built over a cellar, a cellar built over a locked box, a locked box built over the last living piece of everything she had loved and lost. Her whole existence was a series of containers, each one sealed inside the next, and the innermost one she did not let herself think about, because thinking about a thing was the first step toward touching it, and touching it was the first step toward dying for it.

She looked down at her hands. Steady. Of course steady. Twelve years of practice.

*Just rope,* she reminded herself. *Just a silly old custom.*

* * *

Closing took less time than opening. The luck-knots went into their basket, unsold ones coiled loose, sold ones already gone into the pockets and belts and sleeves of strangers. The fever-charms came off their pegs and into the cedar chest, where the wood's sharp sweetness covered the smell of willowbark and hid, too, the fainter smell underneath—the smell of the work, the true work, which clung to everything she touched if she wasn't careful. She was always careful. Careful was the whole of her.

She shuttered the stall, carried the day's coin inside, and locked the shop door with the ordinary lock, the legal one, the one any locksmith in Vharen could pick in a minute. Then she touched the second lock, the one no locksmith could see, and felt it answer—a whisper of resistance against her fingertip, a thread pulled taut somewhere deep in the world's weave—and stepped through into the dark of her own rooms.

One room above the shop. One bed, one stove, one window overlooking the canal. One chair, because a second chair invited a guest, and guests asked questions. Twelve years of one chair.

She lit the lamp and sat, and for a while she simply listened to the market die outside—the fiddler giving up, the last carts rattling home, the canal lapping at its stones. Somewhere far off, on Tanner's Row, she imagined she could hear boots. She probably couldn't. Probably it was only her own blood in her own ears, keeping its own patrol.

On the shelf above the stove sat the only objects in the room she hadn't chosen for their harmlessness: a chipped clay wolf, fired in some kiln before she was born; a spool of thread gone silver with age; a strip of cloth embroidered with a mark she never looked at directly, the way you don't look directly at a scar.

Her guild mark. Her old name, stitched in a language the Purges had tried to burn out of the world.

She didn't look at it now, either. She poured tea instead, cheap black tea, bitter enough to bite, and drank it scalding, and told herself—as she told herself every night, the last litany, the one that closed the day like a hand closing a book—that tomorrow would be the same as today. Knots and charms. Coppers and smiles. Invisible, safe, alive.

It was a good bargain. It had been a good bargain for twelve years.

So why, she wondered, staring into the lamp's small stubborn flame, did tonight feel like the held breath finally starting to shake? Why did the silence feel less like safety and more like the pause before something spoke?

Outside, far down the lane, boots struck cobbles in a rhythm too even for drunks and too unhurried for soldiers. Patrol boots. Gray-glove boots. They passed without stopping, and the sound faded, and Mara sat alone in her one-chair room with her cooling tea and her buried name, and waited for morning the way she always did:

As though morning might, this once, bring something other than another day of being no one at all.

She banked the lamp. She lay down. She did not sleep.

And at the edge of hearing, down where the canal met the dark, something stumbled.
