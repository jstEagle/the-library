## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The stumble became a knock.

Mara heard it first as a sound like a sack of grain falling against her shutters, then as fists—weak fists, the kind that apologized even as they struck—and she lay in her dark one-chair room and made herself count to thirty, because the trick of surviving Vharen was never answering anything on the first knock. Or the second. Let them go away. Let them decide she was empty, uninteresting, a closed shop with nothing behind the door but dust and the smell of old wool.

The fists came again. Slower now. The kind of knocking that would not be repeated many more times.

*Don't*, she told herself. Twelve years of discipline sat on her chest like a hand: *You owe strangers nothing. Strangers are how the Purges happened. Someone talked. Someone opened a door.*

But her feet were already on the cold floorboards, and her hands were already finding the lamp and lighting it low, and she hated herself a little for how quickly she moved, how eagerly the old reflexes woke in her—the guild had trained its children to run toward trouble the way other children ran from it, and some training went deeper than fear.

She unbolted the door.

He fell across the threshold into her arms, and he was heavier than a dying man should have been, or perhaps dying men were always heavier than you expected because they brought their deaths with them like luggage. He was young—younger than her, certainly, twenty maybe, with the canal-weathered look of the lower docks, salt-cracked lips and rope-burned palms—and his shirt was soaked through at the side in a way shirts were not meant to be soaked, black in the lamplight, gleaming wetly when he breathed.

"Shut it," he rasped. "Shut the door."

She shut the door. She shot the bolt. And then, because terror makes people practical, she turned the lamp down until it was barely more than an ember's worth of light, and dragged him by the shoulders into the back room where she did her harmless, honest work—the little bench, the pliers, the spools of dyed cord, the trays of knotwork trinkets that meant nothing and sold well.

"Lie still," she said. "Don't talk."

"Have to talk." He caught her wrist. His grip was astonishing, feverish, the grip of a man who had been holding something for a long way and could not put it down yet. "Are you the charm seller? Nightmarket lane, gray shutters, the woman who—" A cough took him, and what came up with it was dark. "The knots. Your knots. They're *real*. I was told—they said if I got to Vharen, find the luck-seller whose knots hold true longer than they should."

Mara went very cold, very carefully, the way water freezes around a stone.

"You've been misinformed," she said. "My knots are craft. Pretty things. Nothing more."

His eyes found hers in the near-darkness, and there was fury in them, and something worse than fury: pleading. "Please," he said. "I don't have time for your lie. I'm spending my last minutes asking you to drop it."

* * *

Here is what Mara should have done, and knew even as she knelt there that she should have done: she should have gone to the door, thrown it open, and screamed for the watch. A wounded man, bleeding out in a charm seller's shop—a citizen's duty was clear. The Crown rewarded loyalty. The Crown forgave small oversights for loud allegiance.

Here is what she did instead. She fetched her needle and her strongest thread and her good scissors, and she cut the man's shirt open along the seam, and looked at what the night had done to him.

The wound was in his side, low, angled upward. A blade wound, deliberate, meant to open rather than to kill outright—or meant to kill slowly, which was sometimes the same intention wearing different clothes. He had bled a long time before reaching her. The blood had that thin, watery look of blood that is running out of ideas.

"I can't fix this," she said quietly. It was true, and it was also, in part, a lie. There were sigils for bleeding. There was one stitched into the lining of her own coat that could hold a gut wound closed for three days, long enough to reach a surgeon or a grave. But using it meant channeling through her own name, her buried name, and a working that strong left residue—a taste in the air, a wrongness that certain trained noses could smell through walls. Inquisitor noses.

"Didn't ask you to fix me." He fumbled at his breast with clumsy fingers. "Asked you to take it."

"No." She heard her own voice say it from very far away. "Whatever this is, no. Find someone else. Find anyone else."

"There isn't anyone else." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; he winced around it like a man biting tinfoil. "That's the whole point of everything. There's no one else. You're the last."

"The last of *what?*" But she knew. Even saying it, she knew, and the room seemed to tilt, the little shop with its trays of harmless trinkets tilting like a deck in bad weather. "I don't know what you're talking about. I sell charms. Fever-knots. Luck-braids for fishermen's wives. That's all."

"That's all," he agreed softly, watching her. "And your knots hold true longer than they should."

* * *

He gave her the packet before she decided whether to take it, which she understood afterward was a courier's oldest trick: make acceptance unnecessary. Press it into hands, let instinct close the fingers. Her fingers closed. Wax cracked faintly under them—a seal, pressed deep, and even in the guttering lamplight she recognized the impression, and her whole body flinched as though the wax itself had burned her.

A compass rose, quartered, with a wolf's head at its center.

The guild mark. Her guild's mark. Stamped by a signet ring she had last seen on the hand of a man twelve years drowned in her nightmares: Master Aldric, who had walked into the Guildhall on the last night and never walked out, who had been named traitor in the same breath as the rest of them, whose face she had spent a decade deliberately forgetting line by line.

"That's impossible," she whispered.

"He said you'd say that." The courier's voice was thinner now, fraying at the edges like old rope. "He said—he said to tell the keybearer the vault remembers. Those exact words. He made me repeat them forty times on the road. *The vault remembers.* I don't know what it means. I hope to every god it means something, because it's all I've got to give you besides this."

"The keybearer." Her mouth was dry. "I'm not—I don't know what that means. Who sent you? Who sealed this?"

"He did. Aldric." The name landed in the room like a dropped lantern. "He's alive, lady. Whatever you thought. He's alive, and something is very wrong in this city, and he said the only person who could see it truly was the keybearer, and the keybearer was here, selling luck to fishermen's wives." His breath hitched. "There's a map inside. Don't open it here. Don't open it where there are eyes. Burn it after you've memorized it—that's what he said. Memorize and burn."

"I can't—" Mara's voice broke and she steadied it with craft, with the flat merchant tone she'd built over twelve years like a second set of shutters. "Listen to me. You need a surgeon. On Cordwainer Street, there's a woman, Maret, she doesn't ask questions—"

"I know about Maret." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, that smile, patient and gray. "I passed Cordwainer Street two hours ago. I came to you instead. That should tell you how important this is. That should tell you exactly what I thought of my own chances."

Outside, far off, boots on cobbles again.

Both of them froze. The courier's hand found hers and gripped it hard enough to grind bone, and his eyes were wide and fixed on the shuttered window, and Mara listened the way she had taught herself to listen for twelve years—not to footsteps but to their rhythm, their number, their confidence. Four men at least. Even spacing. The short stride and long pause of men checking doors.

They passed.

They stopped.

Twenty paces down the lane, maybe less, the boots halted, and a voice said something too quiet to parse, and another voice answered, and Mara sat in the ember-light of her lamp with a dead man's errand in her lap and a dying man's hand crushing hers, and understood that the next minute would decide whether the last twelve years had been worth anything at all.

* * *

The boots moved on. Perhaps they had been checking shutters at random. Perhaps the night was full of closed shops and they had grown bored. It didn't matter. What mattered was that when she could breathe again, the courier was looking at her with an expression of vast, exhausted satisfaction, as if her terror had been the proof he'd needed.

"Now you know," he said. "Now you feel it. Good. That's what he said. He said the keybearer would try to refuse, and that I should make her afraid instead. Fear opens faster than argument." His head sagged back against her workbench. "Clever old bastard."

"Don't talk. Save your—"

"Lady." He said it gently, the way you correct a child. "Lady, I've been talking on borrowed time since the weir gate. Let me spend what's left properly." He swallowed, and she watched the swallow hurt him. "The king is dead."

The words hung in the dim room among the hanging trinkets, among the luck-knots and fever-charms and the little woven wolves she sold to children, absurd and enormous.

"That's not possible," Mara said. "The king appeared at the Spring Assizes. The king—"

"The king appeared at the Spring Assizes." He nodded slowly, gravely. "So did I, once. Doesn't mean I was alive." His laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought more of that thin dark blood, and she pressed a cloth against his side and he pushed her hand away with what tenderness he had left. "Listen. The Binding is broken, or bent, or being eaten—I don't understand it, I carried messages, I'm not a scholar. But the city knows. Feel it. The luck's gone out of Vharen like heat out of a dead hearth. Children born gray-eyed. Crops failing in the valleys. Ships losing fair winds they've had for two hundred years. Something is drinking this kingdom, and it started drinking the day the old guild burned, and the man who sealed this letter says the only thing that can stop it is under Mount Serel, and the only hands that can open the way are yours."

"My *yours*?" Her voice came out very small. "I was a child when the guild fell. I was the least of them. I mopped floors and learned knotwork and—there's nothing under my hands but trinkets. Whoever he thinks I am—"

"He thinks you're the keybearer." Each word cost him visibly now, paid out one at a time like a miser's coins. "He's been right about everything else. Trust me on that. Trust me the way I trusted him, carrying this through four checkpoints with my heart hammering and my name written on no list anywhere because lists get people killed—" Another cough, longer. When it ended he was quieter, grayer, and his eyes had begun to do the terrible drifting thing that eyes do when the body starts negotiating terms with its ending. "Map's inside. Wax seal keyed to... keyed to your blood, he said. Only opens for you. Don't ask me how. I don't know how. I carried it. I just carried it."

"Why me?" she asked, and heard the crack in her own voice, twelve years of loneliness cracking open like river ice. "Why would he choose me?"

"Because you ran." The courier smiled again, that gray patient smile. "Everyone else stayed and died. You lived. He said—his words—he said, *tell her the ones who survive are the ones the craft chooses to keep.*"

Then his eyes lost their focus, and his breathing changed, and Mara Almeida, the last of the Sigilmakers' Guild, held a stranger's hand in the dark and watched the life leave it with the methodical, unhurried patience of water filling a boat.

He said one more thing. It was barely a word at all, more breath shaped briefly into meaning, and she leaned close to catch it, and what she caught was: "*Longer than they should.*" And then nothing, for a while, and then, much later, one final exhale that meant nothing anymore except physics.

* * *

She sat with him for a length of time she could not afterward measure. The lamp had gone to almost nothing. The trinkets hung around them, turning slightly in some draft she couldn't feel, dozens of small harmless charms clicking together like teeth.

Then the boots came back.

Closer this time. Deliberately closer. And this time there was no mistaking it: they stopped at her door.

Mara moved. She did not remember deciding to move; she simply found herself already doing it, and later she would understand that this, too, was guild training—the body acting while the mind stood paralyzed, the old oaths living on in the muscles long after the conscious woman had renounced them. She pinched out the lamp. She rolled the courier's body onto her largest trading cloth, the heavy waxed canvas she used for carting stock to market, and she dragged him—God, he was heavy, they were all heavy—to the trapdoor in the corner of the back room that opened onto the canal-side cellar where she stored cord and dye vats and, once a year, performed the small private ceremony of remembering her mother's face.

The bolts on her front door began to turn. Someone outside had tools, and skill, and all the patience in the Crown behind them.

She lowered the body into the dark. It went badly—one shoulder catching, the canvas slipping—and she lowered herself after it, hauling the trapdoor nearly closed above her head, leaving a finger's width of gap through which she could see a slice of her own back room, her workbench, the spilled thread, the smear of blood she had not had time to address.

The door above opened. Lamplight bloomed across her ceiling, thrown long and hard from the front room.

Boots entered her shop. Four sets, at least. They moved through her front room the way she imagined a tongue moving through a mouth, tasting everything. Drawers opened. Trays lifted and set down. One of them knocked something over—a spool, rolling—and she listened to it roll and stop and counted her own heartbeats and found eleven of them.

"In here," a voice said. Not loud. Inquisitors' voices were never loud; loudness was for people who needed volume to feel powerful.

The back room. The slice of lamplight widened. Mara pressed herself into the canal-cold dark between dye vats, the dead courier's cooling blood soaking slowly through the canvas against her knee, and watched the boots cross her ceiling through the gap overhead.

One of them crouched. She saw the shadow of it bend across the floorboards above her—the shadow of a man kneeling beside the place where a dying man had lain not half an hour past, beside the blood that was still wet.

"Wet," the voice above confirmed, to someone else, quietly. "Still wet."

A pause. Then, from a second voice, older, dryer, unhurried—the voice of a man who had done this many times and expected to do it many more:

"Check the cellar."

* * *

Footsteps crossed toward the trapdoor. Mara slid her hand into her coat, to the lining, to the sigil she had sworn on the night of the Purges never to work again unless her life depended on it, and she felt the stitched threads warm under her fingers, waking, recognizing her name—

—and above her, the dry voice spoke again, and there was something in it she had not expected. Not eagerness. Weariness.

"Leave it."

"My lord, the blood—"

"The blood is an hour old and the tide gate opens at third bell. Anything dumped in the canal will be at sea by dawn." A rustle of paper. "Besides. If our courier delivered what I think he delivered, then whoever received it is long gone, and kicking over cellars in the Nightmarket will catch us nothing but fishwives." The dry voice moved toward the front of the shop, and with it, mercifully, the boots. "Log the break-in. Note the blood. The charm seller will have questions to answer when she's found. Leave a writ on the counter."

They finished. They withdrew. The door closed; the boots faded; and Mara knelt in the pitch dark beside a corpse, her hand still buried in her coat, her fingers still resting on the warming threads of a forbidden sigil, and wept—silently, furiously, in the manner of someone who has not permitted herself the practice in twelve years and finds her technique ruined.

When she finally climbed back up into her ransacked shop, dawn was still an hour off. The writ lay on her counter where promised, naming her, describing her, commanding her appearance before the Office of Inquiry within three days.

On the floor, the courier's packet lay where he had pressed it into her hands. The wax seal with the compass rose and the wolf's head glinted dully in the pre-dawn gray, waiting.

*Keybearer*, she thought. *The vault remembers.*

She picked it up. She did not open it. But she carried it with her to the window, and stood looking out over the sleeping Nightmarket—at the fishwives' stalls going up, at the gray-eyed child who sat listless on a doorstep where children used to play, at the beautiful, unlucky, slowly starving city she had chosen over her own name—and understood, with a calm that frightened her more than any boot on any stair, that whatever she decided next, the invisible life was over.

Something had come stumbling out of the dark and died in her shop, and it had known her.

It had known her by name.
