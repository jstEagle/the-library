## 3. What the Map Shows

The body was the hardest part.

Mara had buried her fear of corpses years ago—death came through her shop in small ways, in fever-charms that failed, in luck-knots sold to sailors who never came home—but burying a man was different from mourning one. She worked through the small hours with a shovel she kept for turning the garden soil behind her shop, digging into ground still soft from autumn rain, and she did not let herself think about how young he had been, or how his hand had felt pressing the map into hers, warm and insistent as a heartbeat.

She wrapped him in the canvas drop-cloth she used for dyeing, said the few words she remembered—the old words, the ones that would have cost her her tongue if anyone had overheard—and filled the hole. The rain helped. By dawn there was only a rough patch of turned earth among the turnip rows, and Mara sat on her back step with wet hands and shaking knees and watched the sky go the color of skimmed milk.

*Keybearer.*

She hadn't heard that word spoken aloud in twelve years. She had almost convinced herself she'd dreamed it.

When her hands finally stilled, she went inside, bolted the door, drew the shutters, and lit no lamps. In the gray half-light of her workroom she unrolled the map on the scarred oak table where she'd spent a decade carving trinkets that were only ever trinkets.

* * *

It was good vellum. That was her first thought, absurdly professional: someone had spent real money on this, or stolen it from someone who had. The edges were stiff with dried blood—the courier's blood, soaked through from the wound in his side—and when she smoothed it flat against the table, the blood crackled like old lacquer.

The map itself showed the northern reaches of Vharen's kingdom, but not as the Crown drew them. No surveyor's grid, no toll roads marked in red ink. This was drawn by hand, by someone who loved the land enough to render its temper: the river Vharen rendered as a long silver stitch, the pine forests of the north as clusters of tiny patient strokes like fur lying flat, and rising out of all of it, west and high, Mount Serel.

Mara's breath left her in a thin, controlled stream.

Serel. The mountain every child in Vharen learned to call the King's Tooth, because it stood alone and white-capped above the foothills like something broken off a larger jaw. Pilgrims climbed its lower slopes to visit the shrine of Saint Edvarda. The Crown maintained a weather station at the tree line. It was, as far as any honest citizen knew, just a mountain.

But the map showed more than the mountain. Beneath it—in fine cross-hatched lines that only resolved when she tilted the vellum toward the window's gray light—a structure unfolded. Chambers. Corridors. A descending spiral of rooms labeled not with letters or numbers but with names.

*Hall of Ash. The Weeping Stair. Vault of Names.*

Her fingers trembled against the parchment.

She knew those rooms. Not from maps—from songs. From the lullabies her mother had hummed while braiding her hair, the ones with nonsense words that weren't nonsense at all. From the teaching rhymes the guild sang children to sleep:

*Down beneath the mountain's root,*
*where the quiet waters run,*
*every name is written true,*
*and the vault remembers everyone.*

She had sung those verses herself, once. To younger children, huddled under blankets in the guildhouse dormitory, while the wind worried at the shutters of a house that officially did not exist.

Because that was what the Hollow Vault was. Not a legend. Not a story the guild told to make itself feel ancient and important, though they told plenty of those too. It was real. It was theirs. And it had been sealed for twelve years—since the night the Purges came.

Mara sat down slowly, because her legs had informed her they were done pretending.

*Vault of Names.* She traced the label with one fingertip, barely touching the vellum, the way you touch a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking. Every sigil the guild had ever bound, every craftwork anchored to a living name—that was where they were kept. That was why the vault mattered. That was why the Purges had come for it first, before the guildhouses, before the masters, before the children. Chancellor Rell's inquisitors understood something that even most sigilwrights didn't: you could kill every witch in Vharen and the craft would survive, carried in blood and memory, waiting. But destroy the vault—

Destroy the vault, and everything the guild had ever made came loose from its mooring.

Except they hadn't destroyed it. The map lay open under her hands, precise and lovingly detailed, showing corridors that no Crown surveyor could have drawn. Someone had been inside. Recently, or for a long time, or both.

And then she saw the seal.

It sat in the lower corner where a cartographer would put his mark: a circle of interlocking knots, and within the knots, a wolf. Not snarling—the wolf of the guild's oldest sigil, the guardian-wolf, head raised, listening. Mara had that same wolf inked into her own skin, low on her left forearm, hidden beneath three layers of charm-work that made it read as a faded birthmark to any eye that looked. She hadn't uncovered it in twelve years. She wasn't sure she dared to now.

Around the wolf-knot, in ink that had gone brown with age but was unmistakable to anyone who had spent six years grinding ink for the man who mixed it, ran a single line of script:

*I held the door. Come hold the lamp.* —A.V.

Mara did not move for a long time.

* * *

The rain on the roof sounded like the rain had sounded that night, twelve years ago, and so—because grief is lazy and takes whatever road is open—she fell backward into memory without meaning to.

She was seven years old again, sitting cross-legged on cold flagstones in the guildhouse at Cael Row, which everyone in the neighborhood believed was a hospice for retired sailors. It was, in a way. It was also the last free school of sigilcraft in Vharen.

"Again," Aldric Voss said.

He had been kneeling beside her, though he was a tall man and kneeling pained him, because he believed you taught children at their own height. His hands were enormous, scarred across every knuckle, stained permanently gray from silver-ash ink. He smelled of cedar smoke and lamp oil. When he spoke, other masters went quiet, because Aldric had argued the guild's case before two kings and won both times, and there was a weight to his voice that even eight-year-olds could feel.

"Again," he said, gentler now, "and this time mean it."

"I don't know how to mean it."

"You mean things all day long. You meant it when you lied to your mother about eating the honeycakes. You mean it when you tell little Tam his fever-charm is working better than it is." He tapped the knotwork laid out between them on the floor—a simple three-loop binding, the kind used to steady a frightened animal or calm a colicky infant. "Meaning isn't a mystery, Mara. It's just honesty with consequences. Say what the knot is for. Believe it will do that thing. Accept that it will cost you something to make it true. That's all sigilcraft has ever been."

"And then I get the power."

Something flickered across his face—not anger, but an old tiredness, like a man hearing a bell he'd hoped was cracked.

"No," he said. "Then you get the responsibility. The power was already there, child. It's in everything—the luck of a city, the health of its children, the fortune of its king. We don't make power. We're plumbers. We fix the pipes. And when we do it right, nobody ever knows we existed." He smiled, and the tiredness folded away into warmth. "That's the whole oath. *We serve, we do not consume.* Can you say it?"

"We serve, we do not consume."

"Louder. The stones should learn it too."

"We serve, we do not consume!"

"Adequate." But he'd laughed, and pulled the ribbon from her braid and retied it in a luck-knot without asking, and for one moment—one bright, ordinary, unrecoverable moment—she had been perfectly happy, kneeling on cold stones in a house full of people who would all, within five years, be dead or scattered or burned.

Mara opened her eyes. The workroom was darker; the gray light had thickened toward proper morning, muffled by the shutters. Her cheeks were wet. She hadn't noticed crying. Lately she never did—it happened the way weather happened, somewhere above her, beyond her jurisdiction.

Twelve years, and she had built her life like a wall around that night. She had taken the guild's craft and shrunk it, starved it, disguised it as market-stall junk—fever-charms with no anchor, luck-knots that were honestly nothing but pretty cordage, because an unbound knot couldn't be detected, couldn't be traced, couldn't hang her. She had told herself for twelve years that she was keeping the flame alive. Keeping the faith. Being the last keeper.

Sitting alone in the dark with a dead man's map, Mara Almeida admitted, for the first time, the uglier truth: she had been hiding.

Not preserving. Hiding. There was a difference, and the difference was the vault, and the vault had been sitting under Mount Serel this entire time—sealed, or breached, or guarded by a dead man's handwriting—and she had known it and done nothing, because doing something meant being seen, and being seen meant dying the way her mother died.

*I held the door. Come hold the lamp.*

*Aldric Voss.* Her master. The man the Purges swallowed whole—or so the survivors had believed. They'd found his quarters ransacked, his grimoires seized, his students interrogated until the interrogators grew bored. No body. But in those days there were so many bodies that a missing one meant nothing except mercy, and Mara had chosen, deliberately, deliberately, to believe in the mercy.

Now a courier had died on her floor calling her *keybearer*, carrying a map sealed with a dead man's wolf, pointing the way to a vault that should have been empty.

Which raised questions she did not want to ask, in a voice she did not want to hear.

If Aldric had lived—if he'd been inside the vault, holding the door, all this time—then he had known what she had known: that the Binding was still active. That the great royal anchor the guild had woven generations ago still hung beneath the mountain, still tied to the throne of Vharen, still feeding on—what? The guild had bound the kingdom's fortune to its kings as an act of service. Nobody had ever asked what would happen if something started drinking from the tap.

The blighted villages. The gray-eyed children. The luck draining out of the city like water from a cracked bowl.

Mara pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the map, to the dead. "No, no, no. I sold charms. That was the whole life. That was the deal."

The map said otherwise. The map, bloodstained and patient, said: *you were never the shopkeeper. You were always the keybearer. You just stopped answering the door.*

* * *

She should burn it.

That was the sane choice, the safe choice, the choice she had rehearsed for twelve years without knowing it. Burn the vellum, dig up the courier's satchel and burn that too, shave her head and move to some southern port town and sell honest rope bracelets until she died of old age surrounded by strangers. The inquisitors would come asking about a dead courier eventually—they always came asking—and the safest answer was a shopkeeper who'd never seen him.

She gathered the map's edges in both hands.

Held them there.

The vellum was warm. Not sun-warm; the shutters were closed, the room cold. Warm like skin. Warm like the courier's hand. And as she held it, faint beneath her fingertips, she felt—she would swear afterward she felt—a pulse. Slow. Deep. Rhythmic as something vast turning over in its sleep.

Under the mountain. Under twelve years of her silence. Something down in the Vault of Names was beating.

Mara set the map down very carefully, the way you set down a sleeping animal you are now definitely afraid of waking.

Then she crossed to the chest at the back of the workroom—the one with the false bottom, the one she opened twice a year to air its contents and hate herself—and lifted out the bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside: her mother's braid-knife. A guild seal-stamp with the wolf worn nearly smooth. Three letters she had never finished reading. And her old working apron, dyed guild-blue, smelling impossibly of cedar smoke after twelve years in the dark.

She laid her left arm on the table. With her mother's knife, she cut the charm-work off her forearm, thread by thread, sigil by sigil, each severing a small sting like pulling a splinter that had grown into the bone.

The birthmark faded. The wolf emerged.

It was darker than she remembered. Angrier-looking, maybe, or maybe that was just her. Head raised. Listening.

"Well," she told it, and her voice came out steadier than anything else in her. "Looks like we're going home."

Outside, boots rang on cobbles—more than one pair, moving with the unhurried discipline of men paid to walk in formation. They slowed as they passed her shuttered windows.

Mara blew out the candle she'd forgotten she lit, and stood very still in the dark, and listened.
