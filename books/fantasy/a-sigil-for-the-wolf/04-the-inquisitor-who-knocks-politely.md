## 4. The Inquisitor Who Knocks Politely

The dead courier had been lying in the Nightmarket for six hours before anyone official noticed him, and Dain Ferro found this more telling than the body itself.

He crouched beside the man in the narrow gutter behind the charm-sellers' row, rain dripping off the eaves of the tarpaulins above in a slow, arrhythmic patter. The corpse had been moved — dragged, by the scoring of the heels through the wet muck — and laid out with a strange, almost tender care beneath the overhang of a shuttered stall, as if someone had wanted the rain off his face. Nobody in Vharen treated a stranger's corpse with tenderness unless they had a reason to.

"You see this?" Ferro said to Sergeant Ilse, who stood behind him with her collar up and her expression set to permanent grievance. "Drag marks. Ten feet, maybe twelve. Someone pulled him out of the open and covered him."

"Rats would've had him otherwise," Ilse said. "Maybe they were being neighborly."

"Nobody in this city is neighborly at three in the morning." Ferro leaned closer. The courier's clothes were soaked through, cheap wool gone dark with water and blood both, though there was surprisingly little blood — a single wound high in the abdomen, already clotted, the kind of injury that killed slow rather than fast. A man stabbed like that could walk for an hour. Could climb stairs. Could knock on doors.

His hands told Ferro more than his face did. Calloused across the palms from riding, but fine-grained at the fingertips — ink stains worked deep into the whorls, old and new layered over each other. A document courier, then, not cargo. High-value work. And his right hand was closed.

Ferro pried it open gently. Nothing. Just rainwater pooled in the palm, and a crescent of dried blood where something had been gripped hard enough to break skin.

"He was carrying something," Ferro said.

"Robbed, maybe."

"Robbers don't drag men under cover." He stood, knees protesting — he was forty-one years old and felt every one of them in the damp — and looked up the alley toward the market proper. The charm-row. Luck-knots and fever-charms and painted beads, the harmless end of a trade that had once run deeper, before the Purges burned the deeper end out of existence. Twelve years now since the last guild sigil-craftsman had been hanged in Crown Square, and the Nightmarket still sold its little charms because people needed to believe in luck the way they needed bread.

And somewhere along that row, someone had opened a door to a bleeding man in the dark, and then thought carefully about what to do with him afterward.

* * *

The Crown's inquisition kept its Vharen office in a converted counting-house on Salt Lane, all iron fittings and whitewashed walls, and Ferro hated every inch of it. He'd been in the Order twenty-two years — joined at nineteen with a believer's fire, certain that magic was a rot and he was the knife that cut it out. His mentor, old Prelate Anselm, used to say the Purges were the kingdom's great cleansing, that every sigil burned was a plague-spot lanced.

Anselm had been dead nine years now, and Ferro had spent those nine years watching the crusade curdle into routine: raids on herb-wives, interrogations over knotted thread, a twelve-year-old girl in Ironhold for selling a charm that turned out to be dyed wool and chicken bones. The magic was mostly gone. What remained was the machinery, grinding on, because nobody in power remembered how to stop it or cared to try.

He wrote up his preliminary report on the courier by lamplight, and stopped at the line where he should have written *cause of death*, because he hadn't determined cause of death yet, not properly. The abdominal wound was days old. Days. A man doesn't ride two days with a gut wound unless something is driving him harder than pain.

Something was being delivered. Something worth dying slowly for.

He sent word to the harbor master's office requesting the manifests for the past week's river traffic, and then, because he was honest with himself if nothing else, he wrote a second request — one he sealed privately — asking the palace physician's office for records of any courier contracts commissioned under royal seal in the past month. He wasn't sure why. It was instinct, the shapeless kind, the kind that had gotten him promoted twice and nearly dismissed once. The Crown didn't send couriers into the Nightmarket. The Crown barely acknowledged the Nightmarket existed except when it burned something in it.

The private request came back within the day, stamped and sealed and utterly unhelpful: *No such contracts exist.* Which either meant no such contracts existed, or someone very high up wanted Ferro to stop asking. Both possibilities interested him equally.

* * *

The charm-shop sat third from the corner of Lantern Row, wedged between a dyer's and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, and it announced itself with a hand-painted sign: *Almeida Charms & Knotwork*. No sigils anywhere, of course. Sigilcraft was death; knotwork was craft, and craft was permitted so long as it stayed humble. Ferro had walked the charm-row a hundred times in his career and never given these shops more than a bored glance.

He gave this one a long look now.

It was clean. That was the first thing. The other stalls on the row displayed their wares in cheerful clutter, but Almeida's window held perhaps thirty items arranged with geometric precision — knots in red cord and blue, small woven bands, wooden beads strung on waxed thread. Each one identical to its neighbors down to the tension of the weave. Ferro had spent enough years studying forged documents to know what that uniformity meant: a hand trained to exactness, repeating a pattern until perfection became habit.

Or a hand trained in something older than knotwork, hiding inside it.

He knocked politely. He always knocked politely. It unsettled people more than kicking the door did.

The woman who opened it was perhaps thirty-five, dark-haired, with ink-stained fingers and the composed, slightly tired face of someone accustomed to being interrupted by customers. She looked at the gray-and-silver badge on his coat for exactly as long as politeness required, and not a moment longer.

"Inquisitor Ferro," he said. "Crown Inquisition. May I come in? It's about the man found behind your shop yesterday morning."

Something crossed her face — quick, controlled, gone. Not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing a name they'd been expecting without knowing it.

"Of course," she said, and stepped back. "Mind the boxes. I'm reorganizing."

She was not reorganizing. The boxes were stacked too neatly, too deliberately, in configurations that blocked sightlines to the back room door. Ferro noted it and said nothing, taking the chair she offered amid shelves of cord and dye pots while she settled herself across the counter from him with the ease of a woman who had nothing to hide and the stillness of one who had everything.

"Your name?"

"Mara Almeida. You know that — it's on the sign."

"I like to hear it from people. Names sound different in the mouth." He produced his notebook. "Did you know the deceased?"

"No."

"See him yesterday evening?"

"I close at dusk. I heard some commotion in the alley later, but I assumed it was drunks. I didn't look."

"You didn't look." Ferro wrote nothing down. "A man dies ten feet from your back door, Ms. Almeida, and you sleep soundly."

"I sleep soundly because I didn't kill him, Inquisitor, and because in Vharen, looking out your window at the wrong moment is how people disappear." Her voice stayed level, almost pleasant. There was no fear in it at all, and that itself was interesting. Most people performed fear for inquisitors. She performed patience instead — the patience of someone humoring a slow child.

"And if I told you he was carrying something valuable?" Ferro said. "Something that might have been passed to whoever opened their door to him?"

"Then I'd say you're interviewing everyone on the row, and you'll find nothing here, because I sell knots." She spread her hands, palms up — ink-stained, calloused at the fingertips in that same precise way as the dead man's hands, though hers were calloused differently, in patterns Ferro couldn't quite place. Weaver's patterns. Or something else. "Knots, Inquisitor. Cord and dye and good intentions."

Ferro studied her a moment longer than necessary, letting the silence do its work. She let it sit there between them without flinching, which most people couldn't manage for five seconds.

"The wound," he said finally, closing the notebook. "Abdominal, days old. He walked a long way on it. Whatever he carried mattered enough that dying slowly was preferable to stopping."

"That's very sad," Mara Almeida said. "Will there be a collection for his family?"

"There's no family listed."

"Then I hope wherever he was going, someone received what he brought."

For just a moment — half a heartbeat, less — her eyes flicked toward the back room. Not a glance. A refusal to glance, corrected too late into a glance. Ferro had built a career on catching the difference.

He stood, thanked her for her time, and asked — casually, already at the door — whether she'd mind if the Order searched the premises, purely as formality, since the death occurred adjacent to her property.

"I'd mind very much," she said pleasantly. "I keep my accounts and my suppliers' letters back there, and I don't hand my business correspondence to the people who burned Madel the herb-wife's shop two streets over last spring. But you have your writ, Inquisitor. If you want to search, search."

It was beautifully done. A guilty person would have capitulated too fast, or argued too hard. She'd done neither — offered resistance with legal grounds and moral force, and left him holding the choice like something hot.

He didn't search. Not because he lacked the right, but because he suspected he wouldn't find anything, and because — and this was the part that troubled him on the walk home, in the rain, past the shuttered stalls of the dying market — because some part of him didn't want to find anything.

That was dangerous. That was the rot Anselm had warned against, sentiment wearing the mask of judgment. But twenty-two years of this work had taught Ferro to distinguish the smell of guilt from the smell of fear, and Mara Almeida's shop smelled of neither.

It smelled, faintly, of beeswax and cord and something else underneath — something mineral and cold, like the air off a mountain lake in winter.

Like a vault, he thought, though he couldn't have said why.

* * *

That night Ferro sat in his rooms above the Salt Lane office with a cup of wine going stale and the case file spread across his desk, and he did what he always did when a case refused to resolve: he laid out the facts and let them argue with each other.

Fact: a royal-grade courier, wounded days earlier, died delivering something in the Nightmarket. Fact: the palace denied any such commission existed. Fact: the nearest shopkeeper showed no guilt, no fear, and a single microsecond of misdirected attention toward her back room. Fact: the body was covered by unknown hands. Fact: the courier's right hand had gripped something hard enough to bleed, and that something was gone.

Conclusion: someone in the Nightmarket was now holding an object the palace officially denied ever existing.

He turned his wine cup slowly on the desk. Twenty-two years ago he'd have written all this up and requested a full sweep of the charm-row by dawn. Ten years ago he'd have written it up and quietly buried it, because sweeps turned up nothing but weavers and widows and made the Order look like thugs. Tonight he simply sat with it, feeling the strange weight of the case settle onto him like wet cloth.

There was something else, too. Something he hadn't put in any report.

When he'd stood in Mara Almeida's shop, beside her shelves of harmless knots, he'd felt — briefly, absurdly — watched. Not by her. By the room. The way a man feels watched standing in an empty church, aware of architecture meant for someone larger. Those precisely arranged cords in the window, each identical to its brothers. He'd seen knotwork all his life. Nobody tied knots that perfect. Perfection wasn't human. Perfection was practiced the way monks practiced prayer, for decades, in secret, for purposes that had nothing to do with luck.

He thought again about his sealed request to the palace, and the answer stamped back at him: *no such contracts exist*, in a clerk's careful hand, over a seal that had been smudged — deliberately, he was now almost certain — so it couldn't be authenticated. Someone in the palace chain had touched this case already. Someone who wanted the courier erased before he was ever investigated.

And the courier had died carrying whatever it was to a charm-seller on Lantern Row, who denied everything with the calm of a woman who had decided, years ago, exactly what she would say if the inquisition ever came knocking.

Ferro blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the rain.

He was a tired man doing an ugly job in a city that had stopped believing in the job. He had arrested herb-wives and burned charm-stalls and told himself for two decades it was necessary, and lately the telling had stopped working. And tomorrow he would file his report — thin, inconclusive, noting the denial from the palace and recommending continued observation of the Lantern Row businesses — and Chancellor Rell's office would read it or not, and the machinery would grind on.

But he knew, with the certainty of a man who has walked a beat so long he can feel weather coming through his boots, that the case wasn't closed. The courier had finished his delivery. Somewhere behind a neat stack of boxes on Lantern Row, whatever he had bled two days to deliver was sitting in the dark, waiting.

And Dain Ferro, who had knocked politely for twenty-two years, understood that sooner or later he was going to have to stop knocking.

* * *

Three days later, the harbor manifests arrived, and with them a hook he couldn't ignore.

Ferro had requested them as routine — river traffic, past week, anything anomalous. The manifests themselves were dull: grain barges, salt, dye-wood, the ordinary blood of the city. But clipped to the ledger was a note from the harbor master's junior clerk, a nervous young man named Pell who owed Ferro a favor from an old smuggling case.

*Saw the dead man's face in your circular,* the note read. *Remember him now. He came up-river on the Coriander, night tide, eight days back. Paid his berth in new-minted silver. Palace mint stamps. And he wasn't alone — traveled with a guard detail, gray cloaks, no badges. They put him off at the weir steps and rowed straight back downstream without docking. Didn't log out. Didn't sign the tide-book.*

Gray cloaks. No badges. Ferro had been in the Order long enough to know every uniform in the kingdom, and he knew of no unit that wore plain gray and avoided tide-books. He also knew what it meant when men traveled without records: they belonged to someone so powerful that records were considered optional.

He sat with Pell's note for a long time, the lamp burning low, the rain starting up again against the shutters. Then he pulled a fresh sheet of paper and began — not a report, but a letter, addressed to no one, filed nowhere, the kind of letter an honest man writes when he needs to see his own thoughts in ink:

*A courier moves under palace silver and gray escort. The palace denies him. He dies delivering an unnamed thing to a knot-seller who fears me less than she should. Somebody above me is erasing the edges of this case faster than I can draw them.*

*Twenty-two years I've served the Crown, and I have never once been lied to by a seal.*

*I don't think I've been lied to tonight, either. I think I've been managed. And a man who is being managed is a man who has stumbled onto something his managers want buried.*

He signed nothing. He folded it away into the false bottom of his document case, next to the letter from his sister and the medal he'd never worn.

Outside, the rain thickened over the Nightmarket, drumming on the tarpaulins of the charm-row, running in bright threads down the windows of a small, immaculate shop where a woman with ink-stained fingers was, at that very hour, doing something with a length of red cord that no knot-seller in Vharen should have known how to do.

Ferro didn't know that. Not yet.

But he had learned, over twenty-two years of knocking politely, that every polite knock was a promise: *I will come back, and next time I will not be polite.*

He intended to keep it.
