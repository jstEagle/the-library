## 5. Debt of Salt

The *Saltwife* rode low in the water at Berth Nine, which was how Oskar Vey liked her: heavy enough to look legitimate to the harbor inspectors, light enough in the ledger to be invisible to the tax men. He stood at the rail with his third cup of chicory going cold in his hand and watched the gulls argue over a fish head on the pilings, and he thought, as he thought every morning, that today would be the day nothing went wrong.

He should have known better. Nothing going wrong had never once been the shape of his luck.

She came down the pier at half past morning bell, walking like a woman who had somewhere to be and no intention of telling anyone where. Grey cloak, hood back, that dark braid of hers pinned up tight the way she wore it when she meant business instead of charm. Mara Almeida. The charm seller of the Nightmarket. The woman who sold fever-knots for two coppers and luck-knots for five and never once haggled, because—Oskar knew this, had known it for six years, had promised never to say it aloud—every knot she tied was a small working of forbidden craft, and the price was never really about the money.

He set down his cup. "Berth Nine is closed," he called across the gap before she could reach the gangplank. "Terrible fire last night. Very tragic. Whole ship ruined."

"The *Saltwife* doesn't have a mark on her."

"Fire damage is mostly internal." He leaned on the rail. "Go away, Almeida. I'm busy being respectable."

"You've been respectable for eleven minutes. I watched you bribe the harbormaster's clerk from the fishmonger's steps."

"That wasn't bribery, that was *friendship*. Friendship has a market rate, same as anything else." But even as he said it, something cold slid down through his chest, because Mara Almeida did not come to Berth Nine. In six years she had come twice: once to buy passage for a crate she wouldn't explain, and once—the time that mattered—to sit three nights at his sister's bedside while Brenna coughed blood into a rag and the physicians of Vharen shrugged their expensive shoulders and took their fees anyway.

Mara came aboard without asking. Her boots made no sound on the deck; she walked like that always, soft-footed, a woman built by habit out of someone who'd spent years not being heard. Up close he could see what the cloak was hiding: the shadows under her eyes were the color of storm water, and there was a smudge of something dark dried along her sleeve that he very much hoped was ink.

"Whatever it is," he said, "the answer is no."

"I haven't asked anything."

"You will. You've got your asking face on."

"I don't have an asking face."

"Everyone has an asking face. Yours looks like a woman about to apologize for a thing she absolutely intends to do anyway." He crossed his arms. "Out with it."

She looked at him for a long moment. The gulls screamed. Somewhere behind them, in the tangle of masts and cranes and tar-smell that was Vharen's harbor, the city went on shouting itself awake, vendors crying fish and bread and luck, sweet luck, fresh-tied luck—and Oskar watched Mara flinch at that last one, a small flinch, quickly buried, like a man hearing his own name read off a warrant list.

"I need passage out of the city," she said. "Tonight. Two people. Through the Weir Gate, not the main channel."

"Weir Gate's closed to civilian traffic since spring."

"I know."

"It's patrolled."

"I know."

"It's patrolled by inquisitors' men, Mara, not harbor guards. Harbor guards take coin. Inquisitors take heads and then bill your family for the rope."

"I said I know." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse—it went flat and quiet, the way water goes still right before it goes over the edge. "I'm calling it in, Oskar. The favor. The debt."

And there it was. Six years of careful silence, six years of him never saying the word *sigil* within a mile of her shop, six years of pretending that his sister's recovery from a wasting fever that killed four other people on Salt Row that winter had been a miracle of good broth and stubbornness—all of it balanced on a scale, and now she wanted the weight moved.

He turned away from her and gripped the rail and looked at the water, which was grey-green and honest and asked nothing of anyone.

"You know I hate this," he said.

"Yes."

"You know I'd rather owe the Chancellery itself. At least Rell's people only want money and obedience. You want me to run the Weir Gate with a passenger who's wearing ink on her sleeve that I am choosing very hard not to look at."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

She was quiet long enough that he made himself turn around. She hadn't moved. Her hands were folded in front of her like a woman at a graveside, and when she spoke, her voice had lost all its edges.

"Then I'll find another way, and I'll probably die doing it, and that will be my problem and not yours. That's what a debt means, Oskar. It means I ask. It doesn't mean you can't refuse."

"*That's* not what a debt means. A debt means you get to stand there looking noble while I feel like a heel either way. That's the whole cruelty of the system." He dragged both hands down his face. "Where are you going? And don't say you can't tell me, because I'm the one steering the boat, Mara. I need coordinates, or at least a coastline."

"Mount Serel."

He laughed. It came out wrong, too sharp. "Serel's not a port. Serel's a graveyard with a mountain attached. There's nothing on Serel but the old vault roads and rock that eats anchor chains."

"I know what's on Serel."

"Do you?" He studied her then, properly, and what he saw frightened him more than the ink on her sleeve. He'd seen fear before, plenty of it, in eleven years of moving cargo that preferred not to be named. Fear made people twitchy, loud, fast. What was in Mara's face wasn't any of those things. It was grief wearing duty's clothes, and it was aimed inward, at herself. "Who died, Mara?"

Her jaw worked. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "A courier. Last night. In my shop." Her hands unfolded and refolded. "They're looking for whoever he was running from. Or running to. Both, probably."

"They'll be looking for you."

"They already knocked. Politely."

Oskar closed his eyes. *Politely.* With inquisitors, polite was the worst register there was. Loud meant they suspected you of something small. Polite meant they suspected you of something large and were enjoying the process.

"Brenna knows," he said quietly. It wasn't a question. His sister had a nose for trouble the way sharks had a nose for blood; she'd have smelled Mara coming up the pier before Mara cleared the fish stalls.

"She told me to tell you that you've been an ungrateful stone about this debt for six years and it's time you paid interest."

"Of course she did." He opened his eyes. "She's recovering beautifully, by the way. Fully insufferable. Whatever you did to her, it came with side effects."

Something flickered across Mara's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile used to live. "Knotwork," she said. "Just knotwork."

"Just knotwork," Oskar repeated. "Tied by a woman whose shop smells like cedar and secrets, and my sister gets up out of a deathbed and asks for second helpings. Just knotwork." He shook his head slowly. "One day somebody's going to hang for those knots. Do you understand that? Not you—somebody. Some poor devil who bought a luck-charm off you and got searched at a checkpoint. Every knot you sell in that market is a fuse, and you've been lighting them for twelve years."

"I know what they are, Oskar." Her voice cracked, just barely, just at the seam, and he pretended not to hear because she would have hated him for hearing. "I've always known what they are."

The wind came off the water and pushed at them, carrying tar and brine and the distant copper smell of the foundries. On the pier below, a harbor inspector ambled past the *Saltwife*'s lines, glanced up, saw nothing worth climbing aboard for, and ambled on. Oskar waited until the man's back was turned before he spoke again.

"Two people," he said. "You and who?"

"A girl. Liss. Ten years old. Her parents—" Mara stopped. Started again. "She's mine to look after now. That's all anyone needs to know."

"Parents dead?"

"In a fire I couldn't stop."

Oskar looked at her hands. Charm-seller's hands, clever and quick and stained faintly with dye at the knuckles, and he thought about how many times he'd watched them tie a knot in under a breath and thought nothing of it, and how he would never think nothing of it again.

"All right," he said.

Mara blinked. "All right?"

"Don't sound so surprised. You called in a debt; I'm paying it. That's commerce. That's the only religion I keep." He pushed off the rail and started giving orders before the feeling in his chest could catch up with the words, because the feeling was dangerous, the feeling was the kind of warm that got honest smugglers killed. "We sail on the evening tide. I've got a cargo of salted herring going up-coast to Merrow's Bend—legitimate papers, boring as sin, exactly what we want. Weir Gate opens for fishing traffic at slack water, quarter hour either side of the turn. We go through slow, stupid, and lawful-looking. Nobody runs the Weir Gate. Running attracts eyes. We *drift* through it, half asleep, smelling of fish, and the guards wave us past because their feet hurt and their supper's waiting."

"And if they board us?"

"Then you're below decks with the girl, behind the false bulkhead in the hold, and you do not come out until I whistle the gull-call twice. Can you sit still in the dark with a scared child for however long it takes?"

"I sat still in the dark for twelve years, Oskar. I'm very good at it."

He looked at her again and wished he hadn't, because she meant it literally, and it showed.

"There's bread and water behind the bulkhead," he said gruffly. "And a lamp. Don't use the lamp unless you hear fighting. If you hear fighting, use the lamp, find the grate above the bilge stringer, and get out onto the weir wall. There's a maintenance ladder every forty yards. Climb, walk north, don't stop till you see the shrine lights at Tallow Cross. Can you remember that?"

"Gull-call twice. Lamp only if fighting. Grate over the bilge stringer. Ladder every forty yards. Tallow Cross."

"Hm. You'd have made a decent smuggler."

"I'd have made a hanged one. Same skillset, shorter career."

Despite everything—despite the courier, despite the polite knock, despite the mountain-shaped dread sitting down between them—Oskar laughed. It startled him, the sound of it, rough and real in his own throat. Mara almost smiled again, that ghost of a thing.

"What about payment?" she asked. "For the run."

"Your debt pays for the run."

"No. The debt paid for Brenna. This is new work. New work costs."

"Then you can pay me by never tying me another one of those knots as long as I live. I mean it. I don't want luck that comes from wherever yours comes from. I want bad honest luck like the rest of the world."

"Most people beg me for the opposite."

"Most people are idiots." He said it lightly, and then, because the morning had earned some honesty: "I watched what your craft cost you, Mara. Even before whatever happened last night. Six years, I've watched you sell charms to people who needed them and hate yourself for selling them. That's not a life. That's a sentence being served in installments."

She went very still. When she answered, her voice was low and strange. "And after tonight, what would you call it?"

"An escape," he said. "Even if you're going to Serel. Especially if you're going to Serel."

* * *

The rest of the day was work, and work was mercy.

Oskar threw himself into the loading like a man fleeing a fire, which was more or less the truth of it. Herring barrels went down into the hold in their proper order, ballast shifted, papers forged and re-forged until even he believed the *Saltwife* was nothing more than she appeared. He argued with the cooper about stave widths. He drank chicory he didn't taste. He let the rhythm of honest dishonest labor carry him through the hours until the light went amber and the tide began its slow turn, and through all of it he was aware of Mara moving through the ship's shadows like a rumor, learning the *Saltwife* the way she seemed to learn everything—quietly, completely, and without ever quite letting you catch her at it.

Brenna found him at dusk on the aft deck, leaning on the taffrail, watching the sun drown itself in the smelter-smoke west of the city.

"You said yes," she said.

"I said yes."

"You hesitated first, though. Long enough to enjoy it."

"I savored it, Brenna. Like a fine wine."

His sister leaned her elbows next to his. She was thin still, thinner than she'd been before the fever, but her color was good and her eyes were sharp, and every day he woke up grateful in a way he didn't have clean language for. Gratitude, in Oskar's experience, was like ballast—you didn't admire it, you just lived differently because it was there.

"She's going to die, you know," Brenna said. "If this goes wrong. They don't exile sigil-workers anymore. They haven't for years."

"I know what they do."

"So why'd you say yes?"

He was quiet for a while. Below them, lamplighters were moving along the quay, and the Nightmarket was waking up in its corner of the world, and somewhere in that sprawl of canvas and smoke a woman had spent twelve years keeping a promise to dead people by making trinkets for living ones.

"Because she saved you," he said finally. "Because she sat three nights in a row without sleeping and sent me out of the room so I wouldn't see what she was doing, and whatever she did, it worked, and she's never once held it over me. Never mentioned it. Never priced it. Six years, Brenna. People don't do that. People aren't built to do that." He turned the cold dregs of his chicory over the side. "So when she finally does ask for something, the only question is whether I'm the kind of man who pretends he doesn't know why he owes her. And I'd rather be hanged than that man."

Brenna bumped her shoulder against his. "You're a sentimental wreck, Oskar Vey."

"Not a word of this to the crew."

"Obviously not. I'll save it for your eulogy."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

They came aboard after full dark: Mara with a sea-bag over one shoulder, and beside her a girl.

Liss was small and hollow-eyed and clutching a carved wooden bird like the world might take it if she loosened her fingers one degree. She had ash still worked into the creases of her ears, Oskar noticed, ash no one had washed out, and when Mara put a steadying hand between the girl's shoulder blades the child leaned into it the way iron filings lean to a lodestone—without deciding to, without any choice left in the matter.

"This is Captain Vey," Mara said gently. "He's going to take us up the coast."

Liss looked at Oskar. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which was somehow worse than tears, and she said, in a voice sanded flat by grief: "Are you a good man or a bad one?"

Oskar crouched down to her level, which hurt his knees and didn't matter. "I'm a smuggler," he said. "Which is mostly a bad man with excellent timing."

The girl considered this with terrible seriousness. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and let Mara lead her below.

Oskar straightened up and found Brenna watching him from the wheel with an expression of unbearable knowing.

"Not one word," he warned.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said everything with your whole face."

"My whole face says *cast off*, captain." She nodded past him, toward the harbor mouth. "Tide's turning."

It was. Oskar felt it before he saw it, in the way the mooring lines sighed and the pilings groaned—a vast slow shifting beneath the world, the sea remembering its schedule. Out beyond the breakwater, past the chain-lights and the customs house and the black teeth of the weir, the open water waited, cold and enormous and indifferent.

He took the wheel. Mara came up from below and stood at the rail beside him, hood back, watching Vharen begin to slide away astern—the Nightmarket's glow, the palace on its hill with its windows burning yellow and blind, the spires, the smoke, the whole loud wounded animal of the city that had raised her and hunted her and never learned her name.

"Twelve years," she said softly. Almost to herself.

Oskar eased the *Saltwife* out of Berth Nine and turned her bow toward the Weir Gate, and the city fell away behind them like a coat taken off at the end of a long shift. He told himself the tightness in his chest was just the tide working against the rudder. He was a liar by trade; he was very good at it.

They were halfway to the gate lights, the patrol lanterns swinging lazily on their posts, when Mara went rigid at the rail.

"Oskar," she said. "Stop the boat."

"We're in the channel, I can't—"

"*Stop the boat.*" Her voice had gone strange and thin, and when he looked at her she was staring at the water alongside the hull with an expression he had never seen on a human face and hoped never to see again.

In the black water, far down where the lantern light barely reached, something pale was turning over. Many somethings. Shapes without reflections, drifting up from the deep channel toward the surface, toward the light, toward the ship—

toward them.
