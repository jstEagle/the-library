## 6. Out Through the Weir Gate

*Mara*

The Weir Gate smelled of tar and old fish and fear.

Mara had passed through it a hundred times in her life without thinking twice—once a year, maybe, when she bought salt cod directly off the harbor boats instead of paying Nightmarket prices, or the winter Oskar's sister had been sick and she'd carried fever-charms down to the Saltrow tenements wrapped in brown paper like sweets. The gate had never been anything but an arch of wet granite and two bored customs men who waved through anyone with a barrel or a basket.

Now there were four men at the checkpoint, not two. Now they had a brazier burning even though the morning was mild, and above it hung a wire cage with something dark inside—a raven, she realized as they drew closer, dead and stiff, its feathers crusted with frost. A warding display. Old magic, ugly magic, the kind the Crown's witch-finders had used during the Purges: kill a bird at dawn, hang it where it can see the road, and any sigilwork passing beneath will gutter like a candle in wind.

She kept her eyes on the cobbles and let Oskar do the talking.

"Vey," the nearer customs man said, and spat to the side, though not quite at Oskar's boots, which for Oskar constituted warmth and welcome. "You're up early. Or late. Which is it?"

"Neither. Both." Oskar leaned against the customs table like he owned it, like the harbor itself was a thing he'd personally invented. "Haul of dried herring going out on the *Kestrel's Mercy*. Captain wants it aboard before the tide turns. You know how he gets about the tide."

The customs man—broad, gray-whiskered, with a lead stamp hanging from his neck on a leather cord—looked past Oskar at the cart. At Mara. At the girl.

Liss had been Liss for six days now, and in that time Mara had learned three things about her: she could sleep anywhere, she ate like a stevedore, and when she was frightened she went very still, the way small animals did, as if stillness might make her invisible. She was very still now, sitting among the herring barrels with her hands folded in her lap, wearing Oskar's spare coat with the sleeves rolled back four times.

"New crew?" the customs man asked.

"My sister's girl," Oskar said. "Come to see the boats. And the woman's my factor. Counts the barrels so nobody robs me, which everyone does."

"Everyone robs you because you deserve it." But the man was already reaching for his stamp, already bored, already turning back toward the ledger—and that was when the second customs man, younger, with the raw red knuckles of someone new to outdoor work, stepped away from the brazier and said:

"Wait. There's a notice."

Mara felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into still water.

*There's a notice.*

Of course there was a notice. A courier had died in her shop with a king's seal in his blood. Inquisitors had come to her door with polite questions and patient eyes. Of course the harbors were watched; she'd known it, had counted on Oskar's face and the herring and the tide to carry them past it anyway, and hope was exactly the kind of stupid, expensive thing she could no longer afford.

"What notice?" Oskar said, in the voice of a man mildly inconvenienced by paperwork. He was good. He was better than good. Mara watched him frown at nothing, scratch under his cap, radiate the specific boredom of the perpetually harassed. "If this is about the tariff dispute again, I've paid my—"

"Three people." The young one had a paper now, holding it close to his face against the harbor glare. "A woman, dark-haired. A man, bearded, smuggler's build. And—" he squinted, "—a child. Girl, eight or nine years. Traveling together. By order of the Crown Chancery."

"A child," Oskar repeated. He laughed, short and easy, and Mara marveled at it even as her hands went cold inside her gloves. "What kind of criminal travels with a child? What's she wanted for, stealing bread?"

"It doesn't say what for."

"It never does. Come on, then, look at us. Woman, yes, I'll grant you the woman. Bearded man of smuggler's build—that's half the harbor. And a girl." He gestured grandly at the cart, at the barrels, at Liss sitting among them with her folded hands. "This is my sister's daughter, Bess, and if you're going to drag her off your own mother will skin you, Tomas Ellery, and I'll hold your coat."

The young man—Ellery—hesitated. His eyes went to Liss, and Liss, bless her frightened animal heart, looked back at him with wide, blank, bored eyes, the eyes of a child dragged out of bed before dawn to watch herring loaded, and for one long breath Mara allowed herself to believe.

Then the older customs man said, quietly: "Show me your hands, missus. Both of you. It's the notice—says to check hands."

*Check hands.* For guild marks. For ink, for scars, for the burn-scar of a removed brand. Twelve years Mara had worn long sleeves and fingerless work-gloves, twelve years of keeping her left forearm turned discreetly inward, and now, at a herring checkpoint, on an ordinary gray morning, it all came down to bare skin.

"I'm holding the manifest," Oskar started.

"Hands, Vey, or no tide for your herring."

Mara breathed once, slowly, the way Aldric had taught her thirty years ago in a cold room above a bakery, breathing down into the belly, letting the fear move through and out. *Fear is fuel,* he'd said, back when saying such things aloud was merely dangerous rather than fatal. *It burns hot. Don't waste it. Spend it.*

She pulled off her gloves.

Her hands were a craftswoman's hands, scarred across the knuckles, nails trimmed blunt, thread-calluses thick along the pads of her fingers. Nothing on them. Nothing on the backs, nothing between them. The guild mark lived higher, inside her left forearm below the elbow, hidden by sleeve and habit and twelve years of careful geometry—the angle of her arm at rest, the way she stood in doorways, the hundred small choreographies of concealment that had become so automatic she sometimes forgot she was performing them.

Ellery took her right hand, turned it over, frowned at the calluses, let it go. Took her left—

And she saw him see it. Saw his gaze snag at the wrist, at the edge of the old faded line where a mark had been burned away—not hers, that scar was a story for another day—but his eyes narrowed anyway, professional suspicion waking up like a dog hearing a floorboard creak, and he reached for her sleeve.

* * *

*Oskar*

Oskar Vey had been in perhaps four hundred fights in his life, depending on how you counted, and he had learned early that most of them were won or lost in the two seconds before the first blow. Two seconds to read a room. Two seconds to know where the exits were, which man was scared, which man was stupid, whether the odds demanded charm or violence or a well-timed apology.

He was counting his two seconds now, and the arithmetic was bad.

Four customs men. One of them old and tired, one of them young and eager and currently pulling at a woman's sleeve. Beyond them, the harbor road ran straight and open to the *Kestrel's Mercy*, moored at the third quay, and between here and there stood a second checkpoint he'd have to talk through all over again—if they got past this one. Behind them, the city, and somewhere in the city, Ferro, the polite inquisitor with the honest eyes who would figure it out eventually, because men like that always did.

Two seconds. No exits. Bad odds.

So naturally, in the middle of his two seconds, Mara Almeida did something insane.

She moved—not away from the young customs man's grip but *with* it, stumbling forward off the cart-step like a clumsy woman, like a factor who'd had one drink too many at breakfast, and her free hand came up and knocked over the brazier.

It wasn't much of a brazier. Harbor-standard, dented iron, coals banked low against the mild morning, and it went over with a clatter and a scatter of dull orange, and the young man flinched, everyone flinched, and in that flinch—in that half-second of universal, human *oh shit*—Oskar saw Mara's fingers move.

Not grab. Not push. *Move.* A quick weaving motion, thumb and forefinger, the way she braided a cord, the way she tied off a knot, fast enough that if you weren't looking for it you'd have seen nothing but a woman catching her balance.

And the light changed.

That was the only way Oskar could ever describe it afterward, and he described it often, usually after the third drink, and every time he told it the light got stranger. It didn't flash. It didn't glow. It simply *changed*—the gray morning glare coming off the water seemed to bend, to pool, to pour itself directly into the young customs man's eyes, all of it at once, a whole harbor's worth of reflected sun funneled into one pair of pupils.

Ellery screamed.

He went backward over the customs table, arms over his face, screaming about being blind, and he wasn't blind, Oskar could see that even from the cart—he'd see fine by nightfall, maybe sooner—but for those first few seconds he was a man staring into the sun, except the sun had reached out and taken him by the throat.

"Gods—" The older customs man was scrambling around the table. "Tomas! What happened, what did you—"

"The light—" Ellery was curled up, heels drumming the cobbles. "It's in my eyes, it's *in my eyes*—"

And the other two, the ones who'd been standing at the far end of the checkpoint, came running, and Oskar made his decision the way he made all his decisions: quickly, selfishly, and in the direction of survival, except that this time—for reasons he would examine later with considerable irritation—the direction of survival included a woman, a child, and a cartload of herring.

"Go!" he shouted, and whipped the mule into motion.

The cart lurched forward. The customs men scattered—one diving for Ellery, one grabbing uselessly at the cart wheel and losing his grip, the fourth just standing there with his mouth open, watching a dead raven swing in its cage above a spilled brazier while sigilcraft, actual forbidden sigilcraft, rolled away down the harbor road on a wagon of dried fish.

"*Stop!*" the old one roared. "STOP IN THE CROWN'S NAME—"

Nobody stopped. Nobody ever stopped for the Crown's name in Vharen unless there was a blade behind it, and the blades were busy.

Oskar drove. The mule, offended by the shouting, found a pace Oskar hadn't known it possessed. Behind them the checkpoint dissolved into chaos—Ellery weeping and blinking at a world gone white, the brazier hissing as seawater from a dropped bucket quenched it, and above it all the dead raven turning slowly on its wire, frost melting off its feathers, its ward broken by nothing more than a spilled fire and a knot tied in empty air.

Beside him, Mara pulled her glove back on. Her hands weren't quite steady. Her face was lit up like a festival.

"That," Oskar said, "was the stupidest thing I have ever seen."

"You're welcome."

"They'll describe it. The blind man, the other three—they'll all describe it. A woman, a gesture, a *light*—Ferro will have that testimony by sundown and he'll know, he'll *know*—"

"He already knows," Mara said quietly. "He knew when he sat in my shop and asked me about knots. This doesn't tell him anything he doesn't know. It tells everyone else." She looked back once, at the shrinking chaos of the checkpoint, and Oskar saw the festival-light in her face gutter and go out, replaced by something heavier. "Twelve years," she said. "I was invisible for twelve years. Do you understand what I did back there? I don't think you can. I just ended it. Permanently. There's no coming back from working sigilcraft in front of four witnesses."

"So why'd you do it?"

She was quiet for a moment. Ahead of them the *Kestrel's Mercy* grew larger, her patched sails already loose in her rigging, waiting.

"Because he was going to find the mark," she said finally. "And because Liss is in this cart. And because—" her jaw tightened, and Oskar watched her wrestle with it, whatever true thing lived underneath, "—and because some part of me has wanted to do that for twelve years, and I hate myself for how good it felt."

"There it is," Oskar said.

"Don't."

"Oh, I'm not judging. I set fire to a tax office in Corvo Bay when I was nineteen and I *still* feel good about it." He flicked the reins. "I'm just saying, if we're cataloguing sins, mine come with receipts too."

* * *

*Mara*

The *Kestrel's Mercy* took them aboard with the efficiency of people who had done illegal things quickly before. A mate with a face like a closed fist counted heads—three, not four, and something in Mara's chest twisted at the arithmetic of the waystation, at the family whose wagon had burned, at the daughter who was currently sitting in the ship's shadow eating hardtack like it was a feast—and then they were moving, down the gangway, into the hold's warm darkness that smelled of pitch and brine and old rope.

Which meant one checkpoint remained: the harbor chain.

Every vessel leaving Vharen passed the chain tower at the harbor mouth, where a final inspection boat came alongside and a Crown officer boarded to review papers. Oskar had explained it the night before, drawing lines in spilled flour on his kitchen table: the trick wasn't avoiding the inspection, it was surviving it. Papers could be forged. Cargo could be mislabeled. Faces could not.

"Stay below," Oskar told her. "Both of you. Hold's got a false bulkhead behind the salt barrels—cramped, but it's held before. Inspection takes a quarter hour, maybe less. The captain's paid. Just sit tight and don't breathe on the salt."

"And if they search deeper?"

"They won't."

"Oskar."

"They won't," he said again, and there was something in his voice she couldn't name—confidence worn thin over dread, the sound of a man repeating a thing until it became true. Then he climbed the ladder and was gone, and it was just Mara and Liss and the dark.

The false bulkhead was exactly as cramped as promised. They wedged themselves between salt barrels and hull planking, knees to chests, and Mara put her arm around the girl because the girl was shaking, and because the shaking reminded Mara that she herself was not.

"Liss." She kept her voice low and even, the voice she'd used on frightened customers, on feverish children back when she'd still dared to treat children. "Look at me. Can you look at me?"

The girl's eyes found hers in the dark, huge and gray.

"In a little while, a man is going to come down that ladder. He'll walk around. He might tap things. Whatever you hear, you don't make a sound. Not a whimper, not a sneeze. Can you do that?"

"What if they find us?"

"They won't."

"What happens if they find us?" Liss persisted, with the terrible logic of children. "At the waystation, the men with the gray faces—my papa said run, and Mama said run, and I ran, and—"

Her voice cracked, and Mara gathered her in, and the girl cried silently into her shoulder the way children cry when they've learned crying loudly costs something. Mara held her and stared into the blackness and thought about hands. About the young customs man rolling on the cobbles with the light in his eyes. About the exact configuration of her fingers, the knot she'd tied in the air itself, the oldest trick in the guild's book—a simple dazzle, a child's charm, the kind of thing she'd once taught apprentices as a first lesson, harmless, reversible, gone by dusk.

She had blinded a man in front of the Crown and fled the city of her birth on a herring boat.

And the awful, unforgivable truth, the truth she'd confessed to Oskar on the harbor road and now tasted again in the dark: it had felt like opening a window in a room sealed for twelve years. The power had risen through her like water through dry ground, familiar, sweet, *hers*, and for one shining half-second she had been Mara Almeida of the Sigilwrights' Guild entire, and not a trinket-seller, not a ghost, not a woman pretending to be less than she was.

*We serve,* the old oath whispered in her memory. *We do not consume.*

But oh, she had consumed that moment. She had drunk it down.

Above deck, boots thundered. Voices—the captain's, oily and expansive; another voice, clipped, official. The ladder creaked. Light fell in a blade-thin line through the hatch and swept across the hold, and Mara pressed Liss's head down against her shoulder and held her breath and listened to the officer's footsteps come closer, closer, stopping—

—at the salt barrels. Three feet away. Close enough that she could hear the rustle of paper, the scratch of a quill, the wet sound of the man chewing something as he worked.

Tap. Tap. Tap. Knuckles on wood.

Liss's small fist clenched in Mara's shirt. Mara closed her eyes and, in the darkness where no one could see, where no witness stood, where it was only her and the girl and the god of desperate women, she began to tie a knot.

Not in rope. Not in air. In nothing—in the space between heartbeats, the place Aldric had called the loom, the quiet room behind the world where all binding began. A tiny pattern. A hush-pattern. A mother's charm, the kind woven into crib-clothes in the old days to keep infants sleeping through storms.

She laid it over Liss like a blanket.

The girl's trembling stopped. Her breathing slowed and deepened, and she sagged against Mara's side, asleep, genuinely asleep, in the last place on earth a child should sleep peacefully.

The officer tapped the barrels one last time, muttered something about herring, and climbed back into the light.

* * *

*Oskar*

The chain tower passed astern an hour later, and Oskar stood at the rail and watched it shrink and hated himself with tremendous efficiency.

It was a talent of his. He could hate himself in eleven languages. He could hate himself while doing arithmetic, while drinking, while apparently—this was new—while rescuing people. Because the fact of the matter, the fact he'd been dodging since the waystation fire, since the night he'd found himself holding a bucket line beside a sobbing child while her parents' wagon went up like a pyre, was this: Oskar Vey had planned to take Mara out of the city alone. One passenger. Clean job, quick payment, debt settled, sister avenged in the only currency he had.

Instead he had a woman who threw light at customs officers, a child who ate his provisions, and a course set for Mount Serel, which every sailor's superstition agreed was where luck went to die.

"You're brooding," Mara said, coming up beside him at the rail. The coast was sliding past, green-gray cliffs, gull-white spray, Vharen already a smudge of smoke behind them. Liss was asleep in a coil of netting, looking younger than eight, which should not have been possible.

"I'm calculating," Oskar said. "Brooding is for poets."

"What's the calculation?"

"How much trouble I'm in." He squinted at the horizon. "Answer: all of it. Every bit of trouble in the kingdom is currently assigned to me personally."

"You could turn back at the next port."

"No."

It came out faster than he meant it to, and flatter, and Mara turned to look at him with those sharp dark eyes that saw entirely too much, and Oskar discovered he had suddenly developed a passionate interest in the rigging.

"No," she repeated softly.

"The tide's wrong to turn back." Which was a lie; the tide was perfect. "Besides, you're hopeless on your own. You'd blind yourself inside a week. And the kid—" He jerked his chin at the netting. "Someone has to teach her to swear properly. You'd raise her saying things like 'please' and 'thank you.' Criminal. Negligent."

Mara smiled. It was small and tired and real, and Oskar looked away from it because looking directly at it caused symptoms.

Then she said, "There's a village past the headlands. Sallowmere. The map route goes through it."

"And?"

"And when we stopped for water yesterday, did you see the fields?"

He had. He'd said nothing, because sailors learn early not to comment on other people's bad harvests, but he had seen them—blighted rows, gray stalks, orchards standing bare in high summer as if it were midwinter.

"The children," Mara said. "Did you see the children?"

He hadn't. He'd been fixing the axle.

"Eyes like ash," she said quietly. "Listless. An old woman grabbed my wrist in the square and asked me—I'd worn my sleeves pushed up, stupid, careless—and she saw the edge of the mark and she *begged* me, Oskar. She begged me to help them. And I gave her a fever-charm, a fake one, a trinket, and I got back in the cart." Her knuckles whitened on the rail. "Whatever is happening to this kingdom—it's not just in the capital. It's in the roads and the fields and the children, and I spent twelve years not looking, and I don't know how to keep not looking anymore."

Oskar was quiet for a long moment. The *Kestrel's Mercy* creaked and leaned, and ahead of them the coast bent northward, toward the mountains, toward Serel, toward whatever waited in the ruins of a guild he only knew from Mara's nightmares.

"Well," he said at last. "First rule of smuggling: once you've seen the cargo, you're responsible for it."

"That's not a real rule."

"It is now." He pushed off the rail and headed for the galley, because the child would wake hungry and the woman would forget to eat and somebody on this doomed expedition had to be practical. "Sallowmere, then. One stop. Then mountains."

Behind him, Mara said nothing. But when he glanced back, she was watching the northern horizon with her sleeves rolled up and her marked forearm bare to the open sky, and she wore the expression of a woman stepping off a cliff on purpose.

Oskar Vey sighed, and followed her over the edge.

They made landfall at Duskfall Cove near evening—and the first thing they saw on the shore road, standing in a knot around a posted notice, were three soldiers in gray, their faces smooth and pulseless, and one of them turned its head toward Mara as if it had heard her name spoken across a mile of water.
