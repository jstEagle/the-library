## 7. The Road Remembers

The road out of Vharen climbed through country that had once been famous for its orchards.

Mara knew this from her mother's stories—pear trees heavy enough to break their branches, cider pressed in every farmhouse, roadsides so thick with wild plum that travelers ate their fill for free. What she saw from the cart was something else. The orchards still stood, row after row of them marching up the foothills, but the trees were bare in a season when they should have been heavy with green fruit. Not pruned-bare. *Starved*-bare. Their bark had gone gray as old ash, and when the wind moved through them there was no rustle of leaves, only a dry creaking, like doors in an empty house.

"Been like that since Serel's Folly," Oskar said, following her gaze. He drove with his elbows, reins loose across his knuckles, the mule plodding at its own pace. "Three years back. First it was just the late frost took the blossom. Then the next year the trees leafed and dropped everything by midsummer. Now nothing. Farmers keep planting because what else is there to do."

"The soil?"

"Soil's fine. I've seen them dig it. Black and rich as ever." He shrugged. "It's like the land forgot how."

Mara said nothing. Her hands lay folded in her lap around the wax-sealed map she had not let out of her sight since the shop. Beneath her sleeve, the guild mark sat quiet against her wrist—a small knotwork wolf, inked when she was fourteen—but she could feel it all the same, faintly warm, the way a stone stays warm after you take it away from a fire.

They passed a farmstead where a woman stood in a field of wheat that came up to her knees and no higher. It should have been past her waist by now. She wasn't working it. She was just standing among the stunted stalks, looking south toward the city, and Mara turned away before the woman could turn toward them.

*The Binding*, the courier's map whispered in her memory. The stolen sigil, the hollow king, the luck of a kingdom being drunk down by something that wore a crown. She had thought, on the road out through the Weir Gate, with a guard clutching his blinded eyes and her heart hammering like a smith's hammer—that she had thought it was about the city. About the palace, the inquisitors, the vault beneath Mount Serel.

She was beginning to understand it was about all of this. Everything the road touched.

* *

The first village was called Brant's Hollow, though the sign at the crossroads had weathered until only *Hollow* remained legible, and Mara thought there was a grim joke in that somewhere if she'd had the heart to laugh.

They stopped because Liss needed milk and because the mule needed water, and because Oskar claimed—loudly, in his way of claiming things he didn't believe—that a man could smell a decent loaf from half a mile off. The village was small, forty roofs maybe, clustered along a stream that ran thin and strangely clear. Too clear. Mara crouched beside it while Oskar watered the mule and watched the water move over stones without a single weed bending in the current, without a single fish shadow holding against the flow. A stream like a drawn line rather than a living thing.

Children played in the lane outside the well house. Three of them, a game of knucklebones tossed against a wall. And it was the children who made Mara stop walking.

Two of them had eyes the color of dishwater. Not blue, not gray in any lively sense—gray the way ash is gray, gray the way fog is gray when it means to stay a week. The third child, younger, still had brown eyes, but even from ten paces Mara could see the gray creeping in at the rims, like frost advancing across a windowpane.

The children didn't look up when she passed. That was the worst of it. Children always looked up. These three kept tossing their bones and catching them, slow and rhythmic, their faces smooth and untroubled as river stones, and one of them missed a catch and did not react at all, simply picked the bone up and resumed the same rhythm, the same toss, the same catch.

"Mara." Oskar's voice, low, behind her. "You seeing this?"

"I'm seeing it."

"Kids look like that in the capital too. Near the palace district." He shifted his weight, uncomfortable, a big man trying to make himself smaller. "I figured it was the smoke. Bad air. City thing."

"It's not the smoke." Her mouth was dry. "Something is being taken out of them. Slowly. Something they need."

An old woman was watching them from the doorway of the well house—a small, bent figure with a shawl the color of undyed wool and a face seamed like cracked clay. She had been watching, Mara realized, not the strangers, but Mara herself. Specifically her wrists. Her sleeves.

Mara pulled her cuffs down. An instinct twelve years deep, fast as breathing.

The old woman crossed the lane with surprising quickness and caught her hand before she could finish, turning the wrist up, baring the knotwork wolf to the daylight. Her fingers were cold and light as dried stalks.

"You're one of theirs," the old woman said. "One of the Sigilborn."

"I don't know what you mean," Mara said, automatic, the lie rising so smoothly it disgusted her. "That's just a tattoo. Girls get them. It doesn't mean—"

"Child." The old woman's grip tightened, surprisingly strong. Her eyes were wet and fierce and utterly unfooled. "I sat at Hesta Vane's table when your guild mothers came through selling fever-knots for a copper apiece. I watched a woman pull a burning fever out of my son's chest with two fingers and a word. You think I've forgotten what the mark looks like? Twelve years of the Crown telling us it never existed, and you think I've forgotten?" Her voice cracked. "I have not forgotten."

Around the lane, doors had opened. Not many—a crack here, a shadowed gap there—but Mara felt the attention of the village gathering like weather. Oskar had gone very still by the mule, one hand resting easy near the knife at his belt, not threatening, just present.

"Please," the old woman said, and now her voice broke entirely, and she bent her head over Mara's captured hand as though it were an altar. "Please. My granddaughter. Neya. She's eight. Since spring her eyes have been going, going gray, and she sleeps sixteen hours a day and wakes tired, and the healer in Corren's Ford says it's bad blood and bleeds her, which does nothing, nothing, and I—" She looked up. "I heard the stories. Before the Purges. That the guild could mend anything that was *taken*. Please. I'll give you anything. I have almost nothing left, but I'll give it."

Mara's throat had closed.

Because here it was. Here was the bill, presented in full, with interest. Twelve years of keeping her head down. Twelve years of harmless trinkets and luck-knots and fever-charms that worked only a little better than they should, calibrated to the width of a hair, never enough to draw notice, never enough to matter. Twelve years of telling herself that survival was resistance, that staying hidden was keeping the flame alive, that one day—

And all the while, children like Neya had been going gray in villages exactly like this one, and there had been no one. No guild mothers pulling fevers out of small chests. No sigilborn walking the mountain roads. Just empty orchards and clear dead streams and healers bleeding children for bad blood.

*I'm sorry,* she wanted to say. *I'm so sorry. I was afraid.*

What she said was: "Take me to her."

* *

The house was poor but clean, and it smelled of boiled grain and beeswax, and in the back room a girl named Neya slept in a narrow bed beneath a quilt stitched with patterns of pears—the old abundance remembered in thread, because the trees themselves had failed.

She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful. Pale, fine-featured, still. Her chest rose and fell with the patience of tide. And her eyes, when the grandmother eased them open, were nearly all gray now, only a thin ring of true brown holding at the center, besieged.

Mara knelt beside the bed and did the thing she had not done in twelve years.

She closed her eyes and reached.

Not with her hands—with the older self underneath her hands, the one she kept folded up small inside her like a moth in winter. She found the girl's thread. Everyone had one; the guild had taught her to see them young, bright filaments running through the world, each person a knot where their thread gathered and held its shape. A healthy knot sang. This one barely hummed. It was frayed, unraveling strand by strand, and Mara followed the fraying down and down, past the body, past the breath, into the place where the girl's luck and vitality pooled—and found it shallow. Found it being drawn away through a channel she couldn't see the end of, flowing somewhere far and dark, uphill, toward the city on the plain.

Toward the crown.

Her own mark flared hot against her wrist, and she gasped and pulled back.

"Well?" The grandmother hovered. Oskar filled the doorway, arms crossed. "Well, what do you see?"

"She's not sick," Mara said slowly. "There's nothing wrong with her. Something is being *taken*. Steadily. Like—" She searched for it. "Like a tax. Levied on her whole life at once, collected daily."

"Can you stop it?"

"I can ease it. Maybe. For a while." She flexed her stiff fingers. "I can't cut the channel. I don't know where it leads well enough to cut it safely, and cutting blind might kill her faster than the drain will." The honest answer. The useless answer. She hated it. "But I can weave a knot that slows the outflow. Buys her years instead of months. Will that do?"

The old woman made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed Mara's hand between both of hers. "Child, it would do for a lifetime."

* *

The work took an hour and cost her more than she expected.

Knot-craft was the gentlest discipline the guild had practiced—no carving into flesh, no blood on the page, just cord and pattern and intent braided together until the world agreed to hold a shape. Mara used the red wool Oskar bought in the village, and she wove with her eyes half-closed and her breath timed to the sleeping girl's, laying a sevenfold interlace that the oldest guild-mothers called *the cradle*: a pattern designed to shelter what it held, to slow every loss, every leak, every theft.

Twelve years since her fingers had done this honestly, at full strength, without sanding the power down to something safe and sellable. Her hands remembered. Her hands had always remembered. That was the bitter joke of it—she could have done this any day, any village, any year. She had chosen not to.

When the knot was done she tied it above the girl's bedframe and sat back on her heels, sweating lightly, feeling pleasantly emptied, the way she used to feel after work in the old days.

Neya woke within the hour.

It was not dramatic. She simply stirred, blinked, asked for bread and honey in a voice rusty with sleep—and her eyes were still gray, would stay gray until the source of the drain was dealt with—but the ring of brown at their center was wider. Clearer. The grandmother wept into her apron and fed the child honey straight from the jar, and Neya ate it solemnly and then, wonder of wonders, asked to go outside.

Mara stood in the doorway and watched her join the other children in the lane. Watched her pick up a fallen knucklebone and, for the first time since they'd arrived, actually *look* at it—turn it over, frown at it, ask the others a question with the sharp, irritated brightness of a healthy eight-year-old. One of the gray-eyed children stared at her, and something flickered in that ash-colored gaze, some drowned spark noticing the surface of the water overhead.

"That's the third one," Oskar said quietly beside her. He'd been leaning on the doorpost. "Third village since the pass with the same story. Blighted fields, dead streams, kids going gray-eyed. And you sit there looking like someone kicked your dog."

"They should have had me twelve years ago," Mara said. "Or eleven. Or five. Last month. Any of them."

"You'd have been hanged."

"Yes." She watched Neya laugh—a small, startled sound, like the girl herself was surprised by it. "I did the arithmetic a long time ago, Oskar. My life against the work. I chose mine. Every day, for twelve years, I got up and chose mine again. And I told myself it was strategy. Keeping the craft alive. Being the last vessel." Her jaw tightened. "But a vessel that never pours out is just a sealed jar. I wasn't keeping the flame alive. I was keeping it for myself, because I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up in noble language so I wouldn't have to call it what it was."

Oskar was quiet for a moment. He was better at silence than most men, having grown up in a house with six sisters.

"For what it's worth," he said finally, "the ones who poured freely got hanged anyway, and the kids went gray regardless. Your hiding didn't cause this." He nodded at the fields, the pale children, the whole gray sickness of the valley. "Whatever's drinking these people dry started long after the Purges. You not selling charms didn't dig this well."

"No." Mara exhaled. "But I could have spent twelve years digging people *out*. And I didn't. Neutrality isn't innocence, Oskar. It's just innocence wearing a nicer coat."

He grunted. From him, that was agreement.

* *

They stayed two nights. Word traveled the way it does in hill country—slowly, and then all at once—and by the second morning there were people at the grandmother's gate: a farmer whose wife hadn't felt warmth in her hands for two winters; a boy of fifteen whose hair had gone white at the temples; a mother carrying a toddler who slept too much and laughed too little. They came shyly, hat in hand, the way people approach a priest or a magistrate, and Mara understood with a lurch that to them she *was* a kind of authority—the return of an order they'd been taught was extinct, a rumor made flesh with cord and red wool.

She worked until her hands shook. She refused payment beyond food and feed for the mule, which caused genuine offense until she reframed it as guild law—"the Sigilborn take no coin for mending what was stolen"—and then they paid her in eggs, in onions, in a jar of last-year's honey, in blessings murmured so fervently she had to leave the room.

On the second evening the old woman—her name was Sorrel, she was Neya's grandmother, she had buried two husbands and one king's promise—sat with Mara by the hearth and talked, as old women will, and gave her things no archive held.

"There were four of them came through here, the last time. Guild folk. Two men, two women, and one of the women was young enough to be a daughter. They stayed a week and mended half the valley, and then the king's men came through after, asking questions, and we told them nothing." Sorrel's eyes glinted. "We weren't fools. But the guild folk went on toward the mountain, toward Serel, and none of them ever came back down. That was the autumn before the Purges."

Mara's blood went cold and slow. "Toward Mount Serel."

"Toward the mountain, yes." Sorrel poked the fire. "And I'll tell you something else, since you've the mark and the manners both. My mother was a dairy-woman on the mountain's lower slopes, before they closed the high passes. She used to say there was a door under Serel. Under the snowfield, in the black rock, a door the old guild kept. She said the mountain hummed sometimes, on certain nights, like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing. The old folk said it was the guild's great work, humming away under the rock, holding us all up." She glanced sideways at Mara. "You'd know better than me whether that's a fairy tale."

"It's not a fairy tale," Mara said.

Her wrist burned. Not the mild warmth of the past two days—this was heat, insistent, a coal laid against the pulse point, and when she pushed her sleeve back the knotwork wolf seemed darker than it had been that morning, the lines sharper, as though freshly inked.

Sorrel saw it. Of course she did.

"It's waking up," the old woman said softly. "Isn't it. The mark. It knows where you're going."

Mara looked into the fire for a long time.

"Sorrel. When your mother heard the mountain humming. Did she ever say what happened on those nights? In the villages. Whether anything... changed."

The old woman was quiet. When she answered, her voice had dropped, gone careful, the voice people use indoors during a storm.

"She said the humming nights were the good nights and the bad nights both. Good, because the harvests came in fat those years, and babies born on humming nights rarely sickened. Bad because—" Sorrel hesitated. "Bad because of the gray children. They existed then too, my mother said. Fewer. Far fewer. But the old folk noticed that after every humming night, there'd be one or two more of them come spring. And nobody ever connected it out loud, because the guild was holy and the harvest was plenty, and who bites the hand that feeds?" She looked at Mara with terrible gentleness. "But you're connecting it now, aren't you, child. Sitting right there, I can see it happening behind your eyes."

Mara could not speak.

*The guild anchored the kingdom's fortune to its kings.* The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, wearing Aldric's voice. *And something learned to feed on the anchor.*

If the Binding had always cost—if every season of plenty had been purchased with a handful of gray-eyed children scattered through the generations like a tax paid in secret—then the thing under the mountain hadn't been corrupted recently. It had been *hungry* all along. The hollow king hadn't broken a sacred machine. He had merely taken the brakes off one that had been grinding children fine for centuries, politely, in quantities small enough to ignore.

Small enough for a guild to ignore. Small enough for a girl with a wolf on her wrist to spend twelve years not asking questions.

"Thank you," she managed at last. "For telling me."

"Somebody has to remember," Sorrel said. "That's mostly what old women are for."

* *

They left before dawn on the third day, because Mara could not bear to accept more eggs, and because the mark on her wrist had begun to ache in earnest overnight—a steady pulse now, synchronized with her heartbeat, pulling her east and upward the way a compass needle pulls, and she was afraid of what she'd agree to if she stayed longer among people who looked at her like salvation.

The village turned out to see them go, which embarrassed Oskar terribly and undid Mara completely. Neya ran alongside the cart for the first quarter mile, brown-ringed eyes bright, shouting that she was going to learn knots too, that she'd find Mara someday at the mountain, that she'd be a Sigilborn when she grew up. Mara made her a simple sleep-knot from the cart's bench, a child's pattern, nothing binding in it, nothing but comfort woven into wool—and Neya received it like a coronation.

Then the road bent, and Brant's Hollow fell behind, and it was just the mule and the cart and the gray orchards again, climbing.

Oskar drove in silence for a long while. When he finally spoke, it was without looking at her, eyes on the switchbacks ahead.

"You know the kids are going to keep going gray," he said. "All up and down this road. Whatever you slowed in that girl, there's a thousand more like her between here and the capital, and you can't knot your way through all of them."

"I know."

"And the map leads to the mountain. Where the guild's vault is. Where whatever's doing this probably lives." He scratched his jaw. "So really, the fastest way to help every kid on this road is to do what you're already doing. Go up. End it at the source. Assuming the source can be ended."

"That's the plan."

"Right." He flicked the reins unnecessarily; the mule hadn't changed pace. "Only you've got the face of a woman who just learned the church she was praying in has a basement she never knew about. So before we get much closer, you want to tell me what the old lady said that's sitting on you like a wet blanket?"

Mara watched the mountain grow ahead of them. Even at this distance she could see the snowfield below the summit, glaring white against the gray rock, and beneath the snowfield—if her mother's stories and Sorrel's mother's stories were both true—a door, and behind the door a hum.

"The guild's great work," she said slowly, "may have always run on children. A few. Here and there. Quietly. For hundreds of years." Her hands were steady in her lap; she was proud of that. "And my master built it. And I swore an oath to it when I was fourteen years old."

Ahead, the mark on her wrist flared so hard she hissed—and this time Oskar saw it too, saw the wolf-lines glow faintly through her skin like embers under cloth, pointing home.

"Well," Oskar said, after a moment. "That's not ominous at all."

The road climbed, and the mountain waited, humming.
