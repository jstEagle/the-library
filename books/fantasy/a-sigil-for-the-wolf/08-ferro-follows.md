## 8. Ferro Follows

The summons came at dawn, as the Crown's ugliest work always did.

Dain Ferro had been awake anyway—he slept badly these last years, in the manner of a man who has spent too many mornings signing death warrants and has begun to hear the names read aloud when the lamp gutters out. He was standing at his window above the Inquisitors' Quarter, watching the fog peel off the Vharen river like old skin, when the boy arrived with the black-lacquered tube and the Chancellor's seal pressed into wax the color of dried blood.

He broke the seal on the walk to the palace, because he was not a man who liked to be surprised in front of witnesses.

*By order of Chancellor Rell, acting in the king's name: the courier Corvas Ellery, found dead in the Nightmarket district, is confirmed an agent of the Sigilwright Guild. Surviving evidence indicates a "keybearer" has been identified among the guild's remnants. All resources of the Holy Office are directed to the recovery of said keybearer, alive if practicable. The matter bears directly upon the security of the realm.*

*Alive if practicable.* Four words that meant nothing and everything. Ferro had written enough of those orders himself to know how they were read by the men who carried them out.

He looked up from the letter and saw the palace for what it always became at this hour—a pale bulk rising out of the mist, its towers dissolving halfway up into cloud, so that it seemed to float unmoored above the city, a ship whose anchor rope had rotted through. He had served the Crown for nineteen years. He had never once, until recently, thought of anchors.

The audience chamber smelled of lilies.

That was new. Someone—Rell, presumably, or one of his people—had decided that cut flowers would soften the room's usual atmosphere of cold stone and colder politics, and so now braziers of white lilies stood at intervals along the walls, their sweetness thick enough to taste. Ferro found it worse than the stone. Sweetness laid over rot only advertised the rot beneath.

Chancellor Rell stood at the far window with his back to the door, hands folded behind him, a spare gray figure against the gray morning. He did not turn when Ferro entered. He let the silence stretch, which was a technique, and Ferro knew it was a technique, and stood there anyway letting himself be worked upon, because there was no percentage in refusing.

"You've read the letter," Rell said at last.

"I have."

"The courier is dead. Poisoned his own veins rather than be taken—that's the guild way, they train them to it young." Rell turned then. He was not an imposing man; he had never been. He was narrow and dry and precise, with the complexion of paper kept too long in a drawer, and eyes the color of iron filings. His power lay entirely in the fact that he never blinked first. "But before he died, he passed something along. A map, we believe. And he spoke a word—*keybearer*—to a shopkeeper in the Nightmarket. A woman. Charm-seller. Almeida."

Ferro kept his face still. He had interviewed Mara Almeida two days ago, in her little shop that smelled of beeswax and river mud, and had walked away telling himself she was exactly what she appeared: a tired woman selling luck-knots to gullible dockworkers, guilty of nothing but a certain watchfulness behind the eyes. Watchfulness was not a crime in Vharen. If it were, half the city would hang.

"You interviewed her," Rell said. It was not a question; the Chancellor received copies of every field report within a day, often sooner.

"I did. She was cooperative. Grieving, frightened. She'd hidden the body herself rather than raise an alarm—foolish, but human. I found no evidence she was anything more than a bystander."

"And yet the dying man chose *her* shop. Out of every doorway in the Nightmarket." Rell came away from the window, moving with that dry economy of his, and stopped an arm's length distant. Up close there was something wrong with his smell—not unpleasant, just faintly off, like air from a cellar. The lilies couldn't quite cover it. "Coincidence is a luxury, Inquisitor. It is also frequently a disguise. She is the keybearer, or she knows who is. Find her. Bring her to me."

"Alive if practicable," Ferro said.

Rell smiled. It reached his mouth and stopped there, going no further north. "Alive, Inquisitor. Emphatically alive. The keybearer is of use to us breathing. Dead, she is merely a martyr, and martyrs breed sympathy, and sympathy breeds sedition." He paused. "You will want to know why a single charm-seller matters enough to move the Holy Office. You will not ask, because you are disciplined, but you will wonder. So I will tell you this much: the guild left things behind when we burned it. Things buried. The keybearer is the lock to those things, and the Crown intends to hold the key. That is all you need. Is it?"

It was less than all, and both of them knew it. But Ferro had learned long ago that pressing Rell for more yielded less—the Chancellor gave information the way a miser gave alms, grudgingly and always with a hook in it. "It is, my lord."

"Then go. Take whatever resources you require. And Ferro—" Rell's voice followed him toward the door, mild as milk, and all the more chilling for it. "You have a reputation for thoroughness. Keep it. What I cannot abide is a man who finds only what it is convenient to find."

*Neither can I,* Ferro thought, and bowed, and left.

---

He took six riders and went back to the Nightmarket first, because thoroughness was the one virtue he had left that he trusted.

Almeida's shop was shuttered. Dust already furred the doorstep where two days ago luck-knots had hung in bright rows like strange fruit. He questioned the neighboring stallholders—fishwife, candle-dipper, a knife-grinder with a stutter—and got the same story three times with variations: she'd closed up sudden-like, gone without a word, owed the fishwife for two weeks' mackerel. Nobody had seen her leave. Nobody had seen which road.

"She ran," said Sergeant Brakke, when they regrouped in the lane. Brakke was a big man with a face like a dropped loaf, honest as bread. "Guilty conscience. We should put word at the gates."

"She ran," Ferro agreed slowly. "Or someone helped her run. Or she was taken." He looked down the lane, at the shutters, at the gulls squabbling over the fish-stall guts, and felt again that small cold prickle he'd felt in the shop itself—the sense of a room holding its breath around a secret. "Post the word at the gates. Description, reward. But quietly. I don't want the Nightmarket turning this into theater."

Brakke hesitated. Ferro knew that hesitation; he'd seen it on men's faces more and more these past two years, a flicker of something between deference and doubt. "Sir—if she's guild, truly guild, isn't this a burning matter? The Purge protocols?"

"The Purge protocols burned midwives, Brakke. And a man who taught letters, once, in Sallowford, because he owned a book with a knot drawn in the margin." Ferro heard his own voice come out flatter than he intended. "If she's guild, we do this properly. Evidence. Examination. Trial." Though he said *trial*, and knew, and hated that he knew, that no sigilcraft case had come to genuine trial in eleven years. They went from examination to execution with the smoothness of a blade leaving a sheath.

Brakke nodded and went off to post the notices, and Ferro stood alone in the empty lane outside the empty shop, and admitted to himself, in the privacy of his own skull, what he had been declining to admit since the interview.

Something was wrong with the courier's death.

Not the poisoning itself—that was consistent with guild training, the bitter-almond trace, the self-administered dose sewn into a collar. What was wrong was everything around it. Corvas Ellery had been a good courier; Ferro had pulled his file from the archive of the old purges and read it twice by lamplight. Twelve years in the guild's service, routes across three provinces, never taken, never compromised. Such men did not die by accident in a charm-seller's doorway. Such men died *on purpose*, at the end of a planned route, delivering a planned thing to a planned recipient. Which meant Almeida was not a bystander who happened to be chosen. She was the destination.

Which meant either she was deep guild—and had fooled him, sitting there among her trinkets with her steady tired eyes—or she was something stranger. Something the guild had trusted above its own survival.

And there was a third thing, the thing he turned over at night like a stone with something under it: the Chancellor had known about the keybearer before the courier's corpse was cold. Ferro had reported the death at midday. Rell's summons came at dawn. Between those hours, someone had extracted from the scene—or from sources elsewhere—precisely the intelligence that mattered. Either the Crown had ears faster than its own inquisitors, or the Crown had known what Corvas Ellery carried before Ellery ever reached Vharen.

He wrote none of this in his report. He wrote a clean, professional account: subject fled, likely assisted, gates posted, pursuit authorized. Then he sat looking at the page a long while, because he understood that he had just done the thing Rell accused lazy men of doing—found only what it was convenient to find. Or rather, concealed what it was dangerous to find.

Nineteen years, and his hand had finally learned to lie on its own.

---

The denial came three days later, and it was the denial, more than anything, that cracked something open in him.

Because Ferro had done one other thing, in the margins of his investigation, that he had told no one about. He had filed a routine request—an inquisitor of his rank could request access to any person or record bearing on the security of the realm, and the request was so ordinary it should have vanished into the machinery without a ripple.

He had asked to examine the king.

There was precedent, even. Twice in the old days—inquisitors had been granted brief audiences to administer the Oath of Clarity, a formality dating back two centuries, in which the sovereign affirmed before the Office that no enchantment, compulsion, or binding touched his person. It hadn't been performed in decades because it was considered insulting, a relic, a formality observed by kings who wished to demonstrate they had nothing to fear. Which was precisely why Ferro had requested it. Not because he believed the king was enchanted. Because he wanted to see what happened when the request touched whatever it was that Rell was protecting.

What happened was this: within a day, the request came back stamped. Denied. No reason given. Within another day, Ferro was summoned to a smaller room—a windowless chamber in the administrative warren, paneled in dark oak, lit by a single lamp—and received a visit from a man named Heshel Vane, whom Ferro had never met and immediately wished he never had. Vane wore the gray of the Chancellery and had the smooth, unhurried manner of someone who had never once been interrupted in his life.

"Inquisitor Ferro." Vane set a folded paper on the table between them. "Your request. It has been reviewed."

"So I'm told. May I ask by whom? The Oath of Clarity is statute law. It requires no one's review."

"Everything requires review, Inquisitor. That is what government *is*." Vane smiled without warmth. "You understand, of course, that the king's health is delicate. Audiences are restricted to essential persons. The strain of ceremony—"

"With respect," Ferro said, "the Oath takes four minutes and involves no ceremony beyond speaking. I have administered it myself, to a duke, in a hunting camp, with hounds barking through the whole of it."

Vane regarded him for a moment with an expression that might have been reassessment, or might have been the look of a man marking a ledger. "Then let me speak plainly, since you're a plain man. Your investigation of the keybearer proceeds. Your other inquiries"—he tapped the denied request once, lightly—"cease. The Office serves the Crown. The Crown does not serve the Office. When you bring me the woman Almeida, your standing will be considerable, and questions of statute will seem very small to you indeed. Good day, Inquisitor."

He left. Ferro stayed seated in the lamplit oak room for a long time afterward, listening to the building settle around him, feeling nineteen years shift inside his chest like ballast sliding in a hull.

He was not a naive man. He had known for years that the Crown was cruel in places, wasteful in others, and rotten in a few corners everyone agreed not to name. Every institution is; he had made his peace with that the way a sailor makes peace with weather. But this was different in kind. This was a wall where there should have been a corridor. A king too delicate to swear a four-minute oath. A Chancellor who moved before events instead of after them. A courier who died on schedule.

And underneath it all, faint as a smell of smoke where no fire should be, the thing he had noticed in the palace audience chamber and had refused to name: the lilies. Fresh flowers, daily, in a room the king had not entered publicly in months. Who were they for?

---

He rode out at dusk with his six riders, following the east road, because the gates had turned up nothing and a fugitive charm-seller from the Nightmarket had exactly one direction available to her: the mountains. Everyone knew the guild vaults had been in Mount Serel. Everyone had known it for twelve years, and everyone had agreed, with the same careful blindness the city brought to all its uncomfortable knowledge, that the mountain was empty now. Burned out. Sealed. Nothing left up there but stone.

Ferro was beginning to suspect that everything everyone agreed on in Vharen deserved reexamination.

They made a cold camp at the shrine of Saint Verene, nine miles east, where the road began to climb. His riders slept. Ferro sat up with the horses and a map spread across his knees, tracing the route a lone woman and whoever helped her would take—through the blighted villages, past the failing farms. The eastern districts had been sick for two years now; the reports crossed his desk regularly, crops thinning, children listless, wells tasting of iron. The agronomists blamed soil exhaustion. The physicians blamed bad water. The priests blamed God's weather. Nobody's explanation quite held, and nobody pursued the question, because pursuing questions was not, apparently, what the realm paid anyone to do anymore.

Except him. He was paid to pursue. It occurred to him, sitting there in the dark with the mountain a black shoulder against the stars, that he might be the last man in Vharen whose job description still included the truth.

Brakke stirred by the fire and joined him, offering a tin cup of something hot and terrible. For a while neither spoke. Then Brakke said, carefully, "You didn't write everything in the report. About the shop. I saw you thinking."

Ferro almost laughed. "You're wasted as a sergeant. You should be an inquisitor."

"No thank you. Inquisitors get summoned to oak rooms." Brakke looked at him sidelong, firelight catching the flat honesty of his face. "I heard about the denial. Word travels. Sir—is it true they wouldn't let you near the king?"

"It's true."

Brakke turned his cup in his hands. "My mother used to say the king was blessed. Touched by the old luck, she called it. When I was a boy my father took me to the Procession, and the king—you remember, the real processions, before—they say his shadow fell across the whole square and the crops came in heavy that autumn." He shook his head. "When did you last see him, sir? In the flesh?"

Ferro thought. He counted backward, and the counting took longer than it should have. "Three years ago. An audience. Brief." He did not tell Brakke what he remembered of it—the king's stillness, the way the man had spoken a sentence of greeting and then, some minutes later, spoken the same sentence again, word for word, inflection for inflection, as though the first utterance had been a coin spent and returned. He had filed it then as fatigue. He filed it now somewhere else entirely, in the drawer where he kept the courier's punctual death and the scent of lilies and the cellar-smell of Chancellor Rell.

"Three years," Brakke said softly. "Funny. That's about when the east started going gray."

"Funny," Ferro agreed, and did not smile, and neither did Brakke.

Far up the mountain, where the road disappeared into dark, something glimmered—briefly, faintly, there and gone. A light, perhaps. Or a reflection of starlight on wet stone. Or nothing. Ferro watched the place where it had been for a long time, his tea cooling in his hands, and understood with the calm of a man reading his own death warrant that he had already crossed the line. There was no report he could write, no oak room he could sit in, no order he could follow that would put him back on the side of the ledger where he'd spent nineteen comfortable years. He was going to find Mara Almeida. And when he found her, he realized—one certainty arriving whole, the way the truest ones do—he was not going to arrest her.

He was going to ask her what was buried under the mountain, and pray to a god he'd stopped believing in that he could bear the answer.

"Mount us at first light," he said to Brakke. "We ride for Serel."

Above them, high on the black shoulder of the mountain, the glimmer came again—and this time it did not fade.
