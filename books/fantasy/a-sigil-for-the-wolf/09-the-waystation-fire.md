## 9. The Waystation Fire

The waystation sat in the crook of the road like an old man hunched against the wind—low stone walls, a sagging roof of river slate, a stable that leaned east as if listening for something coming from that direction forever. A sign above its door had been painted and repainted so many times the letters were a bruise of colors: *The Kindly Hearth*, though no hearth showed smoke.

"We can't stop," Oskar said, not slowing the cart.

"We have to stop." Mara shaded her eyes. "Liss hasn't slept properly in three days. Neither have you."

"I sleep standing up like any respectable mule."

"Oskar."

He swore under his breath, the particular oath he used when he was losing an argument and wanted her to know it cost him. The cart creaked off the road onto gravel gone soft with autumn rain.

There were others at the waystation—a tinker's family camped beside their wagon under the leaning stable's shadow: a broad-shouldered man splitting kindling, a woman stirring a pot over a small cook-fire, and a girl of perhaps nine years sitting on the wagon's step with a stick, drawing shapes in the dirt. She looked up as they rattled in, and Mara's chest tightened at the sight of her—thin, dark-haired, with the watchful stillness of children who had learned early that being noticed could be dangerous.

Liss. Though Mara did not yet know that name. She would learn it the worst way there is.

The woman by the pot smiled at them, easy and unafraid. That was what undid Mara later, when she lay sleepless replaying it: how unafraid everyone had been. How ordinary the evening began.

* * *

They ate in the waystation's common room, which was really one room with a cold fireplace and two tables worn smooth by generations of travelers' elbows. The tinker—Brannick, he said his name was—shared their salt and told stories about the southern roads, where the tolls had doubled since the king's "renewed vigor" and travelers spoke of gray-cloaked riders who asked questions no traveler wanted to answer.

"Inquisitors?" Oskar asked carefully.

"Outriders. They ride ahead of the circuits now. Sweeping." Brannick shrugged the shrug of a man who had decided some things were weather—you couldn't fight them, only dress for them. His wife, Sela, pressed a heel of barley bread into Liss's hands without asking, and Liss held it like it might be taken back.

Mara watched the girl eat. There was flour dust in the child's hair and a scab on her knee going yellow with age, and she ate the way small animals do—fast, eyes up between bites—and something in Mara's ribs ached in a place she didn't permit herself to visit. She thought of her own guild's youngest years, of eating fast in the Vault's antechambers because Aldric said hunger dulled the hand that drew sigils, and she pushed the memory down before it could bloom.

"You've a kind face," Sela said to her, gathering bowls. "You and your man traveling far?"

"He's not—" Mara began, and gave up, because correcting it took longer than letting it stand. "North. To family."

"Family's worth the road." Sela glanced at her daughter. "Most days."

Outside, the light was failing the way mountain light fails—not gradually but all at once, dusk dropping over the valley like a lid. Mara stepped out to see to the horse and stopped.

On the eastern ridge, thin as scratches on slate: riders. Four of them, maybe five, moving without lanterns.

Her skin knew before her mind did. Twelve years of hiding had built a second nervous system, one that lived in her spine and spoke directly to her hands. She went back inside walking slowly, unhurried, and put her mouth near Oskar's ear.

"Riders on the ridge. No lights."

He didn't turn his head. He reached for his cup, drank, and set it down with a small click. "Outriders?"

"Maybe."

"And if maybe?"

"Then we're travelers sharing bread with a tinker's family, and I'm a charm-seller with nothing in my pack but knots and dried lavender."

Oskar looked at her then, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but a kind of grim readiness, as if some part of him had been waiting twelve years too. "And if it's not maybe," he said quietly.

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. Outside, the first hoofbeats sounded on the gravel.

* * *

They came through the door without knocking, four of them, and the fifth stayed mounted outside with the horses stamping in the cold. Their cloaks were the gray of old ash, their badges the crowned eye of the Crown's inquiry, and the leader was young—younger than Mara expected—with a voice like a clerk reading a list.

"All persons to present papers and state business."

Brannick stood, wiping his hands on his apron, easy and unafraid even now. "Tinker's circuit, south loop. Papers are in the wagon—"

"The wagon will be searched. All wagons will be searched." The young inquisitor's eyes moved across them: Oskar, big and scarred and trying to look bored; Mara with her charm-seller's pack; Liss frozen mid-bite; Sela with her arms full of dishes. "Names."

They gave names. Oskar used a false one so smoothly Mara almost admired it. When the leader came to her, she produced her market license—the real one, issued under her real registered name, because the best lies are built on load-bearing truth.

"Mara Almeida. Charm-seller, Nightmarket, Vharen."

"Luck-knots." He said it with the faint contempt the Crown trained into its people, the sneer that made ordinary citizens proud to despise what they'd been taught to fear. He nodded to one of his men. "Search the packs."

Here is what Mara had learned in twelve years: panic is loud, but craft is quiet. Her fingers were already moving beneath the table's edge, out of sight, working a knot she'd tied ten thousand times in the dark of her shop—a simple dampening weave, nothing that would register as sigilcraft even if an inquisitor stared straight at it, just thread and intent and the smallest borrowing of luck, enough to make searching hands careless.

The soldier dug through her pack and came up with lavender, thread, wooden beads, a child's rattle-charm. He frowned at it all like it had personally disappointed him.

"She's clean," he said.

But the leader wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking at Liss.

"You. Girl. Come here."

Sela's arms stopped moving. "She's a child—"

"A child of Vharen, subject to inspection like anyone." He snapped his fingers. "Come here."

And Liss—nine years old, brave in the way only frightened people are brave—slid off the bench and started toward him, and Mara saw the girl's hands shaking and saw something else besides: the faintest shimmer around the child's fingers, a heat-haze distortion, as if the air itself flinched away from her.

No. Not flinched. *Gathered.*

Mara's blood went cold. The girl had the gift. Untrained, raw, leaking like a cracked jug—but there, unmistakable, the same pressure she'd felt in the Vault as a child, the same hum in the teeth. And if Mara could feel it across a table, an inquisitor trained to sense resonance would feel it like a shout.

"Don't," Mara said, before she could stop herself.

The leader turned. "Don't what?"

"Don't frighten her. She'll cry, and then none of us eat tonight." She smiled the smile she sold charms behind, warm and stupid and harmless. "You know how children are."

Something flickered in the young inquisitor's face—something almost human, almost tired—and Mara thought, absurdly, *he doesn't want to be here either,* and then the moment broke, because the soldier searching Brannick's wagon called out:

"Sir! Under the floorboards!"

* * *

What he pulled out was nothing. A bundle of tinker's templates, copper wire, a soldering iron—perfectly lawful, perfectly dull. But the shout had been made, and shouts are their own justification. The soldiers tore the wagon apart with sudden enthusiasm, and Brannick's easy calm finally cracked.

"Those are my tools! I have a license, I have—"

"Hold him."

They held him. He fought, because any man would, and fighting a soldier of the Crown is a confession regardless of innocence, and the world tilted very fast from inspection to arrest. Sela was screaming now. Liss had backed against the wall, and the air around her small hands was rippling like water, and the young inquisitor had gone very still, staring, his hand drifting toward the iron rod at his belt—the resonator, the tool Mara had spent twelve years learning to dread.

"There's something—" he started.

Mara moved.

Later she could never reconstruct the decision. It simply happened, the way a struck bell happens. Twelve years of careful invisibility, of harmless trinkets and buried craft, and she crossed that room in three strides and put herself between the inquisitor and the child, and her hands were already moving, weaving, drawing on stores she hadn't touched in public since she was nineteen years old.

"Almeida," Oskar breathed behind her, half warning, half awe.

"Step back from the girl." Her voice didn't sound like hers. It sounded like the guild's voice, like every voice that had ever sworn an oath in the dark below Mount Serel. "Whatever you think she is, she's a child."

"Sigilcraft." The young inquisitor's face had drained. "In the open. You're under arrest—all of you—the woman especially—"

The world chose that moment to end.

It happened fast, the way catastrophes do—one moment a room, the next a riot. Oskar moved like a landslide, taking two soldiers into the wall. Brannick wrenched free and got an elbow into someone's throat. Someone knocked the cook-pot off its hook in the yard, and burning oil spread across the packed earth, and the wind, which had been waiting politely all evening, picked up.

The fire caught the wagon's canvas first. Then the wheel grease. Then everything.

* * *

Mara remembered the rest in fragments, the way fires are always remembered: in light and noise and single images burned brighter than the whole.

The resonator's white shriek as the young inquisitor triggered it and her own counter-weave meeting it, the two forces grinding against each other like millstones with her bones between them.

Oskar's roar, wordless, as he threw a man through the doorway.

Brannick, on his knees in the yard, screaming a name—his wife's name—into smoke.

And Liss, alone in the burning common room, because in the chaos of adults breaking the world apart, no one had been watching the child, and the child had run for the back room where her mother's shawl was, because children do not understand that things lost to fire cannot be fetched.

Mara understood it. She understood it with perfect, terrible clarity, and she went in anyway.

Heat like a fist. Smoke that tasted of tar and wool and something sweeter underneath, the smell of a home ending. The common room was an orange roar, the ceiling breathing fire, and somewhere in the black a small voice crying—a sound Mara felt in her teeth, in her spine, in whatever animal place lives below thought.

"Liss!" She didn't know where the name came from either. Perhaps the girl had screamed it. "LISS!"

A shape in the corner, curled small behind a toppled bench. Mara grabbed her, and the girl fought her, wild, screaming for her mother, and Mara did the thing she would carry the rest of her life: she lied.

"She's outside. Your mother's outside. She's waiting for you—we have to go *now.*"

The lie worked because lies built of love work best. Liss went limp enough to be carried, and Mara ran, shielding the girl's face with her own shoulder, feeling the fire take the sleeve of her coat, her hair, feeling the doorframe ahead swim through smoke—

—and then they were through, and the night air hit like a blessing, and they rolled together on the cold gravel as the roof came down behind them in a geyser of sparks.

* * *

Sela did not make it out.

Neither did Brannick, though Mara learned later he had nearly reached the door. The soldiers had scattered when the fire spread—the young inquisitor shouting orders no one followed, the wind driving flames toward the dry hillside—and by the time anyone could move again, the waystation was a skeleton of black stone and the family's wagon was a pyre, and Sela and Brannick were inside both.

Oskar found Mara sitting in the gravel with the girl wrapped in her coat, both of them streaked with ash. He crouched down. For once he had nothing sharp to say.

"They ran," Mara said. Her voice came out flat, strange. "The soldiers ran, and the fire took them, and I told her—" She stopped. Started again. "I told her mother was outside. She believed me. She walked out of that fire believing me."

"That lie kept her moving."

"It's the first thing I ever gave her." Mara looked down at the child asleep—or unconscious—against her chest, fists clenched even in sleep, ash in her dark hair like premature snow. "A lie. And now she's mine."

Oskar was quiet a long time. Overhead the sky had cleared, indifferent and enormous, stars in their cold arrangements.

"You could give her the truth someday," he said finally. "When she's older. When it won't break her."

"And until then?"

"Until then you remember it for both of you." He stood, joints cracking, and offered his hand. "Come on. We can't stay here. Riders or no riders, this beacon's visible from the next valley, and someone always comes to look at fires."

Mara let him pull her up. The girl's weight shifted in her arms, trusting even in sleep, and Mara thought about weight—about how she had spent twelve years making sure she weighed nothing in anyone's life, carried nothing, owed nothing, was owed nothing. Invisible. Safe. Dying quietly by inches in a shop full of harmless knots.

Now she was carrying a child out of a fire, and her hands were burned, and her secret was spilled across a hillside in front of Crown soldiers, and there was no invisible left to return to.

The past hadn't just found her. It had handed her a future with a heartbeat.

* * *

They walked through the dark for an hour, following the stream down from the waystation because Oskar said water meant people eventually, and people meant a road, and roads meant choices. The girl woke once, disoriented, and began to cry for her mother, and Mara knelt in the wet grass and held her and said nothing true and nothing false, only: "I have you. Whatever else, I have you." And the girl—Liss, she finally learned, whispered into Mara's shoulder like a confession—clung to her with the desperate strength of the newly orphaned.

Behind them, far off, the glow of the waystation still stained the clouds.

Ahead, north, the mountains waited—Mount Serel among them, wearing its snow like a crown, and somewhere beneath it the Hollow Vault, and somewhere beneath that the answers she had been walking toward since a dying man bled out on her shop floor.

Mara shifted Liss higher on her hip and kept walking.

She did not see, in the darkness behind them, the young inquisitor kneeling in the ashes of The Kindly Hearth—did not see him lift a scrap of scorched cloth on the point of his knife and study it in the firelight, nor hear him say quietly to his surviving men:

"Send word to Chancellor Rell. We found the keybearer."

But the words were already moving, faster than any cart, faster than any horse, north along the roads she would have to travel—riding ahead of her, as outriders always do.
