## 10. Three at the Table

The fire was Oskar's idea, and he resented it already.

A fire meant smoke, and smoke meant visibility, and visibility meant inquisitor outriders finding three travelers huddled in a hollow of pines off the old salt road like amateurs who'd never run cargo through a checkpoint in their lives. But Liss hadn't stopped shivering since sundown—not the ordinary shiver of a child gone cold, but the deep, stuttering tremor of a girl whose parents had burned alive in a wagon two nights ago while she watched from Mara's arms—and no amount of dry blanket or gruff reassurance had put a dent in it.

So: fire. Small, ringed with stones, tucked into a fold of earth where the light couldn't climb higher than knee-height. Oskar told himself it was practical. He told himself a lot of things these days, and fewer and fewer of them were true.

He sat with his back against a pine trunk, boots crossed at the ankle, cleaning a knife he didn't need to clean. It was something to do with his hands that wasn't watching them—the woman and the girl sitting close together on the far side of the flames, their faces lit orange and gold, shadows pooling behind them like an audience.

Mara Almeida had a length of cord in her fingers. Plain hemp, the kind Oskar used for lashing crates, worth maybe half a copper. She held it up between them so the firelight caught it, and Liss watched it the way children watch things they're afraid to hope for.

"Now," Mara said, "watch my hands. Not the knot. The hands."

"I'm watching the knot."

"You're watching the knot." There was a smile in her voice, tired but real. "Everyone watches the knot first. The knot is just where the hands end up."

Oskar's knife stilled on the whetstone.

He'd seen her do this before—once, years ago, in the back room of her shop, when his sister's fever wouldn't break and the physicians' tinctures did nothing but make her vomit. He'd watched Mara's fingers move over Sefa's wrist, quick and strange, tying nothing, untying nothing, just *moving*, and he'd told himself then what he told himself now: it was theater. Comfort-work. A trick to calm the sick and separate the desperate from their coin.

Except Sefa's fever broke by morning. And except that on the weir gate, four days ago, a guard had grabbed Oskar's collar and Mara had flicked two fingers and the man had stood there blinking at nothing while they walked past him like ghosts, and there was no theater in the world that did *that*.

And now here she was, teaching it. To a nine-year-old. Out loud. In the open air. Twelve years of hiding, twelve years of harmless trinkets and luck-knots sold to superstitious fishermen, and she was crouched by a fire teaching forbidden craft to a child who'd lost everything, as if the inquisitors were a story and the Purges a fable grandmothers told.

Either grief had broken her, or something else had changed. Oskar wasn't sure which worried him more.

"Loop it under," Mara was saying, guiding Liss's small fingers. "Under, not over. Over is how you bind. Under is how you hold. We only ever hold."

"Why?"

"Because binding takes. Holding gives." Mara folded the girl's fingers closed over the loop. "Feel that? That little give in the cord? That's the whole craft. Everything else is just manners."

Liss frowned at the half-formed knot in her fists. Her hair was still singed at the ends—Oskar had cut away the worst of it himself with a fish-gutting knife, hating every second, hating how small her head was under his hand. "My mother tied knots," she said suddenly. "In my hair. In the mornings. She said—" Her voice caught, that awful hitch of a child remembering mid-sentence that remembering hurts. "She said hers were just for pretty."

"They can be both," Mara said quietly. "Pretty and useful. Most good things are."

"My father said magic was for witches."

Oskar's jaw tightened. He looked at the fire instead of at them.

"He wasn't wrong to be careful," Mara said. "There are people who use the craft badly. Who take instead of hold. Being afraid of that is smart." She turned the cord gently in Liss's grip. "But fear isn't the same as knowing. Your father was afraid because he'd never been shown. I'm going to show you. And then you get to decide what you think. That's more than most people ever get."

Liss considered this with the grave, total seriousness of childhood, the way other children weighed the merits of sweets or fairness. Then: "Will it help me sleep?"

"It might."

"The dreams are bad."

"I know."

"The wagon's burning but nobody's screaming. That's the worst part. Nobody screams. They just—" She stopped. Her knuckles went white around the cord.

Mara's hands came over hers, steady, warm-looking even across the fire. "That's why we tie it slow," she said. "Slow knots hold best. Fast ones slip. So we go slow, and we think about nothing but the cord, and the cord holds us while we rest. Can you do that? Just the cord?"

Liss nodded. Her chin wobbled once and steadied.

And Oskar looked away, because there are things a smuggler should not witness, and this was one of them: a woman who owed him nothing and a child who owed them both everything, building something fragile out of hemp and firelight in a country that would burn all three of them if it knew.

* * *

He made himself useful instead. Useful was safe. Useful was a language he spoke fluently—cargo manifests and tide tables and the exact weight of a bribe versus the exact weight of a blade. He dug out the travel bread and the hard cheese, cut slices with the knife he'd been pretending to sharpen, laid them on a cloth within reach without interrupting. He refilled the water skin. He checked the horse lines, though he'd checked them ten minutes ago. He counted their supplies twice and got the same answer both times and found no comfort in it whatsoever.

Three days out of Vharen. Two days to the mountain passes, if the weather held, and the weather hadn't held since the reign of the last honest king, whenever that was. Behind them: an inquisitor who'd looked Mara in the eye and let her lie to him, which was either mercy or a trap, and Oskar had survived thirty years in the salt trade by assuming trap. Ahead of them: Mount Serel, a map drawn in a dead man's blood, and whatever waited in a vault beneath the rock that was worth killing couriers over.

And him. Oskar Vey, smuggler, debtor, fool, who had agreed to haul a fugitive sigilwright and an orphaned child toward the one place in Vharen where the Crown's killers would be thickest, because a woman had saved his sister's life and he'd never figured out how to stop paying for it.

He sat back down. The fire had settled into its long-burning phase, coals glowing under low flame, throwing heat instead of light. Good. Coals didn't show from the road.

"—and then you tuck it through itself," Mara was saying. "Like tucking a child into bed. Gently. It has to want to stay."

"That's silly."

"That's true. Cord has opinions. Ask any sailor."

"Did you learn from sailors?"

"I learned from someone better than sailors." A pause, brief as a skipped heartbeat. "Someone I'll tell you about someday, maybe. When you're older."

"When I'm older," Liss repeated, with the flat skepticism of a child who has heard that promise before and knows it means *never*. But she bent back over her knot, tongue between her teeth, working the loop through with clumsy, determined fingers.

Oskar chewed his bread and watched the fire and tried very hard not to feel what he was feeling.

It kept creeping up on him anyway. That was the trouble with fires, with shared meals, with the small domestic machinery of a camp—someone cutting cheese, someone braiding cord, someone's cloak draped over someone's shoulders. It built things without asking permission. He'd spent his whole adult life keeping his arrangements temporary: cargoes, crews, harbors, women, all of it entered into with one eye on the exit. A man in his trade who forgot he was leaving was a man who ended up in a cage or a shallow grave. Counting the days until departure wasn't cynicism. It was arithmetic. Survival arithmetic.

He'd started counting the day they'd left Vharen. Passage to Serel and back, he'd told himself. Deliver the woman to her mountain, collect his debt cleared in full, sail home to Sefa and the *Kestrel* and a life that made sense.

Somewhere in the last three days—he couldn't say exactly when; somewhere between the checkpoint and the ambush and the moment he'd cut burnt hair from a sobbing child's head—he'd stopped counting.

He noticed it now the way you notice a sound stopping: the absence of a thing you'd lived with so long you'd mistaken it for silence. No tally in his head. No exit scouted. No mental ledger of days remaining. Just this: fire, bread, the smell of pine resin, a girl's voice asking questions and a woman answering each one like it mattered.

*You're getting soft,* he told himself. *Vey, you sentimental wreck of a salt-rat.*

He cut another slice of cheese and passed it over without being asked, and hated how easily his hand knew the motion already, three days in, like he'd been doing it for years.

* * *

Liss got the knot right on her ninth try.

Oskar knew because he'd been counting that, at least—counting her failures, each one meted out with a patience that surprised him coming from her and surprised him more coming from himself, because he'd caught his own breath catching on tries six and seven, willing the cord to behave for her, and what business was that of his?

"There!" Mara said softly.

Liss held it up. A small, ugly, lopsided thing—a loop with three crossings and a tail hanging crooked, nothing like the neat examples Mara had tied and untied. But it held. When Liss tugged it, it tightened. When she loosened her grip, it stayed.

"It's crooked," Liss said.

"It's yours," Mara said. "Crooked is fine. Crooked is honest. Now—here's the part that matters." She took the girl's hands again, knot and all, and lowered them together until they rested in Liss's lap. "Close your eyes. Breathe slow. And say it with me: *hold, don't take.*"

"*Hold, don't take.*"

"Again."

"*Hold, don't take.*"

"Now keep saying it inside your head, quiet as you can, and let the knot do the rest. If your mind wanders to the bad dream, come back to the cord. Feel it in your fingers. That's your anchor. Nothing gets past an anchor unless you invite it."

Liss's eyes closed. Her face, which had been gray and pinched for two days, went—slowly, by degrees, like a tide going out—something closer to young. Her breathing lengthened. Her shoulders dropped from around her ears.

Within five minutes she was asleep, sitting up, hands still curled around the crooked knot, and Mara eased her down onto the bedroll with a tenderness so practiced it hurt to watch, tucking the blanket to the girl's chin exactly the way, Oskar thought, someone must once have tucked Mara, or wished someone had.

He didn't say anything. Neither did she, for a while. The fire ticked. Somewhere upslope an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"She's gifted," Mara said finally, quietly, settling back beside the sleeping girl. "You saw how fast she took to it. Nine tries. I was eleven before I managed a sleep-knot, and I was raised in it."

"So don't teach her," Oskar said. It came out harder than he intended. "Almeida. Listen to me. You spent twelve years hiding this craft from the whole rotting kingdom, and four days after leaving your shop you're handing it to a child by firelight. If outriders find us—if Ferro finds us—that knot is a death sentence for her, not just you. Children don't hang for it. They take them. You know they take them."

"I know what they take." Her voice didn't rise. That was the unnerving thing about Mara Almeida; she never rose. She just got quieter and heavier, like deep water. "I was almost taken once myself, Oskar. I remember the room they'd have put me in." She looked at Liss's sleeping face. "And I look at her, and I think: she can spend her whole life afraid of a thing she carries anyway, jumping at every boot on cobblestone, hiding every gift she has until the hiding eats her hollow. Or she can spend it knowing what her hands are for." She turned to look at him across the coals. "I hid. Look how well that served me. Look how well it served anyone."

"And what does it serve, teaching her? Where does it end? She ties sleep-knots today and next year she's—"

"Next year she's whatever she chooses. That's the point. That's the entire point." Mara picked up another length of cord from the supply roll, began winding it slowly around her fingers, an unconscious habit, a fidget that was also, he suspected, a prayer. "The guild used to say knowledge was a debt. Every craft you're given, you owe forward. I've been in arrears for twelve years, Oskar. I'm done."

He wanted to argue. He had arguments ready—good ones, sharp ones, the kind that won dockside disputes. But the words wouldn't come, because the girl slept peacefully for the first time in two days, and the woman wound her cord like a monk counting beads, and the fire held its small circle of warmth against a cold dark country, and every argument he owned suddenly seemed like something shouted into wind.

"You could've just given her poppy syrup," he said instead. "Cheaper. Legal."

Mara laughed—a short, real laugh, surprised out of her. "Where's the poetry in that?"

"There isn't any. That's why I suggested it."

"You would." She shook her head, still smiling faintly, and for a moment she looked younger than her years and older than her sorrows, both at once, and Oskar looked away first.

* * *

Later—much later, when the coals had gone gray-rimmed and Mara had taken first watch and Liss breathed slow and even under her blanket—Oskar lay on his own bedroll staring up at the black lattice of pine branches and did his accounting, because accounting was the one prayer he knew.

The *Kestrel* sat at anchor in Vharen harbor with a skeleton crew and a hold full of nothing, and every day she sat there cost him money he didn't have. His sister expected him home within the month; he'd promised, and Oskar Vey kept promises to exactly two people in the world, and Sefa was one of them. The salt contracts for the autumn run wouldn't wait. The harbor master's patience certainly wouldn't. By any sane ledger, he should have turned back three days ago. Should have set them down at the crossroads shrine with a purse and a blessing and washed his hands of blood-maps and mountain vaults and the whole doomed, luminous madness of it.

He ran the numbers one more time, out of habit, out of discipline, out of some stubborn loyalty to the man he'd been a month ago.

They didn't balance. They hadn't balanced since the waystation fire, when he'd pulled Liss from under a burning wheel and she'd gripped his sleeve with both hands and called him by name—his actual name, not the three aliases he traveled under—as if he were someone worth knowing the name of. Since Mara had knelt in the road ash and held the girl and cried without making a single sound, and he'd understood, watching her, that he would follow this woman into the mountain itself if she asked, and that the debt to her had stopped being a debt somewhere along the way and become something with no ledger entry, no interest rate, no terms.

Belonging, sneaking up on him like a thief in his own house. He'd have respected it more if he weren't the mark.

He rolled onto his side. Across the dead fire, Mara sat her watch with her back straight, cord wound and unwinding around her fingers, face turned toward the dark road below. Keeping them safe. Both of them. Him included, apparently, whether he liked it or not.

*Vey,* he told himself, *you are in serious trouble.*

From the darkness down the slope, where the trees gave way to the old salt road, a light appeared.

One lantern, swinging. Then a second. Then the low murmur of many voices, and the unmistakable metallic rhythm of shod horses on packed earth—a column of riders, moving north along the road toward the pass, moving toward *them*, close enough that Oskar could hear, threading under the hoofbeats, a sound that turned his blood to harbor ice:

Singing. Low and tuneless and perfectly synchronized, a dozen throats in unison, a marching cadence with no words he recognized—no words at all, just tone, rising and falling like breathing.

Mara was already on her feet, hand clamped over Liss's mouth before the girl could wake crying, her eyes huge and fixed on the road, and Oskar saw her lips shape a word he'd hoped never to hear her say:

*"Gray guards."*
