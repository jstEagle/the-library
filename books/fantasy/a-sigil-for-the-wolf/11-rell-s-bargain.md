## 11. Rell's Bargain

Chancellor Rell had not slept a full night in twelve years.

He did not count this as suffering. Suffering was for men who still believed the world owed them rest. Rell had surrendered that belief on a winter night in the palace's upper galleries, when he had stood before the king of Vharen and watched him fail to blink for four hundred seconds by candlelight. He remembered the exact number because he had counted it the way a drowning man counts waves. After that night, sleep became a country he visited briefly and without trust, like an ally whose loyalty he could no longer verify.

Now, at sixty-one, he rose before the bells, dressed himself without assistance—a habit from leaner years that scandalized his valet—and stood at the window of his study watching the city wake below the Palace Hill. Vharen in the early light looked almost innocent: slate roofs going gold at their edges, harbor smoke rising straight as incense, the Nightmarket already unrolling its awnings like a flower deciding to trust the sun.

It was a beautiful city. That was the whole tragedy of it. If Vharen had been ugly, if it had been cruel or squalid, what Rell did every day would have been mere governance, and no one—not even Rell—would have needed to call it anything else. But Vharen was lovely, and lovely things required lies to stay lovely, and so the Chancellor of Vharen began each morning by looking at his beautiful, doomed city and renewing his vows to it the way other men renewed vows to wives.

On his desk lay the morning's reports, arranged by urgency. The harvest projections from the eastern valleys, down eleven percent and falling. The physician-general's census of gray-eyed children—two hundred and six now, up from one hundred and ninety the previous month, all listless, all fading, none dying yet. Yet. The word *yet* had been migrating through Rell's correspondence for two years, attaching itself to more and more sentences like a parasite finding new hosts.

And beneath those, sealed with black wax, the report he read first and always alone: the daily accounting of the king's condition.

*His Majesty took breakfast. His Majesty received the ambassador from Kessrin and repeated the formula of welcome three times. His Majesty wept between the second and third repetition. The tears were clear, then dark. Attendants changed the tablecloth.*

Rell set the paper down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations.

Dark tears. That was new. That was very bad.

The thing that wore the crown of Vharen—it had never had a name Rell could bring himself to use aloud, though in the privacy of his own skull he called it *the king*, because the alternative names were worse—had been unraveling slowly for years, the way a rope unravels: imperceptibly at first, then all at once in a single catastrophic week. It ate. It sat council. It signed what was put before it, provided the documents were worded simply enough. Once, it had even ridden through the city at midwinter and waved, and the crowds had cheered, because people see what they have decided to see, and no one decides harder than a crowd that loves its king.

But lately it spoke in voices that were not its own. Dead voices, the attendants said—old courtiers, a woman singing, once, horribly, a child counting. And it wept ink. And the Binding that sustained it was starving faster than any projection Rell's secret clerks could chart.

The Binding. There it was, then. Twelve years ago, a dying guildmaster had come to Rell in the rain with a proposition instead of a confession, and Rell—young then, newly chancellor, already tired—had listened. The guild, the man said, had anchored the kingdom's fortune to its royal line for nine generations. Luck as infrastructure. Rain where rain was needed, harvests that bent but never broke, plagues that passed Vharen by while they gutted her neighbors. All of it drawn from the Vault beneath Mount Serel, all of it fed through the blood of kings.

And the Purges—the great righteous fire the old king's zealots had lit to burn sigilcraft out of the world—had severed the guild from its Vault and left the anchor spinning loose, attached to nothing, feeding nothing, devouring everything.

The old king died within a year. Not assassinated. Simply *emptied*, the way a house is emptied when the family leaves. What remained had kept walking, kept eating, kept signing, and Rell had made his choice in the space of one long sleepless winter: the kingdom would keep its king, whatever the king had become, because the alternative was a succession crisis wrapped inside a famine wrapped inside a religious panic, and Vharen would eat itself alive before spring.

Order, Rell had told himself then, is not the absence of rot. Order is the discipline of containing rot.

He had repeated that sentence to himself for twelve years, and it had grown thinner each time, like a coin rubbed smooth by handling, until now it was barely a shape at all.

* * *

At the ninth bell, Inquisitor Dain Ferro was shown into the study.

Rell liked Ferro, which was inconvenient. He had cultivated liking few men over the decades precisely because liking complicated decisions, and Rell's life was decisions. But Ferro had something the other inquisitors lacked—an actual conscience, worn openly, unarmored, like a man carrying a lantern through a room full of valuables. Most men's consciences were hidden things they negotiated with in the dark. Ferro's hung out in daylight where everyone could see it, and somehow this made him both easier to manipulate and harder to respect.

"You wanted me, Chancellor." Ferro stood rather than sat—he rarely accepted seats, a habit Rell had catalogued years ago as either humility or suspicion—and his eyes went, inevitably, to the window, to the city below, the way they always did. A man who looked at the city while being addressed by power. Either sentimentality or surveillance. With Ferro it might be both.

"I did. Sit, Dain. This will take longer than your patience usually allows."

Ferro sat. Rell poured wine himself—another habit, another performance, though by now the performances and the habits had fused beyond separating—and studied the inquisitor's face. Weathered. Honest-looking, which was dangerous in a servant. There were new lines around his eyes since the courier business in the Nightmarket.

"The courier," Rell began. "Tomas Weir, licensed runner, thirty-four years old. You closed the case."

"Stab wound. Robbery, most likely. The Nightmarket eats strangers."

"And the map he carried?"

Ferro's face did something small and controlled. "No map was recovered."

"No." Rell sipped his wine. "Of course not. And the charm-seller he died in front of—Mara Almeida, knots and trinkets, harmless little woman—she reported nothing unusual."

"She reported the death. She was shaken."

"Were you convinced?"

"I was convinced there wasn't enough evidence to hold her." Ferro's voice was level, but Rell heard the seam in it. "Chancellor, if there's a question I can answer, ask it plainly. I'm too old and too tired for the winding approach."

There. That was Ferro exactly—conscience worn in daylight, impatience worn beside it. Rell set down his glass and leaned back, and let a silence stretch just long enough to make the younger man uncomfortable, because discomfort loosened tongues faster than wine.

"What do you know about the Purges?" Rell asked.

Ferro blinked. "That they were necessary. That sigilcraft corrupted everyone who touched it. That the guilds bound luck and health and grief into knots and sold them, and the price was always paid by someone who hadn't agreed to pay it. Every inquisitor learns the history."

"Do you believe it?"

Another silence. Ferro chose his words like a man crossing ice. "I believe some of it. I've seen what binding-magic does. I also—" he stopped.

"Say it."

"I also think necessity is the favorite word of every cruelty I've ever investigated." Ferro met his eyes. "You asked plainly. That's my answer."

Rell smiled, and knew it was the smile he used for ambassadors, and hated himself mildly for using it here. "Good. That's exactly why I'm promoting you."

Something flickered across Ferro's face—the instinctive wariness of a man being handed a gift by someone who wants something. "Promoting me."

"First Claw of the Inquisition. My direct authority, reporting only to me and the Crown." Rell let that land. "You'll have forty investigators, access to the archive vaults, jurisdiction in every province. And one primary commission."

"Which is?"

"A courier died carrying something toward Mount Serel. A woman who may or may not be involved has left the city with a smuggler named Oskar Vey and, we believe, a child. Somewhere on those roads is an object—or a person—that certain parties want found before anyone else finds them." Rell folded his hands. "Find them first, Dain. Bring me what the courier was carrying. Do that, and the Inquisition becomes yours to reform however your conscience dictates. Purge the corrupt ones. Rewrite the procedures. I'll sign anything you put in front of me."

For a moment—just a moment—Rell saw the hook sink. He saw it in the way Ferro's breath changed, in the slight forward lean. Reform. Every honest man in a rotten institution dreamed of reform the way prisoners dreamed of open windows, and Rell was offering him the window along with the wall to climb through it.

"And if the woman's innocent?" Ferro asked quietly.

"Then you'll determine that and act accordingly. You'll have authority I never had at your age, and I expect you to use it better." Rell spread his hands, the picture of candor. "I am not asking you to do anything dishonorable, Dain. I'm asking you to do something difficult, quickly, before worse men do it slowly."

It was, Rell reflected, not even a lie. It was merely incomplete, and incompleteness was the mortar of all statecraft.

Ferro was quiet for a long time. Through the window behind him, the city glittered in its morning lie, and Rell watched the inquisitor look at it, really look, the way a physician looks at a patient he suspects is dying.

"When do I leave?" Ferro said at last.

"Tomorrow. Provisions tonight. And Dain—" Rell rose, came around the desk, and gripped the man's shoulder, feeling the tension in it, the coiled unwillingness. "One more thing. While you're on the road, you may receive requests from the palace. From the Gray Guard, specifically. They answer to the Crown directly, above my authority. If they instruct you to break off pursuit—to return to the capital, to stand down—you will comply."

Ferro turned. "The Gray Guard doesn't give field orders. They're ceremonial."

"They are whatever the Crown requires them to be." Rell held his gaze, steady, paternal, everything a mentor should be. "Promise me."

"...If they countermand me, I'll comply. Within reason." Ferro hesitated. "May I ask one thing in return? Unrelated to the commission."

"Ask."

"Last year I petitioned for a routine audience. An oath renewal before His Majesty—it's customary for senior inquisitors, and I'd never had mine. The request was denied twice, then deferred indefinitely. I only want to know why."

The room went very still. Outside, a gull cried over the harbor, absurd and ordinary, and Rell felt the familiar cold weight settle behind his sternum, the weight he carried everywhere, the true burden of his office, heavier than any crown.

"The king's health fluctuates," Rell said. "Audiences are limited to protect his dignity. Nothing more."

"He didn't seem ill at midwinter. He waved to the crowds for an hour."

"Kings are excellent performers. It's most of the job." Rell released the shoulder and stepped back, and his voice softened into something that was almost—almost—true. "Dain. There are things in this kingdom that must work whether or not they are understood. The succession of command is one of them. You don't need to understand the palace to serve it. You need only trust that the men maintaining it lose more sleep than you ever will."

Ferro held his eyes a moment longer than protocol allowed. Then he bowed—precisely, correctly, a bow with a knife hidden somewhere in its geometry, though whether the knife was aimed at Rell or at himself, Rell could not yet tell—and left.

When the door closed, Rell stood alone with the wine and the reports and the city, and permitted himself ten full seconds of honesty.

He had liked Ferro. He had given him the truth shaped like a career, and Ferro had taken it, because honest men always took the bait when it was baited with reform. By tomorrow Ferro would be riding east with forty men and a conscience like a lit lantern, straight toward a woman who knew things that could unmake everything.

Perhaps Ferro would catch her. Perhaps Ferro would join her. Rell had planned for both outcomes; planning for both outcomes was the entire art of survival.

He returned to the window. Below, in the Nightmarket, tiny figures moved among the stalls, buying bread and luck-knots and fever-charms, believing—as he had once believed—that someone wise was minding the machinery of the world.

"We are the same, you and I," Rell murmured to the city, and meant it, and this was the most chilling thing about him, though he would never know it: that he believed it completely. "We both sell charms against the dark. Mine simply cost more."

Behind him, on the desk, the black-sealed report stirred in a draft from the window, though the window was shut.

In the palace above, something wearing the crown was weeping ink onto a breakfast it could no longer taste, repeating a dead woman's song under its breath, and the Binding beneath Mount Serel pulled tighter around the kingdom's throat, and far to the east, on a road Mara Almeida was already walking, the mountain waited with its mouth open.

Rell rang the bell for his clerk and dictated orders for forty riders.

Twelve years of sleepless nights had taught him one law above all others: whatever wakes, wake first.
