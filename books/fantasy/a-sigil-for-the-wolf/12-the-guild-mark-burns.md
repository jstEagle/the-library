## 12. The Guild Mark Burns

The pain woke her before dawn, a hot wire pulled tight beneath the skin of her left forearm.

Mara sat up in the dark of the ruined shepherd's hut, breath held, hand clamped over the old tattoo as though she could smother it like a candle flame. Outside, the wind came down off the high country carrying the smell of stone and old snow, and somewhere in the bracken Oskar's mule stamped and blew through its nose, discontented with the cold. Liss slept curled against the far wall with Oskar's oilskin draped over her like a second skin, her breath a small, steady tide.

Mara peeled back her sleeve.

In the gray light leaking through the broken shutters, the guild mark glowed. Not brightly—not the way it had in the old days, when she would press it to a client's palm and let them feel the warmth of honest work—but with a deep, sullen ember-light, as if someone had banked a fire inside her arm and the wind had just found it again. The wolf's-head circle, three interlocking knots for the three vows, pulsed once, twice, in time with her heartbeat.

She knew what it meant. She had known since the second night on the road, when they'd crossed the river at Vell's Ford and the mark had woken for the first time in twelve years, aching like a healed bone before a storm. It meant they were getting close. It meant the mountain was pulling.

It meant the map was answering to her blood.

She sat with it a long while, watching the wolf's head breathe its slow light, and thought about how she had spent half her life teaching her hands to be still, her voice to be flat, her whole self to be a woman made of nothing but thread and small talk. Twelve years of stillness. And all that time, the mark had waited under her sleeve like a letter she'd never opened, patient as only ink can be patient.

Now it was burning. Now it wanted something.

"Mara."

She flinched. Oskar stood in the doorway with his coat on and his hair flattened on one side, silhouetted against a sky just beginning to bruise pink at the edges. He was holding two tin cups and looking at her arm with the expression of a man who had seen exactly what he'd expected to see.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." He came in and set the cups down on the flat stone they'd been using as a table. Tea, from the smell of it, over-steeped the way he always made it. "It's been doing that for three nights. You think I don't notice things?"

"I think you make a habit of not noticing things. It's how you stay cheerful."

"I'm not cheerful. I'm paid." He lowered himself onto the bench across from her, joints cracking, and wrapped his hands around his cup. "It's getting worse, isn't it. The closer we get."

Mara pulled her sleeve down. The heat of it came through the wool, faint but unmistakable, like holding her arm near an oven door. "Yes."

"To what? The vault? The map? What's it answering to, Mara?"

She looked at him. He was watching her over the rim of his cup with those flat harbor-gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and she felt the old reflex rise in her—the reflex of twelve years, the smooth deflection, the joke, the change of subject, the harmless trinket held up to the light while the real work stayed hidden underneath.

And she found she was tired of it. Bone-tired. The way you get tired of carrying a bucket you forgot, years ago, that you were even holding.

"Pack up Liss's things," she said. "We'll walk while I talk. I can't do it sitting still."

* * *

They walked north through the morning with the mountain growing ahead of them, Mount Serel, blue-gray and snow-capped, wearing its clouds like a hood. The road was old here—older than the kingdom, some said—paved in places with stones worn round and smooth as river pebbles, sinking into the earth as if the land were slowly swallowing the memory of everyone who had ever walked it.

Liss ranged ahead the way she did now, picking up stones and discarding them, naming the birds she didn't know the names of, a small bright thing moving through a gray landscape. Oskar led the mule. Mara walked between them and told the truth for the first time in twelve years, and it came out of her in pieces, like pulling stitches.

"You know I had a guild," she began. "I've never pretended otherwise. Luck-knots, fever-charms—the Nightmarket trade. Everyone assumes that's all it ever was. A little hedge-craft, the kind the Crown tolerates because it can't be bothered to hang every old woman who ties a red thread."

"You're saying it wasn't."

"The Sigilwrights' Guild was four hundred years old when I was born into it." The words felt strange in her mouth, like a language she'd stopped speaking and was now discovering she still dreamed in. "We weren't charm-sellers. Charm-selling was the mask. What we were—" She searched for it, and the mark on her arm pulsed as if to help her. "We were the keepers of names. True names. The old craft holds that everything has one—the true word of a thing, the word it was called before it was called anything else. And a sigil written around a true name doesn't trick the world the way a charm does. It *binds* it. Changes what a thing is at the root."

Oskar was quiet for a long stretch of road. The mule's hooves clicked on the old stones. "That's why it's a hanging offense," he said at last. "Not because it's magic. Because it's *powerful* magic."

"Because it's magic that can't be undone by burning the sigil-paper," Mara said. "The Purges weren't fear, Oskar. Fear burns witches and moves on. The Purges were a *land grab*. Someone in the palace understood what the guild kept, and wanted it, or wanted it gone. Probably both."

"And you were—" He glanced at her. "You said you were hiding the guild's last magic. In the shop. Under the trinkets."

"I was hiding more than that." She looked down at her arm, at the sleeve covering the burning wolf. "I was the youngest sworn member in the guild's history. Sworn at fourteen. There are—there were tests. Aptitude, yes, but more than that. The mark doesn't take unless the name underneath is clean. Untangled. Children's names are like that. Mine was." She laughed, short and without humor. "Master Aldric said I had the cleanest name he'd ever tested. Said the knots would hold on me for a hundred years."

"Aldric." Oskar turned the name over. "The seal on the map."

"My master. The man who taught me everything worth knowing." The road tilted upward, and she felt the mark flare with each step, a slow drumbeat under her skin. "He vanished the night of the raid. The night of the Purge."

"Tell me about the raid."

She had never told anyone about the raid. She had not told it to herself, if she was honest—not whole, not in order. She had kept it the way she kept everything, in pieces, in a dark room, each piece too hot to hold for long. But the mountain was ahead of them and the mark was burning and Liss was twenty paces ahead naming birds, and something about the ordinariness of the morning made it possible.

"There were forty-one of us in the Vharen chapterhouse that night," she said. "It was the autumn feast. The Feast of the First Knot—we tied a great binding each year, a blessing on the city's harvest and harbor. Everyone came up from the cellars for it, even the elders who never left their work. Even the children." Her voice caught on *children*, and she made herself go on. "The Crown's men came at moonrise. Not soldiers. Inquisitors and their—helpers. Men with gray faces. I never understood, that night, what was wrong with them. I understand better now." She thought of the map in her pack, of the words she hadn't yet said aloud. "They came through the front and the back and the *cellars*, Oskar. They knew every door. Every hidden door. Someone had given them the keys."

Oskar's jaw tightened. He didn't ask who. Not yet.

"I was in the scriptorium," Mara said. "I wasn't supposed to be. I was fourteen and I'd been caught skipping the feast to practice, and Master Aldric set me to copying sigil-frames as punishment. So I was the only one upstairs when they came. I heard it start below—the screaming, and then the *silence*, which was worse. And then Aldric was there, out of breath, blood on his sleeve, and he grabbed my arm—" she touched her left forearm through the sleeve, over the mark, "—and he did something to the mark. A working. He said, *It will hide you, and it will call you home, and I am sorry for both.* And he pushed me into the old flue behind the scriptorium wall. The chimney the founders used, before there was a road up the mountain. And I climbed."

The morning was bright and cold and utterly indifferent. Liss had stopped ahead and was holding up a flat gray stone for their inspection, and Mara nodded at her, and Liss put it in her pocket, satisfied, and ran on.

"The flue came out in the ravine behind the chapterhouse," she said. "I could see the windows. I could see—" She stopped. Breathed. The mark burned. "I watched until there was nothing left to watch. They took everyone. Everyone alive, I mean. And they burned the rest, and they burned the records, and by morning it was just a ruin with smoke coming out of it, and I was fourteen years old sitting in a ravine with a mark on my arm that was hiding me so well I could have walked past my own mother and she wouldn't have seen me."

"That's why no one came looking for you."

"No one came looking for me," she agreed. "That was the mercy of it, and the curse. The hiding-work was too good. It hid me from grief, too, almost. For years I could go a whole day and not think of them, and then I'd wake up at night and remember I was the only one, and the *only one* would sit on my chest like a stone." She looked at him sideways. "You want to know the worst part? The part I've never said? I didn't escape because I was brave or clever. I escaped because I was being *punished*. If I'd gone down to the feast like a good little sworn member, I'd have died with the rest of them in the cellars. My whole life since—the shop, the trinkets, the hiding—it's all been built on top of the fact that I was a disobedient child."

Oskar walked in silence for perhaps a hundred paces. When he spoke, his voice was rougher than usual.

"My sister wasn't sick."

Mara looked at him.

"You healed her, six years ago. The fever that wouldn't break. And you never asked why a harbor-rat like me could pay what you charged, and I never told you." He kept his eyes on the road. "It wasn't fever. It was a purge-squad's work. They'd marked her door in the Salt Quarter—some informer said a smuggler's sister was a hedge-witch, which is a lie, but the gray men don't check, they just *mark*. She was in bed three days after they came and she didn't get up for a year. Whatever they touched her with, it wasn't in any physicker's book." He shrugged, a small violent motion. "So. You hid your guild's last magic under trinkets. I hid my sister's curse under a fever. Seems to be what we do, people like us. We rename things so they can survive."

Mara felt something crack open in her chest—not painfully. The way ice cracks on a river in spring: a sound, and then the water moving underneath.

"Oskar Vey," she said, "that is the first honest thing either of us has said in six years of acquaintance."

"Don't get used to it. It's the altitude."

She laughed—actually laughed, a startled, unguarded sound that made Liss turn around twenty paces ahead to see what was funny, and then, finding nothing to steal or throw, turn back to her birds. And the mark on her arm pulsed, and pulsed, and Mara realized with a strange floating clarity that the confession hadn't killed her. Twelve years of believing that the truth would be the death of her, that the hiding was the only wall between herself and the fire—and she had told it, all of it, walking on an ordinary road in the morning, and she was still alive, and the sun was still climbing, and the only thing that had burned was the wall.

It was relief. That was the astonishing thing. It was relief, like a bandage coming off a wound that had healed crooked but had, in fact, healed.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a shrine—just a roadside alcove, really, a carved niche in the rock face where travelers had left offerings for so many generations that the stone beneath the little shelf was worn hollow. Someone had left a clay lamp there, long cold. Liss, who had appointed herself the company's quartermaster of found objects, immediately claimed it.

Mara sat apart, on a flat rock facing the mountain.

Serel was close now. In the last light she could see the pass they'd take tomorrow, a jagged notch in the ridgeline that the old maps called the Wolf's Throat, and above it the peak wearing its snow like a shroud. And with every hour that passed the mark burned hotter, until she no longer had to imagine the pulse—she could *hear* it, almost, a low rhythm under her skin like a second heart learning to beat.

She unrolled the map across her knees.

In the dark it was just paper and old wax, the bloodstain on its corner long brown. But when she laid her bare left arm against it, the guild mark flared, and for one heartbeat the lines of the map seemed to *move*—the road to the pass darkening like a vein filling, the little drawn mountain seeming to breathe. Responding to her blood. Aldric's voice came back to her across twelve years and a burned chapterhouse: *It will hide you, and it will call you home, and I am sorry for both.*

"You knew," she whispered to the dark mountain, to the dead man who had made her. "You knew I'd come. You built the calling into the hiding."

The mark pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a knock on a door she had nailed shut.

"Mara?" Liss's voice, small from the shrine. "Oskar says you have to eat. He says if you don't he's allowed to describe the fish he caught in Sarnholt and he says it was *this long*."

"Tell him I'm coming."

She rolled the map away, but she didn't roll down her sleeve. She sat a moment longer with the wolf's head glowing on her arm, facing the mountain, feeling it call.

And it was then, in that last full dark before the pass, that she heard Liss cry out in her sleep—a thin, frightened sound, nothing like her usual dreams—and Mara was moving before she'd decided to move, and the mark on her arm burned hotter than it had ever burned, as if something on the mountain had heard the child cry out too, and was answering.
