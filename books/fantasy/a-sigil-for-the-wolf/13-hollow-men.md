## 13. Hollow Men

The rain came down on Vharen like a verdict—slow, gray, and without mercy—and Dain Ferro stood beneath the eaves of a shuttered chandlery and watched the palace gates through it.

He had been standing there for two hours. Long enough that his boots had soaked through to the skin, long enough that the sentries had stopped glancing at him and begun pretending he didn't exist, which was its own kind of answer. Inquisitors were not meant to be ignored at the palace gates. Inquisitors carried the king's own seal, and men who saw that seal stepped aside with their eyes lowered and their sweat running cold.

These men did not step aside. These men looked at him the way a door looks at wind.

"Third time this week," said Brannic beside him, hunched into his oilskin, a young inquisitor with a good heart that Ferro privately feared would not survive the year. "They won't even take your petition."

"They took it." Ferro turned up his collar. "It went into a man's hands. The man walked inside. Nothing came back out."

"That's not taking it."

"No," Ferro agreed. "It isn't."

He had filed four requests in as many days: to examine His Majesty's health following the incident at the Nightmarket—a dead courier, a wax-sealed map, a charm-seller whose pulse had jumped like a snare drum when Ferro mentioned the word *keybearer*—and each request had gone into the palace the way stones go into wells. No splash. No echo. Only Chancellor Rell's smooth-voiced secretary arriving instead, smiling, offering Ferro a promotion to the Eastern Circuit where the air was clean and the heretics were simple.

Simple heretics. That was what the Crown wanted him hunting. Men who burned sigils into fence posts. Women who knotted luck-charms and called them craft. Small sins, easily punished, satisfying to catalog.

And behind the palace doors, something that would not let an inquisitor look at the king.

Ferro had served the Crown for nineteen years. He had learned to read institutions the way other men read weather—the pressure drop before a purge, the stillness before a decree. And every instrument he owned was telling him the same thing: the storm was not coming from outside the walls. It was already inside them.

"Come on," he said. "We're done here."

"Where are we going?"

"To talk to a man who used to be a gatekeeper."

* * *

Old Sef was a gatekeeper the way rust is a metal—technically true, mostly past it. He had kept the Weir Gate watch for thirty years until his knees gave out, and now he kept a stool outside a tavern on Salt Row and charged two coppers for opinions. He'd known Ferro since Ferro was a green lieutenant vomiting off the harbor wall after his first body-float, and he had never once lied to him, which made him nearly unique among Ferro's acquaintances.

"The night courier died," Ferro said, crouching by the stool, rain streaming off the awning between them. "In the Nightmarket. You'd have seen him pass."

Sef chewed on something toothless and slow. "Everyone passes my gate, boy. That's the point of a gate."

"This one was carrying palace dispatches. Gray livery."

The chewing stopped.

Ferro waited. Rain filled the silence, drumming on canvas, gurgling in a gutter, and somewhere down the row a drunk was singing a song about a fisherman's wife with more enthusiasm than accuracy.

"You know what I saw," Sef said finally, "three nights back? Not your courier. Earlier. A man walking up from the harbor road in the dark, no lantern. I called out—gate rules, you call out, everyone knows it—and he stopped." The old man's eyes came around slowly, like a ship turning. "He stopped all at once. Not like a man stopping. Like a *thing* stopping. And I says, state your business, and he turns his head, and Dain—" Sef's hand found Ferro's sleeve and gripped it with surprising strength. "Dain, there was no face moving when he talked. The mouth moved. The rest of him didn't. Like talking was something he remembered how to do but hadn't practiced in a while."

"What did he say?"

"'The king keeps his own counsel.'" Sef spat, delicately, into the rain. "That was all. Then he walked on, and I'll tell you the part that's been keeping me up, boy, so lean in close because I'm only saying it once."

Ferro leaned in.

"He didn't have no reflection in the harbor water. Puddles, tide-pools, the wet cobbles—everything in this city has a reflection, even cats, even ghosts if you believe in 'em. But the water just showed the street behind him. Like he wasn't standing there at all. Like the light went *through* him and couldn't be bothered to stop."

Ferro sat back on his heels. His heart was doing something quiet and terrible.

"Sef. Did you report this?"

"Reported it to the watch sergeant. Sergeant reported it up the chain." The old man smiled with most of his gums. "Two days later I got transferred off gate duty on account of my knees. My knees that have been bad for ten years and never bothered anyone official before." He released Ferro's sleeve. "You want my opinion, and it's worth three coppers tonight on account of the weather: whatever's wearing the palace these days, don't knock on its door expecting manners."

* * *

He found the gray man on the fourth night, or rather, the gray man allowed himself to be found, though Ferro would not understand that until much later, turning it over in the dark like a stone with something under it.

It happened in the Lantern District, in the hour past midnight when the lamps burn low and honest men are asleep and dishonest ones are elsewhere. Ferro had taken to walking the city alone at that hour—not officially, never officially; Brannic thought he was sleeping, and perhaps it was better if he did. There was a thing happening to the city that lived only in the small hours. He could feel it the way you feel a draft from a door left open somewhere in a large house. Luck draining. Children grayer than they should be. Bakers whose bread fell flat three days running. A whole city quietly, imperceptibly *dimming*, and no one able to say why, only that the candles seemed to gutter sooner and the dice seemed to come up wrong and the babies cried in a listless way, as if even their lungs had lost interest.

He turned down Cordwainer's Lane and the gray man was there.

Standing. Under the eave of a glover's shop, in the space where the lamplight ended and the shadow began, positioned precisely on the seam between the two, as though he had measured it.

Gray livery. Palace cut, but old-fashioned, the collar high, the buttons dull. A man of middle years, or what had been a man of middle years. Tall. Still. So still that Ferro's first instinct was that he was looking at a tailor's dummy, some prank of apprentices—

Then it spoke.

"Inquisitor Ferro."

The voice was the worst part, and Ferro would spend many nights afterward trying to describe it to himself and failing. It was not cold. Cold voices he knew, cold voices belonged to killers and chancellors. This voice was *unused*. It was a voice that had been packed away in a chest with camphor and brought out for special occasions, and speaking was clearly not one of them. Each word arrived slightly late, like thunder counting distance from lightning.

"You know me," Ferro said. His hand had already found the hilt of his sword. He hadn't told it to. Good. He liked to think some parts of him still worked on instinct.

"The king keeps his own counsel."

"So I've heard. From another of you." Ferro stepped forward, deliberately into the lamplight, deliberately making himself visible, because nineteen years of doctrine said that fear loved the dark and starved in the open. "Or are you the same one? Do you lot share a tongue between you?"

The head turned. All at once, the way Sef had said. Like a thing stopping.

And Ferro looked into its face and understood, finally, completely, what the old gatekeeper had been too frightened to say plainly.

There was nothing wrong with the face. That was the horror of it. It was a perfectly ordinary face—weathered, stubbled, a scar over one brow, eyes that had once been brown. Every feature present and correct. And none of it *moving together*. The eyes tracked him with a lag, like a portrait learning to follow. The breath—if there was breath—did not stir the collar. Rain struck the man's cheek and ran down and the skin did not flinch, did not tighten, did nothing that skin does when water touches it, because water touching skin is a conversation and this face had resigned from all conversations.

"State your name," Ferro said. Doctrine again. Names had power; the guilds had taught the Crown that much before the Purges, and the Crown had learned it well enough to hang them for it. "In the king's law, I'm entitled to it."

A pause. Long enough that Ferro counted his own heartbeats against it. Seven. Eight.

"Tomas Wrenn," the thing said.

Ferro's blood went cold in a new way, a specific way, the way it goes cold when a case opens underneath you like a trapdoor. Tomas Wrenn. He knew the name. Every inquisitor who'd served six years or more knew the name. Tomas Wrenn, palace guard, listed dead twelve years ago—drowned, the record said, in the flooding of the old cisterns, one of nine men lost, bodies never recovered because the cisterns had been sealed by direct order of the Crown before recovery could be attempted.

Sealed by order. Sealed, Ferro now understood, the way you seal a jar.

"You're dead," Ferro said quietly. "Tomas Wrenn drowned twelve years ago."

"Yes," said the thing wearing Tomas Wrenn, and it might have been agreement, might have been memory, might have been nothing at all. "The king keeps his own counsel."

"Is the king alive?"

The lag stretched. Somewhere down the lane a cat knocked over a bottle and fled, and the sound cracked across the silence like a whip, and the gray man did not startle. Did not blink. Ferro realized he had been watching for a blink and had not seen one, not once, and his stomach began to learn what it would fully know in a few minutes' time.

"The king," the thing said, and here the unused voice did something worse than threaten, worse than menace—it *hesitated*, the way a man hesitates over a wound he's been ordered not to mention—"the king is *kept*."

"Kept by whom?"

"By the hand that holds the thread."

"Whose hand, Wrenn? Whose thread?" Ferro took another step, and another, close enough now to see the fine wrongnesses: the pupils that did not contract in the lamplight, the lips gray as ash, the total absence—now that he was near enough to feel for it—of any warmth radiating from the body at all. A man puts out heat. Even a corpse puts out heat for hours. This thing put out nothing. It was a hole in the world shaped like a guardsman. "Who commands you?"

Something moved behind the dead brown eyes. Not thought. Struggle. Like watching a man fight his own arms.

"I am commanded," it said, and the voice frayed at the edges, threads pulling loose, "to keep the counsel of the king. I am commanded to—" and then it stopped, all at once, the way it had stopped on the harbor road, and the struggle behind the eyes simply *ended*, snuffed like a pinched wick, and what looked out at Ferro afterward was nothing at all. No lag. No ghost of a man. Just absence, wearing a face, regarding him with the mild attention of weather.

"Inquisitor Ferro," it said. Same words. Different speaker. Or the same speaker with the man-shaped mask removed. "You have asked questions above your station."

"I ask questions for a living," Ferro said. His sword was out now. He didn't remember drawing it either. "It's my station."

"The king keeps his own counsel."

"Then the king can tell me so himself. To my face. In a lit room, with witnesses, with a mirror in the room—" and he saw it, saw the flicker, the first true reaction he'd gotten, a contraction of the whole body like a nail drawn halfway out of wood. *Mirrors.* "What's the matter, Wrenn? Never cared for mirrors?"

The gray man moved.

Later, reviewing it a thousand times, Ferro could never make the motion resolve into sense. There was no wind-up, no weight-shift, none of the honest grammar of violence he'd spent a lifetime reading. One moment the thing stood six feet away; the next its hand was at his throat and the strength in it was not muscle-strength but *anchor*-strength, the grip of a mooring post, of something built to hold and never trained to let go, and Ferro's back hit the glover's shutter hard enough to crack a slat and the lamplight swung and their shadows lurched on the cobbles—

One shadow. His.

He got his sword up between them on pure animal faith and felt the flat catch the gray man's forearm, and the arm did not deflect, did not recoil, simply *pressed*, bending the blade with a patience no living swordsman owned, and Ferro understood with perfect clarity that this thing would press until his ribs folded, without anger, without effort, forever, the way water presses, the way debt presses.

So he did the thing he'd sworn he'd never do again.

His free hand dove into his coat and came out with the knife he kept against regulations—a short blade, plain-hilted, and along its spine, so faint you'd swear it was only a scratch, so faint he'd told himself for eleven years it was only a scratch, a single line of silvered script. Sigilwork. Confiscated evidence he had never turned in. A cutting-edge, a truth-blade, a thing that should have gone into the Purge fires with everything else, kept by a decent man the way all decent men keep their one corruption: close, hidden, and ready.

He drove it up under the gray man's ribs.

The thing looked down at the knife in its body with something that was almost curiosity. No blood came. Of course no blood came. But where the blade had entered, the gray flesh was *unraveling*, quietly, like a knot picked loose from the end of a rope, threads of whatever held Tomas Wrenn together lifting off the knife's edge and dissolving into the rain, and the dead brown eyes came back up to Ferro's face and for one instant—one flickering, human, damnable instant—there was a man in them. A drowning man. Twelve years underwater, conscious, holding a thread someone else was pulling.

"*Cold,*" whispered Tomas Wrenn, in a voice suddenly, horribly alive. "*It's so cold down there. Tell them it's cold. Tell them we never—*"

And he came apart.

Not dramatically. That was the worst of it. He didn't burst or burn or scream. He simply ceased to cohere, folding down into the wet cobbles like a garment slipping off a hook, and what the rain found where a man had been was a drift of pale gray dust already washing toward the gutter, and a palace-gray uniform lying empty on the stones, buttons dull, collar high, holding the shape of nothing.

Ferro stood over it for a long time.

The lane was silent. The lamp hissed. His sword arm shook, and he sheathed the sword because shaking hands shouldn't hold blades, and then he turned aside, braced one hand on the cracked shutter, and vomited into the gutter—vomited until his throat burned and his eyes streamed, vomited the way he hadn't since he was a green lieutenant, because some things the body knows before the mind consents to, and his body had known from the moment the thing spoke his name.

When there was nothing left in him he straightened, wiped his mouth with the back of his glove, and made himself look at the empty uniform again. Made himself kneel. Made himself search it, because he was still an inquisitor, God help him, and evidence was evidence even when the evidence was a nightmare.

In the breast pocket, behind a palace seal he recognized and would remember until he died, he found a folded paper. Orders, written in a clerk's neat hand:

*W. — Cordwainer's Lane. Observe the inquisitor. If he approaches the Vault-road question, correct him.*

Correct him. As if Ferro were an error in a ledger. As if he were a sum that had come out wrong.

And beneath the orders, in different ink, older ink, pressed hard enough to tear the page—a single line that no clerk had written, in a hand that shook:

*we are still in here*

Ferro sat back on his heels in the rain, the paper trembling in his fingers, and felt the last load-bearing wall of his nineteen years' faith come down without a sound.

Treason he knew how to fight. Treason had faces, names, trials, scaffolds. But this—dead men walking with living men screaming inside them, a king who was *kept*, a Crown sealing cisterns full of drowning guards and calling it order—this was not treason. This was something older and hungrier wearing treason's clothes, and it reached all the way up, and it had just given him orders to be corrected.

He rose. He tucked the paper inside his coat, over his heart, where the regulation knife used to sit.

Brannic would need to know. No—Brannic was young, and Brannic had a mother. Brannic needed to be sent east, far from this, and soon.

Because there was one more thing Ferro knew now, one thing the dying guard had shown him in that last flicker of humanity, one word half-heard as the man came apart in the rain: *Vault*.

The Vault-road. The mountain. The keybearer.

Whatever was buried beneath Mount Serel, the dead were still trying to testify about it—and Dain Ferro had just decided, alone in the rain with an empty uniform at his feet, that he was done taking orders from anything that feared a mirror.

At dawn, he would ride for the shrine road.

At dawn, he would find the charm-seller's trail—or die discovering who had given him orders he could no longer obey.
