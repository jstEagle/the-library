## 14. Liss Runs

The dream always began the same way: with doors.

Not real doors—Liss knew real doors, the sagging plank kind on farmhouses and the iron-banded ones on inns—but doors made of stone, cut into a mountainside, each one carved with symbols that moved when she looked at them. They slid like water bugs. They watched her.

In the dream she was small again, small as she'd been before the fever took her parents' farm and her father's laugh with it, and she was standing in front of the middle door, the biggest one, and it was breathing. Not wind. Breathing. In and out, slow, like something enormous asleep behind the stone, and every time it exhaled the carvings glowed a little dimmer, like coals going gray.

*You're late,* said a voice from behind the door. It wasn't a scary voice. That was the worst part. It was tired, and polite, and it sounded like an old man who had been waiting so long that waiting had become the whole shape of his life. *You're late, keybearer. You're late, keybearer. You're late—*

"Liss."

She woke with her mother's name dying in her throat—not her mother, not anymore, never again—and for three heartbeats she didn't know where she was. Then the smells arrived: woodsmoke, wet wool, pine pitch, and under it all the faint mineral cold of high mountain air seeping through the waystation's broken shutters. The ruined shelter they'd found at dusk, half-swallowed by the treeline, its roof holed by some long-ago storm. Oskar snoring on the far side of the dead hearth like a man arguing with the world even in his sleep. Mara on the near side, wrapped in her cloak, facing the door even in sleep. She always faced the door.

Liss lay still and listened to her own heart.

The dream was getting worse. Every night since the pass—the Pass of Teeth, Oskar called it, saying it the way other men said a prayer or a curse—the doors came closer. First she'd only seen them from far away, down a long corridor of dark. Then she'd been close enough to touch them. Last night she'd woken with her fingernails aching, certain she'd been scratching at stone.

And tonight, tonight there'd been the voice, and the voice knew her name—or knew *a* name, the word Mara kept saying quietly to Ferro and Oskar when they thought Liss couldn't hear. *Keybearer.* Liss didn't know what it meant. She only knew that Mara went white around the mouth whenever she said it, the way people did in her village when they spoke of the plague year.

The fire was down to embers. Through the broken shutter, the sky over the peaks was the deep bruised purple that came just before true dawn, with stars still caught in it like sparks in cloth.

Liss sat up. Her legs decided before she did.

Later—much later, in the years after, when she would tell this story to children in a bright room full of knotwork and laughter—she would try to explain why she ran. There was no good reason. That was the honest answer, and she learned to give it honestly: fear doesn't need a reason, it needs only a direction. The dream had told her the doors were behind her, back down the mountain, and everything in her small exhausted body believed that if she could just get away from the doors—if she could put enough miles between herself and that breathing stone—then the voice would stop knowing her name.

So she ran.

She took nothing. Not the bread in her pack, not the little knotted cord Mara had taught her, the sleep-charm with its three simple loops that she wore at her wrist. She didn't even take her shoes at first—she carried them, because the ground outside was frost-stiff and full of stones, and only when her feet went past hurting into numbness did she stop and pull them on, crouched behind a fallen pine, breathing in white clouds.

Behind her, the waystation sat dark and silent. No one had woken. She was eleven years old and light as a bird and she had spent her whole life learning how to move without being heard, because in her house, quiet had been survival too, a different kind.

The road wound down through black pines. Or up—she didn't know which way she meant to go, only *away*, and away split itself in her chest into two directions at once until she simply picked the path that went along the stream, because the sound of the water covered the sound of her own breath, and her own breath sounded, in the dark, like the breathing door.

*You're late.*

"Stop it," she whispered to the trees. "Stop it, stop it."

The trees did not stop it. The trees were just trees, frost-silvered and dripping, and the sky went from bruise-purple to ash-gray while she walked, and the stream chattered beside her about nothing, and slowly, horribly, the terror drained out of her and left something worse behind: ordinary morning. Ordinary cold. Ordinary knowledge of what she had done.

She had run away from Mara.

Her eyes stung. She scrubbed them with her sleeve and kept walking, because crying while walking downhill on frost was how you broke your neck, and her mother had taught her that much at least. *Feet first, tears after.* Her mother's voice in her head was getting fainter. That frightened her more than the doors. Every day the voice got a little fainter, and every day Mara's voice got a little clearer, and Liss did not know which loss to grieve first.

That was when she heard the horses.

Not hoofbeats—horses don't sound like anything on frost-softened forest loam, she'd learn that later—but the small sounds: the creak of tack, the snuffle of an animal held quiet, the soft metallic whisper of a bit chain. Three sounds, from three places, spread out along the road ahead of her like fingers closing.

Liss stepped off the path and pressed herself against a pine trunk, heart slamming, and saw them come.

They rode single file through the gray light, and they were wrong in a way her body understood before her mind did. Their armor was pale—pale as bone, pale as the inside of things—and they sat their horses without moving, without the little constant adjustments living riders make, shifting weight, touching rein, rolling shoulders. These men did not adjust. They rode like sacks of meal that had learned to balance.

Their faces, when the lead rider turned his head to scan the treeline, had no color at all. And their eyes—

Liss had seen gray eyes all summer. Whole villages of them, children mostly, listless and luckless, the blight-marked. But those eyes had been gray the way fog is gray, gray with something drained out. These men's eyes were gray the way slate is gray, gray with something packed in, and when the lead rider's gaze swept across her tree, it stopped.

It stopped.

Liss stopped breathing.

For a moment that lasted a season, the man stared at the trunk of her pine, and his nostrils flared once, delicately, like a wolf testing wind, and Liss understood with absolute animal certainty that he could smell her—that whatever these things used instead of blood, it had caught the scent of her fear, or her warmth, or maybe the little charm at her wrist, the knots Mara had tied, and she thought, absurdly, *I should have taken the cord off, I should have—*

A branch cracked somewhere upslope.

The rider's head snapped toward the sound. A deer burst from the brush, brown and frantic, crashed across the road, and was gone. The riders watched it go with the same motionless patience. Then, one by one, without a word, without a signal, they turned their horses and continued down the road, in step, in silence, passing within ten feet of her hiding place, close enough that she could see that the pale armor had no dents in it anywhere, no scratches, no wear, as if it had never been used for anything but looking at.

Liss stayed against the tree until her legs shook. Then, very carefully, she did the most foolish thing she ever did in her life, and she did it because she was eleven and alone and the dream had hollowed her out:

She followed them.

Because they were going the way she'd been going, and because a terrible arithmetic had begun working in her: if the gray men wanted the keybearer, and the gray men were going *down* the mountain, then the doors, the voice, the thing in the dream—it was all down the mountain, and Mara was up the mountain, and Mara was safe, and Liss could find out what was hunting her without leading it home.

She was brave that way. She would be brave that way her whole life. It nearly killed her four times before she turned twenty.

* * *

Mara woke the way she always woke: instantly, completely, with her hand already closed around the knife beneath her cloak.

Something was wrong. She lay still and read the shelter the way she'd once read sigils—for structure, for disturbance. Embers ticking. Oskar snoring. Ferro on watch by the door, or pretending to be; the man slept in shifts of twenty minutes like a cat and called it vigilance. Cold air through the broken shutter. Pine and frost and—

No.

She sat up so fast that Ferro's hand went to his sword. "What?"

"The girl." Mara was already moving, already at Liss's bedroll, already pressing her palm flat to the wool where a small body should have been and finding only cold. "Liss. LISS—" She caught herself, dragged the shout down to a hiss. Waking Oskar shouting would help no one. "She's gone. How long has—"

"I looked not two hours past," Ferro said, and his face did the thing it did when his certainty failed him, a small tightening at the corners of his eyes, a man watching a wall crack. "She was asleep. I swear it on—"

"Don't swear. Think." Mara spun, scanning the floorboards. Small footprints in the frost-dust by the shutter. Bare feet, then shod. Heading out, not in. "Ferro, the road. Which way does the road go from here?"

"Down, mostly. Toward the river crossing. Up, toward the pass we crossed." He was buckling his sword as he talked, awake now in the whole of him. "Mara. Downhill is toward Vharen's reach. Toward patrols."

Toward patrols. Toward the relay stations. Toward, though Ferro didn't say it and his jaw said it for him, whatever the Crown sent out when Rell wanted something found.

Oskar was awake. She didn't know when he'd woken—he had a smuggler's trick of surfacing without transition—but he was up and pulling his boots on and saying, in the flat reasonable voice he used when he was furious, "Tell me the child didn't take food."

"She didn't take anything."

"Wonderful. So we're chasing a hungry eleven-year-old through Crown territory before dawn. I've had worse mornings. I can't think of when." He stood, rolled his shoulders, and Mara saw him do the other thing he always did, the thing he thought nobody saw: he touched the little wooden bead at his throat, the one she'd carved for his sister years ago, the one he wore like a saint's medal and denied like a smuggler denies cargo.

"Ferro takes the road down," Mara said. "Fast. Oskar, the streamside path—she'd pick the water, it hides noise. I'll—"

"You'll what?" Oskar said. "Walk? You'll walk, will you, at your famous pace, with your famous stamina, after the pass nearly folded you in half?" He jabbed a finger at her. "You're running on fumes and stubbornness, Almeida. Everyone can see it but you."

"Then it's good I have you both to be stubborn at me." She was already untying the cord at her own wrist. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook. "I'm not walking. I'm doing the thing we agreed I wouldn't do unless the sky fell."

Ferro went still. Oskar swore softly, comprehensively, in three languages.

"The tracking sigil," Ferro said. "On the child."

"On the charm I tied for her." Mara held up the matching cord, its twin, the knot she'd worked the night she'd taught Liss the sleep-loop, telling herself it was a lesson, telling herself it was a comfort, telling herself every lie a careful woman tells when she is really building a leash and calling it love. "It's a finder-knot. Guild standard, third circle. It needs blood-warmth and a name spoken true, and it will show me where she is as long as the paired knot stays intact." She paused. "And it will cost me what it costs."

"How much?" Ferro asked. Directly, the way he asked everything. She had come, grudgingly, to respect that in him—the honesty of a man who had spent his life among liars and hated it.

"I don't know yet. That's what *costs* means." She met Oskar's eyes. "Go. Both of you. Find her with your eyes and your legs and bring her home, and if you find her first, keep her still and hidden, because if the Crown is looking for us, they'll be looking for a girl traveling with us."

"And if we don't find her first?"

Mara looked down at the twin knot in her palm, and felt, faint and far, like a heartbeat through a wall, the small warm pulse of the charm she'd tied to a child's wrist.

"Then the sky has fallen," she said, "and I'll come burning."

* * *

The sigil-work, when she finally surrendered to it, was nothing like she remembered and exactly like she remembered.

She knelt in the frost at the edge of the camp, cords in her lap, and spoke Liss's name—*Liss, daughter of Annet, born under the harvest moon, afraid of deep water and loud doors*—and the guild words rose in her like water rising in a dry well, cold and sweet and familiar, twelve years buried and alive the whole time, waiting. Of course they were alive. You don't bury a language. You only stop speaking it.

She fed the knot a thread of her own strength, the way the old teaching had it—*the sigil drinks, the sigilite pours, and know the well's depth before you pour*—and the paired cord warmed in her hands and then *pulled*, gently, insistently, like a sleeping animal tugging its lead. South and east. Downstream. Not fast. Walking pace, then stopping, then walking.

Alive. Moving. Hers, for the finding.

Mara stood, swayed, caught herself on a pine trunk, and discovered that the world had gone slightly too bright at the edges, the way it did when she'd poured too deep. Fine. Manageable. She had done harder workings with less sleep, back when—back when—

The memory slid sideways and wouldn't come. She frowned at it. Tiredness did that. It was only tiredness.

She followed the pull.

The forest gave way to streamside alder, and the alder to open ground above the river meadows, and the pull grew stronger and warmer and then, with a lurch that dropped her stomach through the frost, it *stopped*. Not faded—stopped, the cord going slack and cool as a cut rope.

Mara stood very still in the gray dawn and made herself think instead of feel, because feeling was screaming and screaming was useless.

Slack meant distance beyond the working's reach, or the paired knot damaged, or—

Or the wearer had removed it. Or someone else had.

She was running before she finished the thought, crashing downslope through frozen bracken, following the last known bearing, and the world was too bright at the edges and her lungs were full of knives and none of it mattered, none of it had ever mattered, because somewhere ahead of her was a small girl in borrowed boots who had run into the dark because of dreams Mara hadn't asked about, nightmares Mara hadn't tended, fears Mara had been too busy managing her own to notice, and if anything happened to her—

*Anything*, said a voice in Mara's head that sounded like Aldric, young Aldric, the master who had taught her the first oath. *You've known the child five weeks. Five weeks, Mara. You are pouring out your craft, your strength, perhaps your life, for five weeks of a girl.*

"Yes," Mara said aloud, to the empty hillside, to the ghost, to the frost. "Yes, I am. Watch me."

She crested the rise above the river meadow and dropped flat, because below her, in the silver grass, were horses.

Three of them. Pale riders, dismounted, standing in a loose triangle around a small figure sitting in the grass. Even at this distance—even through the too-bright blur eating the edges of her vision—Mara knew them for what they were, because she had seen one once, twelve years ago, on the worst night of her life, and some sights the mind keeps whole when it loses everything else.

Gray guards. The king's silent men. Here, forty leagues from the capital, in a frost meadow, gathered around her child.

Around *Liss*.

The girl was holding the cord out in both hands—her charm, her sleep-knot, the paired finder—holding it up to the nearest rider the way you'd hold a lantern to a locked door, and Mara realized with a horror that came in colors that the guards had come for the knot. For the sigil-smell of it. They had scented guild-work on the wind like wolves scenting blood, and they had followed it, and they had found not the keybearer but a child wearing a child's charm, and they were deciding what to do about that.

One of them reached out. Gray fingers, unhurried, toward the cord, toward the wrist, toward—

Mara did not decide anything. Later she would search that moment for a decision and find none, only motion, only the oldest truth of her life arriving at last in its final shape: that she had spent twelve years hiding her fire in trinkets and calling it wisdom, and that wisdom was over.

She came down the slope with a warding pattern blazing between her hands—a true sigil, unhidden, drawn in the old script in light the color of deep water—and the meadow lit up blue-white, and the gray guards turned as one, and their slate eyes reflected her fire with no expression at all.

"Let. Her. Go." Each word landed like a struck bell. The ward expanded as she walked, a dome of pressure that made the frost smoke and the grass flatten, and the horses screamed and bolted, and the guards did not scream, did not bolt, only raised their pale arms against her light and stepped backward, one pace, two, three, driven not by fear—they had none, she could see that, see it and feel her skin crawl—but by the simple physics of her will against their wrongness.

The nearest one looked at her. Only that. Looked at her with those packed-slate eyes, and tilted its head, and its mouth opened, and what came out was a voice like dust settling:

"*Keybearer.*"

Then all three of them simply folded, the way puppets fold when the hand withdraws, and lay in the frost, and did not move, and did not breathe, because they never had.

Mara stood in the smoking grass with her fire guttering, and the cost arrived all at once, the way costs do. Her knees went. The bright edges of the world closed in like a book shutting. The last thing she registered was small arms catching her—impossibly strong small arms, a girl's arms, shaking—and a voice cracking apart against her hair:

"You came. You *came*, you weren't supposed to—you came—"

"Of course I came," Mara said, or tried to say. The meadow tilted. "Of course I—"

Darkness took her, gentle as snowfall, and the last thing she felt was Liss's heartbeat against her cheek, quick and alive and *hers*, and she thought, falling: *well. Now I know what I am.*

Not a charm seller. Not a keeper of secrets. Not the last of anything.

A mother. Late to it, unwilling, terrified—and arrived all the same.

Above them, on the ridge, unnoticed by either, a fourth figure in pale armor turned its horse away from the meadow and began, at an unhurried walk, to ride south toward Vharen—to carry home the one thing its masters had sent it to learn:

The keybearer's face.
