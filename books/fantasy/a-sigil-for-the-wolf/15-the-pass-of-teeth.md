## 15. The Pass of Teeth

The mountain did not want them there.

Oskar Vey had crossed enough bad ground in his life to know the difference between a road that was merely hard and a road that was hostile, and the pass ahead of them was the latter. The locals called it the Pass of Teeth, and standing at its mouth, Oskar could see why: jagged spires of gray stone jutted from the snowfield like the jaw of something ancient and patient, and the wind came through the gaps between them with a sound like slow, deliberate breathing. Not howling. Breathing. As if the mountain were considering them.

"Charming place," he said. "I've been in prisons with better ventilation."

Nobody laughed. Liss was pressed against Mara's side, her small face pale under the windburn, and Ferro stood a little apart from the rest of them, arms crossed, watching the pass the way a man watches a dog he suspects of biting. The inquisitor had been quiet for two days now—since the shrine, since Mara had burned whatever she'd burned in that little tracking sigil to find the girl, since the four of them had become something none of them had a name for yet.

Oskar checked the rope. Twice. Then a third time, because he was the kind of man who checked things three times, and because the rope was going to matter.

"Listen," he said, and made himself look at each of them in turn. "The crossing is a quarter mile of scree with a drop on the left side. Not a fall—a *drop*. Four hundred feet, maybe five, straight down into a gorge with a river at the bottom that you will not hear coming because the wind eats the sound. We rope up. Ferro takes point—he's heaviest, he'll cut a trail the rest of us can follow. Then the girl, then Mara, then me at the back, because I'm the lightest and because if anyone slips on my watch, I'd rather it be me."

"I can walk a rope line," Liss said, chin up, in the voice of a child who had decided that being frightened was a thing that happened to other people.

"I know you can." Oskar crouched to her level, and something in his chest did the uncomfortable thing it had started doing around her, the thing he refused to examine. "But walking it and *falling* it are two different walks. You hold the line, you keep three points on the ground at all times—two feet, one hand, always. You don't look down. You look at the back of Ferro's coat and you pretend it's a game and the prize is not dying. Can you do that?"

Liss looked at him with those too-serious eyes, the eyes of a child who had already buried more than most adults. "The prize is not dying," she repeated, solemn as an oath.

"Good. That's the spirit of commerce." He stood, and caught Mara watching him with an expression he didn't like—something soft and knowing, like he was a puzzle she'd already solved. "What?"

"Nothing," she said. "You're good at this."

"I'm good at *not dying*. It's a related skill." He shouldered his pack. "Let's move before the wind changes its mind."

* * *

The first hundred yards were almost easy. The scree was loose but predictable, and Ferro set a pace that was slow without being insulting, planting each boot with the deliberate care of a man who had learned somewhere that haste on bad ground was just a slower way of falling. The wind pushed at them from the left, from the drop, as though it were trying to herd them over the edge, and Oskar kept his eyes on the rope in his hands and the back of Mara's coat and the small, stubborn figure of Liss between them.

He was thinking—foolishly, he'd think later—that they might actually make it clean, when the mountain changed its mind.

It started as a sound. A low, grinding groan beneath the wind, deep in the bones of the pass, and Oskar felt it through his boots before he heard it, a vibration like the mountain clearing its throat. He had time to shout—"Down! Everyone down!"—before the rockslide came.

It wasn't a big one. That was the horror of it. A shelf of stone the size of a wagon bed, maybe two, shearing loose somewhere above and coming down the slope in a hissing, bouncing avalanche of rock and ice. Ferro flattened against the mountainside and pulled Liss in under his body. Mara dropped and covered her head. Oskar had time to see the largest piece—a slab the size of a door, tumbling end over end, coming straight for the rope line—

—and then it hit the line.

The world became very simple. There was the jerk of the rope going taut, the sickening lurch of weight, and then Oskar was sliding, boots skidding on scree, the rope burning through his gloved hands as he threw his whole body backward against the pull. The line had snagged on a stone spur, and the tension was dragging it toward the edge, and if the line went over, everything went over—the girl, Mara, all of it, all of them.

"OSKAR!" Mara's voice, thin and shredded by wind.

"I have it!" He didn't have it. He had forty feet of rope and a grip that was already failing, the rope sawing against the stone spur, fibers parting one by one with small sounds like whispers. His boots found a ledge, a shelf of rock barely wide enough for his heels, and he leaned back with everything he had, every muscle, every ounce of the wiry strength that had kept him alive through twenty years of smuggling and worse, and the mountain leaned back.

The wind screamed. The drop breathed. Below him, four hundred feet of gray air, and somewhere at the bottom a river he couldn't hear.

His left boot slipped.

It was a small thing. A shift of an inch, maybe two, scree rolling away beneath his heel, and suddenly he was past the point of balance, the rope the only thing between him and the long fall, his body swinging out over the gorge like a pendulum, and the stone spur was cutting the rope and he could *see* the fibers going, one, two, three—

"OSKAR!"

A hand closed on his wrist.

He looked up and Mara was flat on her belly at the edge of the ledge, one arm down the rock face, her fingers locked around his forearm, and her face was the face of a woman who had decided something. Not panic. Decision.

"I have you," she said.

"You can't—" His voice cracked. His weight was dragging her toward the edge; he could see it in the way her body strained, in the scree trickling past her elbows and whispering down into the dark. "Mara, let go, you'll—"

"Shut up, Oskar." Through gritted teeth. Through the wind. Her grip didn't loosen. If anything it tightened, and he saw her other hand move—saw her press two fingers against the rock, saw her lips shape a word he couldn't hear, saw the stone *shift*, a ridge of granite swelling up out of the cliff face like a bone pushing through skin, and her boot found it, braced against it, and the slide stopped.

For a moment neither of them moved. The wind howled. The rope groaned against the spur. Below his swinging boots, the gorge breathed its soundless breath.

"Pull," she said. "Now. While the stone holds."

He pulled. It took everything—his arms screaming, his shoulders on fire, the world narrowing to grip and grit and the sound of his own blood in his ears—and then he was over the edge, rolling onto solid ground, gasping like a landed fish, and Mara was beside him, her hand still on his arm as if she didn't quite trust the mountain to have given him back.

"You used a sigil," he said, when he could speak. "Out here. In the open."

"I used a sigil." Her face was gray with exhaustion. Using her craft always cost her; he'd watched it drain her for days now, the way it had drained her at the waystation, at the shrine. "And I'd do it again. So don't start."

"I wasn't going to start." He lay back against the rock and stared up at a sky that seemed very far away and very indifferent. "I was going to say thank you."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a shallow cave of wind-scoured stone that Ferro pronounced defensible and Liss pronounced "smelly," and Oskar, who had not argued with either assessment, sat with his back against the wall and his hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone lukewarm and watched Mara across the small fire.

She was braiding Liss's hair. Sitting behind the girl, working the tangles out with her fingers, patient as water, while Liss talked—about the pass, about the rockslide, about how Ferro had sworn under his breath in a way that was *definitely* a swear, and Ferro, on watch at the cave mouth, had the grace to look mildly ashamed. The firelight moved on Mara's face, and Oskar looked at her hands, the clever, scarred hands of a craftswoman, and thought about a night eleven years ago in a cellar in the Salt Quarter with a girl of nineteen kneeling over a dying woman and doing something with her fingers and her voice that Oskar had never been able to explain to himself since.

He owed her his sister's life. He'd told her that once, years ago, in the flat merchant's voice he used for debts: *You saved Sela. I pay what I owe.* And Mara, being Mara, had said something gentle and deflecting, something about how anyone would have done the same, and he had let her believe the debt was simple, because the truth was complicated and the truth was also a door, and Oskar Vey had spent his whole life keeping doors shut.

His hands were still shaking. From the rope, he told himself. From the rope, and the drop, and the four hundred feet of gray air.

"Mara." His voice came out rougher than he intended.

She looked up, Liss's braid half-finished in her hands.

"Can you spare a minute? The girl can wait. Her hair isn't going anywhere."

Liss shot him a look of profound offense, but Mara kissed the top of her head and said, "Five minutes, little wolf. Then we finish," and came and sat beside him against the cave wall, close enough that the firelight reached them both, far enough that no one could hear over the wind.

"You almost died today," she said.

"You almost died today. I just provided the falling."

"Oskar."

"I know." He turned the cup in his hands. The tea was cold. He drank it anyway, because his throat needed something to do besides talk. "I know. And I keep thinking—today, on that ledge, when you grabbed me. You didn't hesitate. Not for a second. You just—decided. And I've been asking myself all evening why a woman who's spent twelve years being careful, twelve years being *invisible*, would risk her neck and burn her strength on a smuggler who's done nothing but complain since Vharen."

"Because you'd have done the same."

"I'd have complained more." He set the cup down. His hands were still shaking, and this time he let them. "Mara, I have to tell you something. About Sela. About why you actually saved her. Because I've been carrying it for eleven years and today the mountain nearly took the chance out of my hands, and I find I don't want to die with it still in my pocket."

She said nothing. She was good at that—waiting. He'd noticed it on the road. Most people filled silence like a hole in a boat, frantic, any sound at all. Mara let silence be silence, and so the words, when they finally came, came in their own time.

"She wasn't sick."

Mara went very still.

"The fever, the wasting—I told you it was a fever. A bad one, a lung-rot, the kind that takes the poor. That's what I said. That's what everyone in the Salt Quarter said, because it was safer that way." He was looking at the fire now, because looking at Mara was not possible and looking at the fire was barely possible. "Sela was a knot-worker. Not like you—not guild, not trained, nothing formal. Our mother taught her. Little charms. A knot to keep the rats out of the grain. A knot to cool the room in summer. Harmless things. The kind of things half the women in the Salt Quarter did and half the men pretended not to see.

"And then the Purges came to our street."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wind changed pitch, and Ferro shifted at the cave mouth, a shadow against the deeper dark.

"There was a squad. Crown men, inquisitor's writ, the whole theater—black coats, white armbands. They went door to door. They weren't looking for anything in particular. That was the worst part, I understand that now. They weren't hunting a specific witch. They were just—harvesting. Taking whoever they took. And they came to our door, and my mother, may she rest, she was a proud woman, and she stood in the doorway and said *my daughters are no one, we are no one, look at us, we are nothing*—and Sela, Sela was sixteen and she had her mother's pride and none of her sense, and she was wearing a luck-knot at her wrist. A little one. A *harmless* one. The kind you sell in the Nightmarket for two coppers.

"And one of the black coats saw it. And he smiled. Oskar, I have seen a lot of smiles in my life, I have smiled a lot of smiles myself, and I have never seen a smile like that. He took her wrist. He looked at the knot. And he said, *This is a binding. Bindings are forbidden.* And Sela said, *It's a knot. My mother tied it.* And he said—" Oskar's voice failed, and he had to stop, and Mara's hand came over his, cold from the cave air and steady as bedrock, and he held on to it like he'd held on to the rope. "He said, *Then your mother tied a crime.*

"They didn't take her to a cell. They didn't take her anywhere. They did it right there, in the doorway, in front of my mother, in front of me. One of them had a— a thing. A rod, a sigil-rod, a piece of guild-work turned inside out, I don't know what it was, I only know what it did. He pressed it to her wrist, to the knot, and he said some words, and Sela screamed, and then she stopped screaming, and she was never right again. They didn't kill her. Killing would have been *mercy*, and mercy wasn't the point. The point was the knot. The point was the lesson. They unmade her luck, Mara. They reached into her and they turned her fortune inside out, and after that day, everything that could go wrong for Sela Vey, went wrong. She fell down stairs. She cut herself on nothing. Infections took root in scratches. The house caught fire twice. Once a cart horse kicked her for no reason and broke her arm in two places. And she wasted, the way people waste, gray and thin and frightened of her own shadow, because the world had become a place that was *for* her, and she knew it, in her bones, the way you know weather.

"And I took her to you. Because I'd heard—everyone in the Salt Quarter had heard—there was a charm-seller in the Nightmarket who was more than she seemed. And you looked at her wrist, at the scar the rod left, and you didn't ask what happened. You just—" His throat closed. "You just worked. Four hours. I watched you spend something I could see you couldn't afford, some piece of yourself, and when it was done, Sela slept. Really slept. For the first time in two years. And she woke up hungry. And she *stayed* well. You didn't just treat it. You *unmade* what they did. And I never asked how, because asking how was a door I couldn't afford to open, and I had a sister to keep alive, and doors are for people with nothing to lose."

The fire had burned low. Mara's hand was still on his. She was crying, silently, the way she seemed to do everything—without waste, without display—and she didn't wipe her face, and he was grateful, because if she'd wiped it, he'd have had to acknowledge it and he was at the absolute limit of what he could acknowledge.

"You never told me," she said finally. Her voice was rough. "Eleven years, Oskar. You let me think it was a fever."

"A fever was safer. For you. For her. For me." He laughed, and it came out broken. "And because if I told you what they did—if I told you it was the *Crown* that cursed my sister, the same Crown you've been hiding from for twelve years—then it wouldn't be a debt anymore, would it? A debt you pay off. A debt has an end. But what I felt when I watched you work on her, and what I've felt every day since—" He shook his head. "That's not a debt. Debts are for strangers. I just didn't have a word for it. I still don't. But I know it isn't a debt, and I'm tired of pretending it is."

Mara was quiet for a long time. The wind breathed at the cave mouth. Liss, wrapped in her blanket by the fire, was asleep—Oskar didn't know when that had happened, but the girl could sleep through anything, a skill he both envied and mourned—and Ferro was a silhouette against the stars, and Oskar sat in the small circle of firelight with a woman he'd have died for today and would die for tomorrow, and found that saying the truth had not, in fact, killed him. The mountain had tried. The truth had been easier.

"Sela," Mara said at last. "Is she—"

"Married. Two children. A boy and a girl." He smiled, and it hurt his face, because he wasn't used to it. "The girl ties knots. Little ones. Harmless ones. Sela pretends not to see, and I pretend not to see her pretending, and we're all very good at it." He looked at Mara. "When this is over—if this is over, if we live—take me to Vharen's south gate, and I'll show you a house with blue shutters, and you can see what you bought with four hours of your life. Because that's what it cost you. I watched it cost you. And I never said."

"You just said."

"I just said."

Mara squeezed his hand, once, hard, and let go, and looked into the fire, and Oskar watched her profile in the flickering light and understood that he had just done the bravest thing he'd ever done, braver than the rope, braver than the ledge, and that the mountain had nothing on a woman's quiet face in firelight.

"Get some sleep," she said softly. "We cross the high shoulder tomorrow. And Oskar—" she paused, and something moved through her expression, grief and resolve tangled together like thread, "—what they did to Sela. What the Crown did. It's the same thing the Binding does to the whole kingdom. The same hand. The same theft. I've felt it since the villages. And I think—" She stopped herself, and shook her head. "I think the map is taking us somewhere that answer lives."

He wanted to ask what she meant. But she was already moving, already tucking the blanket closer around Liss's shoulders, already the keeper of everyone's rest, and Oskar lay back against the cold stone and pulled his coat around himself and looked up at the slice of sky above the overhang, where the stars wheeled, indifferent and vast.

He was nearly asleep when he heard it—a sound from the dark beyond the cave mouth, from the direction of the pass they'd crossed and the road they'd left behind. Ferro heard it too; Oskar saw him stiffen, hand drifting toward his blade.

Hoofbeats. Many of them. Coming up the mountain road.

And beneath the hoofbeats, threading through the wind, a sound Oskar had heard only once before in his life, on a doorway in the Salt Quarter eleven years ago, and had never forgotten: the thin, high, harmonic whine of a sigil-rod charging.

Mara's eyes were open. She'd heard it too. In the firelight her face had gone very still, and her hand had found Liss's shoulder, and her voice, when it came, was barely a breath:

"They found us."
