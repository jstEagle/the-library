## 16. Letters from a Dead Master

The shrine had no name that any of them knew. It crouched at the bend of the pilgrim road where the mountain first showed its teeth—a squat stone building with a sagging roof of slate, half-swallowed by ivy, its door hanging on one hinge like a drunk leaning on a friend. Whoever had tended it last had left decades ago; the offering niche held nothing but rainwater and a bird's nest, and the little god carved above the lintel had been worn faceless by weather, so that it smiled at everyone and no one.

Mara stood in the doorway while Oskar carried Liss inside, the girl still boneless with sleep against his shoulder. Ferro took the rear, boots crunching on frost-brittle grass, one hand never far from the short blade beneath his coat even now, even here. The sky was doing that thing mountain skies did in late autumn—piling up gray wool in the west, promising snow without committing to it.

"It'll hold for a night," Mara said. "Stone remembers warmth longer than we do."

"Stone's never slept on this floor," Oskar muttered, but he laid Liss down gently on his rolled cloak all the same, tucking the edges around her with a care he'd deny under torture.

Inside smelled of cold ash and old incense, a ghost of myrrh gone sour. There was a hearth, mercifully, and dry wood stacked by some long-dead pilgrim who'd believed in courtesy to strangers. Mara knelt and built a small fire while Ferro checked the back wall, the single narrow window, the corners where shadows pooled. He did everything quietly, methodically, the way a man does when checking corners has become the shape of his whole life.

"You keep waiting for them to be behind us," she said.

"I keep being right about worse things." He settled against the wall opposite the door, equidistant from every exit, and she understood it wasn't distrust—it was habit worn into devotion. A man who had spent years making himself the Crown's instrument didn't stop listening for its voice just because he'd started doubting the mouth it spoke from.

Liss slept through the fire-building, through Oskar's grumbling, through the wind finding the gap in the shutters and worrying it like a dog with a bone. Only when the room had warmed did the girl stir, blink at the strange ceiling, and find Mara's face in the firelight with an expression of such naked relief that something in Mara's chest turned over and lay still.

"You're here," Liss said, as if confirming a fact she'd been afraid to trust.

"I'm here."

"The bad dream didn't come back. Not yet." She said it carefully, the way children handle fragile things—testing weight before trusting. Since the waystation, since the fire and the wagon and the parents who hadn't come out, the nightmares came most nights. Mara had given her a knot-charm for sleep, a simple thing, three loops and a binding turn, nothing that would register as sigilcraft even to a trained eye. Craft, not magic. The distinction that had kept Mara alive for twelve years.

"Eat something," Mara said. "Then sleep again if you can."

They ate hard bread and dried plums and a wedge of pale cheese Oskar produced from somewhere in his coat with the smugness of a conjurer. Ferro refused food until Oskar shoved it at him and said, "You're no good to us fainting," which was, Mara suspected, the closest thing to affection Oskar Vey had offered anyone in years.

Later, when Liss had gone down again and the men sat in the low orange light speaking in the shorthand of people who've shared danger—water, distance, the pass ahead, whether the gray guards tracked by scent or by something worse—Mara took out the map.

She had been avoiding it. She knew that now, sitting with it unrolled across her knees, the bloodstains on its western edge gone brown and stiff as old rust. For days she had consulted it only glancingly, reading the route and nothing else, the way you might read a letter from a dead man only as far as the directions and refuse the rest. But the shrine was quiet, and the fire was warm, and there is only so long a debt can go unexamined before it starts collecting interest.

She turned the map toward the light.

It was good work—the courier had been guild-trained, she could see that in the surveyor's precision, the contour lines rendered in the old style with elevation shown as fine hatching rather than the Crown's crude stippling. Mount Serel rose in the northeast corner, drawn with a care bordering on reverence, and beneath it, marked in faded ink, the symbol she'd known since childhood: a circle bisected by a descending line, the sign of the Hollow Vault.

Her eyes went, as they always went, to the seal stamped in red wax at the map's lower margin. Her master's mark. Aldric's mark: a key crossed with a quill, pressed deep enough to bite through to the wood of the table beneath, once, long ago, when the wax was fresh and the world still made sense.

Aldric Vane, who had walked into the Purge raid on the guildhouse twelve years ago and never walked out. Who had been declared dead by the three survivors who saw the inquisitors' fire take the east wing. Whose name Mara had spoken over a grave she'd dug empty, because grief needs somewhere to put its hands.

She was looking at the seal when she noticed the marginalia.

At first she took it for water damage—a stain along the bottom edge, a smudge near the compass rose. Then the firelight shifted, and the smudge resolved into strokes. Deliberate ones. Tiny writing, ink so diluted it was nearly invisible unless the light came at it sideways, written in a hand she knew better than her own.

Her breath stopped.

*The keybearer will come by the north road,* the tiny letters said, curving along the map's border beneath the mountains. *She will not come willingly. No one who buried what we buried comes willingly.*

Mara's hands were shaking. She made herself hold the map steady, made herself angle it to catch the firelight just so, and more words surfaced out of the paper like drowned things rising.

*If you are reading this, you have survived the courier, the road, and your own conscience, which is the hardest of the three. I am sorry for all of it. Especially the third.*

The handwriting slanted left. Aldric's handwriting. She would have known it blindfolded, by touch alone—he had taught her her letters, had guided her hand through her first binding-sigil when she was nine, his fingers warm and steady over hers, saying *pressure, little wren, pressure is everything; too much and you bruise the meaning.* Twelve years, and her body still remembered the exact weight of his hand.

But the ink was wrong. Or rather—the age of it was wrong. Mara had spent her adult life working with inks, dyes, fixatives; she could date a document by its oxidation the way a midwife dates a pregnancy. And this ink, this faint ghost-gray script hiding in the margins of a map that had traveled sealed and secret for—

For how long?

She looked at the courier's bloodstains. Brown. Old. Months at least, maybe a year. And the marginalia lay *over* the route lines but *under* the worst of the staining in places, layered like sediment. Which meant the writing had been done after the map was drawn but before—before the courier bled on it, before he died pressing it into her hands, before the word *keybearer* turned her life inside out.

And the ink itself, the particular gray-brown of iron-gall gone soft with years—that was not months old. That was years. Many years.

*Aldric died twelve years ago,* said the part of her mind that had dug his empty grave, that had mourned him with a completeness she'd built her whole quiet life upon. *Dead men don't annotate maps.*

*Then whose hand is this?* asked the other part, the nine-year-old who still felt warm fingers guiding hers, and knew the answer the way you know your own name.

She read on. The words grew denser toward the map's eastern edge, crowded along the river, climbing the contours of Serel itself, as though the writer had run out of margin and begun writing over the very geography, his warnings laid across the mountains like a second range.

*They will tell you I am dead. I let them believe it, because a dead man's seals cannot be broken and a living man's can be tortured open. Do not hate me for the grave you dug. Hate me, if you must, for what comes next.*

Mara made a sound. Small, involuntary. Oskar glanced over. "What?"

"Nothing. Reading." Her voice came out level, which surprised her, because inside she was a house with every window breaking at once.

Ferro's eyes lingered on her a moment too long. She turned her shoulder, hunching over the map like a woman shielding a candleflame.

*There is a thing in the palace,* the margins continued. *You may already know. You may have always known, the way one knows a limb has gone numb—abstractly, unwillingly. It wears the king. It eats what the Binding feeds it, and the Binding feeds it everything: luck, health, years, names. I have watched it happen from the dark side of a locked door for longer than I can bear to count.*

*I warned the others. I begged the council to unmake the anchor before it learned to drink deeper. They called me a heretic to my own craft. So I did what cowards and saints do—I ran, and I hid, and I kept the outer seals alive with these two hands, alone, year after year, because someone had to and no one else remembered how.*

*But seals are not enough anymore. The Vault needs a keybearer. It has always needed a keybearer. And there is only one left.*

Mara stopped reading.

She set the map down on her knees, flat, and stared at the fire, and breathed, because the alternative was not breathing. The flames moved. Somewhere outside, wind worried the shutter. Liss murmured in her sleep and turned toward the warmth, and Oskar, without looking, reached over and adjusted the cloak at her shoulder.

*There is only one left.*

Her. The youngest sworn member of the Almeida line of the Sigilwrights' Guild, sworn at sixteen in a cellar that burned four years later, the only one who slipped the net—not through courage but through a delivery errand, through dumb luck, through the ordinary miracle of having been sent out for ink. She had spent twelve years telling herself survival was accident. Now a dead man's handwriting told her it was design, or destiny, or bait.

Because there was a third possibility, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone:

*He knew I'd survive. He counted on it. He raised me for this.*

The thought arrived with its twin, inseparable, conjoined: *And what else did he plan?*

She forced herself back to the margins. Near the Vault's mark, the writing changed character—tighter, faster, scratched rather than written, the hand of a man running out of time or light or both:

*One more thing, and then I will let you sleep, little wren, though I doubt you will. Someone else seeks the keybearer. Not the Crown—or not only the Crown. There are hands behind the hands, and they have wanted the Vault opened for a long time. Trust the lawman if his doubt is honest. Trust the smuggler if his debt is real. Trust no one who offers you certainty, least of all me.*

*If I could have spared you this, I would have burned the map myself. But the choice was never mine to make. It was always going to be yours. That is the cruelty of keys: they exist to be used, and only the lock knows for what.*

*Come to Serel. Come angry, come grieving, come armed with every question you have. I will answer what I can and deserve what I don't.*

*—A.V.*

*P.S. The knot you tied at nine, the one that unraveled the moment I let go—you finally have the tension right. I have watched you work from afar, in market stalls and shop windows, and I am prouder than any dead man has a right to be.*

Mara read the postscript twice. On the second reading, her eyes blurred, and she let them, briefly, letting the tiny letters swim and blur into gray water. Prouder than any dead man has a right to be. As if he'd been watching her—as if all these years, while she sold luck-knots and fever-charms and died by inches in a shop that smelled of beeswax and fear, he had been *watching*, seeing her, keeping count of her knots.

Hope hit her like a tide. It was shameful, how fast it came, how completely—twelve years of careful grief washed aside in a heartbeat by eight lines of faded ink. *He's alive. He's alive, he's alive, he waited for me, he kept the seals, he never stopped—*

And underneath the hope, coiled and patient as a snake in a granary, the dread:

*Or someone wants me to believe he is.*

The handwriting was perfect. That was the problem. Anyone who had studied Aldric's hand—inquisitors kept archives; the Purges had generated libraries of confiscated guildwork—could forge it. The postscript was almost *too* tender, too precisely aimed at the soft place where a grieving student keeps her master's memory. And the warning itself—"trust no one who offers you certainty"—was the kind of instruction that isolates a person beautifully, that cuts her away from every ally and leaves her walking alone into exactly the trap the writer designs.

She thought of the courier. Dying man, dying words, *keybearer*—but who had sent him? Who had bled him? A map does not walk to a charm-seller's door on its own. Someone had chosen her. Someone had known where she was, what she was, what she'd buried and what she still carried under her skin.

Her guild mark prickled. It had been prickling for days, ever since the road bent toward Serel—a heat beneath the tattooed lines on her inner wrist, faint as sunburn, growing steadier as the mountain neared. She'd told herself it was the altitude, the cold, imagination. Now she looked at it in the firelight: the old sigil of her line, a wolf circled by thread, and around its edges the skin flushed pink, as if the mark were waking after a long sleep.

*Responding to the map,* she thought. *Or responding to whatever wrote the map. Blood calls to blood. Or bait calls to the hooked fish.*

"Mara." Ferro's voice, low. "You've read that page six times and gone white as the salt road each time. What does it say?"

She weighed lying. It was her oldest skill—lying in small stitches, daily, invisibly, until the lie was indistinguishable from her face. But Ferro had stood in her shop and asked his polite questions and left without arresting her, and he had killed a gray guard and vomited after, and he had chosen, on the road, to walk beside them instead of behind them with irons. And Aldric's margins said *trust the lawman if his doubt is honest,* and she was so tired of carrying everything alone.

So she said, "My master is alive."

Oskar's head came up sharply. Even Liss stirred, blinking.

"Aldric Vane. My teacher. The man who trained me, who—" Her voice caught on the word and she pushed through it. "Who died in the Purges. Or who I buried, anyway. The map has his handwriting on it. Years of it. Warnings, instructions, an apology." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He's been alive this whole time. Keeping seals. Watching me, apparently. Leaving me love notes in the margins of a dead courier's map."

Silence. The fire popped. Oskar said, carefully, "That's either the best news we've had since the weir gate, or the worst."

"Yes," Mara said. "That's precisely the problem."

Ferro leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his tired face into planes and shadow. "Forgive the question. But dead masters who leave annotated maps are a classic lure. I've seen it used twice—once on a smuggler ring, once on a cell of unlicensed healers. Bait shaped like hope works better than any chain." He paused. "I'm not saying it *is* bait. I'm saying I can't tell from here, and neither can you, and that should frighten us both."

"It frightens me plenty," Oskar said. "Question is what we do about it. Turn back? We're three days from the pass. Four from the Vault, maybe, if the weather holds and the Teeth don't eat us."

"And if it's true?" Liss said.

They all looked at her. She was sitting up now, hair sleep-tangled, eyes huge in the firelight, and she said it again, simply, the way children cut through the knots adults tie themselves into: "If it's true—if he really kept the seals all alone for twelve years—then he's been waiting for help for twelve years. And nobody came." Her chin trembled, but her voice held. "Nobody came except us."

Mara felt those words land somewhere below her ribs and stay there.

"He also wrote," she said slowly, "that someone else is hunting the keybearer. Hands behind the hands. And that I should trust no one who offers certainty—including him." She looked around the fire, at the smuggler and the inquisitor and the child, this absurd, impossible family the road had assembled out of debts and damage. "Which means the only person I can fully trust is a dead man's doubts. Welcome to sigilcraft."

"Glorious," Oskar said. "I left a lucrative smuggling career for this."

"You left a smuggling career because your sister would have died otherwise, and because you secretly can't stand the idea of us managing without you."

"That's slander."

"It's in the ledger of things I know about you."

He grumbled, but he banked the fire higher, and when he thought no one was looking, he moved his bedroll closer to Liss's side of the hearth.

Mara sat apart with the map while the others settled. The shrine settled into night sounds—wind, settling stone, the tick of cooling slate. She held the margins up to the last of the light one final time, and found what she had missed before, hidden in the hatch-work of the mountain's northern face, so small she'd taken it for a surveyor's flaw:

A single line, in Aldric's hand, newer than the rest—the ink darker, the pressure firmer, written recently, perhaps within the year:

*The mark on your wrist will burn as you climb. Let it. It is remembering the way home. But Mara—little wren—if the burning stops, if the mark goes cold at the threshold, do not enter. Whatever greets you wearing my voice, check its shadow first. Mine will be long. His will not.*

She pressed her thumb against the words, as if touch could verify them, as if ink had a pulse.

Outside, far off in the direction of the pass, something howled—long, low, winding down into a silence that felt less like ending than like listening.

Mara rolled the map closed with hands that were not quite steady, and understood that whatever waited beneath Mount Serel, she was no longer walking toward a ruin. She was walking toward a reunion—and reunions, in her experience, were just ambushes that had learned patience.

The mark on her wrist pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat.

She did not sleep that night.
