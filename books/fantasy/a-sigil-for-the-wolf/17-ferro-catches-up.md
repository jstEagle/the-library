## 17. Ferro Catches Up

The shrine of Saint Vessia had been a wayhouse once, before the Purges emptied the roads and the roads themselves went to seed. Now it was a stone shell with a sagging roof, half-swallowed by pine, its painted saint long since flaked down to a ghost of blue and gold on the altar wall. Dain Ferro had sheltered in places like it on a dozen cold assignments, and he knew their grammar: one door, two windows, a back wall that would crumble if you leaned on it wrong. He knew, too, that anyone inside could hear him coming from fifty paces if they were worth running from.

So he didn't try to be quiet.

He walked up the overgrown path with his hands visible and his sword slung low on his hip, boots crunching frost-killed bracken, and he let the shrine's broken bell—still hanging by one rusted pin above the door—announce him with a single cracked note when he shouldered the door open.

"Almeida," he said, into the dark. "It's Ferro. I'm alone."

The firelight inside shifted. Three shadows moved against the walls: the smuggler, big and loose-limbed, drifting toward the door with the unhurried menace of a man who'd killed people for less than this; the girl, small and quick, pressing herself behind a stack of rotted pews; and Mara Almeida, standing very still beside the fire with her hands open at her sides, as though she'd been expecting him for hours and had only been waiting for the courtesy of an introduction.

"You're not alone," she said. "You're never alone. You carry the Crown in your pocket like a stone."

"The Crown doesn't know where I am." He kept his voice level. It was true, and truth had a sound that trained liars recognized. "I lost my escort at the ford two days back. Deliberately. If you want to check the road behind me, Vey can do it—he looks like a man who's good at looking at roads."

Oskar Vey had reached the doorway. He filled most of it, arms crossed, and looked Ferro over with the flat appraisal of a man pricing a horse he intended to buy cheap or not at all. "Escort," Oskar said. "Inquisitors travel with escorts now?"

"Inquisitors who matter do." Ferro held out his hands, palms up, letting the firelight show them empty. "I don't matter anymore. As of four days ago I'm a name on a list. Chancellor Rell wants the keybearer found, and I was the last officer seen near Nightmarket before the courier died. Do the arithmetic."

"I did," Mara said quietly. "Days ago. That's why we moved twice since the waystation."

Something in her tone made him look at her properly. She was thinner than she'd been in her shop, he thought—or maybe he'd simply never really looked, and what he'd taken for a plump, harmless trinket-seller had been a woman wearing harmless like a coat. There was ash on her sleeves and a new scar on her knuckles, and her eyes had the particular stillness of someone who had recently watched people die and had decided, provisionally, to keep going anyway.

"The family in the wagon," he said. "At the waystation fire. That wasn't my doing. I want that understood before anything else happens tonight."

"I know whose doing it was." Her voice didn't rise. "Yours was the outriders. Six of them, gray cloaks, no badges. They burned a family alive over a map, Inquisitor. One of your colleagues put a torch to a child's blanket."

"And I've spent every night since reading their after-action report and finding it full of holes." He took a slow breath. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the road, walking alone through the dark with his breath smoking, turning the words over until they stopped sounding like treason and started sounding like duty—which was a lie, but a necessary one, the kind he'd told himself his whole career. "I'm not here to arrest you. I'm here because I think we've all been chasing pieces of the same corpse."

* * *

They let him come as far as the fire, which was more than he'd expected. Oskar sat behind him—not beside, behind—with a skinning knife he made no effort to conceal, working it idly along a whetstone in a rhythm designed to be heard. The girl, Liss, stayed by the far wall, wrapped in a man's coat too big for her, watching Ferro with the flat, unblinking stare of a child who had already seen the worst thing and was waiting to see if adults would pretend otherwise.

Mara sat across the fire from him, knees drawn up, and said nothing for a long time. Letting the silence work. He recognized the technique; he used it himself. Two professionals circling, each waiting for the other to spend something first.

He spent first. He'd decided that on the road, too. Whoever showed their hand second at a table like this was showing it to an enemy.

He unslung his satchel, and felt Oskar's knife-hand go still, and took from it a bundle of oilcloth that he unwrapped with the care of a man handling relics. Inside: three items, laid out on the stone between them.

A ring of keys, blackened, fused together as though they'd melted and refrozen mid-shape.

A soldier's identity tag, stamped with the sigil of the palace guard, bitten at the edge as though by teeth.

And a hand, or what remained of one—a gray, waxy thing, dried to leather, five fingers curled around nothing.

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the mountain breathing.

"Three weeks ago," Ferro said, "a palace guardsman named Corren Hale failed to report for duty. His wife reported him missing. I pulled the file because his barracks were two streets from the courier's route and I was grasping at straws." He turned the identity tag over with one finger. "Two days later I found Hale's effects in a drainage culvert under the Processional Way. No body. Just these. Except the hand isn't decayed—it's *cured*, like meat. And the keys—" he lifted the fused ring, let firelight run along the metal, "—were in his fist. They'd been in a forge fire hot enough to slag iron, and there's no forge within a mile of that culvert, and Hale walked into that culvert carrying them at body heat."

"You think the palace did it," Oskar said. Not a question. A bored voice, almost. But the whetstone had stopped moving entirely.

"I think the palace is *doing* things, plural, and has been for years, and I was too good a dog to smell it." Ferro kept his eyes on Mara. "Six months ago I requested access to the king's private apartments to follow a theft inquiry. Denied. Four months ago I requested a routine health audit of the royal household—the kind done every reign, the kind nobody thinks about. Denied, personally, by Chancellor Rell, with a note saying the king's health was 'not a matter within the Inquisition's remit.' I have served the Crown nineteen years. The king's health has always been within our remit. It is practically the *definition* of our remit."

He paused. The next part was the part he'd turned over longest on the road, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things couldn't be unsaid.

"So I went over his head. I got myself assigned to a honor detail—three days' duty inside the palace, close enough to see him." He laughed, short and without humor. "You know what I saw? I saw the King of Vharen hold audience for six hours and say the same sentence eleven times. 'The harvest pleases me. The harvest pleases me. The harvest pleases me.' Eleven times, same cadence, same pause, same little smile, like a music box with a cracked cylinder. And every courtier in that room bowed and agreed, and not one of them blinked, and I stood there with my spine crawling telling myself I'd imagined it."

Liss made a small sound. Everyone looked at her. She'd gone pale, and her hands were fists in the oversized coat.

"That's him," she whispered. "That's the man in my dream. The one with the smile that doesn't move."

Ferro stared at her. "Your dream."

"She dreams about the Vault," Mara said softly. "She's been dreaming about it since the waystation. Don't ask me how. I don't know yet." But her eyes, when they came back to Ferro, had changed—had gone from assessment to something hungrier and warier both. "Say the rest. Say why you came to *us* and not to your own order."

"Because my own order is the thing I need protection from." He said it flatly, and heard how it sounded, and said it again anyway. "After the honor detail I filed a report. Full protocol. Sealed, direct to the Lord Inquisitor, bypassing Rell's chancellery. Three days later the report came back to me—returned unread, with orders reassigning me to border census work. Census work. Counting goats in the Serevane hills. And then the courier died in your shop, and Rell himself summoned me—you saw the summons—and told me the keybearer was 'an existential threat to the continuity of the realm,' and offered me a promotion to hunt you." He shook his head slowly. "An existential threat. From a woman who sells fever-charms for two coppers. I've hunted heretics for nineteen years, Almeida. I know what an existential threat looks like. It looks like a man who says the harvest pleases me eleven times and a chancellor who won't let doctors near him."

The fire crackled. Oskar's knife resumed its slow whisper against the stone.

"So no," Ferro finished. "I'm not here to arrest you. I'm here because everything I've believed for nineteen years just died in a drainage culvert under the Processional Way, and the only people alive who might understand what killed it are a smuggler, a fugitive, and a child who dreams true. I'm asking you what you know. That's all. Ask me whatever you need to ask first. I'd insist on it, if I were you."

* * *

For a long moment Mara didn't move. Then she rose, crossed to her pack, and knelt with her back to him, and Ferro felt Oskar shift behind him—felt the whole room's weight tilt—and forced himself to stay exactly where he was, hands loose, breathing even. Trust, he was learning, was mostly the discipline of not flinching.

When she turned around she was holding a spool of coarse red thread and a small bone needle, and her face had gone carefully blank in a way he recognized from interrogations: the face of someone about to do something irreversible.

"There's an old guild practice," she said. "Older than the Purges. Older than the guild itself, maybe. When two strangers needed to speak truths to each other—merchants sealing a contract, captains swearing parley—we had a rite for it. We called it the red thread." She wound a length of thread around her fingers as she talked, quick and precise, and Ferro saw the knots take shape with a speed that had nothing amateurish in it at all: loop, cross, cinch, loop. "It's not compulsion. I want to be clear about that, because inquisitors are taught that all magic is compulsion. It can't make you say anything. All it does is... listen. If you speak a lie while wearing it, the knot tightens. That's all. Tightens, and keeps tightening, until it draws blood. The old captains called it 'honest jewelry.'"

"And if I refuse?" Ferro asked.

"Then Oskar puts you out in the snow, and we break camp tonight, and you spend the rest of your career chasing goats and wondering." She met his eyes across the fire. "I don't want that, Inquisitor. I've read your file—I read it years ago, when you were still rising, because a shopkeeper in the Nightmarket learns which wolves patrol which streets. You closed the Kettering case without a single false confession. You resigned from the Purge tribunal rather than sign the Vellamont order. Nineteen years, and those two entries are the only reason you're still breathing instead of bleeding in my shop like the courier. But reasons aren't proof. Reasons are just stories I tell myself about wolves."

"Wolves," Ferro repeated. "Is that what I am to you?"

"You're the Crown's wolf," she said simply. "So am I, if the stories are true. The difference is I've been chewing off my own leg for twelve years to get out of the trap. Tonight I find out if you're trying to chew off yours." She held out the red thread, coiled in her palm, across the fire. "Wear it. Answer three questions. Then I'll answer yours—all of them, everything I know about the map, the Vault, the binding, all of it. And if the thread stays slack, Oskar walks you to the door and you walk away, and none of us ever speaks of this."

Behind him, Oskar snorted. "And if it tightens?"

"Then it tightens," Mara said, not taking her eyes off Ferro, "and we learn something too."

Ferro looked at the thread. Coarse wool, dyed madder-red, nothing remarkable about it except the knots, which his eye kept snagging on the way certain patterns snag—an arrangement that seemed to fold inward no matter how he looked at it, so that counting the loops was like counting reflections between two mirrors. Nineteen years of doctrine said burn it, arrest her, send for the outriders. Nineteen years of doctrine had also said the king's health was within the Inquisition's remit, and the harvest pleased His Majesty eleven times.

He rolled up his sleeve and held out his forearm.

"Ask," he said.

Mara tied the thread herself. Her fingers were cool and quick against his skin, and the knots settled against his pulse point with a strange, faint warmth, like a living thing adjusting to a new perch. Then she sat back, folded her hands, and became very still.

"First question," she said. "Did you know, before tonight, that the courier who died in my shop existed?"

"No."

The thread lay slack. Warm. Silent.

"Second question. Have you communicated with Chancellor Rell, or any agent of the Crown, since you left the city?"

"No." A beat, and then, because the thread hadn't tightened and honesty was suddenly the easiest thing in the world, the luxury he'd been starving for: "I burned the summons. I burned my seal. As far as the Crown knows, Dain Ferro fell off a ferry at the Serevane crossing. There's probably a file being opened on my drowning right now."

"Third question." Mara leaned forward, and the firelight made her eyes very bright. "If it comes to a choice between the Crown and the truth—which do you serve?"

Ferro was quiet for a moment. He looked at the fire, and at the girl in the man's coat who dreamed of smiling kings, and at the smuggler with the knife, and at the fused keys and the cured hand lying on the stone between them like evidence in a trial that had never been convened.

"Nineteen years ago I swore an oath to protect the realm of Vharen," he said. "Not the Crown. The *realm*. The people in it. I told myself the two were the same thing for a long time." He raised his eyes to hers. "The realm. Whatever's left of it. Even from itself."

The thread lay slack against his pulse. Slack and warm and quiet, honest jewelry, and Mara Almeida looked at it for a long moment, and then she did something he did not expect at all.

She laughed—a short, cracked, exhausted laugh, the laugh of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy—and rubbed both hands over her face.

"Aldric used to say that," she said, half to herself. "Almost word for word. 'We serve the realm, not the crown.' First oath of the guild. Gods, you sound like him." She dropped her hands and looked at Ferro across the fire, and something in her expression had shifted—some gate, guarded for twelve years, swinging a few inches on rusted hinges. "All right, Inquisitor. You passed. Ask your questions."

* * *

He asked them all night. Or it felt like all night; the moon crossed the broken roof and slid down the other side of the sky while the fire was fed and fed again, and the answers came out of Mara in a slow, reluctant tide—the guild, the sigils, the hiding, the courier, the map, Mount Serel, the vault beneath it. Oskar added pieces in his dry, gravelly way, mostly corrections and complaints, and Liss crept closer to the fire as the hours wore on until she was sitting between them, wrapped in her too-big coat, listening with the terrible attentiveness of children who have learned that the world runs on secrets.

But it was Ferro's own knowledge that made Mara go white.

When he described the gray guards—the ones without reflections, without pulse, the one he'd killed in the capital and the thing that had poured out of it—her hands went to her mouth. When he described the hollow king, the music-box sentence, the eleven repetitions, Liss began quietly to cry, without any change in her expression, tears simply falling as though they belonged to someone else. And when he laid out the arithmetic of it—twelve years, the blighted villages, the gray-eyed children on the mountain road, the luck draining out of Vharen like blood from a wound—Mara stood up abruptly and walked to the shrine's broken window and stood there with her back to all of them, shoulders rigid, looking out at the pines and the mountain beyond them, black against the stars.

"Twelve years," she said finally, to the glass, to the mountain, to no one. "Twelve years I hid. Twelve years I told myself staying invisible was staying safe, that neutral was innocent, that if I just kept my head down and sold my little charms the world would leave the craft alone and the craft would leave the world alone." Her voice caught. "And the whole time, something was feeding on the kingdom. Something was wearing the king. And the guild—the guild that *built* that thing, that anchored it—" She pressed her forehead briefly against the cold stone. "My master knew. Didn't he. Aldric knew. That's what the map is. That's why the courier found *me*."

"He's alive," Ferro said. It wasn't a guess; he'd assembled it on the road, from the shape of her silences. "Your master. The one who vanished in the Purges. The map bears his seal, and seals don't survive twelve years unless someone's maintaining them."

Mara turned from the window. In the firelight her face was wet, but her jaw was set, and Ferro saw in it, clearly, the woman she must have been at fifteen—sworn youngest member of a murdered guild, carrying its last living magic out of the flames in her own two hands.

"Then we're past time," she said. "Every day we spend arguing is a day the thing in the palace gets stronger and the king—if there's any of the king left—gets weaker. We leave at first light. All of us." She looked around the circle: Oskar, scowling; Liss, tear-streaked and steady-eyed; Ferro, the Crown's wolf with a red thread still knotted at his pulse. "Including you, Inquisitor. You know the palace. You know Rell. You know how the order thinks, moves, and lies. If we're going to unmake whatever's sitting on that throne, I need a man who's spent nineteen years learning how the trap is built."

"Ferro," Oskar said warningly, from behind him.

"Vey." Mara, without heat. "He wears the thread. He sleeps outside the fire line tonight if it makes you happy. But he comes."

Oskar muttered something about sheep and wolves and the general ingratitude of the universe, but Ferro noticed he didn't argue twice.

Ferro looked down at his own forearm, at the red thread lying slack against his skin, and thought about oaths—the one he'd sworn at nineteen, the one he'd just sworn at the fire, the one he was about to swear now, silently, to a fugitive, a smuggler, and a dreaming child, in a ruined shrine under a mountain that ate kingdoms.

"One condition," he said.

Mara raised an eyebrow.

"When this is done—whatever's done—there's a widow in the capital named Sera Hale. Corren Hale's wife. She deserves to know what happened to her husband, and it can't come from a file." He met Mara's eyes. "I carry his tag. When we finish, I give it to her myself, and I tell her the truth. However ugly."

Mara studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and something in her face said the answer had cost her nothing and gained her everything.

"First light," she said.

But first light was still hours away, and it was Liss—small, sleepless, wrapped in a dead man's coat—who broke the silence last, in a voice so quiet the fire nearly swallowed it.

"Mara," she said. "In my dream last night, the Vault door was open. And there was someone standing in it. An old man. And he was smiling at us." She hugged her knees. "But the smile was wrong. Like the king's."

No one spoke. Outside, the wind turned, and came down off Mount Serel carrying, or so it seemed to every soul in that shrine, a smell like hot metal and old blood.

Mara looked at the map spread open on the stone, at her master's faded seal, and Ferro watched her reach out and touch it, gently, the way one touches a wound to learn whether it still hurts.

"We leave now," she said.
