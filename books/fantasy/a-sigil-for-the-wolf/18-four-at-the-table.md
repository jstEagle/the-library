## 18. Four at the Table

The shrine had a hearth that still drew, which was the only miracle left in it.

Oskar Vey had seen a hundred shrines in his years running salt and silk and other people's secrets up and down the Serel road, and most of them were the same: roof half-gone, saints' faces chiseled to blankness by some pious vandal with a mason's hammer, bird droppings on the altar like candle wax gone wrong. This one had been a waystation once, before the Purges made the mountain roads unfashionable, and whoever had built it had known what they were doing. The chimney pulled smoke straight up through a gap in the slates. The floor was flagstone, swept clean by wind. There was even an iron hook for a kettle, black with age but sound.

He hung the kettle on it now and told himself the warmth in his chest was from the fire.

Outside, the last light was going off the peaks like a lamp being carried away down a long corridor. Snow on the high shoulders of Mount Serel caught the red of it and held on stubbornly, the way snow does, as if it knew something the sky didn't. Somewhere below them, back down the switchbacks, the inquisitor's horse stood hobbled next to Oskar's own mule, and the two animals had apparently decided to be friends about it, which was more than could be said for their owners.

Ferro sat against the far wall with his sword across his knees—not drawn, not sheathed either, just *there*, the way a man sits when he's spent twenty years reaching for it. He'd taken off his gray coat and folded it under him, and without it he looked smaller. Older. A big man going soft at the edges, with a face like a door someone had forgotten to lock.

Mara was across the fire from Oskar, cross-legged, turning her hands over the flames. Her guild mark—the wolf's-head knot inked inside her left wrist—was still faintly pink from where it had flared on the pass. She hadn't spoken much since Ferro walked into the shrine with his hands open and his proof spread out on the altar stone: the sketch of a gray guard's face, the description of a man with no reflection, no pulse, no breath fogging in cold air.

Liss was asleep. Or pretending to be. She'd curled up on the bench behind Mara with the girl's shawl—Mara's shawl, really; everything Liss owned now had been Mara's first—pulled up over her ears, and every so one of her eyes would open a crack and track the conversation like a cat watching a bird it wasn't supposed to want.

Oskar set out the food because that was what he did. Dried fish from the last village, hard cheese going soft in the warmth, the flatbread he'd baked on a stone that morning because apparently he'd become the kind of man who baked bread for fugitives. He cut it into quarters with his belt knife and handed pieces around the circle without asking, and everyone took theirs without thanking him, and that was its own kind of language too.

The kettle began its low mutter toward boiling.

"So," Oskar said, because somebody had to say it, and it was never going to be the inquisitor, who treated words like coins he might be taxed on later. "We're all thinking it. I'll say it."

"Don't," Mara said quietly.

"Someone has to." He pointed the knife at the wall, in the general direction of Vharen, three days' ride behind them and a world away. "There's a city back there. There are ships back there. There is a whole ocean full of places where nobody's ever heard of sigils or keybearers or whatever we're calling ourselves this week. We could be in Bravone by the new moon. I know a woman in Bravone who owes me—" he caught Mara's look, "—*likes* me, and she doesn't ask questions. Four tickets out. Done. Over. Alive."

"You'd leave your sister's grave two days' ride from here?" Mara asked.

It was a low blow and she knew it. Oskar's jaw worked. Senna was buried in the churchyard at Coldharbor, up this same road, under a stone he'd paid a stonemason good money to carve and then never once visited sober. That was the trouble with knowing a smuggler: you learned where all the soft spots were, and then you went and used them, because you loved him.

"That's not fair," he said.

"No," Mara admitted. "It isn't. But nothing tonight is going to be fair, so we may as well start there."

Ferro cleared his throat. It sounded like a cart starting uphill. "For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth, coming from me—I can't go back either. Not anymore." He turned his hands palm-up, and Oskar saw again the burn scars along the inquisitor's forearms, old ones, from training fires or worse. "I've filed reports for eleven years. Do you understand what that means? Every strange death, every missing child, every village that went quiet—I wrote it down, sealed it, sent it up the chain, and someone above me filed it somewhere dark and forgot it on purpose. I thought I was keeping order." His laugh was short and had no humor in it, just the shape of humor, like a glove with no hand. "I was keeping the lid on a pot. And the thing underneath has been eating this country the whole time I was proud of my penmanship."

"The king," Oskar said. "You actually believe it. The dead king. The thing wearing him."

"I've stood in front of it." Ferro said it simply, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Three years ago. Audience chamber, midwinter. It looked at me and said the same sentence eleven times. Word for word. Same pause between each. Like a clock that had learned to talk." He looked into the fire. "I told myself I'd imagined it. You tell yourself things, in that building. It's practically part of the uniform."

Silence, except for the kettle and the wind worrying the loose slate on the roof.

Oskar chewed his bread and hated every person at this fire individually and completely. That was the thing nobody warned you about found family—that it arrived exactly like a debt you never signed for. One day you were a free man with a boat and a ledger and no entanglements worth the name, and the next there was a witch who'd saved your sister teaching knots to an orphan girl, and a disgraced lawman sleeping ten feet away, and you were baking bread. *Bread.* He'd smuggled contraband past three navies. He'd talked his way out of a Caldris prison with nothing but a stolen ring and a better accent. And here he was, worried about whether the orphan was warm enough, whether the witch was eating enough, whether the lawman's soul could survive what he'd seen.

It was disgusting, honestly. He should write a pamphlet.

"Mara," he said. "Talk me through it. Not the speech. The actual arithmetic. What happens if we turn around?"

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Firelight moved on her face and made her look younger and older at once, the way grief does.

"If we turn around," she said finally, "the Binding keeps feeding. Whatever Aldric built—or whatever the guild built, and something else learned to drink from—it takes luck out of the land and life out of the small and puts it into keeping a dead man upright on a throne. The villages we passed. Gray-eyed children. Orchards that fruit every third year instead of every year. That's the interest. The principal comes due soon, and when it does—" she spread her hands, "—I don't know. Maybe the hollow thing falls apart in public and Rell starts a war to cover it. Maybe it doesn't fall apart, and it just keeps eating until there's nothing left but the crown and the table it sits on. Either way, Vharen dies slowly, and we live, and we know."

"And if we go on?"

"Then four people walk into a vault under a mountain, against the Chancellor of Vharen and every gray guard he can point at us, on the word of a map written by a man who may be mad or may be lying or both." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and bright as wire. "And maybe we fix it. Or maybe we die in the dark under Serel and nobody ever knows we tried."

"Well," Oskar said. "When you put it like that."

"It's honest arithmetic."

"It's suicide with better scenery."

"Yes," she agreed. "Often those are the same thing."

The kettle boiled. Oskar got up because his legs wanted something to do that wasn't running, and he made the tea—chickory and dried nettle, the road-blend, bitter enough to strip paint—and poured it into tin cups and handed them around, and the ritual of it steadied the room the way rituals do. Even Ferro took his cup with both hands like a man accepting communion.

That was when the bench creaked.

Liss sat up, shawl around her shoulders like a small gray ghost, hair flattened on one side, eyes enormous in the firelight. She'd heard everything. Of course she had. Children always heard everything; that was their whole profession.

"Liss," Mara started gently, "you should—"

"I vote we go on."

The words came out clear and flat, the way a stone drops into a well. Oskar felt them land in his chest.

"This isn't a vote," he said automatically.

"Everything's a vote. You just don't count mine." She slid off the bench and came to the fire and stood beside Mara, and Oskar watched the girl put her hand on the witch's shoulder—small fingers, chapped from the cold, steady as a rivet. "My parents died on this road because of the men who work for the man on the throne. Everyone in those gray-eyed villages is dying slow because of him too. If we turn around, I spend the rest of my life being the girl who ran away while other people's mothers burned." Her chin came up. Oskar had seen that chin before. On Mara. On the dock at Weir Gate, with a guard's baton coming down and her choosing, in half a heartbeat, to burn twelve years of hiding rather than let them take Oskar. "I'm not doing that. I'm going. You can all stay here and drink your terrible tea."

Nobody laughed. It was too true to laugh at.

"She's right," Ferro said, and he sounded surprised at himself, a man hearing his own verdict read aloud. "If I go back, I hang. If I run, I'm the man who knew and did nothing, and I've met that man. He lives in the palace. He files reports." He drank his tea like it was medicine, which it was. "I'm going."

All eyes came to Oskar.

He looked at the fire. He looked at his tea. He looked, briefly and hopelessly, at the door of the shrine, beyond which lay the whole wide indifferent world, the sea, the ships, the woman in Bravone who liked him, the entire unencumbered life he had constructed with such care out of salt water and bad decisions.

Then he looked at Mara, who wasn't looking at him at all, because she already knew. She was looking at Liss, with an expression Oskar had only ever seen on the faces of mothers in doorway at dawn, and it occurred to him—with the force of a boom swinging round and catching him across the head—that he was outnumbered. Three to one. Had been outnumbered since the waystation, probably. Since the dock. Since a fevered girl in a Vharen garret whose sister owed a debt she couldn't pay, and a smuggler who'd stayed one night too many, and kept staying, one night after another, until the nights added up to a life he hadn't chosen and couldn't now imagine giving back.

He was outnumbered by people he would die for, and the worst part, the truly obscene part, was that they'd die for him too, and none of them had ever put it in writing.

"Fine," Oskar said. "Fine. But I'm recording my objection for posterity." He fished the little oilskin notebook from inside his coat—the ledger, the one he'd started keeping somewhere on the road without admitting to himself why—and licked the pencil stub, and wrote, in his careful smuggler's script: *Day nineteen. Motion to abandon this madness, raised and defeated, three votes to one. The one being me. Note for posterity: the one was wrong.*

"What's that?" Liss asked, craning.

"A legal document," Oskar said. "Very official. When we're all dead in a hole under a mountain, historians will find this and say, well, at least one of them had sense."

"When we come out the other side," Liss corrected him, with the terrifying confidence of nine-year-olds everywhere, "you'll have to write that we were right."

"I will do no such thing."

"You'll write it."

"I'll consider writing it."

"You'll write it," Mara said, and there was laughter in her voice for the first time in days, thin and cracked and real, like ice going out on a river in spring, and Oskar closed the ledger because if he kept looking at her face he was going to say something unforgivably sincere, and a man had to keep some things.

They settled in for the night after that. Ferro took first watch, sitting by the door with his sword across his knees again, and Mara banked the fire, and Liss curled back onto the bench—but not before pressing a scrap of bread into Oskar's hand as she passed, solemnly, like a tithe, like payment for services rendered. He ate it. It was stale and it tasted like ash and it was the best meal he'd had in weeks.

He lay on his bedroll staring at the rafters and listened to the shrine breathe: wind in the slates, Ferro's boots shifting on the flagstones, Mara's slow even sleep-breath, Liss murmuring something in a dream—a knot-pattern, maybe, or a name. Oskar found himself counting them. Not days anymore. He'd stopped counting the days sometime on the Pass of Teeth, though he'd never have admitted it under torture, and torture had genuinely been attempted once, in Caldris, so he spoke from experience.

He was counting them now. Four. Four heartbeats in a ruined house of God on the flank of a cursed mountain, and every single one of them his problem.

*God help me,* he thought, with no particular faith behind it. *I'd burn a navy for these people.*

He slept, and dreamed of nothing, which was itself a kind of gift.

* * *

He woke to Mara shaking his shoulder in the blue hour before dawn, and her face in the half-light stopped his heart for one full beat.

Not fear. Something stranger. She was holding the map—the courier's map, bloodstained, wax-sealed, the thing this whole doomed pilgrimage orbited—and the seal was glowing. Not reflecting the coals. Glowing from inside, a deep arterial red, pulsing slow and steady, like a wound learning to keep time.

"It started an hour ago," she whispered. "It's never done this. Oskar—it's pulling. Toward the mountain." Her voice dropped further, and he saw the fear arrive now, sliding in under the wonder. "Something on the other end knows we're coming. And it's not waiting anymore."

Out in the dark, faint but unmistakable, carried down the wind off the high passes, something howled. It was not a wolf. Wolves, in Oskar's considerable experience, never sounded glad.
