## 19. The Palace Above the Clouds

The mountain did not let them sleep.

Ferro had learned that on the second night above the tree line—the way the wind came down the passes like something herding them, the way the stars seemed too close, too sharp, as if the sky itself were leaning in to watch what they carried toward it. He sat the last watch before dawn because he no longer trusted himself to dream.

Across the dead fire, Mara slept sitting up with Liss curled against her side, the girl's hand still knotted loosely in hers—a charm of Oskar's making, or Mara's, Ferro couldn't tell anymore which knots belonged to whom. The smuggler snored with the untroubled rhythm of a man who had made his peace with dying somewhere inconvenient. And Ferro counted the things he had not told them.

That Chancellor Rell had given him a sealed writ authorizing lethal force against the keybearer. That he had burned it two nights ago rather than carry it another mile. That three years ago, he had stood in the presence of the king of Vharen and watched him say the same sentence eleven times without blinking—and had told himself it was grief, and gone home, and slept.

He had been telling himself things for a long time now.

* * *

Three years ago, in the spring of the year the river froze late, Inquisitor Dain Ferro had been summoned to the Palace Above the Clouds for the first time in his career.

They called it that, the palace—though Vharen sat low among the fens and the harbor fog, and no cloud had ever touched its roofs. It was a joke the old courtiers made, or perhaps a boast: that the palace was so far above the common muck of the city that it might as well float. Ferro had always thought it was neither. He thought it was an apology. A building that needed to call itself higher than it was knew exactly how deep its foundations went.

He climbed the outer stair at the appointed hour, past guards in gray livery who did not look at him—not rudely, not with the studied contempt of palace sentinels, but *past* him, the way a man's eyes pass over furniture. Their skin was very clean. He would remember that later, though at the time it meant nothing: skin like wax polished by someone else's hand, faces that held their expressions the way a mask holds a face.

Inside, the halls smelled of beeswax and cold stone and, beneath it, faintly, of river water. That was wrong. He noted it the way he noted everything in those days, with the tidy diligence of a man who believed the world kept proper records. There was no river within a hundred feet of the audience hall. But the smell was there, green and patient, like something standing in water a long way below the floor.

A chamberlain with ink-stained cuffs led him through the Hall of Banners—every guild's sigil hanging in state, the sigilcrafters' wolf among them, though by then the wolf banner had hung empty-framed for nine years, the cloth burned in the Purges and never replaced; only the hook remained, and a pale rectangle on the stone where the fabric had shielded it from smoke—and into the smaller audience room where the king received petitioners of modest rank.

Dain Ferro, thirty-four years old, eleven years sworn to the Crown's inquisition, had killed two men and wept over both, and believed with the whole of his tired heart that the law was the only honest thing left in Vharen. He knelt when the chamberlain struck the floor with his staff.

"Rise, Inquisitor."

And the king said, "It is a fine morning for the harbor."

* * *

Ferro rose. The king was not looking at him.

He was looking out the tall window at the city below—the real city, the one that smelled of fish guts and tar and bread, the one whose noise reached even here, thinned to a murmur like surf. Aldous IV had been king for nineteen years then. Ferro remembered him from the coronation processions of his boyhood: a broad man with a sailor's rolling gait and a laugh that carried across a courtyard, a king who had once waded into a flood in the Weir district in his own boots and come out carrying a child under each arm. The people had loved him the way you love weather—unthinkingly, constantly, as part of the shape of the world.

The man at the window was thinner. That was all Ferro could have said, if anyone had asked him that day. Thinner, grayer, dressed with a precision that looked less like royal indifference to cloth and more like a costume fitted by someone who had described a king but never met one.

"It is a fine morning for the harbor," the king said again.

"Your Majesty honors me," Ferro said, and waited, because he had been taught that kings spoke first and inquisitors listened, and listening was half the craft anyway.

The king turned from the window. His eyes crossed Ferro's face and stopped there, and for one long moment Ferro felt seen—truly seen, weighed and found known—in a way that raised every hair on his arms. Then the moment passed, or seemed to pass, and the king smiled.

It was a good smile. It reached his eyes. Later, Ferro would spend whole nights trying to understand how a smile could reach a man's eyes and mean nothing behind them, the way light reaches the bottom of a well without warming the water.

"You are investigating the deaths at Saltrow," the king said.

"I am, Your Majesty. Three drownings ruled accidental by the harbor magistrate. I believe they were not accidents."

"And why do you believe this?"

"The knots, Majesty." Ferro had brought evidence, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain: lengths of cord cut from the drowned men's wrists, each bearing a luck-knot of a style the guilds had not tied since the Purges. "Someone is tying old work. Guild work. Either a survivor of the purges has returned to the city, or someone is killing with their methods to draw us toward a ghost."

The king listened. That was the thing Ferro could not get past, even afterward, even now, climbing a mountain in the dark with traitors: the king had *listened*. He asked the right questions. When Ferro described the knotwork—the tension of the final loop, the way a true sigilcrafter hid the binding stroke inside a decorative braid—the king nodded at the correct moments, and once said, "So the craft survives in the hands, whatever the law did to the heads," which was such a precise and human observation that Ferro had felt a flash of pride, absurdly, as if the king had praised him personally.

Then the king said, "It is a fine morning for the harbor."

Ferro hesitated. Outside the window, the morning was in fact fine, and the harbor was visible, glittering, three miles distant and downhill of everything.

"Yes, Majesty," he said carefully. "Though I'm afraid my business concerns darker waters."

"Of course." The smile again, warm as banked coals. "You will want your warrant countersigned. Bring it to me."

Ferro produced the warrant. The king took it, and the seal-stamp, and pressed the royal signet into the wax with a firmness and exactness that any chancery clerk would have envied—and while the wax was still soft, while his thumb was still resting in the cooling red, the king looked up at Ferro with those clear, seeing eyes and said:

"It is a fine morning for the harbor."

Four times, now. Ferro began, very quietly, inside the locked room of himself, to count.

* * *

He told himself, the first time, that it was a habit of royal speech—that kings repeated themselves the way bells repeat, that ceremony was repetition and repetition was ceremony. He told himself, the second time, that the king was distracted, that the harbor was a fixed point his mind returned to between matters of state, the way an old man returns to his youth. He told himself, the third time, nothing at all, because there was nothing comfortable left to tell himself, and the fourth time he simply stood in the beautiful cold room and listened to the sentence fall again—"It is a fine morning for the harbor"—and understood that he was hearing something he had no name for yet.

Five. Six. Between repetitions the king transacted business flawlessly. He asked about Saltrow again and remembered Ferro's answers verbatim, improved on them, drew from memory a detail about the tide tables that Ferro himself had missed. He signed a second document—an order releasing grain from the royal stores to the fen districts, unprompted, generous, wise. He laughed once, at something dry Ferro said about the harbor magistrate, and the laugh was rich and easy and perfectly timed.

Seven. Eight.

The room was very quiet. The chamberlain had withdrawn. Somewhere below, the city murmured. The smell of river water was stronger now, Ferro noticed—or thought he noticed; his heart was beating hard enough to make his own senses suspect. Green and patient. Like a cellar. Like a well.

Nine.

The king set down the seal-stamp, aligned it precisely with the edge of the desk, and folded his hands. And in the space of that folding—half a second, no more—his face *stopped*. There was no other word Ferro ever found for it. Every line of expression went out of it at once, evenly, like candles pinched by the same breath, and what remained was not blankness, because blankness is a look and this was the absence of looking. It was the face of a house with no one home, windows clean, door shut, hearth swept. It lasted the length of a heartbeat.

Ten.

Then the warmth came back up from wherever it was kept, poured back into the mold, and the king smiled at Dain Ferro with the full force of a beloved sovereign's regard and said, "It is a fine morning for the harbor."

Eleven.

And Ferro—who had been raised on dockside sermons about the evil of magic, who had hunted sigilcrafters through the Warrens with fire in his belly and law in his fist, who had watched a woman burn in the Nightmarket square for selling fever-charms and told himself the law was the law—heard himself say, in a voice he barely recognized as his own:

"Majesty. Are you well?"

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. Ferro counted those too. He would count them for the rest of his life.

Then the king laughed—warm, rolling, the flood-water laugh from the processions of Ferro's boyhood—and clapped him on the shoulder with a hand that was cool as the flagstones.

"Well? I am the best-used man in Vharen, Inquisitor. Every part of me serves." He turned back to the window. Below, the harbor glittered, and the fishing boats were coming in, and somewhere in that glitter a courier was probably running, and somewhere in the Warrens a woman with clever hands was tying luck into cord for coppers, breaking the law with every knot, keeping someone alive with every knot. "Go and find your ghost, Ferro. And when you find her—"

A pause. The first pause, the only hesitation, in the entire audience.

"—be kind. She is only holding what the rest of us dropped."

Ferro bowed and withdrew, and the doors closed behind him, and he stood a long time in the Hall of Banners looking at the empty hook where the wolf had hung, smelling river water that had no source, before he walked down the stairs into the ordinary daylight and told himself, one more time, one last time, that he had imagined it.

Eleven times. He had counted eleven times. He had gone home and written none of it in his report, because reports require words, and he had owned no words for what he'd seen. He had told himself it was grief—the king had lost his queen that winter; grief does strange things; grief repeats itself. He had told himself it was exhaustion, illness, the weight of the crown. He had told himself every merciful story a decent man can tell, and the stories had worked, the way walls work, right up until they didn't.

* * *

Now, three years later, on a mountain that smelled of snow and old iron, Dain Ferro finally had words for it.

Not possession—he had seen possession once, in a trial record from the Purges, and it screamed and thrashed and wanted things. What he had seen in the king had wanted nothing. It had *performed*. It had been a sigil wearing a man, a great working sustained on something, drawing on something, and the sentence about the harbor had been a groove worn in it, a phrase some craftsman had set spinning to keep the vessel turning when no one was watching. *Every part of me serves.* He heard it now the way you hear a bell all night after leaving the church.

And the courier—the poor bleeding fool who had died in Mara Almeida's shop with a map in his fists—had been carrying proof of it toward the one person alive with the craft to read it.

"Ferro."

Mara's voice, low, from across the ashes. He hadn't heard her wake; she moved like that now, quieter than she'd been on the road, as if the mountain were teaching her its manners. She crouched by the dead fire, feeding it a twist of bark, and the small flame lit the guild mark on her wrist—the wolf, faded, older than the law that forbade it.

"You're staring at the pass," she said.

"I'm counting," Ferro said.

"Counting what?"

He looked north, where the road bent around the shoulder of Mount Serel and vanished into cloud. Somewhere beyond that bend, according to a dead man's map and a living woman's burning blood, lay the vault where his king had been unmade twelve years ago—while Ferro had been a young inquisitor burning charm-sellers and calling it justice.

"How long a lie can stand up," he said. "Before someone counts it."

Mara studied him for a moment with those level, unreadable eyes. Then she glanced at the pass herself, and Ferro saw her wrist-mark flare—just once, just briefly, a heat-glow under the skin like a coal breathed on—and saw her press her thumb over it and say nothing, the way she'd been saying nothing for two days.

But she didn't have to say it. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, in the way Liss stirred and Mara's hand went to the girl's hair without her looking.

Someone was waiting for them up there. Someone who had known the keybearer would come.

And the map, Mara had admitted last night, her voice flat as slate, bore marginalia in a hand she knew better than her own—the handwriting of a master nine years dead.

The fire crackled. The clouds over Serel began, very slowly, to move against the wind.
