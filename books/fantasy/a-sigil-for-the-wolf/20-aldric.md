## 20. Aldric

The mountain had been calling to her for three days.

Not in words—Mara would have distrusted words—but in the guild mark on her left wrist, which burned now like a coal wrapped in skin. It had been a dull ache since the Pass of Teeth, a warmth since the shrine, but as they climbed the last switchbacks above the cloud line, it became a pulse. A heartbeat not her own, keeping time with something buried deep beneath Mount Serel's granite skull.

*The Vault knows I'm coming,* she thought, and did not know whether the feeling was homecoming or summons.

Liss walked ahead of her, small boots finding purchase on scree that should have sent any child sprawling. The girl had changed on the road—not grown, exactly, but sharpened, the way a blade sharpens. Her gray-eyed listlessness from the blighted villages was gone; her eyes were brown now and always moving, cataloguing exits, counting strangers' hands. Twelve years old and already she moved like someone who had learned what the world does to the unwatchful. Mara hated that she'd taught her that by example.

Oskar brought up the rear with Ferro, the two of them having achieved a wary truce somewhere around the second day of the pass—a truce built, as far as Mara could tell, entirely out of shared complaints about her cooking and mutual distrust of everyone else alive.

"Your mark," Oskar said, nodding at her wrist where she'd pushed back her sleeve without realizing it. "It's doing the thing again."

"The thing being?"

"Glowing. Like a dying firefly in a jar." He squinted up at the ridge ahead. "I don't love it. Things that glow on mountains are either miracles or traps, and in my experience the ratio is about one to nine."

"It's responding to proximity," Mara said. "The outer seals are keyed to guild blood. They're... waking up."

"And when seals wake up," Ferro said quietly, "what do they do to people who *aren't* guild blood?"

She had no answer for him, and he hadn't expected one. That was the shape their trust had taken: questions asked honestly, silences given freely, and neither of them pretending the arrangement was anything more than an armed truce between people who needed each other more than they liked each other.

The path crested at a shelf of black stone, and Mara stopped breathing.

Below them, folded into the mountain's shoulder like a secret kept too long, was the Vault's threshold—and she knew it, knew it the way you know your mother's hands. A crescent of worked granite older than Vharen itself, its face carved with the First Knot, the great interlaced sigil from which every charm she'd ever sold was a degraded, whispered descendant. Twelve years ago it had been hidden under soil and pine. Now the earth had shrugged it half-bare, as if the mountain were tired of keeping the guild's secrets, or as if the guild's secrets were getting too heavy to hold.

And standing before it, small against all that stone, was a figure in patched gray robes, moving his hands in patterns Mara's body remembered before her mind did.

Her knees nearly went. She caught herself on Oskar's arm, hard enough to bruise.

"That's—" Liss began.

"*Aldric.*" The name came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh.

Twelve years. Twelve years since the Purge raid, since she'd watched the guildhouse burn from a drainage culvert with her master's last lesson ringing in her ears—*run, little wren, run and don't look back, looking back is how they catch your heart*—since she'd spent a decade believing him dead, then doubting it, then burying the doubt so deep she'd almost convinced herself. And here he was. Alive. Old. His hair gone white as frost-fern, his shoulders curved inward like a man who'd been carrying something heavy in front of him for a very long time. But the hands—the hands were still Aldric's hands, long-fingered and precise, weaving seal after seal into the air above the threshold stone, each one flaring faint gold before sinking into the granite.

"He's holding them alone," Mara whispered. "All this time. The outer seals need renewal every—"

"Every lunar turn," Oskar finished grimly. "You told us. On the boat. Which means he's done this what, a hundred and forty-four times? By himself?" He shook his head slowly. "Nobody does anything a hundred and forty-four times by themselves unless they've stopped being a person and started being a function."

"Oskar."

"I'm just saying what we're both thinking."

But Mara wasn't thinking it. Mara was twelve years old again, sitting cross-legged on a guildhouse floor while a patient voice explained why a binding knot must never be pulled tight in anger. She was running down wet stairs with smoke chasing her. She was every lonely night in the Nightmarket shop, selling fever-charms to people who would burn her if they knew what she really was, whispering guild oaths to a dark ceiling because silence felt like betrayal.

She was down the slope before anyone could stop her, scree sliding under her boots, her name tearing out of her throat:

"MASTER ALDRIC!"

The figure stiffened. The working seals guttered. And then he turned, and Mara saw his face properly for the first time, and everything in her that had braced for joy broke instead into something closer to grief.

He was gaunt. Not thin—*gaunt*, hollowed, as though something had been drinking him slowly through a straw for years. His eyes were the worst part: bright, ferociously bright, lit from inside by a fire that had nothing warm in it. When they found her, they widened, and widened, and then Aldric Sandoval—Master of the Ninth Knot, keeper of the Vault, the most disciplined man she had ever known—made a sound like a hinge rusting open and came down the slope toward her at a stumbling run.

"Mara." Her name in his mouth was broken glass. "Mara. Mara. *Little wren.*"

He caught her arms above the elbows, gripping hard enough to hurt, staring at her face as though memorizing it against theft. Up close she could see the tremor in his fingers, the way his eyes kept flicking past her shoulder to the threshold stone—to his seals—as if even now, even here, he couldn't bear to leave them unattended for more than three heartbeats.

"You got my map," he breathed. "You came. Twelve years I've waited. Twelve years, and the seals failing faster every season, and I thought—I thought maybe the courier died, or you'd burned the map, or you'd forgotten, or you'd become one of *them*, one of the comfortable ones, one of the ones who sell trinkets and call it survival—"

"Aldric." She put her hands over his. His skin was cold as the mountain. "I came. I'm here."

His forehead dropped to hers. He smelled of ash and pine resin and something else, something chemical and wrong, like lightning-struck air. "I knew you'd come," he whispered. "I raised your mark myself. Your blood answered. I knew my wren would come home."

Behind her, boots on stone. Oskar's first, unhurried, deliberately audible. Then Ferro's measured tread, and the lighter skip of Liss.

Aldric's head came up.

Mara felt the change happen through her grip on his wrists—a locking of muscle, a stillness like a trap springing shut. His bright eyes tracked past her, one stranger, two, and landed on Ferro, and everything gentle in his face curdled.

"Inquisitor," he said. The word came out flat and dead, a coin dropped on stone. His hands slid free of hers. "You brought an *inquisitor* to the Vault."

"Ferro is—" Mara stepped sideways, placing herself between them, absurdly aware of how little of her there was to block a sightline. "Ferro is the reason we're alive. Rell ordered him to hunt us. He chose the truth instead."

"There is no choosing truth." Aldric's voice didn't rise, which was somehow worse; it dropped, went soft and precise, the lecture-voice, the voice he'd used on guild members who mishandled bindings. "There is only what the Crown permits you to believe until it doesn't. I watched forty-one good people die because one of them trusted a man with an inquisitor's oath. Forty-one, little wren. I counted. Counting is what I do now. Counting and sealing and waiting." His gaze slid to Oskar. "Smuggler. Salt debt?"

"Paid in full," Oskar said evenly, "and then some, if we're keeping books."

"And the child." Aldric's eyes found Liss, and Mara saw something move behind them that made her stomach drop—not cruelty exactly, but *assessment*. The way a craftsman looks at timber. "A ward? Yours?"

"She's mine," Mara said, sharper than she intended. "*Mine.*"

Something flickered across the old man's face—hurt, quickly buried. He turned away, back toward the threshold, and his shoulders curled in again around whatever invisible weight he carried. "Of course. Of course, everyone is yours now. Everyone gets to be yours. I get the mountain."

"Aldric—"

"Fourteen days." He was already walking, already talking, his hands rising to resume the sealing work as if the interruption had never happened, as if she were a weather event he'd waited out. "The third outer seal fails in fourteen days. Perhaps ten. It has begun to slip a quarter-turn each night, and when it goes, the inner wards go with it, and everything below wakes up hungry. So." He glanced back at her, and for one terrible instant his expression was perfectly, lucidly sane, and infinitely tired. "Whatever you brought with you, wren—you had better hope they're worth the risk. Because the door only opens for guild blood, and I have spent twelve years making certain that nothing gets in."

"Or out," Mara said quietly.

Aldric smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Especially out," he said. "Come. You'll want to see what I've kept alive. And your friends—" the word cost him visibly, "—will want to stay very close, and touch nothing, and remember that I am the reason any of you are standing here at all."

* * *

The threshold chamber swallowed them whole.

Inside the mountain's shoulder, the passage opened into a vaulted hall whose scale made no sense—larger than the mountain's exterior could possibly contain, though Mara had long ago stopped asking how the guild's founders managed such things. Knotwork covered every surface: walls, floor, ceiling, all of it dim, all of it *flickering*, great interlaced patterns of gold light guttering like a city of candles in a draft. Some sections burned steady and strong. Others had faded to ghost-gray traceries, barely legible, and Mara's trained eye read the decay like a physician reading a wasting sickness.

"How bad?" Oskar asked, low, at her shoulder.

"Bad. The northern arc is mostly dead. The anchor lines—" she pointed to thick cables of woven light descending into the floor at the hall's center, seven of them, plunging down into darkness, "—those are still holding. Those are what matter."

"And those are?"

She hesitated. "That's the Binding. Or the outermost edge of it. What's below is..."

"What you told me by the fire," Liss said. Not a question. The girl was staring up at the flickering knots with an expression Mara couldn't parse—wonder, yes, but underneath it something harder. Recognition. "Where the king's luck comes from."

"Where everything's luck comes from," Mara murmured.

Aldric had paused at the hall's center, beside the nearest anchor line, and was watching her with that fierce bright stare. "You remember," he said, and there was naked hunger in it. "They told me the Purges burned it all out of the survivors. They told me the ones who ran forgot everything worth knowing. But you remember. Say you remember, wren. Tell me what this is."

"The Threshold Hall," she said. Her voice shook and she let it. "First work of the Founding Nine. Every seal renewed by hand, every lunar turn, because the mountain forgets faster than stone should." She touched her burning guild mark. "And the man who keeps it alone for twelve years isn't a function. He's the last Master of the Ninth Knot, and he's killing himself doing work meant for nine."

Silence. Even the flickering light seemed to hold its breath.

Then Aldric laughed—a short, cracked sound with no joy in it—and turned away to kneel at the anchor line, pressing both palms flat against the woven gold. "Nine," he echoed. "Yes. There were nine. Now there is one, and one is not enough, and every year I stretch thinner, and every year the thing below stretches wider." His hands began to move, renewing, weaving, and the seal under his palms brightened gratefully, greedily, drinking from him. Mara watched the light pull color out of his knuckles as it fed.

"Stop," she said. "Aldric, stop, it's drawing on you—"

"It always draws." He didn't look up. "That is the first lesson, little wren, the one they teach before knots: *all craft draws.* The only question is what it draws from. These seals draw from me because I have nothing left to protect except them." He glanced up at her, and his eyes were wet and blazing and utterly mad and utterly her master's. "But you. You have blood worth drawing. You have the keybearer's mark. Twelve years I've poured myself into these walls like water into sand, and now—now the map reached you, and you came, and you brought—"

He stopped. His jaw worked. Mara saw him fight for control and win it, the old discipline closing over the madness like ice over black water.

"Rest tonight," he said, in the mild tone of a man discussing supper. "Tomorrow I'll show you the inner door, and what waits behind it, and why I sent for you instead of dying here like a sensible man." He rose, swaying slightly, steadied himself on the anchor line. The gold beneath his palm pulsed once, softly, almost affectionately.

Mara didn't move. "Why did you send for me, Aldric?"

"Because you're the keybearer."

"That's what the courier called me. That's what the map says. Nobody has ever told me what it *means*." She took a step toward him, and behind her she heard Oskar shift, and Ferro's hand move near his coat, and Liss go very still. "You raised my mark on that map. My blood opened the courier's seal. Everything on this road has been leading me here like water downhill, and I have walked into it willingly, because you were the one person in the world I never doubted." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and let it stand. "So tell me what a keybearer is. Tonight. Before I sleep one hour in this place."

For a long moment Aldric only looked at her, and the flickering knotwork painted his ruined face in shifting gold and shadow, and Mara thought: *he's deciding how much of the truth I can survive.*

Then, gently—too gently—he smiled.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I promise you, wren. Tomorrow you will understand everything, and you will finally be home, and nothing will ever ask you to hide again."

He turned back to his seals. The conversation, apparently, was over.

Mara stood in the guttering light with her family gathered behind her—an inquisitor, a smuggler, a child—and understood, with cold clarity, that the reunion she had dreamed of for twelve years had ended the moment she'd climbed over that ridge. Because the man at the center of the hall was not simply her master keeping faith alone on a mountain.

He was a man who had spent twelve years preparing a door that only she could open.

And he had not yet told her what was on the other side—or what it would cost.

* * *

They made camp that night in a side gallery Aldric indicated with a wave, a low-ceilinged chamber off the main hall where the knotwork glowed steady amber and the cold was held at bay by old, patient warmth. Oskar unpacked salt pork and hardtack with theatrical gloom. Liss sat close to Mara, shoulder pressed to shoulder, saying nothing, watching the doorway where Aldric had vanished back into his endless rounds.

Ferro crouched beside Mara as she spread her bedroll, and spoke so quietly the words barely disturbed the air.

"He didn't ask the girl's name."

Mara stilled. "What?"

"All evening. He asked what she was—ward, burden, cargo. Never her name." Ferro's gray eyes were level and unhappy. "In the palace, we had a word for officials who talked about people that way. We called them *assessors*. I arrested six of them during the Purges." He straightened, rolling his shoulders as if setting down weight. "Maybe twelve years alone cracks a man and nothing more. Maybe he's exactly what he looks like—a loyal ghost keeping a lamp lit. But Mara." He held her gaze. "When he looked at Liss, he wasn't seeing a child. He was seeing a measurement."

Across the chamber, Aldric's silhouette passed the doorway, hands already moving, weaving, renewing—faithful, tireless, and terribly alone.

Mara pulled Liss closer and lay awake listening to the mountain breathe, and the guild mark on her wrist pulsed in the dark like a second heart, patient as a hook, waiting to be set.
