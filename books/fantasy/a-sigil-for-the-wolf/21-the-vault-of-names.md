## 21. The Vault of Names

The door did not open so much as consent.

Mara pressed her palm to the stone and felt it think beneath her skin—a slow, mineral deliberation, like a glacier deciding whether to calve. Her guild mark burned cold now instead of hot, a ring of frost around her wrist where the wolf-and-thread had been inked when she was nine years old and too proud to cry. Then the mountain exhaled. Seams of light split the rock face in branching lines, gold veined through black granite, and the wall swung inward on hinges that had not screamed in twelve years because they had been oiled with something better than oil.

"Mind the threshold," Aldric said. "It remembers who crosses."

She stepped over the sill as she had been taught as a child—left foot first, weight low, an old courtesy to whatever slept below—and the moment her boot touched the inner stone, the light rose to meet her.

Mara had prepared herself for darkness. She had prepared herself for ruin: collapsed shelves, rotted paper, the guild's great work crumbled into dust and rat nests. She had not prepared for this.

The Vault went down.

Not stairs—terraces, broad shelves of living rock descending in wide arcs like the rings of a felled tree, and every arc was lined with sigils. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They hung in the air on threads of their own making, each one no larger than a coin, each one burning with a small, patient, individual light. Some were white as salt. Some were the deep amber of resin. A few, scattered here and there, had gone dark—and the darkness around them seemed thicker, hungrier, as though the unlit sigils drank the light of their neighbors just by existing.

"Oh," Liss breathed beside her. The girl's hand found Mara's sleeve without asking permission, the way she'd done since the waystation fire, and Mara found she didn't mind. "Oh, it's like—the sky. It's like someone put the night sky underground."

"It's like a granary," Oskar said quietly behind them. He had gone very still, the stillness he wore when he was afraid and refusing to show it. "A granary full of *people*."

He was right, and that was the horror of it. Mara walked down the first terrace and looked closely at the nearest sigil—a knotwork pattern she recognized from her earliest lessons, a simple binding of thread and intent—and saw what she had always known and never truly understood until this moment. Each sigil was anchored to a name. She could feel them, faint as heartbeats through a wall. Not symbols. Not spells. *People.* Somewhere above, walking the streets of Vharen or dying in some village bed, a person existed whose whole self was knotted into that little coin of light.

"How many?" Ferro asked. He stood at the terrace's edge with his inquisitor's habit of cataloguing, but his voice had lost its official flatness. It sounded like a man in a cathedral who no longer believed in the god and was more awed, not less.

"Forty thousand at the last census," Aldric said. He had descended ahead of them, his lantern held high, and the light made him look older than the mountain. "When I sealed the outer doors, thirty-one thousand were still lit."

"And the dark ones?"

Aldric did not answer immediately. His silence had a texture Mara remembered from her childhood—the texture of a man choosing which truth to hand you first, knowing you could only carry one at a time.

"The dark ones are the dead," he said finally. "Or worse. Come. There is something you must see before you see anything else."

* * *

They descended. The terraces wound down and down, and Mara counted the turns the way she had once counted them as an apprentice, and the numbers matched, and that frightened her more than anything else so far—that after twelve years of abandonment the Vault was exactly as her memory had kept it, perfect and waiting, like a house where no one had told the furniture the family wasn't coming back.

Liss kept close. The girl had not spoken since the door opened, but her eyes moved constantly, reading the sigils the way other children read picture books. Twice Mara caught her lips moving, tracing patterns, and twice Mara felt something flicker along her own mark in answer, a sympathetic shiver, like a tuning fork humming when its twin is struck.

*Gifted,* Aldric had said on the road, watching Liss fold her sleep-knot. *More gifted than you were, girl, if you can believe it.*

Mara believed it. That was part of what terrified her.

At the fifth terrace down, the character of the light changed. The sigils here burned steadier, denser, arranged not in the loose organic drift of the upper levels but in deliberate rows, ranked like soldiers. And at the center of the terrace, set into the rock floor itself, was a dais of black stone, and on the dais—

Mara stopped walking.

On the dais lay a single sigil, vast, the size of a cart wheel, worked in silver wire and something darker that might have been dried blood or might have been iron, and it was bound about with chains—not metal chains, but chains of script, lines of true-name glyphs circling it in concentric rings, hundreds of them, thousands, the handwriting changing style every few rings because different hands had added them across centuries. And at the center of the great sigil, where a name would sit, was a seal stamped in wax the color of old wine.

She knew the seal. Every child raised in the guild knew it. The crowned stag of Vharen.

"The royal line," Ferro said. His voice came out strange, stripped. He had drawn closer without seeming to move, the way men approach a wounded animal or a corpse. "That's the royal seal. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "Inquisitors are taught the heraldry before we're taught to read. I've seen that stag on a thousand documents. On death warrants. On my own commission." He looked up at Aldric, and his face in the sigil-light was terrible with understanding arriving too fast. "What is the king's name doing in the ground under Mount Serel?"

"That," Aldric said, "is the Binding."

* * *

He made them sit. It was such a familiar gesture—Aldric arranging his students in a half-circle on the cold stone, settling himself among them with his hands folded, the lecture posture—that Mara's body responded before her mind could object. She sat. Oskar lowered himself against a shelf with the wariness of a man who intended to stand again quickly. Liss curled beside Mara, knees to chest. Ferro remained standing, arms crossed, a heretic in a temple he couldn't leave.

"The guild is older than Vharen," Aldric began, and his voice took on the cadence of the old lessons, the ones delivered in the attic above the shop while rain came down outside and the world below believed magic was a story mothers told to frighten children. "Older than the kingdom. When the first settlers came down out of these mountains, they found the land hostile—blight, bad water, winters that killed a third of every settlement. So the first sigilwrights did what sigilwrights do. They made a knot. But this knot was too large for any one pair of hands, so they tied it to something that would outlast them all."

"The crown," Mara said.

"The crown." Aldric nodded. "The monarchy was young then, and malleable, and the first king agreed—for a price. His line would be the anchor point. The kingdom's fortune, its harvests, its health, its luck, all of it woven through the royal blood and pinned to the world through the Binding. In exchange, the guild served the crown, and the crown protected the guild. For six hundred years it worked. Six hundred years of mild winters and full granaries. You've heard the stories—Vharen never knew famine until—" He stopped himself.

"Until the Purges," Mara finished.

"Until we stopped tending it." His hands tightened in his lap. "The Binding isn't a machine, Mara. It's a living thing. It needs feeding, adjusting, pruning. The guild tended it the way you tend a garden or a patient. And when the Purges came—when the Crown turned on us and burned us out of the city—" his voice roughened, "—the tending stopped. Twelve years of neglect. And a neglected knot doesn't simply loosen. It *rots*. And rot attracts things."

"What kind of things?" Ferro asked.

Aldric looked at him for a long moment. "You've seen the gray guards. You killed one on the high road—I know; I watch the passes. Tell me, inquisitor. What did it bleed?"

Ferro's jaw worked. "Nothing. It bled nothing. It bled dust and—" he swallowed, "—and voices. When it died, it made sounds. Like several people speaking through one mouth."

"Those were the people it used to be." Aldric let that settle over them like ash. "Something found the Binding, in the years after the Purges. Something that feeds on anchored fortune the way a leech feeds on blood. It got inside the palace—it may have gotten inside the Binding itself—and it has been drinking ever since. The blighted villages on your road. The gray-eyed children. The failing crops. That is not misfortune. That is a *feeding pattern*. The luck of forty thousand souls, siphoned through one rotten anchor, and the thing that drinks it grows fat and clever and begins to make servants of the drained."

Mara looked down at the great sigil, at the crowned stag in its wine-dark wax, and felt revulsion rise in her throat like bile. All those years selling fever-charms and luck-knots, telling herself the guild's great work was sleeping harmlessly, that the Vault was a tomb and tombs were safe. It had never been a tomb. It was a *wound*, left untended, and something had crawled into it.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "You've been up here twelve years. You knew what was happening down in the valleys. The children. You watched me sell trinkets in the Nightmarket for a decade and you never—"

"I kept the outer seals alive!" Aldric's composure cracked, and underneath it was something raw and sleepless and half-mad, twelve years of solitary vigilance worn down to the quick. "Do you know what that costs? Do you know what I have spent? Every day, Mara. Every single day for twelve years I have renewed the wards that keep that thing from finding this place, and I am *one man*, and the seals want renewing faster every year, and I sent for help, I sent the map, I sent everything I had left, and you—" He caught himself. Breathed. The old mask reassembled, imperfectly, like a dropped plate glued back together. "I did what I could. Alone."

Liss's small hand crept into Mara's. Mara held it.

"The thing in the palace," Ferro said slowly. He had not moved, but somehow he now stood between the dais and the stair, as if by instinct. "The king. Is the king—"

"The king is the anchor," Aldric said. "Whatever wears the crown now, it wears him. Or he wears it. After twelve years, inquisitor, I could not tell you which."

Above them, far up through the terraces, the thousands of small lights flickered—all of them at once, a single collective tremor, like a field of grass bending before a wind that touched nothing else.

Everyone felt it. Even Liss, who gasped and gripped Mara's fingers hard enough to hurt.

"What was that?" Oskar demanded, on his feet now, hand going to the knife at his belt.

Aldric had gone pale. He rose slowly, staring upward through the tiers of burning names, and when he spoke his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"A feeding pulse. It happens when the thing drinks deep." He turned to Mara, and in the sigil-light his eyes were the eyes of a man who had read the last page of a book he wished he hadn't opened. "They're coming more often. Weeks apart, now. Then days. The Binding is nearly empty, child. And when the anchor runs dry, the thing that fed on it will come looking for a new one."

The lights flickered again—closer, harder, and this time the dark sigils on the upper terraces seemed to lean toward the sound of it, hungry as mouths.

"And," Aldric said quietly, "that is why I needed a keybearer."
