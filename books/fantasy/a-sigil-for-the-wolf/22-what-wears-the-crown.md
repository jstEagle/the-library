## 22. What Wears the Crown

Chancellor Rell had not slept in four days, and the king knew it.

That was the obscenity of it—the thing on the throne noticed everything now, catalogued everything, the way a dying man memorizes the faces at his bedside. It had begun noticing three weeks ago. Before that, it had been obedient: fed its audiences in measured doses, signing what Rell placed before it, smiling with the correct muscles at the correct moments. A marionette with excellent posture. Rell had maintained it the way other men maintained clocks—cleaned, wound, never once fooled into thinking the hands moved by any will of their own.

Now the clock was dreaming.

"The ambassador from Kessik awaits, Your Majesty," Rell said, and bowed to the precise depth that communicated devotion without deference. The audience chamber was small this morning—he had arranged it that way, whittling the court down season by season until only the blind, the bribed, and the terrified remained—and their footsteps echoed strangely against marble that had never echoed before.

The king turned his head.

It turned too far. Not impossibly far, not past the limits of bone, but past the limits of *grace*, the way an owl turns, or a thing that has read about necks in books. Its eyes found Rell and held him, and Rell felt the familiar cold crawl up his spine, the one he had lived with for twelve years and named Duty so he would not have to name it Fear.

"Rell." The voice was wrong. It had been wrong for months—a dryness in it, a doubling—but today it carried harmonics, as though two throats spoke through one, slightly out of phase. "Rell, Rell, Rell. There were bells last night."

"There were no bells, Majesty."

"There were bells." The king's hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, and Rell saw that the fingernails had gone the gray of old candle wax. "They rang under the river. They rang under the mountain. Someone is pulling threads, Chancellor, and the loom remembers every pull."

Rell kept his face still. He had been a diplomat before he had been anything else, and diplomacy was mostly the art of standing in burning rooms and discussing the weather. "You should rest before the ambassador. I'll have your physicians attend you."

"I have no physicians." The king smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was *sad*. It had never been sad before. Sadness implied an interior, and an interior implied a tenant. "I have jars and wire and your signature on eleven documents I have never read. Isn't that right, Corwin? Isn't that right, Chancellor?"

*Corwin.* Rell's blood went to ice water. Corwin Almeida had been dead twelve years—purged, burned, his guild scattered like ash on the Nightmarket wind—and no living soul in the palace had spoken that name aloud since. No living soul *knew* it. Rell had taken pains.

"Majesty," he said quietly, "the ambassador."

"Send him in. Send them all in. What does it matter?" The king rose, unfolding upward too smoothly, joints gliding rather than bending, and for one vertiginous moment Rell saw it as it might appear to a stranger: a tall man, silver-haired, regal, entirely plausible. Only the stillness gave it away. Living men fidgeted. This thing stood the way water stands in a glass. "They come to see the king, and they see the king. That's all a king ever was, Rell. You taught me that."

The ambassador from Kessik entered, bowed, presented credentials, and asked after the health of the realm. The king answered in perfect form for eleven minutes. Rell counted. Eleven minutes, and then the thing's voice slipped—mid-sentence, mid-word—and for three seconds the throne room heard a woman speak. Young. Terrified. Saying something in a dialect two centuries dead, something that sounded like *please, the well, please, the children by the well*—

Then the king blinked and resumed exactly where he had left off, and the ambassador, bless his merchant soul, noticed nothing, because men never notice the rot until it is in their own timber.

Rell ended the audience early on a pretext of state.

* * *

He did not go to his offices. He went down.

Twelve years ago there had been arguments, committees, a council of seven men who understood what had happened in the royal bedchamber on the night the true king stopped breathing. Twelve years ago there had been seven signatures on the Accord of Continuity, seven men who agreed that Vharen could not survive the news that its monarch was dead—that the grain contracts, the border treaties, the fragile peace with Kessik and the free cities, all of it hung on the single thread of a crown that must never be seen to falter. Six of those men were now dead of various natural causes that Rell had arranged personally. The seventh was Rell himself, and he had long ago stopped pretending the arrangement was temporary. Empires of lies did not stand down. They simply appointed new custodians.

But the Binding was failing, and custodianship meant nothing if the machinery seized.

The lower vaults beneath the palace had been built by masons who believed weight was a form of prayer, and they had made their walls accordingly. Rell descended three hundred steps with a lamp, past storerooms of decommissioned regalia, past the sealed well that no one had drawn from since the Purges, down to the workroom where the instruments waited. Where *she* had worked, once—the sigilwright, the last of them, before Rell's hunters missed her. He had told himself for years that the woman Almeida was irrelevant. A trinket-seller in the Nightmarket. Harmless. And then three days ago his riders reported a courier dead in her shop, an inquisitor sniffing at her door, and a key-shaped absence walking out of Vharen toward Mount Serel with a smuggler's boat ticket in its pocket.

Rell lit the workroom lamps and looked at what he had spent a decade assembling.

The vessel stood in the center of the room: a frame of black iron shaped vaguely in a man's outline, taller than a man, hung with chains of silver and copper braided together in patterns he had copied from stolen guild manuscripts without understanding half of them. Around it, on tables and floor and pinned to the walls, the preparatory sigils—hundreds of them, each one a thread waiting to be tied into the great knot. His scribes called it the Regency Engine when they called it anything, which was rarely, because the men who worked down here had a habit of going quiet and then going missing, and everyone understood without discussion that the Chancellor preferred it that way.

The problem was simple, and it had been simple for twelve years, and Rell was done pretending otherwise.

The original Binding anchored the kingdom's fortune to the royal line—to the blood, the name, the body of the king. When the last true king died, something had gotten into the anchor. Rell had watched it happen through a doorway he still dreamed about: the hollow thing rising out of the dead man's mouth like smoke poured backward, wearing his shape, needing to be fed continuously with the luck of the realm just to hold its borders. The guild could have repaired it. The guild could have re-tied the knot properly. So Rell had burned the guild, and discovered too late that fire does not untie knots; it only leaves you alone with them.

For twelve years he had fed the thing the city's fortune—siphoned through tax ledgers and harvest records and the quiet arithmetic of misery, the gray-eyed children in the mountain villages, the blighted fields, all of it the tithe, all of it the price of order. And it had worked. It had worked right up until the bells under the river.

Now the vessel needed a new anchor. The Binding had to be moved whole, before the hollow king came apart in front of the court, before some sharp-eyed Kessiki ambassador heard a dead woman begging by a well. Moving it required a vessel of clean lineage and unimpeachable authority. Moving it required—

*Rell.*

He had known for a year. He had designed the Engine around the knowledge, actually, adjusting the frame's dimensions in the dark of night when no scribes were watching, tuning the preparatory sigils to his own true name, which he had written out exactly once and burned immediately after. Ambition, people called it, when they dared think it at all. Rell let them. Ambition was a respectable explanation, and it was even partly true—he had wanted the crown since he was a clerk's son counting other men's coins, and he did not intend to apologize for it in whatever private accounting he kept.

But ambition was not why. Ambition was the excuse the truth wore to be presentable.

The truth was that the ritual had no other candidates. The Binding had to pass into someone willing, someone whose name could bear the weight, and every alternative was either dead, compromised, or would simply create a new hollow thing with Rell's face on it. He had run the calculations a hundred ways. If the kingdom's luck stayed anchored to a corpse, the corpse would eat everything within a year. If it passed to a child—his scribes had suggested children, once, in their careful handwriting, and those scribes were gone now—it would devour the child in a month and keep going. It had to be a full-grown will, stubborn enough to hold the knot closed from inside.

And Rell had been holding knots closed his entire life.

He put his hand against the iron frame. The metal was warm, faintly, the way stones are warm after a hot day, and he could feel through his palm the slow pulse of the preparatory sigils idling, waiting, patient as debt.

"Soon," he said to it, and heard how his voice sounded in the stone room, and decided not to hear it.

* * *

The summons from the palace physician arrived while he was washing lamp-oil from his hands, and Rell read it twice, because the first reading had made the words lie.

*Your Majesty asks for you. He will not stop asking.*

The king's apartments occupied the east face of the palace, and Rell had not entered them in six years—the thing preferred to come to the throne room, to council, to the set pieces where it performed best, and its private rooms had been kept sealed on the pretext of royal privacy. The guards at the door would not meet his eyes. Inside, the air was wrong: sweet, in the way a butcher's shop is sweet, over something cold.

The king sat at a writing desk, dressed in full regalia at four in the afternoon for no occasion, a quill in its hand. Paper covered the desk. Paper covered the floor. Every sheet was written over in the same hand—the king's hand, the true king's elegant courtly script—with the same sentence, repeated thousands of times in ink that dried brown-black:

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still here.*

"You see the difficulty," the king said without turning. Two voices again. Perhaps three now; Rell thought he caught a fourth underneath, very old, very tired. "I write and I write and the hand knows how to write but nothing comes up from below except the sentence. It's like calling down a well and hearing only your own voice, except the voice isn't yours, is it, Corwin? Nothing here is mine anymore. Do you understand? Even the fear isn't mine. I borrowed it."

"Majesty—" Rell began.

"Don't." The thing raised the quill. Ink ran freely from the nib, too freely, and where a drop struck the back of the king's own hand it did not bead but sank in, spreading beneath the skin like a bruise blooming in fast motion. "I weep, you know. Have you seen? The maids won't come anymore. I weep ink, Chancellor. Whatever I am made of now has run out of the ordinary materials." It turned, finally, and its eyes were fully gray, no white left at all, and wet, and looking at Rell with an attention that no puppet had ever managed. "Something is pulling my threads from the other end. Far away. Under a mountain, I think. Whoever holds the other end is unraveling me faster than you can pour luck down my throat, and we both know what happens when I go in front of the court and forget which face to wear."

Rell stood very still. Somewhere behind his ribs, beneath forty years of statecraft, a boy who had once counted other men's coins felt the first cold draft of a door opening onto winter.

"How long," he said.

"Weeks." The king smiled again—sadly, terribly, with an expression the true king had never worn, because the true king had never had anything to be sad about. "Perhaps less. Perhaps the next time the bells ring. Go and do what you're going to do, Corwin. Move your knot. But choose quickly, and choose better than you chose me." The gray eyes held him. "Because whoever sits inside the next one, I promise you this: it is very dark in here, and the dead talk constantly, and none of them have anything left to say except the one thing."

The quill touched paper. *I am still here.*

Rell left without bowing, which he had never once done, and the guards outside pretended not to notice, which they were learning to do very well indeed.

* * *

By midnight the palace was asleep in the way palaces sleep—lightly, with one eye, listening—and Rell stood in the map room with candles burning low and his best rider already booted and saddled in the courtyard below.

Mount Serel lay two hundred miles west. Between here and there: the blighted villages, the pass the locals called Teeth, and a fugitive trinket-seller carrying the one artifact on earth that could move the Binding faster than the Regency Engine ever could—the key, the original seal, the guild's masterwork. His scouts' reports sat on the table in a thin folder. A courier dead in the Nightmarket. An inquisitor, one Ferro, off his leash and heading the same direction, which was either catastrophe or opportunity depending on how much of his leash Rell decided to show him. A smuggler. A child. Rumors of gray guards moving west without orders, which should have been impossible, because the gray guards answered to nothing and no one except the hunger itself.

Rell considered the map the way he considered all problems: as terrain to be crossed, costed, and conquered. Send riders to take the woman and the key. Accelerate the Engine's final preparations. Move the ritual up from autumn to the new moon—eleven days. Eleven days to bind the kingdom's soul to his own name before the thing on the throne forgot how to be a king in public, and Rell became either the savior of Vharen or the man who lost it, and history, he knew, would not trouble itself to distinguish.

His pen hovered over the orders.

Under the mountain, something rang—not a bell, nothing audible, but he felt it in his teeth the way one feels a struck tuning fork by proximity, and across the map room every candle guttered at once, all of them, together, and relit themselves leaning west.

Rell wrote the orders. He signed them with a hand that did not shake, because he would not permit it to shake, and if that was not the same as being steady, it had served him for twelve years.

"Double the escort," he told the waiting rider. "And if the woman resists, she is to be brought anyway. Tell the men—" he paused, hearing again the doubled voice, the woman by the well, the sentence written ten thousand times in ink and bruises, "—tell them the fate of the realm goes with them."

The rider saluted and was gone. Rell stood alone with the map, the candles leaning west like grass in wind, and somewhere far above the vaults and maps and machineries, in a gilded room he would never admit he feared, the king wrote on.

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still—*

Eleven days. Rell snuffed the candles himself, one by one, and went down to the Engine to practice dying.
