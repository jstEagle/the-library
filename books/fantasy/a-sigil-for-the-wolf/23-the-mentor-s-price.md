## 23. The Mentor's Price

The antechamber held its breath around Aldric Sarn, and he held his.

Twelve years in the dark taught a man to measure time by other things than clocks. He measured it now by the sound of Mara's breathing two rooms away—still too fast, still angry, but present, alive, *here*, which was more than he had dared hope for on any of the four thousand mornings since the Purges. He measured it by the ache in his knees as he knelt at the outer seal, palms flat against cold stone, feeding it another thread of himself. The seal drank greedily, as it always did. Everything in this mountain drank.

He had not lied to her. That was the thing he clung to in the small hours, when the Vault's whispers came up through the floor like water through a cracked hull. Every word he had spoken since she arrived had been true: the guild's history, the Binding, the anchor, the thing that fed upon it. True, all of it, and arranged as carefully as a merchant arranges fruit—the bruised side turned toward the wall.

*She will forgive the arrangement,* he told himself, as he had told himself for twelve years. *She will forgive it because it is necessary.*

Necessity was the oldest lie in the world, and the most comfortable to wear.

* * *

He had been young once. It startled him, sometimes, how far away that man had gone—young Aldric with ink-stained fingers and a voice that carried across the guild hall without ever being raised, young Aldric who could knot a blessing into a fisherman's net and mean it. He tried to remember that man's face and found only Mara's instead, age nine, sitting cross-legged on the workshop floor with her tongue between her teeth, working her first true sigil while the older members drifted past and smiled and said what they always said: *the little one has the hands for it.*

The hands for it. The blood for it. The name for it.

That last one he had not understood until too late, and by then understanding was a coin he could spend only once.

The Purges had come in autumn, with the Crown's gray wagons and the smell of pitch. Aldric had been away—summoned, or so the message claimed, to a meeting of sympathetic magistrates in the capital. He had left the vault keys with his second. He had left the children with the youngest sworn member, because Mara was quick and quiet and nobody's idea of a threat. Twelve years later he understood that the message had been bait, that the magistrates never existed, that someone had needed him out of the city so that the raid could proceed without the one man who knew how to unmake what the guild had built. But on that autumn night he had known nothing except the fire glow on the horizon and the way his horse failed beneath him, screaming, three miles from the guild gate.

He had crawled the rest of the way through burning vineyards to find the workshop gutted and forty-two people dead and no body among them small enough to be Mara's.

He had wept into the ash. Then he had stopped weeping, because the Vault below was calling him, and it had been calling for years, and grief had finally made him able to hear it plainly.

* * *

The Binding was the guild's first work and its great sin, though which sin, and against whom, depended on where you stood. Nine hundred years ago the guild's founders had anchored the kingdom's fortune to its royal line—a lattice of names woven beneath Mount Serel, each thread a life freely given, the whole structure humming with luck that pooled in Vharen's granaries and shipyards and birthing rooms. Kings prospered because the guild willed it. Harvests bent like wheat toward the city. And the guild asked nothing in return except remembrance, and secrecy, and the slow tithe of its own members' names.

Something had learned to feed on the anchor. Aldric had found the first evidence decades ago: threads gone thin, frayed at the ends, chewed. Luck draining from the outer provinces into the palace, health pooling upward while the villages grayed. He had reported it to the guild council. They had debated. They were still debating when the Purges came, and afterward there was no council, and there was only Aldric in the dark with a problem that grew teeth every year he left it alone.

The hollow king had begun as an emergency measure. When the true king's strength failed—when the anchor threatened to snap with him inside it—Aldric had done the arithmetic no one else survived to do. A snapped anchor meant famine, plague, the kingdom tearing itself apart within a generation. So he had sealed the king into the lattice itself, alive, sustained, his name serving as the battery the feeding thing drained. And he had built the hollow king above—a vessel of air and habit, a puppet of court routine—to keep the throne occupied while Aldric searched for a permanent solution.

A permanent solution required a keybearer. That was written in the founders' hand, in the Vault's deepest chamber, in script that hurt to read: *The Binding is remade as it was made, by a name freely given, from the blood of the makers.* The original anchor had used volunteers—nine of them, guild-sworn, their names knotted into stone. To remake it, to move it, to free the king and starve the feeding thing, someone of the makers' blood must give their own name into the new weave. Willingly. The founders had been emphatic about that part. A coerced name rotted the whole structure. They had learned it the hard way; there was a ruined dynasty under the third seal to prove it.

Freely given. From the blood of the makers.

Mara Almeida, last sworn member of the Guild of the Woven Name. Last living carrier of the makers' blood.

There was no one else. There had never been anyone else.

* * *

Aldric rose from the seal, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed the antechamber to the table where the map lay unrolled. Her map now—the courier had delivered it, dying, exactly as designed, though the design had cost a good man's life and Aldric tried not to think about that either. The map was his own work, drawn over three winters in this lightless place, inked with pigments ground from bone-dry mushrooms and his own thinning blood. On it, in the corner where cartographers put their pride, bloomed the guild mark he had raised for her: the woven circle, the old sigil, rendered in lines that would warm against her skin as she approached the mountain. A compass needle stitched into her own flesh.

He had spent a long night deciding whether to draw that mark. It felt like forgery. It felt like prayer. In the end he had drawn it because the alternative was letting her live out her small safe life among trinkets while the kingdom's luck bled out around her—and because, if he was honest, and honesty came to him rarely enough that he noticed it like weather, he wanted her back. Wanted it selfishly, humanly, with the hunger of a man who had spoken to seals and shadows for twelve years. Come home, the mark said. Come home and be what you were always going to be.

What the mark did not say: come home and die.

Not die, he corrected himself, wincing. Not precisely. The founders' texts were clear that the keybearer's name passed into the Binding entire—the self poured out of flesh and into the weave, immortal in a sense, present in every lucky harvest and safe birth until the end of the kingdom. She would not suffer. She would simply stop being *here*, in any way that mattered, and continue being everywhere, in a way that mattered to everyone except her.

He had rehearsed the telling a thousand times. On the mountain road he had composed speeches: gentle openings, appeals to duty, the story laid out like a surgeon laying out instruments. He had imagined her fury—he deserved her fury—and then, past the fury, he had imagined her understanding, because Mara had always understood things faster than anyone he'd taught, and she would see, once he showed her the gray-eyed children, the frayed threads, the hollow king's face in its long decline, that there was no other door out of this room.

He had not imagined Liss.

Children bound cleanly. The founders' notes mentioned it almost in passing, the way one mentions that a certain wood takes stain well. A child's name, unweathered, unwitnessed, slipped into a weave like a new thread into old cloth. Aldric had read that line a hundred times over twelve years without it meaning anything. Then the girl had walked into his threshold behind Mara—small, watchful, orphaned by inquisitor fire, carrying her grief the way children carry stones they've decided not to put down—and the line had lit up in his memory like a lamp.

No. He shut that thought with the force of a door slamming. No. He was not that man. He had watched the girl knot her little sleep-charms under Mara's patient hands and felt something in his chest crack open along old seams, and he would sooner let the whole Binding rot than—

But the Binding would not rot quietly. That was the cruelty of it. It would fail loudly, catastrophically, taking the harvests and the newborns and the hollow king's fragile pantomime down with it, and Chancellor Rell—who had sent letters down the mountain for years, polite, threatening letters—would seize whatever remained and twist it into something worse. Aldric had seen Rell's handwriting change over the decade, growing cramped and hurried, a man running out of the very luck he was stealing. Rell meant to move the Binding into himself. Aldric had read enough between the lines to know that aBinding moved by coercion, into a vessel of ambition rather than sacrifice, would birth something the founders' horror stories only gestured at.

So it had to be Mara. Freely. Soon. Before Rell's men finished climbing the pass, before the gray guards finished sniffing their way up the mountain, before the girl's face could make a coward of him again.

* * *

He heard footsteps in the outer corridor—hers, still familiar after all these years, that deliberate heel-first walk she'd adopted as a child to announce herself to a hall full of adults. He arranged his hands on the table, open, harmless. He arranged his face into what he hoped was truth.

She came in with Oskar Vey filling the doorway behind her, arms crossed, wearing his distrust like a coat of plates. Good. Let the smuggler watch. Aldric had done monstrous things in the dark alone; doing the next one witnessed might keep him honest.

"You sent for me," Mara said.

"I need to show you the deep chamber," Aldric said. "And I need you to hear everything before you decide anything."

"Decide what?"

He had planned to walk her down gently. Through the Hall of Names, past the thousands of labeled sigils, letting awe do half his work. He had planned a dozen bridges to the truth. Instead he looked at her face—her mother's jaw, her father's stubborn mouth, the faint scar on her brow from the shelf she'd climbed aged seven—and he heard himself say, plainly, the way he'd promised himself he would:

"The ritual requires a keybearer's willing sacrifice. Your name, Mara. Given freely, woven into the new Binding in place of the king's. That is the price of saving this kingdom. That is why I drew your mark on the map."

The silence afterward had texture. He felt it settle over the room like ash.

Oskar moved first—not forward, just a shift of weight, a man recalculating distances—but Mara's hand went up slightly, stopping him without touching him, and her eyes never left Aldric's face. That was the worst of it. She didn't scream. She had never screamed, even as a child, even the day the fever-charm had backfired and burned her palm to the bone; she had looked at the wound with furious concentration, cataloguing it, as if pain were a text she could annotate later.

"My mark," she said. "On the map. You raised it yourself."

"Yes."

"The courier. The dying man who bled out on my shop floor with your seal in his hand. That was you."

"He volunteered," Aldric said, and heard, even as he said it, how the sentence died in the air between them. "He believed—"

"Everyone believes." Her voice stayed level, which frightened him more than screaming would have. "You kept me ignorant for twelve years so that I'd stay alive, and the moment you needed me, you spent a man's life to drag me here. You didn't ask. You *lured*."

"Lured" was a fair word. He let it stand. "I have spent twelve years looking for another way. There isn't one. I have the founders' own hand saying so. I would have taken your place—I offered my name to the lattice a decade ago. It refused me. The blood of the makers, Mara. Only yours will serve. I raised the mark hoping—" His voice failed him for the first time in twelve years, and he was distantly grateful, because the failure was honest. "Hoping you'd come willingly. Because the alternative was watching you live in that little shop until the luck ran out and the city ate itself, and you with it. Because every year I waited, more children in the lowlands were born with gray eyes. Because I am a coward who loves you, and I chose to bring you here before the choice was taken from both of us."

"Willingly," Mara repeated. Something moved behind her eyes—grief, calculation, and underneath both, horribly, understanding. He watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had lived inside for a decade: no other door. He watched her hate the arithmetic.

"And if I refuse?" she asked quietly.

"Then the Binding fails within the year. The king collapses. Rell moves the anchor into himself by force, and what grows out of that—" He shook his head. "You saw the villages on the road. Now imagine them everywhere. Forever."

Oskar spoke for the first time, low and rough. "And the girl?"

Aldric turned to him, and hated him a little for asking, and hated himself more for the half-second his answer took to form.

"The girl is not part of this," he said.

"She's thinking about it," Oskar said flatly. "Don't tell me otherwise. I've hauled cargo for men like you. I know what a man looks like when he's counting exits and prices."

"That's enough, Oskar." Mara's voice, quiet steel. She was still looking at Aldric, and now she crossed the room and stood before him, close enough that he could see the gray threading her dark hair—twelve years of hiding, of fear, of a life shrunk to the size of a market stall, all of it his doing, all of it for her safety, none of it forgiven. "Show me the founders' hand," she said. "Show me everything, every word, every alternative you've dismissed. If my name is the price, I'll read the bill myself."

Relief hit him like warm water, and shame followed it instantly, because her words were not consent—they were audit, and he knew the difference, and he took them anyway.

"This way," he said, lifting the lamp.

They went down together, the three of them, boots ringing on stairs worn concave by nine centuries of guild feet. Behind them the antechamber door stood open, and somewhere above, faint as a pulse in bad blood, Mara's guild mark burned warmer with every step toward the heart of the mountain.

At the threshold of the deep chamber, Mara paused, hand pressed to her wrist where the mark lived, and asked the question he had been dreading more than any other:

"Aldric—if I say no? Truly no?" Her eyes were steady. "What happens to Liss?"

And the silence that followed was answer enough, and he saw her understand it, and turn, and step down into the dark ahead of him—no longer his student walking beside her master, but something else entirely, something he had made and could no longer control.
