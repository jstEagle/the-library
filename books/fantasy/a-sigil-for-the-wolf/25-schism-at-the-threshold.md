## 25. Schism at the Threshold

Oskar Vey had spent thirty years reading rooms, holds, harbors, and the faces of men about to do something stupid. He knew the signs the way a sailor knows weather: the stillness before the squall, the way a man's hands go quiet when his mind has already made up its mind.

He saw it in Aldric's hands first.

The old man had been talking for the better part of an hour at the Vault's threshold, in that antechamber of pale stone where the guild's founders had once welcomed initiates. Torches guttered in iron brackets, and the shadows they threw made the carved names on the walls writhe like living things. Aldric stood among them, gaunt as a winter branch, his voice worn smooth from twelve years of talking to no one, and he was explaining—patiently, reasonably, the way a man explains arithmetic to a child—why the Binding could not simply be unmade.

"A sigil this old has roots," he was saying. "Roots in the bedrock, in the bloodlines, in the luck of every soul born under Vharen's flag. Pull it out whole and you pull out the ground. But a *transfer*—" his hands moved as he spoke, graceful, practiced, and Oskar noticed they kept drifting, almost absently, toward the corner where Liss sat wrapped in Ferro's cloak, "—a transfer needs only a vessel. A clean one. Young blood takes the pattern like wax takes a seal."

Mara stood with her arms crossed, her face lit amber and half-shadow. "No."

"You haven't heard the—"

"I heard it the first time in the corridor, and I heard it when you tried to dress it up as my choice, and I'm hearing it now. No. No vessels. No transfers. We unbind it or we walk away and let the whole rotten thing collapse on Rell's head."

Aldric's eyes—pale as frost on a windowpane—went very still.

And Oskar, who had watched men decide to cut a partner's throat over cards, who had watched a harbor master decide to burn his own warehouse for the insurance, watched the old man's hands go quiet, and thought: *there it is.*

* * *

It happened fast, the way the worst things always do.

Aldric turned to Mara, and his voice softened into something almost tender. "Child. You were six years old when you swore your first oath. Do you remember it? *We serve, we do not consume.* I wrote that oath. I taught it to your mother before you were born. And now you stand in the house of your fathers and tell me you'll murder everything we were rather than pay the price that keeps the kingdom breathing?"

"The price is a *person*," Mara said.

"The price has always been a person! That is what binding *is*! Every charm you ever sold in that little shop, every luck-knot, every fever-sigil—it all drew from somewhere. From the anchor. From the man we sealed in the dark to keep the rest of you in the light. You've been spending his life for twelve years, Mara. You simply never saw the ledger." His voice cracked on the last word, and for one heartbeat Oskar thought the old man might weep, might break, might fold. "I am trying to save you from becoming a murderer of everything you love."

"I'm trying to save you from becoming one," Mara said quietly.

Aldric smiled. It was the worst thing Oskar had seen in a long time—worse than the gray guards, worse than the pass, worse than the waystation fire. It was the smile of a man who has already finished grieving and come out the other side into calm.

"You were always my best student," Aldric said. "Which is why I prepared alternatives."

He moved.

Not toward Mara. Oskar had expected that—expected the confrontation, the duel of wills, the old master and the wayward student circling each other. But Aldric had spent twelve years alone in the dark with nothing but seals and sigils and the long arithmetic of survival, and he had not survived by fighting the strong. He went for the corner. He went for the child.

Liss screamed. Ferro came up out of his crouch like a springing trap, cloak flying, one hand closing on Aldric's wrist—but the old man moved like something younger wearing an old man's skin, and his free hand sketched a shape in the air that burned white, and Ferro went backward into the wall hard enough to crack stone.

Oskar did not think. Thinking was for afterward. He had a knife in his hand—he always had a knife somewhere—and he put himself between the old man and the girl and felt Aldric's fingers close on his collar, felt a pressure like the whole mountain leaning on his chest, and heard the old man say, almost apologetically:

"I'm sorry. Children bind cleanly. It's not cruelty. It's *mercy*, compared to what Rell will do if he finds her first."

"You—" Oskar wheezed, clawing at the grip, his vision graying at the edges. "You absolute—*bastard*—"

Ferro hit Aldric from the side. The big man had blood in his hair and fury in his face and he drove the old man into a pillar with the full graceless weight of a dockside brawl, and for a moment the three of them were just bodies in the torchlight, grappling, cursing, the elegant sigilcraft of it all collapsing into something out of a Vharen gutter.

And through the chaos, Oskar saw Mara.

She had not moved. She stood where she had stood, arms still crossed, and her face was the face of a woman watching a house burn—grief and fury fused into something cold and absolute. Her lips were moving. Her right hand had come up, two fingers extended, and she was drawing in the air with a steadiness that made the hair on Oskar's neck rise, because he had seen her do small things, hidden things, a charm to blind a guard, a knot to steady a rope. He had never seen her do *this*.

The air changed. It tasted like copper and snowmelt.

"Aldric," she said, and her voice carried through the antechamber the way a bell carries through fog. "Let. Him. Go."

The old man froze. Not by choice—Oskar could see it, the way a man freezes when his own body stops answering. Aldric's fingers uncurled from Oskar's collar finger by finger, as if prised open by invisible hands, and the pressure lifted, and Oskar dropped to one knee and sucked in air that felt like swallowing broken glass.

"What—" Aldric's voice came out strangled. "What have you—Mara, what have you *done*—"

"Something they taught us in the third year." Mara walked forward slowly, her hand still raised, still tracing, and Oskar saw now that the sigil she was drawing hung in the air between them, faint as breath on glass, a lattice of lines that seemed to sink into the stone behind Aldric as she completed it. "The one they said we'd never use on each other. It doesn't hurt. It just... takes the fight out. Takes the *reach* out. You'll be able to walk, talk, weep, repent. You won't be able to work. Not for a long while."

"You would cripple your own master." Aldric's voice was no longer tender. It was no longer anything. "In the house of your fathers."

"You went for a *child*." Mara's composure cracked down the middle, and what came through it was worse than shouting—it was the voice of a woman who had spent twelve years being careful and was done. "You stood there and talked about mercy and then you reached for *Liss*. You don't get to call this house yours anymore. You don't get to call *me* yours."

The sigil flared. Aldric's knees buckled. Ferro caught him before he struck the floor—caught him, Oskar noticed, gently, the way you catch a falling elder even when you'd happily have thrown him—and lowered him down against the pillar where he sat, suddenly, terribly old, his hands open in his lap like a man who has set down something heavy and cannot remember what it was.

* * *

They argued about what to do with him for the better part of an hour.

Ferro wanted to bind him properly—rope, gag, the works. "He's proven what he is. We leave him loose, he finds another way. Men like this don't stop. They *wait*."

"He's not a man like anything," Mara said. She was kneeling beside the old man, and she had not touched him, but she had not left him either. "He's the last of the guild. He's the man who taught me to write my own name in light. And he's spent twelve years holding seals alone in the dark so the rest of us could forget any of this existed." Her voice wavered. "I can't just—Ferro, I can't leave him trussed like a pig in the place he gave his life to keep."

"He gave his life to keep a *prison*," Ferro said. "And he tried to put a child in it."

"I know." Mara closed her eyes. "I know what he did. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking what we do with the fact that the man who did it is also the only reason the Vault hasn't already opened and swallowed everything between here and the sea."

Oskar leaned against the far wall, working his bruised throat, and said nothing for a long time. Liss had come to stand beside him—had come there on her own, he realized, without being called, without thinking about it, the way you go to the person who stood between you and the wolf. Her small hand found his and held on. He let it.

"Antechamber," he said finally.

They both looked at him.

"There's a door." He nodded toward the eastern wall, where a carved archway stood half-hidden behind a hanging of rotted cloth. "I saw it when we came in. Sealed, but the seal's guildwork—Mara's mark opens it, or close enough. There'll be rooms behind it. Storerooms, cells, whatever they kept back here." He shrugged. "Put him behind it. Lock it. He lives, he's safe from the gray guards, and we're safe from him. When this is over—if any of us are—she can come back and let him out and they can hate each other properly, with time to do it right."

Mara stared at him. "You'd lock him in the dark. After everything he told us about the dark."

"He locked a *king* in the dark," Oskar said. "I'm putting a man in a room with a door. He can be grateful for the improvement."

Something that was almost a laugh broke out of Mara—wet, ragged, gone as fast as it came. She looked down at Aldric, who had not spoken through any of it, who sat with his ruined hands in his lap and his eyes fixed on the carved names on the wall as though reading them for the last time.

"Aldric," she said.

"I heard the smuggler." The old man's voice was ash. "It's a fair sentence. Fairer than I gave the king." He looked up at her then, and Oskar saw with a lurch that the old man was crying—silently, without drama, tears cutting clean lines through the grime of twelve years. "I told myself it was mercy. Twelve years I told myself that. The Binding needed a vessel and the kingdom needed its luck and I was the only one left to make the arithmetic work." His breath hitched. "And then you walked through that door, Mara, and I looked at you and I thought: *there*. There is the vessel. The keybearer. Willing, sworn, mine. And when you refused I thought—" he laughed, a terrible sound, "—I thought, well. The guild taught us to prepare alternatives. I never once asked myself when I had stopped serving and started *consuming*."

"You're asking now," Mara said.

"I'm asking now." He bowed his head. "Do what you came to do. Unbind it. Burn the whole rotten garden down. And leave me here with their names—" he touched the carved wall beside him, tenderly, the way you touch a grave, "—so I can read them while I wait."

* * *

The seal on the eastern door yielded to Mara's hand like it had been waiting for her, which, Oskar supposed, it had.

The rooms behind it were small and dry and utterly dark until Ferro lit the torches—storerooms, mostly, shelves of sealed jars and bundled cloth, and one narrow cell with a stone bunk and a slot for a tray that made Oskar's skin crawl until he reminded himself it had probably been built for something worse than an old man. They put a blanket in there. They put water, and the last of the bread, and a stub of candle. Liss, of all of them, insisted on carrying the candle in herself, and Oskar watched her set it down on the bunk with the fierce solemnity of a child performing a rite, and watched Aldric watch her, and watched the old man's mouth shape words he didn't have the right to say anymore.

*Thank you. Forgive me. Both, maybe.*

Liss came out without answering. She took Oskar's hand again on the way past, and he pretended not to notice how hard she was gripping.

Mara sealed the door last. She stood before it a long moment, her palm flat against the stone, her forehead nearly touching it, and Oskar—watching, because watching was the one thing he'd always been good at—saw her lips move. He couldn't hear the words. He suspected no one was meant to. A goodbye, or an oath, or the third-year sigil-words for *sleep well and wake changed*, and then her hand dropped and the sigil flared white along the doorframe and sank into the stone, and the door became just a wall.

"He'll be safe in there," she said. Her voice was scraped raw. "The wards will keep the gray guards out. They'll keep him in. And they'll..." She faltered.

"They'll what?" Ferro asked.

"Nothing." She turned away. "They'll hold. That's what matters. They'll hold."

But Oskar had spent thirty years reading rooms, and he had seen the shape her mouth made before she swallowed the word, and he filed it away in the place where smugglers keep the things they're not ready to say: the wards would hold *as long as she did*. As long as her strength lasted. And she was about to walk down into the heart of the Vault and unmake a sigil that had anchored a kingdom for three hundred years, and every thread she pulled, she'd told them, would cost her a piece of herself.

The wards would hold exactly as long as Mara did. And when she ran out—when the unbinding took its last payment—there would be nothing between that door and whatever came for it but a stone wall and a candle burning down in the dark.

* * *

They made ready in silence, mostly. Ferro checked his blades and said nothing about the letter from the capital that Oskar had seen him fold and unfold three times since the fight. Liss sat with Aldric's stolen notes in her lap—Mara had taken them from the old man's satchel before the sealing, and the girl had annexed them without a word, the way she annexed everything now, quietly and completely. Oskar coiled rope and checked the lantern oil and did all the small mechanical things that kept his hands from shaking, because they wanted to shake, because his throat still ached where the old man's grip had been and because some animal part of him kept replaying those fingers closing on his collar and the flat, reasonable voice saying *children bind cleanly*.

Mara stood at the head of the descending stair, where the torchlight gave out and the true dark of the Vault began. The map was in her hand. The guild mark on her forearm was visible where her sleeve had fallen back, and it was *glowing*—faintly, steadily, a thread of gold under the skin, brightening with every breath, as if the thing below had felt the schism at its threshold and was calling its keybearer home.

"Oskar." She said it without turning. "You could still take her and go. The pass is open. By the time anyone came after you, it would be done—either way it goes."

"And leave you two down here with the wolf?" He slung the rope coil over his shoulder. "I told you on the road. I'm bad at arithmetic. I count wrong on purpose."

She looked back at him then, and smiled—small, exhausted, real. "Liar. You count everything."

"Everything except the days until I can leave." He stepped up beside her at the stair's edge. "Stopped counting those a while back. Didn't want to embarrass myself."

Below them, the dark breathed. Somewhere far down, in the heart of the mountain, something that had worn a king's face for twelve years stirred in its chains, and the guild mark on Mara's arm flared bright enough to cast her shadow up the stairwell, long and thin and trembling, reaching down into the dark like a hand.

Mara took the first step.

Oskar followed.
