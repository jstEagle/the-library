## 27. The Descent

The stair went down farther than the mountain should have allowed.

Mara counted the first hundred steps out of habit, the way she'd once counted knots in a charm to be sure no thread had slipped. She stopped counting at three hundred, when the numbers stopped meaning anything and the dark began to feel less like an absence of light and more like a substance—thick, patient, older than the stone it filled. The lantern in Oskar's fist threw a circle of gold that seemed smaller with every turn of the spiral, as if the dark were drinking at its edge.

Behind them, far above, the antechamber door still held. Mara had sealed it with a disabling sigil woven from her own hair and Aldric's own chalk—the last kindness she intended to do him—and every few minutes she felt it strain against whatever pressed on the other side. Not Aldric. He was old, and furious, and half-mad with twelve years of solitary vigil, but he could not have made the walls hum like that.

Something else had woken.

"The gray guards," Ferro said quietly, somewhere behind her. His voice carried the flat calm he wore like armor; she'd learned by now that the flatter his voice, the worse his fear. "They'll have felt the outer seals break. Whatever draws them—it knows we're here."

"Then we don't stop," Mara said.

Liss's hand found hers in the dark. Small fingers, cold at the tips, gripping hard enough to hurt. Mara did not tell her to let go. Twelve years ago a girl not much older than Liss had run through burning corridors while the guild died around her, and no one had held her hand, and she had never quite forgiven the world for that. She would not pass the debt forward.

The stair ended at last in a landing where the air changed—cooler, wetter, tasting of iron and deep water though no water dripped. Oskar raised the lantern and its light fell across a doorway carved into the living rock: an arch of black stone banded with silver, and set into the silver, worn nearly smooth by centuries of reverent hands, the guild mark. The knotwork wolf, jaws open, guarding nothing visible.

Mara's tattoo answered it before her mind did. The mark on her wrist flared white-hot, and she gasped, and the arch *answered back*—a low tone rolling up through the floor, through her bones, a sound like a name being spoken very slowly by something with all the time in the world.

"It knows you," Liss whispered.

"It knew my mother," Mara said. "It knew her mother before that." She flexed her fingers until the pain dulled to an ache. "That's what a key is. It doesn't open doors. It gets *recognized*."

She pressed her palm flat against the arch, over the wolf's carved throat, and spoke the opening words her body had never forgotten even when her waking mind had tried for twelve years to bury them. The silver bands brightened. The black stone parted along a seam finer than a knife's edge, sliding inward on a breath of air that smelled—impossibly, terribly—of the guildhall. Of cedar smoke and beeswax and the particular oil they'd used on the practice-boards. Her eyes stung. Twelve years, and the Vault remembered the smell of home better than she did.

* * *

Beyond the arch lay a corridor unlike anything the mountain above could contain. The walls were worked stone, yes, but the work was wrong in scale—blocks the size of wagons fitted so precisely that the joints were thinner than thread, and every surface carried sigils cut deep enough to cast their own shadows. They glowed faintly as the party passed, each one waking as Mara's blood came near, flaring soft blue-white and dimming behind them like lamps being lit and snuffed in sequence down some endless hall.

Oskar walked close to the wall, one hand trailing the stone the way a sailor trails water. "These are wardings," he said. Not a question. Smuggling had taught him more about protective craft than any honest trade would have.

"Anchor-points," Mara corrected softly. "Each one holds a piece of the great Binding steady. Like—" she searched for the image "—like stitches holding a wound closed. Except the wound is the kingdom's luck, and the stitches are names."

"How many?" Ferro asked.

Mara looked down the corridor, which curved away beyond the lantern's reach, sigil after sigil after sigil fading into blue distance. "I stopped counting at two hundred. And this is only the approach."

Liss had gone quiet in the way Mara had learned to dread—not the silence of fear but the silence of attention. The girl was staring at the nearest anchor-sigil with her head tilted, lips moving, tracing its lines in the air with one finger without touching the wall.

"That one's tired," Liss said.

Mara stopped. "What?"

"This stitch here"—the finger hovered over a junction in the glowing pattern—"it's fraying. See how the light goes thin right there? Like rope about to give." Liss frowned, and the frown was so exactly her mother's, so exactly the face of a child diagnosing a lame horse or a bad harvest, that Mara's chest tightened. "Somebody's been holding it too long."

*The true king*, Mara thought, and could not say aloud. *Somebody has been holding it for twelve years, and he is at the end of his strength.* "You can see that?"

Liss shrugged, embarrassed. "Can't everyone?"

"No," Ferro said, with the weight of a verdict. "No one can. I've watched trained inquisitors read sigil-script for a decade and none of them could see a fray by eye."

Oskar snorted. "Congratulations, sprout. You're either a prodigy or cursed."

"Both run in my family," Liss said, and Mara laughed despite everything—a short, startled sound that bounced strangely off the ancient stones—and then the laughter died, because the sigils behind them began going dark.

Not dimming. Going *out*. In sequence, fast, like footsteps dousing lamps, coming from the direction of the arch.

"They're in the corridor," Ferro said. He had his blade out already, the steel dull and honest in the sigil-light. "How many?"

Mara reached back along her senses, along the thread of her own disabling seal on the antechamber door, and felt it shred like wet paper. The pressure she'd been feeling since the threshold resolved into presence—cold, numerous, walking with the perfect synchronized stride of things that did not need to breathe. "More than four. Fewer than twenty. Moving fast."

"Fast *how* fast?"

"Faster than us."

"Right." Oskar spun the lantern in his hand, considering, then thrust it at Liss. "Hold this. Don't drop it, don't swing it, don't get creative."

"I'm always creative," Liss said, taking it, and Oskar pointedly did not smile, which meant he wanted to.

They ran.

* * *

The corridor became a gallery, the gallery a vaulted hall, and the hall opened without warning onto the descent proper: a shaft in the mountain's heart, ringed by a spiral ramp wide enough for six abreast, plunging down into blue-black depth. And there, strung across the ramp at intervals like the web of some enormous patient spider, hung wards—guild-work, centuries old, curtains of knotted cord and hammered silver that shimmered faintly between the ramp's inner rail and the shaft's outer wall.

Mara skidded to a halt at the first one. Her breath came ragged; the descent had stolen it. Below, the spiral dropped farther than the lantern-light could follow, and at its unseen bottom something pulsed—a slow, vast rhythm, felt in the floor more than heard, like a heartbeat scaled up past sense. The Binding. The heart of it. *Him.*

"The wards," Ferro said, arriving beside her, sword ready. "Will they stop the guards?"

"They'll slow them." Mara touched the nearest curtain of knots and felt its age, its exhaustion, the way she'd feel a fever in a friend. "They were tuned to guild-blood. Mine will pass. Yours—" she looked at Ferro, at Oskar, at Liss "—will not."

"So we leave them behind and run faster," Oskar said.

"We leave them behind and they walk through them like cobwebs, and they're already closing." Mara turned. Far up the spiral, at the edge of sight, the sigil-lights along the outer wall were dying in a long converging line. Coming around. Coming down. "Unless the wards hold *for* us instead of against us."

Ferro understood first. She saw it land in his eyes, that weary inquisitor's arithmetic. "You want someone to re-tune them. On the way past. While running."

"I want someone to re-tune them *fast*." Mara took Liss by the shoulders. The girl looked up at her, lantern-light catching the gray that had crept into her eyes during the blighted months on the road—that road-sickness the Binding's hunger had put in her, the sickness that had cleared in the high passes and might, if the thing below kept feeding, come creeping back. "I'm going to teach you a knot. One knot. Can you learn one knot in thirty seconds?"

"You taught me the sleep-knot in ten."

"The sleep-knot doesn't hold a door against dead men." Mara pulled a length of cord from her sleeve—she'd taken to carrying cord the way other women carried knives—and folded it into Liss's hands. "Watch. Over, under, lock, and *name it*. You don't tie a ward-knot, you *tell* it what it's for. Say 'hold.' Mean it. Mean it like you mean staying alive."

Liss's fingers moved. Over, under, lock. "Hold," she said, and the knot cinched itself tight with a small sound like a struck match, and the cord between her hands stiffened into something with the density of iron.

Mara stared. That knot took apprentices a season. Some never got it. "Do it again."

"Hold." Cinch. Match-strike.

"Do it again."

"Mara," Oskar said, "the dead men."

"Again," Mara said.

* * *

They went down the spiral at a dead run, and Liss worked.

At each ward-curtain they paused just long enough—two breaths, three—for Mara to slice a length of cord from the hem of her coat, for Liss's quick fingers to fold it into the tuning-knot, for the girl to say *hold* in that clear, unhesitating voice, and press the knot to the ward's anchor-point. Each time, the ancient curtain shivered, brightened, and swung its attention outward like a dog turning to face a stranger. Each time, behind them, the pursuit hit the re-tuned ward and stopped.

They could hear it now. Not footsteps—gray guards made almost no sound—but a pressure, a cold displacement of air, and beneath it a faint grinding, like millstones, like teeth. The first ward held. When they reached the second, the first was still holding. When they reached the third, Mara risked a look back up the spiral and saw them: shapes in the blue gloom above, pressed against the shining curtain of the second ward like moths against glass, featureless faces tilted at the same angle, hands flat against the light. Not clawing. Just *pushing*, patiently, endlessly, the way water pushes.

"There's nine," Ferro said. He'd counted too. His voice stayed flat. His knuckles didn't. "Maybe ten."

"They won't push forever," Mara said. "Wards aren't walls. They're arguments. Eventually the argument loses."

"Then keep making new arguments." Oskar jerked his chin downward. "Move."

Down, and down. The pulse from below grew stronger with every turn of the ramp, and Mara felt it begin to pull at her—not her body, her *edges*. The way a tide pulls at sand. Each heartbeat of the great Binding below tugged loose a little of what she was, and she caught herself forgetting, for half a step, why they were descending, or what the word *vault* meant, or the color of her mother's—

She bit her tongue hard enough to taste copper and the thought snapped back into place. *Costs*, she thought. *Everything down here has costs. Keep your teeth together and pay attention.*

Liss, remarkably, was humming.

It was barely a tune—three notes, cycling, the kind of thing children do without knowing they do it—but Mara realized after the fourth ward that the girl had matched her humming to the rhythm of the pulse below, and that her knots were setting *on the beat*. Cinch. Match-strike. Hold. The wards weren't just brightening now; they were *settling*, sinking deeper into the stone, knitting themselves to the rock like roots.

"You're timing them," Mara said, awed despite the terror, despite the grinding pressure overhead.

"To the heartbeat." Liss shrugged, breathless, cheeks flushed, cord wrapped around both fists now like a street-fighter's wraps. "It's easier. It's like—it's like the whole mountain wants to be in rhythm, and I'm just letting the knots fall in."

Ferro had stopped pretending to watch the rear only. He was watching Liss with an expression Mara had never seen on him: naked wonder, quickly buried, like a man catching himself praying. "Twelve years," he said quietly. "Twelve years the Crown burned anyone who tied a luck-knot, and the whole time the next keeper of the craft was a farmer's daughter learning her letters."

"Don't say things like that," Liss said seriously. "You'll jinx the knots."

And Mara—running down a spiral into the dark with dead men at her back and a dying king at the bottom of the world—laughed again, really laughed, because what else could you do with a child who scolded an inquisitor about superstition while weaving wards against the undead?

* * *

The ninth ward. The tenth. The spiral's turns came faster now, the ramp steepening, and the pulse below was no longer something felt only in the floor—it was audible, a vast slow *whoomp, whoomp* like surf in a sea-cave, and with each beat the sigil-lights along the wall flared in answer, the whole shaft breathing light in time. Mara's guild mark had gone from ache to burn to a steady white pain she cradled against her stomach as she ran, and still she counted wards, because counting was prayer, and prayer was focus, and focus was the only thing keeping her edges from unraveling into the tide below.

Behind and above, the grinding had changed character.

"They're through the sixth," Ferro reported. He'd been listening the way he listened to testimony—total, unhurried, missing nothing. "The seventh won't last. Whatever they are, they're learning the wards' rhythm. Adapting."

"Then we're done pausing." Mara grabbed the last length of cord from her hem and tore it free. "Liss. Last one. Make it count."

The girl took the cord. And then she did not tie the knot they'd practiced.

Her hands moved differently—faster, stranger, folding the cord through configurations Mara had never shown her, patterns that surfaced from somewhere below memory, below teaching. Over, under, twist-back, lock-and-cross, a figure Mara knew *knew* but couldn't place, an old shape, a guild shape, one of the forms from the first-year boards that nobody used because nobody could make it *hold*—

"Hold," Liss said.

But this time she didn't say it like a command.

She said it like a promise. Softly. To the knot. To the ward. To the dark above them where ten dead men ground their patient teeth. "*Hold. Please. I've got you.*"

The knot cinched with a sound like a bell.

The final ward-curtain didn't just brighten. It *blazed*—silver-white light flooding the whole shaft, throwing every gray guard above into sharp relief for one terrible instant: ten hollow shapes, faces smooth as eggs, pressed against the seventh ward as it shrieked and gave way. The light of Liss's knot poured down the spiral like water finding its level, and where it touched the older wards they flared in sympathy, and the entire descent became for one moment a column of standing radiance, a ladder of light driven down into the mountain's heart.

And the guards stopped.

All ten. At once. Pressed against the seventh ward—which held, which *sang*, which had become something no guild-mistress in five centuries had ever woven—they simply stopped pushing, and stood, and tilted their smooth faces down toward the light and the small girl who had made it, and Mara felt the moment hang like a held breath.

Then, as one, they turned and began climbing back up the spiral. Away.

"They're retreating," Oskar said flatly. "Dead men don't retreat."

"They're not retreating." Mara's voice came out strange to her own ears, hushed. She was watching the guards go with her heart slamming against her ribs, watching the light of Liss's knot hold steady and serene, and understanding arrived in her all at once, cold and complete, the way understanding does when it's been waiting a long time for you to catch up. "They're *reporting*. Something below just learned about her."

The pulse from the depths stuttered.

One missed beat. Two. Then it resumed—but changed, quicker now, uneven, like a heart that had noticed a stranger in the house. The sigil-lights flickered with it. The whole shaft shuddered, dust sifting down through the blaze of the wards, and somewhere far below, iron rang against stone: a door, or a gate, or a cell, opening.

"Liss." Mara crouched, took the girl's cord-wrapped hands, made herself meet those gray-shot eyes. "Listen to me. What you just did—no one taught you that knot. Do you understand? It came from somewhere in you. From the guild, maybe. From the craft itself. And the thing down there heard it."

"Was that bad?"

Mara thought of the true king sealed in the Binding's heart, begging without yet knowing whom to beg. Thought of Rell, hurrying through the dark with his retinue and his ambition. Thought of Aldric locked above, and of the oath: *we serve, we do not consume.*

"It was necessary," she said. "And it was brave. Both things are true." She stood, drew her knife, and cut the last of the cord from her other sleeve, pressing it into Liss's palms. "From here down, you stay between me and Oskar. You don't weave unless I say. And if I say *run*, you run, and you don't look back, and you don't wait for me."

"I never agree to that part," Liss said.

"I know." Mara touched her cheek. "That's why I love you."

Below them, the stairs ran out. The spiral ended at a landing of black stone where the air itself seemed dense with waiting, and across the landing stood the inner door of the Vault of Names—taller than three men, seamless, unmarked by any sigil, bearing only at its center a single depression the exact size and shape of a human hand.

Mara stepped toward it. Her guild mark burned so brightly now that light leaked between her clenched fingers.

Behind her, Ferro drew his blade. Ahead, the door waited for her hand. And from the darkness on the far side of the stone—muffled, distant, unmistakable—came the sound of many feet, descending toward them from Rell's side of the mountain.

They were no longer alone in the deep.
