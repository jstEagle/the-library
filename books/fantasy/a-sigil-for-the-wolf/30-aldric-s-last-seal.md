## 30. Aldric's Last Seal

The lock on the antechamber was a good one—Mara's work, and she had always been his best student—but a lock is only ever an argument, and Aldric Sarn had spent twelve years arguing with older and crueller things than iron.

He worked the disabling sigil apart with a bodkin pulled from his boot seam, sweating in the cold of the Vault's outer dark, listening to the chaos unfolding below. Shouting. The ring of steel on stone. A woman's voice—Mara's voice—raised not in fear but in command, and beneath it all the vast slow breathing of the Binding itself, like a sleeper turning under blankets of stone.

His hands shook. They had been shaking for years now, but here at the end they shook with purpose, and that made all the difference. Purpose was a kind of strength. He had learned that in the dark.

*You brought strangers,* he had told her, and meant it as accusation. He saw that now—or almost saw it. There was a splinter in his mind, some small stubborn thing that kept whispering *this is wrong*, and he had been trying for days to draw it out like a bad tooth. Better to leave it in. Better to let it ache. Pain was information, and he could not afford information right now. He could only afford the work.

The sigil frayed, unraveled, died. The lock clicked open like a small polite cough.

Aldric stepped out into the antechamber and looked down the long throat of the stair that led to the Vault of Names, where his life's meaning waited for him wearing his student's face and refusing, with every breath, to do what must be done.

"I am sorry, child," he said to no one. The dark did not answer. It never had. That was the thing about the dark that people who had not lived twelve years inside it never understood: it was not cruel, and it was not kind. It simply held you, the way a grave holds.

He went down toward the light and the shouting, and the great seal at the bottom of the world.

* * *

He had been young once. He tried to hold on to that fact as he descended—young, and sworn, and certain. The guildhall above Lowmarsh had smelled of beeswax and cut hemp, and Master Ondine would walk the rows of apprentices with her hands clasped behind her back, asking each of them the same question every morning:

"What is the first oath?"

And thirty children's voices would answer together: *"We serve, we do not consume."*

"And what does that mean?"

"It means we take nothing that has not been freely given."

It had seemed simple then. Everything had seemed simple then. He was ninety years old now if he was a day, though he had stopped counting, and he had learned that simplicity was a country you could only visit, never live in. Twelve years alone with the seals had taught him what Ondine never said aloud—that *freely given* was a door with a thousand hinges, and a desperate man could talk his way through all of them.

*Mara will understand,* he told himself, descending. *Mara came. Mara carried the mark. She is the keybearer; she was born to this.*

But the splinter ached, and he knew why, and he would not look at it.

She had said *no.*

She had stood in the Vault among ten thousand names and looked at him with her mother's eyes and said *no*, and then she had locked him away like a mad uncle at a funeral, and gone down herself to do—he did not know what. To unbind. She had used the word *unbind* the way other people used the word *murder*, casually, as if she did not hear its weight. As if twelve years of his life, holding the outer seals alone, eating rats and rainwater, talking to the stone until the stone began to talk back—as if all of that were a coat she could ask him to take off and burn.

Unbinding meant the end of the craft. The end of everything the guild had been. And worse—the kingdom unanchored. Luck running loose like a flood through low fields. People would die. Children would die. Rell's hollow puppet would collapse into whatever it truly was, and Vharen would tear itself apart looking for someone to blame.

Whereas the ritual—

Whereas the ritual required only one willing name.

The splinter ached. He pressed his thumb against the wall as he climbed down the last steps, steadying himself, and felt the mountain's pulse through the granite, quick and wrong, the heartbeat of a thing being eaten alive from the inside.

"She will thank me," he whispered. "Later. When there is a later."

The dark did not answer, but for the first time in twelve years, he thought he heard it disagree.

* * *

The heart of the Vault was a cathedral of hanging threads.

That was how Mara had described it once, when she was nine years old, on the day of her swearing, and the description had never been bettered. Ten thousand filaments of pale light dropping from the unseen ceiling, each one anchored below in a plinth of black glass, each plinth carved with a true name. The Binding of Serel. The anchor of the kingdom's fortune, woven by hands three hundred years dead, feeding—God help them all—something that had learned, somewhere along the centuries, to drink.

The chamber was full of war when Aldric reached it.

Gray guards—six of them, seven, moving without reflections in the black glass, moving without any of the ordinary awkwardness of bodies—pressed toward the central dais where the royal plinth stood taller than a man. Ferro held the stair mouth with his back against the rock, sword working, his face gray and set. Oskar Vey had dragged a plinth over as a barricade and was firing a crossbow with the calm desperation of a man who had run out of clever plans and settled for loud ones. The girl—Liss, the orphan, the child Aldric had been fool enough to reach for—crouched behind him, both hands raised, fingers knotted in a ward-pattern that blazed thin gold whenever a guard touched it. Clever hands. Guild hands. Even now, even with his purpose fixed, some old teacher in his chest noted it with pride.

And at the center, on the dais, kneeling before the royal plinth with her sleeves pushed back and her fingers buried to the wrist in living light—

Mara.

She was unraveling the Binding. He could see it at once, see it the way a musician hears a false note: thread after thread coming loose under her hands, each one dimming as it parted, and with each one her face went whiter, emptier, as if she were pulling memories out of her own skull and letting them die in the air. Her lips moved. She was saying something—names, he realized. Guild names. Saying them and losing them in the same breath.

"No," Aldric said, and the word came out cracked, hardly a word at all. "No—child—stop."

No one heard him. The chamber was too loud, all shrieking steel and the deep subsonic groan of a system losing its balance. He crossed the floor at a stumbling run, past a gray guard that wheeled toward him and then—strangely—away, called elsewhere by whatever hollow will drove it, past Oskar's barricade, up onto the dais, and he seized Mara by the shoulders and turned her to face him.

Her eyes were terrible. Not empty—that would have been easier—but full, brimming, with tears standing in them and something going out of her gaze even as he watched, some light guttering down thread by thread.

"Aldric." His name, spoken like a stranger's. Then recognition flickered, and grief with it. "You shouldn't be here."

"Neither should you." He gripped her harder. "Look at yourself. You're killing yourself thread by thread, and for what? For nothing. For a kingdom with no luck in it at all. Mara—Mara, listen to me—" He heard his own voice climb, crack, become the voice of the half-mad keeper of the seals, and he hated it, and could not stop it. "There is another way. There has always been another way. One name, willingly given—one, and the Binding lives, and moves to a vessel that can hold it clean, and everything the guild was survives, and the kingdom keeps its fortune—"

"At whose cost?" Her voice was very quiet. Underneath the exhaustion was steel, and under the steel was pity, and the pity was the worst of it. "Yours? Mine? You came down here hoping I'd say yes. Didn't you. You raised your mark on the map like bait on a hook, and I walked straight into it, because I loved you." Her hands found his wrists and held them, gentle as a nurse. "Master. Say the first oath."

"This is not the time for catechism—"

"*Say it.*"

The chamber roared around them. Somewhere below, Ferro shouted; something heavy fell; the girl Liss cried out and her ward flared. And in the middle of all of it, an old man and his student stood hand in hand among the hanging lights, and Aldric Sarn opened his mouth, and found the oath waiting where it had always lived, worn smooth as a river stone by forty years of mornings:

"We serve," he said. His voice failed on the second clause. He tried again. "We serve—we do not consume."

"And what does that mean?"

He could not answer. The splinter in his mind finally came free, all at once, and he saw it whole: himself in the shrine-dark drafting the map, raising his own guild mark large so that she could not miss it, telling himself it was summons when he knew—it had always known—that it was bait. *We take nothing that has not been freely given.* And a willing sacrifice given under the weight of a dying kingdom, summoned by a beloved mentor's plea—is that freedom? Is anything?

"Oh," he said. Very softly. "Oh, child. What have I become."

What he had become was the thing the guild had sworn against. Not a wolf, exactly. Something older and shabbier: a man who had loved an institution more than the people inside it, and called the love service, and let it eat him until only the appetite remained, dressed in oath-clothes.

"You became what survived," Mara said, as if she had read it off his face. "I know. I nearly did too." Her thumb moved across the back of his hand, once, the way she had done as a girl when a knot defeated her and she wanted to pretend it hadn't. "Help me instead. Not the ritual. Help me hold the pattern while I finish."

For one moment—one whole, entire moment—joy went through him cleaner than any magic ever had. She was asking. His student was asking him to serve. He turned to the great weave with his fingers already moving, the old forms rising in his hands like birds going home—

And the world went white at the edge, and cold.

* * *

He saw it the way you see lightning: already finished, already burned into the eye.

One of the gray guards had broken through. Later—if there was a later—he would assemble it from pieces: Oskar's crossbow empty, Ferro pinned at the stair, the girl's ward stretched too thin across two directions at once. The guard had come up the dais from behind, reflectionless in the black glass, soundless, and its arm was drawn back, and the blade it held was not steel but absence, a shape cut out of the light, and it was aimed—not at Aldric.

At Mara. At the keybearer, mid-unraveling, her hands still buried in the weave, her back to it, defenseless in the way that only someone doing the most important work in the world is defenseless.

There was no time for thought. There was barely time for motion. But Aldric Sarn had spent twelve years doing one thing, and doing it until it lived in his marrow: he had kept the seals. He knew every warding form the guild had ever cut, and four of his own devising, and his body knew them the way a drowning body knows the sky.

He put himself between.

He threw the last seal he had—the oldest one, the first one, the little interlocking knot of protection that Master Ondine had traced over every apprentice's cradle in the guildhall above Lowmarsh, beeswax and cut hemp and thirty voices saying *we serve*—and he threw it wide open, not as a shield but as a door, pouring into it everything he had left, which was not much, which had perhaps never been much, which was at least entirely and freely given.

The absence-blade came down and met the oath, and for a heartbeat the two of them hung there, consumption and service, and the light of the seal was very beautiful, and then it broke.

The blade did not break. The blade passed through.

Aldric felt it enter under his collarbone and exit, he realized with distant interest, somewhere near his spine, and there was astonishingly little pain—only cold spreading outward, and a strange lightness, as if something long clenched had finally let go. His knees folded. Mara caught him. Of course she caught him; she had been catching falling things her whole life, knots and strangers and dying couriers, and now him, and they went down together onto the dais with the ten thousand lights burning above them like patient stars.

The gray guard drew back its arm again.

It never finished. Gold light—thin, furious, a child's ward pushed far past anything a child should survive—caught it across the chest, and Oskar Vey was suddenly there with a plinth-shard in both hands like a spear, and Ferro, and between the three of them the guard came apart into gray dust and a smell like snow on iron. Aldric watched it from a great and widening distance, with something like satisfaction.

"Mara," he said.

"I'm here. I'm here, don't talk—"

"Let an old man talk. It's the last craft I have left." Blood filled his mouth; he swallowed it and went on, because the splinter was out now and the truth sat in the wound it had left, plain as daylight. "I meant to spend you. I dressed it up as duty. I have been telling myself for twelve years that keeping the seals was service, but it was—it was the consuming, Mara, all of it, I just—" A cough took him, wracking. Her hands pressed the wound as if love were a tourniquet. "You were the best thing I ever taught. Do you hear me. The best thing. And I tried to eat you, because I could not bear for the world to change while I was still in it."

"Don't." Tears fell on his face now, warm where he was cold. "Don't do the deathbed speech. We're going to get you out, we'll—Liss knows field-knots, Oskar—"

"Liss." He smiled; he felt it crooked and strange on his face. "Guild hands, that girl. Teach her everything. Start with the cradle-seal. Start with the oath. Make her say it every morning until she hates it, and then until she doesn't." He got a hand up—so heavy, his hand, as if the mountain were already reclaiming it—and laid it against her cheek. "We serve, Mara. In the end. Tell them I served in the end."

"In the end," she repeated, and her voice broke on it, and he chose, generously, to believe she meant it as absolution.

Above them the great weave shuddered, half-unraveled, threads swaying in a wind that did not exist. Below, the fighting redoubled—new voices now, armored, disciplined: Rell's retinue arriving through the shattered stair, the whole tangled war of the world converging on this one room of light. Aldric felt none of it. He felt the oath in his chest, still glowing faintly where the seal had broken against the blade, spending its last warmth into a body that no longer needed it.

Twelve years in the dark, and the dark had finally answered after all. It had answered *her*. That was enough. That was more than enough.

"The pattern," he whispered. "Hold the pattern, child. I'll buy you—whatever time—"

And with the last of his strength, Aldric Sarn raised both ruined hands and wove the first seal one final time—not over a cradle now, but over his student bent above him—and stepped, gladly, out of the argument.

* * *

Mara stayed kneeling a long moment, one hand flat on the cooling breast of the man who had been her whole sky once, and the Vault shook around her like a ship in weather. Down the dais, Rell's voice rose above the din—smooth, urgent, promising order—and gray shapes poured in behind his guards, and Ferro shouted her name, and Oskar swore in two languages.

Mara closed her mentor's eyes with two fingers, the old gesture, the guild gesture, and stood.

Below her hands, the royal plinth had begun to glow—not the pale anchor-light of the Binding, but something deeper, redder, restless. From within it, faintly, muffled as a voice through a wall, came the sound of a man beating his palms against the inside of the world.

"He's awake," Liss breathed, at her elbow, white-faced, ward-scarred fingers still trembling. "Mara—the king—he knows we're here."

Mara wiped her face with the back of her wrist, leaving a smear of her master's blood like a badge, and turned toward the plinth as the last threads of the great weave shivered overhead, waiting for her decision like held breath.

"Then it's time somebody answered him," she said.
