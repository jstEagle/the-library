## 32. What Mara Forgot

Liss had learned, on the road and in the dark, to count by sounds.

Oskar's breathing when he braced the corridor—short, hard pulls through the nose, the way a man breathes when he's holding something heavy and refuses to say so. Ferro's boots, two quick steps and a scrape, two quick steps and a scrape, pacing the width of the passage he'd sworn to keep clear. The gray guards made almost no sound at all, which was the worst thing about them, but their absence of sound had a texture, a felt-sweater wrongness Liss could feel against her ears like a change in pressure before a storm.

And Mara's voice, threading through it all, low and steady, pulling the world apart one thread at a time.

Liss crouched at the edge of the sigil-chamber, close enough to see, far enough to stay out of the way, which was what everyone had told her to be. *Stay out of the way. Stay behind the line. Stay small.* She had spent the whole journey learning to be small, and she was tired of it, tired the way you get tired of a coat you've outgrown.

The great sigil filled the chamber like a frozen lake seen from beneath. It was drawn in channels cut into the stone, and the channels were filled with light the color of old honey, and the light *moved*, slow as blood through a heart that had been beating for three hundred years. Threads rose off its surface and climbed into the dark overhead and vanished, and Liss understood, without anyone telling her, that those threads went up and out and away—to the palace, to the fields, to the gray-eyed children in the blighted villages, to every luckless, anchored soul in Vharen. The Binding. The thing the guild had made when the guild still believed making it was a kindness.

Mara knelt at its edge with her sleeves pushed back and her hands in the light.

She was pulling threads.

That was the only way Liss could think of it. The great sigil was woven, not drawn—woven into the mountain, into the kingdom, into the names of everyone it touched—and Mara was unpicking it the way you'd unpick a knot, one strand at a time, with fingers that shook and did not stop.

And every time she pulled a thread free, something left her face.

Liss saw the first one go. Mara had just loosened a strand near the sigil's western curve, a strand that hummed when she touched it, a low note like a struck bell, and she'd said—almost to herself, almost fondly—*that's the winter Aldric taught us the frost-knot, we were all half-frozen and he made us laugh about it*—and then she'd stopped talking. Stopped in the middle of the sentence. Her brow had creased, the way it did when she was trying to remember where she'd put her keys.

"Aldric," she'd said slowly, testing the name like a loose tooth. "I know the name. I can't—" Her hands kept working. "I can't see his face."

She'd looked up at Liss then, and her eyes were wet and calm at the same time, which was the most frightening thing Liss had ever seen.

"It's all right," Mara had said. "I knew the price. Keep counting the sounds for me, Liss. Tell me if anything changes."

So Liss counted. The gray guards were coming—she could feel their silence getting closer, a cold spreading through the stone—and Oskar's breathing was getting harder, and Ferro's boots had stopped their pacing, which meant he was fighting, which meant she should be terrified, and she was, she was terrified down to the soles of her feet, but she kept counting, because that was her job, because Mara had given her a job.

Then Mara pulled the thread of the lullaby.

Liss knew the lullaby. She'd heard it twice on the road, in the waystation's ashes and again in the shrine, when Mara had thought Liss was asleep: a low, wordless humming, a tune that went up at the end like a question, like someone asking the dark to be gentle. *My mother sang that,* Mara had said the second time, catching Liss listening, not embarrassed at all. *I never learned the words. Maybe there never were any. It's just the tune, and the tune is enough.*

Now Mara pulled the thread that held it, and her humming stopped mid-phrase.

Liss watched her try to find it again. Watched her lips move through the shape of a tune that wasn't there anymore. Watched her reach for it the way you reach for a rung that isn't on the ladder, the hand closing on empty air, once, twice—and then Mara simply went back to work, because there was nothing else her hands knew to do.

But her eyes had gone somewhere Liss couldn't follow.

"No," Liss said. It came out small. She made it bigger. "No. Mara. That one's not—you can't give up the lullaby, that one's *yours*, that one isn't for the guild, it isn't for any of this, it's just *yours*—"

"It was mine," Mara said. Her voice was steady. Her hands did not stop. "And it bought a few more threads, and a few more threads is a few more minutes for Oskar and Ferro. It was a fair price." She glanced up, and smiled, and the smile was the old smile, the shopkeeper's smile, the one that said *everything is fine, everything has always been fine*—but it sat crooked on her face now, like a knot tied by someone who'd forgotten the first step. "Liss. The chamber door. Behind the third pillar on the left. There should be a satchel—Aldric's notes. Bring them to me. Gently. Don't step on any of the lines."

Liss went. She didn't remember deciding to go. Her feet simply understood that being given a task was the only thing standing between her and falling apart, and they took it.

The satchel was there, just as Mara said, wedged behind the pillar, leather gone stiff with age and dust. Liss hugged it to her chest and picked her way back along the chamber's edge, careful of the honey-light lines, careful the way you're careful in a house where someone is dying.

When she got back, Mara had pulled two more threads, and Liss watched her lose them.

The first was small. Mara frowned at her own left hand, flexed it, and said, "I had a scar here. I don't remember what from." The scar was still there—a thin white line across the heel of her palm—but the story of it had been paid out like thread, spent on the unraveling, gone.

The second was not small.

Mara went still over the sigil, both hands sunk in the light, and her face did something Liss had no word for. It emptied. Not like sleep. Like a page being turned.

"Mara?"

"Mm." Mara blinked. Looked around the chamber as if she'd just arrived. Her eyes found the great sigil and she said, wonderingly, "I know what this is. I know how to unmake it. I know every step." A pause. "I don't know why I know. Isn't that strange? It's like reading a letter in my own handwriting that I don't remember writing."

"You're Mara Almeida," Liss said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "You sell charms in the Nightmarket. You tie luck-knots. You have a shop with a green door and the hinge squeaks and you pretend it doesn't. You found me in a burning wagon. You—"

"I did?" Mara said. Gently. The way you'd speak to a child reporting a dream.

And something in Liss went cold and hard and clear, the way water goes when it starts to freeze.

Because Mara was still kneeling at the sigil, still working, still *paying*, thread after thread after thread, and she was going to keep going until there was nothing left of her to spend, and she would call it a fair price, because that was what Mara did—she priced everything, she priced her safety and her craft and her twelve hidden years, and she always, always undercharged herself.

Well. Liss was done letting her.

* * *

The satchel held Aldric's notes, and Aldric's notes were a disaster.

That was Liss's first thought, kneeling over them in the honey-light: *he was a genius and he wrote like a madman.* Pages in three different hands—no, two hands and one clawed, trembling scrawl that must have been his from the last years, the vault years, the alone years. Diagrams of the Binding from every angle. Marginalia in the margins of the marginalia. Arrows pointing at arrows. And everywhere, threaded through it all, the same phrase, repeated like a refrain, like a prayer, like a warning:

*The pattern must hold. The pattern must hold. The pattern must hold.*

Liss turned pages with shaking fingers. She was not a sigilist. She was a child from a wagon, a girl who'd lost her parents to a fire that inquisitors started, a girl who'd learned exactly one knot-charm on a cold night beside a campfire—Mara's own knot, the sleep-knot, the one that made the bad dreams sit down and be quiet. That was the whole of her education.

But she had watched. Watching was what she was *for*, she'd decided somewhere on the mountain road; the world had taken everything else from her, so she'd kept her eyes, and her eyes had never stopped working. She'd watched Mara's hands for a hundred nights. She'd watched the way the threads of a sigil weren't separate things but a *conversation*, each one answering the others, and if you held one steady, the ones it loved would hold too.

And here, on the seventh page of Aldric's disaster, was a diagram she almost understood.

It was the Binding itself, drawn small, with each thread labeled in Aldric's cramped hand. Not the great sigil in the floor—the *anchor pattern*, the shape that held all the threads in relation to one another while they were being pulled. The shape the unbinding had to keep, or everything it touched would come loose at once: the palace, the fields, the gray-eyed children, all of it unraveling together like a sweater with the collar cut.

*The pattern must hold,* Aldric had written underneath, and then, smaller, in that clawed last-years scrawl: *the unmaker must not unmake herself. Someone must hold the shape who is not paying. Someone must hold the shape who is only watching.*

Someone like a girl who was only watching.

Liss looked up. Mara had pulled another thread. Liss saw her lose the green door—watched her frown at nothing and say, "There was a shop. Was there a shop? I think the hinge squeaked," and then shake her head and go back to work, and Liss felt the cold clear thing inside her settle into a decision the way a knot settles when you've finally got the tension right.

She crawled to the sigil's edge. Not into it. To the edge, where the outermost thread lay across the stone like a strand of honey-colored hair.

"Mara. Listen to me. I have Aldric's notes. There's a shape—the anchor pattern. He says someone has to hold it from outside while you pull. Someone who isn't spending themselves. He wrote it down. He *knew* this would happen, he knew you'd try to do it all alone, because you're exactly like him—"

"Liss." Mara's voice was fraying at the edges now, like rope that's been rubbed too long. "The notes are for scholars. You'd need years—"

"I don't need years. I need you to tell me if I'm wrong, and you can do that, because you still know the *craft* even if you're forgetting the *life*, and I—" Liss's hands were shaking and she let them shake. "I watched you the whole way here. Every night. I know how your hands move before I know why they move. That has to count for something. It counted when you taught me the sleep-knot. You said it counted. You said *the knot remembers even when you don't.*"

Mara went still.

For a long moment the only sounds were the far-away violence in the corridor—Oskar's breath, Ferro's boots, the felt-sweater silence of the gray guards pressing closer—and the slow honey heartbeat of the great sigil, and Mara's breathing.

Then Mara said, "Third strand from the outer edge. On the north curve. It wants to curl left. If you hold it curling left, it will hold the six strands that depend on it, and I can pull the ones beneath without the whole western arc collapsing."

It wasn't agreement. It was something better. It was *trust*, handed over like a blade, handle first.

Liss took the strand.

The light bit her—cold, then warm, then something like a voice saying her name from a long way off—and she gasped and held on. The thread wanted to move. It pulled at her grip like a living thing, like a fish on a line, and she thought of the sleep-knot, of Mara's voice saying *don't fight it, guide it, a knot is a conversation, not a cage*—and she didn't fight it. She guided. She curled it left, the way she'd been told, and felt—*felt*, in her palms, in her teeth, in the backs of her closed eyes—the six strands it loved settle and hold, a chord resolving, a family holding hands.

"Oh," she breathed.

"Oh," Mara echoed, and there was something in her voice that hadn't been there before—relief, and grief, and underneath them both, fierce and unmistakable, pride. "Yes. Just like that. Now don't let go, no matter what I say, no matter what I forget. The pattern must hold. *You* hold it."

And Mara pulled the next thread.

* * *

The forgetting didn't stop. That was the terrible arithmetic of it, and Liss did the arithmetic over and over as she knelt at the sigil's edge with her hands full of light: every thread Mara pulled bought the unbinding a little further along, and cost Mara a little more of herself, and the only thing Liss could do was hold the shape so that the cost bought as much as possible. She couldn't stop the spending. She could only make sure none of it was wasted.

She watched Mara lose the guild's first oath—watched her lips shape *we serve, we do not* and then stop, puzzled, like a woman who'd walked into a room and forgotten what she came for.

She watched her lose the mountain road, and the waystation fire, and Oskar's sister's name.

She watched her lose the Purges—watched her face go through horror and grief and then, worst of all, *nothing*, because the events were gone and only the knowledge that she should feel something remained, a scar without a wound behind it.

And each time, Liss said the thing back to her.

That was the other thing she'd figured out, somewhere in the middle of it, hands burning, arms aching, tears running freely down her face and doing nothing to her grip: that the memories weren't gone *yet*, not while they were still being pulled, and that a voice saying them aloud could hold them a moment longer, the way a held note lingers after the string is stilled.

"The frost-knot winter," Liss said, when Mara's brow creased. "You were half-frozen and Aldric made you laugh."

"The lullaby goes up at the end," Liss said, when Mara's lips moved through the shape of nothing. "Like a question. Like asking the dark to be gentle."

"The green door. The hinge squeaks and you pretend it doesn't."

"Your name is Mara Almeida. You were the youngest ever sworn. You escaped the Purge raid. You hid for twelve years and it was *not* nothing, it was the bravest thing anyone ever did, it kept the craft alive, it kept *you* alive, and you're not dying, you're *paying*, and I will remember the difference even if you can't—"

"Liss." Mara's voice, very quiet, in one of the pauses. Her hands never stopped. Her eyes found Liss's and held them, and they were clear, clearer than Liss had seen them since the chamber, clear the way the sky is clear after the last cloud has gone. "Do you know what you're doing?"

"Holding the pattern."

"No." Mara smiled—the real smile this time, the crooked one, the one that belonged to her and no one else, and Liss grabbed the sight of it with both hands and refused to let it go. "You're doing what the guild was always supposed to do. Not the binding. The other thing. The keeping. We were never meant to be the ones with the power, little wolf. We were meant to be the ones who *remember for each other*." Her hands pulled another thread. Something left her, Liss never learned what. "That's the whole secret. That was always the whole secret."

Then the corridor exploded.

Liss heard it more than saw it—stone and shouting and Oskar's roar and Ferro's voice cracking out orders to no one, and the felt-sweater silence of the gray guards flooding in like cold water through a broken door—and Mara said, "Liss. Whatever happens. The pattern must hold," and Liss said, "I know," and meant it with everything that was left in her, and held on.

The strand in her hands pulled. The six strands it loved held. Somewhere behind her, Mara began the final thread—the deep one, the keel-thread, the one that ran all the way down to the true king and the hollow thing above and the twelve stolen years—and Liss felt the whole great sigil shudder like a beast waking, like a heart deciding.

She held on.

Her hands were a child's hands. They were the strongest things in the mountain.
