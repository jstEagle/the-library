## 35. The Kingdom Without Luck

The gray guards crumbled on the ninth morning.

Ferro watched it happen from the steps of the provisional council hall, a cup of chicory going cold in his hand. They had been standing their posts all week—the king's guard, or what remained of it—gray men at gray doors, patient as furniture. Then, between one bell and the next, they simply... let go. Not falling so much as settling, like coats slipping off hooks no one was holding anymore. By noon there were heaps of fine gray dust along the palace colonnade and men with brooms who did not know whether to sweep it or bury it.

A boy ran through the dust cloud laughing, kicking up ghosts.

Ferro had killed one of those things in a shrine on the mountain road. He remembered the way it had come apart under his blade without bleeding, the way its face had worn an expression like a memory of an expression. He had vomited afterward in the snow, and he was not ashamed of it now. Some part of him wished every man in Vharen could have seen what he'd seen, just once, so they would understand what had been walking among them for twelve years wearing their king's face.

Most of them would not believe it anyway. That was the trouble with miracles of deliverance: they arrived looking exactly like ordinary Tuesdays.

He drank the chicory. It was terrible. He was glad of it.

* * *

The council chamber had been the chancery court, back when Vharen still pretended to have laws instead of merely enforcing them. The benches were the same. The seal on the wall had been scraped off and painted over twice already—one faction wanted the old arms restored, another wanted nothing but blank stone, as if the kingdom could heal by refusing to remember its own name.

Ferro stood before the seven of them and gave his testimony for the third time.

They were a strange assembly: two guildmasters whose guilds had been ash for a decade, a harbor magistrate with ink-stained cuffs, a priestess of the Undivided Dawn who kept glancing at Ferro's inquisitor's ring as though it might bite her, a grain factor named Osselmann who had clearly been chosen because someone owed him money, and at the center, Magistrate Ilva Corren—a narrow woman with a voice like a file drawn across iron, whom the city had decided to trust mostly because she had once jailed a tax collector.

"State your name and former office," Corren said, though she knew both. Procedure was the one thing holding the room together, and everyone knew that too.

"Dain Ferro. Senior Inquisitor of the Crown—" he paused, corrected himself, felt the old reflex break like a bone setting wrong, "—formerly of the Crown. Twelve years' service."

"And you attest that His Majesty King Aldous III died approximately twelve years ago, and that the figure seated upon the throne since that time has been—" Corren consulted the transcript, though she did not need to, "—a construct sustained by a binding sigil."

"I do."

"You understand, Inquisitor Ferro, how this sounds. The king is dead. The king has been dead. And you are one of perhaps nine living souls who saw the thing below the mountain with your own eyes." She folded her hands. "Give us something a reasonable person can hold onto. Not the Vault. Not the sigilcraft. Start smaller."

Ferro thought about it. This was the third time, and each time he tried a different door into the truth, hoping one of them would be unlocked.

"The king," he said, "had a habit. A small one. When he listened to petitioners, he turned his signet ring—one quarter turn clockwise, then back. Nervous habit. Every servant knew it; there are jokes about it in the palace ledgers, written in margins, dated years back." He let that sit. "Three years ago I had my last audience with him. He repeated the same sentence to me eleven times. *The harvest will be sufficient.* Eleven times, identical inflection, and he never blinked past the fourth repetition. And his hands were perfectly still."

Silence, except for the scratch of the harbor magistrate's pen.

"A nervous man stops fidgeting only when the nerves stop too," Ferro said. "Or when whatever is wearing him hasn't learned yet that hands are supposed to move."

Osselmann cleared his throat. "You're asking us to convict a dead man of being dead on the evidence of a ring."

"No, Master Osselmann. I'm asking you to walk down to the palace colonnade and put your hand in the dust where the guards stood. There's enough of them left to fill an urn apiece, and not one urn will hold a tooth or a knucklebone. Dust doesn't have teeth." He looked around the room, at their tired, frightened, hopeful faces. "I don't need you to believe me. I need you to believe the children."

That landed. He had known it would. It was the one thread everyone in Vharen was holding.

* * *

Because here was what the city knew, in its bones, even if it couldn't say why: the children were getting better.

It had started within days of the Unbinding—not dramatically, not all at once, but the way a fever breaks in the small hours, so gradually that a mother only notices at some point near dawn that her daughter's breathing has changed. The gray-eyed children, the listless ones, the ones who had been fading like photographs left in sun—they were hungry again. Cranky again. Alive in the loud, inconvenient, magnificent way that children are alive.

The physicians had no explanation. The priests had several, all contradictory. Mara Almeida, when Ferro had asked her, had given him the only answer he believed:

"We built a dam across the river of everything, and we called the reservoir luck. The water didn't go anywhere. It just went where it wasn't supposed to go—into the Binding, into the Vault, into that poor hollow creature on the throne—and everything downstream went dry. Children dry out first. They're closest to the ground."

Now the dam was broken. The river ran wherever rivers run, which is nowhere in particular, which is everywhere.

And the city was learning, slowly and painfully, that it had been living on credit.

* * *

The granary reports came in that afternoon, and they were bad.

Not catastrophic. Not famine. But the autumn wheat in the lowland farms had come in light—blighted ears, thin grain—and the orchards east of the Serel foothills had dropped half their fruit green. Old-timers said they hadn't seen a season like it since before the Purges. Which, Ferro reflected, made a certain grim sense. For twelve years the kingdom's fortune had been anchored, pinned, held fast by the great sigil below Mount Serel. Crops had grown obediently. Dice had fallen kindly. Storms had veered off. The whole country had been a garden growing inside a glass bell.

Someone had shattered the bell.

He sat with the reports long after the clerks had gone, lamplight pooling on the figures, and understood what was coming. Next week the bread riots, maybe. The month after, the blame. People did not want freedom, most people; they wanted dinner, reliably, forever. The bell had given them dinner. The open sky gave them weather.

A knock at his door. Liss poked her head in—she seemed to have appointed herself the household's herald, along with half a dozen other offices nobody had granted her.

"Mara says come eat. She made fish. Well—" Liss wrinkled her nose, honest as a struck bell, "—she burned the fish. But she says you have to come anyway, because Oskar's telling the story about the rope bridge and he keeps making himself braver every time he tells it."

"How much braver?"

"This time he caught Mara with one hand."

"Tell him I'll verify nothing."

Liss grinned and vanished. Ferro looked at the reports a moment longer—the blighted fields, the thin grain, the ledger of a kingdom learning to stand unassisted—and found, to his mild surprise, that he wasn't afraid of them. Numbers could be survived. Weather could be survived. What could not be survived was the slow drain he had spent three years standing next to without seeing, the quiet arithmetic of a city feeding itself, child by child, into a dead man's mouth.

He went to eat burned fish with people who had pulled him out of a mountain.

* * *

The second hearing was worse than the first, because this time Chancellor Rell's name came up.

Rell had been in custody for five weeks—held, technically, on charges of misappropriation of palace funds, because no one yet dared speak aloud what he had actually done. There was no law against sustaining a hollow king. No statute covered it. The legal scholars were drafting frantically, and meanwhile the man who had fed a kingdom to a corpse for over a decade sat in a comfortable cell awaiting charges that did not exist yet.

"He'll argue necessity," said Corren, when the session turned to it. "He's already arguing it. Order above all. The alternative was collapse, civil war, panic. You've seen the granary numbers, Ferro. Imagine them twelve years ago, with no warning, no preparation."

"I have imagined them," Ferro said. "I imagine them every night."

"And?"

"And he had twelve years to prepare the kingdom for the truth and spent every one of them preparing himself instead." Ferro leaned forward, elbows on the rail. "Chancellor, Magistrate—there's a version of Rell's story where he's a tragic guardian. I've met that version. He believes it. That's the horror of him; he's not lying to us, he's lying to himself with tremendous discipline. But look at the end of the story. Look at what he was planning when they took the Vault. He wasn't going to free theBinding. He wasn't going to confess. He was going to climb inside it. Move the anchor from the dead king to himself. Whatever necessity bought at the beginning, ambition owned by the end."

Corren studied him. "You were his creature once."

"Yes." No flinch. He had practiced not flinching. "He gave me my commission. He promoted me to hunt the keybearer, and I went, and I am alive today because the keybearer chose not to kill me when every reason of self-preservation said she should." He spread his hands. "I know what mercy looks like from the receiving end, Magistrate. It looks like Mara Almeida deciding that a man is more than his worst orders. I recommend the practice."

That night, walking home through the Nightmarket—which had reopened, stalls and lanterns and hawkers shouting prices, louder than Ferro had ever known it—he passed the charm-seller's row and stopped.

The stalls were different now. Before, they had sold knots and charms the way everyone sold them: pretty things, harmless things, superstition as decoration. Now there was a new stall, run by a round-faced girl of maybe fourteen, teaching knotwork to a cluster of children while their mothers shopped. Simple patterns. Knots that held. Luck made by hand, the girl kept saying, luck you make yourself, not luck that gets done to you.

One of the children was tying a clumsy wolf-shape in red cord, tongue between his teeth.

Ferro watched for a while. Then he bought a knot he did not need, paid too much for it, and carried it home in his pocket like a talisman, knowing perfectly well it held no magic at all, which was, he was beginning to suspect, the entire point.

* * *

On the day of the final hearing, the true king was buried.

They buried him quietly, at dawn, in the crypt beneath the palace—no state funeral, no lying-in-state, because the council had voted that Vharen had mourned a fiction long enough and deserved to grieve a man instead. Aldous III, last of his line. Keeper of the Binding, prisoner of it, released from it by a woman he had never met. Ferro stood honor guard with four soldiers who had volunteered, and read the words of committal in a steady voice, and did not cry until the stone was sealed, and then only briefly, and told no one.

*Twelve years awake inside the dark,* he thought. *And his last words were thanks.*

There should have been a sermon in that. Maybe someday there would be. For now it was enough to carry it.

Then the hearing.

This time the chamber was full—guildmasters, merchants, the priestess, a delegation from the farming villages east of the mountains, gray-eyed children's parents sitting in the gallery with the particular stillness of people who have stopped being afraid and haven't yet learned what to do with the space where fear used to live.

Corren called him early. "Inquisitor Ferro. Former Inquisitor Ferro." A ripple of something—not quite laughter, not quite grief. "The council must decide, in the coming weeks, three questions. First, what to do with Chancellor Rell. Second, what to do about the harvest. Third—and this is the one that frightens my colleagues most—what to do about the return of sigilcraft."

Every eye in the room came around to him.

"There are those," Corren continued, "who say the Purges were justified—that the craft is corruption, that the Binding proves it, that any power which can anchor a kingdom's soul can enslave one. And there are those who say the opposite—that the guild was Vharen's immune system, and cutting it out left us open to exactly the infection we suffered. You served the Crown that conducted the Purges. You fought beside a sigilcrafter who undid the worst act of the Crown's history. Speak to both factions. We will listen."

Ferro rose. He had prepared remarks. He abandoned them.

"Twelve years ago I would have told you that magic is a knife and knives are dangerous, and I would have been right, and it would have been the smallest true thing available," he said. "Here is a larger one. The Binding didn't corrupt because it was magic. It corrupted because it was *secret*. Twelve people in a vault, holding a kingdom's luck in the dark, accountable to no one—that's not a guild, that's a wound with a membership roster. And the answer the old Crown found wasn't to bring it into the light. It was to burn the light-keepers and leave the wound alone to fester until it grew teeth and learned to wear our king's face."

He paused. Somewhere in the gallery a baby fussed and was hushed.

"So no, I don't fear the craft returning. I fear the dark returning. If the guild reopens—and I'm told it intends to, openly, with a charter anyone can read—then let it reopen under the same law that binds the rest of us. Let them teach in daylight. Let them keep records. Let any citizen walk in and see what's being done and ask why. Sunlight isn't the enemy of power. It's the only thing that's ever kept power honest—including mine." He looked down at the ring on his hand, the inquisitor's band he had not yet taken off. "Especially mine."

Silence. Then Osselmann, of all people, grunted: "The harvest won't wait for philosophy, Inquisitor. What do we tell the farmers?"

Ferro almost smiled. "Tell them the truth. The luck is gone. All of it. We're going to have thin years and fat years and no one to blame but weather and ourselves." He looked around the chamber, at the scraped seal, the blank stone, the frightened competent faces of a government being born middle-aged. "And tell them something else. Tell them their children woke up. Tell them the price has already been paid—in advance, by people most of them never thanked. Everything from here is interest on a debt we didn't incur. We can afford to be unlucky for a while. We cannot afford to forget."

* * *

Afterward, Corren caught him in the corridor, alone.

"The vote on Rell comes Thursday," she said without preamble. "Treason. There's no statute, so we're writing one, and applying it retroactively, which every jurist in the city says is monstrous and every citizen says is late. I want your testimony again. And I want something else." Her eyes were sharp and tired. "He's asked for you. Refused to speak to anyone else. Says he'll explain the Binding—how it was made, what it cost, what safeguards existed—if he can address the man who hunted the keybearer."

Ferro stood very still. Outside the corridor's high windows, evening was coming down over Vharen, smoke and gulls and the first lamps of the Nightmarket kindling in the streets below, the whole sprawling ordinary miracle of a city that had nearly died of order and was now, messily, expensively, learning to live without it.

"Why me?" he asked, though he suspected he knew.

"Because you're the only man in Vharen," Corren said, "who hated him and owes his life to his enemies, and he knows it. He thinks you'll understand him." She turned to go, then added, flatly: "Thursday. Think hard about whether understanding him is a service to this council or a gift to him."

She left him in the corridor with the question, and with the little red knot in his pocket—luck made by hand, worth nothing, worth everything—and somewhere below in the gathering dusk a child laughed, loudly, inconveniently, alive.

Thursday, then. He had three days to decide what a decent man owes to the devil who believed he was building heaven.

He started walking toward the Nightmarket, toward the smell of burned fish and the sound of a smuggler inflating his own legend, and the lamps came on around him one by one, unanchored, unbound, burning anyway.
