## 36. The Empty Shop

The key still fit. That was the first cruelty of it.

Mara had expected the lock to have changed, or rusted shut, or to have been bricked over entirely by an inquisitorial writ—some physical sign that the woman who last turned this iron stub of a key no longer existed. But the mechanism gave its familiar grinding cough, the little hitch at the third tumbler she'd always meant to have repaired and never had, and the door of the Nightmarket shop swung inward on hinges that complained in exactly the voice she remembered.

The smell hit her next. Dust, yes, and the sweet rot of abandoned dried lavender, but underneath it still the ghost of beeswax and river-salt soap and the faint coppery tang of dye—the smell of ten thousand luck-knots worked and sold and carried out of this room into the pockets of strangers. It was the smell of her own hands' labor. She stood on the threshold with her travel bag digging into her shoulder and breathed it in like a woman testing whether a well still held water.

It did. And that was the cruelty: everything was here except her.

* * *

Three weeks since the mountain. Three weeks since dawn came up gray and cold over the scree and Oskar wrapped his coat around her shoulders because she was shaking and could not say why. She knew why now, or she knew the shape of it: the Unbinding had taken its price thread by thread, and the price had been paid in the coin of herself. She had come down from Mount Serel carrying a body that knew how to tie a guild knot and a mind that could not remember learning.

Faces without names. That was what remained. A bearded man laughing over a ledger—her master? Her father? An old woman's hands guiding hers through a five-loop weave. A song hummed in a kitchen she could not place, in a voice that might have been her mother's, that stopped mid-hum whenever she reached for it, as if the memory were a bird that flew when approached.

She had written to Ferro about it once, a letter she never sent, and the sentence she had labored over most was this one: *I am a stranger wearing my own calluses.* The hands were hers. The hands remembered everything. Only the woman who owned them was missing.

And now here was the shop, and here was the workbench with its worn groove where she'd braced her elbow for twelve years, and here was the little clay pot—still!—holding her bone needles, and Mara understood that she had not come home. She had come to visit a dead woman's rooms.

"Mara?" Liss's voice from behind her, bright and careful at once, the way the girl had learned to speak around silences. "You're standing in the doorway like it might bite."

"Everything bites if you wait long enough," Mara said, and stepped inside, and set her bag down on a floor that creaked welcome.

* * *

They spent the first hour simply opening things.

Liss went through the back room where the stock had been left under dust cloths, and every cloth she pulled away released a small weather system of lint and memory. Bundles of dyed cord, hanging in hanks from their pegs—madder-red, woad-blue, the ochre that only took properly if you mordanted it in rainwater collected during a storm, a piece of craft-knowledge Mara discovered she still possessed, intact and useless, like a song in a language whose country no longer existed on any map.

"There must be four hundred knots here," Liss said, lifting an armload of finished charms from a cedar box. Luck-knots with their traditional three interlaced loops. Fever-charms braided tight as closed fists. The pretty nonsense trinkets Mara had sold for twelve years while hiding the true work beneath them, harmless as river stones, legal as bread.

Mara picked one up. Her fingers moved without instruction, reading the braid the way a blind woman reads a page: *over two, under one, capture, release.* Twelve years of daily practice lived in her tendons. She could feel exactly where the maker—you—had doubled the tension on the third pass, could feel the small hesitation where you'd nearly done the forbidden thing, the real thing, the sixth loop that would have made it not a trinket but a sigil, and had caught yourself and backed out.

*I did that,* she thought. *I made this. I remember making it and I do not remember being the one who made it.*

She set it down gently, as one sets down something sleeping.

"Mara." Liss had gone quiet in the way that meant she'd noticed. The girl was eleven and had learned more about grief in one burning waystation night than most people learn in forty years, and she watched Mara now with those too-serious eyes. "Does it hurt? Being here?"

"No," Mara said honestly. "That's what hurts. It should hurt. Grief is supposed to hurt, and I look at all this—my whole life—and I feel like a visitor reading someone else's letters."

Liss considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she held up a fever-charm, the tight-braided kind. "Did I ever see you make these? Before?"

"On the road. In the camps. You watched me dozens of times."

"Do you remember watching yourself?"

The question landed with a child's unerring precision. Mara laughed—a short, surprised sound, half pain. "No. I remember your face watching. That's all I have of it. Your face, and my hands."

"Then keep both," Liss said, as if it were simple, as children do, and went back to unpacking, and Mara stood among the ghosts of her own industry and let the words settle somewhere deep.

* * *

By afternoon the shop had begun, grudgingly, to resemble itself. They opened the shutters and the thin autumn light of Vharen fell across the counter in long dusty bars. Outside, the Nightmarket moved in its old rhythms—cries of chestnut-sellers, the clatter of the fish-cart wheels on cobbles still slick from morning tide, two women arguing cheerfully over the price of eels. The city sounded the same. That was the strangest thing. Vharen did not know yet that it had changed.

Or perhaps it hadn't changed, Mara thought. Perhaps they had only cut away the lie that had been holding it upright, and what remained was simply—Vharen. Ungoverned by anything but weather and work and the mercy of neighbors. Some days that felt like freedom. Some days, in the hour before sleep, it felt like abandonment, and she would wake Oskar asking whether the crops in the eastern villages had come in, and he would groan and tell her that Ferro's last letter said the barley was thin but standing, *thin but standing, woman, go to sleep*, and she would lie awake listening to the city breathe and think: *I did this. I broke the thing and gave them the world with no net under it.*

She was reaching for a shelf of ribbon spools when her hand betrayed her.

It happened the way it always happened now—without warning, the body reaching for a tool the mind had forgotten owning. Her fingers brushed the ribbon, measured a length by eye alone, found the end, and began to tie. Overhand. Loop. The beginning of a weaver's catch-knot, the foundation of every binding sigil the guild had ever worked, the first stitch of magic that could anchor a kingdom's fortune or devour it.

Her hands knew it the way her lungs knew air.

Her mind stood outside them like a mourner at a window.

She watched her own fingers move for perhaps four seconds—an eternity, a whole small life—and then she slammed her hand flat against the counter hard enough to sting, and the knot collapsed, and she stood there breathing like a woman who had surfaced from deep water.

Liss was beside her instantly. She didn't ask. She had seen it before, on the road down from Serel—Mara reaching in sleep for knots that unraveled even as she tied them, waking with tears on her face and no knowledge of why. The girl simply took Mara's stinging hand and held it flat between both of her own small ones, pressing it to the counter as though pinning a moth, until the trembling stopped.

"It's just your hands being lonely," Liss said. "That's all. Hands get lonely when they can't talk to each other anymore."

"What do you mean by that?"

"I don't know," Liss admitted. "But it sounds right, doesn't it."

It did. God help her, it did. Her left hand and her right, trained apart for twelve years—one hand for selling trinkets, one hand for hidden truth—now both unemployed, both fluent in a language that no longer existed, trying to converse across the silence where the guild used to live.

* * *

Oskar arrived at dusk with boxes.

He came the way he came everywhere, loudly, filling the doorway with shoulders and complaint. "Fourteen boxes," he announced, setting the first down with a thump that rattled the bone-needle pot. "From the warehouse. Your landlady's cousin has been charging me harbor rates to store them, which is theft, and I told him so, and then I paid him, because apparently I'm honest now. Do you know what that does to a man's reputation? I'll never smuggle again. People will *talk*."

"You retired," Mara said. "Ferro witnessed the papers."

"I retired," Oskar agreed darkly, "and my reputation hasn't accepted it." He looked around the shop—at the opened shutters, the cleaned counter, the hanks of cord rehung on their pegs—and something in his loudness quieted, the way it always did in small rooms that mattered. He had been there, at the end. He had held the corridor. He never spoke of it, and he never needed to; it lived between them now the way a scar lives in the skin, felt in cold weather without being discussed.

"Well," he said. "Here it is. Your life. Fourteen boxes of it." He hesitated, and then, gruffly, almost angrily gentle: "You don't have to open them all tonight. Or ever. Some things keep."

"Some things are meant to be kept," Mara said, and heard in her own voice an echo of something Aldric used to say, and flinched, because she could not remember Aldric saying it—only that the words had arrived in her mouth from somewhere, freighted with a weight she couldn't see.

Oskar saw the flinch. He pretended he hadn't, which was his particular kindness, and began ostentatiously prying the lid off a box of packing straw. "Right. If I'm to be a shopkeep's porter I'll want supper. There's a pie man on the corner of Tanner's Row who owes me—"

"Everyone owes you."

"—who owes me nothing anymore because I'm *honest*, but who sells a mutton pie that will change your religion. Liss! Supper run. Two coins on the hook by the door. Don't let him short you on the crust, the crust is the best part and he hoards it."

Liss was gone in a jingle of coins and flying elbows, and the shop settled into the amber quiet of early evening, and Mara stood before the seventh box—the one she had been circling all afternoon—and made herself open it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were her mother's tools.

She had known they were there. She had packed them herself—or, rather, a woman who was her had packed them, and that woman's reasons were sealed away with everything else. Now Mara lifted the oilcloth and uncovered the guild kit: the bone awl with its worn-w smooth handle. The silver measuring cord, coiled like a sleeping adder, marked at intervals with tiny stamped glyphs. A horn box of knot-stones, each one engraved with a single character of the old name-script, each one, she knew without knowing how she knew, holding the true name of some long-dead guildswoman, carried in trust so that no name would ever be lost.

Names. The Vault below Serel had been full of them—thousands of them, a cathedral of bound names, and she had pulled them apart thread by thread while her memories bled out of her in payment. She had freed the dead king. She had freed the living kingdom. And the toll had been exacted from the one account she could not refill.

She held the horn box and understood, finally, the precise shape of her loss. It was not that she had lost her power. Power was a thing of anchors and bindings, and the world was better off without it—she believed that, she had *chosen* it, she would choose it again. No: what she had lost was her *story*. Every person is a story they tell themselves in the dark, and hers had been taken apart to pay for the ending, and now she sat in the middle of her own book like a stranger reading it, admiring the craft, recognizing nothing.

"I don't know who I am without it," she said aloud. Saying it to the box. Saying it to the empty shop. Saying it, perhaps, to the woman she'd been, wherever that woman was—in the threads of the great sigil, in the memory of the mountain, in the ledger Oskar kept and would not show her.

The shop said nothing. Shops are good at that. It had listened to twelve years of her silences and it offered her another one, companionable as an old dog.

* * *

Liss came back with three pies and news.

"The market's all talking," she reported through crust, sitting cross-legged on the counter because chairs were for other people. "About the wolf."

Mara looked up. "What wolf?"

"Not a real wolf. Painted. On the old customs house—the big wall facing the square. Somebody painted a huge wolf on it overnight, white on the dark stone, and nobody knows who. And under it somebody chalked—" Liss screwed up her face, reciting carefully, "—'*the house that serves, does not consume.*'" 

The words went through Mara like cold water through cloth.

*We serve, we do not consume.* The guild's first oath. She knew that now—from Ferro's letters, from the pages of Aldric's notes that Liss had studied all through the descent, from the fragments others had carried for her. She had spoken those words once at the Vault's threshold to a mad old man who had forgotten them, and they had broken him open like a chisel breaks stone. But she did not remember learning them. They had come back to her secondhand, her own heritage delivered to her like a parcel, addressed in someone else's hand.

"Who would paint that?" she asked, though her heart had begun, quietly, terribly, to guess.

"Lots of people are asking. Ferro says the provisional council wants it scrubbed off by tomorrow. But the fishwives won't stop touching it, Liss says—I mean I says—they go up and put their palms on the wall like it's a shrine. Marta of the eel-boats told everybody the wolf watched over her stall all through the bad years. People are leaving bread under it." Liss licked butter off her thumb, matter-of-fact as weather. "I think it means the city's ready."

"Ready for what?"

But Liss only looked at her—that level, ancient-child look she had, the one that saw straight down through eleven years and one burned wagon and a mountain full of secrets—and did not answer, and went on eating her pie, and outside the shuttered window the Nightmarket murmured on in the dark like a tide coming back in.

Oskar, wisely pretending to be absorbed in a box of ribbons, said nothing at all. But Mara saw him glance at the girl, and then at her, and saw the corner of his mouth move—the expression of a man watching a fire catch in a hearth everyone thought was cold.

* * *

Later, after Oskar had gone home to his lodgings above the chandlery and the lamps were trimmed and the door locked against the night, Mara stood alone in the middle of the shop floor and let the emptiness speak.

It was not, she discovered, an empty emptiness. It was the emptiness of a held breath. Of a stage before the play. Of a knot not yet tied, cord lying loose on the table, all its possibility still gathered in it, waiting for hands.

Her hands lay at her sides, lonely, silent, fluent in a dead language.

But language dies and is reborn—it always had been, always would be—and the child asleep upstairs in the loft, dreaming whatever fierce and practical dreams she dreamed, had already begun tying her first clumsy, holy, unmagical knots for the neighbor children in the yard of the tenement, and the fishwives were putting their palms on a painted wolf, and somewhere in Oskar's lodgings a ledger sat in a drawer containing every lost day of her life, written down by a smuggler's careful angry loving hand.

She was not the woman who built this shop's trade. That woman was spent, paid out, honorably discharged into the making of a freer world.

But the light came in these windows in the mornings. The chestnut-man cried his wares at the corner. Children ran in the street below, and children needed fever-charms even if the charms were only cord and care and the patience of someone who stayed. The work was not the magic. It never had been. She was beginning, dimly, in the dark of the empty shop, to understand that the magic had only ever been the excuse for the work.

Mara touched the counter's worn groove—her groove, whoever she had been when she wore it into the wood—and whispered something. She wasn't sure to whom. To the guild. To the empty air. To herself.

Then she banked the lamp and climbed the stairs, and passed the loft where Liss slept tangled in blankets like a kitten in yarn, and paused, because on the small table beside the girl's bed lay a scrap of paper covered in pencil marks—diagrams, loops, notes in a careful childish hand. Knots. Not sigils. Simply knots, annotated with what they did and how they helped and whom they were for, numbered neatly, as if at the head of a list that intended to grow very, very long.

At the top of the page, in letters pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, Liss had written a title:

**Lessons — Book One.**

Below it, smaller, shyly, as if the ink itself was blushing: *for the children, and also for Mara, when she is ready.*

Mara stood a long time in the dark, reading those words by the light of the streetlamp through the shutters, her lost life humming in her hands like struck bells, and understood that tomorrow the shop would not be empty after all.
