## 37. Knots That Hold

The children came on the third morning, the way sparrows come to a crumb of bread—first one, then two, then a whole noisy scattering of them.

Liss had not planned it that way. She had only been sitting on the back step of Mara's shop, the one that opened onto Tallow Row, working a length of red wool through her fingers because her hands got restless when they were empty. Restless hands were something she understood. There had been a whole season of her life when her hands would not stop shaking, and no one could tell her why, and then Mara had sat down across from her at a waystation fire and said, *Here, watch me,* and shown her a knot that was just a knot, nothing bound inside it, nothing hungry, and Liss's hands had gone quiet for the first time since her parents' wagon burned.

So she worked the wool. Over and under and through. The pattern Mara called *the sleeper's coil*, though Mara said she couldn't remember where the name came from anymore—that was one of the holes in her, one of the many, and Liss had learned not to poke at them, the same way you learned not to press on a bruise.

"Whatcha making?"

Liss looked up. A boy stood at the mouth of the alley, maybe seven, with flour dusted up both arms to the elbows like he'd been wrestling a sack of it. She knew him vaguely from the market—a baker's son, always underfoot, always loud.

"A knot," she said.

"It's just a knot."

"It's never just a knot." She held it out so he could see how the loops nested into each other, how the whole thing held its shape even loose, even unfastened to anything. "This one's for sleeping. You put it under your pillow and your dreams stay soft."

"That's magic."

"No," Liss said, and found, saying it, that the word didn't hurt the way it used to. "It's craft. Magic is when you take something from someone else to make it work. This is just—" she turned it over in her palm, hunting for the right words, the ones Mara would use if Mara were sitting here instead of inside counting jars of fever-salve, "—this is just luck you make yourself. With your own two hands. Nobody pays for it. Nobody loses anything."

The boy squinted at it the way children squint at anything adults insist is simple. Then he held out his floured hand. "Show me."

* * *

By the end of the week there were nine of them.

They came after the morning market emptied out, when Tallow Row went quiet and the smell of frying onions drifted down from the cookshop two doors over. They came with string stolen from their mothers' sewing baskets and twine begged off the fishmonger and one girl, Pip's older sister, who brought an entire hank of dyed blue wool that she guarded like treasure and doled out an inch at a time. They sat in a rough half-circle on the step and the cobblestones beyond it, and Liss taught them what Mara had taught her, and what Aldric's notes—the ones Liss had copied out herself, in the dark of the Vault, holding a pattern steady while Mara forgot everything she loved—had added besides.

That was the part Liss kept quiet, mostly. That the knots she knew weren't all secondhand. Some of them had come through her own fingers first, invented in the terrible year after the fire, when sleep wouldn't come and the only thing that helped was making things hold together. The *quiet-knot* for nightmares. The *brave-loop*, which was nonsense, really, pure superstition, but she'd made one for herself before every hard thing since and she wasn't going to stop now. The little three-strand braid she'd worked out that was supposed to keep a family safe on the road, and which she'd tied into Oskar's coat hem without telling him, and which he pretended not to have noticed, and which he also, she was fairly sure, had never once cut out.

She taught them the rule first, every time, new children and old. It was the rule Mara had given her at the fire, and later Liss had found it written in Aldric's cramped hand in the margins of the map, and later still she'd found it carved into the lintel of the ruined guildhouse above the Vault, half worn away: *We serve, we do not consume.*

"You never tie a knot around somebody else's name," she told them, standing over the half-circle with her hands on her hips, feeling absurd and enormous at once. "You never tie a knot to make somebody do something. And if anybody ever tells you a knot needs blood, or a lock of hair, or a piece of somebody—you run, and you come find me, or Mara, or the big grumpy man who buys too many apples. Understood?"

"Oskar's not grumpy," said the baker's boy, whose name was Tam.

"Oskar is extremely grumpy."

"He cried at the wolf ceremony."

"He did *not*—"

"He did. I saw. Ferro saw too and they both pretended there was smoke in their eyes. Indoors."

Liss let them have their laughter. It was good for them, laughing. Half these children had been born in the gray years, when the city's luck was being eaten out of it from underneath, when babies came into the world listless and the wells ran bitter and nobody could say why except that Vharen had stopped being lucky and started being merely alive. The gray years were over. The children's eyes were clear now—all of them, everywhere, the healers said, every child born since the Unbinding came into the light with ordinary brown eyes and ordinary appetites and ordinary, unremarkable, miraculous health. But the gray years lived on in the parents, in the way mothers still counted fingers and toes twice, in the way fathers still touched wood at every small good fortune as if luck were a bird that might startle away.

Which was, Liss supposed, exactly why they came. Not for the children—for themselves. She'd noticed it the second week: the mothers who lingered at the mouth of the alley, pretending to gossip, watching the knots go together. The fishmonger who'd given them twine and then, the next day, asked in a too-casual voice whether a person could learn such a thing at any age. The old widow from the corner house who left a basket of plums on the step and a note that said only, *My mother was guild-kin, before. I remember the signs. Thank you.*

Luck made by hand. That was what Mara called it, when she talked about what the guild should have been all along. Not power hoarded in a vault below a mountain, not fortune anchored to kings and siphoned by hungry things, but this: a knot in a child's pocket, a charm on a doorpost, a stranger teaching a stranger's kid to braid string on a warm afternoon. Small magics. Honest ones. The kind that cost the maker nothing but time.

* * *

Mara watched from the window.

Liss knew she did. She'd catch the shape of her at the glass some mornings, still as a painting, a jar of salve forgotten in her hands, watching the half-circle of children with an expression Liss had never quite managed to name. It wasn't sadness. It wasn't joy either. It was closer to what you'd feel looking at a house you'd lived in long ago, seeing the windows lit again after years dark—gladness and grief sharing the same face, each one making room for the other.

Today Mara came out.

She did it rarely. Liss had learned not to make a thing of it when she did, because Mara hated a fuss, and because there was still a flinch in her, deep down, whenever a crowd gathered around her—even a crowd of flour-dusted children, even a crowd that adored her without knowing why. Twelve years hiding, and then the Vault, and then the forgetting. Liss figured a person earned the right to take the world in small doses.

But today she came out, and she sat down on the bottom step, right in the middle of them, cross-legged like she was eight years old herself, and she picked up a length of twine.

"Show me the sleeper's coil," she said. "I'm told I've forgotten it."

The children went reverent and silent, the way they always did when Mara spoke, and Liss felt the familiar twist in her chest—pride and protectiveness braided tight together, a knot of its own. Mara remembered almost nothing of her guild years. Faces without names, Oskar said. She knew *that* she had been a sigilwright the way you know a story about someone else. Her hands remembered more than her head did—they moved sometimes at night, Liss had seen it, tracing patterns on the blanket—but the meanings had gone into the dark with everything else.

Except. Except that when Liss walked her through the coil, slow, loop by loop, Mara's fingers followed along and then, halfway through, stopped needing to be shown. They finished the last three movements on their own, quick and sure, and Mara stared down at the finished knot in her palm like it was a letter from a dead friend.

"I knew that," she whispered.

"You knew lots of things," Liss said gently. "It's still in you. It just doesn't know how to introduce itself anymore."

Mara laughed—a wet, surprised sound—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and the children, bless them, pretended very hard to be busy with their string.

"Teach me another," Mara said.

So Liss did. She taught her the brave-loop, and Mara made one and tucked it into her sleeve without explanation, and Liss didn't ask, because some knots you don't explain. She taught her the three-strand road-braid, and Mara's hands faltered on that one, slowed, and then moved with a strange deliberate care, like someone walking through a room they'd lived in as a child—everything half-familiar, everything lit from behind.

"My mother," Mara said suddenly, quietly, to no one in particular. "There was a braid. She used to—I can't see her face. But my hands remember the braid."

And she sat there in the afternoon sun with flour-dusted children all around her, braiding air, braiding memory, tears running freely down a face that was smiling, and Liss looked at her and felt the understanding settle over her like a shawl: the guild isn't dead. It was never dead. It had only changed shape, the way water changes shape when you pour it from jug to cup. All those vaults and seals and sworn oaths and hidden names—it had all been the same thing underneath, the same single simple current: people taking care of people, luck passed hand to hand, thread by thread. The Vault had been one vessel for it. This cracked step on Tallow Row was another.

The guild wasn't buried under Mount Serel. It was sitting right here, learning a children's knot in the sun.

* * *

Tam's mother came for him at the end, as she always did, wiping her hands on her apron and calling his name down the row. But today she lingered after he ran off, and stood in front of Liss with a strange, stiff formality, like a woman rehearsing a speech.

"They say you're the keybearer's daughter," she said.

"I'm not anybody's daughter," Liss said, before she could stop herself. "I mean—I am. Just not hers by blood. It's complicated."

"The keybearer saved my Tam's life." The woman's chin trembled slightly. "During the sickness, before. When the luck went out of the city. He was gray-eyed for a month. We thought—we'd already—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "I don't have money. But I've got flour, and eggs, and a strong back. If there's ever work. Cleaning, carrying, whatever the shop needs. I want to give something back. We all do. Half the row does. They're just shy about saying it."

Liss blinked at her. Something hot and unexpected pressed up behind her eyes.

"We'll need help soon, actually," she heard herself say. "Real help. Because—" and here she hesitated, because she hadn't told anyone yet, not even Mara, not even Oskar, because saying it out loud would make it real and real things could be taken away. But the woman stood there waiting, offering her strong back and her eggs, and Liss thought about the note from the plum-basket widow, and the fishmonger's too-casual question, and the way the mothers gathered at the mouth of the alley every morning like tide coming in.

Because the truth was that the shop was too small. Had been too small for a month. Because Mara's salves sold out by midmorning and people were starting to come from other quarters, other districts, asking after the knot-teacher and the charm-witch and the wolf-house, and because last night Liss had lain awake doing sums in her head and realized that what was happening on Tallow Row wasn't a shop anymore. It was a beginning. It needed walls, and a sign, and a ledger—

A ledger.

The thought arrived whole, fully formed, like a knot tying itself: they would need a record. Names of students, patterns taught, charms given, debts owed and paid, kindnesses received like the flour-woman's, all of it written down so it could be handed on. A guild keeps records. That was one of the few things Mara still knew about being a guild, because Oskar had read it to her from his book of memories, over and over, until it stuck.

"Yes," Liss said. "Come tomorrow. Bring Tam. We're going to need everyone."

The woman smiled—wobbly, wet-eyed, real—and hurried off after her son, and Liss stood alone on the step in the gold late light, surrounded by the day's abandoned knots, dozens of them, scattered across the cobbles like bright shed skins. Sleeper's coils and brave-loops and road-braids, luck made by hand, luck made by children, luck that belonged to no king and fed on no one and would still be here, she thought, when every crown in the world had crumbled to dust.

She bent and began gathering them up, one by one, to give back tomorrow.

Inside, she could hear Mara moving among the jars, humming something tuneless and half-remembered—and beneath it, from the front of the shop, the low rumble of Oskar's voice, and Ferro's answering laugh, and the ordinary sounds of a family closing up for the evening. Liss paused in the doorway with her arms full of string, and thought: *someone should write all this down.* Every knot, every name, every small beginning. Someone should keep the record, so that nothing—not a purge, not a vault, not a forgetting—could ever take it again.

She was still standing there, turning the idea over, when Mara looked up from her jars and caught her expression, and frowned, and said:

"Liss? What is it? What are you thinking?"
