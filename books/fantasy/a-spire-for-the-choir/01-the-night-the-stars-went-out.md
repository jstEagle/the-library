## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

I was inking the coastline of a country that doesn't exist when the first star went out.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only inheritance my mother left me that doesn't ache to hold. It was the star at the horn of the Hunter—the bright one, the one children in the valley are taught to wish on, which is a cruel thing to teach children, since wishing on it has never once worked. It didn't flicker. It didn't dim. It simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker dies mid-word, and the dark where it had been was somehow darker than the dark around it, as if the sky had a hole in it rather than an absence.

I remember thinking, with the smugness of the very tired: *bad ink.*

That is the occupational hazard of a cartographer. When the world misbehaves, we assume the fault is in the map. I'd been working for six hours on a coastline I invented whole cloth—a realm I called Vessany, because it sounded like a place where the bread would be good—and I was on the last promontory, the one that curls like a question mark, when the Hunter lost his horn and I decided my ink had gone bad. I capped the bottle. I held the pen to the lamp. The ink was fine. The ink was, in fact, the only thing in the valley that was fine.

The second star went out while I was still lying to myself. Then the third. Then, in the manner of disasters, all of them at once.

I stepped out onto my mother's old porch with the pen still in my hand, because apparently when the sky ends, my instinct is to take notes. The valley was doing what the valley always does at night: breathing. Woodsmoke from the hearths, the river muttering to itself over its stones, the pines doing their slow pine business of being dark and patient. And above it all, where ten thousand stars had been when I went inside, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not fog. The valley's nights are always a little hazy, the stars softened like fruit under cloth. This was different. This was a ceiling. This was the sky deciding to be a lid.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I am telling you now because you are a page and pages keep secrets: I felt it happen. Not saw—*felt*. Each star going out landed in my chest like a knuckle tapping a window, one after another, a polite and terrible knocking, and when the last one died the knocking stopped and something behind the glass of my ribs simply *gave*.

The wards.

Every child in the Hollow Valley knows the wards the way fish know water, which is to say not at all. You grow up inside them. The valley is a bowl of green tucked between ridges that no surveyor has ever successfully climbed, ringed by a mist that doesn't move and doesn't thin, and if you asked anyone what kept the mist out, they'd look at you as if you'd asked what keeps the ground down. Nothing. It just is. The mist is the edge of the world, and we are the world, and that is the whole of the geography lesson.

But I felt the wards shatter like glass in my chest—felt it in the specific way you feel a bone break, which is to say a half-second before the pain arrives, in that golden interval where you know what's happened but your body is still negotiating. Somewhere out along the ridgeline, something old had been holding hands with something else old, and the hands had let go.

The pen was still in my hand. I looked down and discovered that at some point, without my permission, I had drawn a single line on the Vessany map—a long unbroken stroke running off the edge of the page, out of my invented country, into the margin, off the desk, as if the line had somewhere to be and I was merely holding the pen for it.

I did not sleep. I want that on the record. Whatever the elder says happened, whatever the village decides happened, I was awake, at my desk, holding a pen that was drawing lines I didn't command, when the sky went out.

* * *

By dawn, the mist had stopped being scenery and started being a question.

It had come down off the ridges overnight—not all the way, not yet, but visibly, the way a tide comes in while you're watching something else. It stood now at the tree line above the barley terraces, a gray wall with a texture to it, like wool, like the inside of a shell. People kept walking to the edge of their fields to look at it and then walking back, and nobody said what they were all thinking, which was that the mist had never in living memory come *down*.

Old Marta found me on the porch, because Old Marta finds everyone; it is her gift and her punishment. She is the elder the way a keel is the elder of a boat—unlovely, essential, and mostly noticed when things tip. She stood at the bottom of my steps with her stick planted like a flag and looked at me the way she looks at weather she doesn't approve of.

"You're the mapmaker," she said.

"I'm a mapmaker. There's a distinction. There are four of us."

"You're the one who maps things that aren't there."

I had no defense prepared for this, because it was true. I draw realms no one has ever seen. Coastlines of nowhere, mountain ranges of nothing, cities I name after dead pets and grudges. The village finds it charming in the way villages find anything charming that keeps a person fed without being useful. My mother did it before me. Her maps were better. Her maps were *impossibly* better, which is a sentence I will come back to, I promise you, because it is going to matter.

"Marta," I said, "the stars went out."

"I have eyes."

"And the mist is coming down."

"I have legs. I walked here through its edge, and my knees ache where it touched them, which mist has never done, because mist is not *supposed* to have edges that touch." Her stick tapped the boards. "The ward-stones at the shrine are cold. All seven. Cold as river-bottom. You're the one in this valley who deals in the shapes of the world, Quintana. Tell me what's happening."

And there it was. The first night the world had ever asked me for anything.

I want to be fair to her. She was frightened, and Marta frightened is Marta with the fear filed down into demand, and I was the nearest thing the valley had to an authority on elsewhere. But I stood on my porch with my dead mother's morning quiet around me and understood that I had nothing. Not a theory, not a comfort, not even a good lie. I had a map of a country I invented with a line running off it like a wound, and a chest of my mother's papers I had never had the courage to read all the way through, and a feeling in my chest like the memory of breaking glass.

"I don't know," I said.

It came out smaller than I meant it to. Marta's eyes narrowed, which in her face is an act of mercy; when she disbelieves you entirely, her eyes do the opposite.

"You always know," she said. "It's the most irritating thing about you. You look at a ridge and tell me what's behind it. You drew the millrace reroute before the flood did, and the flood agreed with you."

"That was luck."

"That was *twice*." She climbed one step, which for Marta is a military escalation. "The Quintanas have always been strange, girl. Your mother most of all. She used to stand out here at night doing something to the sky with her hands, and the sky did what she—"

The mist moved.

I felt it before I saw it, the way you feel someone enter a room behind you. A pressure, a turning, a sensation like a held breath being taken deeper. Down along the whole crescent of the valley, the gray wall slid forward a hundred paces, smooth as a drawer closing, and swallowed the upper barley terraces whole—stalks, fence posts, a scarecrow I had drawn twice in my childhood maps because I liked its coat. Gone. Not destroyed. *Included.* The mist didn't break things. It absorbed them, the way a page absorbs ink, and I felt a line go through me that was almost exactly like the line my pen had drawn in the night.

Behind me, on the desk, I heard the ink bottle rattle against the glass.

Marta saw it too. Her face did something I had never seen it do, which was to become uncertain. She looked at the mist, and then at me, and then at my window, where the bottle was still rattling, and I watched her assemble a sentence out of forty years of not asking questions about my family.

"Beatrix," she said. First name. She has never once used my first name. "What did your mother *do*?"

"I don't know," I said again, and this time it wasn't small. This time it was the truest sentence I owned. "But I think I'm going to find out."

The mist took another step. Somewhere in it—deep in the gray, at the very limit of what the eye could do—a light moved. Not a star. Stars are far away and indifferent. This light was close, and it was looking, and every hair on my arms stood up and pointed at it like a compass finding north.

Marta's stick hit the porch boards once, hard, the valley's oldest bell.

"Ring the assembly," she called over her shoulder, to no one, to everyone, to the village that was already spilling out of its doors to watch the mist eat its fields. Then, to me, quieter, in the voice you use at a graveside: "Go look in her chest, girl. Whatever you've been avoiding in there. Go look *now*."

I went inside. The ink had gone still. On the desk, the Vessany map had changed—my invented coastline, my invented country, and running through it now, in ink I did not remember spilling, a single word in my mother's handwriting, small and neat and terrible, written across the empty interior of the realm that didn't exist:

*Keystone.*

I had never told her about Vessany. Vessany did not exist. And my dead mother had just written its name across it like a claim.

The mist knocked against the valley's edge, patient as a creditor, and somewhere out under the extinguished sky, I understood with the whole of my skin that whatever came through it next would be coming for me.

I opened the chest.
