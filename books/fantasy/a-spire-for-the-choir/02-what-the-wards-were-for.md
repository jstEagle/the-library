## 2. What the Wards Were For

The chest smelled of cedar and old iron and, underneath both, faintly of my mother's hands.

I had opened it a hundred times before, but always as a thief—lifting the good nibs, the vials of mordant, the brass dividers I still used every day. This time I sat down in front of it like a supplicant, knees on the cold floorboards, lantern turned low, because the word *Keystone* was still drying on the desk behind me and my hands had not stopped shaking since the stars went out.

My mother, Odile Quintana, had been many things: cartographer of repute, keeper of the valley's boundary stones, a woman who could argue with fog and win. She had never once been careless. And yet she'd raised me inside the one place in creation where magic could not follow, with no explanation better than "the air disagrees with you, love," which is what most people say about weather they refuse to understand.

The top layer was ordinary. Survey ledgers. A folded wax-stiff cloak. A bundle of letters from someone whose handwriting looped like ivy, all addressed only to *O.* I set those aside—I was not ready to be jealous of a stranger's loops—and lifted out the false bottom.

Everyone's dead mother has a false bottom in something. Mine was under the tray of pens.

Beneath it lay a map so old it wasn't paper anymore; it was some kind of hide, thin as onion skin, and when I spread it across my knees the lines moved. Not shimmered. *Moved*, slow as glaciers, borders negotiating with each other in the dark. Nine realms, or what had been nine realms before last night's erasure, drawn in ink that glowed faintly like banked coals. At their center, where no honest cartographer would ever put anything, was a valley shaped like a cupped hand.

And written along its rim, in that same small, neat hand I'd seen an hour ago on the Vessany map—the hand I would know anywhere, the hand that had taught mine everything:

*Ward anchored. Child asleep. Do not wake the keystone.*

There was more. Tucked into the fold, a fragment of something larger, torn raggedly, as if the rest had been taken rather than lost. The surviving piece was dense with clauses and signatures—signatures that were not names but events, a comet, a wedding, a war ending—and down in the lower margin, where lawyers put the parts they're ashamed of, ran this:

*...the covenant shall stand upon a living stone, born of both courts, held in the hollow place where neither power may reach, until such time as the stone is asked to bear its weight.*

*Born of both courts.*

I read it four times. My lips were moving by the third, which is a thing I mock other people for doing.

Here is what I knew of my parentage, in full: my mother came to the valley pregnant and tight-lipped. My father was, per her single recorded comment on the subject, "a man of wide country." That was it. That was the entire estate of my inheritance from him—a joke, delivered once, in a tone that closed doors.

*Wide country.*

The valley has no country at all. It sits in the seam between realms like a coin in a crack in the floor, and nothing magical can enter it, which everyone said made it safe, and I had always heard *safe for us*. Lying on my mother's floor with her secret map moving against my knees, I finally heard the sentence the way the rest of the worlds meant it.

Safe *from* us.

Because a child born of two courts—a child with one foot of soul in each of nine realms—couldn't exist anywhere the covenants governed. The magic of any given realm would tear such a child apart trying to decide what they were. So you make a child like that somewhere the arguments can't follow. Somewhere neutral. Somewhere hollow.

And you build wards around it. Not to keep the world out.

To keep the child in.

I thought of the mist at the valley's edge, patient as a creditor. I had spent my whole life drawing maps of places I'd never seen, filling them with coastlines and capitals, and I had always assumed it was ambition. It had never once occurred to me that it might be appetite—that the reason my invented countries felt realer than the road to market was that some part of me was reaching through the wards and *taking* geography, the way other children took apples.

Last night the stars went out. Tonight the mist leaned on the border. And the fragment said, plainly as a bill of sale: *until such time as the stone is asked to bear its weight.*

Someone had asked.

* * *

When I was seven, I tried to leave the valley.

This is not a story anyone tells. There were four adults who saw it happen and they agreed, with the unspoken solidarity of people who have witnessed something wrong, never to mention it. But I remember, because I remember everything, which I now understand may have been part of the problem.

It was midsummer. Old Tammer's ewes had gotten through the gap in the north hedge, and I chased them farther than I'd ever gone, up past the last boundary stone, to where the mist began. I remember being furious at the sheep, and then furious at the mist, and then simply done with both, a seven-year-old's whole sovereign rage: *I live here too, and I will see what's over the hill.*

I stepped into the mist.

I did not pass through it. That's the thing no one believes. I stood there, one boot on valley loam, one boot on nothing, and the mist didn't resist me—it *recognized* me, the way a lock recognizes a key turning the wrong way. Something vast leaned down through the fog and regarded me with an attention I felt in my teeth, and a voice that was not a voice said, in the tone of a clerk finding an error in a ledger:

*Not yet.*

Then Tammer hauled me back by the collar, and the ewes wandered home on their own recognizance, and at supper my mother watched me eat with an expression I'd filed, all these years, under *worried about sheep.*

She hadn't been worried about sheep. She'd been doing sums. She'd been lying awake for years, I now understood, listening to her half-and-half child breathe, waiting for the night the ledger came due, and building wards against it, and teaching me to draw, God help her—teaching the keystone cartography, which is like teaching a match about fire—but loving me so hard and so clumsily that even now, floorless as I felt, I couldn't manage to be angry at her for longer than a breath.

She'd known. She'd warded anyway. She'd named me *keystone* in secret script on a moving map and then kissed my scraped knees and called me her little surveyor.

The floor of my identity did not so much give way as reveal itself, upon inspection, to have been a painted cloth over a chasm the entire time. I sat with that. The lantern hissed. On the desk, Vessany waited, patient as its maker's ghost.

*Until such time as the stone is asked to bear its weight.*

Well. I thought, with a giddiness that was either courage or shock wearing courage's coat. They'd better ask politely. I had conditions.

* * *

They did not ask politely.

I heard the horses first—wrong horses, hooves landing in that muffled way that means the animal is shod with something other than iron—and then the dogs in the kennels went silent all at once, which is worse than barking. Then three knocks at my door, spaced like a judge's gavel.

Through the window I saw them: tall figures in grey livery the color of the extinguished sky, faces hidden behind visors etched with columns of tiny figures—numbers, I realized, ledger entries, marching down their cheeks like tears in a banker's arithmetic. Wardens. I'd seen them in my mother's oldest maps, drawn at the margins of every realm: *the keepers of accord*, annotated in her hand with something that looked, in retrospect, much less like respect than I'd assumed.

The one in front unhooded. A woman, grey at the temples, eyes like weighed-out grain. She did not knock again. She spoke to the door as if doors owed her answers.

"Beatrix Quintana." Not a question. "The Quintana child."

Every soul in the village was suddenly very busy with very interesting nothing. I watched old Tammer study his fence post with the devotion of a man reading scripture.

"The covenant is void," the Warden said, in the voice of someone reciting terms they had not personally written and did not personally mourn. "The stone must be presented for audit."

*Audit.* My whole existence, my mother's whole long vigil, fourteen years of wards and lies and scraped-knee kisses, summarized as an irregularity pending review.

I looked at the open chest on the floor. At the moving map, borders still quietly renegotiating, the valley's cupped-hand outline glowing like a coal that refused to die. At my mother's words: *Do not wake the keystone.*

Then I looked at the back door, which my mother had installed, I now understood, with its hinges oiled and its sightline covered, because she had always, always been planning for this night.

"Beatrix Quintana," the Warden said again, and this time there was something almost human under it, something tired. "Come out, child. The sky is dark, and it does not come back on its own."

That, I confess, is the line that got me. Because I wanted the sky back too. I had watched the stars go out one by one and felt each one go like a tooth pulled from the roof of the world, and if I was the reason—if I was also, somehow, the remedy—then some vain, terrified, cartographer's part of me wanted to stride out there and say *show me the damage, I'll redraw it.*

But my mother wrote *do not wake the keystone* for a reason, and Wardens who shod their horses in silence and made villages forget how to bark were not, I judged, going to let me set my own terms for the bearing of weight.

I took the fragment. I took the map. I took my mother's good pen, because I am who I am, and I went out the back door into the mist-dark garden while the knocking became breaking.

The ward-line was behind the house, past the well. All my life it had been scenery. Now I could see it—or nearly see it, a shimmer in the air like heat off summer stone, and threaded through the shimmer, cracks. Hairline fractures glowing sullen orange, like the seams of a kiln.

Like glass about to give.

Behind me, my front door came off its frame. Ahead of me, the mist parted—actually parted, drew aside like a curtain pulled by a considerate stagehand, revealing a path through country I had mapped a hundred times without ever once seeing.

I have decided to take that as an invitation rather than a trap. A person on the run needs at least one working delusion.

I ran for the crack in the world, with my mother's map against my heart and her handwriting drying on my desk behind me, and the last thing I heard from the valley that made me was the Warden's flat, carrying voice, not angry, not cruel, just inevitable as arithmetic:

"The Quintana child. Present for audit."

The wards shattered at my back like a window breaking inward, and I stepped through.
