## 3. Three Exiles and a Fox

I had never run from anything in my life, and it turned out I was terrible at it.

The Hollow Valley at night is a place of soft edges—lantern-glow on wet stone, the smell of bread and river-mud, the particular hush of a village that believes itself safe because it has always been safe. But the wards were broken glass in my chest now, and every step past the boundary stones felt like walking out through a wound that hadn't decided whether to close.

Behind me: torchlight, shouting, old Marta Vell's voice cracking across the square—*the Quintana child, bring me the Quintana child*—as if I were a lost goat and not a person who had eaten dinner with her not six hours ago.

Ahead of me: the ward-line.

The wards had been invisible my whole life. Now they hung over the valley rim like the skeleton of something enormous, shattered ribs of pale light still flickering their death-spasm into the dark. Where they'd been whole, the world beyond was a rumor. Where they were broken—

Where they were broken, the night sky went on forever, and it was wrong. The stars were going out. Not dimming. Going *out*, one by one, like windows shuttered by some patient hand, and each extinguishing made a sound I felt rather than heard, a small wet click behind my eyes.

"Right," I told myself, hugging my mother's cartography chest to my chest like a shield, which is exactly what it was. "Beatrix. You've mapped realms you've never seen off secondhand accounts and guesswork. This is just another map. One you're standing inside of. With your entire body."

Self-pep-talks are underrated. That one got me three steps before I walked directly into a woman.

She came out of the dark the way weather comes out of mountains—all at once and already everywhere—and we collided hard enough that the chest flew from my arms, burst its latch, and scattered forty years of maps across the frost like enormous, expensive confetti.

"Oh, *brilliant*," she said, which was not an apology.

She was tall, silver-haired despite being perhaps fifty, wearing robes that had once been magnificent and were now magnificent in the way ruins are—a star-priest's vestments, if I remembered my mother's stories right, with the constellation-embroidery burned away at the collar. Defrocked. Struck from the rolls. The empty place where her holy sigil should have sat was sunburned into her skin, a pale circle like a scar from a ring someone tore off her finger.

"You're running from the Wardens," she said. Not a question.

"I'm running from Marta Vell, actually. She's seventy-three and armed with a bread paddle."

"The Wardens will be along shortly." She crouched, snatched up one of my maps—the one of the Ember Reaches, inked entirely from a peddler's drunken description—and went very still. "Who drew this?"

"I did."

"Liar."

"It's true! I've never seen the Ember Reaches. I worked from a salt-trader who'd had four cups and a grudge against the place."

Her head came up slowly. In the light of the dying wards, her eyes did something unsettling—they caught the glow and held it, the way stars do. "You drew this from testimony alone, and the river bend here is correct to within a league."

"Is it? I guessed."

"You *guessed*—" She stood, map clutched in both hands like scripture or evidence. "Child, what is your name?"

And that was when the man stepped out of the trees with a drawn sword and said, "Sera. Step away from the keystone."

* * *

Here is what I learned about Dain in the first ninety seconds of knowing him:

One: he wore Warden grey with the badges ripped off, which meant deserter, which meant either the most dangerous kind of honest or the most dangerous kind of liar.

Two: he held his sword like a man who wished he didn't have to, point angled down, weight back.

Three: he flinched when Sera Voss said *keystone*, and that flinch told me more than anything he said afterward.

"She's not a keystone," Sera snapped, planting herself between us. "She's a cartographer. Look at the work."

"I know what the covenant says. I've read the ledger." His voice was flat, exhausted, the voice of a man reading his own execution order for the tenth time. "The Quintana child. Born of both realms. The valley wasn't hiding them, priestess. It was *containing* them."

"Containing," I repeated. My mouth had gone dry. "That's a strong word for people who fed me plum cake every solstice."

"Plum cake," Dain said, "is a classic containment strategy. Ask any prison cook."

I want it noted that I laughed. It was a horrible, high, slightly unhinged laugh, but I laughed, because the alternative was understanding him, and I wasn't ready to understand him. Somewhere behind us, torches bobbed along the valley road. Somewhere above us, another star clicked out.

"We can stand here debating theology," Sera said, sweeping my maps into a rough pile, "or we can move. Wardens don't walk. They arrive."

Dain looked at me. Really looked, the way you look at a document you've been ordered to destroy and haven't yet decided to obey. "Can you fight?" he asked.

"No."

"Run?"

"Evidently badly."

"Hide?"

"Extremely well. Twenty-six years of practice."

Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, buried under sediment. "Fine," he said, and sheathed his sword. "You come with us until we figure out what you are. Then we reassess."

"And if your reassessment goes badly for me?"

"Then you'll have had a few extra days of living," he said, "which is more than the ledger offers anyone."

Cheerful fellow. I liked him immediately, in the way you like a storm shelter—grateful, resentful, and fully aware it might collapse on you.

* * *

We made perhaps two miles before I was robbed.

Understand: I was robbed by something the size of a loaf of bread.

It came out of the bracken like a thrown scarf—orange-white fur, too many tails, eyes like coins left in a well—and hit my pack at a dead sprint. By the time I shouted, it was ten feet away, sitting in the frost, holding my mother's covenant fragment delicately between its teeth.

"Give that *back*," I said, with all the authority of a person negotiating with a raccoon.

The fox tilted its head. And then it spoke, and its voice was a child's voice and an old woman's voice layered over each other, warm as banked coals. "*Oh.* Oh, this is a good one. This is thick. Who does this belong to?"

"That's mine."

"Mm. No. Memories don't belong to the people carrying them. They belong to whoever lived them." It turned the fragment over—my mother's handwriting, the words about the keystone, about blood and accord—and I watched its whiskers tremble. "This one has grief baked into it. Grief and law. Two of my favorites."

"What are you," Sera breathed, and there was real fear in her, the first I'd heard.

"A fox," it said modestly.

"You're a *wick*," Dain said grimly. "A memory-eater. They're supposed to be extinct."

"Extinct things get so much peace and quiet. It's a shame more of us don't try it." The fox—Wick—hopped closer, tails swaying, and looked up at me with an expression I can only describe as apologetic appetite. "I'll tell you what, keystone-child. I'll trade you."

"I don't have anything to trade. You just stole my everything."

"Not everything." Its nose twitched toward my head. "You're *full*. Full to bursting. A life packed edge to edge—valley mornings, ink stains, a mother teaching your hands the shapes of coastlines. I could take a little. Just a taste. And in exchange, you get your paper back."

"No," Dain said immediately.

"Yes," Sera said at the same moment.

They looked at each other.

"You want to let it eat part of her *mind*?" Dain demanded.

"I want to know what's in the fragment before the Wardens catch us holding it," Sera shot back. "Wick eats memories, Dain, it doesn't destroy them—it *keeps* them. It's the only archive in the world that can't be burned."

"Flatterer," said Wick, pleased.

"It could take something vital. Her name. Her mother's face."

"It takes what it takes," Wick said, and for a moment the playfulness dropped and something ancient looked out of those coin-well eyes. "That's the price. I don't choose the cut. I never choose the cut. Would you trust a knife that did?"

I thought about Marta Vell and her bread paddle. About the wards shattering in my chest. About a mother who had died leaving me a box full of maps and one line of terrible inheritance: *the keystone*.

Twenty minutes ago I had been nobody. A girl who drew places she'd never seen. Honestly? Part of me wanted to know what I was worth on the open market, even if the market was a fox.

"One bite," I said. "Small. And then the fragment comes back, and you answer our questions."

"*Our,*" Dain muttered.

"Company rules already," Sera murmured. "Look at that. Fastest founding in recorded history."

Wick set the fragment down in the frost, took three delicate steps forward, and pressed its cold nose to my temple.

What happened next was not pain. It was subtraction. A single memory lifted out of me like a splinter drawn clean—I felt the shape of the hole it left, smooth-edged, precise—but I couldn't tell you what it was, because that was rather the point. One moment I had a thing; the next moment I had a gap shaped like a thing, and the gap *ached*, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

Wick sat back, licked its chops, and its fur rippled with brief silver light. "Ohhh," it said, muffled and reverent. "Oh, that's a *vintage*. Little Beatrix. You have no idea what I just ate."

"What was it?" My voice shook. "What did you take?"

"Trade secret." It nudged the covenant fragment toward me with its nose. "Paper for supper. We're settled."

"We are *not* settled—"

"Company rules," said Wick, in a devastating impression of my own voice. "Fastest founding in recorded history."

Torchlight flickered on the ridge behind us. Dain swore. Sera stuffed the fragment into my hands and pointed downhill into country I had never seen and could not yet map, and we ran—four of us, a defrocked priestess, a deserter, a thief made of teeth and other people's yesterdays, and a keystone who had just learned the exact market value of her own head.

Somewhere in that first mile, without ceremony or agreement, we stopped being strangers. Nobody said it. There was no oath, no clasped hands. There was just Dain falling back to cover our tracks, Sera catching my elbow when I stumbled, Wick riding in my pack like a smug orange passenger, and the four sets of footsteps finding a single rhythm against the frozen ground.

Found family, I would learn later, rarely announces itself. Mostly it's just people whose footfalls match yours.

We ran until the valley lights drowned behind the hills, until the dying stars thinned overhead to a handful of stubborn coals. And then, on a rise overlooking a river that was—distinctly, unmistakably—flowing uphill, Sera Voss stopped dead and said, in a voice gone quiet and old:

"Geography shouldn't do that while the covenant lives."

Dain stared at the water. "The covenant doesn't live. It broke two nights ago."

"No." Sera turned to me, and the starlight she caught in her eyes had gone thin and frightened. "If it had simply broken, the world would be tearing itself apart in chaos. This—" she gestured at the impossible river, at the hills that seemed, when I looked too long, to have rearranged themselves since sundown— "this is *negotiation*. Something is rewriting the terms. And whatever holds the pen..."

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Every one of us, even the fox, looked at the same place at the same moment: north, toward the horizon, where a shape rose against the last living stars—tall, slender, impossibly far, and glowing faintly, like a candle lit in an empty house.

The Spire of Accord.

And beneath its light, moving toward it across the plain, an army of grey.
