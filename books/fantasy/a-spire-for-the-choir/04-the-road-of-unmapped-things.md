## 4. The Road of Unmapped Things

I have spent forty years reading stars, and I will tell you what no one tells you about the sky: it is honest. A star cannot lie about where it is. It burns, therefore it is, therefore north is north and east is east and a priest can find her way home by faith and arithmetic.

The road out of the Hollow Valley had neither.

We walked for three days through country that could not decide what it was. On the first morning we crossed a river that ran uphill — not dramatically, not all at once, but with the sullen persistence of water that had been told, overnight, that downhill was now a matter of opinion. Dain stood at its bank for a long time, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man auditing the universe and finding the books in order but the philosophy unsound.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Neither am I," said Beatrix Quintana, and stepped into the current.

* * *

Let me tell you how the company stood on that first morning, because companies are made of small arrangements before they are made of loyalty. Beatrix walked in front because they could not help it — they kept stopping to sketch, quick lines in a battered notebook, the way other people breathe. Dain walked behind them because he did not trust anyone else to guard the rear of his own conscience. I walked in the middle, which is where priests walk when they are no longer sure there is anything above them worth addressing. And Wick —

Wick went where Wick pleased, which was everywhere, preferably near anything shiny or edible, and since Wick considered memories edible, that meant everywhere.

"You're staring at me again, star-priest," Wick said, materializing at my elbow without the courtesy of footsteps. "It's flattering, but I'm spoken for. Spoken for by me."

"I'm wondering what you are," I said.

"A fox." It bared its teeth, which were too many and too white. "A very well-traveled fox."

"You ate my memory of my ordination," I said. "Three days ago. I remember being a girl in a temple and then I remember being defrocked, and the middle is a hole shaped like you."

"I improved it," said Wick. "That memory was mostly kneeling. You're welcome."

I did not laugh. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because I wanted to, and refraining from laughter felt like the last professional duty I had left.

* * *

By the second day, the towns began to lie.

The first one was called Marrow's Ford, or possibly Ashen Cross, depending on which signpost you believed, and both signposts were correct, which was the problem. The innkeeper's grandmother had founded the town as a fishing village; the miller's grandfather had founded it as a garrison; the same woman appeared in both histories under different names, married to different men, buried under two separate headstones in the same churchyard, each lovingly maintained.

"They're remembering different dawns," Beatrix said quietly. We stood in the square while the town argued with itself around us. "The covenant held the pasts together. Like stitching. Now every thread remembers its own pattern."

"And the people?"

"The people don't notice. That's the worst part." Beatrix's pencil hovered over the page. "To them, this has always been true. Both of it."

An old woman approached us then, smiling, and asked if we'd come for the founding festival, and her daughter, passing, asked if we'd come for the funeral, and both invitations were sincere, and both events were tomorrow, and I watched Beatrix understand — really understand, in the slow way understanding arrives, like cold water finding the seams of a boot — that the world was coming apart along lines only they could see.

They drew. That was the terrible thing, the thing I keep returning to: they drew. A line here, a name there, the river pinned to its bed like a moth to a board, the town given one history written in Beatrix's careful hand on the back of an old map. And as the ink dried, the second headstone faded. The arguing signs agreed. The river, downstream, remembered which way was down.

"What did you just do?" Dain asked.

"Mapped it," Beatrix said, too lightly.

"No. People can't do that."

"Well." Beatrix closed the notebook. "Apparently this one can."

* * *

I should explain what I lost when I lost the stars, so you understand why the next part undid me.

I was Sera Voss, Reader of the Third Hour, and my whole life was the certainty that the heavens were a text and I was literate in it. When the stars went out — all of them, in one night, like candles pinched by a passing hand — I did not merely lose my vocation. I lost the alphabet. Every night since has been a page of black. I have caught myself, more than once, looking up and mouthing the names of constellations at nothing, a woman reciting poetry to a wall.

So on the third night, when Beatrix unrolled their maps by firelight and I saw what their ink was doing, I confess I forgot myself.

The maps were holding. Not recording — holding. Wherever Beatrix had drawn, the land stayed itself. A bridge they'd sketched on the first morning still stood over water that had elsewhere become fog pretending to be water. A village they'd named still answered to its name. The world, unwritten by its broken covenant, was rewriting itself hourly — and Beatrix Quintana's pen was the only proof against it, the last working stitch in a seam coming undone across three realms.

"You knew," I said. "You've known since the wards broke. Since before."

Beatrix didn't look up. "I knew I drew things and things stayed drawn. I thought everyone's maps worked like that."

"No one's maps work like that."

"Then I suppose I'm no one," they said, and there it was — that flinch, that half-second where the joke was armor and underneath the armor was a person realizing the world had been asking something of them since the night the sky went dark, and they had answered without knowing it was a question.

Dain crouched by the fire, sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen. "If the Wardens learn what those pages do—"

"They already know," Beatrix said. "That patrol in the valley wasn't asking after 'the Quintana child' out of curiosity."

"They'll want the maps."

"They'll want the hand that makes them," I said, and regretted saying it the moment I saw Beatrix hear it.

Silence, then. The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, the impossible river ran uphill toward its source, faithful to a law that no longer existed, and I thought: that is what we are doing. All four of us. Walking uphill toward a thing that has been abolished, because our feet remember the shape of the old law even though the law does not.

* * *

On the fourth day we came to a crossroads that had, according to five separate milestones, always led to six different places.

Beatrix sat down in the dust in the middle of it and did not get up for a long while.

"Choose," I said gently.

"It doesn't matter what I choose. Whatever I draw becomes true. Don't you understand what that means?" Their voice cracked down the middle. "If I map a road to safety, there's a road to safety. If I map a town out of existence—" They stopped. "Yesterday there was a hamlet called Ferrow. Small. Smoke from the chimneys, dogs, a bell. It was in the wrong place — sitting where the old covenant said a border shrine should be. So I moved it. I drew it two miles east, and it *went*, Sera. Chimneys, dogs, bell. Two miles east, like it had always been there. Everyone remembers it always being there." They looked up at me with eyes far too old for the face. "Did I save it? Did I steal it? Is there a version of those people back where they were, still standing in the wrong place, waiting for me to notice them? I don't know. I can't know. And I'm going to have to make a hundred decisions like that before we reach the Spire, and every single one of them will be *true*."

I knelt in the dust beside them. I am not a priest anymore; I have no blessing to give. But I put my hand on their shoulder the way my own teacher once put hers on mine, and I said the only true thing I had left.

"Then be careful," I said. "And be kind. And write everything down, so that someday, when the world is steady enough to judge you, there's a record of what you chose and why. That's all any of us ever had. Even the stars just write themselves down every night, and let the astronomers argue."

Beatrix laughed — wetly, but they laughed — and picked up the pencil.

They drew the road east. It became true. We walked it.

* * *

That evening, Dain found the marker.

He came back to camp at a pace I did not like, holding a strip of pale cloth nailed to a post at the edge of our firelight. Wardens' work — I knew the knot, everyone who has ever been watched by the Wardens knows the knot. The cloth bore a single line in ledger-hand:

*THE QUINTANA IRREGULARITY PROCEEDS NORTH-NORTH-EAST. CORRECTION AUTHORIZED.*

"Correction," Beatrix repeated. "Like I'm an error in an account book."

"To them, you are," Dain said. His voice was flat in the way I was learning meant shame. "The Wardens don't hate you, Quintana. That would require them to see you. They see a discrepancy. A sum that doesn't balance. And they've spent nine hundred years balancing sums." He fed the cloth to the fire and watched it curl. "We should move. Tonight. Correction parties travel light and fast, and they don't stop for weather or mercy."

Beatrix looked at the fire a long time. Then at their notebook — that growing atlas of a world held together by one exhausted hand. Then at the three of us: a deserter, a defrocked priest, a fox.

"If I burn the maps," they said slowly, "I stop being a target. But everything I've pinned down comes loose again. Every town. Every river. Every person I've steadied."

"Yes," said Dain.

"So I can't burn them."

"No," said Dain, and something in his voice, for the first time since I'd met him, sounded less like duty than like hope. "No, you can't."

We packed in the dark and walked north-north-east, deliberately, absurdly, along the exact route our hunters had predicted, because it was also the route to the Spire, and Beatrix had drawn it so, and what Beatrix drew was true.

Wick fell into step beside me near midnight, when the others slept and I kept my useless vigil at a sky full of nothing.

"You're worried," the fox observed.

"I'm a defrocked priest escorting a living keystone through a dissolving world, pursued by accountants with swords. Worried seems proportionate."

"Mm." Wick trotted a few paces in silence. Then, in a voice stripped of all its usual silk: "There's something you should know, star-priest. Something I ate, a long time ago. Something that belonged to Beatrix's mother." It stopped. Its many teeth showed in the firelight, but its eyes were not laughing. "And I think — I think it might be the reason the child is the keystone at all. I think it might kill them to know it."

Ahead, unseen in the dark, Beatrix walked on, drawing the road true beneath our feet, one careful line at a time.

"And if I don't give it back," Wick said quietly, "then I'm the only one carrying the truth, and foxes are such *careless* creatures."
