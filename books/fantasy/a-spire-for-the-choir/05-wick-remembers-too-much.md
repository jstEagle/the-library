## 5. Wick Remembers Too Much

Let me tell you a secret about foxes: we do not hoard. Dragons hoard. Squirrels hoard. Priests hoard guilt, which is the heaviest currency of all. A fox only ever carries what it can run with, and I have always been very good at running.

Which is why it was so inconvenient that I was carrying something too heavy to hold and too precious to drop.

I had told Sera Voss in the dark, because telling her in daylight would have required me to look at her face while I did it, and I am a coward about exactly one thing, and that thing is other people's grief. She had said nothing for a long time — an impressive feat for a woman whose entire vocation had once been talking at the sky — and then she'd said, "Tomorrow. You'll tell them tomorrow."

And now it was tomorrow, and the fire was lit, and Beatrix Quintana sat across from me mending a bootlace with the fierce concentration of someone who maps worlds but cannot defeat a knot, and Dain was sharpening a knife he never used on anything but whetstones, and I thought: *well. There's no elegant way to hand someone their own death.*

I know. You're wondering why I didn't just keep my mouth shut. Foxes are famous for it.

Here is the truth of foxes that no one puts in the stories: we eat memories because we cannot bear to be the only ones who remember things. Every memory I've swallowed sits in me like a stone in a river, and the water of the world goes on over it, and I feel every current. I have eaten the last words of dying kings. I have eaten a wedding vow from a bride who changed her mind at the altar and wanted it gone before her feet could carry her back up the aisle. I have eaten small things — a child's fear of cellars, an old man's shame over a stolen pear — and those are the ones that ache most, because they were never anyone's burden but theirs, and now they are mine.

But the Quintana memory. Ah. The Quintana memory was not a stone. It was a keystone, and I had pulled it out of a wall, and I had watched the wall stand anyway, out of sheer stubbornness, for twenty years, and tonight the wall was walking around drawing maps by firelight and asking me to pass the salt.

"Beatrix," I said.

"Mm." The knot defeated them. They looked up, and their eyes did the thing they always did — that terrible open thing, like a door left unlocked in a country where everyone locks their doors.

"I lied to you," I said. "Not recently. Recently I've been remarkably honest; ask anyone. But twenty-two years ago, in a village you've never mapped because it no longer exists, I ate something that belonged to your mother."

The bootlace went still in their hands. Dain's knife stopped mid-stroke. Sera fed the fire a stick, which was her way of saying *go on* without spending words she might need later.

"They paid me," I went on, because if I stopped I might never start again, and a fox must keep moving or die. "Your mother. She came to the edge of the Hollow Valley with a scroll case strapped to her back and a baby wrapped against her chest, and she found me — I was smaller then, and hungrier, and considerably more charming, if you can believe it — and she said, 'I have something that must not survive me.' And I said, madam, you have come to the right fox."

"What was it?" Beatrix asked. Their voice was very level. Level voices are the loudest ones, if you have ears like mine.

"The terms," I said. "The true terms of the old covenant. Not what the Wardens' ledgers say, not what the star-priests sing. What was actually written, and actually signed, and actually broken." I turned my tail around my feet, because tails are for holding onto when hands won't do. "And the reason your mother wanted it eaten rather than burned is that it could not be destroyed while any of the signatories lived. Only forgotten. Memory is the one vault that can't be forced."

"And the signatories?" Sera asked softly. She already knew. Priests always know; it's why I avoid churches.

"One of them was Mirabel Quintana," I said. "Who died three winters later. Which means—" I looked at Beatrix, and I made myself look, because they deserved to be looked at "—the terms live on in exactly one place in all the realms. Me. And the keystone they describe—" I flicked my ears toward the fire, the road, the whole fragile geography Beatrix held together line by line "—is sitting right there, re-inking the world."

* * *

Silence does interesting things around a fire. It stretches. It snaps. That night it did both.

Beatrix set down the bootlace with exaggerated care, as though sudden movements might shake loose whatever was balancing inside them. "You remember it," they said. "All of it. The actual terms. You've known this entire time."

"I have known it since before you could walk," I said. "I have carried it through four famines, two purges, and one extremely regrettable decade spent in the court of the Marsh King. Yes. All of it."

"Why didn't you say something sooner?"

"Because saying it sooner would have killed you in a village instead of killing you at the Spire, and I judged the Spire had better views." I meant it as a joke. It came out as what it was, which was the truth wearing a joke's coat, badly.

Dain stood up. Of course Dain stood up. Dain stands up the way other men draw swords. "No," he said.

"No?" Sera raised one eyebrow, a weapon she kept polished for exactly these occasions.

"No. Whatever's in that fox's head stays there." He pointed the whetstone at me, which stung, professionally speaking. "You want to hand a person their own execution warrant and call it honesty? I was a Warden. I've read enough ledgers to know what 'the terms' look like when they're enforced. If giving it back kills them, then we don't give it back. We find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't always another way," Sera said. "That's a soldier's superstition, Dain. You survived long enough to believe it because someone else paid the cost of the times there wasn't."

"Sera—"

"She has a right to know what she is." Sera's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse, the way a soft rain floods deeper than a storm. "She has a right to know what her mother chose for her. You don't get to protect Beatrix from their own life. None of us do. Not even the fox. *Especially* not the fox."

I raised a paw. "For the record, I'm standing right here, and I object to being discussed like a larder."

"You're worse than a larder," Dain snapped. "A larder doesn't offer to feed people poison and call it a meal."

"It's not poison." And here my voice did the thing I hate, the thing where it goes quiet and old. "It's hers. It's Mirabel's. Do you understand what I'm offering? Not information. *Her*. Her handwriting. Her voice. The way she said the words when she signed them away. Twenty-two years I have been the only grave that Mirabel Quintana is buried in, and I am a *fox*, deserter, I am not built to be a monument. Foxes forget how to sit still. One bad winter, one hungry century, and I could lose it — spill it into some ditch, trade it for a warm lie when the cold gets into my bones. And then the true terms of the covenant die forever, and the child walks into the Spire with half a truth and seals a half-covenant, and everything her mother broke herself to protect comes undone anyway."

I looked at Beatrix. "So. Here is the shape of it. I give the memory back, and you know exactly what you're writing when you reach the Spire — and the knowing may burn you out like a wick, pardon the phrase, because mortal minds were not built to hold covenant-law whole. Or I keep it, and you go in blind and trusting, and I go on being the loneliest library in creation, running as fast as I can, forever, hoping I don't trip."

Beatrix was quiet for a long time.

Then: "Show me a piece of it."

"Beatrix—" Dain started.

"A piece." Their eyes hadn't moved from mine. "If knowing the whole of it might kill me, fine. But I want to know what her voice sounds like. I was two years old when she died. I don't even have that. You're telling me you've been carrying my mother around in your head for twenty-two years and the argument is whether I should get *any* of her back?"

Oh, that wasn't fair. That was profoundly unfair. I looked at Sera for rescue and found her looking at me with an expression that said, quite clearly, *you opened this door, fox; walk through it.*

* * *

So I did what I have only done twice in nine hundred years, both times by accident and both times immediately regretted: I opened the vault on purpose.

I didn't give the memory. Giving is a whole-body act; it empties the shelf entirely. But a fox can let a thing *show*, the way light through a curtain shows the shape of what's behind it. I sat down across the fire from Beatrix, closed my eyes, and uncurled the edges of it — just the edges — and let the firelight of my mind fall on the first few lines.

Mirabel Quintana, at the valley's edge, in the blue hour before dawn. Snow in her hair. The baby asleep against her heart. And her voice — oh, her voice, dry and steady and cracked straight down the middle where no one could see —

*"Fox. They'll tell my child they were hidden. Write this somewhere I can trust: the valley was never a hiding place. It was a leash. And the day it breaks, the keystone must choose what holds the realms together — not the law. Never again the law. Something kinder, or nothing at all."*

Beatrix made a sound I will hear until I die.

It was not crying, exactly. It was the sound a map makes when a fold is pressed flat after years — the crease still there, but finally, finally smoothed enough to read what's written along it. They put both hands flat on the ground as though checking that the world was still where they'd drawn it.

"That's her," they whispered. "That's — she sounds like someone who's already said goodbye to everything except the work."

"She was," I said gently. "That was rather her whole character. You have her chin, incidentally, and her habit of glaring at terrain."

Sera had her eyes closed. Dain had turned his back to us, shoulders rigid, staring into the dark like it owed him money — but his knife-hand had gone slack, and slack, for Dain, is a kind of weeping.

"Can you give me the rest?" Beatrix asked. "Now. Tonight."

"No," I said, and held up a paw before they could argue. "Not because I'm cruel, and not because the deserter is right — don't look smug, Dain, I felt it from here. Because your mother didn't hide the terms in a fox out of caution. She hid them out of *timing*. The memory wants to come home at the Spire. It was written to be returned there — I've felt it pull northward for two decades like a compass needle with opinions. Give it to you here, on a hillside, with Wardens at our backs, and it lands in you like a dropped star. Take it there, at the forge where the covenant was first cut, and maybe — *maybe* — your mind has somewhere to put it besides the pyre."

"That's a guess," Dain said over his shoulder.

"It's an educated guess," I said. "I'm nine hundred years old. All my guesses have education."

Beatrix laughed — a wet, cracked, unmistakably Mirabel laugh — and wiped their face with their sleeve, and looked at each of us in turn. "Then we go north faster," they said. "We stop wasting days. And Wick—" they pointed at me, and their finger only shook a little "—you don't get to lose it. Whatever it costs you to carry her, you carry her to the door. Promise me."

And there it was. The thing I had run from for twenty-two years, catching me at last, the way all debts catch foxes eventually: not a request for the memory, but a request for *me* — my promise, my word, my worthless, priceless fox honor.

I put my paw over my heart, which is located, in foxes, somewhat to the left of where yours is and twice as heavy.

"On my nine hundred years," I said. "On every stolen pear and every eaten vow. I will carry Mirabel Quintana to the Spire if it kills me, and foxes, dear keystone, are *very* hard to kill."

We struck camp early the next morning, before the frost had finished its arguments with the grass. And it was Dain — Dain, of all people — who fell into step beside me on the road and said, quietly, without looking at me:

"If it starts taking her apart — the memory, when you carry it close to home — you tell us. You don't play the brave fool. You *tell us*."

I trotted on, tail high, silk restored.

"Deserter," I said, "you're beginning to sound like family. It's deeply unsettling. Do it again sometime."

Ahead, Beatrix drew the road north, one careful line at a time, toward the Spire, toward their mother, toward the truth that waited in my skull like a lit fuse — and far behind us, at the edge of hearing, came the horns of the Wardens, following our tracks through a world that was forgetting itself faster than even I could remember it.
