## 6. The Warden's Ledger

I want it on the record that I saw them coming.

That's not a boast. It's an apology. I saw the Wardens' lantern-light stitching through the pines at dusk—six points of white, moving in the old patrol diamond I'd drilled into a hundred recruits—and I said nothing, because saying something meant admitting what the light was, and admitting what the light was meant admitting I still knew.

So when they took us at the ford, I had exactly enough time to think, *Well, this is your own handwriting coming back to sign your name.*

They were efficient about it. That's the thing people never understand about Wardens. We weren't soldiers; we were clerks with swords. Nobody shouted. Nobody gloated. A sergeant with a face like unmarked paper said "Irregulars, detained pending audit," and put iron rings on Beatrix's wrists that hummed like struck glass, and that was that. Sera went quietly with her chin up, the way priests do even defrocked ones, as if arrest were a sacrament she'd simply stopped receiving. Wick vanished—which is fox for *I'll be back when it's funny*—and Beatrix looked at me over the manacles and mouthed one word.

*Ledger.*

They knew. Of course they knew. They'd read my mother's covenant fragment before I had.

* * *

The Warden waystation had been a tollhouse once, back when roads belonged to travelers instead of jurisdictions. They sat us on benches in the counting room, which still smelled of ink and old cedar, and left us under guard while the sergeant opened a book.

Not a warrant. Not a charge sheet. A ledger.

"Name," he said to Beatrix.

"You know my name."

"The ledger requires it."

"Beatrix Quintana."

He wrote it. He wrote it the way you'd write down a shipment of grain: date, origin, classification. Then he turned the book so we could see the entry, and I felt Sera go very still beside me, because neither of us had ever seen a Warden show his work.

**Subject: Q., B. Classification: Irregularity, Class One (Keystone). Disposition: standard.**

"What does 'standard' mean?" Beatrix asked. Their voice was steady. Their hands were not.

"It means," the sergeant said, without cruelty, without anything at all, "that the procedure has been performed four hundred and eleven times since the Covenant's drafting. The method is settled. There will be no surprises."

Four hundred and eleven. I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. Four hundred and eleven children of two realms, born in the cracks of the law, erased by the law's bookkeepers because a dead pact required tidy columns.

"They're going to erase me," Beatrix said. Not a question. A reading. My cartographer, reading their own death off a page.

"The Covenant is in default," the sergeant agreed. "Defaulted instruments are resolved. You are an instrument."

Sera laughed. It was not a kind laugh. "You're executing a person because a contract lapsed."

"I am resolving an irregularity because the ledger says so." He said it the way a man says *the sun rose*. And that—that was the moment I understood the Wardens had never been villains, and that this was somehow worse. Villains can be argued with. Villains want things. This man wanted nothing except that the columns balance. He would have erased Beatrix with the same care he'd use to archive a receipt, and he would have slept well after, because the ledger slept well, and he was only its hand.

I knew that sleep. I'd had it for eleven years.

"Sergeant," I said, and stood up, and every lantern in the room turned toward me like a held breath. "Dain Meric Hale. Third Chair, Office of Irregularities. Service number K-1147."

The silence changed texture. The guards shifted. One of them—young, freckled, terrified—whispered, "That's the Hale who burned the Vellum Bridge."

"That's the Hale who deserted," corrected another.

"The Hale who *wrote the bridge protocol*," said the youngest, with the awful reverence soldiers reserve for competence.

The sergeant studied me. "Deserters forfeit rank."

"Rank, yes." I kept my voice bored, which is the hardest trick there is when your heart is trying to climb out through your teeth. "But records don't forfeit themselves. Someone has to close my file, Sergeant, and no one below Fourth Chair can sign off on a Third Chair's file. Which means either you send for a Fourth Chair—" I let my eyes drift to the window, to the sky where stars used to be, "—through country that is currently eating itself, or you process me locally using my own service ledger as authority. Standard practice. Article Nine. You know it better than I do; you've probably got it memorized."

He had it memorized. I watched him have it memorized—the tiny flicker behind his eyes, a clerk finding the shelf where the answer lives.

"And if I grant you audit authority," he said slowly, "what do you intend to audit?"

"My own capture," I said. "Chain of custody. If the paperwork on me is clean, you'll look magnificent in the quarterly review. If it isn't—" I shrugged. "Well. You've seen the sky lately. Reviews are being conducted by nobody, forever."

It was a bluff stacked on a bluff stacked on the truth, which is the sturdiest kind of structure. The truth underneath was simple: Wardens trusted paper more than they trusted gods, and I had spent eleven years learning exactly how much paper could be made to say.

He sent for my ledger.

* * *

They brought it up from the strongroom in a canvas wrap, and when the sergeant unwrapped it I heard Sera inhale.

My service ledger. Eleven years of entries in my own hand. Every irregularity located, classified, resolved. Four hundred and ten lines of names I hadn't let myself read since the night I walked out—and now here they were, laid open under lantern light while Beatrix Quintana, line four hundred and twelve, waited to become one.

"Read it," Beatrix said softly. Not to the sergeant. To me.

"No."

"Read it, Dain."

"I know what's in it."

"You don't." Their voice cracked down the middle, wonder and horror sharing the rent. "I've seen your face since the day we met. You carry it like it's yours alone. But these aren't your sins, Dain. Look at the dispositions. *Resolved per standing order. Resolved per standing order.* You didn't write those orders. You just—" They stopped. Swallowed. "You just balanced the columns."

And that was the rage, when it finally came. Not hot. Cold as the counting room floor. Because they were right, and I had spent eleven years believing the lie was mine, when all along the lie had been institutional, laminated, filed. I hadn't been a monster. I'd been a pen. And somewhere in a strongroom there were four hundred and ten pens who all slept fine.

"Article Nine grants me audit authority over my own record," I said, and my voice came out level, which surprised us both. "I'm exercising it. Every entry in this ledger is hereby flagged for review against source documentation." I looked up at the sergeant. "Fetch the source documents, Sergeant. The original Covenant terms. The standing orders. I'll wait."

"We don't hold the originals."

"Then the entries can't be verified." I turned the ledger toward him, opened to the last blank page, and picked up the clerk's pen from his own desk. "Unverifiable entries are void. Article Nine, subsection three. You have it memorized."

His jaw worked. Around us, six guards discovered a sudden fascination with their boots, because every one of them had, at some point, executed an entry they couldn't verify, and every one of them knew it.

"You'd void the whole ledger," the sergeant said. "Eleven years of your own work."

"I'd void the whole *law*," I said, "if the law's too dead to show its face. That's not treason, Sergeant. That's accounting."

And then Beatrix did the thing I will love them for until whatever ends me. They reached across the table, took the ledger from my hands, and held it over the lantern.

Nobody moved. The flame licked up the canvas smell of old vellum, and Beatrix looked at me—not asking permission. Asking witness.

"Four hundred and ten names," they said. "They deserve a fire that remembers them. Not a column that closes them."

"Burning evidence is—" the sergeant began.

"Flagged for review," Beatrix said, and let go.

The pages caught the way old paper does—all at once, greedily, light climbing the years. I watched my own handwriting curl and blacken, every *resolved per standing order* becoming smoke, and I swear on whatever's left of the sky that the room got warmer. The young guard made a small sound, half grief, half relief. The sergeant didn't reach for it. He stood with his hands at his sides and watched eleven years of rote duty burn, and his face did something I'd never seen a Warden's face do in all my service.

It doubted.

In the confusion of smoke and shouting, two things happened nearly together. The iron rings on Beatrix's wrists, tuned to the Covenant's authority, flickered—and died, humming out like the stars had. And a fox trotted out from under the counting table with the strongroom keys in its mouth, looking insufferably pleased.

"Wick," Sera said, "how long have you been under there?"

"Since the ford," said the fox. "You people talk so much. It's very convenient."

We went out the back while the front filled with men coughing on the ashes of their own certainty. Behind us the sergeant stood in the doorway, not pursuing, holding his empty hands like a man whose book had finally refused to balance—and I heard him say, to no one, to the smoke:

"Flagged for review."

* * *

We ran the river road until the waystation lights were gone, and then Beatrix stopped, doubled over, laughing or crying or both, manacle-marks still red on their wrists.

"You burned it," they gasped at me. "Your whole record. Your whole—"

"It wasn't my record." I was surprised at how easily it came out now, ash still in my hair. "It was theirs. I was just the hand that held the pen." I looked at the three of them—the priest, the fox, the keystone—and felt the strange new weight settle in, the one that isn't guilt. "From here on, if I write anything, it gets written the way you write maps. In ink I can defend."

Wick dropped the strongroom keys at my feet like a tip. "For the record," the fox said, "I ate the memory of your mother's funeral from a mourner outside Thornhollow, years ago. Lovely service, apparently. You'll want that back eventually."

Beatrix stared. "Why are you telling us *now*?"

"Because," Wick said, tail curling, ears tilting east toward mountains none of our maps agreed on, "we just crossed the last line the old law was watching. Everything past here is unmapped, unledgered, and unrecorded—which means everything past here, someone has to remember on purpose." The fox's amber eyes found Beatrix's. "And I think, little keystone, that what your mother left behind is waiting in the high passes. Along with the thing that killed her."

The wind came down off the peaks carrying snow that fell upward, and none of us said anything at all, because some invitations you answer with your feet.

We started walking.
