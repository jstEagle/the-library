## 7. Winter Finishes Falling

I have prayed to stars for most of my life, so I know how to read an omen. The omen that morning was a river standing still.

Not frozen—*stopped*. A ribbon of water hanging in the air like a dropped scarf someone had forgotten to catch, mid-curve, glittering and utterly motionless. Dain poked it with his sword and the steel came away frosted. Wick licked it, which is what Wick does to everything, and reported that it tasted "like a held breath."

"Magic's freezing," Beatrix said. They stood at the riverbank with their arms wrapped around themselves, breath pluming, watching the suspended water with the expression of a cartographer confronting a typo in reality. "The covenant is failing faster. Magic is part of the accord—all of it—and when the accord goes, everything bound by it goes rigid first. Like a body stiffening."

"Wonderful," I said. "So we're walking through a corpse the size of the world."

"We're walking through a world having second thoughts," Beatrix corrected, and there it was again—that stubborn, infuriating gentleness of theirs, the way they insisted on describing catastrophe as if it were merely unfinished business. I had been a star-priest for thirty years. I had delivered eulogies for constellations. And still this child of two realms could look at a dying sky and say, *it isn't over yet*, as though they had some authority on the matter.

Which, I suppose, they did. That was rather the whole problem.

* * *

The winter did not arrive. It *finished falling*, the way a verdict finishes falling, all at once and retroactively. We climbed out of the river valley into high country where the snow lay three feet deep and the cold had teeth in it—not natural cold but the frozen magic itself, hanging in the air in shapes: half-formed spells, interrupted blessings, a bird made of light that had been mid-flight when the freeze took it and now hung above the pass like a lantern no one would ever light.

We found the refugees on the third day.

They were wedged into the mouth of the Keldrin Pass—a gorge between two realms that had been, until recently, merely unfriendly and was now actively at war over whose fault the dark sky was. Hundreds of them. Families from the ember-lands to the south, frost-farmers from the north, a caravan of glass-singers whose wagons had frozen solid with their cargo still singing inside, faintly, like bells underwater. They had come to the pass because it was the only way through, and the pass had closed.

Not snowed closed. *Warded* closed. Both realms had thrown their border-magic across the gorge at the same moment, and both magics had frozen mid-shape where they met, interlocking like the jaws of a trap. A wall of crystallized enchantment, forty feet high, blue-white and humming, sealing the pass shut with several hundred people trapped against it.

"They say it'll thaw in spring," a woman told us. She had frostbitten fingertips and a baby strapped to her chest who would not stop crying, quietly, the way babies cry when they've learned crying costs too much. "If there is a spring. If anyone comes. If—"

"There'll be a spring," Dain said gruffly, in his I-was-a-Warden-for-twenty-years-and-I-know-how-bureaucracies-die voice. But I saw him looking at the ward-wall, and I saw him do the arithmetic every deserter does: *how many winters of fuel do these people have, and how honest am I willing to be?*

Beatrix didn't say anything. They just unslung their satchel, took out their maps, and began to draw.

* * *

That night, by firelight, I watched Beatrix work, and I understood—for maybe the hundredth time since the wards broke—why the Wardens wanted them dead and the realms wanted them caged.

Their pen moved, and the world listened.

It was small, what they could do. A line drawn along the pass and the snow eased back a handspan. Names inked beside the refugees' fires and the fires burned warmer. A sketch of the frozen river downstream and, miles away, water moved again for an hour. Their blood-keystone nature leaked out through graphite and ink like warmth through a coat seam. The old covenant had been written in blood; Beatrix carried the last of its living ink.

And every line they drew painted them brighter in the sight of anything hunting.

"You should sleep," I said.

"After this ridge." Their pen didn't pause. Under the firelight their face was hollowed, shadowed, older than twenty-some years had any right to make it. "Sera. If I map the pass as open—if I draw the road through—the wards can't hold. Not while my lines are stronger than theirs."

"That's not a small thing you're proposing."

"No," they said. "It isn't."

I sat down across the fire from them. Wick was curled around their shoulders like a living scarf, which it did only when it was worried; fox spirits don't offer warmth for free. "Tell me what it costs."

Beatrix's pen stopped. That frightened me more than anything else that day, because I had never once seen their pen stop.

"When I held the ford open at Marrowbridge," they said quietly, "for the ferry families—I bled from places I don't have words for. My nose. Under my nails. I tasted copper for two days. And that was holding one river for one hour." They looked up at me, and their eyes caught the firelight the way the frozen bird of light caught the dead sun. "The pass is a wound between two warring realms. To hold it open I'd have to stand in the gap and be the covenant. All of it. For as long as it takes everyone to get through."

"How long?"

"With four hundred people and those wagons?" A terrible little laugh. "Days."

"Then we find another way."

"There is no other way. There's never been another way, Sera. That's what being the keystone *means*." They said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Everyone keeps treating this like a tragedy that happened to me. It isn't. It's a job nobody else can do, and I'm the one holding the pen."

"And if it kills you?"

They were quiet for a long moment. Snow fell past the fire, slow and enormous, flakes the size of coins.

"Then it kills me," they said, "and I'll have died doing the one thing I was actually made for. Do you know what that's worth? I spent my whole life in that valley thinking I was nothing special. Just the strange child who drew maps. And now—" They spread their hands, ink-stained, cracked at the knuckles from cold. "Now the whole world needs exactly what I am. I'd rather break being useful than stay whole being spare."

I am an old woman and I have heard a thousand prayers, and I tell you: none of them has ever sounded so much like prayer as that. Spoken casually, over a dying fire, by someone who thought they were explaining logistics.

* * *

At dawn, Beatrix walked to the ward-wall and put their hands on it.

I will describe it as best I can, though description fails. They cut their palm—that was necessary, blood being the original ink—and pressed it to the frozen magic, and began to draw. Not on paper. On the wall itself, in their own blood, lines that glowed gold where the wards were blue-white. And the wall *listened*. The interlocked jaws of two realms' magic groaned apart like a gate remembering it was once a door.

"GO!" Beatrix shouted to the refugees. "Through the pass! Keep moving, keep together, don't touch the walls!"

And they went. Oh, how they went. Four hundred people poured through that gap like water finding the crack in a dam, and Beatrix stood in the middle of it, arms out, blood running down their wrists, holding two realms' worth of frozen hatred apart with nothing but the fact of themselves.

Dain organized the column—he was magnificent, bellowing order into panic, carrying children two at a time. Wick darted up and down the line eating panic out of the air, swallowing terror in little sips so people could think. I did what priests do: I walked at the rear and I lied to people about hope, telling them the stars would return, telling them it would be all right, and for once the lies were load-bearing.

By the second day Beatrix was bleeding from the nose steadily. By the third, from under the fingernails, and their golden lines had gone thin as wire. The gap they held narrowed. Frost crept up their legs like something climbing.

On the fourth day, the last family went through—the frostbite mother with her quiet baby, the glass-singers hauling their singing wagons—and Beatrix was on their knees in the snow, still holding the walls apart with hands that had stopped shaking, which is the worst sign, and they looked up at me and smiled.

"Sera," they said. "Tell them—tell the company—"

"Don't you dare dictate a last testament at me, Beatrix Quintana. Stand up."

"I can't."

"Then listen." I knelt in the snow and took their ruined face in my old hands. "You said you'd rather break than be spare. Well, hear an old priest's reading of the text: a keystone that shatters brings down the whole arch. You die here, the covenant dies with you, and every person you saved walks out of this pass into a world with no accord left to hold it. Your sacrifice isn't yours alone to spend. It never was. So *stay*, damn you. Stay because we need you. Stay because—" and my voice broke, thirty years of composure going like ice off a roof, "because I love you, child. You impossible, self-spending creature. Stay."

And Beatrix Quintana, the keystone of two realms, the last living ink of the old accord, looked at me with snow in their lashes and said the thing I will carry until they burn my body or bury it:

"I know. I know you all love me. That's why it's easy." They coughed, and it was pink. "I'd rather break than let people freeze for my sake—or for anyone's sake. But I'll try to stay. For you. For all of you. Just—don't let go of me yet."

I did not let go. Dain carried them out of the pass when the last of their strength went, and Wick curled around their chest the whole way, and I walked beside them reciting every blessing I remembered and inventing new ones, because the old ones had all been written for a sky that no longer answered.

* * *

They survived. Barely, ugly, fevered, three days insensible in a shepherd's hut while Wick did something unspeakable and merciful inside their dreams—we didn't ask—and Dain chopped wood like a man performing penance, and I sat by the bed and watched their breathing the way I used to watch for the first star of evening.

When Beatrix finally woke, they were weak as a kitten and twice as opinionated.

"The pass?" they croaked.

"Holding," Dain said. "Your lines are still in it. Gold ones. The Wardens sent a survey team and turned right around."

"The refugees?"

"Through. All of them. The glass-singers wrote a song about you. It's embarrassing."

"Good," Beatrix said, and closed their eyes, and smiled.

But that evening, when I brought them broth, they caught my wrist. Their fingers were still bandaged. Their eyes, in the lamplight, had gone somewhere far away and come back changed.

"Sera. When I was in the gap—holding it—I felt something. Through the wards. Someone on the other side, pressing back. Not pushing against me." Their brow furrowed. "*Matching* me. Stroke for stroke, line for line, like a mirror. Like someone drawing with my own hand."

I felt the cold walk up my spine ahead of any thought.

"Someone who draws like you," I said carefully.

"Exactly like me. And Sera—at the very end, when I was nearly gone—it spoke. Not in words. In *lines*." Beatrix stared at the fire. "It said: *the other keystone is coming. And she has our mother's eyes.*"

Outside, the snow kept falling, and falling, and somewhere south of us, something that shared our blood was already walking toward the Spire.
