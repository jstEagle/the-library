## 9. A Covenant Reforged

### Beatrix

The Spire of Accord did not look like a place where the world was kept together. It looked like a lighthouse that had given up on the sea and decided to become a needle instead, stitching cloud to cloud, its base swallowed in the frozen dark of the pass I had held open with my own blood two months ago. The scar of that night still ached in my palm when it rained. It was raining now, sideways, the kind of rain that had opinions.

Inside, the ghosts were waiting.

They stood in the Spire's hollow heart like a congregation that had outlived its church—covenant-makers, eleven of them, translucent and patient and terribly old. And at their center, the one I had crossed a continent and a winter to reach: my mother. Not a memory of her. Not a portrait. Her, or enough of her that when she looked at me, my knees filed a formal complaint.

"You came," she said.

"You left," I said, because I had rehearsed this conversation for a thousand miles and apparently my opening line was going to be *petty*.

"I left so you could exist." Her voice was the voice from the cartography chest, from the fragment of covenant that had named me *keystone* in ink that was still, impossibly, wet. "The old accord was written in our blood and sealed in our forgetting. It failed because we forgot too well. The new one must be written by someone who remembers—and then chooses to give it all away."

"I know." I unslung my satchel. My maps were inside, every one, from the valley I'd drawn a thousand times to the river that ran uphill, to the town that remembered being a fishing village and being a fortress and being, briefly and confusingly, a very ambitious orchard. "I've been writing my whole life. I just didn't know I was drafting."

The Spire shuddered. Dust fell from a ceiling that had no right to have dust, being mostly metaphor. Somewhere far below, horns. Warden horns, the flat grey sound of them, the sound of a law with no law left in it, only procedure.

"They're coming," Dain said from the stair. He had his back to the doorway the way he always did, like furniture that had decided to be a soldier. "All of them. The whole ledger."

"Then we'd best balance the books," Sera said, and went to the windows to begin her impossible arithmetic.

I knelt in the center of the ghosts, unrolled the blank covenant page my mother's fragment had been leading to all along, and cut my palm on the stylus's edge, because apparently in this family we sealed everything with blood and bad timing.

The first line I wrote was a valley.

### Sera

I have lit stars before. I was a priest of the Choir of the Unextinguished; I once relit the festival beacons of three realms in a single night and was praised in verse so bad I defrocked myself partly to escape it. But those were candles. These were stars—dead stars, the extinguished sky, the great black ledger the Wardens had been keeping in place of heaven.

The ritual was simple, which is how you know it will be terrible. You stand in the high window of the Spire, you open yourself to the first light that ever was, and you offer to carry it. That's all. Carry it. Like a cup of water across a burning field.

I had been carrying things across burning fields my whole life. I was, frankly, overqualified.

The first star came back like a struck match in a cathedral. I felt it in my teeth. The second and third followed, and then the sky above the pass remembered what it was for, and the Wardens below—hundreds of them, grey-robed, grey-faced, climbing the stair with the patient horror of bureaucracy given boots—looked up, and some of them *wept*. I want that recorded. Whatever else the Wardens were, bookkeepers of a dead law, executioners of irregularities, they were also people who had lived their whole lives under a snuffed sky and had forgotten, the way you forget anything, that they were grieving.

"Keep climbing," their captain said, voice like a filing cabinet. "The keystone is an irregularity. The sky is not our jurisdiction."

"The sky is *everyone's* jurisdiction," I said, and lit another star, and another, faster now, because each one I lit made the next one easier, the dark losing its argument one point of light at a time.

Below me, on the stair, Dain braced his shoulder against the door and said, in the tone of a man reading his own eulogy, "Sera. If I don't make it, tell Wick it can have the memory of my mother's cooking. It's the worst one I've got."

"Noted," I said, and meant to argue, and didn't have time.

### Dain

I held the stair.

That's the whole report. I held the stair. Twenty years of service, one act of desertion, one burned ledger, and it all came down to a doorway and my body and the simple arithmetic of how many Wardens I could keep from passing, which was: all of them, for as long as it took, and then not one more.

They came in ranks, because of course they did. I knew their drills. I had *written* two of their drills, back when I believed a rule was the same thing as a right. They came with staves that drank warmth and shields that drank light, and they came without hatred, which is the worst way to be attacked. A man who hates you hesitates. A man following procedure does not.

"Deserter," the captain said. "Step aside. Your irregularity will be processed."

"She's not an irregularity," I said. "She's the *correction*. You've been enforcing a dead law for thirty years. She's writing the living one. Your own ledger says the covenant requires a keystone. Your own ledger, captain. Page one. The page you never read because it was under the binding."

Something moved behind his eyes. Doubt, maybe. Or the memory of doubt. The Wardens were not villains; I had to stop expecting villains. They were clerks, and clerks can be stopped with paper, and I had no paper, only a doorway.

So I held it. My arms, my back, my teeth. A stave caught my ribs and I felt the warmth leave them and I thought, absurdly, of the fox, of how it had once offered to eat my memory of the winter and I'd said no, I'd said *I want to keep the bad parts too, they're load-bearing*—and I laughed, in the middle of it, because the load was bearing.

Above me, through the door I was being, I could hear Beatrix writing. I could hear the scratch of the stylus like rain on a roof, steady, steady, and I understood that every second I stood was a second of ink, and I stood.

### Wick

Here is a thing about foxes: we do not guard. Guarding is a dog profession. Guarding is standing still and barking at the concept of tomorrow. Foxes *keep*. We keep what we have taken, and we keep it beautifully, and we do not give it back unless the terms are extraordinary.

The terms were extraordinary.

The keystone's surrendered memories were in my keeping—every mapped line, every valley morning, the whole bright hoard of Beatrix Quintana's remembered life, poured into me at the ghost-mother's instruction while Beatrix wrote the new covenant out of the space it left behind. I carried them the way I carry everything: curled around them, tail over nose, in the little den of myself where I keep the things I have eaten and never digested.

They were *loud*, those memories. The smell of the valley after rain. The specific scratch of a particular pen. The elder's voice saying *answers, child, I need answers* and the dread-wonder of the night the stars went out. The moment on the road when four strangers became, without anyone voting on it, a family. I lay in the Spire's shadow with a hoard that was not mine and understood, with a fox's clarity, that this was the first thing I had ever kept that I would have to give away.

The Wardens' vanguard broke past Dain—I heard him go down, heard Sera scream a word that was not in any hymn I have eaten—and three grey shapes came for the keystone, for the page, for the child writing itself out of existence.

I stood up. All nine tails of me. Every trick I had ever eaten, every stolen face and borrowed hour, and I put them between Beatrix and the Wardens like a wall of everything they had ever forgotten.

"You cannot process me," I told the captain, showing all my teeth, which is a great many. "I am not in your ledger. I *am* a ledger. And I am *closed*."

They came anyway. Procedure. I met them with fire I had eaten from a burning library and a flood I had stolen from a drowned year, and I held, and I kept, and above me the scratch of the pen went on.

### Beatrix

I was writing the last line when I felt Dain fall, felt Wick rise, felt Sera's stars come back one by one like a pulse returning to a throat. I wrote through all of it. There was nothing else in me. There was no *me* in me; I was pouring myself onto the page, valley morning by valley morning, my mother's hands over mine at the cartography chest, the first map I ever drew of a place I'd never seen, the ward-line breaking like glass in my chest, the pass, the winter, the fox's teeth, the priest's terrible hymns, the soldier's stubborn door.

The ghosts sang. It was not a song you hear so much as a song you *agree* with.

The last line was the hard one. The old covenant had failed because they had forgotten *unwillingly*. Mine had to be given. So I wrote: *Let this accord stand not on law, which dies, nor on blood, which spills, but on the choice of the keystone, freely made and freely lost—and let what is surrendered be kept, not by stone, but by love.*

And I signed it.

And the world—breathed.

I felt the covenant take hold the way you feel dawn through a wall: not seeing it, just everything getting less afraid. The realms stopped pulling at each other's seams. The stars—Sera's stars—burned whole and certain. Somewhere below, the Wardens stopped, because their ledger had a new first page, and it was not a page for erasing anyone.

Then the last of me went into the ink, and the valley went with it, and the morning, and the mother, and the name.

* * *

I woke on stone, in light, holding a blank map.

Three faces were above me, and none of them were familiar, and all of them were crying, and I did not know why, but I found I did not mind.

"Hello," said the woman with starlight in her hair. "My name is Sera. We have a great deal to tell you."

"One story at a time," said the soldier, hoarse, holding his ribs.

"And I'll keep the rest," said the fox, "safe. I'm very good at safe."

I looked down at the blank page in my hands. It wanted a first line. Everything, I sensed, would always want a first line.

I drew one—a single stroke, north to south—and beneath it, in a hand I did not remember learning but that knew exactly what it was doing, I wrote a single word:

*Home.*
