## 2. A Proclamation Nailed to Every Door

The morning the world changed, I was elbow-deep in a ledger entry for a bottled storm.

This is the sort of sentence that would have gotten me burned a week later, so let me be precise about it. The Vellum Guild trades in impossible things—bottled storms, borrowed shadows, a vial of last winter's moonlight that some fool in the capital paid forty crowns for because his mistress liked the way it made her jewelry glow. Our ledgers record all of it. My job was to make the impossible look boring. A storm becomes *one (1) vessel, sealed, contents agitated—handle with gloves*. A shadow becomes *rental, per fortnight, returned in original condition*. Boring is armor. Nobody burns a clerk.

I had been at the storm for an hour because the client had insisted on itemizing the thunder separately from the rain, and because Master Aldous Vell—the guildmaster, whose name is a small irony I only appreciated much later—believed that if an entry could be argued with, it hadn't been written properly yet. The thunder was proving difficult. Thunder does not like being a line item. Neither, as it turns out, do I.

Above me, through the clerks' loft's high window, the messenger hawk sat on its perch where it had lived for six years, and it was watching me with one amber eye, and it was thinking—at least this is how it came to me, the way weather comes to you before the rain: pressure, weight, a change in the air—*hurt, wing, wrong*.

I should explain about the hawk. Her name on our books was Quill, which is the sort of name guildmasters give to animals they think of as stationery. She carried contracts between Vellmouth and the coast towns, and she was very good at it, and three days ago she'd come back from a delivery to Greyharbor with her left wing bent at an angle wings are not meant to hold and a smell of dog-smoke in her feathers. The guild's ostler wanted to splint her and sell her on as a breeding bird—"she can still sit on eggs," he said, in the tone men use when they believe an animal's purpose survives the loss of everything the animal would call a life—and Master Vell had agreed, because Quill could no longer carry contracts and therefore, on the great balance sheet of things, had stopped existing.

I had been going up to the loft at dawn and dusk ever since, when the other clerks were abed or abuzz elsewhere, and laying my hands very lightly over the crooked joint, and doing the thing I do not talk about.

I want to be honest here, since honesty becomes fashionable later in this story whether anyone wants it or not: I don't fully understand what I do. I know what it feels like. It feels like listening to someone describe a pain they've never had words for, and then giving them the words. It feels like my grandmother's hands, though she died when I was seven and I never once saw her use the gift—only felt, sometimes, that the sparrows in her garden were paying attention to her the way people pay attention to a speaker, not a scarecrow. The gift runs in families. That is not a comfort. It is a genealogy, and genealogies can be read by other people.

So each dawn and dusk I climbed to the loft, and I pressed my palms to the warm feathers over Quill's broken wing, and I listened until her hurt turned into something I could almost translate—*bone, knit, slow, patience*—and I pushed back the shape of mending, and slowly, across three days, the wing began to remember itself.

She was watching me now with that amber eye, and beneath the ache there was something new in her. Curiosity, maybe. Gratitude, possibly. Or possibly hawks simply have excellent eyesight and I am flattering myself. But she watched me work, head tilted, and I worked, and the morning was ordinary, and ordinary mornings are the ones you never get back.

That was the morning the criers came through Vellmouth.

I heard them before I understood them, which is the usual order of things with bad news. First the bells—not alarm bells, worse, ceremonial ones, the deep bronze pair in the temple tower that rang only for coronations, wars, and royal births, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *who's died*, because my mind reached for grief before it reached for proclamation. Then hooves, many, riding in formation down the Coil Street. Then the voice, amplified by a speaking-horn, flat and official and carrying that particular quality of authority that makes your spine straighten before your brain objects.

"By decree of His Majesty, Aldric the Third, Protector of the Realm and Warden of the Wilds: be it known that the crown has declared a Hunt of Gifts. All persons possessing communion with beasts—the speech of animals, the calling of them, the binding or befriending thereof beyond natural measure—are hereby commanded to present themselves for registration within thirty days at any crown office. Those registered shall be permitted to live under supervision and surety. Those who conceal their gift, or who having concealed it are discovered—" and here the crier paused, because even professional announcers need a breath before a line like this, "—shall be put to death by fire."

Then, because the crown loves thoroughness, he read it again. And again. All down the street, door by door, while clerks leaned out of windows and fishwives stood in their aprons in the mud and everyone did the same thing, which was look at everyone else, quickly, and then away.

I did not look out the window. This is important. Every instinct I had spent twenty-six years sharpening said *be furniture*. I kept my head down over the storm ledger and I wrote *thunder, itemized, per strike* in a hand so steady it surprises me still, remembering it, and I listened.

Around me, the clerks' loft did what lofts do. Bramwen the junior clerk said "about time" in a low voice, because Bramwen's cousin had lost sheep to something in the hills two winters back and had decided it was witchcraft, because everything is witchcraft if you're tired enough. Old Hetty said nothing but her quill stopped moving for a full minute, which for Hetty was a scream. Tam, who is young and thinks aloud, whispered "do you think it's true? Do you think there really are people who can—" and was shushed by three people at once, which told me more than his question did. Fear doesn't shout. Fear goes quiet and starts counting exits.

Quill shifted on her perch. I felt her feel my heartbeat change. Hawks notice these things. I kept my face bored, which is the expression I practice in the little mirror in my room: *bored, competent, invisible*. The face of a woman balancing a ledger. The face no one burns.

* * *

Master Vell called the staff together at midday, in the counting room, under the stuffed marsh-heron that has hung above the guild's accounts for sixty years and watched every transaction with glass eyes and, I always thought, quiet judgment.

He stood with his back to the heron, a small neat man made smaller by the occasion, or perhaps I only noticed his smallness afterward, once I knew what he was. He had ink on his fingers—he always had ink on his fingers; it was practically his heraldry—and he rubbed them together the way he did before difficult entries.

"You will all have heard the criers," he said. "I will not waste time on sentiment. The Vellum Guild trades in unusual goods. Unusual goods attract unusual attention. It is therefore in the interest of every person under this roof that we be seen to comply with the decree fully, promptly, and visibly." He said *visibly* the way other men say *profitably*, and I understood, even then, that compliance for Master Vell was a kind of trade, and trades require goods.

"Accordingly," he continued, "any anomaly observed among the staff or among our clients is to be reported to me directly. Anomalies may include—" and he consulted a paper, which meant the crown had sent him a list, which meant somewhere a clerk in the capital had sat down and written, in a fair hand, *signs of beast-communion*, and I have thought about that unknown clerk more than I probably should, "—persons who linger near stables without cause. Persons whom animals watch unusually. Persons who speak to animals, or are spoken to by them, however the observer interprets such speech. Unusual calm in beasts. Unusual distress in beasts. Birds nesting where they ought not. Dogs refusing orders they previously obeyed."

I wrote them down. We were instructed to write them down, all of us, in the margins of our day-books, anomalies as we encountered them, and I wrote them down because refusing would have been an anomaly. My pen moved. *Lingers near stables. Animals watch unusually. Speak to animals.* Somewhere under my ribs a cold thing unfolded itself, joint by joint, and settled in to stay.

Because here is what I could not stop noticing, standing there with my careful list: every sign on the crown's page described my mornings. Every single one. I lingered near the loft without cause. Quill watched me. I spoke to her, in whatever language that was. Her calm around me was unusual—she had bitten the ostler twice and never once offered to bite me. I was, by the crown's own arithmetic, a catalog of anomalies wearing an accountant's apron.

And the guildmaster, whose ledgers I had balanced for nine years, whose storms and shadows and moonlight I had made legible to the crown's tax assessors, was asking the room to report me to him.

I looked around at the faces. Bramwen, jaw set, already composing his report. Hetty, unreadable, quill still. Tam, frightened and eager in equal parts, the most dangerous combination there is. Marla from the shipping desk, who had once covered for me when I was late, and who would not meet my eyes. None of them met anyone's eyes. That was the worst part, maybe. Not malice. Just everyone quietly deciding, in the space of one proclamation, that looking at each other had become expensive.

"The guild will comply," Master Vell finished, "and the guild will be rewarded for compliance. His Majesty's assessors take note of loyal houses. Loyalty, gentlemen and ladies, is also a commodity, and one currently appreciating."

There it was. He couldn't help himself. He priced everything, including us. I remember thinking, with a strange floating clarity: *he'll want the reward to be substantial. He'll want it itemized.*

The meeting ended. We filed out. On my way past the perch in the loft, I paused—just a pause, just a woman checking a window latch—and let my hand rest a moment on Quill's good shoulder.

*I know,* I told her, in the wordless way. *I know what today was.*

She pressed her head against my palm, briefly, the way she never did when anyone else could see, and I went down to my desk and finished the storm ledger, because finishing things is what I do instead of panicking, and because a clerk who abandons an entry is a clerk people remember.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when I heard the names.

The counting room sits below the guildmaster's private office, and the old building carries sound the way a cupped hand carries water—up through the floorboards, along the beams, pooling in corners. In nine years I had learned the building's acoustics the way I'd learned the ledgers: which stairs creaked, which walls were thin, which hours Master Vell took his supper and therefore which hours his office held only him and whoever he didn't want the household hearing.

He wasn't taking supper. He was dictating.

I had come up to the loft for Quill's dusk visit—I had a whole choreography for it, lamp unlit, footsteps timed to the kitchen bell—but I stopped on the third stair, in the dark, because the voice above me was saying:

"—fourteen. Fourteen is respectable. More than the chandler's house gave, I'm told, and they got their tariff remitted for a year."

A pause. Another voice, lower, dry as old vellum: "Respectable isn't remission, Master Vell. The assessor asked specifically about your house. You employ forty souls and handle goods of... sensitivity. The crown expects the Vellum Guild to distinguish itself."

"I'm aware of what the crown expects." A chair scraped. Ink pot set down—I knew that particular ceramic knock. "Fourteen names, then. Read them back to me."

And the other voice read them back.

I will spare you the full recitation, partly because some of those names belonged to people I ate supper with, and partly because I have spent enough nights since hearing all fourteen in order, in that dry parchment voice, that I don't need to rehearse them here. What I will tell you is what I did while they were read: nothing. I stood on a dark staircase holding an unlit lamp and I did absolutely nothing, because the first name read was a dockhand named Sef who whistled to gulls, and the gulls came, and everyone had always laughed about it, and now his name was worth money. The second was Hetty.

Old Hetty. Who had said nothing at the meeting, whose quill had stopped. I thought of every afternoon she'd spent on the loading step sharing her bread crusts with the warehouse cats, cats that came to her from three streets off, cats that sat in a ring around her like a parliament. I had thought her silence at the meeting was caution. Perhaps it was. Or perhaps she had been counting exits too, and run out of staircases before I did.

The fourth name was mine.

"Freya Joubert," said the dry voice, and Master Vell hummed agreement, the small satisfied hum he used when a column balanced. "The Joubert girl. Clerk, nine years' service. Excellent with figures. The animals—" another hum, "—the animals are remarkably fond of her. The ostler mentioned it. The hawks settle for her, the carthorses lean on her, even the rats in the storeroom don't flee her. I assumed at first it was a knack. Some people have a knack. But the decree specifies 'however the observer interprets such speech,' and I observe that she goes to the loft at dawn and dusk, alone, when she believes no one sees, and the injured hawk's wing has improved against all veterinary expectation."

My heart was a fist beating on a door in a burning house. Above me, unhurried, Master Vell dipped his pen.

"Note the detail on her," he said. "'Improvement against expectation.' Assessors like verifiable claims. Anyone can say a girl talks to birds; half the kingdom's cranks say that. Write instead that the guild's stock-in-trade animal recovered under her sole care. Give them evidence. Evidence commands a better price."

Evidence. Nine years of my competence, catalogued. My invisibility, itemized. I had built my safety out of being overlooked, and he had gone and found an assessor willing to look closely, because looking closely was worth coin, and Master Vell had never in his life met a thing that wasn't worth coin.

"And the others?" the dry voice asked. "You listed fourteen. Some seem thin. The gull-whistler?"

"Sef's gulls follow his boat in weather no gull should fly in," said Master Vell. "The harbor master will confirm. As for thin and thick—" I heard him smile; you could hear it, that soft wet click of lips, "—the decree says register or burn. It does not say prove. Let the crown sort the thin from the thick. That's what they're paying us to avoid doing ourselves."

The pen scratched. Fourteen lines on a list. Somewhere in the capital, a fair-handed clerk would copy them onto an official roll, and rolls become warrants, and warrants become fires, and all of it would begin with a small neat man humming over balanced columns in a building I had called home since I was seventeen.

I made it down three more stairs before my legs informed me they had opinions. I stood in the dark of the stairwell between the counting room and the loft, in the smell of paper and tallow and old heron feathers, and I waited for the grief, and the grief came, and underneath it, quieter and colder, something else arrived and unpacked its bags. Not fear exactly. Fear I knew; fear was the mirror-practice face, fear was the steady pen. This was inventory. This was the ledger-voice, my own voice, reading back to me:

*Assets: one gift, concealed nineteen years. One hawk, half-mended. One grandmother's ring, one change of clothes, eleven crowns sewn into a coat hem because a girl who trades in impossible things learns early what's possible. Liabilities: fourteen names on a list, one of them mine. Thirty days minus one. A guildmaster who prices loyalty and sells evidence.*

*Conclusion:* the ledger-voice said, in my own handwriting, *the account is insolvent. Close the book.*

Above me the pen was still scratching, patient as a worm in wood. Below me the counting room lay dark, the stuffed heron hanging in the black with its glass eyes open, watching the accounts as it always had. I thought of my grandmother again, suddenly and completely—her garden, her sparrows, the way she once told me, laughing, *never let them count what you love, Freya, counting is how they own it.* I was seven. I thought she meant toys.

Somewhere upstairs, Master Vell blew out his lamp, satisfied with the day's trade.

Up in the loft, Quill stirred on her perch, feeling for me through the floorboards, through the dark, through whatever it is that connects us—wing, knit, slow, patience—and I stood on the stairs between the man selling names and the bird learning to fly again, and I understood that I had exactly one night, and that the life I had built out of boredom and careful columns was already ash, and that the only question left worth pricing was what I would carry out of the fire.

I went up to the loft. I had a hawk to finish mending, a page to find and burn, and until dawn, one last night of being nobody at all.
