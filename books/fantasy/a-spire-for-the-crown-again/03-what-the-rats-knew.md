## 3. What the Rats Knew

The rats of Vellum House were, as a rule, terrible gossips.

Not because they lacked information—they had more of it than any clerk in the building, being able to go where clerks could not and chew through what clerks would not dare—but because they lacked *priorities*. Ask a rat what the guildmaster had said behind his locked door, and you would receive, in no particular order: the words themselves, a detailed account of the cheese he'd eaten beforehand, an opinion on the state of his boots, a complaint about the cold, and a digression about a rival rat named something that translated roughly as *Biter of Ankles*.

I had learned, over eleven years, to sort the wheat from the chaff. I'd learned it the way I'd learned everything: quietly, at night, alone, with no one to correct my mistakes but the rats themselves, who were honest critics if nothing else.

That night, in the loft above the counting room, I had three of them lined up on the beam over my cot, tails hanging down like frayed bell-pulls. Their names, as best I could render them: *Crumb-Sister*, *Sewer-Old-One*, and a young male I'd taken to calling Pip because his actual name was a sound like a sneeze and I refused to say it with a straight face every time.

"Again," I whispered. "From the top. Just the words. Leave the cheese out of it."

*Cheese was good,* said Crumb-Sister, with feeling.

"I know. I believe you. The words."

*The door was shut,* she said. *The old-one-who-smells-of-ink sat alone. He spoke to a man made of paper.*

"A man made of paper."

*A thin man. Dry. He rustled when he moved. He had a voice like a ledger closing.*

I knew that voice. Everyone at the Guild knew that voice. Serah Vane's clerks came twice a year to collect the tithe of records the crown demanded of every trade house, and the one who did the actual talking was a grey functionary named Ossel, a man so forgettable that I had once watched him introduce himself to the same door-warden three times in an afternoon. But the voice he carried—the voice he quoted, the voice that lived behind him like a shadow slightly taller than its owner—belonged to the Inquisitor herself.

*He brought a list,* Sewer-Old-One said. She was the eldest, her fur gone grey at the muzzle, and she had the rat equivalent of a patience I envied. *Names on it. The ink-one read names back to him. Fourteen.*

My hands, mending a hawk's jess by candlelight, went still.

*Say them,* I said.

She said them.

Rats do not have names the way we do, but they have *sounds*, and a name is a sound like any other. I sat on my cot and listened to a rat recite fourteen human beings, and by the fourth one my heart had climbed into my throat and stopped moving, because the fourth name was mine.

*Freya Joubert,* said Sewer-Old-One, in the careful, flattened tone rats use for human words, the way a child repeats a foreign phrase. *Freya Joubert. The ink-one said it twice. He checked it against a book.*

"A book," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "What kind of book?"

*The kind with a lock,* said Pip helpfully. *I chewed it once. The leather tasted of fear.*

Of course it did. The Vellum Guild kept two ledgers for every account: the open ledger, which any crown inspector could read, and the closed ledger, which recorded the *impossible* side of our trade. Bottled storms. Borrowed shadows. Contracts with things that signed in frost. I had balanced that closed ledger for six years, entry by entry, in my own small neat hand, and I had believed—I had *built my whole life* on the belief—that the guild's silence was a wall around me. That as long as I was useful, as long as the numbers balanced, as long as I asked for nothing and wanted nothing and *was* nothing, the guild would keep me the way it kept everything else: safely, privately, behind vellum and wax.

I had not understood that I was not behind the ledger.

I *was* the ledger. One more impossible thing, entered in a locked book, valued at whatever price the guildmaster could get.

*There is more,* Sewer-Old-One said, and her voice had the flat carefulness of a rat delivering bad news to a friend. *The paper-man said the Inquisitor pays by the head. The ink-one argued. Not against the selling. Against the price.*

I set down the jess. Very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands want to do something else entirely.

"He argued about the *price*."

*He said fourteen was a poor lot. He said the guild's reputation deserved better terms. The paper-man said the Inquisitor does not bargain with heretics' brokers.* Pip's whiskers twitched. *The ink-one laughed. He said—* Pip stopped. Rats can hesitate. It's one of the more human things about them.

"Say it," I told him.

*He said: "Burn the lot of them, then. It saves me the cost of their keep."*

The candle guttered. Above us, in the mews, the hawk I'd been tending—Wren, a messenger bird with a torn wing and an opinion of everyone—shifted on her perch and made a small sound, the one she made when she felt my mood change and disapproved of it.

I had thought, in the two nights since the Proclamation went up on every door in Copperlane, that my fear had a shape: the crown, the pyres, the zealous machinery of the Gift-Hunt grinding toward me from outside, from *them*. I had imagined the guildmaster as a frightened man, weak the way most men are weak, and I had already begun—God help me—to compose excuses for him. He has a house full of staff to protect. He has the guild's charters to think of. He is old, and old men buy their lives the way they buy everything: cheaply, and without looking at what they're buying.

But he hadn't been frightened. He'd been *negotiating*. He'd argued the price of my life the way he argued the price of a bolt of storm-silk, and when the buyer wouldn't meet it, he'd laughed.

I sat very still for a long time.

The rats waited. They are good at waiting. It is most of what they do.

"Crumb-Sister," I said at last. "The locked ledger. The one Pip chewed. Where does the guildmaster keep it at night?"

*The iron cabinet,* she said. *In the counting room. The lock is old. I could open it in a night, if the door were open.*

"The door will be open," I said. "I have the key."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt, walking down the back stair at the hour past midnight, because honesty is the one thing I have never been able to trade away, even when it would have been cheaper to lie.

I was not afraid. That came later. What I felt, descending into the dark of the house that had been my whole world since I was fourteen years old, was something worse than fear. It was *grief with its face turned away*. The kind of grief you feel for a person who is still alive, which is the cruelest kind, because there is no body to bury and no grave to visit and everyone keeps acting as though the person still exists.

The Vellum House at night smelled of tallow and ink and cold stone, and it had smelled that way for eleven years, and I loved it. That is the thing I could not get around. I *loved* it. I loved the counting room with its forty-one drawers, each labeled in the guildmaster's crabbed hand. I loved the loft where the hawks muttered in their sleep. I loved the smell of the vats where storm-silk was set, the hiss of the bottling room, the way the whole building settled at night with a sound like an old man turning over in bed. I had made a life inside these walls out of almost nothing—a cot, a candle, a talent I could never speak of, and work done so well that no one ever looked at the person doing it. I had told myself that was safety.

It was not safety. It had never been safety. It was a stall in a market, and the guildmaster had been fattening me for sale since the day I walked in, and the Proclamation had simply told him the season had arrived.

The counting room door opened to my key without a sound. I had oiled it myself, years ago, for reasons I had never let myself examine. Perhaps some part of me had always known I would leave this way: at night, quietly, by a door I'd prepared.

The iron cabinet stood in its alcove, and my hands did not shake as I put the key in the lock. I had balanced this guild's impossible accounts for six years. I knew every entry. I knew which contracts hid behind which innocent line in the open ledger. I knew, with the particular intimacy of a bookkeeper, exactly where the bodies were buried, because I had recorded the invoices for the shovels.

The closed ledger lay where it always lay, on the second shelf, spine toward the room. I took it to the counting table, lit the lamp, and turned to the page.

There it was. My own name, in my own handwriting, entered eleven years ago by the guildmaster and copied fair by me in my first month, before I understood what I was copying. *Freya Joubert. Beast-speaker, unregistered. Retained for value of animal liaison in trade negotiations. Discreet. No family to make inquiry.*

*No family to make inquiry.*

I read that line four times. Eleven years ago, when I'd inked it fair, I had read it as a kindness. The guildmaster had been noting, in his cold way, that I had no one—no one to worry about me, no one to come looking. And I, a fourteen-year-old orphan with a secret that would have gotten me burned in any city in the realm, had been *grateful*. Grateful that my emptiness was useful. Grateful to be entered in a book like a bolt of cloth: *discreet, no inquiries.*

I had built my life on being worth keeping. And the whole time, I had been worth *selling*. The only question was the price, and he had argued it, and lost, and laughed.

Thirteen other names shared the page with mine. I read them all. The cook's boy, Tam, who could make sourdough rise in half the time because the yeast *liked* him. The night-porter, old Hesk, who whistled the stray dogs of Copperlane out of the gutters and into warm doorways. A laundress at the Guild annex whose name I didn't know, entered only as *the washerwoman, gift: small, domestic, harmless*. Fourteen souls, and the guildmaster had priced us as a lot, and Serah Vane's grey clerk had called it a poor lot, and the guildmaster had laughed and said burning us would save him the cost of our keep.

I did not cry. I want that recorded, by someone, somewhere, because I have been called cold by people who never had to decide, at fourteen, whether crying was a luxury they could afford. I did not cry. I sat in the counting room of the only home I had, in front of the only work I had ever been proud of, and I felt something in my chest go quiet and hard and *clear*, the way a forge goes clear when the metal is ready.

Then I took out my knife and cut the page from the ledger.

It was a crime. It was, in the eyes of the Vellum Guild, possibly the greatest crime I could have committed—the destruction of a record, the theft of an account, the unbalancing of the books. I had spent six years making sure those books balanced. I cut the page out with the same steady hand I'd used to keep them true, and the blade went through vellum with a sound like a small, clean sigh.

I did not take only my page. I took all fourteen. If my name was going into the fire, so was Tam's, and Hesk's, and the washerwoman's whose name I would carry with me now because no one else would. I folded the pages flat and put them inside my shirt, against my skin, where they lay like a second heartbeat.

Then I did the other thing. The thing I had decided on the stair, in the dark, with grief's face turned away.

I went to the mews.

* * *

Wren was awake, of course. Hawks keep their own hours, and she had been awake for most of my midnight visits for three years, watching me with one yellow eye while I tended her wing and told her things I could tell no one else. She could not answer—not in words. She was a messenger bird, not a speaker's familiar, and whatever she understood of me, she understood it the way animals understand weather: completely, and without the need for commentary.

"I'm going," I told her, opening the mews window to the cold. The city lay below, grey and moonless, the Proclamation's fresh paste still visible as a pale rectangle on the door of the chandlery across the lane. "I want you to know that. In case it matters to you. I don't know if it matters to you."

Wren stepped onto my wrist. Her grip was careful, the way it always was now, her talons sheathed in the soft leather wraps I'd made her. Her wing was three weeks from flight-ready. I had counted the days. I had been counting the days the way you count anything you're afraid to want.

"You could stay," I said. "The guild will keep feeding you. You're an asset. They don't burn assets." My voice did something on the last word, and Wren tilted her head at me, and I made myself finish. "I'm not asking you to come. Your wing isn't ready. If you come, you'll be dead weight, and I can't—"

She bit me. Gently, on the knuckle, the way she did when I was talking nonsense, and then she did the thing that broke me, finally, after a full night of not breaking: she pressed her head against my jaw, feathers soft as ash, and made the small sound that in hawk means *yes*, and in Freya, apparently, meant *yes, you fool, obviously*.

"Right," I said, when I could speak again. "Right."

I wrapped her in my cloak against the cold, because a hawk with a healing wing cannot be carried in the open wind, and I took the back stair, the oiled door, the alley behind the tannery where the smell covered everything and no one looked at anyone. And as I went, I felt the house behind me—not the building, the *institution*, the great blind ledger of the Vellum Guild—close over the space where I had been, the way water closes over a stone.

Eleven years. One candle. A cot, a talent, and a page in a locked book. That was the whole of it. That was my life, weighed and entered and sold at a price the guildmaster had argued was too high.

I did not look back. Looking back is for people who expect to return, and I had cut myself out of the book.

* * *

The rats came with me as far as the city wall.

I hadn't asked them to. But when I paused in the shadow of the tannery to get my bearings—north, toward the river, toward the marsh country beyond Copperlane where nobody goes because there's nothing there to take—Crumb-Sister appeared on a barrel head, then Sewer-Old-One, then Pip, and behind them a dozen more, flowing out of the cracks of Copperlane like spilled ink finding its level.

*Where will you go?* asked Sewer-Old-One.

"North," I said. "Away."

*The city is full of doors,* she said. *The country is full of teeth.*

"The country's teeth," I said, "haven't been paid to bite me."

She considered this, in her slow grey way. *The old-one-who-smells-of-ink will tell them where you went. He will tell them you had a way with animals. He will tell them everything, because it costs him nothing and buys him a week.*

"I know."

*Then hear this, before you go.* She came closer, onto the toe of my boot, which no rat had ever dared, and her voice dropped into the register rats use for things that are true and heavy. *The paper-man was not the only one the ink-one wrote to. There was another letter. It went east, to a knight. A knight who owes the guild a debt and hunts runaways for it. The ink-one sold your name twice, little speaker. Once to the Inquisitor. Once to the man with the sword.*

The cold went through me like a draft under a door.

"A knight," I said. "Which knight?"

*The one the mare remembers,* said Pip, and then, when I stared at him: *The horses talk. Not the way you talk. But the horses remember you. The bay mare with the scarred shoulder—she remembers the hand that pulled the thorn from her hoof and the fever that would have killed her, two winters past. She belongs to a knight who is called Corvath now, and nothing else, because they took the rest of his name when they broke him. And the mare has told every horse between here and the east road: the small one who smells of hawk and ink, the one who is kind—if the sword-man comes for her, the sword does not rise.*

I stood in the tannery shadow with a hawk asleep against my chest and a folded page of fourteen names against my heart, and I laughed. It came out of me sideways, cracked and half-hysterical, the first sound I'd made all night that wasn't a whisper. Because that was my life, apparently: sold to an inquisitor, sold to a knight, and *still*—still—the animals were keeping accounts of their own. Still there was a mare somewhere on the east road telling other horses that I was the one who was kind. Still there was a debt in the world that ran the other way.

"Thank you," I said to Sewer-Old-One. "For all of it. The eleven years."

*You balanced the granary accounts,* she said, as if that explained everything, and perhaps it did. Rats keep ledgers too. Theirs are simply simpler: who fed, who starved, who kicked, who was kind. By the rats' arithmetic, I had been in credit for a decade. By the guildmaster's, I had been inventory.

"Tell the others," I said. "The gifted ones. Tam, and Hesk, and the washerwoman. Tell them to run. Tell them a small voice says run *now*, before the paper-man comes with his list. They won't know who it's from. Tell them anyway."

*It will be done,* said Sewer-Old-One, and I believed her, because rats do not make promises they don't keep. Their whole civilization runs on kept promises. It occurred to me, standing there, that the rats of Copperlane had a more honorable society than the Vellum Guild, and that this was perhaps the saddest thing I had learned all night, in a night that had been aggressively educational.

I went over the wall at the tanner's corner, where the stones were old and the mortar was older, and I dropped into the ditch on the far side with Wren a warm weight against my ribs and the city at my back. The marsh country opened ahead of me, black and whispering, full of reeds and cold water and things that moved.

I had never been outside the walls alone. I had never been outside the walls at all, except to walk to the annex with a ledger-chain and an escort. I did not know the marshes. I did not know the east road. I did not know where a person went when she had cut herself out of every book that ever held her name.

But the reeds knew I was there. I could feel it—a stirring, a turning, a thousand small attentions swiveling toward me through the dark, the way a room of people turns when a door opens. The marsh did not know what I was. But it knew I was *something*, and it was listening.

"North," I said aloud, to no one, to everything. My breath made a small ghost in the cold. "Away."

And from deep in the reeds, from a throat I could not see, something made a sound back.

Not a word. Not yet. But not nothing, either—the first voice of the wild, answering the first voice of the small runaway who smelled of hawk and ink, calling across the dark like a question.

I stood in the ditch with my heart hammering and my hawk breathing softly against my chest, and I understood, with the certainty of a woman whose books have finally, irreversibly, stopped balancing, that I had just left the only world I knew—

—and that the world I was walking into had been waiting for me to answer.
