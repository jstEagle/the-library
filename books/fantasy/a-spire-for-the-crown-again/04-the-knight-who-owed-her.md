## 4. The Knight Who Owed Her

There are three ways a man learns he's been broken, in my experience. The first is the moment it happens—the sword coming down, the knee going out, the captain reading your name off the dishonor roll with that particular flatness they save for men who used to matter. The second is the first night after, when you reach for the person you were and find nothing there to hold.

The third way is slower. The third way is a mare named Briar refusing to cross a river because she doesn't like the look of the reeds, three years into your disgrace, on a bounty job you took because eating felt preferable to pride.

"Move," I told her.

Briar flicked one ear back at me, which for her constituted a full debate, and went on snuffling at the marsh water like it owed her money.

We'd been three days tracking the runaway out of Corvane, and I'll be honest about why: the notice had described a woman, alone, guild-stamp still fresh enough to smell of ink, headed into the low country where nobody goes unless they're fleeing something or hiding something or both. Fifty crowns. Fifty crowns was two months of oats and bad rooms and not asking the innkeeper's opinion of discharged knights. I'd stopped asking people their opinions of discharged knights around the time I stopped being able to afford the question.

The marshes east of the Thornwood are the color of an old bruise this time of year—green going purple at the edges, water lying flat and patient between the hummocks like it's waiting for someone stupid. The road gave out a mile back and became a suggestion, then a rumor, then reeds. I should have turned around. Disgraced men develop an excellent sense for when to turn around, and we ignore it professionally.

Briar stopped entirely at the edge of a wide black pool and stood there radiating the equine conviction that somewhere ahead of us was a thing she did not wish to meet.

"You've never once been wrong," I said. "That's the problem with you."

Because here's what I hadn't told the magistrate who signed my bounty license: horses talk. Not in words—I'm not mad, whatever the men at Greyharbor say—but any cavalry soldier knows a horse reads the world the way we read a page, all at once, whole sentences in a twitch. And Briar had been reading something in these marshes for the past hour that made her walk like a nun in a tavern.

Then the wind shifted, and she went very still, and I understood that whatever she'd been reading had just read *her* back.

She turned her head and looked at me. Directly. Which horses don't do, generally—it's a dominance thing—and if you'd told me a mare could communicate *well, this is your fault* with one brown eye, I'd have called you a liar, but there it was.

"Briar." I put my hand on her neck. Her muscles were jumping under the skin like plucked harp strings. "What is it?"

What it was, was a woman standing up out of the reeds thirty feet ahead of us with a billhook in her hands, and I swear on every oath I no longer hold that she had been there the entire time, motionless, and neither Briar nor I had marked her until she chose to be seen.

* * *

I knew her face before I knew her name, which is a strange order for things to happen in.

You don't forget the woman who saved your horse. Three years ago, at the Vellum Guild's stables in Corvane—this is the part where I confess that a disgraced knight sometimes does honest work hauling trade goods, and the Vellum Guild trades in goods no honest warehouse will touch—I'd watched a chestnut carter-horse colic so badly the farrier said kill her and be done. And I'd watched a small, plain, quiet clerk from the ledgers' room come out in the rain and put her hands on the horse's barrel and do something—I never saw what, exactly; I looked away, because whatever it was had a shape to it that felt like eavesdropping—and twenty minutes later the horse was eating mash and the farrier was saying he'd seen miracles but never one wearing an apron.

Briar wasn't even the horse in question. Briar was in the next stall, and she had watched the whole thing with her ears forward like a woman at a sermon, and from that day forward she loved that clerk with the uncomplicated totality that horses reserve for maybe four people in a lifetime.

Freya Joubert. I'd delivered enough crates to the Guild to learn the ledger-keepers' names, and hers stuck because she was the only one who never spoke to me like furniture. She'd asked about Briar's lameness once, actually listened to the answer, and written something in the margin of her book while I talked. Nobody writes down what a disgraced man says. It had unsettled me for a week.

And now here she was, up to her knees in marsh, holding a farm tool like she'd only heard of farming, with three days of flight written on her in mud and thorn-scratches, looking at me the way you look at a door when you're already calculating the window.

"Corvath." My name, flat. Of course she knew it; I'd hauled her guild's crates for a season. "Fifty crowns on your head as of last week."

"Fifty," I said. "Inflation hasn't hit runaways yet."

Something moved behind her eyes that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Her knuckles were white on the billhook. Behind her the pool lay flat and black, and I noticed—finally let myself notice—that the reeds along the far bank were moving wrong. Swaying against the wind. In a pattern.

"There's no road through here," she said. "You'll lose the marsh light within the hour. It takes strangers."

"I'm not a stranger. I'm a bailiff of the crown now, technically. Very glamorous work."

"You were a knight."

"For eleven months longer than I deserved to be." I dismounted, slowly, keeping my hands visible, because I have been on the other end of a frightened person's weapon and it teaches you things. "Freya. Put the billhook down. You're holding it like a pen."

Her jaw tightened. But she shifted her grip—lower, balanced, actually correct—and I revised my estimate of her upward. Three days in the wilds and she'd learned something, or remembered something, or both.

"They say you hunt runaways now," she said.

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them were truer before."

"The Gift-Hunt. Registration or burning. You're collecting names for Serah Vane."

"I'm collecting fifty crowns," I said, "and I'm discovering that saying it out loud makes it sound worse than it did in my head, which is a service I perform for myself free of charge."

That got the almost-laugh again, killed quicker this time. And then, because the marsh light was going amber and long and because I am, at core, a man who asks questions too late, I finally asked the one that mattered.

"Why did Vane want you? Fourteen names came out of your guild. What's in your ledger?"

* * *

Here is the thing about the next sixty seconds: I drew my sword.

Not quickly. Not with any intent I could have articulated. It's just that when a woman you've cornered looks past your shoulder and says, very quietly, "*Don't*," in a voice that isn't aimed at you—and the reeds behind you go silent in a ring expanding outward like a stone dropped in water—a man reaches for the only authority he has left, which is iron.

Or I reached for it. My hand found the grip. I got it halfway up—

—and Briar stepped into me.

Not shied. Not spooked. *Stepped*, sideways, deliberate as a dance figure, putting her whole warm bulk between my sword arm and Freya Joubert, and swung her head around and set her teeth against my wrist above the gauntlet. Not biting. Holding. The way you hold a child's hand at a funeral.

"Briar." My voice came out strange. "Let go."

She didn't. Her eye rolled white-rimmed to me, and I have faced charging boar with less fear in my stomach, because what was in that eye wasn't fear. It was *judgment*. It was three years of a mare who had watched a quiet clerk kneel in the rain and mend what medicine couldn't, and it was the flat, immovable certainty of an animal deciding that some debts are collected by proxy.

"She saved you," I said stupidly. "Not me. She saved Ashfall. You didn't even—" 

But that's wrong, and I knew it was wrong as I said it, because Briar had stood at that stall door and *watched*, and I have since come to believe that watching counts. That witnessing is its own kind of debt. Horses keep better accounts than the Vellum Guild ever did.

Freya hadn't moved. The billhook stayed low. The ring of silent reeds held its perfect circle around us, and in the black pool behind her, something surfaced without a ripple—just a pair of nostrils and two flat eyes, patient as the water itself—and submerged again.

"Tell her to release me," I said.

"I don't tell them anything." First time her voice shook. "They're not mine to command. They just—" She stopped. Started over. "They know things. That's the whole crime, Corvath. That's the list I'm on. I hear them. That's all the gift is, and Vane burns people for it, and your mare is telling you what she thinks about that, and I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I know what it costs you to be contradicted by your own horse."

The marsh light was nearly gone. Somewhere north, faint as a thought, a hound gave voice—then a second, then a chorus, and none of them sounded like they were hunting rabbits.

Vane's dogs. Vane's hounds, working the marsh line, and every hair on Briar's body said she had known about them before I did.

I looked at my sword, half-raised, held by nothing but habit and fifty crowns of oat-money. I looked at the woman whose grandmother's guild I'd hauled crates for, shaking with cold and fear and still holding her ground because running would tell the hounds exactly where to run *to*.

And I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I have been paid to be on the wrong side before. At least this time it pays worse.*

I sheathed the sword. Briar released my wrist at once and bumped my shoulder hard enough to stagger me, which I choose to interpret as forgiveness rather than commentary.

"East," I said. "The hounds will swing wide of the deep channels—they hate peat, it eats scent. There's a drover's causeway two miles south of here, half-drowned, that leads to old saltern ruins. Dry floors. Nobody hunts it because the maps mark it cursed, and the maps are wrong, it's just poor drainage."

Freya stared at me. "You're letting me go."

"I'm giving you directions. Letting implies I could stop you, and you've got a horse's endorsement and a marsh full of witnesses."

"Corvath—"

"Don't." I swung back up into the saddle, because it's easier to be decent at a height. "Whatever you're about to say, don't. I haven't decided what I am yet. Ask me again in Greyharbor and I might still sell you. That's the honest answer and you deserve one honest thing tonight."

"That's two honest things," she said. "Greyharbor. And the causeway."

And then she was gone—down into the reeds, quick and low, and the silence-ring followed her like a tide, and within ten heartbeats the marsh had swallowed her whole. The pool's flat eyes surfaced once more, regarded me with what I can only describe as professional interest, and sank.

* * *

I rode north toward the hounds, because a man covering his tracks must leave tracks worth finding, and I let Vane's handlers take my description of a lone woman heading west toward the coast road. It cost me nothing but a lie, and lies are the one currency I'm rich in.

But Briar wouldn't turn south at the fork. I gave her her head toward the coast road and she ignored it, planted her feet, and pointed her ears east—toward the drowned causeway, the cursed salterns, the poor drainage.

"We're not doing this," I told her.

She stood there. Wind came off the flats and smelled of salt and coming rain.

"Fifty crowns," I said. "That was the whole plan. Oats. A roof. Dignity, eventually, in installments."

One ear rotated back toward me. *Well?*

And the truth, which I have promised myself one per night, is this: somewhere between the sword and the wrist and the eye, something had shifted in me that I couldn't shift back. For eleven months I had worn a knight's oath and discovered it fit a liar. Since then I'd worn nothing, because nothing fits reliably. But Briar had just shown me a garment I hadn't considered—debt, witnessed and unpaid, three years accruing interest in a horse's heart—and it fit the way ill-fitting things sometimes suddenly do, all at once, with a sound like a bone setting.

I have no oath. That's the whole of my condition. But I know what I owe, and to whom, and it has recently come to me that a man with no oath and one debt may be more dangerous than a hundred sworn knights, because he cannot be ordered to forget it.

"All right," I said. "East. But we're not following *her*. We're following the hounds' flank, so that when they find her—and they will, Vane doesn't send two packs for a clerk—we're between her and them. That's all. Debt discharged, then Greyharbor, then I'm done with impossible women and the marshes that produce them."

Briar stepped out at a trot so brisk it was nearly insubordination.

The rain caught us at dusk. Behind us, the hound-chorus swung wide of the deep channels exactly as I'd predicted, and I took a certain sour comfort in being right, right up until the moment the wind changed again and brought me a new scent-thread of information, in the old cavalry language of instinct:

The second pack wasn't behind the woman.

It was ahead of her. Waiting at the saltern ruins. Which meant somebody had read the marsh the same way I had—which meant Serah Vane didn't send dogs after runaways.

She sent them ahead of where the runaways were *going.*

I turned Briar east, and we ran.
