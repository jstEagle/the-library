## 5. Where the Beasts Went Silent

Three days in the Thornwood taught me two things: that I could survive on foraged sorrel and stubbornness, and that I had never once in my life appreciated silence properly, because I had never before been punished by it.

The first day out of the marshes, the birds still sang. I remember being annoyed at them, actually — a thrush somewhere overhead announcing my position to the entire canopy, as if the crown's bounty men needed a herald. *She's here, she's here, the beast-speaker with the muddy boots and the guild ledger's ink still under her fingernails.* I'd shushed it. It had ignored me, which was fair. I'd ignored plenty of creatures in my time.

By the second day, the birds had stopped. Not migrated. Not gone quiet for the night. Stopped — the way a room stops when someone says the wrong name at a funeral.

By the third day, I was walking through a forest that had decided I wasn't there.

Now, you'd think a woman whose entire life had been an exercise in not being noticed would find that a comfort. And for about an hour, I did. Then the wrongness of it settled into my bones like damp. I am not a person who needs company, exactly. I'm a person who needs the *option* of company. There is a difference, and the difference is the difference between choosing an empty room and being locked in one.

The Thornwood was locked.

I crouched at the base of an oak and put my gift out the way I'd done ten thousand times before — gently, a hand extended palm-up, no demands. *Anyone home?*

Nothing.

Not *no*. That was the terrible part. A refusal has texture. A squirrel who doesn't want to talk to you flickers something like *busy, busy, nuts, go away* across the back of your mind. A hawk ignores you with magnificent, pointed dignity. But this was a door where a door had been, and behind it, nothing — not an empty house, but a house holding its breath.

"Still nothing?" Dain asked.

I hadn't heard him approach, which was its own small humiliation. For a man in half-plate he moved like a rumor. He'd kept his distance for three days, trailing me at fifty paces like a debt he hadn't decided whether to collect, and somewhere around the second morning the fifty paces had quietly become twenty, and then he'd started carrying firewood, and now here he was, standing over me with that face like a man perpetually braced for bad news he intends to be disappointed by.

"Still nothing," I said. "Not even the worms."

"Worms talk?"

"Everything talks. Or did." I stood, brushed off my knees. "Worms mostly complain about drainage. You'd like them. Single-minded. No politics."

"I like politics fine."

"You liked politics fine when politics was paying you. Now you're a fugitive's pack mule. See how your opinions evolve."

He didn't smile. Dain Corvath rationed smiles the way misers rationed gold, and I'd only ever seen him spend one, and it had been at the mare, not at me. "The silence started at the river," he said. "Before that, the woods were normal."

"Before that, the woods hadn't met me." I said it lightly. It didn't land lightly, because it wasn't meant lightly, because some cold little voice in my chest had been whispering the same thing for a day and a half: *they know what you are, and they are afraid, and they have gone where you cannot follow.*

The mare — Bracken, a name I'd learned from the horse herself, who considered Dain's name for her (*Bess*, gods help us) an insult she was too dignified to litigate — stood hipshot under a holly, ears swiveling. She was the only creature still speaking to me, and even her voice had gone careful, hedges and qualifiers. *Bad country,* she'd said that morning. *Old bad. The kind that was here before the paths.*

Horses don't usually say things like *the kind that was here before the paths.* Horses say *grass* and *run* and *that stallion is a menace.* When a horse starts philosophizing at you, you pay attention.

"We should go back," Dain said.

"To what? Vane's list has my name on it. Possibly yours now too, knight. *Corvath, Dain — failed to kill one (1) runaway, whereabouts suspicious.*" I was already walking. I don't know why I was walking. When a house holds its breath, the idiot thing to do is knock. "The Thornwood goes through to the high fells. No roads, no waystations, no inquisitors. If the animals won't talk to me, fine. I'll be invisible the old-fashioned way."

"And when the food runs out?"

"I'll eat your boots."

"They're good boots."

"I know. I've been looking at them for three days with growing interest."

He fell into step behind me, because that was the arrangement we had and neither of us had ever said aloud: he followed, I pretended not to notice, and we both pretended this wasn't the first kindness either of us had accepted in years.

* * *

The silence deepened through the afternoon the way cold does — not all at once, but in layers, each one a degree worse and harder to name.

It wasn't only that nothing spoke. It was that nothing *was*. We passed a stream that should have been loud with frogs. We crossed a clearing that should have been mobbed by finches and midges and the small busy commerce of a summer wood. We saw tracks — deer, boar, fox — all of them fresh, all of them *fleeing*. Every track ran the same direction, deeper into the Thornwood, away from us, and not in the loose scattered way of animals going about their evenings. In the way of a crowd leaving a building.

"They're not avoiding us," I said, stopping so suddenly Dain nearly walked into my back. "Look."

He looked. He was better at reading ground than I'd expected from a man who'd spent his life reading people's faces for a living, and I watched him arrive at it — the same direction, the same urgency, mile after mile.

"A stampede," he said slowly, "with no fire behind it."

"With *me* behind it." My mouth had gone dry. "Dain, they're not running from a predator. They're running from — " I gestured at myself, at the whole muddy, exhausted, guilty shape of me. "Whatever I am. Whatever I carry. They felt me coming three days ago and they've been leaving ever since."

"You're a beast-speaker. Shouldn't that be the one thing they *don't* run from?"

"You'd think." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You would think. That's the entire — that's the whole *point* of me. That's the one thing I've ever been good at, and the one thing I've ever been, and the woods have taken a vote and decided I'm a plague."

Bracken snorted and pressed her nose against my shoulder. *Not you,* she said. *Not exactly.* And then, when I pressed, which was rude of me: *Not only you.*

*Not only me.* Wonderful. I filed it under things I'd examine when I wasn't standing in a forest full of exits.

We made camp in the hollow of a deadfall, no fire, because Dain had opinions about fires in hostile country and for once his opinions and mine agreed. I lay on a bed of bracken listening to a silence so complete I could hear my own blood, and I thought about my grandmother.

This is what I did, those first weeks on the run. I thought about my grandmother, because she was the one who'd taught me the gift — or rather, she was the one who'd never once acknowledged it existed while making absolutely sure I could use it. *Mind the hawk, Freya, she's touchy in this weather.* *Don't stand behind the mare, girl, she'll tell you if you listen.* Lessons disguised as chores. A whole apprenticeship smuggled past my own notice, hidden in plain sight the way everything in our family was hidden, the way I hid, the way the guild hid its bottled storms behind dry invoices.

She'd been dead six years. She'd been dead six years and she was still the most present person in my life, and I missed her the way you miss a wall that's been holding up your house.

The silence pressed in. Somewhere in it, I finally slept.

* * *

The wolf woke me at moonrise.

I didn't hear it. That's the thing people get wrong about wolves — they think you hear the wolf coming. You hear nothing, and then the wolf is simply *there*, the way winter is there, the way grief is there, occupying space it has decided belongs to it.

It stood at the edge of the deadfall, grey as the moonlight it stood in, and it was old. I could see the age in the grizzled muzzle, the clouded eye, the deliberate way it held its weight off one hip. A wolf that had survived to grey had survived everything, including, apparently, the general evacuation of its species.

And it was looking at me.

I sat up very slowly. Dain's hand found his sword hilt; I put my hand over his without looking and pressed it down. *Don't.* Not because I wasn't afraid. Because the wolf had come a very long way through a country its own kind had abandoned, and you do not greet an ambassador with steel.

*You're loud,* the wolf said.

Her voice — and it was her, I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall — was like the silence itself had learned to speak. Dry. Weighted. A voice that had not been used in a long time and had not atrophied so much as *compressed*, the way coal compresses.

"I'm sorry," I whispered.

*Don't be sorry. Be quiet. You're loud.* She came a step closer, and I saw Dain's jaw set and his knuckles whiten and the mare shift nervously behind him, and I also saw that he didn't draw, because a knight who owes a debt to a woman who talks to horses will extend a provisional visa to a wolf. *Three days you've been walking through this wood with that thing in your chest, ringing like a struck bell. Every living thing for ten miles has been deafened. Do you know what it's like, hearing you?*

"No," I said honestly. "I don't. I've never — I didn't know I was loud. I've spent my whole life being quiet."

*You've spent your life being quiet to them.* The clouded eye fixed on me. *To the two-legs. Quiet voice, quiet face, quiet little ledgers. But the gift — * she said the word like something spat out and carefully retrieved — *the gift isn't quiet. It never learned to be. It's a shout with no one left who remembers what it's shouting.*

"Left?" My mouth said it before my mind caught up. "Left. You mean — there used to be more."

The wolf sat down. It took her a while, the old hip complaining, and she did it with the dignity of a queen who refuses to be seen managing her skirts. And then she told me.

* * *

I will set down what she said as best I can, though a wolf's telling doesn't translate cleanly into human words. It came to me less as sentences than as weather — images with weight, memory with teeth. But the shape of it was this:

There had been a covenant. Not a treaty the way humans write treaties, ink and parchment and clauses to be wriggled out of, but a real one, woven, the way a bridge is woven from both banks. The beasts had given the humans their voices — not all, but the *crossing*, the shared tongue, the place where horse and rider meet, where hound and hunter understand, where the hawk consents to the falconer's fist. In return the humans had kept faith: named the beasts, honored the kills, spoken for them in their councils, kept the wild places wild and the herds honest. Two realms, one conversation.

And the conversation had been the world. Not a feature of it. The *frame* of it.

Then the humans had learned they could take without asking. There had been a breaking — she did not say when, and when I pressed she showed me only a shape: firelight, a table, humans in fine cloaks, and something old and patient being *unpicked*, thread by thread, while one thread of it was kept, hidden, hoarded. The covenant shattered, and the crossing closed, and the beasts' half of the voice went dark in human ears. They could no longer speak to us. And we — we who had been their mouths, their advocates, the keepers of our side of the bridge — we forgot. Within a few generations we forgot there had ever been a bridge at all. We taught our children that animals were meat and mystery, and the mystery got smaller every year, and the meat got cheaper.

The beasts remembered. That was the worst of it. They remembered every word, every promise, every named winter and honored kill. They remembered, and they could not say so, and the ones who could still half-hear — the rare broken-threaded ones like me — rang in their ears like a bell tolling for a house that burned down generations ago.

*Your gift isn't a gift,* the old wolf said, and her voice was not cruel, which somehow made it worse. *It's a fragment. A shard of the crossing that didn't close all the way. Your line kept a thread. And every beast that feels you feels the whole broken covenant ringing through it — the promise, and the breaking, and the forgetting. You are not loud because of who you are, girl. You are loud because of what was done.*

The hollow was very quiet. Dain was staring at me; he could hear none of this, only watch me listen, and I think what he saw frightened him more than a wolf would have.

"The guild," I said. My voice came out strange, small. "My family. The Vellum Guild — we've traded for two hundred years. Privileges. Charters. Land grants no one questioned." The cold little voice in my chest had stopped whispering and started saying it plainly. "Someone bought those privileges. Didn't they. With something worth more than gold."

The wolf looked at me for a long time with her one good eye, and what was in it was not hatred. That was the thing I couldn't have predicted, lying awake nights fearing the beasts' judgment. It was *tiredness*. Ancient, bone-deep, generational tiredness, the tiredness of a creature who had waited so long for a human to understand that the understanding itself was almost too late to matter.

*Now you're asking the right question,* she said. *The one your blood has been running from since before your grandmother's grandmother. There is a place in the high fells where the breaking happened. There is a waystation, human-built, long abandoned, at the Thornwood's far edge. Your thread came from there. Go and see what your family kept, and what it cost, and then decide what you are loud *for*.*

She rose, slowly, and turned to go, and stopped.

*The others fled because they are old and tired and have been hurt too many times to hope,* she said. *I am old and tired too. But I have been hurt exactly enough times to be curious. Do not make me regret it, shard-bearer. I am too old to regret anything new.*

And she was gone, and the moonlight where she'd stood looked, for a moment, like a door.

* * *

Dain didn't speak for a long while. He'd built a small fire after all — I think the wolf's presence had broken something loose in his caution, or maybe he'd simply decided the forest couldn't hate us more than it already did. He sat turning a stick in the flames.

"You spoke with it," he said finally. "The wolf. For how long?"

"An hour. Maybe more."

"What did it say?"

I looked at him. Three days ago he'd been hunting me with a warrant and a sword, and now he was sitting across a fire asking me, in the voice of a man who had decided the answer mattered, *what did it say.*

I could have lied. I'm good at lying; I'd been good at it my whole life; invisibility is just lying with your whole body. But I was so tired, and the silence was so complete, and somewhere in the last hour a wolf had called me *shard-bearer* instead of *plague*, and something in me had cracked open along a line I hadn't known was there.

"It said the beasts used to talk to us," I said. "All of us. Everyone. And that we broke it. Not them. Us. And that my family—" My voice caught. I pushed through it, because if I couldn't say it to one disgraced knight by a fire, I'd never say it to a court, or a Spire, or a wolf. "My family helped break it. And I think I know where the proof is."

Dain fed the stick to the flames and watched it burn, and when he looked up, he didn't say *that's madness* or *you're a heretic* or *I should bind your hands and collect the bounty.* He said, in the flat practical tone of a man rearranging his entire world and refusing to make a fuss about it:

"Then we'd better reach that waystation before anyone else does."

We broke camp before dawn. And all through the grey light of that last morning in the Thornwood, as we climbed toward the fells and the abandoned waystation at the forest's edge, I noticed something I couldn't explain and didn't dare name:

The silence was still there. But it had changed. It was no longer the silence of a room I'd been locked out of.

It was the silence of a room where something old was waiting to see what I'd do next.
