## 6. Grandmother's Confession

The waystation looked like something the world had tried to swallow and given up on halfway.

It crouched at the edge of a clearing where the Thornwood thinned, all collapsed roof and moss-eaten timber, its chimney leaning at an angle that suggested it had been drunk for a century and saw no reason to change. Dain circled it twice before he'd let me inside, which I found excessive until I remembered he was a disgraced knight following a fugitive through cursed woods for reasons he hadn't yet admitted to himself. Caution was probably the only thing keeping him upright.

"Clear," he said, in the tone of a man who wished it weren't.

"Wonderful," I said. "I've always wanted to sleep in a ruin. It's on my list, right between 'die in a marsh' and 'be burned for having opinions about hawks.'"

"You could be less unpleasant."

"I could also be less hunted. Neither seems likely."

He grunted and went back out to see to the mare, which suited me fine. Three days in the Thornwood had taught us both the shape of our arrangement: he watched the treeline, I watched everything else, and neither of us mentioned that we were traveling companions now rather than captor and captive. The mare had settled the matter of his sword arm on the first night, planting herself between us with the immovable conviction of a creature who has decided how things will be. He hadn't argued. I suspect he'd stopped arguing with that horse years ago.

I went inside to make the ruin habitable, which mostly meant evicting the squirrels.

They were living in the walls — a whole family, fat and indignant — and when I reached for them with my gift they scattered in a clatter of claws and outrage. *Thief. Thief, thief, this is OUR house.* "I'm not taking your house," I muttered, gathering dead branches for a fire. "I'm borrowing it. There's a difference. Ask any guild ledger."

*Ledger-voice,* one of them chattered from the rafters, mocking. *Soft voice. Thief voice.*

Squirrels have no respect for anything. It's their single redeeming quality.

The fire caught on the third try. The wood was damp and smoked like a guilty conscience, but smoke meant warmth, and warmth meant I might sleep tonight instead of lying awake cataloguing every sound in the forest and deciding whether it wanted to eat me. I set my satchel down against the hearthstone and began pulling out what remained of my life: flint, a heel of bread gone hard as scripture, the burned corner of a ledger page I couldn't bring myself to throw away.

And then I saw the loose stone.

It wasn't hidden, exactly. That was the strange thing. The hearthstone sat crooked, lifted at one corner like a book left open, as though whoever had placed it there had wanted it found — eventually, by someone, someday. I told myself I was only curious. I told myself a great many things that evening, and believed approximately none of them.

Beneath the stone, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a journal.

* * *

I knew the handwriting before I knew the name.

I'd grown up with it. Every ledger in the Joubert household storeroom bore it — those crisp, slanted capitals, the numbers written with a precision that bordered on devotion. My grandmother, Maren Joubert, had kept accounts the way other people kept faith. When she died I was nine, and my clearest memory of her was watching her rule a line beneath a column of figures with a straightedge and a small, satisfied exhale, as if she had just put the universe itself back in order.

The first pages were ordinary. Trade routes. Grain prices. Notes on which guild factors could be trusted and which would sell their mothers for a favorable tariff. I read them half-smiling, homesick in a way that surprised me. This was the world I'd fled — ledgers, ledgers, ledgers — and here it was again, in her hand, comforting as a litany.

Then the dates turned backward. Past her apprenticeship. Past her mother's entries, because the journal had been Maren's mother's before it was hers, and hers before that. Four generations of Joubert women, one book, passed down like a debt.

I should have stopped reading when the handwriting changed hands. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, in some ledger of my own mistakes: I knew, the way you know weather coming, that nothing good lived in these later pages. And I turned them anyway. Curiosity is just greed wearing a nicer coat.

The entry that changed everything was dated a hundred and sixty years ago, in a hand I didn't recognize — the oldest, the founder's, my blood at its source.

*We met with the Spire's wardens tonight. Aldric says the beasts have grown proud, that they set the terms of trade as though they were lords and we were petitioners. A hide costs a season's labor because the wolves demand it so. A river crossing requires the permission of things with feathers. I am done asking leave of animals.*

*Aldric has found another way. There are three keys that bind the old covenant — the pact that gives the beasts their voice among us and us our passage among them. Break the keys, break the covenant, break their leverage. The Spire sleeps. The wardens grow fewer every year. No one will stop us.*

*Maren-to-be, if you ever read this: know that we did not do it out of hatred. We did it out of arithmetic.*

Arithmetic.

I sat very still in the smoking firelight while a hundred and sixty years rearranged themselves around me.

Every child in the guild districts learned the story the way they learned their letters. Once, long ago, men and beasts spoke together under an accord sealed at the Spire; then the accord frayed — no one said why, no one taught why — and the beasts went silent, and the wilds went wild, and mankind built walls and called it progress. A sad old tale. A fable with a moral nobody could locate.

We didn't lose the covenant. We broke it. My people broke it. On purpose. For margins.

I turned the page because my hands had decided to keep going without consulting me.

*The first key we took from the drowned-folk, and cheaply — a lie and a bribe, and the harbor folk have hated our kind ever since, and who can blame them. The second key the wardens hid well, but Aldric's man found where it slept. The third they say cannot be taken, only given, and so it remains in the stone at the Spire's root, and there let it remain, a toothless relic.*

*When the last binding failed, the silence came all at once, like a held breath released. I will not pretend I felt nothing. The hounds in the yard looked at us afterward — looked, and did not speak, and their eyes were the eyes of strangers. Aldric celebrated. I went home and washed my hands four times and they still felt unclean.*

*But the trade privileges came. Oh, how they came. The crown granted the Vellum Guild exclusive charter within the year. Every road, every market, every ledger since — all of it built on that silence. We are rich because they are mute.*

*If any daughter of mine reads this: I do not ask your forgiveness. I ask your attention. The covenant was not destroyed. It was wounded, and wounds can close. Someone must speak the words again, someday, at the Spire, with the keys. We made sure such a someone would be hard to find. We killed the speakers first.*

I closed the journal.

Outside, the wind moved through the Thornwood, and for the first time since I'd entered it I heard nothing answer. No birdsong. No rustle of small bodies in the brush. Just wind, going about its business alone, the way wind does when there's no one to talk to.

*We killed the speakers first.*

That was the Gift-Hunt, then. That was Serah Vane and her proclamations nailed to every door. Not new at all. Just the old crime, come around again, wearing a fresh coat of crown-livery — the descendants of the breakers hunting down the last fragments of what they'd broken, terrified of the bill finally coming due.

And I had spent my whole life hiding my gift behind ledgers. Ledgers. Built on *this*. I had balanced the accounts of a house whose foundation was a silenced throat, and I'd been proud of my neat columns. Proud of my invisibility. Invisible, I'd thought. Safe. As if safety were the same thing as innocence. As if the granddaughter of thieves could hide inside the theft and call it a life.

The squirrel chattered at me from the rafters. *Sad-voice. Stone-voice.*

"Yes," I told it. "Thank you. Very helpful."

* * *

Dain found me like that an hour later, sitting cross-legged with the open journal in my lap and the fire down to coals, not moving. To his credit, he didn't ask if I was all right. Men who ask that question usually don't want the answer, and he at least had the manners to skip the ritual.

"The wolves stopped following us today," he said instead.

I looked up. "What?"

"Three days they've shadowed us. Kept to the trees, never showed themselves, but I counted them each morning by the tracks. Today — nothing. They turned north." He crouched across the fire from me, forearms on his knees, studying my face with the flat attention he probably used on battlefields. "You know something."

"I know a great many things. Most of them are worse than whatever you're imagining."

"Try me. I'm a disgraced knight sleeping in a ruin with a fugitive beast-speaker. My imagination has already been thoroughly ruined."

So I told him. Not all of it — I couldn't have said all of it aloud without my voice doing something humiliating — but enough. The journal. The keys. The guild's founding bought with a broken pact. The speakers murdered so no one could mend what the Jouberts had torn.

He listened the way he did everything: silently, completely, annoyingly. When I finished he was quiet for a long moment, feeding a stick into the coals.

"So the hunt," he said slowly. "Vane's hunt. It isn't madness. It isn't even policy, really. It's inheritance. The crown's been afraid of this reckoning for six generations, and now the beasts are restless enough that the fear's turned into law."

"That's the shape of it."

"And you—" He hesitated, which he almost never did. "Your family did this."

"My family did this." Saying it aloud was like swallowing a stone. "Four generations of Joubert women kept that journal and none of them mended it. They just... kept the books. Balanced the column. Passed the debt down with the good china." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I used to think my gift was the secret shame of my house. Turns out it's the least of what we're hiding."

Dain poked the fire. "You know what I did to earn my disgrace?"

"You've never said."

"I refused an order. A lord ordered his levies to burn a village that had sheltered a speaker — an old woman who talked to bees, nothing more, she kept his rival's orchards healthy. I stood in front of the granary and said no." His jaw worked. "They took my spurs, my name, my place in the rolls. The village burned anyway, two days later, when I wasn't there to stand in front of it." He looked up at me. "I've spent three years telling myself the order was the corruption. One bad lord. Now you're telling me the rot goes back further than the kingdom itself."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." He nodded at the journal. "Those keys. The drowned-folk hold one, you said. Greyharbor way?"

"According to this. If it still exists. If the drowned-folk haven't thrown it into the sea out of spite, which, given what my ancestors did to them, would be entirely reasonable."

"Then tomorrow we go to Greyharbor."

"*We?*" I stared at him. "Dain. You heard the same proclamation I did. You're harboring a fugitive. You're past harboring one — you're *escorting* one toward an artifact of a forbidden covenant. There is no version of this where you come out clean."

"There was no version of that where I came out clean either," he said, nodding at the burned-village story, "and I notice the clean versions are always the ones where I do nothing." He shrugged, that heavy, economical shrug of his. "Besides. You saved my mare's life. She's decided you're hers now. I've learned not to argue with her about decisions."

"That's your reason? The horse?"

"She's a very good judge of character."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to tell him to ride south, back to whatever tattered honor he was trying to stitch together, and leave me to walk into my family's mess alone, because that was the arithmetic of it — my blood, my debt, my column to balance. But the fire was low, and the woods were silent, and the truth was that the journal had taken the ground out from under me, and the thought of tomorrow without another pair of eyes at the treeline made something in my chest go cold and small.

"Greyharbor," I said finally. "Fine. But if the drowned-folk ask for payment, we're negotiating. I'm not paying with anything I'll miss."

"Everything you own fits in one satchel."

"Which is why I can't afford to miss any of it."

He almost smiled. It was the closest he'd come in four days, and I counted it a victory and pretended not to.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the coals with the journal open on my knees and read it through twice more, cover to cover, because it seemed suddenly obscene not to know every word my grandmothers had written — every figure, every justification, every flicker of guilt quickly smothered under trade notes. Maren's final entry, near the end of her section, was dated the winter before I was born:

*I have a granddaughter now. They say she is quiet. I find myself watching her and wondering what the beasts would say to her, if they could say anything at all. Perhaps it is better they cannot. Perhaps it is a mercy, being born after the silencing, into a world where nothing wild expects anything of you.*

*Or perhaps we stole something from her too, and simply never wrote it in the ledger.*

I read that last line perhaps twenty times. Then I wrapped the journal back in its oilcloth, tucked it inside my coat against my heart like the contraband it was, and went to stand in the doorway of the ruin and look at the silent trees.

Somewhere north, the wolves had turned away. Somewhere south, an inquisitor was burning names off a list, mine among them. And somewhere ahead, in a drowned city that had every reason to hate me, lay the first key to a door my family had helped slam shut.

The beasts owed me nothing. That was the thing I kept circling, the thing that sat in my chest heavier than the journal. Every animal I'd ever soothed, every hawk and rat and trembling cart-horse — I'd thought I was giving them a kindness with my little fragment of the old gift. But if the covenant had never broken, they wouldn't need soothing. They'd have voices. They'd have terms. They'd have justice.

My gift wasn't a kindness. It was a scrap of stolen goods, handed down like the china.

Behind me, Dain shifted in his bedroll. Above me, the squirrel muttered in its sleep. And far off — so far I might have imagined it, and chose, for once, not to decide — I thought I heard a wolf begin to howl, low and long and patient, like something that had been waiting a hundred and sixty years and could wait a little longer, provided you started walking.

I picked up my satchel.

Greyharbor was five days east, and for the first time in my life, I was in a hurry to arrive somewhere.
