## 7. Serah Vane's Method

The guildmaster of the Vellum Guild wept before I had finished unrolling my instruments, which told me two things: he was guilty, and he was a coward, and only one of those was going to be useful to me.

"Master Aldous Renwick," I said, laying out my papers on his own table—his table, in his own counting room, because I had found that people confessed more readily surrounded by the evidence of their own comfort. Candlesticks. A decanter of southern wine. A ledger bound in calfskin so fine it probably cost a farmhand's yearly wage. "You have been trading in impossible things for thirty years."

"That is—that is our specialty, Inquisitor. Bottled storms. Borrowed shadows. Perfectly legal under charter—"

"There is no such thing as a bottled storm." I let that sit while he blinked at me. "There are only things men pretend not to understand so they can put a price on them. Every 'impossible thing' your guild has ever sold was made possible by a gift. And every gift in this realm now belongs to the crown's inquiry. Which means every impossible thing belongs to me."

He reached for the wine. I moved the decanter six inches out of his reach without looking at him. It is a small cruelty, but interrogations are built of small cruelties, like masonry.

"I have cooperated," he said. His voice had gone up half an octave, into the register men use when they want you to think of them as harmless. "I gave you the list. Fourteen names. I did it to protect my people, truly—"

"You did it to protect yourself," I said. "Which I respect, actually. Honesty is rarer than honesty about honesty, if you follow me. But let us not dress it up. You sold fourteen souls to save one, and the one you saved was yours."

His hands were fat and soft and they wrung each other like a hen being plucked. "The law is the law," he whispered.

"Yes," I said, and for a moment—and this will surprise no one who knows me, and would surprise everyone who thinks they do—for a moment I meant it completely, cleanly, the way I had meant it at nineteen years old standing in the ashes of my family's house. The law is the law. The law is all there is between what happened to me and what happens to everyone else.

Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and I was just tired again.

"Read me the list," I said.

* * *

I keep the names in my own hand, copied out in iron-gall ink, because clerks make errors and errors hang innocent men. Not that innocence enters into it much anymore. The Gift-Hunt edict does not distinguish between the raven-speaker who murders a magistrate's household and the hedge-witch who tells a farmer his cow is breeched. A plague does not distinguish either. You burn the field to save the harvest. My father taught me that about blight; life taught me the rest.

Renwick read. I wrote. Joubert, Freya. That name I underlined twice, and Renwick noticed, because cowards notice everything.

"The beast-speaker," he said carefully. "She worked my ledgers for eleven years. Quiet girl. Never caused trouble."

"Gifted people never cause trouble," I said. "That is rather the point of them. They cause *events*. The trouble arrives afterward, wearing feathers, or smoke, or a magistrate's blood."

"You speak as though you've—" He stopped himself. Clever enough to stop himself.

"As though I've what, Master Renwick? Lost someone? Been wronged?" I capped my inkwell with a small precise click. "Everyone in this room has lost something. That is not an argument. It is a weather report."

But he had seen the crack in the wall, the way cracks show themselves to anyone looking for a way through, and he pressed it the way pressed men always press, stupidly, hopefully, like a starving man gnawing a boot.

"My niece saw a speaker once," he said. "In the eastern provinces. She said it was beautiful. The woman spoke with a horse and the horse—wept, she said. Wept like a person. How can that be a plague?"

I could have told him. It is the question I have answered a hundred times, in a hundred rooms like this one, and I have never once given the true answer, because the true answer takes eleven years and a dead family and cannot be compressed into an interrogation.

Instead I said: "Your niece saw a horse weep. I saw what came after. Shall we compare notes?"

* * *

Here is what I remember, and I have arranged the memory so many times over the years that it is less a memory now than a well-kept archive, cross-referenced, indexed, immune to doubt. Doubt is a luxury of people whose houses did not burn.

I was nine. Autumn, the year the eastern magistracy changed hands. My father was a wool factor—a plain man, honest ledgers, nothing impossible traded in our house except perhaps hope. My mother kept bees. My brother Tomas was four and afraid of geese, which I mention because it is the sort of detail that makes a person real, and I need him real tonight, the way I need him real every night, or else the whole architecture comes down.

The magistrate's name was Corvel. He held the eastern provinces' assizes, and he was, by every account that survived him, a man who took payment from both sides of every dispute and ruled for whichever hand was heavier. There was a shepherd, that autumn, who had caught Corvel's tax-collectors skimming, and who said so, loudly, in front of witnesses. Corvel could not arrest him without making a martyr. So he bought a different kind of justice.

He bought a speaker.

I learned the word much later—*raven-master*, they called such people then, before the polite euphemisms and then the ugly ones. A man who could put his voice inside a bird's throat and ride it through the sky. Corvel paid him to teach three ravens a single lesson: that grain left on a certain windowsill belonged to them.

My mother's bees kept to their hives. My father slept with the window latched. But Tomas was four, and four-year-olds leave windows open, and ravens are patient, and a child's face is soft.

They say he did not cry long. They say it kindly, the way people say things kindly when the truth is unbearable and kindness costs nothing.

I was at a neighbor's, learning to card wool. I came home to a house full of neighbors doing that terrible thing neighbors do, which is standing very still. My father sat on the step with my brother wrapped in his coat, and he did not weep, and I remember thinking—with the cold clear idiocy of a nine-year-old—that Father must be very strong. He was not strong. He was finished. He lived another nine years in exactly the posture he assumed that evening, a man waiting for a door to open, until the day it opened inward instead and took him too.

And here is the part I do not tell the priests, or the crown, or the fourteen names on my list, or anyone: I went back. Weeks later. A child alone, drawn the way mourners are drawn to graves. I stood in the lane below the windowsill where the grain had been left, and I looked up, and in the bare tree opposite there was a single raven watching me with one flat black eye.

And I spoke to it. Out loud, like a fool, like a grieving child. *Why?*

And the raven tilted its head, and opened its beak, and what came out was not a croak. It was almost words. It was the shape of words, the ghost of grammar, a voice straining against the bars of a bird's body—and I understood, with a certainty that has never left me, that it was trying to say *not me*. Or possibly *not mine*. Something between apology and denial, something that meant: *a man wore my voice like a glove and I could not take it off.*

I ran home. I did not sleep for a week. And somewhere in that sleepless week, a nine-year-old girl made a decision that has governed her entire life since, though she did not know it yet:

If a voice can be stolen and worn, then voices must be taken back. All of them. From everyone. Because any voice that lives outside the law is a weapon awaiting a buyer, and I had already seen what the weapon buys.

People think hatred is hot. Mine has never been hot. It is a ledger. Cold entries, balanced columns, every entry dated. Corvel died in his bed, fat and unpunished—the law failed me first, you see, before it ever failed anyone else, and that is why I serve it anyway, why I *am* it, why I have spent twenty years becoming the thing that should have come for Corvel and didn't. The raven-master was never found. Some say he fled east. Some say the birds turned on him in the end, which I confess I find satisfying, in the way one finds a fever satisfying.

So when Master Renwick asks how a weeping horse can be a plague, I do not answer with philosophy. I answer with arithmetic. One speaker, three ravens, one child. The column balances. It has always balanced. I have simply never shown anyone the working.

* * *

Back in the counting room, Renwick had gone quiet in the way of men who have talked themselves into a corner. Good. Silence is where confessions grow, like mold in a cellar.

"Fourteen names," I said. "And I believe there is a fifteenth you are withholding."

His jowls went grey. "No. No, Inquisitor, the list is complete, I swear it on the charter—"

"Swear it on something that matters. Swear it on the guild." I watched his eyes flick toward the ledger on the shelf behind him—the real ledger, not the pretty one on the table—and I rose and fetched it down myself, because a good inquisitor carries her own ladder.

It was exactly what I expected: a trade-house book, columns of goods and prices, utterly mundane. Except that the marginalia was not mundane. Small marks beside certain entries. A system. Guilds mark their anomalies; it is practically liturgy among people who traffic in the impossible. And one page had been torn out—not neatly, not recently, the stub browned at the edges. Someone had removed a record some time ago and thought the removal itself would go unnoticed.

Cowards always think the absence of evidence is the same as the absence of a thing.

"What was on this page?" I asked.

"Nothing. An error. A misbound leaf—"

"Master Renwick." I set the ledger down gently, which frightens men far more than slamming it. "I am going to tell you how the next hour goes. In the next hour, you will either remember what was on that page, or you will spend the next year remembering it in a cell, at leisure, with excellent light for reading and nothing whatsoever to read. Your choice. Take your time. I have wine."

He lasted four minutes, which for a merchant is nearly heroic.

"A girl," he said. "Freya Joubert. She kept the books—kept them *beautifully*, better than I ever managed—and there was a page, early, from her grandmother's time, a covenant-page, old guild business, and she burned it when the proclamation went up and she burned her own entry too and she *ran*, Inquisitor, she ran the night before you came, and I never saw her again, I swear it, I swear it on my mother's grave."

I wrote the date down. Cross-checked it against the watch reports. She had run the night before my arrival—which meant she had known I was coming before my warrant arrived, which meant she had ears in places ears should not be, which meant rats, most likely, because in my experience it is always rats.

"And her grandmother," I said. "The covenant-page. What covenant?"

"I don't know. Old trade terms. Old privileges. The Jouberts were founding families—they helped build the guild, they had arrangements with—" He faltered.

"With whom?"

"With the wilds," he said, very quietly, as though the walls might be listening, and in my experience they usually are. "Arrangements with the wilds."

I closed the ledger. Outside the counting-room window, Greyharbor's gulls wheeled over the rooftops, screaming their empty screams, and I looked at them longer than I meant to, the way I always look at birds longer than I mean to, checking, always checking, for that flat black intelligence behind the eye. Just gulls. Gulls are honest idiots. It is the clever ones you must fear.

A girl who burns her own page and runs the night before the hunt arrives. A grandmother with arrangements in the wilds. A torn page about a covenant nobody will name aloud.

I have hunted gifted souls for eleven years, and in all that time they have run like rabbits—fast, blind, in straight lines toward whatever cover was nearest. This one had erased herself from a ledger with a fire and a steady hand and left, behind her, a question shaped like a hole.

Rabbits do not ask questions. Rabbits do not keep grandmothers' secrets.

"Master Renwick," I said, gathering my papers, "you have been extremely helpful, and I want you to know that I noted, in my report, the phrase *cooperated fully*. Whether that phrase protects you depends entirely on whether it remains true. If the girl sends word. If anyone sends word. If a rat so much as sneezes in your cellars—you will send for me before you send for anyone else. Am I understood?"

He nodded so hard his chins agreed with each other.

One more thing, before I left. I paused at the door, because I had earned this, and because a man should know what he has done.

"The fourteen names," I said. "Did any of them ever harm anyone? Think carefully. It matters to me."

He blinked. "I—no. No, Inquisitor. They were quiet folk. Hidden folk. That was rather the tragedy of it."

"Yes," I said. "It usually is."

And I walked out into the grey harbor morning with my list of fourteen names, one of them underlined twice, and a hole in a ledger where a fifteenth used to be, and I told myself—as I tell myself every morning, in the voice of a nine-year-old girl standing in a burnt lane—that the column still balanced.

It is getting harder to say. The numbers are getting stranger. Somewhere out in the wilds, a girl who keeps impossible accounts is running, and everything in me that is still a wool-factor's daughter wants to know what her grandmother bought, and what it cost, and who is holding the debt.

I would find her. I always found them.

But for the first time in eleven years, I found myself hoping—if hope is the right word for it—that when I did, she would make me work for it.
