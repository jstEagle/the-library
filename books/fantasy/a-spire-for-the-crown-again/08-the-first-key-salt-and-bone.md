## 8. The First Key: Salt and Bone

*Freya*

Greyharbor smelled like a promise the sea had broken. Brine and tar and old fish gone grey in the sun, and underneath it all something sweeter and wronger—the smell of deep water that had come up to visit and left things behind.

Dain walked half a step behind me the whole way down the harbor road, which was his way of pretending he wasn't following me. The mare, Bramble, plodded at his shoulder like a chaperone who had chosen my side of the argument weeks ago and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

"You don't have to come," I said, for the fourth time since the Thornwood.

"I don't," he agreed, for the fourth time. "You also didn't have to steal a boat's worth of dried cod from a tollhouse, and yet here we are."

"That was trade."

"You left a copper on the counter."

"A *fair* copper." I adjusted the strap of my pack. "The drowned-folk deal in exchange, Dain. Everything with them is exchange. You can't walk into Greyharbor empty-handed unless you plan to leave empty of something else."

He was quiet for a stretch. Then: "What do they take?"

I had been avoiding that question the way you avoid looking directly at a hole in a bridge. Now the harbor opened out before us—black water between rotting piers, boats moored three-deep, nets hanging like funeral linens—and there was no more avoiding it.

"Things said aloud," I said. "Promises, mostly. Oaths. Names spoken true. They were witnesses once, back when covenants meant something. Witnesses need payment in weight, not coin."

"Weight," he repeated flatly.

We came down onto the lowest pier as the tide turned. The fishermen had all retreated up the shingle—that was the first wrong thing. Greyharbor's people never retreat from water; they argue with it. But today the whole town stood back from the tideline like children told not to touch a stove, and at the end of the pier, where the boards were green and slick, the water had gone very still.

Not calm. *Attentive.*

I knelt at the pier's edge and put my palm near the surface, not touching. This part of my gift has always felt less like speaking and more like opening a door I keep hoping no one notices is there.

"I've come to bargain," I said to the water. "For the salt-key."

The stillness rippled once, from edge to edge, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep. Then the sea divided its attention—and I felt it settle on me, and then slide off me, deliberate as a landlord's eye, onto the man standing behind me with a sword he hadn't drawn and a face like a man doing arithmetic about his own soul.

*Freya*

*That was the moment I understood we'd brought the wrong currency and the right one at the same time.*

*Let me explain, because Dain certainly didn't understand it, standing there gripping his sword-belt like a man at his own trial.*

*The key I needed hung below the harbor, in a bell of woven coral that the drowned-folk kept the way misers keep their first coin. My grandmother's journal had named it—the salt-key, one of three, forged when the covenant was young, given into the keeping of the sea's forgotten dead. The journal had also named the price, and I had read that passage four times and each time hoped the ink would change.*

*"They will ask you what you hold most binding," she had written, in her terrible slanted hand. "Do not lie to them. They weigh words the way we weigh gold, and they will hear the hollow ones."*

*I had assumed—I see now how much of my life has been built on comfortable assumptions—that they would take something from me. A secret. A name. Some piece of the invisibility I'd spent thirty years perfecting. I had rehearsed surrenders on the road. I'd decided which lie about myself I could afford to lose.*

*But the sea wasn't looking at me.*

---

*Dain*

*I have taken two oaths in my life. The first was at fourteen, kneeling on cold flagstone with a sword someone else held, swearing my life and judgment to the crown until death or dishonor took them from me. The second was quieter and worse: the oath I swore to myself the day Serah Vane struck my name from the rolls, that I would get through what remained of my life without needing anyone, owing anyone, or being seen.*

*Both oaths were about to be repossessed.*

"The beast-speaker bargains poorly," the water said.

I want to be precise about this, because people who weren't there never believe it properly. It did not come from a mouth. It came from everywhere the water touched—from the harbor, from the wet boards under our feet, from the fog rolling in off the strait—and it was layered, dozens of voices speaking almost together, some deep as shipwreck, some thin as spray. The hair on my arms stood up. Bramble, behind me, planted her hooves and refused to move another inch, and I have learned in these weeks of exile that when Bramble refuses, arguing is wasted breath.

"We came fairly," Joubert said. Her voice was steady. Hers always was, even in the marshes with my sword an inch from her ribs, which should have frightened me more than it did. "Name your price."

A shape rose beneath the surface—not breaking it, just suggesting itself. Something long. Patient. Old enough to have opinions about the crown.

"Price is not named," the voices said. "Price is *weighed*. Show us what you carry that binds."

Joubert glanced back at me. In three weeks of travel together I had catalogued her glances the way I used to catalogue troop movements: the quick one meaning *shut up*, the slow one meaning *think*, and this one—a new one—which meant *I am about to do something and I am sorry for it.*

"We have cod," she offered.

The harbor laughed. I did not know water could laugh. It sounds like drowning, if you're wondering.

"Cod rots," said the deep voice. "Words do not. The knight carries a heavy thing. The knight steps forward or the knight goes home."

Now, I want it recorded that I considered going home. There is a version of me—call him the man I became after Vane crossed my name off her list with that little ink-scratch sound—who would have shrugged, said *she's not my charge, I'm not her keeper,* and walked back up the shingle to find a tavern and forgettable work. That man owed Joubert a debt, but debts can be paid small. A warning here, a coin there. Nobody ever died of paying a debt small.

Except that Bramble had nearly died once, in a flooded ford outside Carrenmoor, colicking and thrashing while I stood in the water useless as furniture. And a woman with mud to her knees and no badge and no reason to stop had knelt beside my dying horse and *spoken to her*, quietly, in the voice you use for frightened things, and the horse had lived. And when the Gift-Hunt was proclaimed and every gifted soul in the realm became kindling, that same woman had looked at me across a marsh and not used whatever it was she could do. She had trusted a king's hunter with nothing but the fact of a saved horse.

Small payments. That was how I'd been living. She had paid large and never once mentioned the balance.

I stepped forward.

"What binds you?" the water asked, and it had stopped being many voices. It was one now, old and vast and tired, the voice of something that had watched oaths accumulate for centuries like silt.

I put my hand on my sword hilt—out of habit, not threat—and found I already knew the answer, the way you know a wound before you look at it.

"My oath," I said. "My oath to the crown."

*Freya*

"Dain—" I started, and he cut me off with a look I hadn't seen on him before. Not stubbornness. Something older than stubbornness. He looked like a man finally reading the fine print on his own life.

"It's mine to spend," he said. "You've spent enough of yours getting us here."

"You don't know what they'll—"

"I know exactly what they'll do." He turned back to the water and squared his shoulders, and for a moment I saw the knight he had been before the disgrace—broad, formal, ridiculous in the best way. "Take it, then. If that's the weight you want."

"Oh no," said the sea, and the amusement in it was worse than anger. "We do not take. We *witness surrender*. Speak it, knight. All of it. Words weighed, remember. Hollow ones float."

So Dain Corvath, disgraced of the Third Banner, stripped of rank and pension and the right to call himself anything, stood on a rotten pier in Greyharbor and gave his oath away out loud.

"I, Dain, son of Corvath, did swear my life and judgment to the crown of this realm, to hold until death or dishonor." His voice cracked once on *dishonor*—there's a story there I still haven't pried loose—and steadied. "I swear its surrender now. Not transferred. Not given to another master. Surrendered. Done. I will wear no livery again. I will kneel to no throne again. What loyalty remains in me I will choose myself, person by person, day by day, or not at all."

The harbor went silent. Even the gulls, I realized, had stopped screaming somewhere in the middle of it.

Then the water beneath the pier began to glow—a green-gold light rising from far below, up through fathoms of black, and the shape uncoiled and ascended, and this time it broke the surface.

It was a woman, or had been. Drowned centuries ago, remade by salt and patience, her hair a drifting garden, her eyes two coins at the bottom of a well. Around her neck, on a cord of kelp, hung a key the size of my forearm—bone-white shot through with veins of blue, dripping light instead of seawater.

She held it out to Dain, not me.

"The oath is weighed true," she said. "Heavy. Heavy as any sworn this century. Take the key, unsworn man. And take counsel with it: you have just discovered what the crown never taught you. An oath surrendered freely weighs more than an oath enforced ever did. Remember that when next someone asks you to guard something."

Dain took the key. His hands shook. I had never seen his hands shake, not in the marsh, not in the Thornwood when the silence got so thick we could hear our own heartbeats. He held the salt-key like it was both heavier and lighter than any sword he'd ever carried.

The drowned woman turned her coin-eyes on me, and smiled with too many teeth, kindly.

"And you, little speaker. Granddaughter of Maren Joubert."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach. "You knew her."

"We knew what she did." The smile faded into something older. "And we know what she wrote, and what you carry in your pack wrapped in oilcloth. Read it properly, girl. All of it. Before the mountain asks what you cannot answer." She sank as she spoke, the light folding down after her like a lamp going into a well, and her last words came up through the boards under my feet: "Two keys remain. One waits among the hidden ones who trust no one—including, soon, you. Choose quickly whether your invisibility is worth their lives."

Then the harbor was only a harbor. Behind us, up the shingle, the fishermen began moving again all at once, like puppets whose puppeteer had blinked.

* * *

*Dain*

We walked up out of Greyharbor with the sun going red behind us and the key wrapped in Joubert's cloak across my saddlebow, because she said carrying it felt like carrying a lit lantern through a dry field and I didn't argue. I hadn't argued about anything since the pier. My throat still had the shape of the oath in it, the way your mouth remembers a confession for days.

Bramble walked close enough to bump my shoulder. She'd been doing that since Carrenmoor, but it felt different tonight. Less like affection. More like acknowledgment.

"You didn't have to do that," Joubert said, when the town was a smudge of smoke behind us.

"No," I said. "Turns out that's rather the point."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't gone out of fashion in her family around the time they helped break a covenant between all living things. "Does it feel lighter? The books say surrendered oaths feel lighter."

"It feels like being naked in a wind I've been hiding from for twenty years." I looked at her. "Ask me tomorrow. Tonight I'm going to feel sorry for myself into a mug of whatever Greyharbor roadhouses serve."

But that night, at the fire, with her asleep sitting up against her pack the way she always did, one hand curled around her grandmother's journal like it might escape, I sat awake and turned the thing over. Twenty years I'd defined myself by what I wore and whom I served, and when both were taken I thought I'd lost myself. Turns out I'd only misplaced the costume.

Somewhere south, an inquisitor was tightening a net around people like Joubert—people who could talk to horses and hawks and, apparently, the drowned dead. Somewhere south, a child I'd never met was probably hiding from Serah Vane's writ. And here I sat, oath-free, free to choose, holding a covenant-key that hummed faintly against my knee like a sleeping cat.

Choosing, it seemed, was heavier than obeying. Nobody warns you of that.

Joubert woke at dawn with the journal already open, which meant she'd been reading before her eyes fully worked. Her face had gone the color of the harbor fog.

"There's word of a market," she said, not looking up. "Underground. Hidden gifted, traders in secrets. The second key passed through there a month ago and hasn't left." She finally raised her eyes, and they were scared in the specific way of someone about to walk back into the exact trap she'd escaped. "Dain. There'll be children there."

I swung the pack onto Bramble's back and thought about oaths, old and new.

"Then we'd better arrive before Vane does," I said. "Which way?"

She pointed south, toward the hills, and I noticed her hand wasn't quite steady—and that above the treeline, high and sharp against the morning, a single raven was flying our direction, fast, like a bird carrying news it had been paid to deliver.

Neither of us said the name we were both thinking.

We broke camp quickly anyway.
