## 9. A Market of Stolen Names

The rats told me how to find the market, which felt appropriate. Everything else in my life had been arranged by vermin lately.

"Third arch past the tannery," a rat named — well, I never learned her name; rats introduce themselves by smell, and hers was *sewage-with-ambitions* — had told me, sitting up on her haunches in the reeds while Dain pretended not to listen and failed spectacularly. "Knock like you're ashamed. Everyone there is."

Dain had wanted to come. I'd wanted him not to. We compromised the way people compromise when one of them is bigger: he came.

"You understand," he said as we walked the drainage road toward Greyharbor's lower quarter, "that I am a knight in a city where my face is on at least four warrants."

"You're a knight in a city where your face is on at least four warrants," I agreed, "and you're going to wear this." I handed him the fishmonger's apron I'd stolen from a drying line. It smelled like a crime scene.

He looked at it the way he looked at everything — like it might be a trap, and if it wasn't, he was disappointed. "I fought at Redford Bridge. I held a ford against forty raiders for six hours."

"And now you'll hold a stall against suspicion for six hours. Growth is painful."

He put the apron on. I will treasure until I die the memory of his face while doing it.

* * *

The third arch past the tannery was exactly as advertised, which is to say it looked like nothing — old brick, green-slicked, a rusted grate that opened when I knocked twice and then, feeling foolish, once more with shame. A woman peered out through the gap. Her eyes were the flat amber of a hawk's, and they went over me like a customs inspection.

"Speaker?" she asked.

"Something like that."

"Everyone here is 'something like that.' What kind?"

I hesitated. This was the moment I'd rehearsed for two days — the lie, the deflection, the small dishonesty that had kept me alive for twenty-six years. Behind the woman I could hear the market breathing: low voices, the clink of trade, something that chirped in a cage.

"Hawk," I said finally, because the truth was a door I wasn't ready to walk through yet, but I could at least stand near it. "Hawks, mostly. Small birds. Rats."

"Rats." She smiled without warmth. "Useful. Come in, rat-speaker. Don't touch anything that touches you back."

* * *

You have to understand what the Vellum Guild taught me about impossible things: they are inventory. Bottled storms have shelf numbers. Borrowed shadows have interest rates. I grew up believing magic was a commodity, priced and crated and sold to men who wore better boots than mine.

The market under Greyharbor was what magic looked like when nobody could afford to sell it.

It was a flooded cistern, half-collapsed, lit by lamps that burned — I later learned — on rendered fat from animals whose speakers had asked permission before the slaughtering, so the fat remembered being loved and gave light willingly. Stalls made of driftwood and salvage lined the wet stone. And everywhere, everywhere, people.

A man selling weather he coaxed down from the mountains — small jars of fog, a vial of the specific silence before snowfall. Twin girls who shared one gift between them: they could speak to moths, and moths carried messages across the city better than any royal courier, because no one suspects a moth. An old man whose hands were wrapped in bandages, selling nothing, just sitting by his empty stall with a scorpion on each shoulder like epaulettes, and everyone giving him a wide, respectful berth.

And fear. Fear hung over all of it like the damp. These people whispered. They flinched at footsteps. The twins kept their moths in painted boxes so no one would see them cluster. The weather-seller had a false wall behind his stall with a second identity nailed to it, ready to become.

I had spent my whole life thinking I was clever for hiding. Standing in that cistern, I realized I hadn't been hiding. I'd been *comfortable*. The Guild had given me ledgers and a room and a routine, and invisibility had cost me nothing except everything I didn't know I was missing.

"What are we doing here?" Dain murmured at my shoulder. He'd abandoned the apron within minutes — some dignity survives even disgrace — and now stood with his arms crossed, radiating *former knight* so hard that three separate people had already tried to sell him contraband on principle.

"Learning," I said. "Shopping."

"For what?"

"The second key." I'd told him about the wolf, about the covenant, about the keys — most of it, anyway, in the marshes and on the road, in the voice you use when you're still deciding whether to believe yourself. "The drowned-folk said the first key opens the way. But the wolf spoke of three, and the second one—" I touched the salt-white thing wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat, the key we'd paid for with Dain's oath, which he did not like to discuss, "—the drowned-folk said the second key answers to *kinship*. To the bond itself. If anyone here knows where such a thing is hidden—"

"You're going to ask frightened people to trust a stranger."

"I'm going to ask frightened people to trust a fellow fright. There's a difference."

He grunted, which from Dain is a full paragraph.

* * *

I worked the market the only way I knew how: sideways. I bought a jar of mountain fog I didn't need. I complimented the moth-twins' boxes. I let a hedge-witch read my palm and told her she was wrong about everything, which delighted her so much she told me three rumors for free.

That's how I pieced it together, rumor by rumor, the way I used to piece together accounts: the second key wasn't an object anyone carried. It was a *person*. Or had been. An old speaker, a woman named Maren Oduya, who could speak to anything — anything at all, beasts and birds and, some swore, the river itself — and who had spent thirty years as the market's anchor. Its keeper. When someone new came in terrified, Maren talked them steady. When the crown's patrols got close, Maren negotiated with the gulls to raise alarms along the coast.

"She was the covenant's last friend in this city," the palm-reader told me, lowering her voice. "Then the Proclamation came. She stopped coming. Some say she's dead. Some say she's gone to ground so deep even her own rats can't find her."

"Some say she's the key?"

The palm-reader laughed, a dry crackle. "Sweetling, everything here is the key to something. That's the trouble with us."

I drifted on, and the market drifted with me, and that was when I heard the voice.

Not with my ears. With the older listening, the one under my skin, the one I'd spent a lifetime pretending was imagination. A small voice, high and cracked with disuse, coming from behind a curtain of sailcloth at the market's far edge.

*Help. Help. Please. Nobody hears.*

I went through the curtain without deciding to.

* * *

A child sat on an overturned crate in a lean-to that smelled of pitch and cat. She couldn't have been more than eight. Thin, dark-skinned, hair braided tight against lice, wearing a man's shirt cut down badly. She was talking to a cat — actually talking, lips moving, eyes fixed — and the cat, a scarred gray torn with half an ear, sat perfectly still and listened the way animals only listen to one kind of person.

The way they listen to me.

"Oh," I said.

The girl spun, and I watched her do the thing I had done ten thousand times in mirrors: the flinch, the shutter, the quick rearrangement of the face into harmless blankness. "I wasn't doing anything," she said. "I was just — I talk to myself. Ma says it's fine."

"She does say that," the cat said. Cats always sound unimpressed. "She also cries into my fur at night, which I tolerate because I am generous."

"Hush," the girl hissed — then froze, realizing what she'd done, realizing I'd heard, realizing the cat had answered loud enough for a human ear.

"It's all right," I said, and knelt, slow, hands open, the way you approach anything wild. "My name is Freya. I hear them too."

The lie she'd built collapsed so fast it hurt to watch. Eight years old, and she already had a mask, and it fell off her face like wet paper. "You *do?*" she breathed. "Really truly? Not like Tam who says he can and then just makes bird noises?"

"Not like Tam." Something in my chest was cracking open, and I didn't know whether it was joy or grief or both wearing each other's clothes. "What's your name?"

"Wren."

"Of course it is," I said, and she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the first one this place had probably heard in weeks.

Wren talked the way children talk when they've been silent too long: all at once, a burst dam. She'd known since she was four. Her mother knew and was scared of it and made her promise never, ever, ever. Her mother worked the fish-docks. Her mother had sent her down here two weeks ago when the criers came through the dock district reading the Proclamation, told her to find "the cat lady," and Wren had found the market instead, and the market had taken her in, and nobody here could do what she could do, and she was lonely in a way that eight-year-olds should never learn the shape of.

"They don't talk right," she confided, nodding at the cat, whose expression suggested he had opinions about being discussed. "I mean, they talk, but it's all — hungry, scared, *mine*, warm, *go away*. Mister Gray says grown-up speakers can hear more. Whole sentences. Stories. Is that true?"

I thought of the wolf in the Thornwood, telling me about the Broken Covenant in words like weathered timber. I thought of the hawk in the Guild loft, years ago, saying *hurt, sky, home* and meaning all of it at once.

"It's true," I said. "It comes with time. And with — " *with the covenant mended*, I almost said, and swallowed it. "With practice."

"Mister Gray says there used to be more. Before. That the animals used to talk to everybody, long ago, and somebody broke it." She leaned forward, deadly serious, eight years old carrying a weight that had no business on those shoulders. "Do you think whoever broke it is sorry?"

The question went through me like a spear through wet canvas.

"Yes," I said, and meant my grandmother, and meant myself, and meant every Joubert whose ledger entries had built a guild on the interest of a broken vow. "I think they're very sorry."

* * *

I should have left it there. Found her mother, made arrangements, moved on. I had a mission. I had a key to find and an inquisitor presumably two steps behind me and a knight in borrowed suspenders loitering outside my curtain looking like a very depressed scarecrow.

But Wren caught my sleeve as I rose — caught it with both hands, the way children hold on, with their whole weight.

"Are you staying?" she asked. "At the market? Because if you stay, then — " she faltered, and I watched her try to be brave about it, and fail, and be braver for failing. "Mister Gray says the cat lady might be gone forever. And everyone here is scared all the time, and I'm scared too, and if there's another one — if there's *you* — "

"Wren."

"If the soldiers come," she said, fast, before she could stop herself, "will you hide me? Like Ma hid me? Will you hide me better?"

There it was. The exact question, the one I had been dodging my entire life in every form it took. My whole self was built around never being asked to protect anyone — around being so small, so quiet, so *ledger-bound* that no one would ever need me enough to endanger me. Invisibility as safety. Invisibility as virtue, I'd told myself. Careful, careful Freya, who never promised anyone anything.

I looked at her hands on my sleeve. Small, chapped, gripping like she already knew the answer might be no and was going to hold on anyway until it happened.

Here is what nobody tells you about cowardice: it feels exactly like prudence right up until the moment you see it from the outside. And I was seeing it from the outside now — twenty-six years of it, my grandmother's caution worn smooth into mine, the family talent for surviving other people's catastrophes.

"No," I said.

Her face started to fall.

"No hiding," I corrected. "Hiding is what got fourteen people sold to the burnings, because we were all so good at it that nobody knew who to stand beside. I'm not going to hide you, Wren. I'm going to *keep* you. There's a difference. Hiding is something you do alone. Keeping is something we do together."

It was a beautiful speech. I believed maybe sixty percent of it. I had no plan, no resources, no legal existence, a bounty on my head, a companion whose face was on four warrants, and a coat pocket containing one salt key and a growing collection of rumors.

Wren believed it one hundred percent. Children do. That's either their greatest strength or the reason adults owe them everything.

"You promise?" she said. "Truly promise? Because Mister Gray says promises to little ones are the ones that count most, because little ones remember."

The cat looked at me with one yellow eye, and I swear on every ledger I've ever cooked, that animal assessed my soul and found it wanting collateral.

"I promise," I said.

Somewhere above us, through thirty feet of stone and brick, a gull screamed — and I felt it land on the promise like a seal on wax. Wren felt it too. Her eyes went wide, and her grip tightened, and for one moment neither of us said anything, because we both understood that something had just been witnessed.

Then the gull screamed again. Different. Sharper. And this time it carried words, ragged and urgent, tumbling down through the cistern's broken vents:

*Riders. Riders. Coats of the crown. The tanner's boy sold the door.*

* * *

The market came apart the way markets do — quietly first, then all at once. Lamps pinched dead. Stalls folded. The moth-twins' boxes vanished into coats. In the space of ten heartbeats the cistern went from a bazaar to a tomb, everyone flowing toward exits I hadn't known existed, and I stood in the dark holding a child's hand and understanding, with perfect clarity, that I had just made a promise in front of God and a cat, and the bill for it was arriving early.

"Freya." Dain's voice at the curtain, low and hard. "Soldiers. A dozen at least, moving on the arch. And—" a pause, and I heard him choose the words like a man choosing which wound to show first, "—they're not searching randomly. They have a list. Someone's calling names."

From somewhere in the blackness of the market, a voice began to scream — not a soldier's voice, a seller's, a man's voice breaking on the word *please* — and then stopped.

Wren's hand crushed mine. "That's the fog man," she whispered.

Above, the gulls wheeled and shrieked, and beneath their cries I heard the thing I'd been dreading since the marshes: boots on stone, descending, unhurried, certain. And threaded through the boot-beat, faint but unmistakable, a woman's voice giving orders in a tone I recognized from the rats' reports — patient, reasonable, absolutely merciless.

Serah Vane had found the market.

And I had just promised a child I would keep her.
