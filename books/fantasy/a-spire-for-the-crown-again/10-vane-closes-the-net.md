## 10. Vane Closes the Net

The informer smelled of tallow and fear, which in my experience are the two most reliable perfumes of guilt.

"A market," I said, turning his coin purse over in my palm. "Under the tannery district. Gifted folk, buying and selling."

"Names," he whispered. "They trade in names. Hidden names. The kind you can't find on any register."

I weighed the purse. Lighter than it should have been. Everything was, lately—the crown's coffers, my patience, the list of fourteen names that had gone cold the moment Freya Joubert slipped out of Vellum Guild like smoke through a keyhole.

"Here is what will happen," I told him. "You will take me to this market. If it exists as described, you keep the coin and your freedom. If it does not—" I smiled, the way I'd been taught to smile by a woman who once broke a suspect's fingers while complimenting her embroidery. "Well. Tallow burns, and so do liars."

He existed. That was the trouble with markets of stolen things: they always exist, because there is always someone desperate enough to sell and someone desperate enough to buy. Fourteen nights of following tallow-scented men through drainage tunnels had taught me the geography of the city's underside better than any surveyor's map. And on the fifteenth night, standing in a flooded cistern beneath the tanneries while torchlight rippled across black water, I heard it.

Singing.

Not the kind you'd expect. A lullaby, low and cracked, in a voice that wasn't entirely human—a woman with a fox's throat, or a fox with a woman's grief. Around the curve of the cistern, lanterns strung on wet rope lit a cavern of stalls: bottled voices, feathers sold by the handful, a man trading secrets with a rat perched on his shoulder like a small gray confessor.

My hand went to my sword out of habit. My heart went somewhere else entirely—somewhere I didn't permit it to go—and came back chastened.

*Gifts are a plague,* I reminded myself. *A plague needs quarantine.*

But even plagues have faces. Even plagues sing lullabies.

* * *

We took the exits first. That was method; that was discipline. Twelve soldiers, four exits, and me walking down the center of the market with my badge held high, calling out in the flat voice I used for arrests:

"In the name of the Crown, by decree of the Gift-Hunt: no one leaves. No one resists. Compliance is mercy."

I want to record, for whatever ledger eventually judges me, that most of them complied. They raised their hands. They wept quietly. An old man selling bottled birdsong set down his wares and knelt without being asked, and when I passed him he said, "You'll take the girl gently? Promise me you'll be gentle with the little one?"

"The law is gentle," I said, which was a lie, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said anything more.

Then the screaming started—not from the arrested, but from the shadows at the far end of the cistern, where the water went deep. Rats poured out of the walls like spilled ink. Not fleeing. *Charging.* They swarmed the soldiers at the western exit, biting, shrieking in voices that carried words if you bent your ear to them—words like *run* and *warn* and *the little one, get the little one out*—

They were organized. That was the detail I would carry home like a stone in my boot. Animals don't organize. Animals panic. Whatever was happening in this market, it wasn't mere criminality. It was coordination. It was almost—

Almost a covenant.

And then I saw her. The child.

She couldn't have been more than eight years old, barefoot on the wet stone, and she was holding a pigeon against her chest the way other children hold dolls, and the pigeon was holding her back, wings folded around her arms like a mother's shawl. When the soldiers came toward her she didn't scream. She looked up at the biggest of them, a man named Ossler with a wife and three daughters, and said something too quiet to hear.

Ossler stopped. Stood very still. Then turned and walked away from her, back toward the exit, moving like a man sleepwalking.

"What did you say to him?" I demanded, closing the distance.

The child looked at me. Her eyes were the brown of river water, and in them I saw something that made my scarred old certainty shift its weight uncomfortably: not fear. Pity.

"I told him his horse misses him," she said. "He cried about his horse every night in the barracks. She remembers him. She waits at the gate."

"That's a trick," I said.

"It's true," she said. "Those aren't the same thing."

I should have taken her then. Gently or otherwise. Instead I stood there for one breath, two, listening to rats shriek warnings and a fox-throated woman sing and a child tell me truths I had spent nine years forbidding myself to hear—and in that stolen time, everything happened at once.

* * *

Later reconstruction, assembled from twelve soldiers' accounts and my own furious memory, runs thus:

Freya Joubert had been in the market all along. Of course she had. Where else would a hunted speaker go but among her own kind—she who had hidden behind ledgers for thirty years, invisible as ink. She was at the eastern stalls when the raid began, and when the rats charged she moved with them, toward the deep water at the cistern's end, where an old woman kept a shrine of river-glass and, I would learn later, a covenant-key wrapped in oilcloth beneath a stone that no soldier could lift because the stone did not wish to be lifted.

Joubert lifted it.

Witnesses describe the moment strangely—one says the stone *melted*, another says it *opened like a mouth*. What matters is what came after: the water rose. Not violently. Deliberately. A wall of black cistern-water standing between Joubert and my soldiers, and beyond it, the eastern tunnel mouth.

"Seal the east passage!" I shouted, and six men ran to obey, and that is when Dain Corvath made himself known.

I knew his silhouette before I knew his face. Every knight carries his disgrace in the set of his shoulders; his hung there like a second cloak. He came out of the crowd at the eastern tunnel with his sword drawn—not pointed at Joubert, who was wading into the dark with a bundle under her arm, but pointed *backward*. At us. At me.

"Halt," he said, in the parade-ground voice they teach you before they teach you shame. "In the name of—"

"Don't," I warned him. "Don't you dare invoke it."

He dared. Corvath invoked the Crown, the decree, the honor of the order he'd been cast out of, and held the eastern passage alone against six soldiers while the water wall collapsed behind him and a beast-speaker vanished into the tunnels with half the underworld's treasures and, I now knew, a key I hadn't even known to hunt until that night.

It lasted ninety seconds. Maybe less. But I watched a disgraced knight spend the last of his honor buying time for a fugitive, and I watched him do it knowing exactly what it would cost, and there was a moment—when Ossler's blade caught him across the thigh and Corvath dropped to one knee and still did not move aside—when I understood something I have spent every night since trying to un-understand:

He believed what he was saying. All of it. The Crown, the honor, the oath—he'd lost all of those, and here he was, spending the memory of them like a man burning furniture for warmth.

We took him. Of course we took him. Six against one, and one of them already bleeding. But as they bound his wrists he looked past me, past my soldiers, into the dark of the tunnel where Joubert had gone, and he did not look afraid. He looked *relieved*. As if losing everything had finally settled some account he'd been dreading.

"Serah Vane," he said, as they dragged him past. "You're hunting the wrong plague."

"Am I?" I said. "Then enlighten me, Sir Corvath. What should I hunt instead?"

"The ones who taught the beasts to hate us." His smile was blood and irony. "Ask yourself why they hate us. You've never once asked why."

They hauled him away. The market emptied. The rats melted back into the walls, taking their terrible organized silence with them, and I stood in ankle-deep black water among abandoned stalls—bottled voices, jars of feathers, a child's shoe—and felt the hunt change shape around me.

* * *

The child sat where we left her, cross-legged on wet stone, cradling her pigeon. When I approached, she didn't flinch. That frightened me more than screaming would have.

"You're the one hunting everyone," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because gifts break the world's fairness. Because a person who can command a raven has power over everyone who can't. Because—" I heard my own voice fraying and hated it. "Because I've seen what gifted people do."

"To whom?" she asked, with the dreadful simplicity of children. "Or to whom were they done?"

I crouched to her level. Water soaked through my trouser knee. Somewhere above us, the city went about its lawful business, ignorant of the drowned church beneath its streets.

"You'll come with me," I said. "You'll be registered. You'll be fed and housed and protected, and in exchange you'll tell me everything you know about Freya Joubert and the keys she's gathering. Do you understand?"

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate.

"She's going to fix it," the child said. "The broken thing. Grandmother Wolf says so. The covenant, the silence, all of it. She's going to give the beasts back their voices, all the way, everywhere." She looked up at me. "Is that why you're scared? Not of her. Of what happens when the animals can tell everyone what we've done."

I stood. I gave the order to move her to custody—gently, I specified, and hated how much gentleness suddenly mattered—and I walked out of that cistern through the eastern tunnel, alone, my boots splashing in the dark, because I could not bear to ride in the wagon with the child whose eyes saw straight through nine years of my certainty.

Corvath's question followed me like a rat along a wall.

*Why do they hate us?*

I knew the answer. Of course I knew it. I'd built my whole life on the answer: because they're wicked, because power corrupts, because a girl with ravens—because a girl with ravens—

Because a girl with ravens.

I stopped in the tunnel. The torches had burned low. In the dark I let myself remember it fully, for the first time in years, without the official version softening the edges: the ravens coming down out of a red sky in perfect formation. Not scattering, not panicking. *Flying in formation.* Like cavalry. Like soldiers under orders. I had wondered, even then, even at eleven years old, crouched under my father's body—wondered and been punished for wondering—who teaches ravens to fly in formation?

I had buried the question. Buried it under doctrine, under decree, under a decade of arrests. Because if the ravens were commanded, then someone commanded them, and if someone commanded them, then the evil that killed my family wore a human face—and the crown I served might have chosen not to look for that face, because looking would cost powerful men their comforts.

No. No, that way lies madness. The gifted are dangerous. The gifted must be registered, controlled, contained. That is justice. That has always been justice.

Hasn't it?

* * *

At dawn I stood over the inventory of the raid: forty-three souls taken, one key recovered—no. One key *lost*, taken by Joubert into the dark. Forty-three prisoners, including a child who spoke to horses' hearts and a knight who had chosen treason with his eyes open.

Ossler found me at the tannery door, cradling his sword arm where a rat had bitten through his gauntlet.

"Inquisitor," he said. "The prisoner Corvath asks to speak with you. Alone. Says he has a bargain worth hearing."

"Bargains are for the guilty."

"He also said," Ossler hesitated, and I saw that the big man's hands were shaking, and that he kept glancing toward the stables where his mare waited, "he said you'd want to know that the girl told him something before we took her. About your family."

The morning went very quiet.

"She doesn't know anything about my family," I said.

"No, Inquisitor." Ossler wouldn't meet my eyes. "She said—she said the ravens remember. She said they've been waiting nine years for someone to ask them who sent them. She said..." He swallowed. "She said you already know the answer. You've just never let yourself finish asking the question."

I walked to the dungeon alone. It seemed important, suddenly, to walk—to feel the stones under my boots, the weight of my badge on my chest, the ordinary honest weight of a lawful life.

Dain Corvath sat in his cell like a man waiting for a coach he'd scheduled years ago. Through the bars, he studied me with the weary patience of someone who has already lost everything except the truth, and knows the truth is the only weapon left worth wielding.

"Well," he said. "Shall we talk about who really killed your family, Inquisitor?"

Above us, gulls wheeled over the prison roof, crying out—and for the first time in my life, I could swear their cries sounded less like birdsong and more like testimony waiting to be given.
