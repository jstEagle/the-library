## 11. The Prisoner's Bargain

They took my boots. That's the part that galled, if I'm honest. Not the cell, not the irons, not the way Serah Vane's soldiers marched me through the market square with my hands bound like a common cutpurse. The boots. Twelve years of knighthood, a disgrace, a desertion, and a cell, and it was the boots that made me feel properly ruined.

The cell itself was generous, as cells go. Stone floor, straw that had seen better decades, a slot in the door through which they passed me bread that could have been used to mortar a wall. A single high window let in a blade of afternoon light that crawled across the floor like something dying slowly. I'd been counting its progress to keep from counting worse things.

I had a lot of worse things to count.

The child, for one. The girl from the market, the little speaker with Freya's gift sitting plain and unhidden in her face, dragged away by Vane's soldiers while I stood in the street and let it happen. I told myself it was arithmetic: one child in a warm cell beats one child dead in a market square, and Freya escaped, and Freya was the one who mattered. I told myself that about six hundred times. It had not improved with repetition.

Freya, for another. Freya, who had watched me get arrested covering her retreat, who had no reason to believe anything about me except that I'd followed her for weeks, lied to her about why, and was now in the crown's custody wearing the crown's colors, which I had never actually stopped wearing, if you wanted to be technical about it. I did not want to be technical about it. Technical was how I'd ended up here.

And the third thing, the thing I kept circling like a dog around a wound: I didn't know anymore whose side I was on. That's a strange thing to admit even to yourself. I'd spent my whole life serving masters—the Order, the Crown, the code, the oath, the shame of having broken all three—and I'd always known exactly whom I was failing. Now I had the novel experience of not knowing whom I was *serving*, and it turned out that was worse.

The light blade had crawled to the third flagstone from the wall when the boots came back.

Not my boots. Hers. Serah Vane walked the corridor outside my cell in those hard-heeled riding boots that announced her the way a war drum announces a battle, and she stopped in front of my door and looked at me through the bars with those gray eyes that never blinked quite when a person's eyes should.

"Corvath," she said.

"Inquisitor." I got up off the straw with as much dignity as a man can manage in irons and straw, which is none, and decided to own it. "If you've come about the boots, I want to file a complaint."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement, or it might have been indigestion. With Vane you learned to take your entertainment where you found it.

"You've been here four days," she said. "You haven't asked for a lawyer. You haven't asked for a priest. You haven't asked for anything." She turned the key in the lock herself—no gaoler, no witnesses, which told me this conversation was going to be one of those conversations. "A man who asks for nothing either has nothing to lose, or he's waiting for something. Which are you?"

"Both, probably." I sat back down on the bench, because standing felt like conceding her the room. "You could have sent a subordinate. You came yourself. So you want something from me that a subordinate can't get."

The door swung shut behind her. She didn't sit. Inquisitors never sit; sitting is surrender of the vertical advantage, and the vertical advantage is half their arsenal.

"You were seen," she said. "In the market. Before the raid. Talking to the woman. Not chasing her. *Talking*."

"Interrogating."

"For how long?"

"An hour, maybe."

"An hour." She let the word hang there, the way she let everything hang, like a man she'd hanged. "In an hour of interrogation, Corvath, a trained knight does not learn the fugitive's destination. He learns her name, her associates, her habits. He does not stand in a doorway while she escapes through the back of a burning market, and he does not put his body between my soldiers and her retreat."

"I put my body between your soldiers and a *child*," I said. "The woman I simply failed to catch. There's a difference. Ask anyone I've ever failed to catch."

"You were seen putting your body between my soldiers and both."

Ah. Well. There it was, then. I'd wondered how much they'd seen, in all that smoke and screaming. Apparently: everything.

"Here's what I think," Vane said, and she began to walk, slow, the way she did, circling the small cell the way a hawk circles a field. "I think you found her in the marshes months ago. I think you had her, and you let her go, and your mare had something to do with it—I've heard the story, Corvath, the beast-touched mare that wouldn't obey her own rider. I think you followed her after that, and somewhere in the Thornwood you stopped following and started *accompanying*. And I think that when the moment came, in that market, you chose."

"Chose what?"

"That's the question." She stopped. She was close now, close enough that I could see the fine scarring at her hairline that I'd never noticed before, and the tiredness underneath the gray eyes that she probably thought she'd buried deeper than that. "So I'm going to offer you a chance to choose again. Deliberately. On the record. Because I believe you're a man who wants to choose correctly and simply doesn't know how anymore. That, I can work with."

I should tell you what I know about Serah Vane, since I was about to spend the most consequential hour of my life negotiating with her. She is not a sadist. I want that understood. Sadists are easy; sadists want you to hurt, and you can give a sadist your pain like a coin and walk away with your soul intact. Vane is worse. Vane is a *believer*. She thinks the gifted are a rot in the law, that a realm where some people can whisper to beasts and others cannot is a realm built on a lie, and lies, in her cosmology, are the only unforgivable sin. She burned a woman in Corleigh last spring and slept well afterward, I'd stake my pension on it, if I still had a pension.

She also, I happened to know, had a dead family and a raven-shaped hole where her mercy used to be. Freya had told me. Freya, who collected secrets the way other people collected debts.

"I'm listening," I said, which was a lie, because I'd been listening since she unlocked the door, and she knew it, and I knew she knew it, and that was how negotiations with Serah Vane always went: two liars politely pretending the other one was sincere.

"The girl," Vane said.

The word landed in my chest like a dropped stone. "What about her?"

"She hasn't spoken since the raid. Not a word. Not to my clerks, not to the priest, not to anyone. She sits in her cell and she *listens*, Corvath. To the walls. To the rats in them. My gaolers say the rats have stopped coming up on her block." Vane's jaw tightened, just a fraction. "I know what that means. I'm not a fool. I know what she is."

"And?"

"And I have a cell for her in the Register Halls. Warm, dry, fed. And I have, if it comes to that, a pyre. The law gives me both, and I will use whichever the situation requires." She said it flatly, without relish, which somehow made it worse. "You can prevent one and not the other. That's the bargain I've come to offer."

I made myself breathe slowly. In through the nose. Out through the nose. The way you breathe when the alternative is letting a man see your hands shake.

"Say it plainly," I said.

"You lead me to the Joubert woman. To the Spire—I know that's where she's bound; everything she's done since the Thornwood points there like an arrow. You lead me to her, she surrenders, she registers, she stands trial in the open like a citizen instead of dying in a ditch like vermin. And the girl goes free. Unregistered. Unmarked. I'll strike her from my own lists with my own hand. No one will ever look for her again." Vane folded her hands. "That's the offer. It's the only one I'll make, and it expires when I walk out that door."

I want to be honest, since honesty is the whole theme of what I did next, however badly I executed it: for about four full seconds, I wanted to say yes.

Not because I'd betrayed Freya in my heart. Because the arithmetic was *beautiful*. One woman, grown, capable, extraordinary, trades her freedom for a child's whole life. Freya herself would do it. Freya, who promised a market full of terrified strangers protection she couldn't give, who burned her own page out of a ledger rather than let it be used, would trade herself for that girl without blinking, and we'd all walk away with our souls more or less accounted for. Vane gets her lawful trial. The girl goes free. I stop being a man who chose in a burning market and become a man who chose *deliberately*, on the record, correctly.

Four seconds. Then I looked at Vane's hands, folded so neatly, and I remembered that Freya surrendering meant Freya in the Register Halls, and the Register Halls were where the crown kept its gifted before trial, and the crown's prisons had a way of losing people between the cell and the courtroom. And I remembered that Vane said *surrenders*, not *survives*, and that the law gave her both a cell and a pyre and let her pick, and that a woman who could sleep after Corleigh would not lose much sleep over a technicality.

And I remembered, finally, the thing I'd been not-thinking for four days in the straw: that I didn't want to deliver Freya to justice. I wanted to deliver her to the Spire. Whatever she meant to do there—reforge some broken covenant, mend the world, I still only half understood it—I wanted her to *do it*. Which meant I had stopped serving the crown somewhere in the Thornwood and hadn't had the decency to inform myself.

"Corvath," Vane said, into my silence. "You're doing arithmetic. I can hear it."

"Occupational habit." I looked up at her. "Here's my counter."

"There is no counter."

"There's always a counter. You came to a prisoner's cell alone, without witnesses, without a clerk. You don't want this offer on the record, Inquisitor. Which means you're not as sure of your law as you pretend, or not as sure of your superiors, and either way, you need me more than you're saying." I let that sit. Her eyes narrowed a hair's width, which from Vane was a shout. "So. I'll take you to her. On my word as—"

I stopped. That was the trouble with oaths; you collect them your whole life and then one day you go to spend one and find the purse empty.

"On my word as a man who's tired of lying," I finished. "I'll take you to her. To the Spire. And the girl walks out of this dungeon today, before I take a single step, free and clear, off your lists, out of the city if she wants, with coin for the road. Not after. Before. You'll forgive me for not trusting the crown's follow-through."

"The girl is a flight risk. She's a *speaker*. Unregistered—"

"Then put a guard on her road as far as the border, if you must. But she walks out today, or I rot in this cell and you explain to your superiors why you came to me and left with nothing."

We looked at each other for a long moment. Somewhere above us, water dripped through the dungeon's old bones, patient as a clock. Vane's face did the thing I'd seen it do in the market square before the raid: the thing where the certainty and the tiredness fought behind her eyes and certainty won, but by less than yesterday.

"Today," she said at last. "Before dark. She'll be escorted to the east gate and released. My personal seal on the release order, so no one can countermand it." She moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the lock. "You understand that if this is a trick, Corvath, if you're playing me—if you lead me into some ambush of wolves and witchcraft—I will burn the girl anyway, and I will burn you first, and I will not lose a night's sleep."

"I know," I said. And the worst part, the part I'll carry, is that I believed her completely.

* * *

They gave me back my boots for the road, which I chose to interpret as a good omen, and a horse, which I chose not to interpret at all. The girl's release went through an hour before sundown. I watched from the courtyard as they led her out the east gate—a small figure in a borrowed cloak, escorted by two of Vane's own, walking stiff and strange, the way a child walks when she's been listening to walls for a week.

She stopped at the gate. She turned around. And across the whole width of that courtyard, over the heads of soldiers and clerks and cart-horses, she looked directly at me, and she raised one small hand, and—this is the part I still can't explain, and I've stopped trying—every horse in the courtyard turned its head toward me at once.

Twenty horses. All at once. Like a congregation.

Then the gate closed behind her, and the horses went back to their hay, and I stood there in the cold with my bargain struck and my soul in approximately the condition you'd expect.

Here is what nobody in that dungeon knew, what Vane didn't know and my new brothers-in-arms didn't know and, four days ago, I hadn't fully known either: I did intend to lead Serah Vane to the Spire. Every word of my word was true. I just hadn't told her *when*.

Because the girl had told the beasts everything. I'd known it since the courtyard, since the horses, since that look—she'd heard the bargain through her walls, every word of it, the way she heard everything, and she had sent it out into the world on rat-feet and raven-wings to whoever was listening. Which meant Freya already knew I was coming, and who was coming with me, and how many swords.

Which meant the only question left—the question I'd be turning over on every mile of that mountain road, with Vane riding at my shoulder and my old oath rattling in my chest like a coin in an empty purse—was whether I was leading the Inquisitor into a trap, or leading Freya into the worst surprise of her life, and whether, when the moment came on that mountain, I would finally know which master I served.

The east gate opened. The road went up, toward the mountains, toward the Spire, toward whatever I was about to become.

I rode.
