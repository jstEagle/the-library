## 13. Two Traitors at the Treeline

### Freya

The wolves had stopped singing at dawn, and I should have paid more attention to that.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is apparently the theme of my life now: I noticed. I noticed the way the Thornwood went quiet in that particular register—not the silence of empty forest but the silence of held breath, the kind a room makes when someone is standing behind the door. The wolves had left me at the treeline an hour before sunrise, the grey she-wolf pressing her muzzle against my palm one last time as if to say *this far, no farther*, and I had stood there in the cold watching the Spire's roots break the earth ahead of me like the ribs of something buried alive, and I had thought: *the silence is because we're close. The silence is reverence.*

That's the trouble with wanting a thing too much. You start interpreting every warning as a blessing.

The stone guardian was supposed to be here. That was what the journal said—*the third key sleeps at the root, in the keeping of the warden who remembers*—and Grandmother's handwriting had gone strange on that page, pressed hard enough to tear, as if she'd been angry or afraid or both. But there was no guardian. There was only a hollow between two vast roots, moss-cushioned, sheltered from the wind, and a flat stone that might have been an altar or might have been nothing at all.

And there was Dain.

He was sitting against the root with his sword across his knees like a man who'd been waiting a long time and had opinions about it. His armor was gone—he'd been arrested in it, last I'd seen him, dragged out of the burning market with three soldiers on his back and me running the other way with a covenant-key burning a hole in my coat like a coal I couldn't put down. Now he wore a soldier's drab wool, and there was a cut over his eyebrow that someone had stitched badly, and when he saw me he didn't stand up.

"You look terrible," I said.

"You should see the other cells." He rose anyway, slow, favoring his left side. "Freya—"

"How did you find me?"

"The wolves let me." He said it flatly, the way a man says a thing he still doesn't believe. "Walked three days through that forest and not one beast touched me. Horses won't carry my old company anymore, did you know that? They plant their feet and scream. The whole realm's animals have gone on strike and somehow I'm the only one who finds it reasonable."

"Dain." My hand was on the key inside my coat. Two keys now, the salt-white one and the market one, and they hummed against each other through the leather like two tuning forks that couldn't decide on a note. "Why are you here? Truly."

Something crossed his face. I've watched men lie for a living—the Vellum Guild kept several on retainer—and I know all the tells. Dain skipped every one of them. He just looked tired, and looked at me, and said:

"Vane offered me a bargain. The child goes free if I lead her to you."

The world went very simple and very cold, the way it does right before something breaks.

"And you took it."

"I took it." He didn't flinch. I'll give him that; he never flinched, even now. "Tamsin walks free tonight. Out of the capital, out of Vane's ledgers, with an escort I paid for with the only coin I had left." His jaw worked. "I told myself I'd lead them into the trees and lose them. I told myself I'd warn you first. I told myself a great many things, and then I put on their uniform again, and you want to know the worst part? It fit."

I should have run then. The wolves were gone, the guardian hadn't come, and the man I'd trusted with my life was telling me—in his backward, honorable, maddening way—that he'd sold me to save a child. And the truly stupid part, the part I keep turning over even now, is that I understood. Tamsin was seven years old and spoke rat, and the rats adored her, and I had promised her protection in a burning market while ash fell like black snow. If Dain had asked me to trade myself for her, I'd have done it before he finished the sentence.

So instead of running, I did the worst possible thing. I hesitated.

"Freya," Dain said, and his voice cracked down the middle, "they're already here."

* * *

### Dain

There are perhaps four honest moments in a man's life, and I have come to believe you don't recognize any of them until afterward.

Mine came on a mountainside at first light, standing in a traitor's uniform with thirty crown soldiers fanned out behind me in the treeline, watching Freya Joubert's face do arithmetic she couldn't finish. Betrayal, hope, betrayal again. I had rehearsed a dozen speeches on the march north and used none of them, because the truth was shorter than any lie: I had traded her for a child, and I meant to unmake the trade, and I had no plan for how, and the soldiers behind me had orders to take her alive but Serah Vane had that particular serenity of a woman whose orders have a wide margin of interpretation.

"Signal," Lieutenant Marsh had told me at the treeline. "Raise your sword arm when you've made contact. Standard capture protocol."

Standard capture protocol. As if there were protocols for capturing someone who talks to wolves. As if anything about the last month of my life had been standard.

Here is what I actually did: I raised my arm—and I made it a warning. A knight's salute, arm straight, blade vertical, the gesture they drilled into us at twelve years old until it lived in the shoulder bone. Every soldier behind me read it as *target confirmed*. Any knight would have read it as *stand and be counted*, which was the traditional salute given to an enemy worth honoring.

It wasn't enough. I knew it wasn't enough even as I did it. You cannot fit a whole confession into a gesture, and I had spent three weeks learning that lesson in a cell.

"Freya," I said. "Run."

She ran. Of course she ran; she'd been running since the night she burned her own name out of a ledger, and she ran like it, low and clever, angling for the roots where horses couldn't follow. The treeline broke apart into shouting and motion, soldiers crashing downslope through the bracken, and somewhere behind them all, riding at the rear like a woman attending a funeral she'd personally arranged, came Serah Vane in her grey traveling cloak with her ledger under her arm.

I did the only thing left in my inventory. I drew my sword and turned around.

Marsh saw it happen. I watched his face go through the same arithmetic Freya's had—betrayal, disbelief, betrayal confirmed—and then his hand went to his own hilt and the morning got very loud very fast.

"Corvath," he said. "You're making a mistake."

"I've made several," I agreed. "This isn't one."

Three of them came at me at once, which was flattering; Marsh had been at Harrow Ford with me, back when I still had a name, and he knew better than to send fewer. I gave ground downhill, giving them my left side where the ribs weren't cracked, and I will confess here what I confessed to no one: some part of me was grateful. This was the one problem in my life with a clean shape. Stand on a stair—or a slope, close enough—hold the line, refuse to move. No ledgers, no bargains, no impossible arithmetic of debts. Just steel and the brothers I'd betrayed and the woman behind me running for the roots, and if I bought her ten minutes it would be the first ten minutes of my life I hadn't owed to anybody.

Then something screamed overhead, and it wasn't a bird, exactly. It was the memory of a bird, the way a nightmare is the memory of a fire.

Ravens. Dozens of them, coming off the crags above the Spire in a black river, and they didn't attack—they *spoke*. All at once, in voices like gravel poured through a crack in the world, and every horse in the crown column reared at the sound, and every soldier heard the same words, because the ravens made sure of it:

*SHE IS THE ANCHOR. SHE IS THE DEBT. BRING HER TO THE ROOT AND NO FARTHER.*

And then, threading beneath the ravens' thunder, small and clear and seven years old, a voice I knew:

*Tammy told us everything! Tammy told the crows and the crows told everyone! Mister Dain, don't let them hurt her, the mountain's been waiting so long!*

A child. A child I had ransomed Freya for, free and safe and apparently leagues away, shouting through the mouths of birds across half a mountain. The bargain had worked. Both halves of it. I stood there with my sword up and my ribs screaming and understood, with the peculiar clarity of a man being hit, that I had been the only person on this mountain playing checkers.

"Hold!" Vane's voice cut through everything, thin and iron. "Hold the line! It's trickery—it's witch-voice—"

But the horses had stopped listening. That was the thing nobody had planned for, the flaw in the whole machinery of the hunt: you can order a man to do anything, but a horse has always had opinions, and somewhere between the market raid and this mountainside, the horses' opinions had changed.

Freya reached the roots. I saw it between the bodies and the wheeling birds—saw her drop to her knees at the flat stone, both hands pressed to it, head bowed, hair whipping in a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else on the mountain.

And Serah Vane, dismounted, unhorsed, furious, walked past me without a glance and started up the slope toward her.

I caught Vane's shoulder. She turned, and for one moment the inquisitor and the disgraced knight looked at each other across twenty years of shared oaths, and I said the truest thing I knew how to say:

"Serah. Listen to them. For once in your life, just *listen*."

Her eyes went to the ravens circling the roots—to the child's voice still spilling out of them, bright and fearless—and something moved behind her face. Something old. Something with dead ravens in it.

She struck my hand away and climbed on.

* * *

### Freya

The stone was warm. That was the first impossible thing: frost on everything, breath hanging white, and under my palms the flat rock warm as a sleeping animal, beating faintly, once every few seconds. Not my pulse. Its own.

*I'm here,* I told it, the way I'd told the hawk in the guild loft, the way I'd told the wolves. *I know what my grandmother did. I know why you'd hate me. I came anyway.*

The stone beat faster. And beneath my knees I felt it—the whole root system shivering, the buried ribs of the Spire shifting like something enormous rolling over in its sleep, and a pressure built against my mind, vast and slow and patient, like an ocean deciding whether to notice a swimmer.

*A JOUBERT.* The voice didn't come through my ears. It came up through the ground, through my bones, older than any wolf. *THE LAST ONE STOOD HERE AND LIED.*

"I know," I said aloud, and my voice shook, and I didn't try to hide it. "She confessed everything in her journal. She helped break the covenant and she spent forty years being ashamed and not one single day trying to fix it. I've read every word." I swallowed. "I'm not her."

*NO,* said the mountain, and I felt something ancient turn its attention fully upon me, and it was like being looked at by the sky. *YOU ARE WHAT COMES AFTER HER. SHOW ME YOUR HANDS.*

Behind me the world was chaos—steel ringing on steel, Vane's voice cracking orders the horses ignored, ravens wheeling and chanting, and threaded through all of it, impossibly, a child laughing. Tamsin. Alive, free, telling the beasts everything, and the beasts telling each other, and the news arriving here ahead of me like a letter posted by the wind itself. Dain's bargain had bought more than either of us knew. He'd freed a speaker, and a speaker freed is a bell that cannot be unrung.

*SHOW ME YOUR HANDS,* the mountain said again.

I pulled off my gloves—guild-issue calfskin, the gloves I'd worn for ten years so no one would ever see me touch an animal and know—and laid my bare hands on the warm stone, and I opened every door in myself at once. The hawk. The rats. The wolves. The child in the market whose gift mirrored mine. Grandmother's crime, spoken aloud to a witness older than kingdoms.

The stone began to glow.

Not softly. Not gently. It lit from within like a forge, like dawn compressed, and through the roar of it I felt the third key wake—a hook of light turning over somewhere deep in the root, feeling for the lock it had waited centuries to open—and the mountain's voice rolled up through my bones one final time:

*THEN WE BEGIN. BUT FIRST, LITTLE SPEAKER—TURN AROUND. YOU ARE NOT ALONE AT THIS ALTAR.*

I turned.

Serah Vane stood at the edge of the light, grey cloak snapping, her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, her face lit orange and gold and utterly stripped of certainty. Her soldiers were scattered and unhorsed behind her. Dain was down on one knee, bleeding, sword still somehow in his fist. And the ravens had gone silent—all of them, the whole sky of them—perched in rows along the great roots like a congregation waiting for a service to begin.

Vane looked at the burning stone. At my hands on it. At me.

"Joubert," she said, and her voice was not the inquisitor's voice at all. It was smaller. Younger. "What is happening to this mountain?"

I could have lied. Gods know I'd had practice.

"It's waking up," I said. "And I think—I think it wants to talk to you."

Her knuckles went white around the ledger. Behind her, Dain forced himself upright, and every raven on every root leaned forward, and the third key turned another notch in the dark below, and the whole world held its breath—

—and Serah Vane, hunter of the gifted, burner of names, took one step closer to the light.
