## 14. Trial by Crown

They washed the marsh out of my hair with rosewater, which I considered a personal insult. The mud had been mine. Earned. And now here I stood in the Hall of Assizes in a borrowed grey dress that itched like a penance, my hands scrubbed pink, my gift locked behind iron cuffs etched with sigils I could not read but which every rat in the building assured me were *very rude*.

That was the first thing they never understand about people like me. The chains don't silence us. They just give us something to complain about.

*Iron tastes of old cruelty,* said a mouse under the judges' bench, in the tone of one who has chewed on worse. *But there's bread behind the third pillar, if you can believe it. Judges. Hoarding bread.*

Focus, I told her, and myself.

The Hall of Assizes was built to make you feel small, and it succeeded the way cathedrals do—through arithmetic. Pillars thick as oaks. A ceiling so far up that the banners hanging from it seemed to float in weather. Three hundred seats filled with merchants and magistrates and the particular species of courtier who attends executions hoping for a fainting spell to gossip about. Above them all, on a dais of black stone, sat the Lord Chancellor and six justices in white wigs, and beside them, upright as a drawn blade, Serah Vane.

She would not look at me. That frightened me more than if she had.

* * *

I had been given four days in a cell to consider my situation, which is three days more than most prisoners get and roughly four fewer than I needed. In that time I learned several things from the mice in the walls, who are the finest intelligence network in the capital precisely because nobody has ever once thought to worry about them.

I learned that Dain Corvath was alive—he had been seen being marched through the courtyard in irons, jaw set, refusing the escorting sergeant's offer of a chair. Of course he refused the chair. Dain would refuse a chair at his own beheading on principle.

I learned that the child—the girl from the market, Pip, whose gift mirrored mine—had been released. Vane had kept her word. I turned that fact over for hours, because it didn't fit. Zealots don't keep bargains. Unless the bargain had cost Vane something, or unless she intended to collect later at interest.

And I learned the shape of tomorrow: I would be offered pardon. Everyone knew it. The Chancellor wanted this tidy—a public confession, a list of names, a pardon signed with theatrical mercy. Fourteen names had already been bought from Guildmaster Ossen. Mine was the fifteenth, the prize, the one they'd chased across half the realm to get. All I had to do was stand on the marble and sell the other speakers the way Ossen had sold me.

The mouse behind the pillar asked me if I wanted the bread.

"No," I said aloud, and then remembered I wasn't supposed to answer aloud, alone in a cell, and felt briefly mad. "No thank you."

*Suit yourself,* she said. *More for me. What will you do?*

I looked at my cuffed hands. Somewhere under my skin, faint as a pulse, I could feel them—not just the mice but everything. The ravens on the courthouse roof arguing about a dead pigeon. The horses in the stables across the square dreaming their long, anxious dreams. Even, impossibly far off, a whale somewhere in the grey northern water singing a note that took a full minute to finish, and when it finished, it began again.

My grandmother had helped break the covenant that once made all of that lawful and open and shared. Her journal lay hidden in the lining of my coat, which the guards had confiscated and catalogued and, thank every patient god, not searched thoroughly enough. It was waiting for me even now, folded small against my heart, because a nervous clerk had returned my "personal effects" and I had palmed the pages the way you palm anything from a man who isn't looking at you but at your wrists.

"What will you do?" the mouse asked again.

"Something stupid," I said.

*Good,* she said. *The clever ones always die bored.*

* * *

They brought me up at the ninth bell.

The hall went quiet in that specific way crowds go quiet when they've paid to see something. I walked the length of it on legs that behaved themselves out of sheer spite, past the dock, past the bar, to a low stool set in the center of the floor—no, not a stool, I realized as I neared it. A platform. Barely a hand's height off the ground, just enough to lift me onto display. Just enough that everyone could see the moment I broke.

The Lord Chancellor, Aldous Wren, was a soft-spoken man with the face of a disappointed librarian, which is the most dangerous face in politics. He let the silence ripen. Then:

"Freya Joubert. You stand accused of the practice of beast-speech, an abomination under the Edict of Unnatural Gifts. You stand accused of flight from lawful authority. You stand accused of—" he consulted a paper without any appearance of needing to, "—the corruption of a sworn knight of the crown."

"Dain Corvath corrupted himself," I said. "It's called conscience. You may have read about it."

A ripple through the gallery. Someone laughed and strangled it. On the dais, Vane's eyes finally came up and found mine, and what was in them was not hatred. It was worse. It was *hope*, tightly leashed, the look of a woman who had built her whole life on one load-bearing truth and desperately needed me not to pull it out.

"The crown," Chancellor Wren continued smoothly, "is prepared to be generous."

There it was.

He spoke of mercy the way merchants speak of discount prices—as though generosity were a limited inventory I'd be foolish to pass over. Pardon, full and free, sealed with the royal signet. No registration. No pyre. My name scrubbed from the lists.

All I had to do was speak. Name the gifted. The ones in the market. The ones hiding in cellars and hedge-farms and the spaces between parishes. Give the crown its map, and walk away clean.

He made it sound reasonable. That was the horror of him. Standing there, I understood how fourteen people had ended up sold by a guildmaster who probably wept while he did it. Reasonableness is the sharpest blade in the room.

I looked at Serah Vane.

"You lost family," I said.

The hall stiffened. Wren's eyebrow climbed. Vane went very still, the stillness of a held breath.

"Ravens," I said. "Coerced, the stories say. Driven mad by a speaker and turned on your household. I'm told you keep their feathers pressed in a book." I hadn't been told; the harbor gulls had gossiped, and gulls are terrible gossips, but it was true enough. "I am sorry. I have been sorry since I learned it, and I'll be sorry after today, whatever happens. But I won't buy my life with other people's names, and I won't pay it into a lie."

"Lie?" Wren's voice stayed gentle. Everything about him stayed gentle. "Child, the law is not a lie."

"The law says gifts are a plague," I said. "The law says beasts are dumb. The law says the crown stands between civilization and savagery, and that anyone who hears a hawk think is one step from putting knives in its talons." I turned, slowly, letting my voice carry to the rafters, to the galleries, to the three hundred faces leaning in like plants toward sun. "Your whole edict rests on a story. So let me tell you a different one. A true one. With dates."

Wren's smile thinned by a degree. "You are not here to give testimony about history."

"I'm here because you want names," I said. "Fine. Here is one. Joubert."

Silence. Even the mice went quiet.

"My grandmother. Maren Joubert. Founder of the Vellum Guild's trading charter, friend of chancellors, donor of windows to three separate cathedrals." I reached into my bodice where a folded page waited, because I'd smuggled it separately from the journal, because I am paranoid in ways that save lives. "Sixty years ago, she stood in a grove outside Thornwood with two men whose descendants still sit in this building—I have their names too, written in her own hand—and together they broke the Old Covenant. The pact between human speech and beast speech. The one that made the realms one country instead of two armed camps wearing the same skin."

"You cannot possibly expect this court to—"

"They broke it," I said over him, louder, "to buy the guild its privileges. To make trade cheap and secrets cheap and gifted people *controllable*. Because a covenant freely kept means beasts choose us, Chancellor, and chosen things can't be owned. Your grandfather's generation couldn't abide that. So they shattered it, and the shards became your law, and the silence became your order, and every speaker born since has been born into the wreckage of a crime committed by the very families writing your edicts
