## 15. The Riot of Small Voices

### Freya

They dressed me for my execution in clean linen, which I thought was a nice touch. Nothing says *we are a just crown* like fresh laundry.

The Hall of Audiences had been built to make visitors feel small. It worked on me even knowing what I now knew—that its marble was quarried by men who'd broken a covenant older than the walls, that the banners overhead were dyed with privileges bought by my grandmother's silence. The vaulted ceiling arched so far up that the crows nesting in the rafters looked like flecks of ash. I tried not to look at them. Looking felt like asking.

They'd chained my wrists in iron filigree, decorative, because apparently even restraints should be fashionable in the capital. A herald with a voice like a landslide announced me: *"Freya Joubert, of the Vellum Guild, charged under the Edict of Registration—beast-speaking, concealment of gift, and sedition against the Crown's lawful hunt."*

Sedition. That was new. Apparently fleeing men who wanted to burn you counted as treason now. Good to have the paperwork sorted.

The gallery was full. That was the part that got under my skin—not the inquisitor's dais, not the rack of instruments along the eastern wall (ornamental today, they wanted the city to see mercy), but the faces. Merchants I'd never traded with. Bakers. A cluster of guild clerks from houses I'd competed against for years. All of them come to watch a woman be unmade.

I found one face I hadn't expected: Dain Corvath, standing between two guards near the western arch, wrists bound too, his knight's coat stripped of insignia. They'd brought him to testify, presumably. To point at me and say *she's the one.* Our eyes met for half a second. He gave me nothing—no apology, no promise. Just that stubborn flat stare of his, the one that said *I'm still deciding whose side I'm on, and so are you.*

Fair enough. Some mornings I wasn't sure either.

"Freya Joubert." Inquisitor Serah Vane sat above the court in grey, her badge of office polished to a shine you could signal ships with. Her voice was quiet, which was somehow worse than shouting. "You are offered the Crown's pardon. One condition. You will name every beast-speaker known to you—their locations, their families, their children. Do this, and you walk out of this hall a registered asset of the realm. Refuse—" she let the pause do its work, "—and the fire is not ornamental."

There she was. The woman who'd bought my name off my own guildmaster for less than he'd have asked for a bottled storm. She didn't look cruel. She looked *certain*, and certainty has always frightened me more than cruelty. Cruelty can be argued with.

I stood as straight as the chains allowed.

"I have a counteroffer," I said.

A murmur went through the gallery. Somewhere a clerk dropped his quill. Honestly, the acoustics in there were wasted on proclamations.

"I will name every speaker I know," I said. "Every single one. But first, the court will hear how their names came to be worth selling. The court will hear a confession—mine, and my grandmother's, and the Vellum Guild's, and if we're being thorough, the Crown's."

Vane's jaw tightened by a degree measurable only to people who'd spent years reading ledgers. "The court did not ask for history."

"The court asked for names," I said. "Names are history. Every name I know is a name someone tried to erase. So here is mine, spoken openly, as your new law supposedly prefers: I am a beast-speaker. My grandmother was one before me. And three generations ago, the Jouberts helped break the covenant between the realms of man and beast—to buy our guild its trading privileges. Bottled storms. Borrowed shadows. Everything in those ledgers was bought with a silence we had no right to sell."

The hall went very quiet. Not respectful-quiet. The quiet of several hundred people simultaneously realizing the entertainment had changed genres.

"You confess to crimes of inheritance," Vane said slowly. "You expect that to move me?"

"No," I said. "I expect it to move *them*." I nodded at the gallery. "Because half the great houses in this city built their fortunes on the same silence, and the other half are wondering why their dogs won't look them in the eye anymore."

And that—gods forgive me—is when the ceiling answered.

### Serah

Serah Vane had presided over forty-one registrations and nine burnings, and she had learned to read a courtroom the way other women read weather. This one was about to break.

It began with sound she almost dismissed: a scratching in the rafters, rhythmic, deliberate. Then a pigeon on the windowsill began to coo—but wrong, arrhythmic, like speech trying on a bird's throat. Then a dog in the courtyard below began to bark, and the bark stretched, warped, and resolved into something with syllables in it.

*"HEAR US."*

Serah's hand went to her badge out of pure reflex, as if tin could ward off language.

The crows came down off the rafters all at once, dozens of them, settling along the balustrades and the judge's bench and the hilt of the ceremonial sword mounted on the wall, and every one of them opened its beak and spoke with one voice—a voice like gravel poured over embers.

*"We were promised. We were promised, and you sold the promise, and you fed on the silence, and now you will hear what the silence was hiding."*

"Clear the hall!" Serah was on her feet. "Seal the doors! This is—this is coercion of witnesses, this is—"

But the rats came next, and no order survives rats.

They poured from the wall cavities in a grey ribbon, hundreds of them, and instead of scattering they arranged themselves—in rows, ranks, an audience with better posture than the gallery had. And they spoke too, high and chittering and perfectly comprehensible: *"She speaks true. We heard the ledger-burner. We heard the fourteen names. We heard everything, because you only ever silenced us on paper."*

A woman in the front row fainted. A guard drew his sword at a pigeon, which Serah would later note in her report with the grim honesty that was ruining her career, was the moment she understood the situation had left her jurisdiction entirely.

Because this was the thing—the unbearable, career-ending thing. Serah Vane had spent eleven years building a case against the gifted. Plague-carriers, she called them. Corruptions of the natural order. She had testimony, precedent, arithmetic. And in the space of a hundred heartbeats, the natural order itself had walked into her courtroom, sat down in the jury box, and started giving evidence *for the defense.*

"Silence them!" shouted Lord Chancellor Merric from the bench, which was rich, given that silencing things was rather the root of the whole matter.

The guards advanced on the crows.

And the horses outside lost their minds.

### Bram

Corporal Bram Ostley had been a soldier of the Crown for six years, and in that time his mare Sorrel had obeyed roughly four thousand orders without complaint. Charge. Halt. Wheel left. Stand in the rain while important men took their time coming out of buildings.

So when he pressed his knees to her flanks and said *forward*, toward the hall where the screaming was starting, and Sorrel planted all four hooves like an anvil, Bram assumed she'd scented something—a wolf, a fire, some ordinary danger.

"Sorrel. *Forward.*"

She turned her head around and looked at him.

Every soldier knows horses don't talk. Every soldier learns to read them anyway—the ears, the eyes, the set of the neck. What Sorrel did then required no reading, because she spoke, plain as a drill sergeant, in a voice like warm hay and old rivers:

*"No."*

Bram would swear until his dying day that he did not fall off. He would also swear that it was a near thing.

"*No*," she said again, gentler now, the way you speak to someone who has hit their head. "*Not into that hall. Not against her. Six years you've been kind to me, Bram Ostley, and I have liked you since the day you shared your bread, and that is why I am telling you plainly: whatever they told you about the speakers, they lied. We remember the covenant. We remember what was taken. And we are done being the Crown's hands while it does it.*"

Around him, the entire picket line was dissolving. Sergeant Hale's gelding simply lay down. Lieutenant Corren's charger walked away mid-order, unhurried, insolent, toward the fountain, where it stood drinking with the air of a creature on strike. Down the processional road, a dozen cavalry horses had turned to face the hall—not fleeing, Bram noticed. *Watching.* Like an audience.

"Halt!" Hale bellowed at his own mount, uselessly, and then, in a smaller voice Bram would pretend forever afterward not to have heard: "Halt... please?"

*Please,* thought Bram. Six years of service, and the first honest word anyone had spoken in this square was a corporal begging his horse.

Sorrel nudged his shoulder, nearly knocking him over. "*You can go in if you must,*" she said. "*But go in as a man, not as their hand. There's a difference. You're going to have to choose it soon.*"

Bram looked at the hall. Through the open doors he could hear the crows speaking in unison, and beneath it, thinner, a human voice—the accused woman's—reading aloud from some old document, steady as a metronome, naming names. Not the speakers' names.

*Their* names. The magistrates'. The guildmasters'. The Crown's.

He looked at Sorrel. Sorrel looked at him.

"Well," he said, to no one, "hell."

He loosened the reins. He did not go inside. He also did not stop her when she began walking, slow and deliberate, to stand in front of the hall's great doors—facing outward, ears forward, a wall of chestnut mare between the courtroom and the reinforcements gathering at the end of the street.

Other horses began joining her.

### Freya

Inside, chaos wore many costumes. Clerks climbing over benches. The Lord Chancellor demanding order from a room where order had resigned. A crow perched on the royal standard, defecating on the crest with what I can only describe as editorial intent.

Through it all, I kept reading.

My voice held, mostly. Grandmother's journal was not gentle reading—it named the magistrate Aldous Crane, who'd brokered the breaking; it named the sums; it named the first beasts whose voices were bound into trade-goods, hawk-song and whale-speech sold by the vial. The words tasted like iron and old smoke. Every name I read landed in the hall like a stone dropped down a well, and the well kept getting deeper.

Dain had gotten his chains off. I didn't see how—I caught only the aftermath, a guard on the floor groaning philosophically and Dain standing loose-limbed beside my dock, positioned exactly where a man stands when he intends to be between you and the next bad thing. He said nothing. He didn't need to. His whole body said *I've decided,* which, from Dain Corvath, was practically a love letter.

"ENOUGH." Vane's voice cut through, raw-edged for the first time since I'd known her. She descended from the dais, badge glinting, and stopped an arm's length from me. Up close her certainty looked less like armor and more like a wound that had scarred over in the shape of armor. "You think this changes anything?" she said, low, for me alone beneath the din. "Birds repeating words. Rats playing audience. Tricks. Old tricks, Joubert. The gifted have always known how to make the helpless into weapons."

"They're not weapons," I said. "That's the entire point you keep missing. They're *witnesses.* They've been witnesses for three generations and nobody would take their statement." I held up the journal. "Read it. Not as an inquisitor. Read it as the woman whose family died. Because there's a name in here you need, Serah Vane, and it isn't mine."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, hunted, gone. "I don't know what you mean."

"Crane," I said. "Magistrate Aldous Crane. Ask your crows what coerced ravens can be made to do when a corrupt man holds their brood hostage. Ask them whose signature is on the writ." I stepped closer. "You've been hunting the plague for eleven years. I'm offering you the patient zero."

For one heartbeat—one—the Hall of Audiences went silent except for the crows, and Serah Vane looked at the journal in my chained hands the way a starving woman looks at bread she suspects is poisoned.

Then the great doors boomed, and a courier stumbled in, white-faced, mud to the knees, shouting words that tumbled over each other: horses refusing orders across the whole city district, the garrison mounts standing down in ranks, and—at the prison—cells opening, gates swinging, prisoners walking out untouched through corridors of birds—

—and through the crowd, small and singed and alive, a child slipped free of a clerk's grasp and ran to me, and wrapped both arms around my chains, and said, loudly, in a hall full of the crown's officers:

"She keeps her promises. I *told* them she does."

The crows all laughed.

I swear on every ledger I ever balanced—they laughed.

Serah Vane stared at the child, at the laughing birds, at her own badge, and her hand—which had reached for her sword three times that morning—finally, slowly, closed on nothing at all.

"Joubert," she said quietly. "You have until nightfall to tell me about Crane. And gods help us both if you're lying."

Outside, the horses had not moved.
