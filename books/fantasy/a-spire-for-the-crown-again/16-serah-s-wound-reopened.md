## 16. Serah's Wound Reopened

I have interrogated forty-one gifted souls in my career. I keep the number in my head the way other people keep prayers. Forty-one confessions, forty-one registers signed in my own hand, and not one of them—not a single one—had ever looked at me the way Freya Joubert looked at me when they brought her into the holding room of the capital gaol.

She wasn't supposed to be here, of course. She was supposed to be on a cart to the trial court, chained, silent, filed away until the Crown decided what shape her ending should take. Instead she stood in front of my desk because some functionary with more deference than sense had seen her escort's papers—*my* papers, the ones I'd signed an hour ago in a fit of lunacy I still hadn't fully admitted to—and waved her through.

Which left me alone in a room with the woman I had chased across half the realm, and no witnesses, and no procedure, and a badge in my pocket that had stopped feeling like authority somewhere around Greyharbor and started feeling like a debt.

"You have five minutes," I said. "Then I call the guard."

"You'll call him in twenty," she said. "You want to hear what I have to say more than you want to be rid of me."

Arrogant. Accurate. The two traits so often travel together that I've stopped being surprised by the pairing.

"I know what you want me to say," she went on, before I could arrange my face into something less transparent. "You want me to beg. Or confess that the gift is a rot and I'm sorry for it. You've heard forty-one people say it, haven't you? I can tell. It's in your shoulders. You carry their apologies like ballast."

"Thirty-eight," I said. "Three died before registration." I don't know why I corrected her. Habit, perhaps. Inquisitors are meant to be precise; precision was the last thing I had left that still worked.

"Thirty-eight apologies," Freya said, "and did any of them ever tell you the truth?"

"The truth," I repeated, and I will admit—I hated how it came out of my mouth. Not cold. *Tired.* "You'll forgive me if I've grown skeptical of that particular commodity. Everyone in this building is selling it. Nobody's buying."

* * *

Let me tell you about the ravens, since she was going to make me anyway. I could see it in her face—that terrible gentleness some people wear when they're about to hurt you and wish they didn't have to. I've worn that face myself. I know its geography. So I sat down before my legs decided for me, and I let her do it, because there was nowhere left in me to run.

My family died in the autumn I turned eleven. A farmstead in the Aldervale hills, three days' ride from anything worth riding to. My father owed money to the wrong man—which is to say, to a magistrate, which is to say, to the law itself, wearing a finer coat than usual. Magistrate Corren. I have carried his name in my mouth for thirty years like a stone I couldn't spit out, though not for the reason I thought.

The ravens came at dusk. Hundreds of them. My mother was hanging washing; she never even screamed, or if she did, none of us lived long enough inside that moment to remember it. I survived because I was in the root cellar stealing blackcurrant preserves, which saved my life and ruined it in equal measure. When I climbed out, the birds were gone and everything they touched was quiet.

And the birds had *spoken*. That was the testimony at the inquest, whispered and disbelieved—the surviving child swore the ravens had called her father's name before they fell on him. Sworn statements from a traumatized girl count for little, but I made a career out of that whisper. I turned it into a doctrine: gifts are weapons that answer to no court. A beast-speaker can turn the sky against you and there is no warrant you can serve on a flock. Therefore the gift must be registered, collared, burned out where it cannot be collared. I built forty-one confessions on top of my family's graves and called the structure justice.

It was a good structure. It held my weight for thirty years.

"Magistrate Corren," Freya said quietly, when I finally stopped talking. My voice had gone somewhere without my permission; I'd told her things I have never told a tribunal, and she'd listened the way no one listens in this city—with nothing being recorded. "Was he ever investigated? After?"

"He fled the province within the year. Embezzlement charges, unrelated. He died wealthy in Vessany." I shrugged, and the shrug cost me more than I expected. "Justice has a long stride but it doesn't always walk our way."

"There's a journal," she said. "My grandmother's. She was a trader for the Vellum Guild, back when the guild was buying its privileges in every province that would sell them. Corren was one of those sellers. And Corren—" she paused, and I watched her decide whether mercy or truth was the greater cruelty, and I realized with a lurch that she had chosen truth *as* a mercy, and I hated her for it and needed it in the same breath— "Corren found a beast-speaker. A boy who could call corvids. He didn't burn him, Serah. He hired him. Coerced him. Threatened his family. Your family's ravens were sent."

The room did not move. The room was very still and very ordinary, with its water stain shaped like a hare and its guttering lamp, and I sat in it while the floor of my life opened under me without making a sound.

"That's a lie," I said. "That's exactly the kind of lie a condemned woman tells her jailer."

"Yes," Freya agreed. "It is. And you should check it. I'd insist on it, if I were you. The journal names two witnesses to the bargain—a carter named Odile Brask, who I believe is still alive in Greyharbor, and the guild ledger itself. The old one. Payments to 'C.' Fourteen entries over three years. My grandmother kept meticulous accounts." A ghost of something crossed her face—grief, maybe, or its long shadow. "She kept accounts of everything. It was her penance and her sin in the same habit."

* * *

Here is what nobody warns you about certainty: it doesn't shatter like glass. Glass is quick. You get your wound and your bleeding and your sweeping-up, all in an afternoon, and grief knows what to do with glass.

Certainty goes like a foundation goes. Slowly. One hairline crack you refuse to look at, then another, then a season of small sounds in the night, and then one morning you put your foot down in the place your foot has gone down ten thousand times before and the whole floor gives way at once, and you understand—too late, uselessly, obscenely late—that every step you ever took was taken over a hollow.

I sent for Odile Brask's record before Freya had even been escorted back to her cell. Alive, aged seventy-one, resident of Greyharbor's salt-quarter. I sent for the old Vellum Guild ledgers, the pre-reform ones everyone assumed had burned in the warehouse fire of my second year of service. They had not burned. They had been *sold*, which in the capital amounts to the same thing, except when someone like me goes looking with a writ instead of coin.

I did not sleep. I sat in my office with the ledgers open under lamplight and I read fourteen entries in my grandmother-tongue arithmetic, payments to a single letter, dated across the autumns of my childhood, and beside the ninth entry—in some clerk's dutiful hand, because clerks are the true historians of every empire—a note: *"Corvids, delivery as contracted, Aldervale."*

Aldervale.

I am told I did not cry. I am told this because I don't remember, and the absence of memory where tears should be is the only evidence I trust. What I remember instead is standing at my window at the grey hour, watching the gulls wheel over the courthouse roof, and thinking—with a clarity that felt like a blade being drawn—that every one of those birds could be listening. That they probably were. That for thirty years I had hunted children for the crime of a thing that had been done *to* them, by men like Corren, with ledgers like these, and that the ravens above my window knew it and I could not ask them to hold their tongues because I had spent my whole life teaching myself that their voices were the enemy.

Vengeance is a simple creature. It wants a throat and it does not check pedigrees. Mine had wanted the gift itself—all of it, everywhere, in every hidden mouth—for thirty years, and now the object of its hunger had dissolved like frost at noon, and what was left was just a woman of fifty-two standing at a window with an ache in her chest shaped like her mother's washing line.

* * *

Freya's advocate—a nervous, excellent young man whose name I have already chosen to forget—filed a motion the next morning to admit the journals and the ledger as evidence. I did not oppose it. I watched my own signature go onto the non-opposition like it belonged to a stranger, a woman who had once believed that evidence was a thing you gathered around a verdict you'd already reached.

Word travels fast in the gaol. Faster in the ministry. By midday I had three summons on my desk—one from the Lord Chancellor, one from the High Provost of my own order, and one unsigned note that said only: *We know what you've read. Consider your pension.* The threat was so clumsy it nearly made me laugh. They still thought I was a creature who could be bought with comfort. They had not yet understood what Freya Joubert understood on the day she ran: that once you see the trap you built for yourself, the only unbearable thing is to keep living in it.

In the evening I went down to the cells. Not on the record. No clerk, no register, no witness. Freya sat cross-legged on the floor of her cell, and the rat sitting near her bars did not flee me, which unsettled me more than anything else that day, and she looked up with that same terrible gentleness and said, "You checked."

"Odile Brask confirms the bargain," I said. "Corren's correspondence surfaces next week, according to the archivists. It's over, Joubert. Your grandmother's confession is going to hang a dozen powerful men, and the Gift-Hunt is going to collapse in scandal, and you will be a footnote." I swallowed. "Congratulations."

"That's not why you came down here."

"No."

The rat washed its whiskers. Absurdly, I found it steadying.

"For thirty years," I said, and my voice did something I did not authorize, "I thought the sky itself had killed my family. Do you understand? Every bird on every wire. Every dog that watched me pass. Every horse that shied. I saw murder in all of them, so I made it law. And it was a *contract*, Joubert. A paid delivery. Men with inkstains and warm houses sent those ravens the way one sends a letter, and the boy who called them was eleven years older than me and probably died hating himself." I pressed my hand flat against the cold stone of the wall, because the alternative was pressing it against something softer and worse. "I burned thirty-eight people to punish a crime committed against me by a magistrate with a ledger. Tell me what your beasts call a person like that."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Then she said: "They'd call you wounded. Wolves don't blame the limping deer for leading the pack into a ravine. But they do leave her behind if she won't let them help."

"I don't need parables."

"No," she agreed. "You need a target. And yours just evaporated. I know how that feels—my whole inheritance did the same thing to me in a ruined waystation three weeks ago." She rose, unhurried, and stood close enough to the bars that I could see the exhaustion underneath the composure, the same exhaustion I imagine was written on my own face. "So here's mine, inquisitor, plainly. The covenant can be reforged. At the Spire. With the three keys. If it is, then every speaker in the realm stands revealed and bound to every beast by open oath—no more hidden gifts, no more coerced ravens, no more Correns hiring the sky. It doesn't erase what happened to your family. Nothing does. But it means no one else's cellar becomes their grave."

"And if I refuse? If I decide the journal is forgery and doctrine is easier than truth?" I asked it honestly. I genuinely wanted to know what I would do, and asking her seemed the only way to find out.

She smiled, tired and unflinching. "Then you'll be at the Spire anyway, trying to stop us, and you'll see it reforge regardless, because Dain is already on the stair and the wolves are already climbing. You can arrive as a shield or as an obstacle, Serah Vane. The birds don't care. But I think—" and here she did the cruelest, kindest thing anyone had done to me in thirty years; she believed out loud at me— "I think the girl in the root cellar would like to arrive as a shield."

* * *

I dismissed the guards from the west postern at the third bell. I gave her escort my signet and my cloak, because the escort was a stablehand and a knight with no oaths left and both of them looked terrified, and terror responds well to borrowed authority. And then I walked back through the sleeping gaol, past the registers of forty-one souls, past the cell where the rat still sat at the bars like a small brown chaperone, and I unlocked my office and pulled out a fresh sheet of paper.

If I was going to commit treason, I was going to do it precisely. Precision was the last thing I had left that still worked, after all.

*To the Lord Chancellor,* I wrote. *Effective immediately, I resign my commission. Effective immediately, I reopen the matter of Magistrate Corren. Effective immediately, the Gift-Hunt is suspended pending review of evidence enclosed. You will want to arrest me for this. I would. But you cannot, because by the time this reaches you, I will be on the road north, and everything I know will already be in the hands of people you cannot buy.*

I blotted it. I sealed it. Through the window, the gulls had settled along the courthouse ridge in a long, patient row, heads cocked, watching my lamp with an attention I no longer had any right to call imagination.

"All right," I said to them, to the girl in the cellar, to no one. "Show me the way north, then. If any of you are speaking."

Every single one of them lifted off at once, wings cracking the dark like a page turning, and wheeled toward the mountain road, and waited.

I took my coat, and followed.
