## 17. Escape on Wings Not Ours

*Freya*

I had been awake for two days in the Spirehold cells, and I can report with authority that the crown's dungeons are exactly as bad as the ones in my novels, only with worse plumbing. The difference between fiction and fact, I have decided, is that fact smells.

The key I'd taken from the burning market lay against my ribs where I'd sewn it into the lining of my shift — a sliver of grey stone shaped like a feather that hummed faintly whenever I stopped trying to pretend it didn't. Two guards had already patted me down for it. Neither had felt it. The key did not wish to be found. I understood the impulse completely.

Then, on the third night, the ravens came.

Not one raven. Not ten. A *weather* of them, pouring down the chimney shafts like black rain, and every single one of them was talking at once.

*"Freya Joubert. Little ledger-keeper. Get up. Get UP."*

I got up. When several hundred birds scream your name in unison through a stone ceiling, you develop a certain alacrity.

The cell door unlocked itself — which is to say, a raven the size of a small cat had spent what looked like an entire day learning the mechanism of the lock from the outside, and now turned the key with the smug deliberation of a locksmith presenting an invoice. Its name, it told me, was Brackle. It had opinions about my posture.

"The corridor," I said. "There are guards."

"There were guards," said Brackle. "Now there is a guard who is asleep, because we flew into his ear and told him about sleep until he believed us. Ravens do not lie, little speaker. We merely insist."

I will spare you the details of the escape except to say this: a dungeon built to hold humans is not built to hold a flock. The birds went ahead of me like a tide, and everywhere they landed, doors opened, torches guttered, and grown men with swords sat down on the steps to think pleasant thoughts about their mothers. I moved through the dark of Spirehold laughing in a thin, unhinged sort of way, because I had spent thirty years whispering to rats and pigeons in the cracks of the world, and here was the whole sky come down the chimney for me.

They had freed Dain first. Of course they had. The beasts liked him better than he deserved.

* * *

*Dain*

I woke to a beak in my ear and a voice saying, *"Get up, horse-kisser."*

In my defense, I struck at it before I was fully conscious, which is how I ended up flat on my back in a crown dungeon swinging at empty air while three ravens watched from the window ledge with the weary patience of men watching a drunk fight his own shadow.

"He's always like this," one said.

"Freya says he improves under pressure," said another.

"Freya," I said, sitting up, "where?"

"Coming," said the largest raven, whose name I would learn was Brackle and who I would come to regard with something adjacent to respect. "Your brothers-in-arms put her two floors below you. Sloppy work. We've already handled the door."

I want to be honest here, because honesty is apparently the theme of my life now: when they told me the plan, I hated it. The plan was *birds*. The plan was follow the birds out of a fortress designed by the realm's finest engineers, past four gates and a garrison, and run for a mountain that remembers things while an inquisitor with a warrant for all our names either chased us or didn't. There was no part of the plan that involved weapons or tactics or anything I had spent twenty years being trained in.

But I owed Freya Joubert my life twice over — once for the mare, once for the marsh — and debts, unlike oaths, I still knew how to pay.

We went over the wall in the fourth bell of the night, on ropes the rats had chewed loose from the supply stores and dragged to the parapet one strand at a time over three nights, which is the sort of patient, terrifying competence I have stopped questioning. Below us, the courtyard garrison slept like children. Above us, the watchtowers stood empty, their crossbowmen dreaming of geese.

At the bottom, on the frost-slick cobbles, stood Serah Vane.

My hand went to a sword I no longer carried. Hers went to hers. And then she did the last thing in the world I expected.

She looked up at the sky, where the flock wheeled in a slow black spiral, and her face did something complicated — grief and fury and something underneath both that looked almost like relief — and she said, quietly, "Horses first. Then the east gate. My relief arrives at dawn and I intend to be elsewhere when it does."

"You're letting us go," I said.

"I am *escorting* you out," said Vane, "because if I let you go, someone will eventually ask why, and if I chase you, you'll die, and I find lately that I am no longer certain you should." She drew her badge from her collar — the silver sunburst of the Inquisition — and held it up to the gate sentry, who saluted and opened the postern without looking at the two hooded fugitives standing behind her. "Walk quickly. Do not thank me. If you thank me I will reconsider everything."

Freya, beside me, opened her mouth.

"Don't," Vane said again, sharper. "You especially. You have questions, woman. I can see them lined up behind your teeth like soldiers. Save them. Ask me when we're past the walls and I might answer one."

* * *

*Freya*

Here is what nobody tells you about your enemy changing sides: it doesn't feel like victory. It feels like walking on ice that has just informed you it's water.

Serah Vane walked us out of Spirehold under her own seal. She spoke to the gate captain in a voice like a whetstone. She produced forged travel writs from her coat with the brisk shamelessness of a woman discovering, late in life, that rules were only ever tools. And all the while I studied her the way I used to study ledgers, looking for the hidden column, the false sum.

She caught me at it, halfway down the causeway, and answered the question I hadn't asked.

"The ravens that killed my family," she said, not slowing, eyes fixed on the road, "were coerced. I learned it yesterday. There was a magistrate named Corvel, twenty years dead of comfortable old age, who bound them with a stolen covenant-word and aimed them like arrows at witnesses against him. My parents were witnesses." Her jaw worked. "The gifted didn't kill my family. The covenant-breakers did. Which means I burned—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Which means the hunt I built was aimed wrong. So now I'm aiming it right. At Corvel's heirs. At the guild houses. At the men who broke it first." A pause. "And at whatever else gets in the way. Don't mistake me for an ally, Joubert. I am a corrected error, and corrected errors are dangerous to everyone."

"That's the most honest thing any official of the crown has ever said to me," I said.

"It's the only kind of sentence I have left," she said, and gave the east gate a look that made it open faster.

Dain, riding point on horses the stable-boy swore had saddled themselves, kept glancing back at her the way you glance at a storm cloud that has agreed, provisionally, to be on your side.

"She'll turn on us," he muttered to me as we cleared the walls and took the river road at a canter.

"Probably," I said. "But not today, and not before the Spire."

"How can you know that?"

"Because the crows have been following her since the prison," I said, "and crows know a convert when they see one. They say she talks to herself at night. Except she isn't talking to herself anymore, Dain. She's answering."

He was quiet for a long moment. Behind us, Vane rode alone, half a field back, her badge catching the last moonlight, and above her the crows kept pace like a jury that had voted to acquit but wanted to see the sentencing anyway.

* * *

*Dain*

Three days to the mountains. Three days of hard riding, cold camps, and the increasingly surreal experience of traveling in company where the outriders were sparrows.

I'll admit something here that I have admitted to no one living: I missed the order. Not the crown — the crown could rot — but the order. The rhythm of it. Watches and roads and a brother's shoulder in the dark. For twenty years, loyalty had been a thing handed to me whole, stamped with a sigil, and I had never once wondered what was underneath the stamp until the stamp was torn off and I was left holding nothing but a mare who loved me and a debt I couldn't stop paying.

Somewhere on the second night, huddled under a rock shelf while sleet came sideways off the foothills, I realized the debt was paid. Had been paid since the market, probably, when I stood in front of a child and let myself get arrested. What I felt for Freya Joubert now wasn't obligation.

It was just... choosing. Every morning, getting on the horse. Every night, taking the first watch so she could sleep. Nobody commanded it. That was the strange part. That was the part that felt like falling.

She was sitting up against the rock, turning the stone feather over in her fingers, and the wolves had come out of the dark to sit at the edge of the firelight — five of them now, silent as furniture, escorting us the way soldiers escort a general who doesn't like to admit she needs one.

"They're arguing about you," she said, without looking around.

"About me."

"The grey one thinks you're soft. The scarred one says soft is not the same as weak, and the others are betting on whether you'd actually stand on the Spire stair against your old company." She glanced back at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the careful blankness she wore like armor, but worry, aimed at me specifically. "Would you? Because the wolves tell me the crown's reinforcements reached the pass yesterday. Serah bought us a day, maybe two, and then there will be forty men on that mountain, and every one of them trained by you."

I fed a branch to the fire and watched the sparks go up, and thought about Sir Aldous Marren, who taught me the sword and never once asked whether the orders attached to it were just. Thought about the oath I'd sold in Greyharbor for a key, and how light I'd felt afterward, and how heavy I'd felt every day before.

"Ask them what happens if I do," I said.

The wolves' ears went back. The scarred one made a sound low in its chest that was almost laughter.

"They say," Freya translated softly, "that the stair will remember."

* * *

*Freya*

On the fourth morning, Serah Vane did not ride out of camp with us.

We found her at the treeline, already dismounted, checking her sword the way a person checks a tool rather than a weapon, and she cut off Dain's question before he could shape it.

"Cavalry crossed the ford at midnight," she said. "Forty lances, give or take. They'll reach the lower switchbacks by dusk tomorrow, and they'll be led by Captain Ivell, who is a decent man with terrible judgment and no imagination whatsoever." She pulled her badge out and looked at it — really looked at it, the way you look at a thing you're deciding whether to keep — and then she tucked it back inside her coat, over her heart, where badges are supposed to go and where hers had hung like a borrowed coat for twenty years. "So I'm going to ride back down the road, put this on properly, and meet Captain Ivell at the head of his column. And I am going to inform him that the Spire approach is under Inquisition seal pending investigation of covenant-crimes by the estate of Magistrate Corvel, and that any officer who advances past the treeline without my written warrant will be arrested, tried, and very possibly hanged, because honestly, at this point, who's going to check?"

Dain stared at her. "That buys hours. Not days."

"Hours is what I have to sell." She swung back into the saddle. For a moment she looked down at us — at the fugitive and the disgraced knight and the wolves sitting in the bracken like an honor guard — and something moved across her face that on anyone else I would have called regret, and on her I decided to call arithmetic. "Joubert. When you get up there. Whatever it is you mean to do with that voice of yours." She paused. "Make it true. I've had enough of the other thing to last two lifetimes."

She turned the horse and rode back down toward the road and the forty lances, and I watched her go with my throat tight, thinking: *there goes the woman who burned fourteen names, going back alone to stand in front of an army for mine.* The world, I reflected, does not divide into good and evil. It divides into people running from truth and people walking toward it, and sometimes — rarely, at terrible cost — the same person does both, in order.

"She'll hold them," Dain said quietly.

"For a while."

"For long enough?" He wasn't asking about Vane. He was asking about himself, about the stair, about the conversation with the wolves he pretended he hadn't heard.

I looked up the mountain. Above the treeline the Spire stood against a bruised morning sky, older than the crown, older than the guild, older than my family's sin, and the stone feather against my ribs was humming now, loud enough that I could feel it in my teeth, and far below — impossibly far, at the edge of hearing — I could feel the third key waking too, like a heart starting up under the mountain's skin.

"No," I said. "Not long enough. Come on. We climb."

Behind us, at the foot of the valley, the first horns of the crown's army sounded — and ahead of us, from the summit, something answered.
