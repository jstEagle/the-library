## 18. The Spire Awakens

The mountain did not want us climbing it.

I don't mean that in the dramatic way — no avalanches, no screaming winds, no convenient boulders raining down to teach me a lesson about hubris, though I've read enough guild ledgers to know the realm loves charging extra for those. I mean it in the way a locked door doesn't want a key. The Spire's slopes were granite and old snow and a silence so complete it had texture, like wool pressed against my ears. Every beast for fifty leagues had gone quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters.

Or when someone is about to be judged.

"Your wolf friend keeps looking at me like I owe her money," Dain said behind me. He was breathing hard. Former knight of the Crown Guard, current oathless man, current man-who-had-surrendered-his-oath-to-drowned-folk-in-exchange-for-a-key, which meant every step up this mountain was a step he took with nothing in his pockets but regret and a sword he probably wasn't allowed to use anymore anyway.

"She does think you owe her money," I said. "I told her about the ferry incident."

"The ferry incident was *your* fault."

"History will decide."

Marrow — because that was the wolf's name, because she had told me it herself in a voice like dry leaves and old bones, and because I had learned by now not to argue with anything that could eat me — paused at a shelf of black rock and looked back at us both with pale amber patience. She'd been guiding us for three days. She hadn't spoken much since Greyharbor. Wolves ration words the way misers ration gold; I respected it even if it made conversation lopsided.

*Close now,* she said. Her voice arrived inside my skull the way all beast-speech did — not sound exactly, more meaning wearing sound's clothing. *It will ask you things. It asked your grandmother things.*

That stopped me with one boot on a ledge. "You knew my grandmother?"

*I knew her smell,* Marrow said. *Fear smells the same across generations.*

Which was, I reflect, the single most unsettling thing anyone has ever said to me on a mountainside, and I once had a mule explain tax law to me.

* * *

We reached the roots of the Spire as the sun went down behind the western peaks, and I use the word *roots* deliberately, because the tower that rose out of the mountain's crown was not stone. Not entirely. It looked the way an old tree looks when it has decided, after long consideration, to be architecture instead: pale as bone, veined with channels that glimmered faintly like sap under bark, spiraling up and up into a sky gone the color of a healing bruise. The wind moved through it and the Spire *hummed*, low, a note below hearing that I felt in my teeth and in the scar on my palm and somewhere behind my ribs where I keep the things I don't say out loud.

Two keys hung at my throat. The first, salt-crusted and heavy, that the drowned-folk had traded for Dain's spoken oath — I still remembered the sound he made giving it up, like a man coughing out his own heart. The second we'd won in blood and terror at the market, clutched in my fist while Serah Vane's soldiers burned the stalls and a child named Wren screamed my name as they dragged her away. That memory rode up my spine with every step. It had been riding up my spine for two weeks. I'd stopped expecting it to get off.

The third key was not hanging around anywhere. Of course it wasn't. Nothing in this covenant business had been simple; why would the last piece be sitting politely on a shelf?

*Inside,* Marrow said, and nodded her grey head at the Spire itself. *The guardian holds it. It has held it since the breaking. It has been waiting a very long time, small speaker.*

"For what?"

*For someone foolish enough to climb all this way.* A pause. Her tongue lolled slightly, which I have learned is wolf for amusement. *Or for you. Possibly the same thing.*

There was a door. There is always a door; the world is sentimental that way. It stood at the Spire's base, tall as three men, without hinges or handle, and when I put my hand against it the surface was warm and faintly alive, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Words rose through my palm and into my head, and the words were not beast-speech. They were older, heavier, a voice like a mountain deciding to have opinions.

***WHO COMES TO THE COVENANT?***

"Freya Joubert," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, which was embarrassing, but the door didn't seem to care about delivery. "Daughter of Aldric, granddaughter of Maren Joubert."

The humming changed. It deepened. And the door opened — not swinging inward, but *unseaming*, the way a bud opens, petal by impossible petal of pale stone — and warm light spilled out smelling of summer and thunderstorms and something underneath that I can only describe as *attention*.

*It knows the name,* Marrow said quietly. *It has been chewing on that name for four generations.*

"Wonderful," I said. "I love being expected."

Dain caught my sleeve. His hand was shaking, just slightly, and I noted — not for the first time — that the disgraced knight who had once cornered me in a marsh with murder in his orders now looked at me like I was the one walking into the fire and he couldn't follow.

"I'll wait here," he said. "With her." He tipped his head at Marrow. "If anything comes up that path that isn't you—"

"You'll fight it with a sword you technically surrendered?"

"I'll fight it *very technically*," he said, and there was the ghost of the old dry humor in it, the thing that had survived disgrace and dungeons and drowning-folk bargains, and I found I was absurdly glad of it. "Go. Get your key. Come back down. Those are the terms."

Those were the terms. I should hold onto that word *terms*. Everyone in this story keeps making bargains without reading the fine print — me included, though I balanced ledgers for eleven years and should have known better than anyone that the fine print is where the whole contract lives.

I walked into the Spire.

* * *

Inside was hollow, all the way up. The walls curved away overhead into darkness threaded with those glowing veins, and the light came from everywhere and nowhere, and the floor beneath my feet gave slightly, springy, like moss over root. My footsteps made no sound at all. The two keys at my throat had begun to hum in harmony with the walls, a chord building slowly, and I realized the entire tower was an instrument and I had just become the note it had been waiting to play.

In the center of the hollow stood the guardian.

It was roughly a person the way a mountain is roughly a chair. Ten feet of pale living stone, shaped like a woman kneeling, or perhaps rising — the longer I looked the less sure I was whether it was bowing or standing up from a long kneel — with its face smooth and blank except for two hollows where eyes should be. In the hollows, something moved. Not fire. More like weather seen very far away.

***FREYA JOUBERT,*** it said, and its voice was the mountain's voice, and the floor trembled with it. ***GRANDDAUGHTER OF THE BREAKER. YOU CARRY TWO KEYS.***

"I do." I held them up. Salt-and-bone in my right hand, storm-grey iron in my left. They pulsed like hearts.

***THE THIRD IS NOT GIVEN. IT NEVER WAS. YOUR GRANDMOTHER STOLE THE OTHERS' SISTER AND SHATTERED WHAT THEY HELD TOGETHER. DO YOU KNOW THIS?***

"I know." My grandmother's journal had been very thorough on the point, in handwriting that got shakier as the confession went on. Maren Joubert and five others had broken the covenant to buy the Vellum Guild its trading rights — had pried the pact apart at the seams and pocketed pieces of it, and the pieces had become keys, and the beasts had lost their voices to human ears, and everything after — the hunt, the burnings, the fear, the fourteen names sold out of my own guildhouse — grew from that original theft like rot grows from a wound.

***THEN YOU KNOW WE DO NOT HONOR YOUR BLOODLINE HERE.***

"I'd be worried if you did."

Something shifted in the guardian. I couldn't swear it moved, but the quality of its attention changed, sharpened, the way a cat's attention changes when prey stops running and turns around. The distant-weather eyes brightened.

***YOU ARE NOT WHAT I EXPECTED,*** it said. ***YOUR GRANDMOTHER CAME FLATTERING. SHE CAME WITH OFFERS. YOU COME WITH SARCASM AND DEBTS.***

"All my best qualities are inherited from the other side of the family."

***THE THIRD KEY HAS A PRICE, FREYA JOUBERT. NOT A PAYMENT SUCH AS THE DROWNED ONES TAKE. NOT AN OATH SUCH AS KNIGHTS CARRY AND DISCARD.*** The guardian rose. All ten feet of it unfolded upward and kept unfolding, twelve feet, fifteen, until its blank face looked down at me from a height that made my neck ache and the keys at my throat sing louder, urgent now. ***A LIVING ANCHOR. THE COVENANT MUST BE REFORGED IN A VOICE, AND THE VOICE MUST REMAIN. BIND YOURSELF TO EVERY BEAST THAT LIVES. GIVE THEM YOUR HEARING AND YOUR SPEECH UNTIL YOUR DYING DAY, AND AFTER IT, UNTIL ANOTHER TAKES UP THE ANCHOR. YOU WILL NEVER AGAIN BE ONLY YOURSELF. YOU WILL BE THE DOOR.***

The hollow went very quiet.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is apparently the theme of my life now, whether I auditioned for it or not: terror is a strange, specific thing at the top of a mountain with a stone god explaining your permanent conscription. It isn't screaming. It's cold. It starts in the hands. I looked down at mine and they weren't shaking, which surprised me, because inside I was shaking enough for a whole regiment.

Forever. That was the price. Not a battle, not a trial, not even death — death I could imagine, death had a shape. This was a door that never closed again. Every rat in every gutter, every whale in the deep channels, every cart-horse beaten by a drunk driver, every hawk snared in a poacher's line — their voices, their pain, their small wants and enormous griefs, pouring through me until I died and possibly past it. I had spent eleven years perfecting invisibility. I had hidden behind ledgers and downcast eyes and a gift folded up small as a coin sewn into a hem. And the Spire was asking me to become the single loudest, most visible thing in the realm. Permanently.

"No take-backs?" I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "No trial period? Because I've signed guild contracts before and there is *always* a trial period."

***THERE IS NO CONTRACT,*** the guardian said. ***CONTRACTS ARE HOW YOUR KIND BROKE IT. THERE IS ONLY CHOICE, MADE IN FULL KNOWLEDGE, NEVER RETRACTED. THAT IS WHY IT MUST BE CHOSEN. THAT IS WHY IT CANNOT BE DEMANDED.***

"And if I walk away?"

***THEN THE COVENANT STAYS BROKEN. THE BEASTS STAY SILENT TO YOUR KIND, AND FEARING YOU, AND IN THEIR FEAR, DANGEROUS. THE KEYS SLEEP AGAIN. SOMEONE ELSE MAY CLIMB, IN ANOTHER GENERATION, OR TEN.*** A pause, and the distant weather in its eye-hollows turned almost gentle. ***OR YOU WALK AWAY, FREYA JOUBERT, AND YOU LIVE. QUIETLY. HIDING. AS YOU HAVE ALWAYS LIVED. NO ONE WOULD BLAME YOU. I HAVE WATCHED YOUR KIND FOR FOUR GENERATIONS. BLAME IS CHEAP. COURAGE IS NOT.***

And that — that was the moment, gods help me, when clarity came.

Not courage. I want to be careful with that word. What came was simpler and colder and truer: I saw my life laid flat, like a ledger page, and I finally read it properly.

I thought back to the hawk I'd soothed in the guild loft, its heartbeat stuttering under my palms, and how after it healed it used to land on my windowsill at dawn and I'd pretend not to be waiting for it. I thought about the rats in the walls who'd told me truths no human would, and how their loyalty had cost some of them their lives. I thought of Wren, small and ferocious, mirror-gifted, dragged away screaming while soldiers laughed — and the promise I'd made her that I might not be able to keep. I thought of Dain surrendering his oath in a flooded harbor because he'd decided some things weighed more than the crown. I thought of Marrow saying *fear smells the same across generations* and understanding, suddenly, that she hadn't meant it as cruelty. She'd meant it as recognition. She'd smelled my grandmother's fear in me and climbed a mountain anyway to see what else I carried.

My whole hidden life — every swallowed word, every hour spent listening when I was supposed to be deaf, every ledger entry in my own private cipher cataloguing creatures instead of commodities — none of it had been cowardice. It had been training. Eleven years of apprenticeship for a job posting that had never existed until this exact moment.

The Spire hadn't summoned me. It had simply finally gotten around to interviewing the only candidate who'd been preparing all along.

"You should know," I said to the guardian, "that I'm told I'm difficult. I argue with wolves. I once made a customs officer cry."

***NOTED,*** it said, and I would swear on every ledger in Vellum Guild history that the mountain sounded amused.

"And you should know the people who broke this covenant are still alive. Some of them. Their heirs run guilds and courts and sit in high seats, and they will fight a reforging with everything they have. If I anchor this thing, I'm not just becoming your door. I'm putting my head on the block in front of the entire realm, and the blade has already been falling for weeks. There's an inquisitor—" my voice caught on her name and I pushed through it, "—there's an inquisitor who has taken someone I promised to protect, and a guildmaster who sold me like inventory, and a crown that burns people like me for existing. Binding myself won't make any of that gentle. It'll make it worse first."

***YES,*** said the guardian.

"Good," I said. "I wanted that on the record."

I took off the two keys. I held them out. They were warm as live things, and they turned toward the guardian like flowers toward sun.

"But I need to say this plainly, before you take them," I said, and now my voice shook, and I let it, because the guardian had said choice must be made in full knowledge, and this was the full knowledge, the fine print, the part I'd balance in red ink: "I'm afraid. Not of dying. Of never being alone in my own head again. I built my whole life on being alone. It was the only place I felt safe. You're asking me to demolish the only house I've ever lived in and leave the doors open forever."

The guardian knelt then. All that height folding down, patient as geology, until its blank face was level with mine and the far-away weather in its eyes was close enough to see clearly, and what I saw was sorrow. Old, worn-smooth sorrow, four generations deep.

***THAT FEAR IS THE TRUEST THING YOU HAVE SAID,*** it said. ***AND SO I WILL TELL YOU THE LAST TRUTH, WHICH YOUR GRANDMOTHER NEVER LEARNED. THE ANCHOR IS NEVER ALONE EITHER. THAT IS THE ENTIRE POINT. THE COVENANT WAS NEVER MEANT TO BE A CHAIN OR A CAGE. IT WAS A PROMISE BETWEEN THE LONELY KINGDOMS — THAT NEITHER BEAST NOR HUMAN NEED WALK UNHEARD. YOUR GRANDMOTHER HEARD ONLY COST, SO SHE TOOK. WILL YOU HEAR ONLY COST, SO YOU LOSE?***

I stood there at the top of the world with my fear in my hands like a ledger I'd finally finished balancing, and the columns added up, and they did not say *cost*.

They said *belonging*.

"Yes," I said. "I choose it. Freya Joubert chooses the anchor. No contracts. No retraction. Doors open forever."

The guardian took the keys.

* * *

What happened next, I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe, so consider this a first draft.

The keys left my hands and did not fall. They rose, turning slowly, and the guardian opened — its chest unseamed the same way the door had, petal by petal of living stone — and inside, where a heart would be, was the third key. It looked like all of them and none of them: salt and iron and bone braided into a single shape that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts, the way truth hurts when you've lived in the dark.

It sang.

All three sang together, and the Spire answered — the whole tower, root to crown, every vein of light flaring — and the chord that filled the hollow was the chord the world had been missing for four generations. I heard it with my ears and my bones and the secret folded place behind my ribs where my gift had always lived, and I heard something *answer* it. Far below, at the treeline, a sound rose up through the dusk: wolves, first, dozens of voices, then hawks, then the vast subsonic groan of whales out in the deep water answering across forty leagues of ocean, and under it all a thousand smaller voices — mice, crows, foxes, the whole chattering terrified silenced world — finding the melody.

***COME,*** the guardian said. ***THE ANCHOR STANDS AT THE SUMMIT. THE SPIRE WAKES, AND THE WORLD WILL FEEL IT — EVERY GIFTED SOUL IN THE REALM WILL FEEL IT BEFORE MORNING. INCLUDING,*** and here the distant weather turned sharp, watchful, ***WHOEVER IS RIDING TOWARD US WITH STEEL.***

"How many?" I asked, already moving toward stairs that hadn't existed a moment ago, spiraling up inside the wall like a nautilus shell, lit by veins of waking light.

***MANY. RIDING HARD. THEY CRESTED THE LOWER PASS AN HOUR AGO.***

Crown colors. Vane's soldiers, or whatever was left of her net after she'd started tearing it open herself — I didn't know yet, couldn't know, and there was no time to know. Somewhere down that dark pass was an army coming to stop the reforging, and between it and me, on the narrow stair, stood exactly one disgraced knight with a surrendered oath, a technical sword, and the most stubborn mare in the realm.

I put my hand on the first stair and paused, one last time, listening — really listening — to the world I was about to marry.

*Dawn soon,* Marrow's voice reached me even here, faint and steady through stone and distance. *Whatever happens at the top, small speaker — the pack remembers. Climb.*

So I climbed.

Behind and below me, faint through the singing walls, I heard hoofbeats on the pass, and steel drawn, and Dain Corvath shouting something defiant into the dark — and ahead and above, at the summit, under a sky just beginning to bruise toward dawn, the third key waited in its cradle of stone, singing my name.
