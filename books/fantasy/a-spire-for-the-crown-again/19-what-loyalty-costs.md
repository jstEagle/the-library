## 19. What Loyalty Costs

I had been a knight for twenty-two years, and in all that time I had never once stood on a staircase and refused to let anyone pass. It was not the sort of thing they trained you for. They trained you to hold gates, to hold lines, to hold your tongue when a lord said something stupid at dinner. Nobody trained you to hold a stair against your own brothers with nothing but your sword, your word, and a growing suspicion that you were about to die in a very undignified position on a mountain.

The Spire's stair was old work, older than the crown, older than the guild, older than any oath I'd ever taken. Each step was a slab of grey stone worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of feet, and the whole thing wound up the mountainside like a rope someone had dropped from heaven. Below me, the treeline. Above me, Freya Joubert, climbing toward whatever the Spire wanted from her. And coming up the switchback trail through the pines, with the particular rhythm I'd marched to half my life—left, left, left, right, and don't think about it—came the Third Crown Company.

My company. Formerly.

I counted them out of habit. Sixty shields, maybe seventy. Torches in the front rank even though the sun was still up, because torches are not for seeing, they're for being seen. Serah Vane rode at the rear on a grey mare, which surprised me. I'd expected her at the front, the way zealots like to be first through the door. Then I understood: she wasn't here to fight. She was here to witness. That was worse.

Beside me, a wolf the size of a yearling calf sat down on the step and sighed.

"Don't," I said.

The wolf—her name, as near as the beasts' names translated, was something like *Frost-Over-Stones*—sighed again, deeper, the way she did when she thought I was being dense. Which, historically, was often.

"I know what you're going to say," I told her. "You're going to say there are seventy of them and one of me and that arithmetic is bad. I did the arithmetic. I was always good at arithmetic. It's the part of soldiering I liked best, because the numbers never lied. Seventy to one is bad. I've decided not to care."

Frost put her great head on her paws, which was the wolf equivalent of *suit yourself, meat-thing*, and I felt the strange pressure at the back of my mind that meant she was calling to the others. The wolves had been filtering up through the trees all morning, silent as weather. I didn't ask how many were coming. I'd learned that some gifts are better received than counted.

Here's the thing about oaths that nobody tells you when you're seventeen and kneeling in a cold chapel while a bishop drips oil on your head: an oath doesn't live in the words. The words are just the receipt. An oath lives in the body. It lives in the way you stand, the way you hold a line, the way your feet know left from right before your mind weighs in. For twenty-two years my body had belonged to the crown the way a sword belongs to its smith. And now I was asking it to do the one thing it had never been shaped for.

To stand still. To refuse. To be, in the oldest and most shameful sense of the word, a deserter.

The first rank came around the switchback below me and stopped.

I knew every face in it. That was the cruelty of it. Captain Aldous Reeve, who'd pulled me out of the Sedgeford mud when I was a green lieutenant and my horse had gone down under me. Bren of the Mill Road, who owed me a month's wages from a card game and paid his debts like scripture. The twins, Harl and Osric, who finished each other's sentences and each other's brawls. Seventy men, and I could have named sixty-five of them, and every one of them had eaten at my fire.

"Dain." Reeve's voice carried up the stair the way it always did, flat and carrying, a voice built for battlefields. He'd taken the company after me. He'd earned it, too, which made this harder, because I couldn't tell myself they were led by a fool. "Dain Corvath. Step aside."

"Good morning, Aldous," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You look well. The promotion agrees with you."

"Don't." He said it tiredly. "Don't do the thing where you talk until we all feel like idiots. I've watched you do it to tax collectors. Step aside, Corvath. The inquisitor has a writ. The woman on the stair is under arrest for treason, heresy, and unlawful gift."

"Which order would you like those in?" I asked. "I only ask because treason's the only one with a real punishment anymore. The other two are just dressing."

"Dain."

"Aldous." I let the word sit there a moment. Then I did the thing I'd been rehearsing since dawn, the thing that had kept me awake half the night while Frost watched me with her yellow eyes and judged me silently, as is the wolf way. "I'm not stepping aside. And I'm not fighting you either. I want that understood before anyone's pride gets involved. I will not draw this sword against the Third Company. I will also not move. You'll have to go through me, and I'll make it expensive, and then you'll have to explain to the crown why you killed a knight on a holy mountain over a woman who hasn't hurt a single soul."

Reeve's jaw worked. Behind him, the ranks shifted. Seventy men learning, in real time, that the man on the stair was serious.

"You were at the trial," he said. "You heard what she read out. The confession. You heard what her grandmother did."

"I heard every word." I had, too. I'd stood in the back of the great hall in my stolen guard's cloak and listened to Freya Joubert read her grandmother's confession in a voice that didn't shake, and I'd watched the faces of the court, and I'd thought: *there it is. The moment the world changes or doesn't.* "I heard a family buy a guild's trading privileges by breaking a pact older than the crown. I heard the crown take the guild's money for two hundred years and never once ask where the privileges came from. You want to talk treason, Aldous? Ask who broke the covenant first. It wasn't the girl on the stairs. It was the men who signed the ledger."

"The inquisitor says—"

"I know what the inquisitor says." And here was the thing I had to be careful with, because I meant it and because it was true and because truth, spoken carelessly, is just another kind of weapon. "Serah Vane watched ravens kill her family when she was a child. I've read her file. I broke into the archive for it, which is its own confession, so let's not pretend I'm standing on clean stone. She's not a liar, Aldous. She's a woman with a wound who's been told the whole world is the knife. But the ravens that killed her family were coerced. There was a magistrate. Freya found the proof. The beasts didn't betray her—the men did. The men always do."

I hadn't meant to say that last part out loud. It came out anyway, and it went down the stair like a dropped coin, and I watched it land in faces I knew. Harl and Osric looked at each other. Bren's shield dipped an inch.

"Vane says the woman's lying," Reeve said, but his voice had lost its flatness.

"Vane said a lot of things six months ago," I said. "Vane is at the back of this column on a grey mare, and I'll tell you why. It's not because she's a coward. It's because she's not sure. I watched her buy us time at the prison gate with her own badge. I watched her let us ride. She's not sure, Aldous, and she's up there in the rear because she's hoping we'll all be sensible before she has to decide what she believes. Don't make her decide. Don't make any of you decide. Just—wait. Wait one hour. The woman's climbing to the Spire to do something that either fixes every wrong thing in this realm or kills her. Either way, in an hour you'll know. Is an hour worth seventy men's blood on a holy mountain?"

The wind came up the switchback and moved the pines below, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind and the creak of leather and the small, human noises of seventy men standing in armor longer than they wanted to.

It was Bren who broke it. "Corvath." He said it the way he used to say it across a card table, half a complaint, half a joke. "You always were a bastard with the talking."

"Bren of the Mill Road," I said, "you still owe me eleven crowns."

"Nine. You counted the pot wrong and you know it."

"Ten. And if you put that shield down, I'll knock it to eight."

Someone laughed. It wasn't a big laugh, but it was a human one, and it did more damage to the column's discipline than any speech I could have made. Because that's the secret about soldiers that officers hate: we don't fight for writs. We fight for the man next to us, and the man next to us was laughing at a card debt on a stair where he was supposed to be dying.

"Company," Reeve said, "stand fast."

And then, because Reeve was Reeve, and because I had taught him half of what he knew, he did the thing I'd been dreading and respecting in equal measure. He handed his shield to the man beside him, unbuckled his sword belt, and climbed the first three steps toward me, alone, unarmed.

"Dain." Quietly now. Just the two of us, and seventy men watching, and a wolf at my side who had risen to her feet with her hackles up, and whom I stilled with one hand without looking, because that's the other thing nobody tells you about oaths—you can make new ones with your hands before your head agrees. "I'm going to ask you once, and I want the truth, because you never lied to me in twelve years and I'll know if you start. Is she right? About all of it? The covenant, the beasts, the keys, the Spire?"

I thought about lying to make it easier. I thought about the soft answer, the one that would let him turn his company around with his pride intact.

"Yes," I said. "She's right. And it's going to cost her everything, and the least I can do—the least any of us can do—is make sure she doesn't have to pay it alone."

Reeve looked at me for a long time. Then he looked past me, up the stair, toward where Freya had gone, though she was long out of sight. Then he looked down at his own hands, which were empty for the first time in twenty years, and I watched a man decide something that no writ and no inquisitor and no crown could undo.

"Company," he said, without turning around. "Stack shields."

The sound of seventy shields being stacked on stone is not a dramatic sound. It's a clattering, clumsy, human sound, and it will stay with me longer than any battle cry I ever heard. They came up the stair in ones and twos after that, and they sat down on the steps of the Spire like pilgrims, like tired men, like brothers, and Bren found a place beside me and said, "Eight crowns, final offer," and Frost put her head on his knee and he went very still and said, in a small voice, "Corvath, there's a wolf touching me," and I said, "Yes. There are a great many wolves touching all of us now. Get used to it."

I looked up the mountain. Somewhere above, in the light that was starting to come off the Spire like heat off summer stone, Freya Joubert was paying whatever price the old covenant demanded. I could feel it starting—the pressure in the air, the hush, the sense that every living thing on the mountain had turned its face toward the summit.

I had lost an oath that morning. I'd expected to feel lighter. Instead I felt the weight of a new one settling onto my shoulders, unasked, unsworn, written in no ledger.

Below us, far down the trail, Serah Vane turned her grey mare and rode away from the mountain, alone, at a walk—and I understood that she wasn't leaving.

She was going to tell the crown. And the crown would come.
