## 21. A New Law, Written Openly

### Freya

The crown's scribes arrived at dawn with forty pounds of vellum, which I found darkly funny, given where I'd learned my trade. They spread their documents across the long table in the Hall of Accord—formerly the Hall of Judgment, renamed overnight by a king who had learned, rather suddenly, that walls have ears and ears have teeth.

"You'll want to read every clause," the chief scribe said, dipping his quill. "His Majesty insists on precision."

"His Majesty insisted on burning us a month ago," I said. "Precision is an improvement."

The scribe's hand trembled. I didn't blame him. The last time a quill had trembled in that hall, it had been writing my death warrant, and the ink had barely dried before three hundred rats had marched across the dais like a small, squeaking army of the damned. Kings remember such things. Scribes remember them more vividly, because scribes have to write them down.

I read the Accord. All of it. Clause by clause, because I had spent my whole life watching powerful people hide things in the fine print, and I was done being surprised.

*No soul shall be registered, branded, taxed, or burned for the manner of their hearing or speaking.* Good.

*No beast shall be compelled to serve against its nature.* Better.

*The bond between the realms shall be kept by an Anchor, whose voice is given to the covenant, and whose word shall stand as law in matters between human and animal kind.* That was me. That clause was me, and every time I read it my stomach did a small, traitorous flip, because I had agreed to it at the top of a burning Spire with my voice already half-wolf and half-hawk and half a hundred other things that don't fit in one throat, and I had not, strictly speaking, been thinking about paperwork.

"Anchor Joubert." The scribe again, careful. "There is a matter of the enforcement provision. The crown proposes an office. An honest enforcer of the Accord, answerable not to the throne but to the covenant itself."

"And who do they propose?"

He looked at me the way men look at a door they suspect is trapped. "Inquisitor Vane."

I laughed. I confess it freely—I laughed until the scribe's quill stopped dripping and a raven on the windowsill gave me a look of professional disapproval. Serah Vane, the woman who had hunted me across half the realm, who had arrested my only friend, who had put a child in chains, now proposed as the first guardian of the law that ended the hunting of children.

But here is the thing I had learned in the Spire's fire, and learned again in the quiet after: the people most dangerous to a corrupt system are the ones who believed in it honestly. Vane had never been a hypocrite. She had been wrong—terribly, catastrophically wrong—but she had been wrong the way a blade is wrong when it's aimed by a liar. Give her the truth and she would cut for it.

"Send her in," I said.

* * *

### Serah

I had rehearsed this. I had rehearsed it in my cell, in the corridor, in the antechamber while a page kept trying to bring me wine I did not want. I had rehearsed an apology, a defense, a resignation, and a speech about justice, and I had abandoned all of them in order, one by one, like a soldier discarding armor that no longer fits.

What I had not rehearsed was the smell of the hall. Beeswax and parchment, and underneath it, faintly, animal—the whole building had taken on the scent of a stable since the Reforging, as if the city itself had remembered it was made of wood and wool and horse. It should have disgusted me. Once it would have.

The ravens had killed my family. I had built ten years of fire and iron on that truth, and it had been true, every word of it—ravens had killed my family. What I had never asked, in ten years of asking everyone else everything, was *who had asked them to*. The answer had been a magistrate named Corvel, dead now by his own hand, and I carried his name the way other people carry a stone in the boot: small, constant, maddening.

Freya Joubert sat at the end of the table with a ledger open in front of her, because of course she did. Even now. Even as the Anchor of a covenant older than the crown, she was doing bookkeeping.

"You want me to say something," she said.

"I want to know why you'd trust me."

"I don't." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Trust isn't the point anymore. That's rather the whole point. The old law ran on trust in the crown, and look where it got everyone. The new one runs on something else. Loyalty, chosen. Truth, spoken openly. You lied to no one, Vane. You just served liars. There's a difference, and I've decided it matters."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you refuse, and you walk out of here a free woman, and the office goes to someone else, and you spend the rest of your life knowing what Corvel did and doing nothing about it." She turned a page. "I've been invisible my whole life because I thought safety was the same thing as silence. I don't recommend it."

I looked at the document. *Enforcer of the Accord.* Not inquisitor. Not hunter. The word *enforce* had a different weight when what you enforced was a promise instead of a fear.

A hound lay by the door—one of the palace hounds, I knew it, a brute called Marrow who had once dragged a fugitive out of a river for me. It was watching me with its head on its paws, and it did not growl. It did not bare its teeth. It simply watched, and I understood that it was waiting to see what I would do, and that it would remember.

"I have conditions," I said.

Freya smiled for the first time. "Of course you do."

### Dain

They gave me back my sword.

I want that recorded properly, because it was not a ceremony. There was no herald, no kneeling, no king's hand on my shoulder. A clerk came to the barracks where I was mending a saddle—my own saddle, my own mare, my own hands, because I had discovered in the last month that I liked working for myself—and handed me a bundle of oilcloth and said, "From the Office of the Accord. Sign here."

Inside was my sword, the one they'd taken when they took my knighthood, and a letter of reinstatement, and a second letter offering me a captaincy, and a third letter, which I read last because I already knew what it said: *the crown would be honored to have the Defender of the Stair serve again.*

The Defender of the Stair. That was what they were calling it now, the songs already being sung in the taverns, how Ser Dain Corvath stood alone on the Spire's stair against forty men and would not move. What the songs leave out is that I stood there because I had no idea whose side I was on, and my body had finally, after thirty years, made the decision my head couldn't. What the songs also leave out is that two of those forty men were friends of mine, and that one of them, Bram Holt, had lowered his spear and said, "Step aside, Dain," and I had said, "No," and he had said, "I know. I'm sorry. Me neither," and sat down on the stair, and then three more had sat down, and that was how the battle was lost—not to me, but to the sitting.

I signed for the sword. I declined the captaincy. I kept the letter of reinstatement, folded, in the inside pocket where a man keeps things he isn't ready to burn.

Then I went and found the mare, because the mare had opinions, and the mare had been right about everything from the beginning.

"You knew," I told her, tightening the girth. She flicked an ear. "Before I did. Before any of it. You wouldn't let me kill her in the marsh, and I thought you'd gone soft." I swung up. "I'm not saying thank you. I'm saying you were right, which is worse."

She carried me down through the city, past the Hall of Accord where the scribes were still working, past the market stalls where a boy was selling roasted chestnuts to a queue that included, patiently waiting, three crows and a badger. Nobody screamed. A month ago the badger alone would have caused a riot. Now the boy just charged the badger extra, and the badger paid, which raised questions I decided not to have.

At the gates, a guard stopped me. Not to bar my way—to hand me a summons.

"Office of the Accord," he said. "You're requested. Something about the Vellum Guild."

I looked at the seal. It wasn't the crown's. It was a drawing of an open book with a wolf's paw print on the page, and beneath it, in Freya's neat, ledger-straight hand: *Bring your oath. You'll want it for the reading.*

### The Guildmaster

Aldous Vell, guildmaster of the Vellum Guild, had spent forty years teaching apprentices that a ledger never lies. It was the first thing he told them, on their first day, standing in the counting room with its smell of ink and tallow: *the ledger never lies, which is why men lie to it.*

He had sold fourteen names to save himself. He knew the number because he had written it down, because he was, in the end, a bookkeeper to his bones, and a bookkeeper records. He had recorded the price Serah Vane's agents paid—three hundred crowns, a pardon, and the continuation of his trading privileges—and he had recorded, in the same hand, in the same neat columns, the names. Marta of the tannery, who could quiet dogs. The twins at the ferry, who spoke to fish. A boy in Southgate whose name he'd had to look up, which had somehow been the worst part, that he'd had to *look it up*, that the boy had been so invisible even to the man selling him.

Now he sat in the Hall of Accord, in the dock where traitors used to sit, and the Anchor of the covenant opened a ledger in front of him—his ledger, seized from his counting room—and began to read.

Not accusations. Entries.

"Fourth month, ninth day," Freya read. "Received of the Crown's inquisitor, three hundred crowns, against delivery of the following." She read the names. All fourteen. She read them slowly, and she read them correctly, pronouncing each one as if it mattered, and in the gallery sat eleven living people who heard their own names spoken aloud in a hall of law for the first time without fear attached.

Aldous had prepared defenses. He had prepared them for weeks, in his cell, the way he prepared everything: the necessity, the threat to the guild, the greater good of the many employed, the regret. He opened his mouth to begin.

And a rat came out from under the dock.

It sat up on its haunches not three feet from him, washed its face with both paws, and looked at him. Not at the hall. At *him*. And it spoke, in a voice like dry leaves, in the accent of the Vellum Guild's own cellars, where it had lived its whole life among his stores.

"You counted us too," it said. "In the inventory. *Vermin, one lot.* We heard you."

The hall made a sound Aldous had never heard a hall make—half gasp, half laugh, entirely merciless. And he found that his prepared defenses, so carefully kept, had the same quality as everything else in his ledgers: they balanced nothing. They had never balanced anything. He had simply never been audited before by the things he'd written off.

"Guilty," he heard himself say. "On all entries. Read them all."

### The Child

They called her Little Wren, which was not her name but had become it, the way names do when a crowd decides to love you. She was nine. She had spent six days in an inquisitor's cell and one week as the most famous person in the capital, and she had opinions about which was worse.

"They keep asking me to *say things*," she complained to Freya, kicking her heels against the bench in the Hall of Accord while the adults did the slow, tedious work of law. "The lords. They want me to talk to their horses and tell them what the horses think of them."

"And what do you tell them?"

"That the horses think they're fat." She grinned. "It's usually true."

Freya laughed, and Wren climbed onto the bench beside her, because that was the arrangement now, unspoken and unratified: Wren had appointed herself the Anchor's apprentice, and the Anchor had not yet found a way to explain to a nine-year-old that being the Anchor was not a trade one could take a girl on at. Mostly because every explanation she drafted sounded, when she tested it in her own head, exactly like the lies adults told children, and she had sworn off those.

Below them, the guildmaster's sentencing was read out: his assets to the reparations fund, his freedom to be earned back year by year in service—recording, of all things, testimony. The man who had sold names would spend his years writing down what the silenced had to say. Freya had suggested it herself. Justice, she was learning, was not a fire or a pardon. It was a shape you built and then lived inside.

Serah Vane stood at the foot of the dais in her new office's plain grey coat—no more white and gold, no more fire sigil—and swore the Accord's first oath. The hall went quiet for it, and the hound Marrow stood up, and every animal in the building, and it seemed to Freya every animal for a mile around, went still at once, the way a forest goes still, listening.

"I will enforce no law that requires a lie," Vane said, "and I will serve no master I have not chosen. This I swear, on the covenant, before witnesses."

A raven on the rafters said, clearly and distinctly, "Witnessed," and the hall erupted.

### Freya

Evening found me on the Hall's steps with a copy of the Accord in my hands, the ink finally dry, the seal finally set—a wolf's paw on an open book, because I had insisted, and because the scribes had learned by then that the Anchor's insistence was not a thing one argued with.

Dain sat down beside me on the cold stone, armor-less, sword across his knees, a man still learning how to be off duty after a lifetime of it.

"They offered you a captaincy," I said.

"They offered you a palace."

"I'm keeping the Spire."

"I'm keeping the mare." He turned the summons over in his fingers. "The guild thing. You want me there when they read the rest of the ledger?"

"I want you there because eleven people are going to hear the names of everyone who sold them, and someone in that room should be carrying a sword who isn't on either side." I looked at him. "Also, I want you there because you're my friend, and I've decided to practice saying so while saying it is still new and strange."

He was quiet a moment. "The oath I sold to the drowned-folk," he said. "I've been trying to remember what it felt like. I can't. It's like a tooth that's gone—you keep finding the hole."

"What does that tell you?"

"That what I have now weighs more." He stood, and offered me his hand, and I took it, and we went down the steps together into a city that smelled of horses and beeswax and, faintly, from the direction of the river, of salt—the drowned-folk's salt, carried up on a wind that had traveled a long way to find me.

The wind smelled of salt, and under the salt, of deep water, and under the deep water, of something older that had begun, very softly, to sing.
