## 22. The Voice That Is Never Alone

The first thing you should know about being the living anchor of a covenant older than kingdoms is that nobody warns you about the mornings.

I had imagined—back when I still imagined anything about this life—that binding my voice to every beast in the realm would feel like drowning. A great rushing tide of wings and teeth and whale-song pouring through me until there was nothing left of Freya Joubert but a hollow reed for the world to whistle through.

It is not that. It is mostly bookkeeping.

I wake before dawn because the gulls on the Spire's western face are gossips and they cannot bear to let a sunrise go unremarked. I wake to the hounds of the capital kennels reporting, in their solemn way, that the night watch passed without incident, though one of the younger dogs would like it noted that he did *not* bark at the moon, whatever his kennel-mates claim. I wake to the slow grey patience of the whales beyond Greyharbor, who think in seasons and consider my nightly silence between their messages a charming human eccentricity rather than an insult.

And then I get up, and I light the lamp, and I open the ledger.

Not the old ledger. The Vellum Guild's books of impossible things are ash now, or evidence, depending on which magistrate you ask. The ledger I keep is new, bound in grey leather Dain hunted down from a tanner who owed him a favor, which is how Dain acquires most things: not by asking, but by standing near people until their debts surface like fish.

*Promises Kept Between Realms*, the cover reads, in the child's looping hand, because she insisted on lettering it herself and I have learned that some battles are not worth fighting. Inside, in mine:

*Item the first: The wolves of the Thornwood promised passage to any speaker traveling under moonlight. Kept, eleven times this season.*

*Item the second: The crown promised the drowned-folk of Greyharbor clean water and no nets across the deep channel. Kept, four months running. The drowned-folk returned a drowned bell as thanks. It rings only when someone lies nearby. Serah Vane hates it.*

*Item the third: I promised the messenger hawk with the ruined wing that she would never carry another message she did not choose. She has chosen three. All were letters to me. She is vain and I adore her.*

That last one is true, and if you think a woman bound to the souls of every living beast should be above favoritism, then you have never been loved by a hawk, and I envy your peace.

* * *

Dain stayed.

I want to record that plainly, because he would never say it plainly himself, and because there was a season—I will admit it here, where only the ledger and the gulls can hear—when I expected him to leave. Men like Dain Corvath do not stay in places. They stand in doorways of them, armed and useful, waiting for the next oath to come collect them.

But his oath was spent. We paid it to the drowned-folk, salt and bone and thirty years of kneeling, and what grew back in its place was not an oath at all. Oaths are given. What Dain has now was chosen, which is a different animal entirely, and as a general rule I do not speak for animals unless they ask.

He keeps the Spire's stair. Not guards it—there is precious little left to guard against—but sweeps it, repairs it, argues with the stone guardian about the proper angle of the seventh step. The guardian, who tested me once with my grandmother's crimes and has since decided I am tolerable, answers him with long silences and the occasional grinding shift of masonry that everyone present understands to be sarcasm.

"You could live in the city," I told him once, over the stew he pretends he doesn't cook better than I do. "You're a hero of the Accord now. They'd give you a house with a roof that doesn't leak."

"The roof leaks," he said, "because you keep inviting birds in."

"They're *reporting*, Dain."

"One of them nested in your boot."

"That was a diplomatic visit."

He looked at me the way he looks at things he has decided not to argue with, which is a look I have come to treasure more than I can decently put in a ledger, and said nothing, and stayed, and that was the whole conversation. Some loyalties are so quiet you only hear them when you press your ear to the floor of ordinary days.

The crown offered him reinstatement, of course. Full honors, sword polished, name struck from the disgrace rolls. He sent back a letter two lines long. I know because Serah Vane read it aloud to me while trying very hard not to smile, which for Serah is roughly equivalent to weeping with joy.

*"I already serve a higher authority. She snores."*

I do not snore. The wolves confirm I do not snore. But the wolves also find Dain hilarious, so their testimony is compromised.

* * *

The child comes on the first market-day of every month, riding up from the lowlands in the cart of a turnip farmer who has, since the Reforging, discovered that his oxen work twice as hard if he asks politely, and who has become something of a evangelist for politeness as a result.

Her name is Wren. Of course it is. The realm appears to have a finite supply of names and simply redistributes them among the gifted according to some law I have not yet found in the covenant's terms.

She arrives loud, always. She arrives with pockets full of things—a crow feather, a river stone "that wants to see the top of the Spire," half a honeycomb she swears the bees *gave* her, and I believe her, because the bees confirmed it, smugly—and she arrives with questions, which are the true cargo.

"Freya. Freya. If the covenant says truth spoken openly, does that mean I have to tell Mistress Aldery the pie was only *mostly* stolen?"

"Yes."

"But it was going to be thrown out."

"It was cooling on a windowsill, Wren."

"Cooling is nearly thrown out."

I make her write it in the small ledger I gave her—her own book of promises kept—and she writes *ITEM: TOLD THE TRUTH ABOUT PIE. MISTRESS ALDERY LAUGHED, WHICH WAS UNEXPECTED,* and then she sits with me on the stair while the crows deliver the morning's intelligence, and for a little while the Spire sounds like a home instead of a monument.

Because that is what the crows do now. Someone—Serah suspects a specific conspiracy of six birds operating out of the capital's bell tower—has organized the corvid population into something between a news service and a scandal sheet. They report troop movements and grain prices and, with relish, the private embarrassments of magistrates. Last month they informed the entire realm that Lord Chancellor Merrick, who voted against the Speaker's Accord three times before abstaining, talks to his cat in a baby voice and calls her "the Little Chancellor."

Merrick has since voted yes on everything.

"I don't control them," I told Serah, when she came up the mountain to lodge a formal complaint about crow-based journalism.

"I know," she said. "That's what terrifies me. It used to be that power listened to no one. Now it listens to *birds*, and the birds listen to *everyone*, and somehow the realm is better governed than it was under the old law." She paused at the landing, wind pulling at her grey coat, and added, quieter, "My sister kept pigeons. Before."

Before. Before the ravens, before the magistrate who coerced them, before she built a life out of the certainty that gifts were plagues. She did not finish the sentence. She didn't need to. The covenant hears all of it anyway—the sentences people don't say, carried in the pulse of blood and breath—and I have learned to hold those gently and never repeat them, even to the ledger.

Especially to the ledger. Some things are not mine to write.

* * *

Here is what the binding actually is, for the record, since Wren asked once with her head tipped and her eyes too old for her face, the way gifted children's eyes go old early:

It is not that I hear every beast at once, all the time. That would have burned me to a wick inside a year. It is more like this: imagine you lived your whole life in a walled house, alone, and then one day the walls became windows—not clear ones, ones you could look through when you chose, and through which others could look in when they needed you. The wolf-pack's grief when a hunt fails. The ox's contentment in a warm barn. The rat's ancient, unglamorous pragmatism. The hawk's contempt for everyone, including me, especially me.

And beneath all of it, constant, the hum of the whole—the knowledge that the silence I thought was the natural state of the world was in fact a wound, and that I spend my days now inside the healing of it.

Loneliness, I have learned, was never the absence of people. It was the absence of being *heard*. I spent twenty years invisible in a guild of impossible things, balancing accounts of bottled storms and borrowed shadows, and I thought invisibility was safety. It was safety the way a locked room is safe. Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out. You starve in perfect security.

Now the walls are windows and the door has no lock and the gulls wake me at dawn to tell me about the sunrise, and I would not trade one morning of it.

* * *

Autumn came, and with it the anniversary, and with it a delegation from the Vellum Guild—or what remains of it, rechartered under the Accord as a house of mediation rather than trade. Impossible things, it turns out, should never have been commodities. They should have been witnesses.

The guildmaster came with them.

He is smaller than I remember, or perhaps I am larger. He stood at the foot of the Spire's stair with his ledgers of sold names in a satchel at his hip—the originals, surrendered to the tribunal, every page of fourteen lives priced and traded—and he looked up at me the way men look at weather they failed to predict.

"Freya," he said. "I—" And then he stopped, because the stone guardian shifted its weight, and the crows went silent along the parapet in that way they have, and the whole mountain leaned in to hear whether the truth would be spoken openly or the old way, the careful way, the way that built the trap I once called sanctuary.

"I was afraid," he said finally. "I sold fourteen people because I was afraid, and I dressed it up as prudence, and I have brought every record I ever falsified to give back to the people it cost."

It was not eloquence. It was not even, fully, courage—it arrived late and dragged its feet the whole way. But the covenant does not require eloquence. It requires truth, spoken openly, and loyalty chosen rather than commanded, and I watched a man choose it on my doorstep, trembling, and I wrote it in the ledger that evening:

*Item the ninth: The guildmaster confessed without conditions. The tribunal weighed it. The fourteen names were read aloud in the capital square, each one, each price paid for it, and the crowd did not riot. They listened. Wren says listening is the hardest magic. Wren may be right.*

He asked, at the end, if I could forgive him. I told him the truth, because that is the law now and I am its anchor whether I like it or not: forgiveness is not mine to dispense wholesale. It belongs to the fourteen, and to time. But I could offer him this—the same thing the covenant offers everyone, beasts and kings alike.

A hearing. A voice. An end to silence.

He wept on the stairs. Dain swept around him without comment and fixed the seventh step.

* * *

Winter, and the whales came close to shore.

They do this now, sometimes—come into the shallows off Greyharbor in the grey months, and sing, and the whole harbor town comes down to the seawall to listen, fishermen with their caps off, children on their fathers' shoulders. The songs are old ones, mostly, older than the covenant's breaking, things they had stopped singing when there was no one on land who could hear. Now they sing them back into the world, and the world holds still to receive them.

I went down for the singing, because the whales asked, and because Dain pretended he wanted to inspect the harbor's breakwater and I pretended to believe him, and we stood together on the seawall in the cold while the songs rolled in like weather.

"What was it like," he asked, "the day it changed? The Reforging. You've never said."

I considered lying, for tradition's sake. Then I considered that the entire coastline could fact-check me.

"It was like coming home to a house I'd been told had burned down," I said. "And finding every light lit, and everyone waiting, and my grandmother's ghost in the corner holding the debt she'd left me—and realizing the debt wasn't a chain. It was a key. Somebody just had to pick it up."

"And the cost? Your voice, forever shared. Wasn't it—"

"Loud?" I laughed. "Everyone asks. No. Here's the secret, Dain, the one they don't put in the proclamations." I turned to look at him, sea-wind in my face, gulls wheeling overhead with opinions. "I was never alone in my own head again. Do you understand what that's worth? Twenty years of hiding behind ledgers, of soothing wounded hawks in the dark and telling no one, of being *safe*—and the whole time, the cure for all of it was just—company. Company at a scale no one had thought to try."

He was quiet a long moment. Out past the breakwater, a whale breached, enormous and deliberate, and came down in a sheet of white spray, and every child on the seawall screamed with joy.

"You could have had that," I said, gentler. "The Accord gives every speaker the bond now, if they want it. You never took it. Why?"

He looked out at the water. His hands, scarred and steady, rested on the stone.

"A knight takes oaths," he said at last. "I took mine to you. One voice, one loyalty, chosen." A pause, and then, in the tone he uses for things that cost him: "Besides. If I could hear what the horses say about me, I couldn't bear it."

"Dain. They *adore* you."

"I know," he said. "That's worse."

* * *

So: the ledger, the lamp, the stair, the sweep of ordinary days.

Wren visits monthly and lies about pie and is slowly, gloriously learning that her gift is not a thing to hide but a hand to extend. Serah enforces the Accord with a severity tempered, lately, by the drowned bell that rings whenever she is tempted toward her old certainties; she has taken to arguing with it, which the whole capital finds deeply reassuring. The guildmaster files his confessions and plants a garden outside the rebuilt guildhall, and the garden's rabbits report that he talks to them, awkwardly, hopefully, learning. The crows run their scandal-sheet empire unchecked. The wolves send word each solstice: *the Thornwood remembers you. Come run.* One day I will. The mountains are patient, and so am I, and patience, I have discovered, is just loyalty to the future.

And every morning I wake before dawn, and light the lamp, and open the grey leather book, and write down what was kept.

*Item the last, for this volume:* Tonight a child in the lowlands heard her first whisper from a sparrow, and laughed, and her mother wept, and neither of them will ever have to burn for it. The silence is ending everywhere, one small voice at a time. Somewhere out there, other anchors are waking—Wren among them, someday, if she chooses it, and she will choose it, and I will be there to hand her the pen.

The gulls tell me dawn is coming. The kettle is on. Dain is pretending not to cook.

Home, it turns out, was never a place you hid in.

It is the place where every voice knows yours—and stays to hear what you'll say next.
