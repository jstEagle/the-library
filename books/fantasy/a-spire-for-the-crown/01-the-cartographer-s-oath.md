## 1. The Cartographer's Oath

The ink was still wet when Lena Brightwater heard the knock at her door.

She did not startle. Ten years in the Thornfell mountains had taught her that a cartographer who startled at every sound died young, or worse, drew sloppy lines. But she did set down her pen with deliberate care, cap the bottle of iron-gall ink, and cover the map on her table with a sheet of oiled canvas before she crossed the room.

"Who is it?"

"Marrowgate's courier," came the muffled answer. "With the seal, as agreed."

Lena unlatched the door and let in a blast of mountain air and a young man wrapped in a gray wool cloak gone dark with mist. He stamped snow from his boots on her threshold without being asked—she'd have scolded him otherwise—and held out a leather dispatch case bearing the wax stamp of the Free City: a bridge over three rivers, pressed deep and unbroken.

"You're later than the contract," she said, taking it.

"The pass below Kestrel Ridge is washed out. We had to come around by the low road." He said it lightly, the way people said things they didn't understand the weight of. The low road added two days. The washed-out pass might well have killed him if the rains had come a week earlier.

Lena weighed the dispatch case in her hand. Payment for the finished commission, plus a bonus clause for early delivery—which she would not earn now, thanks to the very roads she was paid to improve. There was an irony in that she chose not to mention.

"Warm yourself by the stove," she said. "I'll fetch the work."

Her cottage clung to the shoulder of Mount Cael like a lichen to stone—one room below, one above, a chimney that smoked sideways in the wind, and a view south over the whole spine of the Thornfells. From the window over her drafting table she could see forty miles of ridgeline on a clear day, down to where the peaks softened into the hill country and the free cities began. On most days the clouds took it all, and she worked by memory instead, which suited her fine. Memory was the one map no courier could steal.

The commission had taken her four months. Marrowgate wanted a full survey of their northern approaches: every road, every ford, every defensible height between the city walls and the high passes, rendered at a scale fine enough that a garrison commander could plan a march from it. It was honest work, well paid, and utterly safe—except for the part that wasn't.

Because there was a pass through the Thornfells that appeared on none of Marrowgate's maps. Not because no one had found it. Because no one *could* find it. It opened only to certain eyes, walked only under certain feet, and closed behind its travelers like water closing over a stone. Lena had found it eleven years ago, half-lost in a whiteout, dying of cold, when something in the mountains had looked at her and decided—not unwisely, she sometimes thought—that she was worth keeping alive.

They called it the Veil Pass. She called it nothing at all, because naming things made them real, and real things got people killed.

And she had drawn it anyway. Every switchback, every scree field, every spring that ran sweet even in high summer. Four months of work, and the true map lay beneath the false one on her table, and in two hours she would burn it, and no one would ever know it had existed except her.

That was the oath. She'd sworn it alone, at nineteen, kneeling in the snow with frostbite eating her fingers: *never be seen.* Never show what she could do. Never draw what only she could walk. Because the world had a long memory for useful miracles, and kings and councils alike treated miracles the way miners treated ore veins—something to be dug out, used up, and left hollow.

She climbed the narrow stair to her workroom. The false map waited on the table, pinned at its corners, beautiful and complete and entirely innocent. The true map lay beneath it, face-down, and she did not look at it as she lifted it away. Looking made it harder. Looking made her want to keep it, and keeping it meant hiding it, and hiding it meant one day explaining it, and explanations were how gifted people ended up in chains or graves.

She rolled the false map, sleeved it in oilcloth, tied it with cord, and carried it downstairs.

* * *

The courier drank her tea and admired her view and asked, as couriers always did, whether the roads were safe this season. Lena gave him the answers she always gave—the passes were open, the wolves were bold near the sheep farms, the crown's riders had been seen less often lately, which some took for peace and she took for the opposite. A hunter who stops showing himself has usually stopped hunting rabbits.

He left an hour past noon, and she watched him pick his way down the goat track until the mist folded him away. Then she went back inside, barred the door, and stood for a long moment in the middle of her own kitchen, listening to the silence settle back into place.

This was the hour she hated. Not the burning itself—that was quick, almost merciful—but the minutes before it, when the house was empty and the work was done and there was nothing between her and the knowledge of exactly what she was.

Alone. Hidden. Safe, and small, and safe.

She built the fire up in the hearth until it roared, then fetched the true map from upstairs and unrolled it across her knees in the firelight.

It deserved better than burning. That was the bitter joke of it. It was the finest thing she had ever drawn. The Veil Pass ran down its length like a vein of silver, and around it she had charted not just the roads but the *truths* of the mountains—the way the light fell into certain valleys at certain hours, the places where the stone hummed faintly under a palm laid flat, the springs that never froze. No guild surveyor could have produced it. No guild surveyor could have even *seen* half of it. It was a map of a country that existed only for her, and it was perfect, and it was hers, and in ten minutes it would be ash.

*Never be seen.*

She thought, as she always did at this moment, about her mentor. Master Corvin had taught her letters and latitudes, inks and instruments, back when she'd been a orphan girl with quick hands and no family name worth speaking. He'd taught her the guild oath too: *a map serves truth, and truth serves all people.* Corvin was the only living soul who knew what she could do—had known since she was sixteen and had come home from a "walk" through a pass that didn't exist, and he had looked at her for a long time and then simply said, *Then we will make sure no one ever asks you to prove it.*

He lived two valleys east now, old and half-deaf, tending his bees and his archive of forbidden surveys. She wrote to him twice a year. She had not visited in fourteen months. She told herself it was caution. She knew it was also cowardice, because visiting him meant sitting across from the one person who understood, and understanding was a kind of mirror, and mirrors showed you what you were actually doing with your life.

Burning maps. Keeping small. Staying safe while somewhere beyond the mountains, the world grew teeth.

She fed the map to the fire corner first, the way you feed anything precious to a flame—slowly, so the heat doesn't leap and take more than you mean to give. The vellum browned, curled, blackened. The silver vein of the Veil Pass glowed briefly orange, like a coal remembering fire, and then it was gone.

"There," she said quietly, to the hearth, to the mountains, to no one. "Never seen."

The silence answered, as it always did, with nothing at all.

* * *

She spent the afternoon on honest work. There was always honest work—a boundary dispute for a shepherd below the ridge, a trail chart for the trappers who wintered at Hollowmere, corrections to her master survey of the Kestrel watershed. Her reputation among the free cities was modest and respectable: Lena Brightwater, reliable, meticulous, unremarkable. A woman who drew good maps and asked few questions. She had spent a decade building that reputation the way you build a wall—one dull, careful stone at a time.

Toward dusk she took her evening walk, because she always took her evening walk, up the switchback path behind the cottage to the granite shelf she called the Ledge. The clouds had torn open in the west, and the whole southern sky burned gold and rose above the blue-black ranks of the peaks. Somewhere down there, forty miles and a lifetime away, Marrowgate's council chamber would be receiving her map, and some clerk would smooth it flat and say, *Brightwater's work, good as ever,* and think no more about it than one thinks about rain.

She sat down on the cold stone and let herself feel the pride. That was the ritual too—she permitted herself this much. Four months of work, flawless, delivered. She was the best cartographer in the Thornfells, and no one knew, and the not-knowing was the price of the knowing, and she had paid it so long she'd almost stopped feeling the weight.

*Almost.*

Below her, far off, a thread of smoke rose from the valley of Ashcombe—one of the small villages, a dozen roofs and a shrine. Woodsmoke, probably. Evening fires.

Except evening fires rose straight in still air, and this one leaned hard south, ragged, and there was more than one column of it.

Lena stood.

She counted without meaning to—old habit, the gift sharpening everything it touched. Three columns. Four. Too many for cooking, too wrong for kilns, and Ashcombe had neither forge nor kiln large enough to explain them. The wind brought nothing at this distance, but her eyes, her *gifted* eyes, picked out along the valley road a line of dark shapes moving in formation. Horsemen. Uniformed. Moving with the unhurried confidence of men who feared nothing in these mountains because nothing in these mountains had ever been allowed to fear them.

Crown riders. In the free territories. Burning a village that raised sheep and mended nets.

Her hand went to the ledge beside her, pressing flat against the granite, and the stone hummed faintly under her palm—the way it always did, the way it had hummed for eleven years, the quiet voice of the mountains that only she seemed able to hear. And for the first time she could remember, the humming felt less like comfort and more like warning.

*Never be seen,* she had sworn. But the oath had never said anything about what to do when the seeing came for other people.

The riders were still a day's travel from her valley. Perhaps less, if they rode through the night. Lena stood on the Ledge a long time, watching the smoke climb and shred against the gold sky, and felt the decade of careful, hidden, small safety begin—stone by stone—to crack.

Then she turned and went down the mountain, fast, to pack.
