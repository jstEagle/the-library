## 2. Smoke Over Thornfell

The smoke reached Lena before the news did.

She had been three days on the high shoulder of the Thornfells, re-surveying the melt-line above Hollowmere for a grazing charter, when she saw it: a gray smear against the blue of the southern sky, too wide for a chimney fire, too steady for lightning. She stood on the ridge with her brass theodolite balanced on one shoulder and watched it for a long time, the wind pulling at her hood, while something cold and patient settled into her chest.

Chimney smoke rose in threads. House fires rose in columns. This rose in columns.

She packed her instruments with hands that were, she noted distantly, perfectly steady. Ten years of practice had taught her body to be calm while her mind ran ahead like a startled deer. By the time the theodolite was wrapped in oilcloth and slung into her pack, she was already moving, cutting off the survey trail and dropping toward the valley by a path that did not exist on any map she'd ever sold.

It existed only in her blood.

* * *

The village of Ashenbrook had been forty-one households when Lena had last mapped it, two springs ago. She knew it the way she knew all her work: the crooked lane that flooded every thaw, the mill with the wheel that sang a flat note, the shrine-stone at the crossroads worn smooth by a century of touching. She knew the names of the headman's dogs.

Now Ashenbrook was a black tooth in a green jaw.

She came out of the trees above the terraced fields and stopped, because the smell hit her first — wet ash and char and something underneath it that she refused, for three full breaths, to name. Then she named it, because a cartographer did not leave things unlabeled, and moved on down the slope with her jaw set.

The riders had come and gone. She could read the ground the way she read any ground: hoof-churned mud in the lane, the wide-spaced prints of warhorses, not ponies; the neat, deliberate pile of burned furniture in the square where they had made their pyre of the shrine-stone's wooden canopy; the door of every house marked with a smear of white paint in a shape she had only ever seen in old survey notes from her mentor's collection — a circle crossed by a line.

The mark of the Gifted. The mark the crown had started using again after forty years of letting it gather dust in archive drawers.

An old woman sat in the ruins of the mill, rocking, singing something tuneless to herself. A man was pulling nails from a collapsed beam, one by one, as if the nails mattered, as if nails were the problem. Two children stared at Lena from a doorway with the flat, unblinking look of children who had watched something they would spend the rest of their lives not talking about.

"Who?" Lena asked the man with the nails. "Who burned it?"

He didn't look up. "Crown riders. Grey cloaks. A woman in black with a silver chain." He pulled another nail. "They said we were harboring. They said someone reported a gifted child."

"Did you?"

He stopped then. He looked at her, and his eyes were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a map where the paper simply ended. "Does it matter?" he said. "They burned it anyway."

Lena crouched, took his hands — they were shaking now, though they hadn't been while he worked — and held them until the shaking slowed. "Which house?" she asked quietly. "The child. Which house was it?"

"Rell's house. At the end of the lane. But they took—" He swallowed. "They didn't find anyone. The Rells' boy wasn't there. The mother wouldn't say where he'd gone, so they—" His voice failed, and Lena finished the sentence for him, silently, in the privacy of her own skull, because someone had to.

She left the old woman her emergency rations and a strip of dried venison for the children, and she walked to the end of the lane, to the black skeleton of the Rell house, and she read the ground again, more carefully this time.

Small footprints. A child's, running. Not toward the road — toward the river, toward the rocks, toward the steep scramble up the goat-track where a grown rider on a warhorse could never follow. And there, pressed into the ash at the threshold, half a handprint, small, five fingers, where a child had braced himself against the doorframe to look back.

The print was outlined in faint silver light that faded even as she watched, like breath on cold glass.

Lena felt the cold in her chest spread outward, into her hands, her teeth. Not fear for herself. Not yet. Fear of a shape she had been dreading for ten years without ever drawing its outline: the day the hunt climbed into her mountains.

She had always known the lowlands would burn first. She had told herself the Thornfells were too far, too poor, too hard to reach — that the crown's long arm would tangle in the foothills and give up. It was a comfortable lie, and like all her best maps, she had drawn it beautifully and believed it completely.

The arm was not tangled. The arm was here.

* * *

She found the boy at dusk, four miles up the goat-track, wedged into a cleft in the rocks with his knees drawn up to his chin, so still that she nearly walked past him. Only the silver — that faint, telltale shimmer that clung to the Gifted when they were frightened, like static on a cat's fur — gave him away. It pulsed in time with his breathing.

Lena stopped six feet away and sat down on a rock, deliberately, the way you'd sit near a spooked animal.

"I'm not one of them," she said.

The boy said nothing. His eyes in the fading light were enormous and dry, which was somehow worse than crying. He was maybe eight. Small for eight. He had ash in his hair and a scratch down one cheek that had crusted brown.

"My name's Lena. I make maps. I came up from the valley." She let that sit. "I saw Ashenbrook."

The shimmer flared — grief and terror in one pulse — and the boy pressed himself deeper into the cleft, and the rock around him *hummed*, a low resonant note like a wet finger on a bowl's rim, and a hairline crack spidered out from where his shoulder touched stone.

Lena's breath caught. Not at the power — she had seen small workings before, hedge-cunning, charm-scraps, the little magics the old law had tolerated. She caught her breath because the crack was not random. It was a line. A clean, deliberate line, running three feet through solid granite, exactly the line a surveyor would draw.

*He doesn't know what he's doing,* she thought. *And the gift knows anyway.*

"Tam," the boy said at last. His voice was scraped raw. "My name's Tam."

"Hello, Tam." She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice she used on spooked mules and nervous clients. "Can I ask you something? Did you crack that rock on purpose?"

He shook his head, small and miserable.

"I didn't think so." She reached slowly into her pack and brought out the venison and the waterskin, and set them on the rock between them. "Here's the thing about gifts, Tam. They're like rivers. You can't stop them, and if you try to dam them up in a panic, they flood. What you need is a channel." She paused. "I know a little about channels."

The boy stared at the food, then at her. "The grey riders said—" His voice broke and reassembled itself. "They said people like me are why the crops failed in Marrowgate. They said we steal the luck out of the land. My mam said that was a lie, but—" He looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I broke the mill wheel last winter. Without touching it. I didn't mean to. And now they burned our house, and it's because of me, and Mam told me to run and I ran, I *ran*, I—"

The shimmer around him flared white, and the whole cleft groaned, and Lena did the only thing she could think of: she talked over the sound, fast and low, a cartographer's voice, a voice for naming things.

"Listen to me. Listen. The crops failed because of a dry wind off the Salt Reach and a council that sold the grain reserves, and I can show you the trade ledgers, because I've read them. The riders didn't burn your house because of what you are. They burned it because fear is a tool and you are a *useful* one." She leaned forward. "Your mother told you to run because she loved you. Running was the right call. Now the next call is mine, and the next call is: we go up. Higher. Tonight, in the dark, on a path the riders don't know exists, because it doesn't exist for anyone but me."

Tam's eyes went to the darkness beyond the cleft, to the black mass of the Thornfells rising against the stars. "Nobody can go up there," he whispered. "The shepherd said the high passes kill people."

"The shepherd's right." Lena allowed herself the smallest, grimmest of smiles. "For people who need maps. I don't need maps, Tam. I *make* them."

* * *

They climbed by moonrise.

Lena took him up the goat-track to where it died against a cliff face — and past it, onto a path that unrolled before her the moment she reached for it, the way it always did: a sensation like a door opening in her chest, like a key turning in a lock she'd been born carrying. The hidden ways of the Thornfells were not carved by any human hand. They were *remembered* into being, she had long suspected, by something older, and they answered her gift the way iron answers a lodestone.

She had never brought another living soul onto them. The rule had stood for ten years, carved as deep as any of her survey marks: *never be seen, never be followed, never share the ways.* Rules kept her alive. Rules kept everyone around her alive.

She broke the first one at moonrise and did not look back at the breaking.

Tam climbed well for a terrified child — better than well, she noted, with a surveyor's automatic precision; sure-footed, reading the rock, his fear burning off with exertion the way morning fog burns off a valley. Twice she caught the faint silver shimmer steadying him on loose stone, the gift acting like a hand on his back. Twice she pretended not to see, because a child should not be taught to fear the thing that was saving his life.

Near midnight they reached a hollow she knew — a shelf of granite under an overhang, screened by pine, with a view down the whole southern valley. She built no fire. She wrapped him in her spare cloak and sat beside him in the dark, sharing dried venison, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the mountain spoke for them: wind in the pines, the far complaint of some night bird, the tick and settle of stone.

Then Tam, small-voiced in the dark: "Will they come after me?"

"Yes."

The word came out of her before she could soften it, and she was glad of that. He had earned the truth in a burned house.

"But not up here," she went on. "Not tonight. And when they come, they'll be climbing ground I know and they don't, following roads that end where I say they end." She turned to look at him. The moon gave her just enough light to see his face, the ash still in his hair. "Do you know what's going to happen now, Tam? I'm going to take you somewhere no rider will ever find. And then I'm going to do something I should have done years ago."

"What?"

"Stop hiding." She said it and felt it land in her own chest like a stone dropped down a well — the splash of it coming up from somewhere deep, from ten years of burned maps and borrowed names and a loneliness she had called *safety*. "There's an old man in the high valley. Master Corvin. He taught me everything I know about — about channels." She almost smiled. "He's been telling me for a year that the world was changing, and I've been telling him that mountains don't change. Well." She looked down at the black smear that had been Ashenbrook, far below, invisible now except to someone who knew exactly where to look. "The mountains haven't. The world has."

Tam had gone very still. "The grey riders had a list," he said suddenly. "I heard them. Before I ran. A woman reading off a paper. She said a name, and the captain said *the old teacher? He's on the second list,* and they laughed."

The cold came back into Lena's chest, all at once, complete.

*Corvin.*

She was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, her body ten years ahead of her mind, already calculating — distance, moonlight, the hidden way down the eastern shoulder that would put her at Corvin's door by dawn if she ran the whole night and the gift was generous.

"Wait—" Tam's voice, small behind her. "You said you'd take me somewhere safe. You *promised*—"

Lena stopped. Stood in the dark with the wind pulling at her and the whole shape of the trap suddenly clear to her: the village burned to flush out the child, the child fled uphill, the child would lead them — or anyone asking after him — straight along the routes she had spent a decade keeping secret. And Corvin, old and slow and *on the second list*, alone in a valley with one door.

She could not be in two places. She had never hated that fact before. She hated it now with a fury that made her teeth ache.

She turned back. Crouched. Took the boy by the shoulders, and felt the faint static of his fear crawl up her arms, and did not let go.

"New plan," she said. "We go together, and we go fast, and you hold on to my pack and don't let go no matter what the mountain does. Can you do that?"

Tam nodded, wide-eyed.

"Then we're going to see an old man," Lena said, and shouldered her pack, and stepped off the shelf onto a path that no map had ever carried and no rider would ever find — *unless*, said a voice in her head, cold as the smoke over Ashenbrook, *unless they already have someone who can read what you've burned.*

She ran, and the mountain opened before her, and behind them, far to the south, a second column of smoke was rising against the stars.

It was coming from the high valley.

It was coming from Corvin's.
