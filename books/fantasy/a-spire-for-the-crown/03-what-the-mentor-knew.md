## 3. What the Mentor Knew

The house at the end of Lantern Row still smelled of ink and juniper smoke, the way it had when Lena was nine years old and too small to reach the drafting table without a stool. She stood in the doorway for a long moment before knocking, her knuckles hovering over wood gone silver with weather, and let herself remember—unwillingly, the way one touches a bruise—the last time she'd come here with a map in hand and pride in her chest.

That had been four years ago. Before the edicts. Before the first burnings in the lowlands that everyone in Thornfell called accidents.

She knocked twice, the old pattern: two quick, one slow, the rhythm Corvin had taught her so he'd know her knock from a client's or a stranger's. From inside came the scrape of a chair, the tap of a cane, and then the door swung open on Master Corvin as thin and upright as a winter birch, his white hair tied back with the same fraying cord he'd worn since she'd known him.

"You're late," he said.

"I wasn't coming."

"You were always coming." He stepped aside to let her pass. "You simply didn't know it yet."

* * *

The interior of the house had not changed in ten years, and that was its own kind of grief. The same charts hung from the rafters—coastlines of drowned kingdoms, trade routes through passes that no longer existed, a great wheel of the known world with the Thornfell range inked across its spine like a scar. The same brass instruments sat in their velvet grooves. The same kettle hung over the same small fire, and Corvin filled two cups without asking whether she wanted any, because he had never once needed to ask.

Lena set her satchel on the table but did not open it. "I finished the Marrowgate commission," she said. "The survey of the eastern watershed."

"I know. I read your letter." He lowered himself into his chair with the care of a man managing an old injury. "Three weeks ago. You said you'd found nothing unusual."

"Did I?"

"Word for word." His eyes, pale as river ice, held hers. "And then Doss's people came through last night carrying rumors of a cartographer who walked up the Sorrowmere valley in a fog thick enough to drown in, and came out the far side by morning, having charted a pass that appears on no map ever drawn."

Lena kept her face still. It was a skill she'd practiced as long as the gift itself: the flattening of expression, the careful emptiness. Never be seen. Never be known. She had written the rule herself at fourteen, in the back of her first field book, after the day Corvin caught her standing in the middle of a path that wasn't there, her hand trailing along a hedge of light only she could perceive.

"The Sorrowmere fog is treacherous," she said. "People wander in circles. Sometimes they wander out somewhere unexpected."

"Lena." He said her name the way he used to when she'd fudged a measurement—gently, and with terrible patience. "Sit down. Drink your tea. And stop lying to the one man in these mountains who has spent thirty years making sure you could."

Her hands wanted to shake. She wrapped them around the cup instead, and the heat of it grounded her the way it always had. Outside, wind moved through the pines above the town, and somewhere a dog barked twice and went quiet.

"How much do you know?" she asked finally.

"More than you think. Less than I should." He rose, crossed to the raftered charts, and pulled down one she had never seen—not because it was hidden high, but because it was hidden *well*: folded inside the false backing of a frame holding a perfectly ordinary map of Marrowgate's harbor. He laid it flat on the table between them, weighting the corners with his ink pots.

It was very old. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea, and the ink had faded to ghost-gray except where someone—Corvin, she realized, in a younger hand than she'd ever seen from him—had traced certain lines again and again until they stood dark. Those lines did not look like roads. They looked like veins. They radiated from a single point in the high Thornfells, a spire-shaped mark labeled in a script Lena did not recognize, letters that seemed almost to move when she wasn't looking directly at them.

"What is this?" she whispered.

"This is the oldest map in my keeping, and I have broken every oath of my order by showing it to you." Corvin sat down heavily across from her. "It was drawn six hundred years ago, before the Spire of Auren went silent. Look at the lines. Follow them."

She followed them. Her breath caught.

They were paths. Not roads—paths, winding in ways no human engineer would choose, threading through stone and across chasms, ignoring every sensible route a traveler might take. And she knew them. Not all of them, not even a tenth of them—but she knew the *kind*. She knew the way they felt under her feet when she walked them, that faint humming certainty, like following a melody only she could hear.

"I've walked some of these," she said. Her voice came out strange. Thin. "Or—I've walked things like them. Paths that aren't there. That only I can see."

"Yes."

"They're on a six-hundred-year-old map."

"Yes."

The tea had gone cold in her hands. She set it down because she was afraid she'd drop it. "Corvin. What are they?"

He folded his hands on the table, and for a moment he looked every year of his age, and more besides—a man standing at the edge of something he'd been circling for decades. "When I took you as an apprentice, you were a foundling girl with a talent for terrain and no family name anyone would claim. Brightwater was mine to give you. Did you know that? I chose it off a list of extinct lineages because it sounded clean, and because no one would go looking for a Brightwater in a mountain village." A dry, humorless smile. "Ironic, in hindsight. The name may be the truest thing I ever gave you."

"Corvin—"

"The spire-folk built the paths." He cut her off gently, the way he used to when her questions ran ahead of her courage. "Before men raised walls, before the free cities had names, the folk of the Spire of Auren walked this whole range as easily as you walk your own kitchen. Their ways were woven into the world itself—into stone, into light, into the space between one place and another. Humans couldn't see them. Couldn't use them. Until the covenant."

"Covenant." The word landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for it to hit bottom and heard nothing.

"There was an accord, long ago. Between the spire-folk and the first human realms. The terms are lost—all the histories burned, or were made to burn—but the shape of it survives in fragments like this map, in songs nobody understands anymore, in the oaths the old guilds swore without knowing why." He tapped one dark-traced vein of a path with a cracked fingernail. "What survived, practically speaking, was this: certain humans, in certain bloodlines, given the sight of the ways. Given the ability to walk where no one else can walk. The spire-folk called them keys, I believe. Living keys to their doors."

"And the gift—" Lena's mouth had gone dry. "The thing I've spent my whole life hiding. The thing that got Marta's daughter driven out of Callow Ford. The thing they're burning villages for now."

"Is a fragment of that covenant." He met her eyes without flinching, and she loved him for it and hated him for it in the same heartbeat. "You are not cursed, child. You are not marked by any demon. You are a thread—one of the last living threads—of an agreement older than every kingdom on this continent. And Aldric knows it. Or his creatures do. That is why the hunt has begun. That is why they take the Gifted alive, if they can."

"Alive," she repeated. "Villages burn, but the gifted children are taken alive."

"A key is useless melted down."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the evening bell rang from the chapel of Saint Aldous, and Lena became aware of how late it had gotten, how blue the light through the windows had turned, and how far she had walked today, and how far she would need to walk tomorrow if she took the advice she could already see forming behind her teacher's eyes.

"So what do I do?" she asked. It was not the question she wanted to ask. The question she wanted to ask was *why didn't you tell me years ago*, and she swallowed it, because she already knew the answer: knowing changed nothing, and knowing endangered everyone near her. Corvin had carried this alone for thirty years so that she could carry her own secret lightly. She owed him better than resentment.

"You run south," he said. "Tonight. Now, if you can bear to leave before full dark. There are ships out of Marrowgate—Doss owes you favors, and smugglers' debts are the only honest debts left in the world. Go past the free cities, past the Salt Marches, to the southern principalities. Aldric's writ doesn't run there. Yet." He pushed back from the table and began moving through the house with sudden purpose, pulling a canvas satchel from a chest, filling it with swift, practiced motions: dried meat, a flint, a roll of banknotes wrapped in oilcloth, a knife with a bone handle that Lena recognized as older than both of them. "Take the coast road as far as Grayhaven, then cut inland. Avoid the garrisons. Avoid churches—they're posting watchers in the chapels now, God forgive them."

"Corvin." She stood. "Come with me."

"No."

"Then I'm not going."

"You are going, and I am staying, and those two facts are not in conflict, however much you wish they were." He stopped in the middle of the room, satchel in hand, and looked at her with an expression she had seen perhaps three times in her life: once when she'd finished her apprenticeship chart, once when her mother died, and once, long ago, when she'd asked him why he'd never married, and he'd told her about a woman in Marrowgate and a fever and nothing else, ever again. "Lena. Listen to me. If we're seen leaving together, we're taken together, and everything I know dies in a crown cellar. If I stay, I am an old man with an unremarkable house full of unremarkable maps. I can lie. I've had practice."

"You're trembling."

"It's cold, and I'm old. Both are permitted." He pressed the satchel into her arms. It was heavier than it should have been, the way gifts from people who love you always are. "There's one more thing. In the satchel. A letter, sealed. Don't read it until you're south of the Salt Marches—or until everything has gone wrong, whichever comes first. If it goes wrong, the letter will tell you what to do next. Trust it as you'd trust me."

"What's in it?"

"The rest of what I know. Which is more than I've told you tonight, and less than either of us deserves." His voice roughened. "Go south, find the ships, live a long dull life drawing harbors for merchants who don't deserve you. Promise me."

She wanted to promise. The words rose in her throat and stuck there, because she had learned long ago—on the day she wrote *never be seen* in the back of her field book—that promises made in fear were the first ones broken. "I'll go," she said instead. "I'll take the coast road. I'll send word through Doss when I'm safe."

"That's not—"

"It's what I have."

For a long moment he searched her face, and whatever he found there made him close his eyes briefly, the way a man does when he accepts a defeat he saw coming. "Stubborn," he murmured. "I made you stubborn. My own fault." He touched her cheek with a hand like old paper. "You were the best thing I ever mapped, Lena Brightwater. Don't you dare let them fold you away."

She laughed, wetly, and hugged him—brief and hard, ink and juniper smoke, the smell of her entire childhood—and then she went to the door because if she stayed another minute she would not leave at all.

At the threshold she paused. "Corvin. The spire-folk. Where did they go?"

His face did something complicated. "That," he said quietly, "is in the letter."

* * *

She made it as far as the mill bridge before the bells began.

Not the chapel bell—she knew its voice, lazy and slightly cracked. This was the alarm bell on the guardhouse, rung fast and hard, the rhythm of *riders in the lane*, and beneath it she could hear hooves, many of them, coming up Lantern Row from the low road at a pace no lawful visit ever used.

Lena stood in the shadow of the millhouse, her heart slamming against her ribs, and made herself count. Six horses, by the sound. Heavy shod. Moving in formation. Crown riders, or worse—inquisitors rode heavy too, but they rode quiet, and these made no attempt at quiet. A proclamation ride, then. A show of force.

Except shows of force didn't turn up Lantern Row at dusk. Shows of force went to the square.

The hoofbeats stopped. She heard the jingle of harness, boots on packed earth, a voice giving orders in a tone like a blade being drawn slowly. Then fists on a door—the door she had walked out of twenty minutes ago—and Corvin's voice, muffled, calm, infuriatingly courteous: "Gentlemen, the hour is late. State your business."

"We have a warrant for the arrest of one Corvin of Lantern Row, on charges of sedition, concealment of the Gifted, and trafficking in proscribed knowledge. Open the door, old man, or we open it for you."

Lena's body moved before her mind agreed to anything. Three steps toward the bridge, toward the town, toward the row—and she forced them back. Forced herself into the millhouse shadow with her nails biting her palms. *Never be seen.* The rule she had written at fourteen. The rule that had kept her alive through every burning and every edict and every whisper. If she went back, she died with him, and everything he'd just poured into her arms—the satchel, the letter, the truth—died with her, and Tam Vessel's village would have burned for nothing, and the covenant would lose its last living thread to nothing but love, which was noble and which was also exactly what the inquisitors counted on.

Wood splintered. Glass broke. Corvin's voice rose once—not a scream, never a scream, he was too proud for screaming—but sharp with anger: "Careful with that frame, you barbarian, it's older than your king's whole—"

Something struck flesh. He grunted. Went quiet.

Lena pressed her back against the millhouse wall and shook, silently, tears cutting hot tracks down her cold face, while six shadows led one stumbling old man out of the house that smelled of ink and juniper smoke and marched him down Lantern Row toward the low road and the dark.

She watched until the last torchlight vanished. Then she opened the satchel, and her fingers found the sealed letter, and she nearly broke her own rule right there—nearly tore it open under the mill bridge with shaking hands—because everything had gone wrong, hadn't it, this was surely the *whichever comes first*—

But south of the Salt Marches, he'd said. South, where his words could still protect her. Breaking the seal here, in the mountains, with crown riders between her and the sea, would only spend his last gift too soon.

So she closed the satchel. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, the way her mother used to tell her to, and she turned north—not south, not yet, because the coast road ran through Callow Ford and Callow Ford had a garrison now, and there was a boy named Tam sleeping in a shepherd's hut above the valley who could not be left behind for men who took the Gifted alive.

Behind her, the house on Lantern Row stood open to the night, its lamps smashed, its charts scattered across the floor like fallen leaves.

Ahead of her, the hidden paths waited, faint and humming, leading where no rider could follow.

She stepped onto the first one, and the mountain closed around her like a secret kept.
