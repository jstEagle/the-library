## 4. The Usurper's Edict

The edict arrived at dawn, as cruel things often do.

Veyra Solenne read it standing in the courtyard of the Inquisitorial House at Caelmark, where the morning frost had silvered the flagstones and the crows were arguing over something dead in the gutter. She did not sit. She never sat to read the king's words; sitting made a body comfortable, and comfort was the first door through which doubt entered.

*By order of Aldric, First of His Name, rightful sovereign of the Nine Reaches and the Thornfell Marches: let it be known that the practice of the so-called Gift is hereby declared treason against the crown and covenant of men. All persons found possessing or concealing such power shall be taken, bound, and remanded to the Crown's judgment. Those who shelter them share their guilt. There will be no appeals. There will be no exceptions.*

Below the seal, in Aldric's own hand, a single line had been added:

*Bring me their names.*

Veyra read it twice. The parchment was heavy stock, imported from the southern looms, and it crackled faintly in the cold like something alive deciding whether to speak.

Around her, the courtyard went about its business with the studied deafness of servants who had learned that hearing too much shortened lives. A groom led a gray mare past. Somewhere inside, the clerks were already copying the edict for distribution to every garrison, every magistrate, every parish priest from the coast to the mountains—because that was how an edict became real. Not when a king signed it. When a thousand ordinary hands passed it along without trembling.

She folded the parchment along its crease and slid it into her coat, against her heart, where she kept the documents that mattered.

"Chief Inquisitor." Captain Rell stood at the courtyard arch, breath steaming, his face arranged into the careful blankness he wore around her. "The prisoner is awake. He's asking for ink."

"He can ask," Veyra said. "He'll get none."

"You said yesterday he might."

"I say many things yesterday." She turned toward the interrogation wing, boots ringing on stone. "Yesterday I believed he was a stubborn old man protecting nothing but his pride. This morning I have a king telling me there are enough Gifted left to require an edict. That changes what stubbornness means."

Rell fell into step beside her, half a pace back, as protocol demanded. "And if it isn't pride?"

"Then it's worse for him," Veyra said, "and more interesting for me."

---

The cell they kept Corvin in was not one of the screaming rooms. Veyra had ordered that herself, over the objections of the local garrison commander, who had wanted the old man softened the traditional way before she arrived. She had looked at the commander—a thick-necked man named Hult with wine on his breath at nine in the morning—and told him that torture was a tool for extracting lies, because pain made people say whatever would stop the pain. If you wanted truth, you needed patience, warmth, and time. Hult had looked at her as though she'd suggested interrogating prisoners with poetry.

He wasn't entirely wrong about the poetry.

Corvin sat on the cell's narrow cot with his hands folded in his lap, as composed as a scholar awaiting a lecture hall. He was past seventy, she judged, maybe well past, with a beard gone the color of wood ash and eyes that had the disconcerting quality of looking *through* things rather than at them. They had taken his spectacles, his satchel, his boots. He sat barefoot on cold stone and seemed not to notice.

"Ink," he said, before she'd even closed the door. Not a greeting. A demand.

"No."

"A man should be allowed to finish his work."

"What work?"

"The map," Corvin said. "I was correcting the survey of the upper Veilwater when your soldiers kicked down my door. The eastern tributary has shifted two miles since the last crown survey. Whoever uses the current chart will drown in what looks like solid ground." He said it mildly, the way a man remarks on weather. "I'd hate for the crown to lose soldiers to bad cartography. You seem short enough of them."

Veyra drew the room's single chair from the wall, set it facing him, and sat. She placed her hands on her knees. She let the silence stretch until it grew teeth.

Most people broke the silence first. Most people couldn't bear it. Corvin bore it the way stone bears rain, and after a full minute Veyra felt the unfamiliar flicker—the one she never named, never wrote in her reports, never allowed past the locked room at the back of her mind where she kept everything inconvenient.

Doubt.

Not doubt of her purpose. Never that. Doubt of the *shape* of it—an old man with ash-colored eyes asking only for ink, and a kingdom answering with fire.

She put the thought away the way she always did: cleanly, completely, like closing a ledger.

"Master Corvin," she said. "Formerly of the Cartographers' Guild at Marrowgate. Formerly court surveyor under the old king, before your guild's charter was revoked. For thirty years you trained apprentices in the making of maps. And for some portion of those years—" she watched his face as she said it, watching for the hairline fracture, the flicker, anything— "you taught them something else."

"I taught them to count paces accurately," Corvin said. "It's harder than it sounds."

"You taught them to find paths that aren't on any chart."

"Good maps are full of paths that aren't on other charts. That's rather the point of making them."

Veyra leaned forward. "Master Corvin. The edict came this morning. Perhaps you've heard the garrison celebrating—they do love a proclamation, it saves them thinking. Every person with the Gift is now a traitor. Everyone who shelters them, likewise. The penalty is binding, and binding, as I'm sure you know, does not leave the recipient much of a person afterward." She paused. "You are old. Old men die slowly in cells, and I have been authorized to take as long as the process requires. But I would prefer not to. I would prefer that you tell me about your students."

For the first time, something moved behind those eyes. Not fear. Recognition, perhaps—the look of a man seeing a familiar shape in an unexpected place.

"And which student would interest the crown?" he asked.

"The one you've been writing to."

"I write to no one. My hands shake. Ask your physician."

"I asked the postmaster at Thornfell village. He remembers you well. He says once a season, sometimes twice, a letter goes south under your seal, and once a season a letter comes back, and that you burn both within the hour of reading." Veyra produced a folded paper from her coat—not the edict, another document, thinner, travel-worn. "This morning my riders brought me the ash from your hearth. Your hearth survives fires poorly, Master Corvin. The edges of the pages curl before they catch. We recovered fragments. Enough."

She unfolded the paper and read aloud, in a flat voice stripped of all music:

"*The pass holds. The new route through the Veilwater is sound—I have walked it three times this year and marked the safe crossings. Do not send anyone else. It is not safe for others, only for me. Burn this.*"

Silence. The crows outside had gone quiet, as if listening.

"That could be any correspondent," Corvin said. "A colleague. A supplier of vellum."

"It could," Veyra agreed pleasantly. "Except that the same fragment bears a second line, in different ink, added later—your hand again, but hurried. *She walked the path in the storm when no rider could cross. She carried Tam out of the burn-line. If the crown comes for the boy, she will come for the boy, and then you must get her south, Corvin, do you hear me, south, before they learn her name.*"

She let the words hang in the frozen air of the cell.

Corvin's hands, folded in his lap, had not moved. But the knuckles had gone white, and Veyra saw it, and she felt the small cold satisfaction that was the closest thing her profession offered to joy.

"There is a name," she said softly, "in that sentence. Half a name. A surname, actually, and surnames are rarer than given ones in the villages—you can change what a child is called, but families keep their names like scars." She rose, crossed to the cell's high slit of a window, and looked out at the mountains. Thornfell's spine cut the eastern sky, blue-white and indifferent, hiding its valleys the way a closed fist hides a coin. "Brightwater."

Behind her, very quietly, the old man exhaled. It might have been resignation. It might have been relief. With scholars, Veyra had learned, those were often the same thing.

"There was a Brightwater family in the hill country," Corvin said at last. "Mapmakers, like me. The mother died of fever eight years ago. The father in a rockslide. There may be a daughter. I don't know. Guild records are imperfect."

"Guild records are imperfect," Veyra agreed. "But guild *letters* are precise, and yours just told me three things. First, that your correspondent has walked a pass in a storm that no rider could cross—which means either she is a liar, or she goes where the Gift takes her. Second, that she pulled a child out of a burning village ahead of the crown's own riders, which means she was there, which means she watches us move. Third—" she turned from the window, and let him see her face fully, let him see that there was no cruelty in it, only arithmetic— "that you love her. Which means she is the thread, and you are the spool, and I have been pulling the wrong end for two days."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lost its fencing, its mildness. What remained was quieter and far heavier.

"You're young," he said. "Younger than your reputation suggests. Do you know what the Gift is, Chief Inquisitor? Truly? Not what the priests say, not what the king's edict says. Do you know what it *was*?"

"I know what it is. An aberration. A corruption of the blood that lets a few walk where no road runs, start fires with a word, hear thoughts through walls. I know it terrified the old king into tolerating it, and I know the new king will not tolerate it."

"The old king didn't tolerate it," Corvin said. "He *knelt* to it. As did every king before him, back to the founding. Ask yourself why, girl. Ask yourself why nine generations of crowned heads, with armies at their backs, chose courtesy toward a handful of hedge-witches and pathfinders." His voice dropped. "Ask yourself what we were being courteous *to*."

For a moment—one moment, no longer—the locked room at the back of Veyra's mind rattled on its hinges. She saw, unbidden, a memory she did not permit herself: her grandmother's kitchen, woodsmoke, a woman's rough hands folding hers around something warm, saying *you listen for the paths too, little wren, don't you—never tell, never tell*. She had been six. She had told no one. She had spent her life making certain the listening died in her, starved it deliberately, the way one starves a stray one has decided not to keep.

She closed the room. She locked it. The rattle stopped.

"I ask questions," Veyra said. "That is my trade. And the question before me is a name: Brightwater. Where does she walk?"

"She walks nowhere," Corvin said. "There is no one."

"You burned your letters to protect no one. You'll die in this cell to protect no one. Forgive me, Master Corvin, but I find that a great deal of someone for a no one."

"Then you understand better than your edict does."

They regarded each other across the small grey room: the old man on his cot, the inquisitor by the window, the mountains beyond the glass keeping their counsel. Outside, a bell rang the third hour, and somewhere below, garrison horses stamped and steamed, ready for the day's burning.

Veyra straightened her coat.

"You'll have your ink," she said. "One page. Write your correction of the Veilwater survey—only the survey, and I will read it before it leaves this building. Consider it a gesture of professional respect between people who make accurate records." At the door she paused, hand on the iron. "You should know that I've requested leave to conduct the Thornfell inquiry personally. The king grants me what I ask. By the time spring thaws the passes, I will have walked every valley you ever mapped, and I will find the person you burned your letters for. And Master Corvin—" she did not turn around— "when I do, I hope for both our sakes that whatever you believe the Gift *was*, it proves worth what the crown is about to spend."

She heard him shift behind her, bare feet on stone, and his voice followed her to the threshold, thin and clear and terribly calm:

"It was worth everything once," Corvin said. "Pray it doesn't cost you everything to learn what changed."

---

She wrote the request that evening, by lamplight, in her precise clerk's hand.

*To His Majesty's Privy Council, Caelmark: I, Veyra Solenne, Chief Inquisitor of the Nine Reaches, request personal commission over the Thornfell proceedings. The terrain is difficult, the quarry evidently skilled, and the local garrison—* here she paused, quill hovering, and finally wrote *—ill-suited to subtlety. I estimate four months. I will deliver the Brightwater woman and any associates bound and living, per the edict's terms.*

She signed it. Sealed it. Set it aside for the morning courier.

Then she sat a long while with the lamp burning low, and the mountain wind worrying the shutters, and the locked room at the back of her mind silent as a grave.

Four months, she had written. But she knew, in the place where honest officers keep the accounts they never show the crown, that it would not take four months. The old man's letters had given her the shape of her quarry already: solitary, careful, loyal, fast. A woman who walked storms. A woman who ran toward burning villages while everyone else ran out.

Veyra had hunted such people before. She knew how it ended. It ended with her standing over them, and them looking up at her with an expression she had never once managed to forget afterward, no matter how carefully she filed the reports.

She snuffed the lamp.

In the dark, the mountains waited eastward, patient as old debts—and somewhere among their hidden valleys, a woman named Brightwater was about to learn that the hunt had a face now, and that the face was coming south.
