## 5. Smuggler's Road

They walked through the night and most of the next day, keeping to the deer trails above the valley roads, and by the time the sun dropped behind the western peaks Lena's legs had gone from aching to something beyond aching—a deep, humming wrongness, as if her bones had been replaced with wet rope. Tam had stopped complaining hours ago. That frightened her more than anything else. A child who complained was a child with strength left over.

He stumbled now on every third step, catching himself against pine trunks, his breath coming in small torn gasps. The soot from the burning village still streaked one side of his face, ground in where he'd wiped it with a dirty hand, and every time she looked at him she saw again the orange light over the rooftops of Hallowmere, the riders moving through the smoke like figures in a fever dream.

"We can stop," she said.

"You said we shouldn't stop."

"I said we shouldn't stop *there*. There is different from here." She scanned the slope below them: dark timber, a stream chuckling somewhere out of sight, no firelight, no dogs. "Here is fine."

Tam sat down where he stood, which was how exhausted children sat, without negotiation or ceremony, and was asleep almost before he finished folding. Lena pulled her cloak off and laid it over him and stood a while watching the treeline, listening to the mountain breathe.

Three days since Hallowmere burned. Three days since she'd cut a sleeping boy out of a cellar window while crown riders went door to door with torches and iron collars. Three days since Master Corvin's warning had stopped being an old man's caution and become the plain shape of the world: they were hunting the Gifted now, house to house, village to village, and the Thornfell mountains—her mountains, mapped in her own careful hand over ten patient years—were no longer wide enough to hide in.

She needed to get south. And there was exactly one way south that didn't run through a crown checkpoint, a toll bridge, or a garrison town full of men paid to look at faces.

Which meant Doss.

* * *

The smugglers' camp sat in a fold of the hills above the Graywater, where the river bent slow and shallow around a gravel bar and the pines grew thick enough to swallow lantern light. Lena smelled it before she saw it—woodsmoke, tar, frying onions—and heard the low clink of tackle being mended. She left Tam hidden in a hollow with instructions to stay silent unless someone who wasn't her came close, in which case he should run downhill toward the river and not stop running for any reason. He nodded with the grim competence of a boy who had recently learned what the world did to people who got caught, and it broke her heart a little, and she put that away where she kept everything else these days.

Then she walked into camp with her hood back and her hands visible, because Doss Merrow shot first at hooded strangers and asked questions over the bodies.

"Saints' teeth." The big man by the cookfire lowered his crossbow with exaggerated care, as though it might go off if he admitted surprise too quickly. He was broader than she remembered, gray coming into his beard now, but the same quick black eyes, the same scar splitting his left eyebrow where a Marrowgate harbor guard had introduced himself with a boat hook. "Lena Brightwater. I thought you were a story I told to frighten new crew."

"Only partly fiction."

"The rest of it true?"

"Depends which parts you tell."

He laughed, and some of the tension in the camp broke with it—the half-dozen smugglers mending nets and fletching bolts by the fire relaxed by degrees, the way men do when their captain decides a stranger is a guest. Doss waved her to a stump near the flames and pressed a tin cup of something hot and terrible into her hands. It tasted like pine tar boiled with ambition. She drank it anyway, and felt it lay down a warm coal in her chest.

"You're alone?" he asked. Too casually.

"No."

His eyebrows went up and stayed up. "That's a change. Ten years I've known you, you've been alone like it was a religion." He studied her over the rim of his own cup. "And you came down out of the high country in autumn, which you never do, looking like you've slept in a rockslide. So either tell me what's happened or let me guess, and my guesses are ugly this season."

So she told him. Not all of it—not Corvin, not the covenant, not the starlight woman who haunted the edges of her gift like a figure standing just outside lamplight—but enough. Hallowmere. The riders. The edict read aloud in market squares from here to the coast: all Gifted to be declared, bound, and delivered to the crown's inquisitors, harboring them a hanging crime. The boy asleep in the hollow above the river.

Doss listened without interrupting, which was unlike him, and when she finished he looked into the fire for a long moment.

"I heard the edict read in Grayharbor," he said finally. "Town crier, gold-trimmed herald, the whole theater of it. Half the crowd cheered." His jaw worked. "Half of them, Lena. Fishermen whose grandmothers probably brewed charms against storm-sickness, cheering."

"People cheer what they're afraid of. It makes the fear feel like agreement."

"That's the wisest thing anyone's said at this fire all year, and I include myself, which costs me." He drained his cup and refilled hers unasked. "So. You want passage south. For you and the boy."

"For me and the boy. Downriver, past the toll stations, to somewhere the king's writ arrives late if it arrives at all."

"And you're asking me because?"

"Because I drew you the chart that got your ship through the Serrated Reach when every pilot in Marrowgate swore it couldn't be done. Because you told me once, drunker than any man has a right to be and still stand, that you owed me your life and both your ships." She met his eyes. "I'm asking you to pay."

Doss winced theatrically, pressing a hand to his chest as though she'd loosed a bolt into it. "You would bring that up. You would bring that up *sober*."

"Sober is the only way I bring it up."

He laughed again, but shorter this time, and then he was quiet, turning his cup around and around in his big scarred hands. Around the fire, the crew had drifted closer without appearing to—smugglers could smell a bargain being struck the way sharks smell blood—and Lena ignored them with effort.

"Here's my trouble," Doss said at last. "It's not that I don't want to help you. It's that helping you has gotten expensive. The toll stations aren't just tolls anymore—they're search points. Every barge going south gets boarded, every hold opened, every passenger looked at. My usual routes are worth half what they were, because half my cargo is now contraband by definition, and the other half gets taxed twice." He set the cup down. "I can move you. I'll be honest, I'd move you for free if free were a thing I could afford this year. But I need something from you first, and you're not going to like it."

Lena felt her stomach tighten. "Say it."

"Veil Pass."

The name landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She'd known, in the cold calculating part of her mind that had been planning since Hallowmere, that this was coming. There was only one reason anyone sought out Lena Brightwater with desperation in their voice, and it was never the maps she sold.

"Doss—"

"Hear me out before you make that face. The river route is dead—you know it, I know it, the crown knows it, that's why they're strangling it. But Veil Pass cuts three hundred miles off the southern road and comes out behind the whole checkpoint line, at the headwaters, where there's nothing between us and the free cities but empty grass. One crossing and I'm rich for a winter. Two crossings and I can retire." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "Every map I've ever bought says the pass doesn't exist. Every pilot says it's a legend. But you—" he pointed at her with one thick finger, gentle as an accusation, "—you drew me a chart through the Serrated Reach that showed water where the charts showed stone, and you were right, and when I asked how, you told me you'd *walked it*. In a dream, you said. Some dream."

"It was a good answer."

"It was a lie with good bones. I've sailed with liars my whole life; I know the breed." He sat back. "There are paths in these mountains that aren't on any map, Lena. Paths that don't behave like paths. My grandfather swore his father took a herd through a gap in the Thornfells that was there in spring and gone by summer, and everyone called him a liar too, right up until he came back with wool from markets that shouldn't have been reachable. I think those paths are real. I think you can find them. And I think that's why you live up in the rocks alone like some kind of hermit saint, drawing maps that are always, always a little bit better than they have any right to be."

The fire cracked and spat. Somewhere downslope, the Graywater murmured over its gravel bar, patient as ever. Lena held very still, the way she'd learned to hold still when a crown patrol passed beneath her hiding place—because movement was what betrayed you, and words, spoken too fast, were a kind of movement.

Ten years. Ten years of the rule: *never be seen.* Never use the gift where eyes could follow. Never draw a path that couldn't be explained by hard climbing and good luck. Never let anyone, not even the people who loved her—not that many did—not even Corvin, know the size of what lived inside her. And now Doss sat across a cookfire naming it, not its name but its shape, and offering her the one trade she couldn't refuse in exchange for the one thing she'd sworn never to give.

If she led him through the Veil Pass, she would be seen. Not by the crown—maybe not by the crown—but seen nonetheless. A door opened is a door that can open again.

And if she refused, there was the river, and the search points, and Tam's face in a collar, and Corvin already in irons because she hadn't been fast enough, brave enough, early enough. The debts were stacking up faster than she could pay them. This one, at least, she could settle.

"All right," she said quietly. "I'll take you through."

Doss blinked. Whatever speech he'd prepared for continued resistance, she'd clearly robbed him of it. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. On conditions." She held up fingers. "One: we leave tonight, before dawn, and we tell no one the route. Not your crew, not your priest, not your horse. They follow where I say, when I say, and they don't ask why the trail bends where it bends. Two: the boy comes first. Ahead of cargo, ahead of schedule, ahead of you. If it comes to choosing, he goes and the goods burn. Three—" and here she paused, because the third condition was the one that mattered, the one that was really for herself, "—when we come out the far side, you never ask me how it was done. Not drunk, not sober, not on your deathbed. The pass stays unmapped. No chart, no notes, no memory written down. As far as the world is concerned, you got lucky and found a gap in the mountains, and it closed behind you."

Doss chewed on that, visibly, like a man working gristle. "No chart at all? Even a false one? Something to show the crew?"

"A false chart is worse than none. If the crown finds a chart, they know the pass exists. If they find nothing, they can spend ten years deciding it's a legend." She looked at him steadily. "You of all people know what a secret is worth. You've built a life on knowing things other people don't. This is mine."

"And the price of learning it?"

"Is that I carry it alone." She turned her cup in her hands. "That's the whole price, Doss. It looks cheap. It isn't."

For a long moment the smuggler said nothing. Then he laughed—one short bark, more breath than sound—and shook his head at himself. "My grandmother used to say the expensive things are always the ones that look free. Fine. Done. All three conditions, sworn on my ships and my miserable soul." He spat in his palm and held it out, an old river-man's oath older than any paper contract and, in his world, considerably more binding.

Lena spat in hers and clasped it. His hand swallowed hers whole, warm and rough as bark.

"Tonight, then," Doss said, rising, and his voice shifted into the register he used for giving orders, rolling down the camp like a keel through calm water. "Rouse everyone. Break camp quiet—no torches, no singing, Kessie, put the *lute down*, I swear by the tides. We move within the hour." He glanced back at Lena. "Your boy eats first. There's stew."

"There's always stew," said Kessie from the shadows, in the tone of a woman who had been making stew for Doss Merrow's crews for twenty years and had opinions about it.

* * *

Tam woke confused, then wary, then ravenous, in roughly that order. He ate two bowls of the stew—mutton, turnips, barley, more salt than any physician would approve of—while sitting as close to the fire as skin allowed, and Lena watched the color come back into his face degree by degree and thought, not for the first time, that hunger was the simplest honest thing left in the world.

The smugglers struck camp around them with a speed and silence that impressed her. Tents folded to bundles, tackle stowed, the mules loaded and muzzled with cloth so they wouldn't call to each other on the trail. Twelve people altogether, counting Doss and Kessie and the boy and her. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything now: who moved well, who breathed loud, who watched her too long. A lean young man with a crossbow named Ferren kept glancing at Tam with something that wasn't quite hostility and wasn't quite anything else. She'd keep an eye on him.

"Where are we going?" Tam whispered, mouth full.

"South."

"Through the mountains?"

"Through a way in the mountains."

"The secret way?" His eyes went wide, and she realized she'd told him more than she meant to, somewhere on the trail, in the low voice she used for truths too large for daylight. Children collected secrets the way rivers collected rain.

"The secret way," she confirmed. "But it's a loan, not a gift. When we're through, we give it back."

"How do you give a way back?"

"You forget where it was." She smoothed his hair, soot-stiff, and felt the old loneliness rise in her like groundwater. *This is what the rule costs,* she thought. *Not danger. Company. You walk every road twice—once with them, once alone inside yourself.*

Doss appeared at her shoulder with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light. "Trail starts at the old quarry road, yes? Then up?"

"Then up," she said. "And after the quarry road, you stop navigating and start trusting. The trail won't always look like a trail. Sometimes it'll look like nothing at all. Walk where I walk. That's the whole art of it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you'll have a very interesting few minutes and possibly a very short life."

He grinned at her, white teeth in the dark, unbothered. That was the thing about Doss Merrow that ten years hadn't changed: fear touched him and slid off, like rain off waxed canvas. "You know," he said, quieter, "the last person who talked about the mountains that way was my grandfather, and he died rich and strange and full of stories nobody believed. I used to think he was lying." He looked north, up the black shoulders of the Thornfells, where the stars came down almost to the ridgelines. "I'm starting to think he just knew somebody like you."

"Everyone knew somebody like me," Lena said. "Once. They just didn't know it."

She shouldered her pack and woke Tam with a hand on his shoulder, and the company fell into single file behind her, twelve shadows and six mules sliding north along the riverbank into the dark. Behind them the coals of the cookfire dimmed and died. Ahead, the mountains waited with their hidden doors.

And somewhere in the high country between here and the pass, walking a path only she could see, something ancient stirred at the edge of her gift—patient, luminous, and waiting to be noticed.
