## 6. Paths No One Maps

The Veil Pass did not appear on any map Lena had ever drawn, sold, or burned.

That was the first thing she had to make the others understand, standing at the mouth of a gorge that looked like every other gorge in the Thornfells—a slot of gray stone choked with scree and old snow, unremarkable as a hundred gorges they'd already walked past.

"It's not that it's hidden," she told them, while Doss's smugglers checked mule cinches and muttered. "It's that it isn't there. Not for you. The mountain doesn't have a door here unless someone opens it."

"And you're the key." Doss spat into the river shallows. His voice was flat with the particular skepticism of a man who had survived thirty years in the trade by believing nothing he hadn't held in his own two hands. "You said the gift lets you walk paths no one else can see. Fine. But my mules can't walk your seeing."

"They can if I lead them," Lena said. "The path holds for anyone who follows me and stays close. It won't hold if you wander. And Doss—" she let him see her eyes go hard, "—if anyone steps off the line I set, they'll step off a cliff. There is no negotiating with this pass. There's only me."

He studied her for a long moment. Behind him, his people had gone quiet, the way men go quiet when the trade talk turns to something older than trade.

"Twelve of us, six mules, single file, nobody breathes wrong," Doss said finally. "And how long?"

"Three days inside the mountain. Maybe four."

"Four days walking a road that doesn't exist." He shook his head slowly, but she could read him now—he'd been readable since the second night, when he'd started asking her questions about the Thornfell high country that weren't really about geography. He wanted to believe her. Believing was dangerous in his profession, so he wrapped the wanting in contempt, but it was there. "My grandmother used to say the Brightwaters were witch-blooded. Thought it was just an old woman's story."

"She wasn't wrong," Lena said. "Get them in file."

* * *

The door opened at dawn.

Lena felt for it the way she always did—with something behind her eyes rather than in them, a sense like pressure against a membrane, like pressing a palm against frosted glass from the warm side. The gorge wall was gray stone, lichened, cracked by frost. And beneath that, threaded through it like a vein of silver under slate, ran the path: not visible, not even quite imaginable, but *there*, the way a melody is there before anyone plays it.

She put her hand on the stone and gave the gift its head.

It came through her the way water comes through an opened sluice—not summoned so much as released—and where it passed, the world went strange. The gray rock didn't vanish or swing open like anything in a fireside tale. It simply stopped mattering. Her eyes still saw stone; her feet found footing anyway, on a floor of pale flagstones that existed a half-step out of rhythm with the world, and when she stepped through, the gorge was gone and the mountain had swallowed her whole.

Behind her, she heard Tam gasp. Then Doss swearing softly, elaborately, in three dialects.

Inside, the Veil Pass was nothing like the mountains around it.

They walked a corridor of stone that glowed faintly from within, the way snow glows at dusk, though there was no sun down here to explain the light. The walls rose smooth and seamless, marked with lines of script in no alphabet Lena knew—or rather, in an alphabet she knew without knowing it, characters that her fingers itched to trace and her mind flinched from tracing. High overhead, the corridor opened into vaults of darkness where something hung like constellations trapped under ice. The air tasted of cold iron and, impossibly, of summer—the green, resinous smell of a forest that had no business existing four days' walk underground.

"Gods below," one of the smugglers whispered. A woman named Sefa, who hadn't spoken a full sentence in two days. "It's like the inside of a cathedral."

"Cathedrals are copies," Lena said quietly. She didn't know how she knew that either. The knowledge arrived the way all the gift's knowledge arrived—unbidden, certain, and slightly ahead of her understanding. "Keep moving. Don't touch the writing on the walls."

"What happens if we touch it?" Tam asked. He had pressed himself close to her since the crossing began, close enough that his shoulder bumped her hip with every stride.

"I don't know," she admitted. "That's why we don't find out."

* * *

The first vision came on the evening of the first day, at a place where the corridor widened into a chamber the size of Marrowgate's great market square.

There was no reason to stop there except exhaustion. The mules needed rest, the people needed sleep and hot food, and Lena judged—by some internal clock the gift kept better than she did—that the day's walking was done. They made camp in the vast chamber, small as insects in a temple, and Lena took first watch because sleep felt suddenly like a luxury she couldn't afford.

She sat with her back against a pillar, watching the false stars in the vaulted dark, and the vision came the way sleep comes—without announcement, without consent.

One moment the chamber was empty. The next it was full.

Not people. Not ghosts. Something else. Figures moved across the far side of the chamber, tall and slight, their bodies catching light the way smoke catches firelight—there and luminous and never quite solid. They moved with unhurried grace, and they seemed to be doing ordinary things: carrying vessels of light, bending over workbenches that were no longer there, gesturing to one another in conversation whose words reached Lena as tone and intention rather than sound. She saw a child chase something glowing across the flagstones. She saw two figures standing close together, foreheads touching, in a gesture of tenderness so old and simple it hurt to watch.

She did not move. She barely breathed. Every instinct honed over ten years of hiding screamed at her to be small, be quiet, be nothing—but this was different. This wasn't the crown hunting her through human country. This was something older looking back.

Because they were looking back. She realized it gradually, then all at once: the figures had begun to pause. To turn. Their faces—if faces was the right word, those shifting arrangements of light where a face should be—tilted toward her pillar, toward the spot where she sat wrapped in shadow.

They couldn't see her, exactly. She understood that too. What they saw was the gift itself, burning in her like a coal in a dark room, and they were turning toward its warmth the way flowers turn toward a window.

Then the woman came.

She separated from the others the way a star separates from its constellation, and crossed the chamber toward Lena, and with every step she became more distinct and more impossible. She was made of starlight—that was the only word for it, and Lena hated how childish the phrase sounded even as her heart hammered against it. Points of white fire woven into the shape of a woman, tall as a doorway, her outline rippling like heat-shimmer, her eyes two deep wells of dark that held more light than the rest of her combined.

She stopped ten paces away. Close enough that the cold coming off her was real—Lena felt it on her cheeks, sharp as a January morning.

"You're early," the woman said.

Her voice was not a sound. It arrived directly, somewhere behind Lena's sternum, resonant as a bell struck inside her chest. Lena's mouth was dry. Ten years of discipline said: say nothing, reveal nothing. Ten years of loneliness said: *she's talking to you.*

"Early for what?" Lena managed.

"For the breaking." The starlight woman tilted her head, and the gesture was so human it was worse than anything inhuman could have been. "The covenant thins. We sealed ourselves away to keep the world from tearing, but seals are made of promises, and promises are made of people, and your kind has forgotten us so thoroughly that the forgetting has begun to eat the promise itself." She raised one hand—fingers of woven light—and pointed past Lena, deeper into the pass. "Can you feel it? Even here? Even asleep, the Spire calls for a hand it remembers."

Lena turned. At the far end of the chamber, half-hidden in shadow, stood a fragment of carved archway—she was almost certain it hadn't been there when they made camp—and around its edges the air shivered, faintly, like heat off summer stone. Like a held breath. Like a door remembering it was a door.

"What covenant?" Lena asked. "Who made it? What does it—" She stopped, because Corvin's face rose in her memory then, unbidden: his hands shaking as he'd told her to flee south, his voice cracking on words he'd clearly carried for decades. *The Gifted aren't accidents, girl. We're remnants.* He had known. He had known and tried to carry it alone, and now he was in a crown dungeon paying for it, and her chest tightened until she could hardly speak. "What do we have to do?"

But when she turned back, the chamber was empty.

Not empty—her sleeping companions lay where they'd bedded down, mules stamping softly, the banked cookfire breathing its thin smoke. Empty of everything except the cold that clung to her skin and the archway at the far wall, which was most definitely not a trick of the light, because Sefa stirred in her bedroll, frowned at it, and pulled her blanket over her head.

Lena sat until dawn, watching the archway, feeling watched in return.

* * *

The visions did not stop after that. That was the worst of it. Lena had spent her whole life treating the gift as a tool—uncommon, useful, hers—and tools behaved themselves. This was not behaving.

On the second day, the corridor filled with rain that fell upward, each drop rising from the flagstones toward the vaulted dark, and through the rain she saw fields—terraced gardens climbing hillsides under three moons, worked by figures of light, harvests of something luminous gathered into baskets that glowed brighter as they filled. On the third day, the walls went briefly transparent, and beyond them she saw a city: towers of pale stone spiraling toward a sky she didn't recognize, and at the city's heart a spire so tall it pierced cloud and kept going, crowned with light that beat like a pulse. She knew that pulse. It matched her own heartbeat exactly, and the realization frightened her more than anything the crown had ever done.

The others saw less. She learned that quickly, and guarded the difference carefully. Tam caught glimpses—once he grabbed her sleeve and whispered, wide-eyed, "Lena, there's someone walking beside us, there's been someone walking beside us all morning"—and she told him it was an echo, a trick of the stone, and hated herself a little for lying. Doss saw nothing at all and said so loudly and often, which was its own kind of comfort to his crew. The gift, apparently, showed itself to whom it chose. It had chosen her. It had been choosing her, she suspected, since before she was born, since whatever ancestor had first accepted whatever bargain had first been struck.

The starlight woman came again on the third night.

This time Lena was ready for her—or thought she was. She'd taken first watch again, deliberately, sitting cross-legged in the center of that night's campsite with her knife loose in its sheath and her fear packed down tight. When the cold arrived, she didn't flinch.

"You keep calling me early," Lena said, before the woman could speak. "Early for what? Say it plainly. I'm a mapmaker. I deal in plain things."

Something moved across the starlit face that might have been amusement. "Plain things. Yes. Your people were always the plainest of the three—we loved you for it. The dwarrows built, and we dreamed, and you humans *recorded*. You wrote down what the rest of us only felt." The woman knelt then, folding herself down to Lena's height with the grace of falling snow, and up close her dark eyes held entire skies. "Very well. Plainly, then, cartographer.

"There was a covenant, struck in the age before your calendars. Three peoples, one accord: the deep folk would hold the stone, we would hold the light, and your kind would hold the *memory*—the keeping of names, the tending of promises, the long accounting. In return, the magic of the world would flow to all three alike, and no one people would hoard what belonged to everyone.

"Then your kings decided magic was better hoarded. They bound our emissaries. They broke the names. And we—who might have burned your kingdoms to ash, and considered it, and were ashamed of considering it—we withdrew instead. All of us. Into the Spire, into the deep places, behind seals woven from the covenant itself. And the Gifted among your kind—the ones born since, scattered and hunted and burned as witches—they are the last living threads of that weaving. Fragments of the accord, walking around in mortal bodies." Her voice, resonant in Lena's chest, softened. "You are not accidents, Brightwater's daughter. You are *remnants*. And remnants are enough to reforge what was broken—if one of you reaches the Spire before the seal fails entirely."

"The seal will fail?" Lena's voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Soon. Your usurper king feels it even if he cannot name what he feels—the power leaking through the cracks. He thinks he wants to seize it. He cannot imagine what he's actually reaching for." The starlight woman leaned closer, and the cold of her was terrible and clean. "When the seal breaks without a new covenant to replace it, the power doesn't flow to anyone. It *floods*. Everything your king has built, everything your free cities have bargained away, everything these mountains are—it drowns. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless a remnant stands in the heart of Auren and speaks the old names with a price attached. The covenant was broken by betrayal; it can only be reforged by sacrifice. That is the shape of it. That is all I may tell you, for the rest must be chosen freely or it means nothing." The woman began to rise, her edges already fraying back into the dark, and desperation clawed up Lena's throat like floodwater.

"Wait—wait! My mentor. Corvin. The crown took him—is he—is there—" 

"He carries his thread still," the woman said, and for a moment her dark eyes held something that in a human face would have been grief. "Threads do not break easily, cartographer. But yours is the brightest I have seen in three hundred years. Which is why I will tell you one thing more, though I should not." She paused at the edge of the firelight, nearly transparent now, a constellation deciding whether to remain a woman. "You are being followed. Not by the crown—not yet. By the mountain itself. The pass chose to open for you. Ask yourself why a road that hasn't opened in three centuries opened now, and who else knows the answer."

Then she was gone, and the cold went with her, and Lena sat alone in the ringing silence with her heart slamming and Tam watching her from across the dead fire with eyes too old for his twelve years.

"Lena," he said quietly. "You've been talking to somebody for an hour. Who was it?"

She opened her mouth to give him the lie she'd prepared—and closed it again. He'd lost his village. He'd lost everything but her. He deserved better than the lies she gave smugglers.

"Someone very old," she said. "Someone who says we're running out of time. Come here. There are things you need to know before we reach the other side of this mountain."

* * *

They exited the Veil Pass at midday on the fourth day, stepping out of pale stone into ordinary gray daylight, onto a ledge above a valley of pine and snowmelt, and the company made the noises of people surfacing from deep water—blinking, gasping, laughing shakily. Doss counted his mules twice and his people three times and pronounced, in the tone of a man refusing to discuss what couldn't be discussed, that they'd camp early tonight because everyone needed sleep that didn't come with visions.

Lena agreed. She was more exhausted than she'd ever been, wrung out like a cloth, her skull humming with starlight and old names. But sleep wouldn't come, and near dusk she climbed alone to the lip of the ledge to look back the way they'd come, and that was when she saw the smoke.

Not theirs. Down-valley, perhaps two miles, where the pass debouched onto the lowland road: a thin black column rising against the evening sky, and beneath it the orange flicker of a fire much larger than a traveler's cookfire. Crown fires were like that—big, wasteful, confident. They burned things because they could.

And between her ledge and the valley, cutting across a slope of scree with the easy, ground-eating stride of a trained soldier moving alone, came a man in armor. Crown armor. No banner, no unit, no comrades—just one big, weary figure staggering north through the dusk, unarmed or nearly so, heading straight for their camp because in all this wilderness their campfire was the only fire there was.

Doss appeared at her shoulder, followed the line of her gaze, and went very still.

"That's a soldier," he said.

"That's a deserter," Lena said. She could read it in the man's gait—the drag of the left foot, the way his hand kept going to a sword belt that carried no sword. She'd mapped enough battlefields to know what soldiers looked like, both kinds. "Look at him. He's not scouting. He's running."

"Could be bait. Could be a tracker leading Veyra's hounds right to us." Doss's hand had found his knife without his seeming to notice. Around them, the smugglers were melting into the rocks with the smoothness of long practice, and Tam was pulling the mules back from the firelight, and the whole camp was becoming invisible the way Doss's people became invisible—fast and silent and total.

The soldier was fifty yards out now. Forty. Close enough that Lena could see the blood on his vambrace, dried black, and the raw hollows of his cheeks, and something else—something in the way he walked toward a stranger's fire in enemy country with his chin up and his shoulders squared, like a man who had already decided how he wanted to be found.

Thirty yards. He saw them. He stopped.

And Lena felt the gift stir behind her eyes, unbidden, tasting the air around the man the way it tasted paths and doors—and finding, wound through his weariness like a bright wire, something that made her breath catch.

A thread. Faint, frayed, unmistakable.

The same kind of thread that burned in her.

"He's Gifted," she breathed. "Doss—he's one of *us*."

Down the slope, the soldier raised empty hands and called out, his voice cracked and carrying across the cold evening air:

"I'm not here to hurt anyone. My name is Bren. And I think—I think I've been looking for you my whole life."
