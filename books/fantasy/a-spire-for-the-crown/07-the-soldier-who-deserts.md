## 7. The Soldier Who Deserts

The girl's name was Wren.

Bren knew that because he'd asked her, the way you ask a prisoner their name before the hanging—not out of kindness, exactly, but because a man ought to know what he's part of. She was nine years old. She had mud on her knees and a crow's feather tucked behind one ear, and when the binding irons came out of the sergeant-major's satchel she didn't cry. She just looked at Bren with the particular patience of children who have already understood something the adults around them are still refusing to learn.

"Say it again," Sergeant-Major Hult said. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with a voice like gravel poured slow. "For the record."

"She made the well water rise," Bren said. "Out of the bucket. Into her hands. It just—sat there. Like a bird cupped in palms." His own voice sounded far away to him. "She wasn't hurting anyone. The village elder called it in because the edict says he has to. Not because anyone wanted her bound."

"For the record," Hult repeated, and unrolled the writ.

They were standing in the dooryard of a farmhouse outside Millbrook, three days south of the Thornfell foothills, and the morning was so ordinary it felt like an insult. Chickens scratched in the yard. Smoke rose straight from the chimney into windless gray sky. Somewhere behind the house a woman was singing to herself while she worked, low and tuneless, and stopped mid-verse when she saw the soldiers.

The binding irons were new work, crown-forged since Aldric's edict. They weren't shackles, not properly. They were a collar and two cuffs joined by short chains, each link stamped with sigils Bren couldn't read, and they were designed to do one thing: press down on whatever lived inside a gifted person until it went quiet. He had seen them used twice before. Both times, the gifted had come out of the fitting changed—not screaming, not broken, just *dimmed*, like a lamp turned down to its last ember. One of them, a ferryman on the Sear, had sat afterward staring at his own hands for an hour, as if trying to remember what they used to be for.

Wren looked at the irons. Then at Bren.

"You're the one who found me," she said.

"I did."

"Why?"

Because the edict ordered sweeps of every settlement within thirty miles of the royal road. Because refusing an order was flogging and repeating it was the rope. Because he was a sergeant of the Crown's Ninth Foot and had been for eleven years, and a soldier's whole self was built out of doing what he was told and calling it honor.

He said none of that. What he said was: "It's my job."

"Is it a good job?" she asked, genuinely curious, the way children ask terrible questions.

Hult barked a laugh. "Sharp little thing, isn't she. Hold her arm."

And Bren reached out—and stopped.

It happened slowly, the way a cart wheel comes loose: a wobble first, then a shudder, then the whole axle giving way. He stood in that dooryard with his hand half-extended toward a nine-year-old girl's wrist and heard, very clearly, his mother's voice from twenty years ago, telling him about his grandmother—the one nobody spoke of, the one who'd been taken away in a cart when his mother was small, who could supposedly make bread rise without yeast and find lost things by holding a ribbon. Taken where? he'd asked. Away, his mother had said, in the flat voice that ended conversations. Just away.

"Corporal," Hult said, and there was an edge under the boredom now. "Hold her arm."

* * *

He didn't remember deciding. That was the thing he would turn over and over in the nights after—that there was no moment of choice, no thunderclap of conscience. There was only the space between one heartbeat and the next, and then his hand was closing not on Wren's wrist but on Hult's sword arm, wrenching it wide, and his mouth was saying words he hadn't rehearsed.

"Run, girl. *Run.*"

Wren ran. She went through the gap in the hedge like a rabbit, crow feather flashing, and the singing woman behind the house dropped her pail and bolted the other way, and for three full seconds nobody moved because nobody—including Bren—could believe what he'd done.

Then Hult hit him.

Bren took the blow across the jaw and gave back better; eleven years of drill lived in his shoulders whether his heart agreed or not. He put Hult down hard, took the man's own baton across the kidney from young Pellis, turned, caught Pellis's wrist, and had the horrible clarity of seeing himself from above—an old soldier beating two of his own men in a farmyard over a child with a feather in her hair—and knew, with total certainty, that he could not stop now even if he wanted to. Wanting had nothing left to do with it.

He broke Pellis's grip, shoved him into the water trough, and ran.

He ran through the hedge gap, through the wet field beyond, through a stand of alders, across a stream that soaked him to the thigh, and up onto the sheep-cropped slopes where the ground went rough and the trees closed overhead, and he ran until his lungs burned and his side stitched and the shouting behind him faded into nothing but his own blood in his ears.

Then he walked. And kept walking. North, mostly, without quite deciding to—north and west, away from the royal road, away from everything he'd been for forty-one years, into the Thornfell mountains where the maps went vague and the king's writ went thinner with every mile.

* * *

Three days later, starving, feverish from a cut on his forearm that had gone bad, and half-blind with exhaustion, Bren followed the smell of woodsmoke down into a hollow between two ridges and found the camp.

There were six of them, maybe seven—a smuggler's outfit by the look of the gear: mules, oilcloth bundles, weapons worn honest at the hip rather than parade-drilled. A big man with a braided beard sat sharpening a knife and saw him first. A lean woman with gray in her dark hair crouched by the fire mending a boot. A boy of ten or so slept under a wagon. Two others were out of sight, probably watching him already—he could feel it, the prickle between the shoulder blades of being sighted along.

"Don't," the bearded man said pleasantly, when Bren's hand drifted toward his belt knife. "You've got maybe four steps left in you and I've got all night. Sit down before you fall down."

Bren sat. Or rather, his legs resigned and the ground received him.

The gray-haired woman came over. Up close she was younger than the gray suggested, weathered brown, with eyes the color of river slate that went over him the way he'd once gone over terrain—reading slope, reading cover, reading threat. Her gaze snagged on his boots, then his sword belt with the crown stamp, then his face.

"Crown infantry," she said. "Sergeant's knots on the shoulder. You're a long way from your line, sergeant."

"Deserted," Bren said. His tongue felt like leather. "Three days ago."

"Well," said the bearded man, rising with the knife still open in his fist, "that's convenient. We were just saying how we needed a crown sergeant."

"Doss," the woman said. Just the name, quiet, and the big man stopped moving—but he didn't sit, and he didn't close the knife.

"You know what they pay for deserters brought back alive?" Doss said, to the camp at large, conversational. "Half what they pay for Gifted, and Gifted fetch plenty. And this one—" he nudged Bren's sword belt with his boot, "—this one can tell Veyra's people exactly which passes we're using, how many we are, what we carry. Or he's bait. Walk him to the nearest garrison, collect the bounty, walk away rich." He smiled. It was not a warm smile. "Either way, sergeant, your luck's looking up."

"He's fevered," the woman said.

"He's a *soldier*. Fevered is what they're like when you catch them between wars." Doss crouched, bringing his face level with Bren's, close enough that Bren could smell pipe smoke and mule sweat. "Here's my question, crown-man. Why'd you run? And think careful, because I've hanged men for lying to me smaller than you."

Bren meant to give the safe answer. Some story about a flogging, a gambling debt, a woman. Anything true-shaped but empty. Instead, fever and exhaustion stripped him down to the bare fact, and what came out was:

"There was a girl. Nine years old. They were going to put the binding irons on her for making well water rise." His voice cracked on the last word, which humiliated him more than the tears would have. "I let her go. I don't know where she went. I hope the hills keep her."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Doss's smile had died. What replaced it was worse—something old and cold. "Millbrook way? Three days back? Gray farmhouse, alder stand below it?"

Bren stared. "How—"

"Because we pulled a girl out of a culvert two nights ago, crown-man. Crow feather in her hair, wouldn't speak, wouldn't eat, flinched from every hand that came near her. Took Tam half the night sitting near her talking nonsense before she'd even drink." Doss stood, and the knife finally closed, and somehow that was more frightening than the blade. "So here's the arithmetic. You set her running. If she'd frozen—if she'd gone the wrong way—she'd be in irons now, or worse. You saved her life. And you led Hult's riders into these hills after us, because they'll have tracked you, because deserters always run somewhere and soldiers always follow." He spread his hands. "Both things are true. Welcome to the mountains."

"Doss." The woman again. She hadn't moved from the fire, but her voice carried the weight of someone used to being obeyed in exactly one thing. "Come here."

They conferred a few feet away, voices too low for Bren to catch more than fragments—*tracked*, *ford*, *can't afford*—and Bren sat in the firelight feeling his fever climb and his chances sink, and found, oddly, that he wasn't afraid. Fear required a future, and he'd spent his on a farmyard floor. What he felt instead was a kind of clean exhaustion, the feeling of a wound finally lanced.

The boy appeared at his elbow while the others argued. Ten years old, sharp-faced, missing a tooth, with the fearless stare of a child raised around dangerous adults.

"You're the one who let Wren go," the boy said.

"I am."

"Wren says you asked her name first. Before anything." The boy considered him with grave seriousness. "Nobody asks names first. Doss says names are leverage. Lena says names are the whole point of everything." He stuck out a grimy hand. "Tam."

Bren took the hand. It was absurd. He was a deserter with a fever, kneeling in a smuggler's camp, shaking hands with a child, and it was the most honest transaction he'd been part of in eleven years.

"Bren," he said. "Late of the Ninth Foot."

"Lena," the boy said, jerking his chin at the gray-haired woman. "She decides who lives. Doss just argues about it."

* * *

The argument lasted until well past midnight. Bren heard most of it in fragments, drifting in and out of fever-sleep against a supply pack: Doss's voice hard and reasonable, the woman's quieter and immovable as bedrock.

"—one rider, Doss. Half-dead. He's worth more to us breathing than his bounty ever was—"

"—he's a *compass*, Lena. Point him at a garrison and he walks us straight into a noose. You want to bet Tam's neck on a soldier's change of heart? I've seen changes of heart. They last about as long as the hunger does—"

"—and I've seen what happens when we start deciding people are things to spend. Corvin told me—"

"Corvin's in a crown cell, if he's alive at all. Forgive me if I don't build policy on his say-so."

A long silence then. When the woman spoke again her voice had dropped to something almost private. "When the edict came down, everyone we trusted started counting who they could give away to stay safe. Every city. Every council. Every friend. I swore I wouldn't be another person who counts." A pause. "He let a child go. In front of witnesses. Knowing what it cost. That's not a change of heart, Doss. That's a man who found out what he actually believes, late, and paid full price for it."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong. And I'll carry it." Another pause. "But I won't be the one who taught this company to kill first and count second. That's Aldric's arithmetic. Let him keep it."

Boots crunched away. Doss's shadow fell over Bren, blocking the firelight, and for a long moment the big man just looked down at him with the knife-hand loose and thoughtful at his side.

"She vouches for you," Doss said at last. "Which means if you betray us, it's her trust you break, and I'll tell you plainly, crown-man: I can stomach killing a soldier. I've done it. But I'd hate to have to look at her afterward and explain why she was wrong. So don't be wrong." He tossed something onto Bren's chest—a strip of dried meat, a twist of willow bark. "Fever medicine. Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow we move, and tomorrow you carry a pack like everyone else, and if the riders following you find us, you'll be standing between them and this camp when they do. That's the price of her mercy. Understand?"

"Understand," Bren said.

Doss grunted and walked off into the dark, and Bren lay back against the pack and ate the meat and drank the bitter willow tea Tam wordlessly brought him, and stared up at the stars through the black lattice of the pines.

He thought about Wren, safe somewhere in a culvert or a barn or a stranger's cellar, crow feather and all. He thought about Hult, who wasn't evil—who'd have said, and believed, that the irons were mercy compared to what Aldric's harder instruments would do. He thought about his grandmother he'd never met, taken away in a cart, just away, and wondered suddenly, with a lurch, whether the well-water trick ran in blood older than any of them knew.

He thought about the woman—Lena—who had looked at a crown sergeant and seen something worth spending her credibility on, and decided that the least he owed her was the truth he hadn't yet told anyone:

They weren't just following him. Before Millbrook, his company had orders to sweep the high passes. And among the papers Hult carried was a list of names, and one name had been underlined twice in red ink, and Bren had read it upside-down across the map table without understanding why it mattered.

*Brightwater.*

He sat up in the dark, fever roaring, and found her silhouette against the fire—lean, watchful, mending that boot as if the world were ordinary.

"Brightwater," he said aloud, hoarse. "That's your name, isn't it? It's on their list. It was underlined."

The mending stopped.

Across the fire, Lena Brightwater looked up at him, and in the shifting light her slate-gray eyes went very still, the way water goes still over something deep.
