## 8. Marrowgate Under Shadow

Marrowgate wore its wealth like armor. Lena had mapped the city three times in her life—once for the harbormaster's ledger, once for a merchant consortium that paid in silver and suspicion, and once for herself, because no city should be known only from above. She knew its streets the way she knew the lines of her own palm: the salt-market where fishmongers shouted down the dawn, the lantern district that burned green with imported oil, the Council Spire at the center of it all, white as a held breath.

She walked into it now with soot on her boots and a child's hand in hers, and felt the armor close around her like a trap.

"You can't just walk up to them," Doss muttered at her shoulder. He'd pulled his hood low and made himself smaller than a man his size should manage. "Councils don't listen to cartographers and smugglers. They listen to coin and fear. We've got neither."

"They'll listen to what I know." Lena kept her voice level, though her heart was beating against her ribs like something caged. "Aldric's supply lines. The passes he'll use to move his armies south. The roads the free cities think are safe but aren't. I've spent ten years drawing this country, Doss. I know its veins."

"And when you tell them," Doss said, "they'll want to know how you know. And then someone clever will ask why a mapmaker was walking paths no soldier has ever found." He shook his head. "You're carrying your death warrant under your tongue, girl. You just haven't said the words yet."

Tam's fingers tightened around hers. The boy had been quiet since Thornfell—quieter than smoke, quieter than ash—but she felt him listening the way children listened, taking everything in and burying it deep where it would grow into either wisdom or scar tissue.

"I need to wash," she said instead. "And Tam needs to eat. Then I go to the council."

Doss stopped walking. Around them, Marrowgate's morning crowd flowed past like water around stones—fishwives and clerks and off-duty watchmen, no one looking twice at three travel-worn figures by the fountain of the Salt Gate. That was the mercy of cities: they swallowed people whole.

"Say we do this," Doss said slowly. "Say the council hears you out. What then? You think they'll shelter a gifted woman and a gifted boy while Aldric's envoys sit in their guesthouses drinking their wine?" He spat into the gutter. "Cities don't take risks, Brightwater. Cities survive. That's all they do."

"Then they'll prove me wrong about them," Lena said. "And I'd rather know."

* * *

The safehouse Doss kept in Marrowgate was above a dyer's shop, behind a door painted the same unremarkable gray as forty others on the street. It smelled of vinegar and wet wool, and through its single window Lena could see the Council Spire rising over the rooftops, catching the afternoon light.

She washed the road off Tam in a basin while he chattered—a thin, fragile stream of talk about the horses on the ferry, about a dog he'd seen, about whether Marrowgate had sweet buns like Vellin did. She answered each thing seriously, one word at a time, because she had learned that children survived horror by insisting on small ordinary things, and adults survived it by letting them.

"You're going to leave me here when you go talk to them," Tam said suddenly. Not a question.

"For an hour. Maybe two."

"What if they come?"

"No one knows we're here." She wrung out his hair gently. "And if anyone knocks who isn't Doss, there's a ladder to the roof and a gap between the chimneys that only looks too narrow. It isn't. I checked the maps."

"The maps don't have that building in them."

"My maps do."

He considered this, then nodded, satisfied, and asked again about the sweet buns.

Lena dressed in the plainest clothes she owned—a wool dress the color of river clay, a shawl, nothing that spoke of guilds or commissions—and stood before the cloudy scrap of mirror nailed by the door. A stranger looked back at her. Ten years ago she had been a girl with ink-stained fingers and a teacher's voice in her ear saying *never be seen, never be known*. Master Corvin's face rose in her mind unbidden: his white beard braided in the old style, his hands trembling as he'd begged her to run south. *They took me knowing what I am,* he'd said. *Don't let it be wasted.*

They had taken him eleven days ago. She had counted every one.

"I'm going to make them listen," she told the mirror, quietly, so only she could hear the lie.

* * *

Getting an audience with the Council of Marrowgate proved easier than Doss predicted and harder than Lena hoped. The easy part was the gate: a cartographer of Lena's reputation—the woman who had charted the Salt Roads, who had mapped the drowned delta after the floods—could get a clerk's attention with a signature. The hard part was the waiting, two hours on a stone bench beneath portraits of dead councilors, while petitioners ahead of her argued tariffs and boundary fences as though the world outside the walls were not burning.

When she was finally admitted, the council chamber was half-empty.

Seven seats ringed the raised dais; four were filled. Speaker Halvard sat at the center, a broad man gone soft, his chain of office bright against a robe that had seen better decades. To his left, Councilor Maren of the Harbor Guild watched Lena with sharp, unhappy eyes. The other two were strangers—young men with the smooth, careful faces of Aldric's trade envoys, seated at the table's edge like guests who had learned to feel at home.

Lena's stomach dropped before anyone spoke. *Guests.* They had let the usurper's men sit in the chamber itself.

"Lena Brightwater." Halvard pronounced her name the way merchants pronounced prices. "The Salt Road cartographer. Your charts saved us two seasons of shipping losses, years back. The council remembers its debts." He gestured to a chair below the dais. "You wrote that you bring intelligence of military value."

"I do, Speaker." She remained standing. If she sat, she would look like a petitioner. If she stood, she might almost look like an equal. "King Aldric massed his army at Cael Ford through the autumn. I've walked the ground. He has forty thousand foot at least, and siege trains moving down the King's Road toward the free cities' border. He'll come through the Thornfell passes in spring, because the western route is flooded until thaw—I've confirmed it myself. That gives Marrowgate perhaps five months to prepare fortifications, coordinate with Vellin and Greyhollow, and—" she hesitated, then committed— "and evacuate anyone the edict endangers."

Silence. One of the envoys smiled faintly at his own hands.

"Evacuate," Maren repeated.

"The Gifted. Their families. Anyone Aldric has named in the edict. There are more of us than the crown admits, and every one taken strengthens his hand—he's binding them, using them. What he's building with bound gift-work, I've seen fragments of it in the mountains. It isn't weapons for a war. It's weapons for an occupation."

Halvard's heavy face did not change. "You speak of these matters openly, Mistress Brightwater. In this chamber." His eyes flicked, very briefly, to the envoys. "Boldly."

"I speak of them because the truth is already in this room," Lena said. "Everyone at this table knows what Aldric is. The question is whether Marrowgate intends to meet him or feed him."

That drew a reaction—Halvard's jaw tightened, Maren leaned forward—but it was one of the envoys who answered first, pleasantly, as though continuing a conversation begun long before Lena arrived.

"The council," the young man said, "is considering terms that preserve Marrovian lives and Marrovian commerce. His Majesty asks little. Sovereignty in name remains local. Trade continues. He requires only compliance on certain imperial matters." He turned his smile to Lena at last, and she saw in it the exact temperature of Corvin's empty house. "Certain persons of interest. A short list. Handfuls of individuals, really, against the welfare of sixty thousand souls."

"Handfuls," Lena said.

"Perhaps a dozen in Marrowgate. Fewer in the outer towns." He produced, unhurried, a folded paper from his sleeve and laid it on the table. "His Majesty's inquisitor general prepared it personally."

Maren didn't look at it. Neither did Halvard. But neither of them told the envoy to put it away, and that silence was louder than any reading.

Lena crossed the floor and took the list herself before anyone could stop her. Her hands were steady. Some part of her, the old part, the part Corvin had trained, simply went cold and precise, the way it did on a cliff path in high wind.

Nine names. Two she didn't recognize—a tanner in the Lantern District, a harbor pilot. One was a midwife she'd bought herbs from in another life. The eighth line read: *Brightwater, L.—cartographer, last seen Thornfell approaches. Gifted. Priority.*

They had spelled her name right. Someone had written it carefully, in good clerical script, with a priority mark beside it, and men in this room had sat across from the man who carried it and discussed grain tariffs.

"You're going to give them to him," she said. Not a question. She heard her own voice from far away, flat and clear. "Sixty thousand souls. Minus nine."

"Mistress Brightwater." Halvard spread his hands, and she hated how genuinely sorrowful he managed to look. "You came to us for shelter. I understand that. But you must understand what you're asking. If we refuse Aldric's terms, he burns our granaries before winter. He has done it at Thornfell—we have reports, whole villages. Sixty thousand people starve or die on pikes so that nine can hide. Any honest ruler makes that count."

"Any honest ruler," Lena said, "would notice that the count never stops at nine."

The words landed in the chamber like a dropped glass. The envoy's smile thinned. Maren looked sharply at Halvard, and Lena understood, watching them, that the debate she'd interrupted had not ended—it had merely paused for her arrival, and her arrival had given the surrender party exactly what it needed: a living example of the price of defiance, delivered to their doorstep, asking politely to be sacrificed.

"There are terms available even for those on the list," the envoy offered smoothly. "Submission. Binding, with crown oversight. Many Gifted live full lives afterward. It is not the gallows, mistress. It is order."

"I watched what order looks like," Lena said. "I pulled a child out of a burning village while crown soldiers shot at him. He's eight years old. Find him on your list." She threw the paper onto the table between the envoy and Halvard, and it slid to a stop at the chamber's center like something dead. "He's not on it. He doesn't exist to you yet. Give it time."

"That child is not Marrowgate's responsibility," Halvard said, and his voice had hardened now, the sorrow burned away. "Nor are you, though I wish it otherwise. The council will deliberate on the crown's terms as it sees fit. You understand, I hope, that we cannot be seen sheltering—"

"No," Lena said. "I understand perfectly. You cannot be seen doing anything. That's what cities are for."

She turned and walked out. No one stopped her. Behind her she heard Halvard resume in a careful, reasonable voice—something about grain reserves, about the necessity of stability—and the doors closed on it, and she stood in the marble corridor shaking with a fury that had nowhere to go.

Ten years. Ten years of maps that kept their ships off the reefs and their caravans off the cliffs and their armies out of each other's throats. She had served these cities with the one thing she was supposed to keep hidden, spending her secret a coin at a time because the work mattered, because people lived because of it. And now, when she came to them bleeding, they had priced her life against their comfort and found the exchange acceptable.

Corvin had told her to run south. She should have asked him south to *where*. There was no south. There was no city left. There was only the mountains behind her and the army ahead, and somewhere between them, a boy upstairs eating bread and asking about sweet buns.

* * *

Doss met her at the safehouse door before she knocked, which meant he'd been watching the street, which meant he'd already guessed.

"They gave you the look," he said. "The counting look. Like you were sacks of grain they might sell."

"Worse." She pushed past him inside. "They showed me the list."

Tam looked up from the window seat, bread halfway to his mouth. He saw her face and set it down.

"We're leaving tonight," Lena said. "All of us. Before dark."

"Brightwater—" Doss began.

"Did I stutter, smuggler?"

He studied her a moment, then shrugged and started gathering his things, because Doss, whatever else he was, knew when a ship had started taking water. "Where to? Vellin's no better. Greyhollow's worse—they've already posted the edict on their gates, my man saw it yesterday. Every free city is going to make the same bargain, because every free city has the same math in its head."

"Then we stop knocking on doors." Lena pulled her satchel from under the cot and began checking it by touch, blades and flint and the oilskin-wrapped true maps she had never shown anyone. Her hands moved without her mind's permission, steady, practiced. "There's a road south that isn't on any council's map. Through the Veil Pass. It comes out beyond the border marches, past anything Aldric holds."

Doss froze halfway through buckling a strap. "Nobody goes through the Veil Pass."

"Somebody does." She looked up and met his eyes. "Me."

"That pass eats travelers, girl. My father lost two brothers in those crags and they were both better climbers than I'll ever be. There's no road through there. There's barely an *through*."

"There is. I've seen it."

"You've—" He stopped. She watched him arrive at it, watched his face do the slow work of understanding, watched the smuggler's arithmetic turn over behind his eyes: the impossible routes she'd led him on, the fords that shouldn't have held, the mountain track that had appeared out of fog precisely when they needed it. His voice, when it came, was very quiet. "That's how you do it. All these years. That's your—"

"Doss."

"—your *gift*." He said the word the way other men said *plague*.

Tam had gone still on the window seat, watching them both, his bread forgotten.

"It's also the reason," Lena said, "that there's a list with my name on it, and the reason this room stops being safe the moment anyone in that council chamber decides selling me buys goodwill. So yes. Tonight. Through the Veil Pass. And I'm sorry, Doss, because I know what I'm asking—but I don't see another road, and I've spent ten years learning every road there is."

Doss stood motionless amid his half-packed goods. Outside, the evening bells of Marrowgate began to toll, bronze and indifferent, calling the city in to supper behind walls that would not hold.

"You know what the worst part is?" he said finally. "I believe you. About the pass. Gods help me, I believe you." He slung his pack over his shoulder. "But we move *now*, because that envoy didn't sit in that chamber today to negotiate. He sat there to take the measure of the council. And the moment they decide, someone will remember the cartographer who walked in off the road asking after evacuations, and someone will send for the inquisitors, and inquisitors don't wait for bells."

Lena lifted Tam's pack. The boy shrugged into it without being told, small and silent and quick, and took her hand.

As they slipped down the back stair into the dye-scented dusk, Lena glanced once at the Council Spire rising black against the last light. Somewhere in it, nine names sat on a table, and hers was written in good clerical script beside a priority mark.

Behind them, in the streets below, a watchman was hurrying toward the spire with a folded message in his fist—and Lena, who had spent her whole life seeing the paths no one else could see, did not need the gift to know where it was going.
