## 9. Veyra Closes In

The confessions of Master Corvin filled eleven pages, and Veyra had read them so many times she could recite them in the old man's cracked, apologetic voice.

*She was my finest student. She never used the gift where anyone could watch. She charted the passes by walking them, and no one ever asked how a woman alone crossed the Thornfell spine in winter and came back with her boots dry.*

Veyra sat in the map room at Fort Cael with those pages spread before her like the entrails of some sacrificial animal, and she did what she did best: she read them as testimony not to a person but to a pattern.

Corvin had broken beautifully. Not quickly—he had held out for six days in the salt cells, and Veyra had let him, because a man who broke slowly gave her everything he had, while a man who broke fast only gave her what he thought she wanted to hear. By the seventh day he had wept through his beard and told her about a girl from Brightwater Hollow who could find paths that were not there. By the eighth, he had told her the girl's name. By the ninth—when Veyra had shown him, gently, the order for his own binding, and then, more gently still, folded it away again—he had told her about the maps.

That was the key. Not the gift. The maps.

"He sold himself to protect her," Captain Ilsen said behind her. He stood by the door with the posture of a man who wished to be elsewhere. "Broke anyway."

"Everyone breaks," Veyra said. She did not look up. "What matters is the shape of the breaking." She tapped the eleventh page. "He told us about the maps because he believed they'd already been burned. He wanted to give me something worthless. But nothing Corvin made was worthless, and neither was anything she learned from him. Listen to this—" she read aloud, flatly, letting the words lie on the air like coins on a table: "*She always kept two routes out of any place she loved. One for herself. One for whoever she'd have to save.*"

Ilsen frowned. "Meaning?"

"Meaning she's not running blind. Meaning every smuggler's den, every river town, every crossroads between here and the southern sea has a second road attached to it that exists nowhere on paper. Meaning," Veyra said, rising at last, "that we will never catch Lena Brightwater by chasing her. We catch her by understanding where mercy would take her—and being there first."

She had spent nine days reconstructing the routes. It was the work she loved most, though she would have cut out her tongue before admitting it: the patient geometry of another mind. Corvin's confession gave her the teacher's habits; the burned village at Harrow's Edge gave her the student's response; the reports from the mountain garrisons gave her the timeline. A gifted boy, Tam, taken from the smoke. Sightings of a woman with a cartographer's satchel moving south along the high trails, always a day ahead, always gone before the riders arrived. Then the smuggler connection—a man called Doss, who owed debts in three free cities and paid none of them, whose barge had left Marrowgate two nights ago with a cargo manifest listing salted fish and listing, in truth, four passengers.

And one more thread, thin as spider silk: a crown sergeant named Bren, stationed at the Marrowgate ford, who had refused an order to bind a gifted child and vanished into the hills the same night the smuggler's barge put in downstream. Desertion, the garrison commander called it. Veyra called it something else. She called it *the shape of the breaking*—not Corvin's this time, but the cartographer's. The woman collected people. That was her flaw, and flaws were roads, and roads could be blocked.

"She'll cross at Greywater Ford," Veyra said.

Ilsen blinked. "There are six crossings on the Sere between Marrowgate and the hill country."

"There are five crossings she could take." Veyra swept the map clean of its scattered papers and laid down instead the thing she had drawn herself over nine sleepless nights: the ghost-road, reconstructed from Corvin's confession, from the sightings, from Doss's known habits, from the simple arithmetic of fear. It was beautiful. She almost hated it. "Greywater has the only bridge. The other fords are swollen with snowmelt—the Sere took two drovers last week at Miller's Crossing, you can ask the toll-keeper. A woman protecting a child won't risk the water. And a woman with Bren in her company won't split from him, because he doesn't know the country and she knows he'll slow them down and she'll carry him anyway. Mercy, Captain. It's the most predictable force in the world. Every route she chooses bends around it like light around a stone."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I lose three days and the crown learns humility." Veyra rolled up the ghost-map and slung it across her back beside her sword. "Ready the riders. Twenty men. No banners—we're hunting a woman, not announcing a war. And Ilsen." She paused at the door. "No bindings at the ford. If we take them, they come back alive. All of them."

"You've never brought one back alive before."

"I know," said Veyra, and did not explain, because she could not yet explain even to herself why the memory of Corvin in his cell—thanking her, at the end, *thanking* her, with the terrible gratitude of a man who had finally been allowed to stop carrying his secret—had begun to sit in her chest like a swallowed coal.

They rode through the night.

* * *

The Sere ran brown and furious with meltwater, loud enough that Veyra heard it a mile before she saw it, a constant grinding roar threading through the pines. Greywater Ford sat in a shallow valley where the river narrowed between granite shoulders and the old bridge—stone, single-span, built by some forgotten king—leapt the water in one gray arc. On the far side, the hill country began: folds of forest and crag running south toward the free cities and whatever refuge Lena Brightwater imagined waited there.

Veyra took the valley in a slow circuit from the ridgeline, and found it empty, and felt the particular cold satisfaction of a trap set well.

"Positions," she said. "Six men below the bridge on our bank, in the trees. Eight on the ridge south—they'll cover the far approach when she comes down to the water. The rest with me at the toll-house. Nobody moves until they're on the span. Nobody looses until I give the word." She looked at each face in turn. "We want them boxed on that bridge, gentlemen. Nowhere forward, nowhere back. Fear does the rest."

It rained toward evening, a thin cold rain that beaded on her oiled cloak and made the world smell of wet stone and pine rot. Veyra stood in the toll-house doorway watching the bridge dissolve and re-emerge through the shifting gray, and she permitted herself, briefly, the luxury of imagining how it would go. The woman would come down to the water with the boy's hand in hers. She would see the riders too late. And Veyra would walk onto the bridge alone—she always made the first approach alone; it disarmed people, or it broke them, and either way she learned something—and say the words she had said a hundred times: *You are not accused of anything yet. Come and talk to me.*

She wondered, not for the first time, whether Corvin had believed her, in the end, when she told him the binding was mercy. Whether any of them believed it, or whether they simply ran out of strength to refuse.

Hoofbeats? No—footsteps. Ilsen, climbing the muddy track from the trees. He crouched under the eave beside her, rain streaming off his helm.

"Riders report movement on the south trail. Five figures. Two children—one's the boy, Tam, matches the description. Three adults. One's big, soldier's bearing, crown-issue cloak with the insignia torn off." He hesitated. "The other two are Doss's crew. Smuggler colors."

"Where's the woman?"

"That's the thing." Ilsen wiped rain from his eyes. "Four coming down the trail toward the ford. The fifth—small figure, hooded—split off west along the ridge maybe half an hour ago. Alone."

Veyra went very still.

Alone. Splitting off. West, along the high ground above the river.

Not fleeing. Flanking. Or—

"No," Veyra breathed, and the word was half admiration, half fury at her own blindness. "She's not flanking. She's giving them a decoy. Four visible, one hidden—that's not a party splitting up, that's a *pattern*, and she knows I read patterns, because Corvin taught her exactly the way Corvin taught me to think." She turned. "Which one do you believe, Captain? The four walking into my trap—or the one walking away?"

"The four. Obviously."

"Obviously. Which is why she sent the four." Veyra was already moving, snatching her sword from the bench, shouting down the valley: "To me! To me—all of you, the bridge, *now!*" Her voice cracked across the roar of the river. "She means to draw us onto the span and slip past on the high trail while we're busy. Riders—cut the south ridge, intercept the lone figure. Everyone else, the bridge—she can't cross without coming down to the water eventually, there's no other way over the Sere in flood—"

But even as she said it, she saw it. Saw all of it, too late, assembling itself in her mind with horrible elegance. There *was* no other crossing—but there didn't need to be, because the four weren't meant to cross at all. They were meant to be *seen*. Meant to drive her riders onto the bridge and hold her attention while—

The first arrow took Ilsen's horse in the neck.

The animal screamed and went down sideways in the mud, and from somewhere on the western slope—above them, *behind* them, on the very ridge where Veyra had posted eight men who were now, she understood, eight men looking the wrong direction—came a ragged volley, five arrows, seven, falling among the horses at the tree line. Not aimed to kill. Aimed to scatter. And scatter they did, mounts plunging, men hauling at reins and shouting, and through the chaos Veyra saw the four figures break from cover below the bridge and sprint—not across it, *toward* it, toward the narrow span and the soldiers converging on it—and the big one, the deserter, planted himself at the near end of the bridge with his back to the river and drew a short sword.

"Hold the span!" the big man roared, in a voice built for parade grounds. "Get them across—I'll keep them honest!"

And Veyra, standing in the rain with chaos blooming on both banks, finally saw the whole design laid bare: the lone figure on the ridge was real and was bait and was also, somehow, the least important piece. The true plan was simpler and crueler. The four would cross the bridge under cover of the arrows, and the deserter would stand at its throat, and twenty of the crown's finest would spend precious minutes fighting one armed man on a narrow causeway where numbers meant nothing—while Lena Brightwater and the gifted boy went to ground in the hills until the hunt moved on.

Mercy, Veyra thought, wading into the mud, sword clearing its scabbard. She builds bridges out of it and makes us pay the toll.

* * *

The fight on the bridge lasted less than ten minutes and felt, to Bren, like a lifetime measured in heartbeats.

He had volunteered before Doss finished explaining the plan, which was perhaps the point of asking him in front of everyone. He knew what he was worth to them: a wall. He was broad, he was trained, he was expendable, and the arithmetic of it sat in his stomach like a stone even as he planted his boots on the wet granite and watched the crown riders come boiling down out of the trees.

"Lena, go," he said, without turning. Behind him he heard the others' feet hammering across the span, heard Tam's smaller stride stumble and recover, heard Doss snarl something profane and grateful. "Go. All of you."

"We'll circle back," Lena said. Her voice came close, closer than he expected, and her hand gripped his shoulder once, hard, through the ruined cloak. "Bren. You hold them as long as you can and then you get in the water if you have to. Promise me."

"I promise," he lied, because the water was a forty-foot drop into snowmelt that would fold him like wet parchment, and because she needed the lie to run. He heard her go. He heard the arrows start falling, and the horses screaming, and then the first rider was on him.

The man was young, terrified, swinging wild. Bren caught the blow on his blade's forte and shoved, and the rider's horse shied off the span's edge with a clatter of hooves. The second came better—veteran, low seat, cutting at Bren's knee—and Bren turned it and answered with a flat crack of pommel against the man's gorget that dropped him gasping across his saddlebow. The third and fourth came together, and the bridge became a narrow gray world of rain and steel and the roar of the river beneath, and Bren fought the way he'd been taught: economically, without anger, buying seconds with his body because seconds were all he had to sell.

He counted them going by. Ten heartbeats for the first pair. Twenty for the next. Somewhere in the rain on the far bank his friends were vanishing into the hills, and every heartbeat he purchased was theirs.

A voice cut through the din—level, clear, a voice accustomed to being obeyed.

"Enough!"

And the riders fell back, and Bren stood swaying on the wet stone with blood sheeting down his forearm from a cut he didn't remember taking, and watched a woman walk toward him through the rain.

She wore no helm. Dark hair plastered dark against a pale, severe face, eyes the color of slate water. She stopped six feet away, beyond his reach, and looked at him the way an assayer looks at ore.

"Sergeant Bren," she said. "Of the Marrowgate garrison. Refused a lawful order on the twelfth of this month and deserted his post." She tilted her head. "You're holding a bridge for a smuggler, a fugitive child, and the most wanted woman in the realm. Tell me, Sergeant—did they tell you what happens to deserters? Because it's worse than what happens to Gifted. The Gifted are bound. Deserters are hanged."

"Then I'm glad I'm not a deserter anymore," Bren said. His arm was shaking. He made it stop. "I resigned. Just now. Loudly. Ask your men."

Something flickered at the corner of the woman's mouth—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "You're funny," she said. "That's rare, in a dying man."

"If you're going to kill me, kill me. My friends are getting further away every minute you talk."

"They're not my priority." She sheathed her sword—an odd, deliberate gesture, unhurried—and came closer, boots ringing on stone, until the rain ran off her chin a foot from his face. "You are. Look at me, Sergeant. You refused to bind a child. So did I, once, in a manner of speaking, and it cost me more than you can imagine, and here is what I learned: mercy is a debt collector, and it always comes due, and today it's collecting from you. You held the bridge. They escaped. Well done. Now—" and her voice gentled, which was somehow worse than the threat had been, "—you're going to come with me, and you're going to tell me everything you know about Lena Brightwater, and I am going to listen. Truly listen. And when you understand what I understand, you're going to wish you'd never seen her face."

"I already wish I'd never seen yours," Bren said.

Behind the woman, the riders were regrouping in the rain, and the bridge was lost, and the hills where his friends had gone were empty and gray and safe. He thought of Tam's hand in Lena's, small and quick, disappearing into the treeline. He thought: *worth it.* He thought: *please let it have been worth it.*

Veyra studied him a moment longer, rain dripping from them both, the river roaring its indifference below.

"Bind his hands," she said quietly. "Carefully. He comes back to camp whole." And then, to Bren, as the soldiers closed around him and the bridge and the battle and the fading hope of the southern hills receded together: "You bought them an hour, Sergeant. I've hunted her for a month. Do you truly believe an hour will matter?"

Bren said nothing. But as they led him away through the rain, head high, wrists bound, he found himself thinking not of escape or regret but of Corvin—the old teacher he'd never met, the man whose confession had set this whole hunt in motion—and wondering, with a cold creeping certainty that had nothing to do with the weather:

*How long did he hold out? And what did it buy?*

Because if the answer was nothing—if mercy's toll was always paid in full and never in victory—then everything Bren had bled for on this bridge was just the first installment of a debt Lena Brightwater would spend the rest of her short, hunted life repaying.

The soldiers marched him north, into the darkening rain, toward the salt cells and the woman with the slate-gray eyes—and toward whatever truth she claimed would make him wish he'd never seen a friend's face at all.
