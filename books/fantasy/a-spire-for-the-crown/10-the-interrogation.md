## 10. The Interrogation

They took his boots first.

Bren didn't understand why until the cell door clanged shut behind him and the cold of the stone floor climbed through his socks like a living thing. Boots, belt, laces, the little knife he'd carried since Vell's Crossing—all of it inventoried by a quartermaster's clerk who wrote down *one folding knife, bone handle* in a voice as flat as a ledger. It wasn't cruelty. It was procedure. And procedure, Bren had learned in eleven years under the crown's banner, was worse than cruelty. Cruelty at least admitted it wanted something from you. Procedure simply processed you, the way a mill processes grain, and what came out the other side was nobody's business but the miller's.

The cell was a room, not a hole. That surprised him. A cot with a wool blanket, a bucket, a candle in a wall sconce that someone had actually lit. A high slit of window let in a gray thread of afternoon. He'd expected the Oubliette under Marrowgate's old keep, the place soldiers whispered about when they'd drunk enough to whisper at all. Instead they'd put him somewhere that looked almost like a guest room, if guest rooms had iron rings bolted to the walls and a door thick enough to stop a ram.

He slept anyway. That was the soldier in him—sleep when you can, eat when you can, because you don't know when either will be taken from you. He slept the way he had after Vell's Crossing too, in shallow slices, his hands curling around the memory of the girl's wrist, the iron cuff, the way she had looked at him not with hatred but with something worse. With *expectation*. As if she'd believed, right up until the moment he refused, that no soldier in the king's army would ever refuse.

*You didn't refuse,* said the voice that had been talking to him since the bridge. *You refused too late. You held the bridge for her, yes. But you marched her to the binding post first. You stood in the line. You counted yourself one of Aldric's men for eleven years and only grew a spine when it cost you nothing but your own neck.*

"That's not true," he said to the empty cell, and the empty cell did not answer, which was a kind of answer.

* * *

They came for him on the second morning.

Two guards, and behind them a woman in a traveling coat of dark green wool, mud-stained at the hem, her hair pulled back in a braid gone loose from the road. He'd expected the inquisitor to be a monster out of a fireside story—hook-nosed, black-robed, eyes like a hawk's. Veyra was none of those things. She was perhaps forty, with a tired face and ink stains on her fingers, and when she looked at him her gaze had the particular patience of someone who had already read the ending of the book and was simply waiting for him to catch up.

"Sergeant Bren of the Third Crown Company," she said. Not a question. She carried a wooden case under one arm and a clay jug in her other hand. "Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit anyway. You'll want to be sitting by the end." She set the jug on the small table bolted to the floor—everything in the room was bolted, he noticed, even the table—and poured two cups of something that steamed and smelled of honey and cheap wine. "This is not a trick. If I wanted to poison you I'd have done it while you were unconscious. You slept like a dead man, by the way. Eleven years of soldiering and you still trust a bed given to you by your enemies."

He sat. He told himself it was strategy. He drank the wine because refusing it would have been its own kind of answer.

"I'm going to tell you how this will go," Veyra said, "because unlike some of my colleagues, I find that men lie less when they know the shape of the trap. You are charged with desertion, theft of crown property—" she glanced at a paper she didn't bother to unfold, "—your boots, technically, and aiding the escape of a convicted Gifted. The penalty for the first is hanging. The second is a fine. The third is the binding post, if the Gifted is recovered, or hanging if she isn't. She wasn't. So: hanging."

"You could skip the wine, then."

"I could." She drank from her own cup, unhurried. "But here is the thing, Sergeant. I have interrogated forty-one people in the last year. Deserters, smugglers, priests, one very stubborn grandmother who kept a Gifted boy in her root cellar for two winters. And I have learned that torture produces exactly one thing: whatever the torturer wants to hear. It is a mirror. I am not interested in mirrors. I am interested in the Brightwater woman, and in the paths she walks, and in why a decorated sergeant with a pension due in four years threw it all away on a bridge over the Sere."

"Maybe I just liked the view."

"Maybe." She set down her cup and unlatched the wooden case. "Or maybe you're about to learn something that will make the view very different. Which would you rather I do, Sergeant—ask you questions, or show you the truth? Because I've brought the truth. It's heavier. It always is."

* * *

What she showed him was paper.

She laid it out on the bolted table the way a merchant lays out cloth—carefully, corner to corner, each document squared with the last. Writs with the seal of the old king, Aldric's father. Ledger pages in a clerk's cramped hand. A fragment of something older, the vellum browned and crackling, written in a script Bren couldn't read but recognized the way you recognize a face from a bad dream.

"Do you know what the Gift is?" Veyra asked.

"A curse," Bren said. "That's what the priests say. That's what the edict says. Blood-touched, spirit-ridden, an open door for things that should stay shut."

"The edict says that. Yes." She tapped the oldest fragment. "This says otherwise. This is a copy—the original is in the royal archive under the old keep, and I will eat my seal if Aldric hasn't burned it by now—of the Covenant of Auren. Three hundred years old. Older. It was signed by the last king of the old line and by a speaker for the spire-folk. Do you know what the spire-folk were?"

"Stories. Nursemaids' tales. Towers that walked away."

"Towers that walked away," Veyra repeated, and for just a moment something moved behind her eyes that was not patience at all. "Yes. That's the version in the schoolbooks. Here is the version in the record." She turned the fragment toward him. "The spire-folk were real. They were the keepers of the paths—the ways between places, the deep roads under the world. When the old realms were drowning in their own wars, the spire-folk offered a covenant: they would open the ways to human wardens, a few in every generation, born with the sight for them. The Gifted. In exchange, the realms swore protection. Sanctuary. *The Gifted are keys, not weapons.* That phrase is in the text. It's in the text three times, Sergeant, because someone three hundred years ago already knew what kings forget."

"Old parchment," Bren said. His mouth had gone dry. "Old stories. Men have forged covenants since men learned to write."

"Men have. This one wasn't forged, because this one was broken in a way no forger would bother to fake." She pulled a ledger page forward, the ink faded to the color of dried blood. "Read the entry. Go on. You can read, can't you? A sergeant of the Third?"

He could. He read it, and the cell went very quiet around the words.

*In the eleventh year of the reign of King Osric, by royal command, the wardens of the Gift were gathered at the town of Harrow's Ford and bound with iron, their gift stripped and given to the crown's use. The spire-folk speaker protested. The speaker was refused. The ways were closed by the spire-folk in grief, and the crown's roads failed within the year.*

"Harrow's Ford," Bren said. The name sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

"You know it."

"Everyone knows it. It's a ruin. They say it burned."

"It didn't burn. It was emptied. Two hundred Gifted, Sergeant. Children among them. Bound with iron on the king's order, their gift *harvested*—that's the word the clerk uses, harvested, like wheat—and poured into the making of the crown's war-roads. Roads that ran where no road should run. Roads that let Osric's armies appear behind walls that had never feared an army." Veyra's voice stayed level, but her hands, he noticed, had gone very still on the table, the way a woman's hands go still when she is holding something down. "The war-roads worked for nine years. Then the ways closed. Then the spire-folk sealed their spires and left, and every Gifted child born since has been born into a kingdom that calls them a curse."

"That was two hundred years ago," Bren said. "Different kings. Different men."

"Yes. And what did Aldric do, in his first year, when the Gifted began to be born again?" She didn't wait for him to answer. She drew out another paper—newer, the ink still sharp. "He didn't rebuild the covenant. He didn't ask what had been broken. He wrote this. *All persons of the Gift to be registered, bound, and held at the crown's pleasure.* The same words, Sergeant. Nearly the same words. I've compared them. I spent three nights comparing them, and I will tell you what I found, because I found it and I have not slept properly since."

She leaned forward. Up close, in the candlelight, he could see the doubt she carried—it was carved into her the way a river carves a bank, slow and permanent.

"I found that the crown did not break faith with the Gifted," she said. "The crown broke faith first, and has been calling the breaking a curse ever since. Every edict, every binding post, every village burned to catch one frightened child—none of it is protection. It is a king repeating his ancestor's crime and calling it law."

* * *

Bren sat with it.

He wanted to throw the papers back at her. He wanted to say *forgery, lies, the tricks of traitors*, and for a moment he actually gathered the breath for it—and then he thought of the girl at Vell's Crossing. Twelve years old. Barefoot, because they'd taken her boots too. She had looked at him with expectation, and he had understood, in that moment, that she had been told all her life that the king's soldiers were good men, and she had *believed* it, and the belief had lasted right up until the iron touched her wrists.

He thought of the binding posts he'd escorted. Four of them, over six years. He had told himself each time that it was law, that the Gifted were dangerous, that the priests said the blood-touched opened doors for hungry things. He had told himself that the crying stopped eventually, and it did, and he had never once asked himself what it meant that it stopped.

"You're showing me this to break me," he said finally. "This is the torture. Not the irons. The truth. You want me to sit here and realize that eleven years of my life were spent enforcing a lie, and then you want me to be so grateful for the realization that I give you the Brightwater woman's routes out of sheer need to have done *something* right."

Veyra regarded him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, tired thing, without much pleasure in it.

"There it is," she said. "Do you know, you're the first one to see it that clearly. The grandmother just wept. The priest started praying. But you—yes. That is exactly what I'm doing. And the question, Sergeant, the only question that matters, is whether it works." She began, unhurried, to square the papers back into their case. "Because here is my confession, since we're trading them. I requested this hunt personally. I told the crown I would bring them the Brightwater woman and the boy, and I meant it. I have served the law my whole life because the law was the only thing between the realms and the knife-fight. And then I read the Harrow's Ford ledger, and I discovered that the law has been the knife-fight, all along, and I have been holding the knife."

"Then why hunt us at all?"

"Because Aldric's armies are marching on the free cities, and the man who commands the inquisition does not stop being that man because he has doubts." She latched the case. "Because if I do not find the Brightwater woman, someone worse will—someone who still believes, and belief, Sergeant, is the most dangerous thing on a battlefield. And because I am not yet certain what I am. Doubt is not the same as turning. I want you to understand that. I could still hang you tomorrow and sleep soundly, or I could sleep soundly because I didn't, and I genuinely do not know which man I'll be when I wake."

"That's a hell of a thing for an inquisitor to admit."

"I'm not in the cell, Sergeant. You are." She stood, and the guards at the door straightened. "You'll have tonight to think. In the morning I'll ask you my questions, and you'll answer them or you won't, and either way the hanging stands unless something changes. That's the shape of the trap. I promised you the shape."

She paused at the door. He watched her hand rest on the iron ring of the handle, and he saw—clearly, the way you see a thing only when it's almost gone—that the hand was not steady.

"The girl at the ford," she said, without turning. "The one you refused to bind. What was her name?"

"Netta," Bren said. "Her name was Netta."

Veyra was quiet for a moment. "Netta," she repeated, as if filing it somewhere no clerk would ever find it. Then the door closed, and the candle burned, and Bren was alone with the truth, which was heavier than she had promised, and which he carried now whether he wanted it or not.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He sat on the cot with the wool blanket around his shoulders and the candle guttering, and he did the arithmetic soldiers do in the dark. Eleven years. Four binding posts. One girl named Netta, whose face he would carry into whatever morning came next. And somewhere south of him, past the Sere and the smuggler roads he had helped Lena Brightwater escape along, a woman was climbing toward a ruin with a child and a smuggler and a debt, carrying a covenant he now knew the crown had broken first.

*Keys, not weapons.* The phrase sat in him like a coal.

In the gray before dawn, he heard boots in the corridor—more boots than a breakfast delivery needed—and the rattle of keys, and he understood, with the flat calm of a man who has already counted his odds, that something had changed in the night. The guards who opened his door were not his two from yesterday. These were Aldric's own, gray-cloaked, and the warrant one of them thrust at him bore not Veyra's seal but a black wax stamp he had only ever seen on orders no one read aloud.

"Prisoner's transferred to the army column," the guard said. "The inquisitor's been relieved."

"Relieved," Bren repeated. "Of me?"

"Of everything," the guard said, and there was something in his voice—something almost like pity, or almost like fear—that told Bren the night's changes ran far deeper than one prisoner's cell. Somewhere in the keep above, Veyra was learning what it cost to doubt a king. And somewhere south, past the Sere, the woman with the ink-stained fingers and the tired eyes would either come after him with her blade or with the truth—and he found, to his own disgust, that he could not yet say which he feared more, or which he hoped for.

The guards took his arms. The candle behind him guttered out, and they marched him down into the dark toward the sound of an army waking.
