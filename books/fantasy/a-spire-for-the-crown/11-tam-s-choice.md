## 11. Tam's Choice

Lena woke to an empty bedroll.

She knew it before her eyes opened—knew it by the quality of the silence, the way the fire's crackle had no small breathing underneath it. For ten years she had slept in the wild places of Thornfell, and she had learned to read a campsite the way other people read a page. Doss snored like a rockslide in the making. Bren did not snore at all but turned twice an hour, a soldier's habit even in desertion. And Tam—Tam breathed the way children breathe when they are finally safe: quick and light and utterly unguarded.

That breathing was gone.

She was on her feet before thought caught up, knife already in her hand, eyes raking the tree line. The camp sat in a hollow below an old shepherd's ruin, three days south of Marrowgate, close enough to hear its bells on the wind if the wind blew right. It hadn't blown right in two days. Since the council's vote. Since the free cities had decided that peace was worth the price of every gifted soul among them.

Since then, Tam had not slept well. Neither had she.

"Tam?" she whispered, loud enough to carry, soft enough not to wake the others. Nothing answered but the wind moving through black pines.

She found his trail without trying—which was itself a kind of grief, because she had taught herself never to try, never to use the gift where anyone might see the way her eyes slid sideways off the world and found the paths beneath. But there was no one here to see, and fear made the choice for her. The boy's passage glowed faintly against the dark of the ordinary ground, a thread of pale gold only she could follow, heading north and down, toward the road. Toward Marrowgate.

Toward surrender.

* * *

She ran. The forest gave way to scree, the scree to the long grey shoulder of the valley, and the gift unspooled the distance for her so that each stride covered what should have taken three. This was the thing she had never told anyone—not Corvin, not Doss—that using the gift felt less like magic than like remembering. As though some older, wiser version of the world had already walked this path and left the walking behind, and all she did was step into its footprints.

She caught him at the ford where the river flattened into shallows, the same kind of crossing Bren had held against Veyra's riders barely a week past. Tam stood ankle-deep in the black water, arms wrapped around himself, wearing the coat she'd cut down for him from one of Doss's spares. He looked very small in it. He looked very small in everything lately.

"Tam."

He flinched so hard he nearly fell. When he turned, his face was wet—not from the river.

"You shouldn't have followed me." His voice cracked in the middle. Eleven years old, and his voice cracked in the middle of everything now. "You should let me go."

"The water's freezing. You'd be numb to the knee before you reached the far bank." She kept her tone level, conversational, the way you spoke to a frightened horse. "And Veyra doesn't post sentries on the Marrowgate road anymore. She posts them everywhere else. You'd walk straight past the one trap she didn't bother setting because nobody's fool enough to walk into Marrowgate voluntarily."

"They voted," Tam said. "I heard them. Hand over the Gifted and the armies go home. That's what they said. That's—" His jaw worked. "That's what my village would have wanted. To be safe."

"Your village burned anyway."

The words came out harder than she intended, and she saw them land, saw the boy fold inward like paper in a fist. She crossed the shallows and crouched in front of him, water soaking through her boots, cold biting up her shins. Close enough to grab him if she had to. Close enough that grabbing him would break something that could not easily be mended.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was cruel, and you didn't deserve it. But it's true, and I think you're old enough for true things now. They burned your village because a neighbor reported a gifted child who'd never harmed anyone. Surrendering yourself won't unburn it. It'll just give them one more pyre."

"They'll bind me, not burn me," Tam whispered. "That's what they say. Bind the Gifted and they can't hurt anyone."

"And what do you think binding is?"

He didn't answer. His eyes went to the water, to his own reflection broken and reassembled by the current.

"It's cutting," Lena said quietly. "They take a blade of cold iron and salt and they cut the gift out at the root, and sometimes the person survives it and sometimes they don't, and either way what's left afterward isn't quite—they smile at the wrong moments. They forget their mothers' faces. Master Corvin called them hollow coats walking around." Her throat tightened on his name. She pushed past it. "I've seen one. A woman in Highvale, years ago. She'd been bound as a girl. She stood in the market square for six hours because someone had told her to wait for her husband, and she couldn't remember whether she'd been waiting minutes or years, and she couldn't make herself stop waiting because the waiting was all that was left of the instruction. That's mercy, Aldric-style."

Tam's chin trembled. "Then why does everyone want to give us up? If it's that bad, why—"

"Because they're afraid." She said it simply, without heat. "Fear makes people trade other people's hands for their own safety. It's the oldest arithmetic there is, and it has never once come out right. The council voted to hand over the Gifted because they believe we're weapons. Dangerous things to be surrendered or destroyed." She paused. "And that, Tam, is the last lie I'm going to let stand between us. Because you've earned better than lies, and frankly, so have I."

* * *

She led him back across the ford to a flat stone above the waterline, and sat him down, and sat beside him, and for a while neither of them spoke. The sky was going grey in the east—the false dawn that comes an hour before the true one—and the river talked to itself in the dark, patient, indifferent.

"When I was younger than you," Lena began, "I found a pass through Thornfell that isn't on any map. Not because no one had looked. Because it can't be found—not by climbing, not by searching. It exists along a line that only certain people can see. I thought I was the only one. Then Corvin found me, and I learned I wasn't, and I learned something else too, something he made me swear to keep quiet until I understood it fully." She turned to look at the boy. "I still don't understand it fully. But I understand enough, and you deserve the truth before you do anything else brave and foolish tonight."

Tam wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Okay."

"The Gifted aren't weapons," Lena said. "We're keys."

He frowned. "Keys to what?"

"That's the part I'm still learning. But here's what I know." She pulled off her glove and held up her hand, and after a moment's hesitation—after ten years of hesitation—she let the gift rise openly between them, the first time she had ever done such a thing deliberately in front of another living soul. The world shifted. The darkness over the river thinned like gauze, and beneath it Tam could see—or she watched his face and knew he could see—the faint gold tracery of paths running through the air and stone and water, ancient lines converging toward the north, toward the high white spine of Thornfell, toward something vast and sleeping.

Tam's mouth fell open. "What—what are those?"

"Roads," Lena said. "Older than any kingdom. Older than the crown, older than the free cities, older probably than the mountains themselves. They were built by a people we've forgotten, and the forgetting wasn't an accident—it was part of a bargain. A covenant. The spire-folk sealed themselves away and wove their protection into the land, and the Gifted were left behind as... hinges. Lock-keepers. People who could open the ways if the day ever came that the covenant needed reforging." She let the vision fade and pulled her glove back on; the ordinary dark rushed in like cold water. "King Aldric calls us abominations. The council calls us liabilities. But we're neither. We're the only reason the old protection can ever come back—and I think, more and more, that it needs to come back soon, because I think Aldric means to tear the seal apart and take what's inside."

"Inside the—" Tam swallowed. "Inside the Spire."

"You've heard of it."

"Everyone's heard of it. It's a story. Ruin at the top of the world, ghosts of starlight, all that." He glanced at her sidelong. "It's real, isn't it. That's where we're going. That's why Veyra won't stop chasing us."

"That's where we're going." Lena let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for a decade. Saying it aloud, plainly, to a child, made it suddenly enormous and undeniable. "And here's the thing about keys, Tam. A key that walks into the enemy's hand doesn't just get bound. It gets used. If Veyra takes you, they won't stop at cutting the gift out. Corvin thought they'd learned how to turn a Gifted inside out—to make the key open doors for whoever holds it. So when you walked toward Marrowgate tonight thinking you were saving us, what you were actually doing was delivering the weapon Aldric wants most directly into his grip."

The boy was silent for a long moment. The grey deepened; the first true light touched the ridgetops and turned them the color of old bone.

"I just want it to stop," he said finally, very small. "The running. Being the reason people burn. Being the reason Bren got taken. Being the reason Doss lost half his crew. I'm tired of being the thing everyone protects."

Lena's chest ached. She put an arm around his narrow shoulders, and he didn't pull away.

"Do you know why I protect you?" she asked. "It's not duty. It's not because Corvin asked. It's because when I was eleven, a woman hid me in a root cellar while riders tore apart my family's farm looking for me, and I swore, lying in the dark listening to her lie for me, that if I ever got out of this life alive, I would be that cellar for somebody else. You're not the reason people burn, Tam. Greedy men and frightened councils and one usurper with a crown are the reasons people burn. You're just the excuse. And I will not let you hand yourself to them to salve a guilt that was never yours." She squeezed his shoulders. "You want to repay us? Live. Grow up. Learn what your gift really is. Someday you may be the one standing between a frightened child and the fire, and I want you there for it."

Tam cried then—quietly, the way boys cry when they've been taught crying costs them, face turned away toward the river. Lena let him. She watched the light climb down the far slopes and thought about Bren in Veyra's hands and wondered, not for the first time, what truth-torture was doing to him, and whether mercy was a debt that always came due at the worst possible hour.

When Tam's breathing steadied, he asked, "The starlight woman. In the Veil Pass. You said she warned you the covenant was breaking. Do you think we'll see her again?"

"I think she finds us when she wants to," Lena said. "I think she's been finding Gifted for a thousand years and getting nothing but ash and silence back. And I think—" She stopped.

Because the river had stopped talking.

Not frozen, not dammed—stopped, the way a speaker stops mid-sentence when someone important enters the room. The water hung in the shallows, silvered, motionless, and in it—no. Above it. Standing on the surface of the halted current as though it were a courtyard floor, was a figure of woven light, tall and sorrowful, her edges dissolving into motes that drifted upward against the wind.

Tam made a sound low in his throat and grabbed Lena's arm.

The woman raised her head. Her face was like the memory of a face—beautiful the way a half-remembered song is beautiful—and her eyes, when they found Lena's, were full of something worse than anger. Urgency.

*"Brightwater,"* she said, and her voice was the sound the paths made when Lena walked them, the sound of the world remembering itself. *"There is little time. The seal thins. Come to the shrine in the elder wood—hear the whole of it, or everything you have suffered will be for nothing."*

The light folded inward like a closing hand, and the river remembered how to move, and the dawn came up ordinary and gold over Thornfell.

Tam's grip on her arm was trembling. "Did she say shrine?"

"She did." Lena rose, pulling him up with her, and looked north—away from Marrowgate, away from safety, toward the elder wood and whatever waited in its ruins. "And for once, Tam, I intend to arrive somewhere before trouble does. Let's wake the others. We ride within the hour."
