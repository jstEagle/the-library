## 13. Prison of Salt

The cell smelled of salt and old iron.

Bren learned the smell before he learned anything else, because for the first three days they kept him hooded in a moving cart, and the world had been reduced to jolt and stench and the wet rasp of his own breathing through rough cloth. Salt, he decided. Sea salt, coarse and mineral, the kind they spread on the floors of storehouses to keep rot down. Which meant the coast. Which meant—his stomach turned over slowly, like something heavy rolling in a ship's hold—that they had taken him south, away from the river ford, away from Lena and Tam and Doss, away from the bridge he had held with a borrowed sword and a promise he hadn't known how to keep.

He had held it long enough. That was the arithmetic he clung to in the dark. Long enough for the mule to carry Tam across. Long enough for Doss's wagons to reach the treeline. Long enough for Lena to look back at him once—only once, her face white and furious and grateful all at once, a face he suspected he would be looking at for a long time—before the inquisitor's men pulled him down into the mud.

They had not tortured him. That was the strangest part. Veyra had sat across from him in a tent that smelled of lamp oil and paper, and she had not raised her voice, and she had not touched him. She had shown him documents. Sealed crown archives, opened with a key she wore on a chain at her throat, and what was in them had done more damage than any iron could have. *The crown broke it first.* He could still see the ink, the neat clerical hands, the dates. He could still hear his own voice saying, *That's not possible,* and hear how small it sounded.

Then the hood, the cart, the salt.

On the fourth day, they took the hood off.

* * *

The prison had been a lighthouse once. Bren understood that from the shape of the room they put him in—a tall cylinder of black stone, a spiral stair climbing the outer wall, cells cut into the rock like nests in a cliff. The Salt Spire, a guard called it, with the flat pride of a man reciting something he'd been told. Below it the sea worked at the rocks without pause, a sound like slow grinding teeth. Above, where the great lamp had burned in the days of the old kings, there was nothing now but a cage of rusted iron and a gull's nest, and through the empty lamp-room the grey light of the coast came down the central shaft and fell in a long pale column onto the cell floors.

Bren's cell was on the fourth ring. Two paces by three. A slot for bread, a bucket, a wall worn smooth where a hundred prisoners before him had leaned their shoulders and stared at the column of light and counted the hours by its slow crawl across the floor.

He counted. It was better than thinking. Thinking led to the girl in the binding wagon, the one he'd refused to shackle, the one whose name he'd never learned. Thinking led to the bridge. Thinking led to Veyra's voice, level as a clerk's: *You were a good soldier, Sergeant. That is precisely why you should have read the archives sooner.*

On the sixth day, the man in the next cell spoke to him.

"You breathe like a soldier," the voice said. Old, cracked, but with a spine of iron under the crack, the voice of a man who had spent decades being listened to. "Short breaths at the top of the hour. You're counting the light too."

Bren sat up from the floor. The column of grey light had crawled to the seam between his wall and the next, which meant the cells were close, which meant a whisper would carry.

"Who's there?"

"A prisoner. Like you." A dry sound that might once have been a laugh. "Though I've been here longer. Long enough to forget my own counting. Tell me yours."

"Forty-two days since the ford," Bren said, and then, because the voice had hooked something in him, "Sergeant Bren Calloway. Fourth Crown Foot. Or I was."

Silence. Then the voice came again, and it had changed—something urgent and raw beneath the iron now, a man gripping a bar of a thing.

"Calloway. The sergeant who held the bridge at the Vell. Who wouldn't give up the girl at Harrow Mill. They talk about you, down the rings. The guards talk. The prisoners talk more." A pause, and then, almost gently: "You're the one who was traveling with Brightwater."

Bren was on his feet before he knew it, hands flat against the cold stone, as if he could press through it. "You know her?"

"I taught her everything she knows," the old man said. "And half of what she doesn't. My name is Corvin."

* * *

They spoke through the wall for six days.

It was not easy. The stone swallowed half of every word, and the guards made their rounds on an irregular schedule designed, Bren suspected, to prevent exactly what they were doing. But the column of light gave them warning—when it reached the iron ring bolted into the ceiling of the lamp-room far above, the guards would come within the quarter hour—and so they learned to talk in the long safe stretches between, two voices in the dark, trading a decade of stolen time.

Bren learned what had happened at the ford after he fell. Veyra had taken him alive because she wanted the route to the Spire of Auren, and when he'd given her nothing—not out of courage, he was honest with himself, but because he genuinely had only fragments—she'd shipped him south to the Salt Spire to rot until she needed him again. He learned that the crown's grip on the coast was thinner than the edicts made it sound; the guards were half of them press-ganged fishermen who muttered about Aldric's levies. He learned that Corvin had been taken from his tower in the Thornfell foothills on the same night Lena fled, and that he had spent the months since being moved from prison to prison, always just ahead of anyone who might look for him.

"And she's looking?" Bren asked. "Lena. She'd look."

"She'd burn down the realm to look," Corvin said. "That is her strength and her curse, and I have spent ten years trying to teach her the difference." A pause, and the old man's voice went quieter. "Tell me about her. When you left her. What was her face?"

Bren leaned his head against the stone. "Angry. At me, I think. For staying behind."

"Good," Corvin said softly. "Anger means she'll carry you with her. Grief would mean she'd already buried you." Then, sharper: "But she must not come here. Do you understand me, Calloway? Whatever happens, whatever you suffer, whatever they promise you—she must go to Auren. The Spire. Nothing else matters. Not me, not you, not the free cities. Auren."

"You keep saying that like I know what it means."

"You don't. Neither does she, not fully. But she's the only one who can learn it in time." The old man coughed, a long tearing sound that went on too long, and when he spoke again his voice had thinned. "I'm dying, Sergeant. I'll say that plainly, because I've no patience for the other way of saying it. The salt gets into the lungs, down here. It's in all of us. I have perhaps a season, and the guards have perhaps less patience. So we will not waste the time we have."

* * *

It was on the twelfth day that Corvin told him about the price.

Bren had been asking—carefully, the way you approach a wounded animal—about the covenant. What it was. Why the crown feared it. What a cartographer and a smuggler and a deserter could possibly do that Aldric's armies could not. And Corvin had answered in his teacher's way, patiently, in pieces, the way you teach a child to swim by degrees: the spire-folk, the old accord, the Gifted as keys rather than weapons, the Spire of Auren as the lock.

"But here is what no one will tell her," Corvin said, "because no one knows it but me and the dead. The rite at Auren is not free. Nothing that old is free. The covenant is woven from human memory—it always was, that was the first betrayal and the first gift, we gave the spire-folk our remembering and they gave us our roads. To reforge it, a Gifted must feed it. And it will not take gold or blood or years of life." His voice dropped until Bren had to press his ear to the stone. "It takes memory. All of it. Every face she loves, every voice, every kindness ever done to her—poured out into the covenant like water into cracked ground. She will save everyone she has ever cared for, and she will not know a single one of their names afterward."

The column of light stood perfectly still in the middle of the floor, the way it did at midday, and Bren found he could not move at all.

"She'd do it," he said. "You know she would."

"I know," Corvin said, and there was something broken and proud in it. "That's why I'm telling you. Because when the moment comes, she'll do it gladly, and someone must be there afterward to remember for her. That is the debt, Calloway. Not to stop her—you can't stop her, and you shouldn't. The debt is to remember. Find her after. Tell her who she was. Tell her who we were." A long breath. "Tell her the price is memory, and that it was worth paying, and that Corvin of Thornfell never once regretted teaching her to read the paths. Can you carry that?"

Bren closed his eyes. He thought of the bridge, the splintering rail, the look on her face. He thought of Tam's thin shoulders. He thought of the girl at Harrow Mill, whose name he would never learn, and of Veyra's documents, and of all the things he had believed that had turned out to be ink on a clerk's page.

"I'll carry it," he said.

"Then listen," said Corvin, "because I've been counting more than the light, these twelve days. And I think I know how you get out."

* * *

The supply wagons came up the coast road on the first day of the month, when the tide ran low enough to beach a boat below the Spire's seaward wall.

Bren knew this because Corvin knew it, and Corvin knew it because he had spent twelve days doing what he had spent a lifetime doing: watching, listening, and teaching. The old man's cell was one ring below Bren's, close enough to the storerooms that he heard everything—the guards' complaints, the cook's curses, the rhythm of the place the way you learn the rhythm of a river before you swim it. The garrison was eleven men. Four would go down to the landing to help haul the wagons' crates up the cliff stair. Two would be on the wall. Two slept nights, one drank, and the turnkey—Bren, a boy of maybe nineteen, with a stammer and a mother in Marrowgate—could be bought with a story about what happened to press-ganged fishermen when the free cities won.

"And they will win," Corvin had said. "Or something will. The wheel is turning, boy. I can hear it from in here."

The plan was not much of a plan. It was, in truth, barely a hope wearing a plan's clothes. But on the morning the supply sails came up out of the grey sea like a promise, Bren stood at his wall slot with his heart going like a drum and waited for the sound of the landing party's boots fading down the cliff stair.

It came. Faint, then fainter, then gone under the grind of the sea.

"Now," Corvin's voice said through the stone, tight with effort. "The bar. Lift and turn. I told you—the fitting's rotted, I've heard it shift in the wind for weeks."

The bar was iron, and Bren's hands had been hands that broke doors for the crown for nine years. He got his fingers through the slot, found the bar's end, and lifted until his shoulders screamed and the blood sang in his ears—and the bar turned, grudging, shrieking, and the door swung open into the column of grey light.

He was down the spiral stair in a dozen heartbeats. Corvin's door was harder—the old man had no strength to help—but between Bren's shoulders and the rotted fitting it gave, and then Bren was looking at Master Corvin of Thornfell for the first time.

He was a ruin. That was the truth of it, and Bren took it in with one soldier's glance and did not flinch from it. White hair gone yellow with salt. Cheeks hollowed. Hands like bundled sticks. But the eyes were still the eyes of a man who had mapped mountains, bright and fierce and amused at the world's expense, and they went over Bren in the same quick soldier's glance and found him sufficient.

"You're taller than she described," Corvin said.

"Can you walk?"

"I can walk," the old man said, "badly, and complain the whole way. Help me to the lamp-room. There's a boat."

* * *

There was a boat. A fisher's coracle, tarred and patched, hauled up into the lamp-room's rusted cage by some prisoner years ago and forgotten, because who escapes downward into the sea? They dragged it to the seaward edge of the lamp-room floor, where the old lighthouse's delivery hoist still hung from a beam of black oak, and Bren lowered Corvin first, hand over hand down thirty feet of rope to the wave-cut shelf below, and then went down himself with the rope burning his palms and the sea exploding white against the rocks beneath him.

The tide was with them. The current off the Spire's point ran south toward the fishing villages, and the villages, Corvin said between ragged breaths, were full of men who hated Aldric's levies and would ask no questions of a soldier and a dying old man in a borrowed boat.

They were a mile out, the Salt Spire shrinking behind them into the grey, when Corvin spoke.

"You could have gone alone," the old man said. "The rope would have held for one. The boat rows easier with one. Every hour you spend hauling my dying weight south is an hour Veyra gains."

Bren pulled the oar. Watched the water. "She told me once," he said, "that mercy costs. That it's the most expensive thing a soldier can buy." The oar turned. The boat slid. "I'm paying."

Corvin was quiet for a long moment. When Bren glanced at him, the old man was looking back at the Spire, at the empty lamp-room where the great light had once burned, and his eyes were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it.

"The price is memory," Corvin said. "Tell her that. Tell her the covenant will ask her for every face she loves—and tell her, Bren Calloway, tell her from me, that it is worth it. That I would pay it again. That a life spent keeping the roads open for others is not a life lost." He turned back, and the fierce bright eyes fixed on Bren's, and his voice cracked at last, all the iron gone out of it. "And tell her I'm sorry I never told her sooner. She'll be angry. Let her be angry. Anger means she'll carry me with her."

"I'll tell her," Bren said. "Every word."

The coast rolled by, grey and indifferent. Somewhere south, past the fishing villages and the salt marshes, past Veyra's patrols and Aldric's mustering armies, a company of smugglers and a boy and a woman who walked invisible roads were climbing toward a mountain. Bren pulled the oar and counted his strokes, the way he'd counted the light, the way he counted everything now—because counting was how a man kept his purpose when everything else was taken, and his purpose had a name, and a message, and a mountain at the end of it.

Behind them, faint on the wind, a horn sounded from the Salt Spire. They had found the empty cells.

Bren pulled harder.
