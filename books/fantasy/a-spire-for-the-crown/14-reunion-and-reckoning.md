## 14. Reunion and Reckoning

The camp woke to the sound of a stick snapping in the dark, and half the smugglers had knives out before Lena could blink.

She was already on her feet, Tam's shoulder gripped in one hand, her other hand raised for stillness. The fire had been burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones, and beyond it the pines stood black and crowded, full of the small noises a forest makes when it is only being a forest. Wind. The tick of cooling wood. Somewhere far off, an owl asking its one question.

Then the owl asked again, wrong—twice where it should have been once—and Lena's heart slammed against her ribs.

That was Corvin's signal. Three years gone, and she still knew it. Two hoots and a pause meant *friend*. One long, broken note meant *come carefully*.

The sound came again from the treeline to the east, ragged this time, the way a voice goes ragged when the throat making it has been screaming recently.

"Stay," she breathed to Tam, and stepped past the fire.

Doss fell in beside her before she'd taken five strides, a hatchet riding low in his fist, his face a pale smear beneath his hood. "Could be bait," he muttered.

"Could be."

"Could be Veyra's people, using what your master told them."

Lena didn't answer. She moved toward the dark, and the dark let her, parting along paths her feet knew without asking. That was the gift working quietly, the way it always did—showing her the line of least resistance through the world, the seam in things. She followed it until a shape detached itself from a pine trunk and collapsed toward her, and she caught it by the shoulders before her mind caught up with her eyes.

Bren.

He was a ruin of himself. His sergeant's coat was gone, replaced by a stolen fishmonger's cloak that smelled of brine and old smoke. There was crusted blood at his temple and fresh blood on his knuckles, and he was so thin she could count the architecture of him through his shirt. But his eyes found hers in the darkness, and they were burning.

"Brightwater," he rasped.

"You're supposed to be in chains."

"I was." He tried to smile and couldn't manage it. His legs went, and she and Doss took his weight between them, hauling him back toward the fire while he apologized under his breath like a man drunk on exhaustion. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I ran when I should have walked. I ran the whole—"

"Hush." They got him down onto a flat stone near the flames. Tam came running despite the instruction, and Lena didn't scold him; the boy pressed a waterskin into Bren's hands with the solemnity of a squire, and Bren drank like a man who had forgotten water existed.

Around them, the camp assembled itself into an audience. Nine smugglers remained of the twelve who had left Marrowgate—three had slipped away at the river crossing after the fight at the ford, and no one had blamed them—but the remaining nine now clustered with their weapons loose in their hands, watching the crown soldier who had held the bridge so their cargo could live.

"He looks like death's bookkeeper," observed Merrit, the oldest of Doss's crew, a woman with grey in her braids and a crossbow scar through her ear.

"He looks like a man who owes us nothing and paid anyway," Doss said quietly. Then, to Bren: "The ford. We saw you take the bridge. We saw them take *you*." His voice did something complicated on the last word. Doss did not like debts, and Bren's capture was the largest kind.

"There were records," Bren said. He said it strangely, as if the words had been handed to him by someone else. "In the prison. Veyra showed me—no. Later. I'll get there." He pressed his palms against his eyes for a moment, gathering himself, and Lena waited. She had learned patience the way mapmakers learn it: by understanding that everything arrives eventually, if you hold still enough.

When he lowered his hands, he looked at her directly.

"They kept me three weeks. Salt cells in an old granary below the fort at Harrowmere. No torture—the worst kind, the kind where they don't need it. And there was another prisoner." His voice cracked on the name. "An old man. Half-blind. They had him chained to a wall in the dark, and he talked to me through the stones every night because he thought I might be the person he'd sent away."

The fire popped. Nobody moved.

"Corvin," Lena said. It wasn't a question. It was barely a word. It came out of her like something extracted.

Bren nodded slowly. "He told me what to say to you. Made me repeat it until I could say it asleep. He said—" Bren closed his eyes, and Lena understood he was not recalling; he was performing, carefully, a thing that had been carved into him. "He said: *Tell my student the covenant is a door, and doors open from both sides. Tell her the spire-folk sealed themselves behind it when we betrayed them, and only a key reforged at Auren can bring them back before Aldric tears the lock out whole.*"

"We know that part," Doss said. "The star-woman told her. We've been walking toward it since the shrine ruins."

"She told you the door opens," Bren said. "The old man wanted me to tell you what it costs." He opened his eyes, and they were wet, and he did not look away from her. "He made me swear I'd say the words exactly. He said you'd try to argue with a paraphrase, and he knew you too well for that." A breath, drawn shakily. "*The price is memory.*"

Lena felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs and sit there, cold as a coin dropped down a well.

"The price," she repeated.

"All of it." Bren's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "Not blood. Not life. He said the covenant is woven from remembrance—that the spire-folk bound themselves to us through shared memory, ours and theirs, and when the bond broke, memory was the thread that frayed first. To reforge it, someone has to feed it the truest thing a person owns." He swallowed. "Every memory tied to the people you love. All of them. He said you'd know what that means better than anyone, because you're the one who remembers paths."

The night seemed to tilt. Lena became aware, distantly, of Tam standing very close to her, his small hand finding the hem of her sleeve and gripping it, and of how automatic her own motion was—reaching back, folding him in, the gesture of ten thousand gestures accumulated over two years of keeping this boy alive. That was what memory was. Not pictures. Motion. Weight. The hand that reached before the mind decided.

And it would all go. Not just the images—*him*. The reaching itself. The boy would stand beside her and she would feel nothing but the weather.

"Lena," Tam said. Just that. Her name, in his careful voice.

"It's all right," she heard herself say, which was the first lie she had told him in months, and possibly the worst.

* * *

They gave Bren food and a place by the fire, and then, because grief is patient and horror takes its time, they sat in silence for most of an hour while he ate and the fire ate and the forest went about its business around them.

It was Merrit who finally spoke what everyone was circling. "So the old man's telling you not to do it."

"No," Lena said.

Everyone looked at her.

She had been staring into the flames, and now she stared harder, because looking at faces was impossible right now—at Tam's, at Doss's, at the rough circle of smugglers who had followed her this far on maps and stubbornness. "He's telling me the cost so I can't pretend I didn't know. That's what he always did. Master Corvin never forbade anything. He just made sure you signed your name to what you chose." Her laugh came out wrong, half a sob. "Ten years I mapped those mountains and he never once told me not to climb the Widow's Stair. He just showed me the ledgers of everyone who'd fallen."

"And this ledger says you forget us," Doss said flatly. "All of us. Every soul in this camp, starting with the ones closest."

"Yes."

"Then we turn around."

"Doss—"

"We turn *around*, Brightwater." He was on his feet, and Doss never stood unless he meant it; he was a man who had built a career on economy of motion. "I've run cargoes past revenue cutters and war galleys. I've crossed the Silt Marches in flood season. I don't spook. You know that. You bought my life with a map and I've been paying it back mile by mile ever since, and I'd have paid it up this frozen mountain and into whatever door the old gods keep." He jabbed a finger at the north, where Thornfell's white spine was faintly visible even here, a bone-colored ridge above the black trees. "But I don't pay debts to people who intend to erase themselves. That's not payment. That's theft. You'd be robbing us."

"Of what?"

"Of *her*!" It burst out of him louder than he'd meant, and several smugglers shifted. Doss never raised his voice. Doss negotiated his way out of hangings with a murmur. When he continued, it was low and shaking. "You think we walk for the free cities? The cities sold you at the first offer. Every one of them. You think I do this for Tam's big eyes or the soldier's bridge? I follow you because you're the only person I've met in thirty years of trade who keeps her word when it costs. And you're proposing to make yourself into a woman who doesn't know what her word is worth. Who doesn't know *me*. That's not sacrifice, girl. That's dying with extra steps, and leaving the rest of us holding the corpse."

The words hung over the fire like smoke.

Tam had gone white. He hadn't let go of Lena's sleeve. She could feel his fingers through the fabric, small and fierce, and she thought—with a clarity that felt like cruelty—*in a month, this will mean nothing to me. This exact pressure. This hand. Gone.*

And the free cities would burn instead. Marrowgate, whose council had offered to hand her and every Gifted child to Aldric's inquisitors. Thornfell's villages. Tam's village, already ash. Everything the covenant protected, everything the spire-folk's return would shield—it would all go to the usurper's fire because Lena Brightwater had flinched at the bill.

"Half," said Merrit into the silence.

Everyone turned. The old smuggler shrugged, unrepentant, arms folded across her chest. "You want my honest read? Half this crew walks tonight. Some because of the forgetting, some because of the army, some because they never believed in spire-folk and don't start believing in ghost women made of stars. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic." She looked at Doss. "You want to stop them deserting, buy 'em something. You want to stop *me*, you'd best start with better arguments than you've got."

"I'm not stopping anyone," Doss said hoarsely. "I'm deciding what I am." He turned to Lena, and for a moment she saw him young—indecipherable, impossible, but she saw it—a younger man on a dock in Marrowgate, holding a rolled map that had saved his neck, looking at a quiet cartographer's apprentice with something like awe. "Last chance to tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong," Lena said. Her voice was steady. She marveled at it from a distance, the way she'd once marveled at a pass that shouldn't exist, drawn in lines only she could see. "I wish to every god you were."

Doss held her gaze a moment longer. Then he took off his cap—an act she had never seen him perform—and ran a hand through his thin hair, and sat back down heavily by the fire, and said nothing more.

* * *

By dawn, the camp had halved.

There was no drama in it. That was the strange mercy of smugglers: they understood exits. Four of the nine slipped away in the last hours of darkness with their shares and their gear and murmured farewells to no one in particular, and by the time the sky went grey, Merrit stood with her bedroll shouldered at the edge of camp, and Doss stood apart from everyone with his back to the group, and Tam slept against Lena's side because he had cried himself out sometime after midnight and sleep had taken him like a tide.

Merrit paused beside Lena on her way out.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the old man was right to send the message. And you're right to go. Both things are true, and neither one helps." She dug in her coat and produced a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, pressing it into Lena's hands. Inside was a knife—good steel, plain hilt—and a strip of dried venison, and a single copper coin worn smooth. "For the road. The coin's for luck. Don't spend it."

"Merrit—"

"Spare me." The old woman's scarred ear twitched in something like a smile. "When you've forgotten us all, girl, spend it or don't. Won't matter to you either way." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone had said to Lena in weeks. Then she walked into the trees, and the trees took her, and that was that.

The sun came up properly. Bren emerged from wherever he'd been sleeping—he had refused the tent, said walls made him itch now—and began silently breaking down the camp, doing a deserter-soldier's work, efficient and uncomplaining. Doss watched him for a while and then, wordlessly, started helping. Something passed between the two men that Lena chose not to examine. Debts, probably. The language Doss spoke best.

Tam woke, ate, and then stood in front of her with his jaw set in the way that meant a question was coming that he'd been building all night.

"If you forget me," he said, "will it hurt you?"

Lena crouched to his height. Ten years old, and he asked the questions philosophers circled for lifetimes. "I don't know," she said, because he deserved truth now; lies were a luxury she was done affording. "Master Corvin used to say that the deepest things leave grooves. Like a cart path worn into stone. Even if you can't remember the cart, the groove knows which way it goes."

"So I could still find you. In the grooves."

"I think," Lena said, and her throat closed, and she pushed through it, "that I will look at you and think: there is a boy I would trust with anything. And I won't know why. And that will have to be enough."

Tam considered this with terrible seriousness. Then he nodded once, the nod of a man closing a bargain, and went to help Bren fold the tents, and Lena stayed crouched a moment longer in the cold morning dirt, memorizing the weight of the moment. Filing it. Knowing she was filing it, knowing the file would burn.

Grief chosen in advance, Corvin had called it once, in another life, about something smaller. She hadn't understood then. She understood now. It was this: loving forward, on purpose, into the teeth of the loss. Saying yes with your eyes open.

She rose. Doss was waiting by the cold firepit, pack on, face unreadable, and beside him stood Bren, and beside Bren, Tam. Four of them. An army of four, marching against the largest force the usurper had ever fielded, to knock on a door that ate memories.

"Well?" Doss said.

Lena looked north, to where Thornfell's spine caught the early light—a blade of white rising out of the world, and at its summit, barely visible at this distance, a needle-thin gleam that might have been snow. Might have been glass.

Might have been the Spire of Auren, waiting.

"You don't have to come," she said.

"I know what I owe," Doss said. "The debt stands. I'm just telling you the ledger's closed after." He shouldered his pack and started walking, and after a heartbeat, the others followed, and Lena took up the rear, and the mountain took them in.

Behind them, unseen by any of them, far to the south across the plain, the dust of Aldric's army had begun to rise—a brown smudge on the horizon, moving north, moving faster than four people on a frozen mountain could ever move.

They had perhaps six days.
