## 15. The Army on the Plain

The army came down out of the hills like a wound opening across the land.

Veyra watched it from the ridge above the Ashen Road, her grey cloak pulled tight against a wind that smelled of smoke and wet iron. Below her, the column of King Aldric's host unspooled for miles—pikemen in the crown's black and gold, supply wagons groaning under salted meat and siege-iron, cavalry riding flank with their lances wrapped against the damp. Banners snapped overhead: the crowned tower of Aldric's new dynasty, stitched by a hundred different hands, none of them old enough to remember what the tower had replaced.

She had marched with armies before. She had been nineteen when she first rode under a royal banner, an inquisitor's apprentice with a warrant case strapped to her hip and a certainty in her chest so clean it had felt like faith. Armies were supposed to feel like that—like certainty made flesh, like order rolling forward over chaos.

This one felt like something being eaten.

"Three days to Marrowgate," said Captain Ilse Rennick, reining up beside her. Rennick was broad, weathered, loyal in the way of soldiers who had never been asked to think past the next order. "Scouts say the council's still arguing. They might open the gates without a fight."

"They won't." Veyra did not take her eyes off the plain. "Marrowgate has walls older than Aldric's grandfather and merchants who can buy his debts. They'll fight."

"Then we'll crack them like the rest." Rennick said it easily, the way one says *it will rain tomorrow*. "The king wants the Gifted delivered to him alive, though. Your writ still stands?"

"My writ stands." She touched the leather tube at her belt where the edict rode, sealed in black wax. *Let every soul who carries the old gift be bound and brought before the throne.* She had drafted half its language herself, two winters ago, back when binding had seemed like surgery—a clean excision of a dangerous thing.

Back when she had believed the dangerous thing was the gift.

* * *

The village was called Hallowmere, though no sign of that name survived the night.

Veyra reached it at dawn with a rear detachment, sent ahead to secure the road's southern fork. What she found was not a battle site. It was not even a resistance site. It was forty cottages and a mill and a shrine to some river-god whose name she'd never learned, all of it burned down to foundations, the ash lying over the fields like dirty snow.

A sergeant met her at the edge of the ruin, saluting with a hand that left a smear of soot across his brow. "Inquisitor. Third Company cleared this ground last night."

"Cleared it of what?"

He hesitated. That hesitation told her more than any report would have. "Gifted activity, ma'am. A woman here was reported. Healing arts. Unlicensed."

"And the woman?"

"Gone before we arrived, most likely. Or—" He looked at the ash. At the well, which had stones stacked beside it in a way that suggested things had been dropped down it. "Or she was never found."

Veyra walked the village while her escort waited at the perimeter. She had done this work for fifteen years; she knew how to read a scene the way other people read letters. There had been no fight. No warding-marks scratched into doorframes, no counter-charms, none of the desperate ingenuity the Gifted always showed when cornered. The doors had been opened from the outside. The people had been gathered—the char patterns in the square showed where they'd stood, huddled close, dozens of them—and then the fires had been set.

A woman who could heal, and no wounds on any defender. A village that posed less threat than a granary fire.

She stopped at the well and looked down into it. The dark below gave nothing back, but the smell rising from it was the smell she had first learned in the interrogation cells beneath the capital, the smell that meant the work had gone past questioning into something else entirely.

"Inquisitor?" The sergeant had followed her, cautious as a man approaching a wolf over a kill.

"How many villages between here and Marrowgate?" she asked.

"A dozen or more, ma'am. Why?"

"Because if Third Company is clearing them the way they cleared this one, we're going to arrive at Marrowgate having already lost the war." She turned and looked at him until he shifted his weight. "You burned farmers, Sergeant. Farmers who healed each other's fevers. And you wrote it down as *gifted activity*. Whose order was it?"

"The company commander's. Under the general's standing directive. Any settlement harboring—" He recited it like scripture, because to him it was. "Any settlement harboring the Gifted is complicit with sedition."

"Was there evidence of harboring?"

"I—" His jaw worked. "There was a report."

"A report from whom?"

Silence. Wind moved the ash across the square in little grey ghosts.

"That's what I thought," Veyra said quietly. She took out her field journal, and in front of the sergeant, she wrote: *Hallowmere. No engagement. No evidence. Civilian deaths: est. sixty.* The sergeant watched her write it with the expression of a man watching someone step off a cliff. Perhaps he was. Perhaps she was.

"Say nothing of this conversation," she told him. "Not yet."

"Yes, Inquisitor." A pause. Then, low, almost against his will: "It wasn't just us, ma'am. It's been like this since the river crossings. Half the companies are doing it. Nobody says anything because—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Because the orders came from the king, and the king had made the Gifted the enemy, and once you decided a whole kind of person was the enemy, every village became a battlefield and every battlefield became an excuse.

Veyra closed her journal. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where her certainty had lived for fifteen years, something shifted its weight and settled lower, like ballast taking on water.

* * *

That evening she sat in the command pavilion and listened to the general plan the assault on Marrowgate, and she understood, finally, what she had spent months refusing to understand.

General Corvane was not a cruel man, which somehow made it worse. He spread his maps with a craftsman's care, moving wooden markers across the free cities' territories, and he spoke of grain stores and garrison strengths and the morale effects of exemplary punishment with the mild tone of a man discussing crop rotation. When a colonel asked about the civilian population of the river towns, Corvane said, "Collateral to the operation," and moved another marker, and no one flinched, because no one ever flinched, because the room was full of men who had long ago learned that the map was easier to love than the land.

"The Gifted are the priority," Corvane said, tapping the eastern territories. "The king wants them bound and delivered before winter. Everything else is secondary."

"Everything else," Veyra said, before she could stop herself, "is the realm we're supposedly saving."

The tent went quiet. Corvane looked up at her, and there was no anger in his face, only the weary patience of a man addressing a useful instrument that had begun to hum out of tune. "Inquisitor Veyra. Your writ concerns the Gifted. I'd thank you to keep your attention there."

"My attention," she said, "has been nowhere else for fifteen years. That's rather my point."

Someone coughed. Corvane held her gaze a moment longer, then returned to his markers, dismissing her the way one dismisses weather. But she saw the flicker in his eyes, the small recalculation—he would mention this to the king's advisors, or he wouldn't, depending on how useful he judged her to remain. Either way, the clock around her had started ticking.

She left the pavilion and stood in the cold among the picketed horses, looking south toward Marrowgate's distant glow, and she let herself think the thought she had been circling for weeks.

*I built this.*

Not alone. Not solely. But the edict bore her phrasing. The hunting protocols bore her design. The interrogation methods she had refined in the capital's cells had been taught to Third Company and Fourth and Fifth, passed down like a trade, and those methods had come down the river valleys ahead of the army like a plague running ahead of its fever. Every burned village wore her fingerprints somewhere, faint as ash, real as bone.

She had hunted the Brightwater woman through the Thornfells with a zealot's thoroughness, certain that the covenant—if it existed—was a weapon that must be kept from reckless hands. She had broken Master Corvin piece by piece in a cell that smelled of salt, and she had told herself it was necessary, and some nights she had even believed it.

But Corvin, in the end, had not screamed curses at her. He had looked at her with something worse than hatred—with pity—and said, *You think you're holding the leash, child. You're holding the whip they hand you.*

She had logged it as manipulation. She had been trained to log everything as manipulation.

Now, standing in the trampled mud of an army's staging ground, listening to ten thousand men breathe and shift and dream of glory, she logged it differently.

* * *

The orders came at midnight, and they were the last straw laid across a back already bowed.

By courier, under the king's seal: the vanguard was authorized to requisition supplies from all settlements along the line of march, with force if needed. Requisition, the word meant, whatever the army wanted, taken however the army pleased. Veyra read the order twice by lamplight in her own tent, and then she read the annex, which listed the settlements, and she recognized three of the names from her own intelligence files—towns that had already paid tribute, already sworn loyalty, already handed over two frightened Gifted children who had done nothing but glow faintly in the dark when they dreamed.

Towns that had cooperated. Towns that had trusted the crown's word.

And the army was going to eat through them like locusts, because the roads were long and the granaries of loyal subjects were closer than the depots.

Veyra sat for a long time with the order in her hands. The lamp guttered. Outside, a sentry called the hour, and somewhere a horse complained in the dark, and the whole vast machine of the host settled deeper into sleep, dreaming its dreams of Marrowgate's fall.

She thought about the girl she had been at nineteen. That girl had joined the inquisition for reasons that had sounded noble even to her own cynical ears: because the world was full of power that went unchecked, and someone had to check it. Because law was the wall between the strong and the weak. She had spent her life reinforcing that wall, stone by stone, warrant by warrant, believing that the wall protected everyone equally.

She had never asked who held the gate.

*They broke the covenant first.* Bren's face rose in her memory—not the prisoner's face, gray and hollow from the salt cellars, but the moment after, when she had shown him the archives, the old treaties, the record of what the human kings had done to the spire-folk four centuries ago. She had shown him the truth expecting it to break him, and instead it had broken something in the room between them. He had looked at her across the table and said, quietly, *You didn't know. Did you? All these years—you didn't know what you were serving.*

She had ordered him back to his cell and sat alone until morning.

Now she folded the midnight order along its crease, and unfolded it, and folded it again. Sabotage was treason. Treason was death, and not a clean death—inquisitors who turned were given to the priests of the king's new creed, and what the priests did lasted days. She knew. She had attended such proceedings. She had stood at the back in her grey cloak and told herself the traitors had earned it.

But there was a supply convoy leaving for the river towns at dawn. Three wagons, escorted by a company that included men who had burned Hallowmere. If the convoy arrived, the river towns would suffer what Hallowmere had suffered, and the two Gifted children already in the crown's custody would be joined by however many the soldiers dragged out of the reed-beds.

If the convoy's route orders were to become... confused. If a copy of the march schedule found its way to the towns themselves, giving them a day's warning to scatter their stores and their people into the marshes where soldiers hated to follow—

That was not treason, exactly. That was weather. That was bad luck, a lost dispatch, a fog on the river. Small sabotage. The kind of thing that could be denied, disbelieved, buried in paperwork.

The kind of thing an inquisitor, who knew exactly how documents moved and how deniability worked, was uniquely qualified to commit.

Veyra rose, took a fresh sheet of paper, and began to write in a fair imitation of a quartermaster's clerkly hand: *Revised route per flooded ford at Stony Crossing...*

Her pen did not shake. That surprised her. Fifteen years of writing warrants and sentences and death-orders, and it was this—an act of mercy, small and criminal—that should have made her tremble, and did not. Instead she felt the strange lightness she had felt only twice before in her life: once the day she'd earned her grey cloak, and once, weeks ago, the moment she had turned her blade away from Bren and begun, without admitting it, to turn.

She finished the forgery, blotted it, and sealed it with the quartermaster's stolen wax impression. Then she wrote a second document, in her own hand, addressed to no one: a true account of Hallowmere, of the well, of the standing directives, dated and signed. She hid it in the false bottom of her warrant case, where she kept the things she could not bear to carry openly and could not bear to destroy.

If she died for this, someone would eventually find it. Someone would know.

* * *

Dawn came grey and slow over the plain. The convoy formed up in the mud, wheels creaking, horses steaming, and Veyra stood at the head of the column in her grey cloak with the forged orders in her satchel, watching the sun struggle up behind Marrowgate's far towers.

Rennick found her there. "Dispatches ready, Inquisitor?"

"All in order." She handed the satchel to the lead rider and watched it go—watched the lie ride south at a trot toward the flooded ford that did not exist, toward the warning that would reach the river towns hours ahead of the soldiers, toward whatever small salvation a single falsified route could buy.

"You look tired, ma'am," Rennick offered.

"I've been tired for fifteen years, Captain. I'm only now noticing."

Rennick laughed uncertainly and rode off to form the column, and Veyra stood alone in the growing light, a hunter who had followed a quarry halfway across a kingdom and discovered, somewhere on the road, that she had been the one tracked all along—tracked by every burned village, every broken confession, every name in her own ledgers.

Southward, beyond the plain, beyond the army's smudge of dust, the Thornfells stood white against the sky. Somewhere in those heights was a spire she had dismissed as legend, a woman she had hunted as a fugitive, and a covenant that was either the realm's last hope or its final delusion.

One way to find out.

Veyra turned her horse south, away from the column, away from the banners, and put her heels to its flanks.
