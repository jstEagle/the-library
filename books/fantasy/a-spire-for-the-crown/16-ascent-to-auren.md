## 16. Ascent to Auren

The mountain did not want them.

Lena had known it from the moment they left the tree line behind—known it in the way the wind came down the col in long, deliberate breaths, like something exhaling through its teeth. Thornfell's spine rose above them now, a sawblade of black rock and old ice running north for twenty miles toward the pale smear on the horizon that was all anyone could see of the Spire of Auren. The maps she had drawn over ten years called this stretch *the Wyrm's Back*, and her maps had never once lied to her.

Her boots had not lied either. They were ruined. Three days of scree and frozen meltwater had eaten through the stitching, and every step north was a small negotiation between her will and her feet.

"Tell me again why smugglers don't own decent climbing gear," Bren said behind her. He had Tam's pack strapped over his own, and the boy stumbling along in his wake looked half-asleep on his feet, cheeks wind-scoured to a dull red.

"Smugglers don't climb," Doss said. "Smugglers go around. Around is the whole point of smuggling." He spat into the wind, which blew it back across his beard, and he pretended not to notice. "This is map-woman's madness, and I've said so."

"You have," Lena said. "Eleven times since dawn."

"And I'll say it twelve before noon if it keeps my legs moving." He adjusted the coil of rope at his shoulder—a real coil, bought at ruinous price from the last shepherd's hut before the snow took everything—and trudged past her to take point, because Doss complained loudest about whatever he intended to do anyway.

They climbed.

The morning was a hard blue nothing, sky and snow indistinguishable except where the rock broke through like ribs. Lena counted their progress not in miles but in breaths—the thin air made each one cost more—and in the sun's slow crawl, because they were racing two clocks now: Veyra's remnant somewhere below and behind, and Aldric's army moving on the free cities far to the south, days from Marrowgate's walls. Corvin's message sat inside her like a swallowed stone. *The price is memory.* She had not spoken of it aloud since the shrine. She did not need to. Every time she caught Tam watching her with those wide, frightened eyes, or heard Doss grumbling about rope lengths he'd already measured twice, she felt the ledger opening, page by page, and understood exactly what the mountain was asking her to carry up it: these people, vivid and warm, so that she could set them down forever at the top.

So she memorized instead of speaking. The way Bren checked the horizon every forty steps without seeming to move his head—a soldier's habit worn smooth as river stone. The way Tam hummed when the fear got bad, three notes of some lullaby, barely audible under the wind. The way Doss swore at the ice in four languages and apologized to it in none.

She would keep these as long as she could.

* * *

The storm found them at midafternoon, as storms do on the Back—suddenly, and with intent.

One moment there was a horizon; the next there was a white wall rolling up the eastern face like a tide deciding to come inland. Lena felt it before she saw it, a pressure change in her ears, a wrongness in the light, and the gift flared open in her chest unbidden, showing her the safe line: a shelf of leeward rock half a mile south, tucked beneath an overhang like a stone eyelid.

"Doss! Shelf, south side, now!"

He didn't argue—that was new, and she noted it even as her heart hammered. He bellowed for Tam, hauled the boy by the collar, and they ran the ridge with the storm screaming down their necks. Bren swept Tam up bodily when the boy stumbled and carried him the last hundred yards, and then they were crammed beneath the overhang while the world outside turned to a howling gray blur, snow coming not down but sideways, filling the air like the mountain shaking out its own fur.

They got the oilcloth strung. They got the little stove going in the lee, its flame flattened by the draft. They ate salt pork and hard bread and drank water melted over the flame, and nobody spoke much, because speaking cost warmth and warmth cost life, and they were all four of them fluent in arithmetic by now.

It was Tam who finally said it, in the dark past the stove's small circle, his voice very quiet:

"Do you think she's still following us?"

Lena followed his gaze back down the way they'd come. Through the storm she could see nothing, but the gift, when she let it breathe, showed her the faintest thread—like a scar across the world—where riders had passed. Two days old. Cold.

"She's following," Lena said. "But we're faster than she thinks."

"That's what worries me," Doss muttered. "Inquisitors aren't built like us. They don't stop for weather. They don't stop for sense." He poked the stove. "Woman burned her own supply wagons at the ford to speed her march. I heard it from a drover who heard it from a deserter. That's not pursuit. That's obsession wearing a uniform."

Bren said nothing, but his jaw worked. He knew Veyra better than any of them—he'd served under her banner, broken under her command, and fled her justice. "She'll take the low pass around the Wyrm's Back," he said at last. "It adds two days, but she won't lose men to the ridge in a storm like this."

"She doesn't care about losing men," Doss said.

"She cares about *me*," Bren said, "and I'm worth more alive at the top of this mountain than dead on it. She wants what Lena knows. She'll come careful." He looked at Lena across the stove's glow, and in his eyes was the question none of them asked anymore, because the answer never changed: *How long?*

"Two days to the Spire's gate, if the weather breaks," Lena said. "Three, if it doesn't. We rest tonight and move at first light. After that—" She stopped. After that, the rite. After that, the price. "After that, it won't matter who follows."

Tam pulled his blanket up to his chin. In the dark beyond the firelight, the storm went on shouting itself hoarse against the stone, and Lena lay awake listening to three people breathe, filing away the sound of it.

* * *

The storm broke near dawn, the way mountain storms do—all at once, like a door slamming shut—and left behind a world so clean and bright it hurt. They broke camp with numb fingers and started up the steepest pitch of the Back, a shoulder of blue ice where the rock steepened and the path narrowed to a ledge no wider than a coffin laid on its side.

Doss went first with the rope. Then Tam. Then Lena. Bren took the rear, as he always did, because he said rearguard was where soldiers earned their keep, and because Lena suspected he still didn't entirely trust himself to walk beside them rather than between them and danger.

For two hours it went well. Too well. Lena should have known.

The gift had been quiet all morning—not silent, never silent anymore, but murmuring, the starlight woman's presence a faint warmth at the base of her skull like sun through a window. Then, as they crossed a bowl of wind-packed snow beneath a hanging cornice, the warmth sharpened to a needle. Lena froze mid-step.

"Doss—"

"I see it," he breathed.

He had stopped three paces ahead, one boot planted, head cocked the way he did when he heard something wrong in the mountain's voice. And Lena heard it too now: a deep, subterranean groan, the sound of tons of snow deciding, slowly, collectively, to become something else.

"Off the bowl!" Bren shouted. "Move—move NOW!"

The cornice let go.

It did not fall so much as pour, a white cataract off the ridge above, and the whole slope beneath them turned liquid. The world became noise and cold and motion. Lena felt the ground slide out from under her like a deck being pulled; she dug her axe in and swung with the avalanche's current, riding it the way she'd once seen a mountain man ride floodwater, and through the roar she heard Tam scream—one short, high note, cut off.

*Tam.*

She wrenched her head around. The boy was gone. Not gone-gone—she could see him, a scrap of dark wool tumbling in the white flood, being carried toward the edge of the bowl where the slope fell away into a crevasse field, blue-black teeth waiting below. The snow was carrying him there gently, almost politely, the way water carries a leaf to a weir.

And Doss—

Doss had anchored. She didn't know when he'd done it, whether instinct or thirty years of smuggler's cunning, but his axe was buried to the haft in the rock rib at the bowl's edge and the rope was paying out through his gloved hands, burning them even through the leather, and when the flood carried Tam within reach Doss threw himself flat and *caught*—one fist closing on the boy's collar, the other locked on the rope, his whole body stretched into a bridge between the mountain's hunger and a twelve-year-old child.

The avalanche spent itself. The roar collapsed into silence, that ringing post-catastrophe quiet, and then there was only the wind and Doss's breathing, ragged and enormous, and Tam dangling over the crevasse field with his boots swinging over sixty feet of blue death.

"Haul," Doss gritted. His arms shook. Blood seeped between his fingers where the rope had cooked through the glove. "*Haul*, damn you, I'm not doing this part twice."

Bren reached him first—of course Bren reached him first—and together they walked the rope hand over hand until Tam came up over the lip and collapsed onto solid snow, gasping, alive, whole. Lena knelt and put her hands on the boy's face, his shoulders, his ribs, cataloguing him frantically, and he grabbed her wrist and held on and said nothing at all, because there was nothing to say that breathing wasn't already saying.

Doss sat back against the rock rib and laughed. It was a terrible sound, cracked and wheezing, and then it turned into something else, and Lena realized with a jolt that the old smuggler was crying—just a little, just the corners of his eyes freezing over, which he would deny to his grave.

"Well," he managed. "That's it, then."

"That's what?" Bren asked.

"The debt." Doss peeled the ruined glove off his hand, wincing, revealing rope-burned flesh beneath. "You remember, map-woman. The day your map kept me off the Gallows Road and out of a crown noose. I told you I'd pay it. You said debts like that don't get paid, only carried." He looked at Tam—at the boy who was not his blood, not his countryman, not anything to him but a passenger he'd agreed to ferry—and something in his weathered face had rearranged itself permanently. "Carried him right through an avalanche, I did. We're square."

"We're not square," Tam said. His voice was small and fierce. "You don't get to be square. You're stuck with me now."

Doss stared at him. Then he laughed again, properly this time, and pulled the boy's hood down over his eyes with his good hand. "Gods preserve me. Another mouth." But he said it the way a man says *family* without knowing he's saying it.

They rested an hour, because shock demands its tax. Lena bound Doss's hands with salve and linen while he protested that he'd had worse paper cuts, and Tam sat pressed against the old man's side like a barnacle, and Bren stood watch on the ridge with his back to all of them, guarding a view of empty white, and Lena thought: *this. This is the price. Not memories in the abstract. This exact hour, this linen, this laughter. I am carrying it up the mountain to give it away.*

She bound the last knot and did not let her hands shake.

* * *

They summited the Wyrm's Back at dusk, and the world opened.

Below them, the northern valleys lay in blue shadow, and far to the south—so far it seemed imagined—a smudge of woodsmoke marked where the army's forges burned. Between them and it, the ridge descended into a high plateau of wind-carved snow, and at the plateau's end, catching the last red light of the sun like a blade catching fire, stood the Spire of Auren.

Lena had seen it in visions. She had seen it in the starlight woman's memories, vast and singing, crowned with lights, alive with a people who had woven the covenant like a tapestry through the roots of the world. What stood there now was its corpse: a shattered needle of white stone, half-swallowed by the glacier, dark where the lights had been.

But not entirely dark.

As the sun died, something kindled at the Spire's crown—a flicker, faint as a heartbeat seen through skin. The gift roared awake in her chest, and for one dizzying moment she felt it answering her, feeling *her*, the way a sleeping man stirs at a familiar voice.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

Behind them, down the long southern slope, torchlight moved. Small, distant, patient—a line of riders winding up the low pass, exactly as Bren had predicted, exactly on schedule. Veyra. A day behind, maybe less.

Doss squinted at the torches, then at the Spire, then worked his burned hand open and closed. "One day," he said. "Snow's holding. We can make the gate by tomorrow night."

"Then we don't sleep long," Lena said.

They made camp in the lee of the summit cairn, and Tam fell asleep mid-chew, and Bren took first watch without being asked, and Doss sat beside Lena looking at the far-off flicker on the Spire's crown.

"What does it cost, really?" he asked quietly, not looking at her. "Your woman of starlight. Corvin's message. 'The price is memory.' What does that mean, map-woman? Truly?"

Lena watched the light pulse, faint and far, like a candle in another room of an enormous house.

"It means," she said, "that tomorrow night, when we reach the gate, I'm going to forget why any of you were ever worth dying for."

Doss was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice was rougher than the wind.

"Then we'd best make tonight worth remembering."

Above them the stars came out in their thousands, cold and ancient and watchful, and at the crown of the dead Spire, the light beat on—waiting for her—while down in the dark, patient as winter, the torches climbed.
