## 17. The Gate of Names

The Spire of Auren did not rise so much as it *remained*.

Lena had imagined it a hundred times over ten years of mapping the Thornfells—she had drawn conjectured towers on her secret charts, guessed at its height, invented spires and buttresses for the pleasure of filling blank parchment. Every guess had been wrong. The Spire was a single seamless shaft of pale stone, wider than Marrowgate's great temple, climbing out of the glacier saddle until the eye gave up and the clouds took over. There were no windows. No doors. No seams in the masonry, no chisel marks, nothing to suggest that hands had ever touched it. It looked less like a building than like a decision the mountain had made to keep standing.

And set into its base, alone in all that vastness, was a gate.

It was not grand. That was the first thing that unsettled her. After the climb they had endured—four days up the frozen spine of the range, the avalanche that had nearly taken Tam, Doss's howl as he'd thrown himself and the boy into a snow trench while the white wall roared past—after all of that, she had expected the gate of the old gods to be something worthy of the road. Instead it was a simple arch, twice the height of a man, framed in stone so dark it seemed to drink the daylight. No handle. No hinges. Only a shallow basin at its foot, carved in the shape of two open hands, cupped together.

Snow had drifted around the basin, and the snow was *melted*. Not scabbed with ice, not crusted—the stone within the cupped hands was dry, and warm, faintly steaming in the bitter air, as if something beneath it still beat like a heart.

"It's been waiting," Tam said softly.

They stood in a ragged line, four figures who should never have made it this far. Lena's hands would not stop shaking, and not from cold. Bren had his sword slung across his back the way he'd carried it since the ford—hilt forward, ready to draw for them rather than against them—and his breath came in slow, disciplined plumes, the way he breathed before a fight. Doss leaned on his ice-axe and said nothing at all, which frightened her more than any of his complaints on the climb. And Tam, twelve years old and frost-burned at the cheeks, stared up at the Spire with an expression she recognized because she had worn it once herself, at Corvin's knee: the look of someone seeing the shape of their own life change.

Behind them, down the long white shoulder of the glacier, came the sound she had been dreading for two days. Boots. Dogs. The thin metal music of a column of soldiers moving fast.

Veyra's remnant. Perhaps an hour behind. Perhaps less.

"We don't have time to admire it," Lena said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Corvin's message said the gate asks something of those who enter. We pay it and we go."

"And what does it ask, exactly?" Doss asked. "Because I've paid a lot of gates in my life, Brightwater, and none of them ever melted the snow off their own doorstep."

"The price is memory." She heard herself say it flatly, the way you'd read a shipping manifest, because if she let the words carry weight they would crush her where she stood. "That's what Corvin sent. Each of us who passes has to give up a name. Someone we—" Her throat closed. She started again. "Someone we love."

The wind moved through the saddle, keening low across the ice, and for a moment Lena could have sworn the sound came from inside the Spire, a long exhalation behind all that patient stone.

"I'll pass," Doss said.

"Doss—"

"No. Hear me." He straightened off his axe, and for once there was no smirk anywhere on him; the avalanche had scraped the last of it away somewhere back on the ridge. "I've got no one. Mother dead, father dead, no wife, no children, no brothers I'd claim. You're asking for a name I love, and I'll tell you true: the only names on that list are standing right here in front of me." He looked at each of them in turn. "So take whichever one of mine you like. Take all three. Cheapest passage I ever bought."

Lena felt her eyes sting and blamed the wind. "Doss. If you give up our names—"

"Then I'll walk out of that Spire knowing three kind faces helped me across a mountain, and I'll buy them a drink in whatever tavern we find." He shrugged, huge shoulders rolling. "Sounds like a fair trade to me. Debts get paid. That's all a man is."

He walked toward the gate before anyone could argue, boots crunching, and knelt beside the cupped stone hands. He spoke a word too low for them to hear—Lena caught only the shape of it, the fall of one name into another—and pressed his palm into the basin.

The stone flared.

Not firelight, not lamplight—a color she had no name for, a pale gold shot through with something deeper, like sunlight remembered rather than seen. It ran up the dark arch in slow veins, and the gate simply ceased to be solid. Where the door had been there was now an opening into the Spire's interior: a corridor of the same seamless pale stone, lit by nothing and glowing everywhere, receding farther than the eye could follow.

Doss got to his feet slowly. He was frowning, working his mouth like a man searching for a word that had fallen out of his pocket. He looked back at them—at Lena, at Tam, at Bren—and his brow furrowed for half a heartbeat, a flicker of something lost, and then he grinned.

"Well," he said. "Warm in there, at least. Come on, then, before I change my mind about the drink."

He stepped through and was swallowed by the light.

* * *

Tam went next.

He didn't ask permission. He simply walked to the basin, small and straight-backed, and stood before it with his fists at his sides, and Lena understood with a lurch that he had known which name he would give long before they reached the gate. Perhaps since the shrine ruin. Perhaps since the night the riders burned his village and took everything else.

"My mum," he said—not to the gate, but to Lena, turning to face her. His voice cracked in the middle and he pushed through it the way he'd pushed through the snow on the ridge. "Her name was Maren. Maren Brightwater—no. Just Maren. She wasn't kin to you, I just—I used to say it like that so it sounded like I belonged to somebody." He wiped his nose with the back of his glove. "She's the only person I ever loved who's already gone. So it doesn't count, see? It's not like forgetting Bren or you or Doss. It's just—it's the only thing I have that I can give."

"Tam." Lena knelt in the snow in front of him, heedless of the soldiers' dogs baying somewhere below, heedless of everything but the boy's chapped face. "Listen to me. You don't have to do this. I can carry it. The covenant needs a reforging, and the rite is mine—maybe the gate will take everything from me and leave the rest of you whole. Maybe—"

"That's not how debts work." He said it gently, which was the worst part; he said it the way she might have said it, the way Corvin would have. "Doss told me that on the mountain. Everybody pays their own share or it isn't honest." He took a shuddering breath. "Besides. I dream about her every night. Every single night since the village burned. And every morning I wake up and remember she's gone all over again, like the first time." His chin trembled and steadied. "Maybe if I give her name away, I can keep the loving without keeping the hurting. Maybe that's what the Spire wants. Not to rob us. To carry the heavy part for a while."

Lena had no answer. Ten years of hidden paths, of burned maps, of a rule she'd built her life around—*never be seen*—and a twelve-year-old orphan had just walked past the far edge of everything she knew.

She pulled him into her arms and held him hard, feeling how light he was, how the bones of his shoulders pressed through the oversized coat. "Maren," she said into his hair. "I'll remember her for both of us. Whatever happens in there, whoever I am when this is done—I'm writing her name on the first map I draw."

"You better," he said into her shoulder, muffled and wet and almost laughing.

Then he stepped back, walked to the gate, and pressed his hand into the basin. "Maren," he said clearly, giving the name up into the cold air like releasing a bird. "Her name was Maren."

Gold light climbed the arch. The gate opened. Tam looked up into it, and for one strange moment Lena saw the reflection of the light move across his face, and within the glow—she would swear this later, to herself, when she was someone who did not know him—there was a shape. A woman's shape. Arms opening.

Tam smiled, small and astonished, and walked through.

* * *

Bren stood where she had left him, and now there was only the two of them, and behind them the sound of the pursuit had grown distinct: the crack of a whip on dog-harness, the ring of hobnails on ice, once, faintly, a shouted order carrying up the glacier in Veyra's clipped voice.

"They're twenty minutes out," Bren said. "Maybe fifteen."

"Then say your name quickly."

He didn't move. He looked at her the way he had looked at her across the campfire at the river ford, the first night, when Doss had wanted him dead and Lena had vouched for him with nothing but her own judgment to stand on. Sergeant Bren of the Third Crown Foot, who had refused to bind a gifted girl and thrown away a twenty-year soldier's life in a single sentence; who had held the bridge so strangers could escape; who had come back from Veyra's prison thinner and quieter, carrying Corvin's message like a coal he'd shielded with his own hands.

"I've been thinking about this since the shrine," he said. "Since Corvin's words. The price is memory." He flexed his gloved fingers. "I thought I knew mine. I was going to give my comrades'. Harl and Vesik and the rest of the eleven who died at Redford Bridge because I led them there for a king who turned out to be burning villages." His jaw worked. "I've carried their names like stones. I figured the Spire was doing me a kindness, taking them."

"But?" Lena said.

"But then I thought about what Tam said. What it means to choose." Bren turned to look down the glacier, toward the distant glitter of steel among the ice—the head of Veyra's column, unmistakable even at this distance. "Those men trusted me. Forgetting them feels like the easy way out, and I've had enough of easy ways. But here's the truth of it, Lena: the gate doesn't want our guilt. Guilt is just love with nowhere to go. That girl I wouldn't bind—Mira, her name was Mira—I love her too, in the way you love someone whose life you saved by ruining your own. Any name I love is fair payment. So." He squared his shoulders, the old parade-ground habit asserting itself one last time. "Harl. Vesik. Odo, Pell, and young Mira. All of them. Let the Spire take the whole weight."

"Bren—"

"It's not sacrifice," he said, and now he smiled, a tired, crooked thing she had seen maybe three times in all their weeks together. "It's arithmetic. I ran the numbers on that bridge at the ford and I'd run them again. Four names against a realm. That's the cheapest bill I've ever settled."

He walked to the gate. He knelt, and pressed his hand into the warm stone, and spoke five names into the basin, slowly, distinctly, the way a sergeant reads a casualty list—giving each man and the girl their full measure, no rushing, no stumbling. When he finished, the light rose through the arch in a flood, and Bren stood in its glow, and for a moment his face was younger than Lena had ever seen it, unburdened in a way that hurt to witness, because she knew exactly what it had cost.

"Lena." He turned back to her, and the gate blazed behind him. "Whatever you're planning in that mapmaker's head of yours—I saw the same thing you did at the shrine. The rite wants a key, and you're the key, and keys turn once." His voice dropped. "Don't try to cheat the price by leaving us out of your reckoning. If you go in there and hold anything back to spare yourself, the covenant will know, and it will fail, and everything we buried on this mountain will be for nothing."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I know what it looks like when a soldier pays half a debt." He held her gaze. "Pay all of it. And if you come out the other side not knowing me—come out anyway. I'll introduce myself. I'm good at waiting. Twenty years in the crown's foot taught me nothing if not that."

Then he stepped through the light, and was gone.

* * *

Lena stood alone before the Gate of Names.

The wind had dropped, or perhaps the Spire had gathered the mountain's breath into itself; the silence was enormous, pressing, alive. Below her, small as insects on the white shoulder of the world, Veyra's column wound upward through the ice-falls, and she could pick out the inquisitor herself by her stride, unhurried, certain, closing like a hand. An hour, perhaps. Perhaps less. Somewhere beyond the eastern peaks, Aldric's army was on the plain, marching on the free cities, and the covenant that could stop it hung by the thread of what Lena was willing to lose.

The basin waited, warm and open-handed.

She thought of all the names she owned. Corvin, who had taught her letters and paths and the shape of the gift, and who had gone to the inquisitors' cells so she could run. Doss, who owed her maps and had repaid them in avalanches and loyalty. Tam, who called her by her name like it was a place he lived. Bren, who had taught her that mercy was a discipline and not a weakness. A decade of small kindnesses scattered through the free cities—innkeepers who asked no questions, cartographers who covered for her, the whole quiet constellation of a hidden life. Every one of them a name. Every one of them loved, though she had never dared say it aloud, because saying it meant being seen.

*Never be seen.* Her oldest rule. And the covenant's final demand was that she be seen utterly—that she hand over the seeing itself, every memory of everyone who had ever looked at her and known her, and become a stranger walking out of a door of light.

Her hand hovered above the basin.

And the gate answered before she could speak. Light seeped up through the cupped stone, gentle as dawn, and within it—for the length of a heartbeat, no more—Lena saw the woman made of starlight whom she had met in the Veil Pass, standing inside the arch with her hands also cupped, mirroring the basin, mirroring Lena. Waiting. Patient as the mountain. As if to say: *whenever you are ready. Whenever you are brave enough.*

Lena drew a breath that shook all the way down to the floor of her.

She thought of Corvin's voice: *the price is memory*. She thought of Tam's: *everybody pays their own share or it isn't honest*. She thought of Bren at the ford, holding a bridge against an army so that other people could keep their futures.

"All right," she whispered—to the Spire, to the starlight woman, to every name she was about to unlearn. "All of them. I'm not holding anything back."

She pressed her palm into the warm stone, opened her mouth to speak the first name—

—and the light in the basin guttered.

It flickered, dimmed, flared sick and uneven, like a lamp in a draft, and high above her the whole face of the Spire groaned, a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat after a thousand years, and the starlight woman's image fractured into pieces. Through the broken glow, from deep inside the gate, Lena heard a new sound: not the Spire's voice at all, but boots—many boots—echoing in the corridor beyond, where no enemy should yet be able to stand.

The gate was already open. And something was coming out of it.
