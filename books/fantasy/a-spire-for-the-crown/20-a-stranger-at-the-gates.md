## 20. A Stranger at the Gates

The bells of Marrowgate had not rung like this in Bren's lifetime.

He stood on the eastern rampart with a cup of small beer going warm in his hand and watched the city spill itself into the streets below—lanterns strung from every balcony, tables dragged out of houses into the mud of the processional way, dancers stamping the frost out of the cobbles because no one wanted to wait for spring to be glad again. Three days since the sky above the Spire of Auren had torn open like a curtain, three days since the starlight people had come down the mountainside in their silent thousands and turned Aldric's vanguard into a memory of drifting motes, and still Marrowgate did not quite believe it. He could see the disbelief in how they celebrated: too loud, too much, everyone laughing a half-beat after the joke as if checking whether joy was permitted now.

It was permitted. Bren was fairly sure it was permitted.

He was less sure what he was permitted to feel. His body still expected orders. Twenty-two years in the crown's colors had trained him to stand where he was told and watch what he was told to watch, and so he stood at his post on the rampart, cup in hand, watching the free cities celebrate a victory they did not understand, won by a woman down there in the crowd who could not have picked him out of a line of two.

"You're doing it again," said Doss beside him.

"Doing what?"

"Standing like a fence post. Like someone's paying you." The smuggler wore a coat of deep green velvet that had clearly been acquired without payment, and he had a flask in one fist and a garland of dried marigolds around his neck that Tam had put there an hour ago, which Doss had not removed, which Bren found more telling than anything Doss had ever said aloud. "Drink something. Sit somewhere. It's over."

Bren turned the cup in his fingers. "Is it."

Doss snorted. "Aldric's armies are broken. The usurper's fled north with maybe two hundred men who still remember whose side they're on. Veyra—" He stopped, and his voice did something complicated around the name. "Veyra's being called a hero by half the council and a traitor by the other half, which is probably the most honest thing anyone's said about her. And the spire-folk are sitting up on that mountain like patient guests, waiting for us to figure out what we're supposed to say to them. So yes, soldier. It's over. What's left is the part nobody writes songs about."

"The part where she doesn't know us."

Doss went quiet. Below them, a fiddle struck up something fast and the crowd roared approval, and the sound washed up against the walls and broke apart.

"She knew you'd say that," Doss said finally. "Before. Before the rite. She made me promise—if she came through it and didn't come through it whole, I wasn't to let you all smother her. Wasn't to let you hover like a bunch of grieving relatives at a bedside. Said it wouldn't be fair to her, having everyone look at her like she'd died." He drank from the flask and passed it over. Bren took it, mostly to have something to do with his hands. "She also said you were the most stubborn man she'd met outside of a mule market, and that you'd ignore her anyway. So I suppose we're both keeping our promises."

Bren looked down at the crowd again, and this time he let himself find her.

She was standing near the great fountain in the processional square, at the edge of the light, the way she always used to stand at the edge of camps—close enough to belong, far enough to leave quickly. She wore a plain gray traveling dress that someone must have given her, because nothing she owned had survived the mountain, and her hair was loose, and she was watching the dancers with an expression Bren recognized from a thousand maps: attention, careful and total, cataloguing everything, committing to nothing. Her hands hung at her sides and then, without her seeming to notice, began to move—a finger tracing slow invisible lines against her thigh, arcs and switchbacks and the long patient spirals of paths only she had ever walked.

Her hands remembered. That was the thing that undid him every time he saw it. Corvin's warning had been *the price is memory*, and Lena had paid it smiling, or close to it, and what the covenant had taken was everything—the smuggler who loved her, the boy she'd pulled out of a burning village, the soldier who'd held a bridge so she could run—and yet her hands still drew the Veil Pass when she thought no one was looking, still found north in a cellar with no windows, still reached, sometimes, toward people she didn't know, as if some part of her had been folded away rather than destroyed and might, given years, be unfolded again.

Tam sat beside her on the fountain's rim. Of course he did. The boy had appointed himself to that post within hours of the battle and had not been relieved since, and he had explained himself to Bren once, fiercely, in the dark of a hospice corridor: *"She saved me first. She doesn't remember saving me, but I remember being saved, and somebody has to remember for both of us until she can again."* Eleven years old, and he had already understood the arithmetic of grief better than any soldier Bren had ever served under.

Lena laughed at something Tam said—a short startled sound, a stranger's laugh—and Bren's chest tightened like a fist closing.

"I'm going down there," he said.

* * *

He did not go straight to her. He had learned that much in three days.

The first day, when she had woken in the hospice beneath Marrowgate's temple of the Wayfarers, blank-eyed and polite and quietly terrified, Bren had made the mistake of rushing. He had crossed the room in four strides, saying her name—*Lena, Lena, it's Bren, it's Bren, you're safe*—and she had pressed herself back against the pillows with her hands raised between them, and the look on her face had not been fear exactly. It had been worse than fear. It had been courtesy. She had looked at this large scarred stranger looming over her bed shouting her own name at her, and she had said, gently, "I'm sorry. I think you've mistaken me for someone," and Bren had felt the bridge under his feet give way all over again, except this time there was no river to justify the falling.

So now he took the long way down through the celebrating city. He bought roasted chestnuts from a vendor because buying things felt like a human act and he needed one. He let a drunk fishwife kiss his cheek because she'd decided he was a hero of the Spire and would not be argued with. He walked past the fountain twice, at a distance, letting her see him the way you let a wild horse see you—not approaching, just existing, becoming part of the landscape. Tam saw him on the second pass and gave him a small nod, the co-conspirator's nod, and murmured something to Lena, and Bren watched her glance up, register him, and look away without recognition and without alarm. Good. That was progress. Two days ago the sight of him had made her shoulders climb toward her ears.

On the third pass he stopped, ten feet away, and held up the paper bag of chestnuts like an offering.

"These are terrible," he said. "The chestnuts in this city. I've eaten them three days running hoping they'd improve. They haven't. I'm starting to think it's me."

She studied him. He watched her study him, and it was the strangest thing in the world—to be known completely by someone and to watch them not know you at all, both at once, in the same face. Her eyes were the same gray-green as the high passes before snow. Nothing about her had changed. Everything about her had changed.

"Why would you eat food you don't like?" she asked.

"Army habit," Bren said. "You eat what's in front of you and you're grateful for it, because tomorrow there might not be anything. Took me six years to stop doing it after I left the colors. Still haven't managed."

Something flickered across her face—not recognition, but its ghost, the shape recognition leaves behind. "You were a soldier."

"Sergeant. Crown Fourth Lances." He said it plainly, without flinching, because she deserved to hear it without flinching. She deserved to know that the man talking to her had worn the enemy's badge for two decades, and had held a bridge against her enemies' enemies' enemies, and had been taken for it, and had survived an interrogation that had never once laid a hand on him because Veyra had understood, better than any torturer, that truth breaks men faster than iron. "I deserted last autumn. There was a girl in a village called Hollowmere who could make plants grow by singing to them, and my captain wanted her bound and carted south, and I found out what binding actually does to someone, and I walked away in the middle of the night and kept walking until I fell into your camp."

"I had a camp?" she said, wry, careful.

"You did. Smugglers, exiles, one boy, and a mapmaker who could walk roads that aren't on any map." He opened the bag and offered it. "You drew me a picture of Hollowmere once. From my description. You got the church spire wrong and I teased you about it for a week and you told me that if I was so clever I could hold my own pencil, and then you fixed it anyway, without admitting it, and handed it back like it had always been right."

Lena took a chestnut. She peeled it slowly, her thumbnail splitting the shell along its seam with an economy that spoke of ten thousand meals eaten in cold places. "That sounds like me," she said at last, very quietly. "I mean—it sounds like a person I'd be. Everyone keeps telling me stories. The boy. The smuggler with the ridiculous flowers. The priestesses here. And none of them are the same person, exactly, but they overlap, and the overlapping parts..." She looked up at him. "They sound like someone worth having been."

"You are," Bren said. "Worth it. You were the best person I ever met, and I met you while you were running for your life through a swamp with a stolen map and a child in tow, so believe me, I've seen you at your worst."

That earned him almost a smile. Almost. It was the closest thing he'd gotten in three days and he treasured it the way he'd once treasured dry socks.

"And you," she said. "In these stories. What am I supposed to know about you?"

Bren considered lying—considered the gentle version, the version where he was just a friend, just a comrade, just a man who'd fought beside her. But Doss had told him what she'd said before the rite, and Tam had told him what she'd believed, and if there was one lesson the whole mad year had taught him it was that Lena Brightwater had never once wanted to be protected from the truth. She'd wanted it drawn accurately, even when it hurt.

"That I followed you up a frozen mountain expecting to die," he said, "and came down alive, and that the reason I'm alive is that you chose to forget me so that everyone else could live. And that I'd trade every one of those chestnuts, and both my arms, to have you look at me the way you looked at the bridge at Marrowgate Ford the day we met—which was like I was something you'd decided to trust, against all your good judgment."

The fountain splashed behind her. Somewhere a fiddle faltered and restarted. Lena held the half-peeled chestnut in her palm and looked at it for a long moment, and when she spoke, her voice had gone thin.

"I don't remember you," she said. "But I dreamed about a bridge. Last night. Water under it, fast and brown, and someone standing on it facing the wrong way, facing toward me instead of away, holding a spear like—" Her hand moved, unbidden, into a guard stance, and Bren's breath caught, because that was *his* stance, drilled into him at fourteen, the one he'd held on that bridge while Veyra's riders came. "—like that. Like they meant to keep something back. And I woke up crying and I don't cry, I don't think I'm someone who cries, and I couldn't tell anyone because why would you cry about a bridge?"

"It was a good bridge," Bren managed. His voice came out wrecked. "Best bridge I ever stood on."

She looked at him then—really looked, the way she must once have looked at a blank stretch of parchment before setting the first line—and something in her expression shifted, some door opening a crack onto a room full of light she couldn't enter yet.

"Tell me the rest," she said. "About the bridge. About all of it. Not tonight—I can't take all of it tonight, I can feel that—but start it. Start anywhere. And then tomorrow, start again. And the day after that." A pause, and her chin lifted slightly, stubbornly, and for one vertiginous second she was so entirely herself that Bren's eyes burned. "Because I've decided something. Everyone keeps treating me like a grave. Like something to visit respectfully and then walk away from. And I may not know who I was, but I know who I am right now, and right now I am a person who does not accept unfinished maps. So you're going to help me finish this one. All of you. Even if it takes years."

"Even if it takes years," Bren agreed.

She held out the bag. "Then sit down, Sergeant, and share these terrible chestnuts, and start with the church spire I got wrong."

He sat. He started there, with the spire and the teasing and the swamp, and Tam leaned in from the fountain's rim with his gap-toothed grin, and one by one, over the course of that long bright evening, the others drifted near—Doss loud and unrepentant, the priestesses soft-footed, even, at the edge of the lantern light, a tall woman in a travel-stained cloak whom the crowds parted around without knowing why, watching with an expression Bren could not read and did not ask about.

And when Lena finally rose to go, escorted by Tam toward the temple, she paused beside Bren and said, hesitantly, like a woman testing ice:

"One more thing. Tomorrow. Will you be here?"

"Every day," Bren said. "However long it takes."

She nodded, satisfied, and walked into the crowd—and Bren noticed, as she went, that her right hand was moving against her thigh again, tracing lines no one else could see. But this time, just before the lanterns swallowed her, the lines curved back. Toward the fountain. Toward them.

Toward home, perhaps, being redrawn.
