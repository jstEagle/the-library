## 21. Maps Without Memory

The first thing Lena learned about her new life was that it had edges.

She discovered them the way a blind woman discovers the walls of a room—by walking into them, patiently, one at a time. There was the edge of Tam's face, which she knew was dear to her because everyone said so and because something in her chest pulled toward him like a compass needle, though the needle pointed at nothing she could name. There was the edge of Doss's voice, gravel and smoke, which made her want to laugh for reasons that had been burned out of her. There was the edge of Bren, who stood at the far side of every room she entered and watched her with an expression she had no word for, and who flinched whenever she looked back.

And there was the edge of the gift itself, which had not burned with the rest of her.

That was the cruelest mercy of it. The covenant had taken her memories—the smell of Corvin's ink-stained study, her mother's lullaby, ten years of dawns on the Thornfell ridgelines—and left her everything else. Her hands still knew how to hold a pen. Her eyes still caught the shimmer of a hidden path the way other people's eyes caught sunlight on water. She was a lock with every tooth intact and nothing left inside to guard.

* * *

Marrowgate in the weeks after the battle was a city learning to breathe again.

Lena walked its streets each morning because staying still made the emptiness louder. The city had been spared—Aldric's vanguard shattered at the Spire's threshold, the usurper's banners trampled into the mud of the Auren plain—and now its people were doing the strange, clumsy work of peace. Merchants repainted their shutters. Children chalked spire-folk sigils on the cobbles, copying the shapes that had appeared in the sky during the battle, getting most of them wrong. In the harbor square, a returned spire-folk envoy stood on a crate answering questions from a crowd that could not decide whether to be reverent or merely curious, and whose starlit skin flickered nervously under so many staring eyes.

Lena watched from the edge of the crowd, as she always did. Watching was safe. Watching required no memories.

"You're up early."

Tam fell into step beside her, breath steaming in the autumn cold. He did this every morning—appeared at her elbow as if he'd never been anywhere else. He was taller than she remembered, which meant nothing, since she remembered almost nothing; but people kept saying it, so she'd adopted it as fact. He carried two rolls from the bakery on Tanner's Row, warm through the cloth, and handed her one without asking if she wanted it.

He never asked anymore. That was part of the new arrangement too.

"Couldn't sleep," she said. It was true most mornings, though she couldn't have said why. Sleep took her down to a place where shapes moved behind glass—faces, voices, a fire in a stone hearth, someone laughing—and she woke reaching for names that weren't there.

"Bad dreams?"

"Worse. Good ones." She bit into the roll. "I dream about people I don't know, and I miss them anyway. How is that possible?"

Tam looked out over the harbor for a long moment. When he answered, his voice was careful, the way it always was when they came near this country. "Maybe missing isn't stored in the same place as remembering."

She turned that over. "You sound like Corvin."

The boy went very still. "You remember him?"

"No." She shook her head slowly. "But everyone tells me I loved him. And when I say his name, my mouth does something strange. Like biting foil." She smiled faintly. "I've decided that counts as remembering. A little."

Tam laughed—a wet, startled sound—and had to look away at the masts for a while. Lena let him. She was learning to let people grieve around her without trying to fix it, because she had finally understood that she was, in some way she couldn't feel, the cause of it. They had all lost someone. Their someone was standing right in front of them, eating a roll.

"I brought you something," Tam said finally, digging in his coat. "From the map-sellers' quarter. I know you said you didn't want—" He stopped himself, changed course, held it out. "Just look."

It was a chart. A surveyor's copy of the Thornfell passes, freshly printed, the ink still sharp. Lena took it and her breath caught before her mind could explain why.

The mountains rose off the page in fine hatched lines, and threaded among them—faint, almost invisible, drawn by some cartographer who must have half-seen them and doubted his own eyes—were paths that should not exist. Ways through cliff faces. Forths across valleys where no bridge stood. The hidden roads.

Her hands began to shake.

"They're wrong," she heard herself say. "Here—" her finger traced a line above the Veil Pass, moving with a certainty that had nothing to do with thought, "—it doesn't cross at the col. It drops along the eastern face, through the notch where the rock folds back on itself. You can't see it from below. You can't see it at all unless—" She stopped.

Unless you had the gift.

Tam was watching her with an expression she was beginning to know well: hope and heartbreak wearing the same face. "Your hands still know," he said quietly.

"My hands still know," she echoed. She stared at the false path on the printed page and felt, beneath the vast blankness of her loss, something small and stubborn stir. Not memory. Instinct. The difference mattered, though she couldn't yet say why. "Take me to buy paper," she said. "Good paper. And ink."

* * *

The room Doss had rented for them sat above a chandler's shop near the north wall, three narrow floors of tallow-smell and salt-rotted beams. Lena claimed the corner room with the best light without knowing why she wanted it, and only later learned—from Bren, in one of his careful, wounded sentences—that it had been hers once, in another life, when she'd passed through Marrowgate on her rounds.

She set up her drafting table facing the window and began, on the second morning, to draw.

It started as discipline. Dr. Vessal—the city physician who had examined her after the Spire and pronounced her "hale of body, whole of faculty, and hollow as a winter tree"—had recommended occupation. So she drew what she saw: the harbor, the rooftops, the line of the Wall hills to the west. Simple things. Honest things.

But by the fourth day, the honest things had begun to grow companions.

A lane appeared on her street-map that led nowhere—or rather, led somewhere, through a courtyard wall that had no gate. A stair descended off her harbor sketch into water, and her hand insisted, in small confident strokes, that at low tide the stair emerged and continued across the harbor bed to the old sea-gate. Paths no one mapped. Paths only she could see.

On the seventh day she drew the Thornfell range entire, from memory she did not possess, and filled it with roads that would make any crown surveyor weep, and then sat back and looked at what her hands had done, and wept herself—not for grief exactly, and not for joy, but for the strange vertigo of meeting yourself in your own handwriting.

Bren found her like that. He came to the door with an armful of firewood, saw her bent over the table with tears on her cheeks, and froze in the doorway like a man at a border crossing.

"I'm all right," she said, without turning. "I think I'm more than all right. Look."

He crossed the room slowly—everything he did around her was slow now, deliberate, a man walking on ice over deep water—and set the wood down and looked at the map. His breath went out of him.

"That's the pass we climbed," he said. "To Auren. Exactly. Even the place where Doss dug Tam out." His finger hovered over a fold in the drawn mountains. "I've never seen it put down on paper. No one has."

"It was in me," Lena said. "All of it. The covenant took the remembering and left the knowing." She wiped her face with her wrist, impatient with the tears. "Do you understand what that means? I lost everyone I love. But I didn't lose *them*, not entirely—they're in here—" she pressed her fist briefly against her sternum, "—the way a road is in a map even when the mapmaker's dead. I just can't read myself anymore."

Bren was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Then maybe," he said, "we read you together. Slowly. However long it takes."

She looked up at him—at this soldier she had vouched for, this man who had held a bridge for strangers and broken in a salt prison and come out the other side carrying a dead man's last words—and she felt the pull again, the compass-needle tug, stronger than it ever pointed toward anyone else. Whatever he had been to her, it lived deeper than the burn had reached.

"Bren," she said carefully. "When you look at me. What do you see?"

He met her eyes. It cost him visibly. "Someone I'd follow up a frozen mountain again," he said. "Even knowing how it ends." Then, gentler: "Especially knowing."

She nodded slowly and turned back to her map, because looking at him any longer made the hollow ache in a way that felt less like absence and more like hunger, and she wasn't ready to name that yet. But she moved her chair slightly to one side, making room at the table, and after a moment he pulled up the second stool and sat, and they worked in silence until the light failed.

* * *

Doss arrived on the ninth evening with wine, noise, and six smugglers in various states of repair, and announced that debts were being settled whether anyone liked it or not.

The chandler's back room filled with the smell of roast onions and spilled ale. These were faces Lena half-knew—the ones who had stayed, who had climbed the ice spine with them, who had watched the sky over Auren tear open in silver fire. They treated her with a careful warmth she was still learning to bear: close enough to touch, far enough not to frighten. One of them, a scarred woman named Pell, kept glancing at her and looking away, and Lena realized with a jolt that Pell had been crying earlier and had scrubbed her face raw to hide it.

They had all lost someone at that table. Their someone was drinking their wine.

"To the Brightwater!" Doss bellowed at last, hoisting his cup. The room roared. "Who owed me nothing and paid me everything! Who drew me maps that saved my miserable neck a dozen times over, and then walked into a mountain and gave up—" his voice cracked, and he covered it with volume, "—gave up more than any of us will ever know, and came back out again, because that's what she does. She comes back out again!"

"To the Brightwater!" they thundered.

Lena raised her cup and drank, and smiled, and felt the smile sit strangely on her face—like a borrowed coat that fit perfectly and belonged to someone else.

Later, when the room had thinned to embers and snoring, Doss lowered himself onto the bench beside her. Up close, in the candlelight, the smuggler looked older than his swagger allowed. There was gray in his beard she was fairly sure hadn't been there nine days ago.

"You hate this," he said. Not a question.

"I hate that you all look at me like a grave with a person standing next to it."

"Aye. Well." He swirled his cup. "Get used to it or get famous enough that they stop. Neither works, in my experience." He was quiet a moment. "You know what I keep thinking? I owed you. Ten years of owing you, maps and lives and one very memorable night in Saltmarsh I'll not detail with ladies present. And I paid it back on that mountain, catching the lad. Paid in full, I told myself. Clean ledger." He snorted. "Then I watch you sit there not knowing my name's worth a damn to you, and I realize I've run up a new debt I can't pay. Because the only thing that settles it is you remembering me. And that's gone, girl. Gone into the fire, spent on saving us all."

Lena studied him. The compass-needle pulled faintly toward him too—toward laughter, maybe, or loyalty, or some long night of maps and arguments she could not see.

"Then start a new account," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"You said I drew you maps that saved your neck. Fine." She leaned forward. "Draw with me. Tomorrow. My hands remember routes nobody living has seen—trade ways, escape ways, ways through the Thornfell that could feed half the free cities if anyone knew them. I can't carry those alone. I need someone who knows which ends are worth putting on paper and which ends get people hanged." She shrugged. "So. Partners? New debt. Yours to run up."

Doss stared at her. Then he laughed—that great rolling laugh she'd apparently once known well enough to miss—and thrust out a hand rough as rope.

"Partners," he said. "Gods help the free cities."

* * *

She woke before dawn, as she always did, to the shapes behind the glass.

This time she lay still and watched them instead of reaching. A fire in a stone hearth. An old man's hands turning pages. A boy's laughter on a high trail. A bridge, and a man holding it. None of the shapes had names, but they had weight, and she was learning—slowly, the way she was relearning everything—to stop treating the weight as theft and start treating it as inheritance.

Something had been given to her, she understood now. Not returned—given. The woman of starlight had taken the past and left her the present, and the present was full: a city rebuilding, a boy who appeared every morning with warm bread, a soldier learning to stand in doorways instead of hiding beyond them, a smuggler with a new ledger to ruin.

Lena got up in the dark, lit the lamp, and unrolled fresh paper.

She drew Marrowgate first, precise and loving, every street she'd walked in her blank-eyed mornings. Then, because her hand insisted, she drew the roads beyond it—the coast road south, the river fords, the high ways east—and then further, past the edge of any chart she'd ever been shown, into country no human map had touched in a thousand years. Country where the spire-folk were waking. Country that would need, very soon, to be woven back into the world.

Her pen slowed at the margin of the known. Beyond it, her instinct hummed like a struck string, pointing south and east, toward Marrowgate's council hall, where in three days' time humans and spire-folk would sit down at one table for the first time since the world broke.

There would be maps needed for that, she thought. Maps of two realms becoming one. And someone would have to draw them.

Lena dipped her pen, and began.
