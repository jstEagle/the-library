## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There is a particular way a ledger smells when it has been lying to you. Ink and tallow, mostly, but underneath it something like a held breath. I have kept the books of the Gilded Meridian for nine years, and I can tell you that impossible things are the easiest to audit. They never argue about their value.

Take entry 4,117: *One storm, caged, minor temperament, sold to Lady Vessick of the Lantern Quarter.* The storm had been delivered in a brass-and-glass sphere no larger than a melon, and it had spent its captivity sulking against the glass in little gray moods of drizzle. When Lady Vessick's footman came to collect it, it pressed itself flat against the far side of the sphere and made a sound I can only describe as *please*. I marked it down at forty crowns, which was theft, but not by me. The guild took sixty percent off the top of everything, including weather.

Entry 4,118 was worse. *Vial of borrowed luck, provenance disputed, seven years' interest accrued.* Borrowed luck is the Meridian's most reliable product, because everyone who buys it already believes they were owed better fortune than they got. The vial sat on my desk that morning, glowing faintly gold, humming like a wasp in a jar. Luck always hums when it's been borrowed too long. Somewhere out in the city, some poor soul's life had gone sideways for seven years while a merchant's daughter won three card games in a row. I wrote *asset, depreciating* beside it and did not let myself think about whose luck it had been.

This is the work. This is what I am good at. Rosalind Quintana, Keeper of Ledgers to the Gilded Meridian, a woman who can put a price on anything except herself.

I finished the storm, capped the vial of luck under its leaden bell, and went down to the cellar, because it was Tuesday, and on Tuesdays the cellar needed me most.

* * *

The Gilded Meridian keeps beasts the way other houses keep wine — in the dark, at a controlled temperature, for later sale. The cellar ran the length of the building beneath Copperlane Street, and it held, on any given day, perhaps thirty creatures whose only crime was being worth money. A pair of moon-oxen with hides like hammered pewter. A clutch of ember-wyrms no bigger than my forearm, bought from a smuggler who swore they'd been "ethically harvested," a phrase I have come to despise. A griffin, half-grown, chained to an iron ring because she had already learned what hands do.

The law is simple enough that even a child can recite it, and children do, skipping rope to the rhythm of it: *No voice between man and beast. No speech, no sign, no parley. The speaker's tongue is kindled with the rest.* Beast-speaking is the one crime in Vantrell punished by fire rather than rope, because the last time speakers walked freely among us, so the histories say, the borders between realms wore thin as old cloth and things came through that wore our dead like coats.

I was eleven years old when I learned I could do it. Not speak, exactly — not in words, not at first. But a horse I was grooming had told me, clear as a bell rung inside my skull, that its left hind shoe pinched. And I had understood it the way you understand your own name. There was no deciding about it after that. You cannot un-know a language. You can only decide whether anyone hears you using it.

For eighteen years, nobody has. In this cellar, I am fluent.

"Good morning, ladies," I said to the moon-oxen, and they turned their great blank faces toward me, and one of them said — in the wordless way, the way of pressure and warmth and image — *cold, and the small ones are cold, and the man with the keys forgot again.*

"I know," I said aloud, softly, because speaking aloud down here felt less like a crime somehow. "I'll see to it."

The ember-wyrms were worse. They are fire-kin, and fire-kin feel the cold as a kind of insult. Their brazier had been allowed to burn down to coals overnight, and when I knelt by their cage they pressed against the bars and their skins dimmed from orange to a sullen brown, and all six of them said at once, overlapping, a chorus of small furious misery: *dying, dying slow, the heat is leaving the world.*

"You're not dying," I told them, and banked the coals, and fed in fresh charcoal, and watched their color climb back to orange, then gold. "You're dramatic. There's a difference."

But they had said *the heat is leaving the world*, and I carried that phrase up the stairs with me like a stone in my boot, because the wyrms were right about one thing, whatever else their drama: this winter was wrong. Nine weeks of snow without a thaw. The river frozen to its bed. Old men in the Copperlane taverns saying they hadn't seen ice like this since their grandfathers' day, and their grandfathers hadn't either. Winter is supposed to be a debt the year pays back in spring. This one showed no interest in repayment.

The griffin watched me work with one yellow eye. She said nothing. She had stopped saying anything months ago, which frightened me more than screaming would have. Hopelessness, in a beast, is very quiet.

* * *

Corvane found me at my desk in the late afternoon, and he did not knock, which he always does, which meant something was wrong before he said a word.

My mentor is seventy if he is a day, a narrow man built like a ledger column himself, all straight lines and ruled margins. He taught me double-entry bookkeeping and how to tell when a seller is lying by where he puts his hands. He has never once asked why I linger in the cellars. I have never once wondered aloud why he doesn't ask. Between us we maintain a fiction so well-kept it might as well be an asset on the books.

"The Prophecy Ledger is writing again," he said.

I set down my pen with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hand wants to shake. "Ledgers don't write themselves, Corvane."

"This one did." He laid a key on my desk — black iron, older than the guild, older probably than the street above us. "Third shelf of the vault, behind the salt contracts. Go and look. Then come to my office and tell me I'm a foolish old man."

The vault smelled of dust and cold iron and, faintly, of the same held-breath smell I know from lying ledgers. The Prophecy Ledger sat where he said, and I knew it at once though I'd never seen it open: a book bound in gray hide gone stiff as roof-slate, its clasps fused with a century of disuse. Every apprentice learns the story. A hundred years ago, at the sealing of the Covenant between Vantrell and Ashfall, the guild's founders commissioned a ledger to record the terms. It wrote them out over a week and then closed itself and never opened again, and the guild, embarrassed by the whole theatrical business, shelved it and got on with the profitable work of selling impossible things.

The clasps were open. The cover lay back. And across the first blank page, in ink still wet enough to shine, someone or something had written:

*Tonight, the third of Deepfrost, the ember comes anew. Let the speaker be found before the stone goes dark.*

Today's date. The third of Deepfrost. Tonight.

I stood in that cold vault for a long moment, listening to my own heart, and I thought: *safety is a debt too, Rosalind. Eighteen years of it, quietly accruing interest. Somewhere there is a page with your name on it, waiting for the date to be filled in.*

I locked the vault. I did not go to Corvane's office. I went back to my desk and I did the bravest, stupidest thing available to a woman of my habits: I pretended it was an ordinary evening, and I balanced the day's accounts, and I waited to find out what the world intended to charge me.

I had gotten as far as the vial of borrowed luck when the front doors of the Gilded Meridian slammed open, and the enforcers came in dragging something between them that was struggling, and snarling, and — I heard it clearly, over the boots and the iron and the shouting, in the wordless voice I have spent my whole life pretending I cannot hear —

It said my name.
