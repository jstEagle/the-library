## 2. Tonight's Date

There are two kinds of trouble in a guild ledger. The kind you write down, and the kind that writes you down.

I learned the second kind at half past nine, by candlelight, with my sleeves still smelling of the cellar and my hands still warm from a badger who had, an hour earlier, been the most frightened creature in Vantrell.

* * *

The audit should have been simple. The Gilded Meridian trades in impossible things, and impossible things require paperwork, and paperwork requires me. I had spent the evening reconciling the autumn entries: a caged storm, acquired from a drowning sailor off the Sere Coast, currently housed in Vault Three and audibly sulking; a vial of borrowed luck, third-hand, which had cost the guild forty crowns and would cost its buyer considerably more, since the luck in question belonged to a man who was, as of last month, dead and therefore owed it back. I entered the storm's feed costs. Storms, it turns out, eat thunder, and thunder is not cheap.

Routine. I am very good at routine. Routine is the wall I built around my life, stone by careful stone, and I have kept it mortared for eleven years.

Which is why, when Corvane found me in the counting room and said, "Rosalind. I need you to look at the Prophecy Ledger," my first instinct was to refuse.

The Prophecy Ledger sits in the deep archive behind a door with three locks, and for a century it has done nothing but gather dust and dignity. It was the founding masterwork of the Meridian — the book that recorded, in its first and only year of use, the covenant between Vantrell and the beast-realm of Ashfall, and then, having said its piece, went silent. A hundred years of blank pages. The guild kept it the way rich men keep a famous horse's skeleton: reverently, and with no expectation that it would ever do anything again.

"It's writing," Corvane said.

"People write in it," I said. "That's what ledgers—"

"Rosalind." He sat down across from me, and I saw then that his hands were not steady. Corvane's hands are always steady; they have signed away fortunes, estates, and once, allegedly, a small island. "The ink is not ours. The ink is not anyone's. Master Ovell checked the seals at midsummer. They were whole. Tonight they were broken from the inside, and there are four lines on the first blank page in a hand that matches the founding entries exactly, and the fourth line—" He stopped. Started again. "The fourth line is today's date."

The candle between us guttered, though there was no draft.

"Come and look at it," he said, "before the masters do. Because if the masters see what I think I see, they will start asking questions, and the first question will be *who*, and I would very much like the answer to that question to be no one."

He didn't say *you*. He didn't have to. Corvane has known what I am since the winter I soothed his dying hound and thought no one was watching. He has kept my secret for eleven years the way he keeps everything — in a ledger, at interest, and I have never once asked what the balance was. Tonight I suspected I was about to find out.

* * *

The deep archive smells of cold iron and old vellum, and the Prophecy Ledger lay open on its lectern like a mouth mid-sentence.

The first three lines were in the old hand, the founding scribe's, elegant and strange:

*When the long winter comes to eat the border,*
*and the covenant thins to a thread,*
*then shall the realms look to a speaker—*

And the fourth line, in that same hand, in ink that was still, faintly, wet:

*—the ember anew, whose voice is given freely, on this day: the twenty-third of Frostmoon.*

Today is the twenty-third of Frostmoon.

I want to tell you that I felt awe. What I actually felt was vertigo — the particular vertigo of standing on a stair you have climbed ten thousand times and finding a step missing. The future had reached backward through a hundred blank years and put its finger on a date, and the date was mine. Not my name, thank all mercies. But dates are names, if you know where to look, and I keep the ledgers. I know where to look.

"Corvane," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended, "I audit this guild's impossible things. This is an impossible thing. It's not in the catalogue."

"No," he said. "And it won't be. We're going to close the book, lock the door, and decide that nothing happened. The masters are at their supper until midnight. That gives us two hours to agree on a story."

I reached out to close the book.

That is when the shouting started.

* * *

You can measure a guild by what makes its enforcers run. At the Meridian, they run for escaped assets, breached vaults, and fire — in that order, though Master Ovell has argued for reversing the last two. The boots on the archive stairs were running for all three at once, and Corvane moved between me and the door with the smoothness of a man who has spent thirty years placing his body between the guild and its problems.

They brought it up from the receiving dock in a warded cage of black iron, six enforcers and a winch, and the thing inside it made the caged storm in Vault Three sound like a lullaby. It was fox-shaped the way a knife is spoon-shaped — technically, and only if you were being generous. It was the size of a hound, ember-colored, with too much awareness in its face, and when the cage light caught its eyes they were the gold of banked coals.

"Caught at the border stones," the enforcer captain reported, breathless. "Crossed out of Ashfall on its own, through the snow, straight toward the city gate. It didn't run when we took it. It *asked* to be taken. And then it said—" The captain's face did something complicated. "It said a name, Master Corvane. It's been saying nothing else since the docks. We thought you'd want to hear it yourself."

"Say it," Corvane said.

And the fox-thing, in a voice like dry leaves and banked fire, in flawless, accentless Vantrelli, said:

"Rosalind Quintana."

The archive went very quiet. Somewhere above us, the supper bell rang for the masters' second course.

I did not move. I have spent eleven years learning not to move — not when a merchant's monkey swears at him in my hearing, not when a cart horse begs me to loosen its harness, not when any of the thousand small voices of the beast-world say something that would cost my life to acknowledge. Stillness is the first law of my survival. So I stood still, and I watched Corvane stand still, and between the two of us we generated enough stillness to sink a ship.

"It has been saying that name," the captain repeated, "for four hours."

"Then it is either a coincidence or a trick," Corvane said, "and either way it is worth nothing until it's worth something. Cage it in Vault Five. No one speaks to it. No one feeds it without my seal. And captain—" his voice dropped into the register he uses for the things he never puts in writing, "—this creature does not exist yet. It will exist tomorrow, after the masters have been consulted and the entry properly made. Tonight it is a rumor, and rumors are not logged."

The captain hesitated. "And the woman's name it keeps saying?"

"Is a common name," Corvane said, "and the creature is a liar from a realm of liars. Go."

They went. The winch groaned down the stairs. And Corvane and I stood alone in the archive with a prophecy and a locked door between us and a creature that knew me — knew my *name*, my whole name, the one I had not spoken aloud in this building in eleven years.

"Corvane." I made my voice a clerk's voice, flat and reasonable. "That creature came out of Ashfall, through a killing winter, on a night that a hundred-year-old book chose to start writing again, and it asked for me by name. That is not a rumor. That is an entry. And you know I'm the only one in this guild who could—"

"Could what?" he said sharply. "Speak to it? Confirm its story? Put your gift on the guild's books, Rosalind, because that is what you'd be doing. The moment you answer that creature, you stop being a rumor too. And the guild does not protect rumors. The guild *audits* them."

He was right. That was the horror of it. He was right, and he had bought my silence for eleven years with kindness and cover stories, and I had paid without reading the terms, because the alternative was a pyre. That was the bargain. Silence for survival. My whole life, itemized.

"I need to hear what it says," I said.

"What you *need*," Corvane said, "is to go home, sleep, and let me bury this. Both of it. The book and the beast. By morning I'll have the ledger sealed and the creature sold to a collector in the Sere Coast who asks no questions, and in ten years you'll be Keeper of Ledgers and this will be a story you never tell."

And that — that comfortable, generous, terrible future — might have been the one I chose. I want to be honest about that. I was afraid. I have always been afraid; fear is the mortar in my wall.

But then, from the stairs below, through three floors of stone and iron, the fox-thing began to speak again. Not my name this time. A plea, worn thin by repetition, in that dry-leaf voice:

"Speaker. Speaker, they are dying. Speaker, the winter is eating the border, and the court has no voice left to give, and I have run so far, and I am so tired. Speaker, please. Please listen. Please. Please."

It went on and on, the same words, patient as snowfall, and I stood with my hand on the locked archive door and understood that I had heard this voice before — not the words, but the *shape* of it. The same shape as the badger in the cellar. The same shape as every creature that has ever begged me in the dark and been answered with silence, because silence was the price of my life.

Eleven years of silence. And the ledger on the lectern behind me had just spent a hundred blank years finding its voice again on the very night something crossed a dying border to find mine.

"Corvane," I said. "What does the guild owe to a debt it can hear begging?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic the Meridian does with everything, the arithmetic that has never once, in eleven years, come out in my favor without him standing on the scale.

"Nothing," he said quietly. "The guild owes it nothing. But you don't. Go down to Vault Five. Listen. And Rosalind — whatever it tells you, whatever you decide — you were never in this archive tonight, and neither was I."

I went down the stairs two at a time.

The fox-thing was pressed against the bars of its cage, and its coal-gold eyes found me in the dark before my candle did, and it did not say my name. It said, in a whisper cracked from four hours of begging:

"You came. The prophecy said the speaker would come. I am Sennet, of the Ember Court of Ashfall, and I need you to understand — before anyone else in this building learns what you are — that the covenant is dying, the winter is not weather, and you are the only voice left that can save both our worlds. Please, Rosalind Quintana. We have until the masters finish their supper."
