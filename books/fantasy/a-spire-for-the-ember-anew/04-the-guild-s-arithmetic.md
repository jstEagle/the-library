## 4. The Guild's Arithmetic

They did not burn me. I want that understood from the first, because it is the detail everyone finds so merciful when I tell the story, and it is the detail that taught me everything I know about mercy as practiced by merchants.

Burning is expensive. You need a pyre, a sanction from the Wardens' Guild, a crowd bought with ale, and—this is the part no one mentions—a fire hot enough that the condemned does not scream too long, because screaming is bad for property values on Copperlane. The Gilded Meridian has burned two beast-speakers in its history, and both times the ledgers recorded the cost under *Losses, Ideological*, right between *Warehouse Fire, Minor* and *Wine Spoiled in Transit*. I know because I entered one of those entries myself, six years ago, in a hand so steady I have often wondered whose hand it actually was.

So no. They did not burn me.

They priced me.

* * *

It happened in the Counting Room at the top of the Meridian's spire, where the ceiling is painted with constellations and every one of them is a trademark. Nine masters sat behind the long table of black walnut, and Master Hollis in the center chair, his rings folded together like a man at prayer, if prayer had interest rates. Corvane stood to the left of the chair. Not seated. Standing, which for him was already a kind of verdict—I had never once seen him stand at a judgment unless he intended to be counted among the judges rather than the judged.

Sennet was below, in the strongroom, chained in iron that made his fur smoke faintly. They had taken my confession from me in fragments over four hours—Hollis asking the same question eleven ways, the way you'd test a scale—and now they deliberated while I stood on a worn square of floor that generations of accused had polished smooth.

"Let us review," Hollis said, and opened the folder before him the way you'd open a mouth. "Item one: Rosalind Quintana, keeper of ledgers twelve years, has concealed a forbidden faculty for a period exceeding—by her own admission—the whole of her employment. Item two: said faculty constitutes a capital offense under the Statute of Clean Speech. Item three: an agent of Ashfall, currently in our custody, crossed the border specifically to find her, citing a prophecy recorded in a guild document of the first order." He looked up. "Have I missed anything?"

"You've missed the part where I saved this guild roughly four hundred crowns in veterinary fees," I said, "and where the storm we keep in the cellar hasn't broken loose because I sing to it."

Someone down the table laughed—one breath of it, quickly strangled. Hollis did not smile.

"Item four," he said. "The accused is flippant."

"The accused is accurate."

"Accuracy," Hollis said, "is what we are here to establish." And then he turned to the matter that had actually convened us, the matter he had been circling for four hours like a hawk that knows the rabbit is tired: the Cinder Compact's letter.

I should explain the Compact, because my sins are all tangled up in theirs. The Gilded Meridian trades in impossible things; the Cinder Compact manufactures them—or rather, manufactures the *conditions* under which impossible things become cheap. They deal in fire-oil, in border-land, in the futures markets of catastrophe. And for years they had watched the covenant between Vantrell and Ashfall the way a vulture watches a sick animal: not with malice, exactly. With arithmetic. If the covenant died—if the beast-realm froze and broke open—the Compact could strip Ashfall bare. Timber that never rots. Ore veins that walk. Feathers worth their weight in wardens' bribes. All of it suddenly available, all of it suddenly cheap, and no treaty to answer to.

Their letter to us, delivered by a rider whose horse had the wrong number of eyes, was brief. It offered to purchase *the asset described herein*—meaning me—for a sum I will not repeat even now, except to say it had more zeroes than the Meridian's founding charter. In exchange, the Meridian would say nothing, see nothing, and the Compact would handle delivery to the border themselves.

"They want her dead or bound," said Mistress Aravelle, who held the shipping interests. "The letter says *transfer*. We all know what transfer means when the buyer is the Compact."

"We know nothing of the kind," said Master Denner, who was old and owned half the grain contracts and had therefore decided, sometime around the last famine, that he knew everything. "We know they pay. The rest is not our affair. It has never been the Meridian's practice to audit our buyers' intentions. We sell the knife; how it is used belongs to the user."

"How convenient," I said, "that the knife gets to have opinions."

"Silence," Hollis said, without heat, and I gave it to him, because there is a time to spend words and a time to count them, and I was counting.

The debate went on for the better part of an hour. I will compress it, because compression is my trade: they weighed burning me against selling me the way you weigh any two entries, and selling won before the second argument began. Burning cost money and bought only reputation. Selling cost nothing and bought the Compact's goodwill, a seat in whatever came after the covenant collapsed, and—Denner said this aloud, in the flat tone of a man reading a weather report—"the removal of a liability from our premises."

A liability. Twelve years of my handwriting in every book they owned. Twelve years of catching the errors that would have sunk them in three audits. Twelve years of soothing the cellar-beasts so the strongroom guards could sleep, though of course that entry appeared nowhere, because I had hidden it even from myself.

Then Hollis said, quietly, "The keeper's mentor will speak," and the room changed temperature.

Corvane came forward. Up close I could see what four hours of deliberation had done to him—he looked like a man who had aged a winter overnight, and I clung to that, God help me, I clung to it like rope. He had raised me since I was fourteen and caught stealing apples from the guild's drying shed. He had taught me double-entry and mercy and which masters to never turn your back on. Whatever he said next, I told myself, would be the thing he said *for* me, wrapped in the language of necessity, the way all his kindnesses were wrapped.

He did not look at me. That was the first warning.

"The Meridian exists," he said, "because it does not confuse sentiment with solvency."

The second warning was that he kept going.

"I found this girl thieving. I trained her. I am, if anyone asks, responsible for the fact that her crime went undetected for twelve years, and I will answer for that before any tribunal the guild cares to convene." His voice was level. Ledger-level. "But the question before this table is not whether Rosalind Quintana has committed a crime. She has. The question is what her crime is *worth*."

And there it was. There it was, laid out on the black walnut like a corpse for appraisal.

He voted yes. He said the word—*aye*—in the same voice he used for approving invoices, and I heard it land, and I heard something in me land with it, somewhere far below my ribs, in the cellar where I keep the things I cannot afford to carry upstairs.

I want to be fair to him. I have spent many nights being fair to him, because unfairness is a luxury and I was raised frugal. I believe—I have to believe—that in his head the vote was a rescue. Sell her to the Compact alive, and she stays alive; burn her, and she is ash on Copperlane. Between the two entries he chose the one with a living column. Perhaps he even meant to intervene later, to buy her back, some scheme within the scheme. Men like Corvane always have schemes within schemes, and I like to think mine was nested somewhere inside his like a coin in a winter coat.

But here is what the vote taught me, standing on that worn square of floor while nine masters and the one man I loved best in the world priced my continued existence: loyalty, in the Meridian, was never a bond. It was a ledger. Every kindness Corvane ever did me had a debit column I had never seen, and tonight the account came due, and the payment was me. He had not betrayed me out of cruelty. He had balanced me. There is a difference, and the difference is that cruelty at least notices you.

* * *

They kept me in the map room overnight—not a cell, because cells require paperwork, and paperwork requires recording what I was, and none of them wanted that written in a hand as legible as mine. Maps of Vantrell on the tables, maps of the borderlands, and one enormous chart of Ashfall with its interior left blank, marked only *UNCHARTED — COVENANT RESTRICTION*. I lay on two chairs and looked at that blank space for hours. Tomorrow the Compact would take me through it, or into it, or against it. The blank seemed less like ignorance and more like a held breath.

Near midnight, Corvane came in. Of course he did. He set a lantern on the table and stood looking at the same blank space I'd been studying, and for a long while neither of us said anything, because we had both been trained in the same discipline of unsaid things.

"You're angry," he said finally.

"I'm itemizing," I said. "Anger can come later. It's a large purchase."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Rosalind. Listen to me carefully, because I will say this once and then deny I ever said it. The Compact's rider leaves at dawn by the Coal Door. The tunnels under the coal stores run past the tannery and surface outside the walls." He set a key on the map table. Not near me. Near the lamp, equidistant, deniable. "The rats in the coal cellar are talkative. I've noticed you notice them."

My heart was doing something unledgered. "You voted to sell me."

"I voted to keep you off a pyre. The Compact's price buys us silence either way, and silence, girl, is a door that swings both directions." He turned to go, then stopped, his hand on the frame, not looking back. "Your gift was never the crime. The crime would have been spending it on people who would price it. I merely declined to be the one who profited twice."

And then he was gone, and I was alone with the maps and the lantern and a key that sat exactly where a guilty man would put it.

I should have felt rescued. What I felt instead was the cold, clean vertigo of a ledger closing: understanding, at last, that I had never been a daughter to the Meridian at all, only a well-maintained asset, and that Corvane's love—real, I still believe it was real—had simply never been permitted an entry of its own. Everything was booked elsewhere. Everything had a counterweight.

I took the key. I am not so pure that I didn't consider refusing it out of spite, but spite is expensive and I was raised frugal.

On my way down through the dark, past the counting floors, past the strongroom where Sennet's chains smoked in the night air, I paused at the great Prophecy Ledger where it lay locked in its glass case—and found the case unlocked, and beside today's date, beneath the line about *the ember anew*, fresh ink that had not been there that morning.

*The speaker flees by the coal-dark road. The mentor pays.*

I read it three times. Then I went to free the fox, because whatever else the future intended to charge me, I intended to choose the currency myself.

Behind me, up the spire, a bell began to ring—the alarm bell, the one that meant someone had found the map room empty or the strongroom guard asleep or both—and I ran.
