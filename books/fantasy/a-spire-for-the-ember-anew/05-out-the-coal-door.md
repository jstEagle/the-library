## 5. Out the Coal Door

The coal door at the bottom of the Gilded Meridian's cellar is the only honest thing in the building. It's iron, it's ugly, and it does exactly what it says: it opens onto the dark where the guild keeps its sins.

I'd walked past it a thousand times without seeing it. That was Corvane's doing, I suspect—he taught me to audit ledgers by teaching me to stop seeing what wasn't on the page. The coal door had never been on any page. Tonight it was the whole ledger.

"Left," said Sennet.

"There is no left," I whispered. "There's a wall of coal."

"Then the wall of coal is lying to you." He sat on his haunches in the shadow of the furnace, and even half-starved and limping, he managed to look offended by architecture. "The door swings inward. Push."

He was right, which was becoming an uncomfortable habit. The coal face swung on a hinge older than my grandmother, exhaling a breath of cold air that smelled of wet stone and old smoke. Behind it, stairs went down into the smuggling tunnels—the Meridian's second circulatory system, the one that moved things the ledgers never recorded. I had audited this guild for nine years. I had found the caged storm, the vial of borrowed luck, the deed to a bridge that existed only on foggy nights. And I had never once suspected that beneath my ledgers ran a vein of pure, unrecorded crime.

It was, I'll admit, a little flattering.

"Rats," Sennet said suddenly, ears swiveling toward the dark.

"I know." Because I did know—I'd known since we came down here, since the first whispering thread of squeak-and-scratch had resolved itself, horribly and wonderfully, into words. *Cold. Cold. Big feet coming. Many big feet. Down-down-down.* "Enforcers. Four of them, maybe five. They've reached the cellar stairs."

Sennet looked at me the way he always looked at me when I did the thing I wasn't supposed to be able to do—like I was a locked box he kept finding new seams in. "What are they saying?"

"That we're rats in a trap." I pushed through the coal door and pulled him after me. "They're not entirely wrong. Move."

* * *

The tunnels were built by smugglers, which means they were built by people who expected to be chased. Every fifty paces there was a branching, a low crawl, a ladder to nowhere designed to waste pursuit time. What the builders hadn't planned for was a fugitive who could hear the pursuit thinking.

*Left fork. They took the left fork,* chattered a rat from atop a barrel of salted eels. *Big boots. Smell like iron and bad intentions.*

"They're behind us, left fork," I translated, hauling myself over a collapsed beam. My palms were bleeding. I decided not to put that in any ledger either.

"You don't need to translate everything," Sennet said, flowing over the same beam with considerably more grace. "Just the parts where we die."

"Fine. Then mostly nothing."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter had been dragged through a hedge. We ran. The tunnel sloped down, then down further, and the air changed—colder, cleaner, tasting of snow instead of smoke. Somewhere above us, Vantrell was lighting its evening lamps against a winter that had already lasted too long, and somewhere beyond the walls, past the sprawl of frozen hovels and charcoal camps, Ashfall waited with its dying covenant and its starving gods.

And behind us, patient as arithmetic, came the enforcers of the guild that had voted to sell me.

I want to be clear about what that felt like, because I've had time to turn it over since, and it still doesn't sit flat. Nine years. Nine years of double-entry honesty, of staying late, of never asking why the storm in cage forty-one needed feeding at midnight. I had given the Meridian my competence, my discretion, my silence about my own nature—my silence being the largest entry of all, the one item I'd carried on both sides of the book. And when they'd learned what I was, the masters hadn't even paused over their wine. Sell her to the Compact. A fair price for a rare commodity. And Corvane—

Corvane had raised his hand.

I could forgive them their greed. Greed is just arithmetic wearing a nice coat; I understood it professionally. But Corvane had taught me my letters. Corvane had told me, when I was nineteen and terrified of my own ears, that some gifts were burdens and some burdens were gifts and the trick was knowing which before you burned. He had voted anyway. Loyalty, it turned out, was a ledger too, and I had finally read far enough down the column to find my own name under *debts receivable.*

*They're turning back,* said a rat, breathless, from a crack in the wall. *No—no, more coming. Different smell. Oil-smell. Fire-oil.*

I stopped so fast Sennet nearly ran up my back.

"What?" he said.

"The rats say there are more of them. Coming from the other direction. And they smell like fire-oil." I looked at him. He looked at me. Neither of us needed to be a beast-speaker to do that math. "The Compact isn't waiting for the sale to close. They're burning the tunnels."

"Burning the—" His ears flattened. "That would kill the enforcers too."

"Yes."

"And us."

"Preferably us."

For a moment we stood in the dark listening to our own breathing, two fugitives in a smuggler's artery while men with fire-oil closed from both ends. And I will tell you something strange: in that moment, wedged between betrayal and combustion, I felt calmer than I had all day. Terror, it turns out, has a shelf life. Past a certain point it curdles into something else—something with better posture. Something that starts counting exits.

"How far to the surface?" I asked.

"Half a mile, maybe. There's an old coal chute near the north sprawl."

"Then we have perhaps six minutes of tunnel left, and I intend to use four of them running." I started moving. "Talk to your cousins. Ask them where the fire isn't."

* * *

The rats earned their keep that night, though I doubt they knew it. They ran the walls ahead of us in a ragged gray relay, passing word hand to paw: *Fire coming, fire coming, oil-men at the south junction, run-run-run.* Twice they turned us back from passages that looked perfectly fine and were, by rodent report, about to become chimneys. Once we flattened into a drainage culvert while flames roared past the grate above us, close enough to crisp the hair on my forearms, and Sennet pressed himself against me—not for comfort, I think, but because he was smaller than the fire and I was smaller than he was, and fear makes mathematicians of us all.

The last stretch was a coal chute, exactly as promised: a rusted iron throat angling up toward a hatch crusted with ice. Sennet went first, claws scrabbling, and threw the hatch open onto night.

Snow fell in.

Not down. In—sideways, upward, spiraling into the tunnel mouth as if the sky outside had reversed its opinion of gravity. I climbed out after him and stood in the frozen sprawl beyond Vantrell's walls, among the charcoal camps and leaning hovels of people too poor for the city's gates, and I watched the impossible thing happen.

For a hundred yards in every direction, the snow fell normally enough—downward, wearily, the way snow falls everywhere this winter. But to the east, where the land rose toward the border of Ashfall, the flakes rose. They lifted off the drifts and climbed, streaming upward in slow silver ribbons, pouring from our world into the other like water finding its level. Where the two currents met—a visible seam in the air, faintly luminous, trembling like heat-haze in reverse—the snow simply ceased to exist.

"It's being pulled through," Sennet said quietly. Beside him. "Ashfall is drinking our winter. Or rather—the broken covenant can't hold the realms apart anymore, so they're bleeding into each other. Our cold flows to them. Their hunger flows to us. Eventually there won't be a border. There'll just be one dead place."

Behind us, orange light flickered up out of the coal chute. The fire had reached the end of the tunnel and found nothing to eat but our footprints. Someone shouted, far away and muffled. The enforcers, presumably, standing in the ruins of their own guild's secrets, deciding whether a burned tunnel was worth reporting and to whom.

I turned my back on it. On the glow, on the walls, on nine years of ledgers and the woman who'd written them. The sprawl stretched away toward the luminous seam, dotted with campfires no bigger than cupped hands, and beyond it the dark shoulder of Ashfall waited under its impossible falling-up snow.

My hands were shaking. I noticed them shaking the way you notice weather—as a fact about the world, not about me. Then I looked at them and gave them an instruction, and they mostly obeyed.

"Sennet," I said. "When we cross, what happens to me? Honestly. You owe me that much."

He was quiet long enough that a snowflake rose off the ground between us and hung there, undecided.

"The Ember Court sent me to find the speaker," he said at last. "The prophecy names a speaker, and you're the only one alive. That's the truth. The other truth is that courts are courts. Some of them will see a miracle. Some will see an omen, and omens get killed for the good of everyone." His amber eyes caught the light of the burning tunnel behind us. "I'm supposed to deliver you. I'd rather shield you. I haven't figured out how to do both yet."

"Well," I said, "you have until the border."

I started walking toward the seam in the air, toward the snow that fell upward, toward the realm that wanted my voice or my death and possibly couldn't tell the difference. Behind me the Meridian burned its sins. Ahead of me the night bent the wrong way.

Terror, I had learned tonight, curdles. Mine had finished setting into something harder.

Resolve.

The first flake crossed the boundary and rose past my face, going home, and I followed it.
