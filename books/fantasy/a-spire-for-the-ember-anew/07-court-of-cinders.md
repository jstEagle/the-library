## 7. Court of Cinders

The Ember Court did not have doors. It had a mouth.

Sennet led me through it—a throat of black basalt that opened, without warning, into a cavern the size of Vantrell's cathedral district. I had spent my life around valuable things, and my first instinct, God forgive me, was to appraise. The walls were veined with ember-glass that pulsed like slow heartbeats. Braziers the size of wagons burned with flames of every color but orange: green fire, white fire, a blue so deep it looked like drowning. And everywhere, everywhere, beasts.

A stag with antlers of smoldering iron stood nine feet at the shoulder and watched me with eyes like banked coals. Serpents wore crowns of ash. Something vast and feathered hung from the ceiling like a chandelier that had once been worshipped. I had spent years whispering to rats in cellars, and now every creature in this room was looking at me, and every one of them was *listening*.

"They know what you are," Sennet murmured. "Word travels faster than feet down here."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to prepare you." He smoothed his coat—still wearing that absurd guild-cut waistcoat, ash-grey fur gone silver at the temples, looking for all the world like a clerk who'd wandered into a war. "The Court will be courteous. Courts always are. Courtesy is what they use instead of honesty."

"And you? Will you be courteous or honest?"

He looked at me then, and there was something raw under all that fox-cleverness. "With you? I keep trying to figure out which one is which."

* * *

They seated me on a throne.

Not for me—for the *idea* of me. A slab of obsidian at the base of the dais, below the Ember Queen's seat of fused glass and bone, positioned precisely so that every eye in the court could take my measure. I recognized the architecture of it immediately. It was the same trick the Meridian played with its negotiation tables: put the guest slightly lower than everyone else, and half the bargaining is done before anyone speaks.

I had audited enough contracts to know when I was being priced.

The Ember Queen was not what I expected, which is to say she was worse. She was small—a vixen no larger than Sennet, though age had turned her fur the color of cooled iron—and she sat utterly still in a way that made the burning court seem frozen by comparison. When she finally spoke, her voice came from everywhere at once, from the braziers and the walls and the marrow of my own bones.

"Rosalind Quintana. Keeper of ledgers. Speaker of the low tongues and the high." A pause, delicate as a blade being unwrapped. "And a criminal, in your own country. Tell me—is it true they burn your kind?"

"It's true they've only ever caught the ones worth burning," I said.

Laughter moved through the court like wind through wheat—a rustle of feathers, a clicking of claws, a sound like distant avalanches. The Queen's whiskers twitched, which I would later learn was her version of a smile.

"Good. You'll need that tongue where you're going."

"Which is where, exactly?"

"Patience." She lifted one paw, and the court went silent so completely I could hear my own blood. "First, the terms. You crossed our border to renew a covenant. Before you agree to anything, you should know what you are agreeing to. We do not make the mistake your guild does—we do not let ignorance sign the contract."

She gestured, and an ancient tortoise—shell scarred with writing older than any script I knew—began to speak. His voice was dry as autumn leaves, and he recited the covenant's founding like a man reading his own obituary.

I listened the way I listen to everything: hungrily, cataloguing, translating law into arithmetic.

A hundred years ago, Vantrell had been drowning in its own excess—smoke, greed, expansion—and Ashfall had been drowning in its own nature: hunger, wildness, winters that ate whole valleys. Neither could survive the other unchecked. So they bound themselves. But a bond between realms cannot be sealed with ink or blood alone. It required a voice that both realms could hear. A voice that spoke beast and stood human. A willing voice, given freely, at the covenant-stone.

"There was a speaker then," the tortoise said. "A woman of Vantrell who fled her city rather than burn. She gave her voice to the stone, and the stone held the two worlds together like a stitch holds a wound."

"What happened to her?" I asked.

The silence that followed had weight. Even the chandelier-thing above us stopped swaying.

"She stayed," the Queen said simply. "That was the price. The seal must live within reach of the stone. The covenant is not a document, Keeper. It is a *custody*. Someone must be kept."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the winter outside.

* * *

They gave me an hour alone to consider—which is how I learned that even their hospitality was a cage, since the door of my chamber didn't open from the inside.

The room was beautiful. That was the first thing I noticed, and the second thing I hated about it. Walls of warm amber-stone. A bed heaped with furs that smelled of cedar and summer. A window—not barred, merely narrow—looking out over a valley where snow fell upward into the darkening sky, returning home to Ashfall's sky like prayers returning to a god.

On the table: bread still warm, fruit I didn't recognize, and a ledger.

Of course a ledger. They'd asked Sennet what I was, and Sennet, damn him, had told them true.

It was the covenant itself, transcribed in a hundred different hands across a century of amendments. I read it the way I read everything, and by candle's end I understood three things with terrible clarity.

First: the covenant was failing because the original seal—the woman, the first speaker—had been *fading*. Not dead; nothing so clean. The stone had been drawing on her for a hundred years, spending her the way the Meridian spends capital, and there was almost nothing left. The long winter wasn't an invasion. It was a *foreclosure*.

Second: the renewal required exactly what the founding had required. A living beast-speaker, standing at the stone, giving her voice freely. No proxy. No substitute. No clever clause.

Third—and this was the part I read four times, hoping I'd misread it—the renewal did not strictly require her *death*. The old speaker hadn't died at the stone. She had simply never left it.

I sat on the beautiful bed in the beautiful room and did the arithmetic I was born to do.

Option one: refuse. Walk back through the border, find another way, let the winter eat both realms while I searched for an alternative that the covenant's own text said did not exist. Cost: everything, eventually, including eventually.

Option two: accept the Court's offer—I could hear them drafting it already, could feel it waiting in the amber walls like a held breath. Sanctuary. Protection. Honor. My own chamber, my own title, my body preserved as the living seal of two realms. The elegant cage. They would keep me the way the Meridian keeps its treasures: carefully, lovingly, behind glass, forever.

I thought of the cellar beneath the Gilded Meridian. Of the beasts I'd whispered to through iron bars, soothing them, promising them nothing because I had nothing to give them except the sound of a voice that understood. I had spent my whole life on the visiting side of cages, pressing my hand against the bars, and I had told myself that made us kin.

It didn't. Kinship is what happens after the door closes and you learn whether it locks from either side.

There was a knock—or the Ashfall equivalent, which was Sennet scratching politely at the stone.

"Enter" felt strange to say in a room I couldn't leave, so I said, "It doesn't lock, you know. From either side. I checked."

He came in anyway, shaking snow off his ears, and looked at the ledger on the table, and then at me, and I watched him arrive at the same sum I had.

"You've read it," he said.

"I've read everything. It's my one talent besides the illegal one."

"Then you know what they're going to offer you."

"I know what they're going to offer me, and I know why it's generous, and I know why generosity from a dying court is the most expensive thing in either realm." I closed the ledger. "They don't want a seal, Sennet. They want a *hostage* the winter can't take. As long as a speaker sits at that stone, Ashfall survives. They don't need me willing forever. They just need me *present*."

Something flickered across his face—shame, maybe, or its close cousin.

"You knew," I said. "When you came through the coal door. You knew what the prophecy meant for whoever it found."

"I knew the prophecy needed a speaker." He met my eyes, and I'll grant him this: he didn't look away. "I didn't know you. There's a difference between delivering a person to a fate and delivering a person to a *door*. What's behind it—" his voice caught, actual caught, the first honest crack I'd heard in him, "—what's behind it was never mine to choose."

"No," I agreed. "But someone paid for the door. And someone built the room. And someone warmed the bread." I gestured at the table. "This isn't a sanctuary, Sennet. This is a purchase order with a velvet lining. I've written enough of them to know one when I'm sitting in it."

"And will you sign?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question.

Because here is the truth I haven't confessed yet, not even to this page: some part of me wanted to say yes. Some tired, frozen, hunted part of me that had spent thirty years hiding in cellars and auditing other people's fortunes wanted nothing more than a warm room where the hiding was over. Sanctuary. Safety. A cage with my name engraved on the bars instead of a pyre with my name shouted over the flames. After a lifetime of being contraband, being *treasured* sounded almost like peace.

I could have been kept. I could have been the most cherished prisoner in two realms, honored until the honor became the wall, until one day I woke up and couldn't remember whether the door had ever been real.

That was the temptation. Not power. Not survival. *Rest.*

"Sennet," I said slowly, "when you found me in the guildhall, and the enforcers were coming, and Corvane bought my silence—you begged me to listen. Why you? Of all the creatures in Ashfall, why did they send a fox who argues like a merchant?"

He was quiet for a moment. "Because I argued with the Court before I left. Because I told them a seal that's kept isn't a seal—it's a hostage, and hostages break covenants, they don't hold them. Because I believed—" he stopped, started again, "—I believed the prophecy meant a voice given *freely*, and you can't give freely what you've been locked in a room to protect."

"So you disagree with your own queen."

"I disagree with everyone. It's my second-best talent." A ghost of his old grin. "What's yours?"

I looked at the window, where the snow fell upward, patient as a debt.

"Reading the fine print," I said. "And the fine print says the covenant needs a willing voice. Not a kept one. Not a purchased one. If I stay in this room—if I let them make me their living seal before the Compact arrives—then whatever I say at that stone won't count. It'll be a voice under duress dressed up as a gift, and the covenant will fail anyway, and I'll have traded my freedom for nothing but the privilege of watching both realms freeze from the best seat available."

Understanding moved through him like dawn through a valley. "You're going to refuse them."

"I'm going to *negotiate* with them." I stood, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the coal door finally loosened—not into calm, but into purpose, which is colder and more useful. "There's a difference. Refusal is what prey does. Negotiation is what a keeper of ledgers does. They offered me a cage. I'm going to offer them a contract—one with terms they haven't thought to ask for, because they've spent a century thinking like captors instead of accountants."

"Which is?"

"That a seal walks to the stone. That a voice is given, not stored. And that if either realm wants this covenant renewed—" I picked up the ledger and tucked it under my arm, because I was raised by thieves and it felt right, "—then the first thing that has to change is that nobody keeps me. Not the Court. Not the Compact. Not the Meridian. Not even the prophecy."

Sennet stared at me, and then he laughed—a short, startled bark of a sound, entirely undignified for a courtier.

"The Queen is going to eat you alive."

"The Queen is going to *try*," I said, and walked to the door that didn't lock, and opened it myself.

Behind us, far away toward the border, the night lit orange—not ember-orange, not hearth-orange, but the ugly orange of fire-oil burning on snow.

The Compact had come early.
