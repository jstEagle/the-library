## 8. The Compact Marches

I smelled the fire-oil three ridges before I saw the men.

That is not a boast. Ashfall's wind carries everything—pine rot, frozen blood, the sour milk-smell of the Ember Court's grudge-keepers—and it carried that particular reek of rendered fat and spite like a bell rung ahead of an execution. I had spent my whole life knowing what humans wanted by their smell. Ambition smells of iron. Fear smells of vinegar. The Cinder Compact smelled of both, boiled together in a kettle of profit.

"They're coming," I told Rosalind.

She stood on the ridge beside me with her breath pluming and her hands buried in her sleeves, because the Court had given her a cloak of ember-woven wool that was either too hot or too cold depending on which elder had cursed it last. She had been in Ashfall two days and had already learned to stand like one of us: weight low, eyes moving, waiting for the world to show its teeth.

"I know," she said. "The rats on the border told me."

"The rats on the border," I said, "are liars."

"They're also terrified, which makes them honest." She squinted south, where the snow fell upward along the covenant-line, flakes rising off the human side of the world like sparks returning to a fire that had gone out. Beyond that shimmering boundary, small and dark as ants poured from a kicked nest, came the Compact's column. Wagons of fire-oil. Hired blades in mismatched armor. And something else, something that made my hackles rise and my tail go still—the smell of paper. Old paper. A great deal of it.

"They mean to burn the Court," Rosalind said quietly. "Kill the elders while they're weak, let the realm freeze through, then come back in spring and strip what's left. That's what the rats say. Strip it bare. Timber, pelts, ore. A dead land is a cheap land."

"And you?" I asked, though I dreaded the answer. "What do you mean to do?"

She looked at me with that expression humans make when they have already decided something terrible and are merely arranging the words around it. "What I was always going to do. Speak."

"You don't understand what you'll be speaking into. My court kills omens, Rosalind. A hundred years ago they drowned a beast-speaker for less than you're planning."

"A hundred years ago they needed one," she said. "That's the arithmetic nobody wants to read aloud."

I wanted to argue. I want it noted, wherever such things are recorded, that Sennet fox-of-the-ash-marches argued, at length, with eloquence, and lost—not to her logic but to the set of her jaw, which is a force no beast has ever outmaneuvered. So instead I did the only useful thing left. I went down to the border to count the enemy, and to decide, once and for all, whether I would deliver her to the Court as the prophecy demanded or stand between her and them if it came to teeth.

I was still deciding when I saw him.

An old man, walking at the rear of the Compact's column without a weapon, carrying a book against his chest like a man carries an infant through a burning house. His coat was guild-fine and his gait was broken, favoring a leg that some enforcer had probably taught him a lesson with. Even at that distance, even across the wrongness of falling-up snow, I knew him. Rosalind had described him too well: the mentor. The betrayer. The vote.

"Corvane," I said, to no one. And then, because the wind was kind for once, I ran to tell her.

* * *

They gave me the rear of the column, which suited me fine. Rear positions are where penitents belong, and also where old men can fall behind without embarrassing anyone.

My name is Corvane, and for thirty-one years I kept the Gilded Meridian's accounts honest, which is to say I kept them legible, which is to say I learned every honest way to write down a dishonest thing. I taught a girl to do the same. She was better at it than I ever was, and kinder, and gifted in a way that should have gotten her burned a dozen times over, and when the masters voted to sell her to the Cinder Compact I raised my hand with theirs because the alternative was to raise it alone, and I have never once in my life managed to be alone.

I have thought about that hand a great deal these past weeks. It occurs to me now that a ledger is only a mirror you hold up to money. Mine showed me exactly what I was: an entry in the debit column of my own life, marked *paid*.

The Compact believed I had come to guide them. I had sold them that story with the fluency of a man selling anything—routes through the tunnels, the Court's dispositions, the girl herself. Master Halvard of the Compact walked beside me the first day and called me *sensible*. On the second day he called me *old friend*, which in trade is a threat dressed as a compliment. By the third day he stopped addressing me at all, because I had begun asking questions about what precisely was meant to happen to the beasts after the burning, and questions are bad for morale among hired blades.

In my pack, wrapped in oilcloth against the cold, I carried the Prophecy Ledger. I stole it from the Meridian vault the night before we marched—a theft that will hang me if I live, which I did not expect to. One hundred years of entries, and then silence, and then, beginning eleven days ago, fresh ink in a hand no scribe claimed: *the speaker comes north. the stone remembers her voice.*

And beneath today's date, which the book had written before any of us lived to see it: *every debt arrives.*

I am an accountant. I know a collection notice when I read one.

We reached the covenant-line at dusk. The snow fell upward there, rising off the ground in long silver threads, and the hired blades refused to cross until Halvard himself rode his horse through the shimmer and came out the other side unchanged except, I thought, slightly smaller, the way men look smaller in a country that has known larger things. Then they crossed, grumbling, fire-oil sloshing in its wagons, and the wrongness of Ashfall closed around us like water around a sinking stone.

The trees here were black and enormous. Things moved between them at the edge of sight—things with too many legs or too few, things whose eyes caught the torchlight and returned it slowly, thoughtfully, the way a lender returns a look. One of the hired blades began to pray. Another laughed at him. Both were wrong.

"Corvane." Halvard reined in beside me, breath steaming. "When we reach the Court, you'll point out the elders. All of them. Especially the big one they call the Hearthwarden."

"I'll point out nothing," I said. "I've brought you this far. That was our bargain."

Halvard smiled the smile of a man reviewing a contract for loopholes. "Our bargain," he said, "was your knowledge. And I find I want all of it." He nodded to two of his blades, who fell in behind me with the loose-limbed patience of men being paid by the day. "You'll walk at the front from here on, old friend. Penitents make excellent shields. Everyone hesitates to cut down a man who looks sorry."

I looked sorry. I had never in my life been sorrier. But sorry was not the thing I had come to be.

I tightened my grip on the oilcloth bundle and kept walking, one step ahead of the knives, toward a court of burning things, carrying a book that had already written down how this night would end—and finding, as an accountant must, that I could not bring myself to check the final line.

* * *

The Court heard the news the way a hearth hears a door opening: all the flames leaning toward it at once.

I stood on the basalt floor beneath the Ember Court's vaulted dark, Sennet at my shoulder, watching the Hearthwarden—a beast like a bear that had been sculpted out of a bonfire and then taught grief—lift his vast head as the scouts came pouring in.

"Fire-oil," the scout said. She was a marten with frostbitten ears and the flat voice of someone reporting numbers she didn't like. "Forty wagons, maybe fifty. Blades beyond counting. They're making for the covenant-stone first. If they crack it before dawn—" She didn't finish. Nobody finishes sentences about cracked covenant-stones. You just watch everyone imagine the winter getting worse forever, and the silence does the rest.

The Court erupted. Elders shouting in three registers of growl; young beasts baying for war; the ash-priests chanting the old protections that everyone knew had thinned to cobwebs. Through it all the Hearthwarden sat motionless, embers guttering slow along his shoulders, until at last his eyes found mine across the floor.

"The prophecy names a speaker," he rumbled. The whole hall went quiet enough that I could hear my own heartbeat, which struck me as rude. "It does not name a human. Our law says the gift is abomination. Our law says burn." He leaned forward, and heat rolled off him in waves that made my eyes sting. "So tell me, keeper of ledgers' daughter, why I should not follow our law tonight, with enemies at the door?"

Because the truth was a hanging offense in one country and a burning offense in the other, and I had run halfway across a dying world to say it anyway. Because safety had always been a debt coming due, and the collector was at the gate.

"Because your law was written by people afraid of voices like mine," I said, "and the covenant wasn't. It was sealed with a willing speaker. You told me so yourself." I stepped forward, out from behind Sennet, into the open floor where every eye—beast and ember and ancient—could see my hands shake. "So here is my voice, freely offered, before witnesses. I speak with beasts. I always have. It is the only true thing I own."

The hall detonated. Somewhere above, an elder shrieked *abomination* in a voice like tearing slate. Sennet pressed close against my leg, warm as a coal, saying nothing, which from him was the loudest loyalty in the room.

And then the far wall of the cavern lit orange—not ember-orange, not hearth-orange, but the ugly orange of human fire, of fire-oil thrown and blazing—and the sound reached us a heartbeat later: the covenant-stone, out on the border plain, ringing under hammers and axes like a cracked bell.

The Hearthwarden rose to his full height, fire streaming off him, and looked down at me.

"It seems," he said, "your debts have arrived early."

Through the smoke and the shouting, through the press of panicking bodies, I heard one voice cut clean above the rest—an old man's voice, cracked and carrying, crying out from the direction of the flames:

*"Rosalind! Rosalind Quintana—I have the ledger, and I have come to pay!"*

Sennet went still against my leg.

"That's him," he breathed. "That's the one who voted."

"Yes," I said, and found, to my astonishment, that my fear had finally made room for something else. "Stay close to me anyway."

The Court broke apart into motion—war-beasts streaming toward the breach, priests screaming the old words, fire spreading along the eastern galleries—and I ran toward the light of human torches, toward the voice of the man who had sold me, toward a stone that was learning how to break.

Every debt arrives, the ledger had written. It had not lied.

It had simply neglected to mention they'd all arrive at once.
