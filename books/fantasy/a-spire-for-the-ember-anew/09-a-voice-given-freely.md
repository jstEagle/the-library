## 9. A Voice Given Freely

The covenant-stone stood at the exact place where Vantrell's maps stopped pretending and Ashfall's began—a black obelisk taller than the guildhall's spire, breathing out heat in slow, shuddering pulses, like a dying man determined to finish a sentence. Frost climbed it to the height of my shoulder and lost its nerve there. Everything above that line glowed a dull ember-red, and everything below was white.

I had audited stranger things. I had once catalogued a jar containing somebody's Tuesday. But I had never audited anything that felt like this: a stone keeping score of a hundred years of promises, and running a deficit.

"They're coming," Sennet said. His ears were flat, his coat singed from the crossing, one eye swollen half-shut from the Compact's first volley of fire-oil behind us. He could smell them, he said. Men who burn things smell like tallow and certainty.

"How long?"

"Long enough for you to be brave or dead. Ideally the order is brave, then not dead."

"You're a terrible envoy."

"I'm not an envoy yet. Neither are you." He looked up at me with his one good eye, and there was something in it I'd only ever seen in mirrors on my worst days—the face of someone deciding how much of themselves they can afford to spend. "Rosalind. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"That's not what foxes say. Foxes say *run*. I'm asking you to ignore three million years of good advice."

I put my hand between his ears, which would have gotten me burned in any market square in Vantrell, and which felt like the most honest gesture I had made in thirty-one years of ledgers and lies.

Then I stepped before the stone, alone, and did the thing that had been a hanging offense since before my grandmother's grandmother: I opened my mouth to speak *with* the beasts, in front of witnesses.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about confession. It is not a weight lifting. It is a door opening, and doors let in weather.

I laid my palm against the warm flank of the stone and raised my voice—not the ledger-keeper's murmur I'd used for a lifetime, but the voice I used in the cellar at midnight, talking rats down from panic and soothing the caged storm when thunder made it weep.

"My name is Rosalind Quintana. I keep books for the Gilded Meridian, and I have committed one crime, continuously, since I was six years old. I speak with beasts. I have always spoken with beasts. They talk to me the way you talk to each other, and I answer, and I have hidden it every day of my life because hiding it kept me alive."

The snow fell upward past the stone, into Ashfall's darkening sky, and every creature on the border—Sennet's scattered kin, the great antlered shadows at the treeline, the rats I'd bribed with cheese rind all the way out of the city—went still and listened.

"The covenant was sealed with a speaker's willing voice," I said. "So I am told by a book that writes itself, a fox who owes me nothing and came anyway, and a court of embers. I don't know if I'm what the prophecy wanted. I know arithmetic, and I know that both realms are overdrawn. So here is my voice. Willingly. On the record. Witnessed."

And then I spoke the renewal—not in human words alone, because the stone wouldn't take those; I had tried them already, twice, and the stone had pulsed colder. I spoke the old terms as Sennet whispered them into my ear in the tongue of Ashfall, the language I'd been speaking in secret since childhood, and my voice cracked on the third clause and I said it anyway:

*Warmth for warmth. Voice for voice. What one realm wastes, the other mends; what one realm hoards, the other spends. Until the last ember forgets its name.*

The stone drank the words. For a moment—nothing. The frost crept another inch up my boot.

* * *

That is when the Cinder Compact arrived, because the world has a sense of timing like a creditor's.

They came over the ridge with fire-oil lanterns and hired blades, forty of them, and their captain—a broad woman with a scar through her lip named Hesse Marrow, whom I recognized from guild gossip as the sort of person who charges extra for witnesses—spurred her horse down the slope toward me.

"There she is," Marrow called back to her line. "The Meridian's little abacus. The Compact pays double for the speaker dead than the court pays for her alive, so do the sums, girl."

I did the sums. They were bad ones.

She came off her horse at a run, blade already swinging, and I had time to think, absurdly, of the vial of borrowed luck in the vault, entry four hundred and eleven, *never redeemed*—and then Corvane hit her sideways.

My mentor. My betrayer. He had come over the ridge among them carrying the Prophecy Ledger under one arm like a hymnal, and I had watched him descend and not known what he meant by it, penitent or profiteer. Now I knew. He took Captain Marrow's blade across the shoulder rather than let it take my throat, wrapped his arms around her waist, and drove her into the snow, and he held her there—he held her—with the whole of his failing strength while the blade found him again, and again.

"Corvane!"

"Keep *talking*," he roared at me, blood in his teeth, and it was the same voice he'd used in the cellar for fifteen years, teaching me double-entry and mercy and which masters to never turn your back on. "You always talked too much, girl. Finally it's *useful*."

And Sennet was there too—I saw it all in pieces, the way you see a fire—leaping between me and two Compact blades, taking a cut meant for my ribs across his flank, another meant for my neck along his jaw, going down and getting up, going down and getting up, snarling words no human present could understand but every beast on that border could, and the border understood.

Because here is what happened next, the part I will tell until I die: the ground heard me.

The covenant-stone flared like struck flint. The frost didn't retreat—it *ran*, streaming off the stone in rivers of sudden water, and past it the wrongness broke. The upward-falling snow reversed midair. Heat rolled out from the stone in a slow tide, and every beast of Ashfall answered it—the great antlered shapes coming out of the treeline not to hunt but to *stand*, between the Compact's line and the stone, a wall of fang and winter-starved fury that had finally, finally been given permission to hope.

Marrow wrenched free of Corvane, saw the wall coming, and did the arithmetic herself. She whistled her line back. Mercenaries know a changed market when they stand in one.

Corvane did not get up.

I reached him in three steps that I will remember forever and got down in the slush beside him, and he was smiling, which was worse than anything.

"Don't," he said. "Don't make the face. I voted to sell you. I want it noted that I've revised the entry."

"You absolute bastard," I said, holding his hand anyway. "You could have just apologized."

"This *is* me apologizing. I've always been an expensive man." His eyes went past me, to the stone, blazing now to its crown, ember-light spilling down over us like something forgiven. "There it is. A hundred years I kept books for people who thought everything was for sale." His breath rattled. "Some things appreciate, girl. You just have to... hold."

He held. And then he didn't.

* * *

The covenant caught the way a hearth catches—one moment kindling, doubt, smoke—and then light ran around the stone's full height like a ring of fire finding its footing, and the long winter cracked.

I felt it pass through me. Not pain. Settlement. A hundred years of debt, paid and countersigned, the balance struck clean, and my name in the margin of the oldest ledger in the world: *witnessed, willing, sealed*.

Sennet limped to me bleeding from three places and leaned his whole weight against my leg, which he would deny later.

"You lived," he said.

"Barely."

"Good. Barely is very traditional for us."

Behind us the Ember Court came down out of Ashfall in their burning regalia, and ahead of us the Compact fled, and somewhere in the middle distance Vantrell's bells began to ring—first confusion, then alarm, then, unmistakably, joy, because thaw after a century-long winter sounds like joy even through bronze.

I stayed kneeling by Corvane until the cold stopped being cruel, and then I closed his ledger-keeper's hands over the Prophecy Ledger he'd carried all this way to give back, and I stood up.

"Rosalind," Sennet said quietly. "The Court is waiting. And they're looking at you the way courts look at useful things."

I wiped my face, took stock—two wounds on a fox, one grave in the snow, one covenant singing at my back, and forty hired blades scattering south with news that would reach the Meridian by morning.

"Let them wait," I said, and turned to face the fire.
