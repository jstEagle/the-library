## 10. Ember Anew

The thaw came the way debts come due—slowly, then all at once.

I woke on the morning after the covenant-stone caught fire to the sound of water dripping off the eaves of the Ember Court, a sound so ordinary it felt like an obscenity. For months Vantrell had lived under a sky like a clenched fist, and now the fist was opening, finger by finger, and nobody quite knew where to put their hands.

Sennet found me on the court's eastern terrace, where the snow had gone grey and slumped overnight, surrendering in patches. He walked with a limp that hadn't been there a week ago, and he carried two cups of something steaming and amber, because apparently even fox-things from a dying realm learn hospitality when the alternative is watching a human shiver.

"You're staring at the border," he said.

"I'm auditing it." I took a cup. It tasted like honey cut with smoke, which is Ashfall's answer to everything. "Snow's retreated four feet since yesterday. The boundary line used to sit right about—" I pointed at a blackened stake, one of the Compact's survey markers, half-swallowed by drift. "There. Now it's over the rise."

"Four feet of world coming back," Sennet said. "And you count it like entries in a ledger."

"It's how I pray."

He laughed—a bark-and-rasp sound, more honest than most human laughter I'd heard in ten years at the Gilded Meridian. Below us, Ashfall stretched out waking: elk-herds moving through melting valleys, the great horned shapes of the court's wardens standing down from their posts along the frost-line, steam rising off stone that hadn't seen sun since before either of us was born. Somewhere in those hills, beasts who had spent a winter turning on each other were remembering they were kin.

It had cost Corvane his life to buy that remembering. And Sennet most of the fur along his left flank. And me, well. We'd get to me.

* * *

Vantrell's guild justice moved faster than I expected, though I suppose scandal accelerates all machinery. The Cinder Compact's captain—the one whose blade Corvane caught with his body, buying me three seconds that turned out to be enough—was tried in the Meridian's great hall, the same hall where I'd once inventoried a caged storm and a vial of borrowed luck and thought myself safe behind my columns of figures.

They made me testify. Of course they did. The woman who confessed her forbidden gift before two realms was the only witness both sides would agree was credible, which tells you something about credibility: it's mostly just damage people can see.

I stood in the witness box and gave my account plainly, because plainness was the whole point now. I described the fire-oil wagons. I described the hired blades with Meridian sigils filed off their buckles but not out of their muscle memory. I described the Compact's masters sitting in this very hall three winters ago, agreeing over brandy that a frozen Ashfall was a harvestable Ashfall, and that a beast-speaker, should one turn up, was inventory rather than person.

That last part got a reaction. Guilds hate being reminded of their own arithmetic when someone else reads it aloud.

The Compact's leadership took the fall with varying degrees of grace. Master Aldery wept, which fooled no one. Master Quill argued jurisdiction until the magistrates ran out of patience. And Master Vess—who had signed the order to sell me to them, priced and crated like the caged storm—looked at me across the hall with an expression I recognized intimately, because I'd worn it myself for years in that building.

It was the look of a person doing sums and disliking the total.

Corvane wasn't there to see it. They buried him outside Vantrell's walls, facing north, which the priests said was irregular and which everyone else understood perfectly. I put one of his ledger-quills in the ground with him. He'd have hated the sentimentality and kept the quill anyway.

* * *

The Gilded Meridian came for me eleven days later, which by guild standards was practically groveling.

They sent not enforcers but the Masters themselves—all six remaining, in their chain-of-office finery, standing in the courtyard of the Ember Court's embassy house like a delegation of crows who'd decided to become diplomats. Master Vess did the talking, because Vess always did the talking; it was cheaper than doing anything true.

"Rosalind." She said my name carefully, the way you set down something that might be valuable or might be venomous. "The guild wishes to express its—"

"Don't," I suggested mildly. "You'll strain something."

Her jaw tightened. Around her, the other Masters performed various small studies of the architecture. "The guild," she tried again, "is prepared to offer restitution. Whatever post you name. Warden of Ledgers. A seat at council within the year. Your record expunged, your gift—your *talent*—recognized and licensed under Meridian protection. You would never want again."

I let the silence sit. It's a technique I learned from Corvane, who could make silence do the work of a cross-examination.

Here's what I knew, standing there in the thaw-smell of Ashfall's edge: the Meridian gutted by scandal was offering me anything, and *anything*, from a guild, is always priced. Every gift has an entry in the debit column. I had spent ten years keeping those ledgers. I knew exactly what line item I'd be: the beast-speaker, licensed, owned, trotted out when useful, burned when inconvenient, protected only so long as protection profited.

Loyalty, I'd learned, was a ledger too. And mine had been balanced against me for years without my ever seeing the books.

"Master Vess," I said, "do you know what I did, the night the Prophecy Ledger started writing again?"

"...Audited it," she said, warily.

"I read it. All the way back to the beginning. Do you know what the first entry says?" I hadn't told anyone this. It seemed like the hour for it. "*'Let the realms keep honest accounts between them, and neither shall devour the other.'*" I smiled, and watched her understand. "A hundred years ago, the covenant was founded as a bookkeeping arrangement. Two realms, mutual obligations, penalties for default. The Meridian trades in impossible things, Master Vess. But the oldest impossible thing on any market is trust, and your guild defaulted on it the moment you priced me like cargo."

"The offer stands," she said stiffly.

"I know. That's what makes refusing it so satisfying."

I didn't say it cruelly. Mostly.

* * *

What I named instead surprised them, and if I'm honest, surprised me a little too—not because I hadn't been turning the idea over, but because saying it aloud made it real in the way ink makes things real.

"I'll take a post," I said. "But not yours. Both realms are naming envoys under the renewed covenant. First ones ever. Sworn to the border, not to any guild or court. I'm taking the human seat."

Vess blinked. "An envoy. To *Ashfall*."

"To Ashfall, for Ashfall, accountable to the covenant itself. Someone has to keep the honest accounts. Apparently I'm qualified—I've been keeping impossible ledgers since I was nineteen, and I've recently developed a talent for telling the truth in rooms that want me dead."

"And your counterpart?"

"Already chosen." I nodded toward the terrace steps, where a russet shape limped into view with the deliberate dignity of someone who has been waiting to be summoned and refuses to admit it. "Master Vess, meet Envoy Sennet of Ashfall. Sennet, the Meridian's finest negotiators. Mind the one in gold—she'll promise you the moon and bill you for the tides."

Sennet sat, curled his tail over his wounded flank, and regarded the assembled Masters of the Gilded Meridian with one eye and then the other, the way foxes audit things.

"They smell afraid," he observed.

"They usually do," I said. "It's how they stay solvent."

Vess's face went through several interesting transactions. In the end she bowed—shallowly, but she bowed—and the Meridian withdrew to lick its wounds and rewrite its image, and I stood on Ashfall soil having refused everything and been given, in exchange, exactly what I'd lost everything to get.

* * *

We walked the border that evening, Sennet and I, from the blackened survey stake to the old covenant-stone, which still bore the scorch-marks of its relighting and, if you pressed your palm flat against it, a faint warmth like a banked hearth. The snow was retreating ahead of us as we walked, or perhaps walking with us; it was hard to tell anymore where the covenant ended and the world began, which I suspect was the entire point of the thing.

My old life fit in one satchel. That was the bitter joke of it. Ten years at the Gilded Meridian, and all I'd kept was three ledgers, Corvane's spare spectacles, and a letter of reference I'd written myself because I'd known, even then, that no one else would ever write one honestly.

"You're doing the counting-face," Sennet said.

"I'm taking stock."

"What's the total?"

I considered. A mentor dead in the snow with a sword through him, holding the line so a confession could finish being spoken. A guild that had raised me and sold me in the same breath. A crime I'd hidden for twenty years, confessed, and survived—and found that survival was the strange part, not the burning. A friend with a healed-over wound who complained about human cooking with such conviction you'd think he'd actually eaten any.

"A debt," I said finally. "Everything I have now, I owe to what it cost. That's not balance. That's carrying forward."

Sennet considered this with his head tipped, ears at work. "Ashfall has a word for that. Roughly. It translates as 'the ember that remembers being a tree.'"

"That's either beautiful or morbid."

"Yes," he agreed.

At the crest of the rise we stopped. Below us, Vantrell glittered in its valley, chimney-smoke going straight up for the first time in months, and beyond it the thaw ran south like good news traveling. On our side, an Ashfall warden—something vast and antlered and patient—lifted its head to watch us pass, and dipped it, the smallest degree. Acknowledgment. An entry in a new book.

I unslung the satchel, took out a fresh ledger, and wrote the first line by the last of the light:

*Day one. Border holds. Thaw advancing. Accounts open.*

Sennet read it upside down, being a fox. "That's not much of a prophecy."

"No," I said, and closed the cover, and turned toward the long road and whatever was written on it that no ledger had predicted yet. "Better. It's a beginning."

The wind came up from the south, warm for the first time in a hundred years, and we walked into it unafraid—which is, when you get right down to the arithmetic, the whole of what truth ever buys you. It doesn't make you safe. It just makes the fear unnecessary.

Somewhere behind us, the covenant-stone breathed. Somewhere ahead, the world was keeping its promises again.

So was I.
