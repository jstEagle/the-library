## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The forge had been cold for six years, but I still swept it every Founding Festival eve. Not because it needed sweeping. Because my mother had done it, and because in Emberhollow we kept the dead alive by doing their small chores for them.

I was on my knees under the quenching trough with a rag and a bucket of ash-water when the floorboard gave.

Not broke—gave. A soft click, like a knuckle cracking, and the board tilted up an inch at one end, revealing a seam of darkness beneath. I sat back on my heels and stared at it. I had scrubbed this floor a hundred times. I had slept in this forge as a child while my mother worked late, curled up on the wool blanket she kept folded over the anvil's stand. I knew every plank of it the way you know your own hands.

The board had never moved before.

"Someone's been here," I said out loud, just to hear a voice say something ordinary. The valley hummed its festival-eve sounds back at me: lanterns being strung along the millrace, children shrieking through the orchard, old Tam Vetch tuning his fiddle badly two houses down. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a valley that had never once, in my twenty-three years, surprised me.

I lifted the board.

Beneath it was a cavity lined with oilcloth, and in the cavity lay a book.

It was bound in grey leather gone stiff as bark, closed with three wax seals pressed into the cover—one over each clasp and one across the spine, so that the book could not be opened without breaking all three. The wax was the color of old bone. Ash-colored. I'd seen that color before, on the ceremonial candles the elders burned at the Festival, the ones nobody was allowed to light themselves.

My mother had hidden this beneath her forge. My mother, who had taught me to read on seed packets and ballads, who had never owned more books than would fit in one crate. My mother, who had died telling me nothing worth hiding.

I should have called for Maren. Elder Maren came by every festival eve to check my lanterns, and she'd be here within the hour, and whatever was under the forge floor was clearly elder business, sealed business, the kind of thing that got handled by people with long robes and longer memories.

Instead I picked up the book, carried it into the light, and set it on my mother's workbench between the cold bellows and the rack of hammers she'd never let anyone touch.

Then I broke the first seal.

* * *

The wax cracked like ice on a spring pond. Underneath, the clasp was simple iron, and it opened without protest, which felt wrong somehow—I'd expected resistance, some ward or trick, and getting none made me feel like a thief who'd found the door already unlocked and no one home.

The second seal broke easier. The third barely cracked at all, crumbling to powder against my thumb, and I remember thinking, distantly, that the wax had been waiting to break. That it had been waiting a long time.

The pages were heavy stock, cream-colored, dense with handwriting in an ink so dark it looked wet. The script was cramped and angular, nothing like my mother's round hand. It read like a ledger crossed with a prayer:

*In the year of the Sealing, the last oath is sworn and the valley is closed. What enters shall not enter. What wakes shall not wake. We give up the world to keep the world.*

Page after page of it. Dates, mostly. Names struck through. And language I didn't understand—words like *wardline* and *oathfire* and *the Ember*, repeated so often they stopped looking like words and started looking like a wound the writer kept worrying with his pen.

I told myself it was history. Some founder's journal, buried for safekeeping, nothing to do with me. I turned pages faster, skimming, looking for the end, for anything that would let me close the book and go string lanterns like a normal person on the night before the Founding Festival.

Near the back, the handwriting changed. Looser now, hurried, the ink thinner, as if the writer were running out of both time and certainty.

*She will be born under the frost moon in the amber month. She will not know what she is. The valley will hold her until the valley cannot.*

Below that, a date. Two dates, actually, written one above the other in the same hand, the second slightly fresher, the ink less faded:

*Ambermonth 14th, the year of the Frost Moon.*
*Ambermonth 14th, the present year.*

I sat down on the forge stool. My legs decided this without consulting me.

Today was Ambermonth 14th. Tomorrow was the Festival—the two hundredth Founding Festival, if Tam Vetch's fiddle-song could be believed, though he added a decade every few years when he wanted applause. And the frost moon... I looked through the open forge door at the sky going violet over the western ridge, and there it was, rising early and pale and ringed in thin cloud like a coin at the bottom of a well. The frost moon. Which came, as everyone in Emberhollow knew, once a generation.

And the amber month. Ambermonth. My name was Yara Amberdale. My mother had named me that, she said, for the color of the river in autumn.

Except she hadn't, had she. Because the river ran brown-green nine months of the year and only went amber for three weeks, and mothers did not name daughters after rivers, and founders' journals did not write dates a century before they happened unless—

Unless it was a forgery. That was the thought I grabbed for, and I held it hard. Someone had planted this. Someone with too much time and a taste for theater, writing prophecies about frost moons and burying them under forges. Except the ink was genuinely faded, unevenly faded, aged the way ink ages over decades and not days, and forging that meant knowing things about chemistry that nobody in Emberhollow knew, including me, and I was the one who mended the village's pots.

Forgery required a forger. There was no one.

So: coincidence. A dead person's ramblings, and dates that happened to land near today, and a frost moon that happened to be rising, and a name that happened to rhyme. Coincidences stacked four deep stop being coincidences somewhere around the third one. I knew that the way you know a blade is dull—not by argument but by the feel of it in your hand.

I reached to turn the page.

That was when the ink moved.

* * *

It didn't move much. That's the thing I keep returning to, later, trying to make it smaller than it was. It wasn't a flood or a fire. It was a single line of text, the line reading *The valley will hold her until the valley cannot*, and as I watched, the last word began to darken. To deepen. As though a pen were pressing down on the page from the other side of the paper, from somewhere behind the world, adding weight to letters that had been finished for a hundred years.

*Cannot*, the word said, and then it kept saying it, the ink blooming outward like blood through cloth, until the whole phrase stood black and glistening on the page:

*The valley will hold her until the valley cannot hold her.*

Her. The word had been *it* before. I was almost sure it had been *it*. I read it again and again, hunting for the earlier version underneath the new one, the way you look for yesterday's face in today's mirror, and there was nothing. No trace. Just the sentence, complete and wet and certain, describing a woman the way you describe a barrel of grain—as cargo. As something held.

Something held *until*.

I put the book down. Then I picked it up again, because putting it down felt like agreeing to something. I carried it to the forge mouth where the light was best and the frost moon was fully up now, huge and white over the ridge, and I held the open page up to the moonlight like a child checking a coin for teeth.

The ink gleamed. Fresh. Wet. A hundred years old and still drying.

"You're wrong," I told it. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Whoever wrote you, whoever you're waiting for—it isn't me. I mend fences. I shoe horses. I've never left the valley. Nobody writes prophecies about people who shoe horses."

The book said nothing, because books don't. But the silence it said it in had a texture, and the texture was patience.

Somewhere down the lane, Tam Vetch's fiddle found its footing and the first reel of the festival-eve spilled out into the dark, and I heard my neighbors laughing, and I thought: they have been laughing inside a sentence. All of us. Every festival, every frost moon, every ordinary day of my invisible life—we have been living inside someone else's handwriting, and tonight the pen picked up again.

Because of me. The dates said so. The moon said so. The word *her*, still shining damply on the page, said so.

I thought of my mother then—not the forge-mother, the chore-and-ballad mother, but the other one, the one I caught sometimes in unguarded moments: standing at the valley's edge on the north road, staring up at the ridge line with an expression I had always filed away as grief and was only now, standing in her cold forge with a forbidden book in my hands, beginning to re-file as vigilance. She hadn't been mourning something. She'd been watching for it.

She had known. She had known what I was—or what someone else had decided I was—and she had spent her whole life standing between me and it, and she had died anyway, and the book had waited under her floorboards like a seed, patient as ink, patient as ash.

Lantern light bobbed up the lane. Maren, right on schedule, coming to check my festival lamps, wearing her elder's grey shawl and her elder's kind face. In an hour the whole valley would gather in the square and the elders would light the ash-candles and tell the founding story, the same story they told every year: how the first settlers fled a burning world and built a sanctuary where magic could never follow, and how the ward protected us, all of us, equally, forever.

I looked at the book. I looked at the candle Maren was carrying up the path, its flame the exact bone-color of the broken seals.

I wrapped the book in the oilcloth, slid it under my mattress, and smoothed the blanket flat. Then I went out to meet Maren on the step, and smiled, and lit my lanterns, and said everything was fine.

It was the first lie I ever told an elder of Emberhollow. It would not be the last.

Above the ridge, the frost moon climbed higher, and somewhere beneath the mountain, in a place I would not see for many days, something that had been waiting a hundred years felt the seal break—and began, very quietly, to move.
