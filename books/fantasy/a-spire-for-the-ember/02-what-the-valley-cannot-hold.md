## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

Yara didn't sleep. She sat at her mother's workbench with the prophecy book open under a single lamp, and every time she looked away from the page and looked back, the ink was exactly where it had been.

*The Ember wakes beneath the forge on the eve of the Founding Festival.*

Her birthday. Tonight's date. Written in a hand that had been dead for a hundred years.

There were explanations. Of course there were. Someone with a cruel sense of humor and access to old paper. A printer's error in some long-forgotten edition. Her mother's handwriting—no. She'd checked. She'd held the page to the lamp and turned it sideways and searched for the hesitation marks a forger leaves, the places where a pen lifts and doubts. There were none. The line flowed as if it had always been true, as if the ink had simply been waiting for her to read it.

By the time the sky went grey at the edges, she'd made a decision that felt less like courage and more like motion sickness: she would go to the valley's edge and see for herself what the ward looked like from the inside of a lie.

She told herself it was about the book. It wasn't entirely about the book.

* * *

Emberhollow's boundary was not a wall. That was the first thing they taught you in school, standing in a row while Elder Maren pointed at the ridgeline. *The valley protects itself,* she'd say. *The founders gave everything so we could live without fear.* No palisade, no gatehouse, no sentries. Just a feeling, like walking through a curtain of warm water when you crossed back in from the high passes—a softness in the ears, a faint sweetness in the air that the older folk called *the blessing* and never explained.

Yara had crossed the boundary maybe six times in her life, always with a trading party, always chaperoned, always with the strange flattening sensation that came over her beyond it—the sense that a room in her head she hadn't known existed had quietly been locked.

She took the shepherd's track north past the last terraces, past the shrine where festival lanterns were already stacked for tonight, and climbed until Emberhollow was a scatter of roofs below her and the ward-line ran somewhere ahead, marked only by a cairn of white stones that no one ever added to and no one ever took from.

The morning was cold and clean. Below, the valley glowed copper with early light. Above, the pass opened onto country she barely knew—grey scree, black pines, a horizon that seemed to lean away from her.

She stopped ten paces short of the cairn.

Here's what no one told you about the edge of the world you grew up in: it doesn't look like anything. The grass on one side of an invisible line is the same grass on the other. But Yara felt it anyway, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm—not outside her, but in her chest, in the hollow behind her sternum where her mother's voice used to live.

*Turn around,* said the sensible part of her, the part raised on Maren's lessons. *You're a smith's daughter with a festival to help run and a book full of someone else's madness.*

She took out the blade instead.

It was nothing. That was the point. She'd pulled it from the scrap bin behind the forge three days ago—a rusted thing, pitted black, its edge eaten to lace, the kind of steel her mother would have melted down without ceremony. She'd brought it because bringing it made the whole errand absurd enough to be deniable. If anyone asked, she was testing scrap for reforging. If anyone asked why she was testing scrap at the top of the world, alone, at dawn—

She knelt at the ward-line and laid the blade flat across the white stones.

Nothing happened. Wind moved the grass. Somewhere below, a dog barked at nothing.

And then the rust spoke.

Not aloud. Not in any voice her ears caught. It happened in that locked room in her head—the one that had been shut her whole life—the door swinging open with the silent ease of something oiled daily by hands she couldn't see. The blade's ruin poured into her: decades of rain, the salt-sour bite of neglect, iron remembering fire the way old men remember youth. And underneath all of it, thin as wire, a whisper that was not the blade's whisper at all but something *through* it, something on the far side of the ward pressing against the metal like a mouth against a window:

*—hear me—so faint—please—*

Yara snatched her hand back. The blade rang once against the stone, a clear sweet note it had no business making, and the locked room slammed shut so hard her teeth ached.

She sat down hard in the wet grass.

Her whole life, the teachers had said the same words in the same gentle voices: *Magic cannot enter Emberhollow. Whatever the world outside became, we are safe here. We are free here.* Free. She turned the word over and found it had two faces now, like the moon. Because magic couldn't get in—but neither, apparently, could whatever lived in her chest, the thing that had just heard a rusted sword speak across a century-old wall. The ward didn't keep the valley safe from power. It kept power from ever waking up *in* them. It was less a shield than a lid.

And lids, her mother had taught her at the forge, are things people put on things they're afraid will boil over.

"Yara."

She knew the voice before she turned. Everyone in Emberhollow knew Elder Maren's voice; it had pronounced half their names at naming-days and buried most of their grandparents. Maren stood twenty paces down the track with her walking stick planted and her shawl pinned against the wind, and her face wore the particular stillness of a woman who has been following someone for longer than she'd admit.

"Elder." Yara got up. Kept the blade behind her back, which was foolish, because Maren's eyes went straight to it the way water goes downhill.

"You're out early."

"Couldn't sleep. Festival nerves."

"Mm." Maren came closer, slowly, favoring her hip. Up close her eyes were pale and terrible and kind, all three at once. "And the scrap? Does it test well?"

"It's rusted through. Barely worth the charcoal."

"Then leave it," Maren said, "and come home. The ward-line is no place for morning walks."

"The ward-line." Yara heard her own voice go flat and strange. "We're allowed to say that word, then. I always wondered. At school it was always just *the ridge*, or *the blessing*. Like the valley didn't have an edge, it just... ended because it felt like it."

Maren's stick tapped once against a stone. Tap. Tap. The sound of a woman buying time.

"The founders named it the ward because they wanted us to feel guarded," she said finally. "A kindness of language. Nothing more."

"Then who built it?"

"The founders built it. You know this. They gave their lives—"

"I know the lesson." Yara stepped aside, deliberately, so that the cairn stood between them and the view of the pass. "I know it the way you know a song you've heard since you were four. *They gave everything so we could live without fear.* But nobody ever says *how*. Nobody ever says what they gave, or to whom, or—" she lifted the rusted blade between them, and Maren flinched, actually flinched, a small collapse around the eyes that Yara would remember for the rest of her life— "*why the edge of our paradise feels like a lock from the inside.*"

For a long moment the wind filled the silence. Then Maren said, quietly, "Where did you get that?"

"Mother's scrap bin."

"Your mother," Maren said, and stopped. Started again. "Your mother believed the valley was a refuge."

"Was it?"

"It was protection."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Maren said. "It isn't." And then, with the awful agility of a woman who had spent forty years deflecting questions from children and grieving widows alike: "Come down, Yara. Wash your face. Help hang the lanterns. Whatever fever this is—this reading of old books, this wandering—it will look different by lamplight, among your own people, with music playing."

"You didn't ask what book."

Something crossed Maren's face then, quick as a fish under ice. Not surprise. Recognition.

"I don't need to," she said. "I've known what book it was since the day your mother died. I've known since the day you were born." She turned, leaning hard on the stick, and began to walk down toward the valley, toward the smoke of the festival fires being lit early, much too early. "Come down, child. Please. While it's still only a book."

Yara stood at the edge of everything she had ever known, holding a dead sword that had whispered her name—or almost her name, or the name of something wearing her shape—and watched the oldest liar in Emberhollow descend into the copper light.

Below, in the orchard by the ford, she could see figures on the river road. Travelers. Strangers, moving fast, too fast for traders, and one of them stumbling as though the road itself were trying to throw him.

The ward hummed under her feet, thin as eggshell, and somewhere beyond it something had begun, very patiently, to knock.
