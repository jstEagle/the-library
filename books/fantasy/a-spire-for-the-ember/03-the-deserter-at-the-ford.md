## 3. The Deserter at the Ford

The river was low that morning, which is why I heard him before I saw him.

I'd gone down to the ford to think, because the ford was the only place in Emberhollow where thinking came easy. Water didn't care about wards. It ran in from the high country, ran out toward the lowlands, and never once seemed to notice that it passed through a valley where magic couldn't follow. I liked that about it. Everything else in Emberhollow stayed put. The water left.

I crouched at the bank with my boots off and my feet in the current, and that's when the birds went up from the alder stand on the far side—all of them at once, a black ragged sheet of them—and then a man came out of the trees at a dead run.

He wasn't running toward anything. He was running away from something, which is a different animal entirely. His coat was grey with road dust, his left sleeve hung in ribbons, and there was blood on his face in that dried-brown way that means it's been bleeding a long time. He hit the shallows without slowing, slipped on the stones, went down hard, got up, kept going. Halfway across he simply stopped working, like a clock someone had shaken too many times. He folded into the water with a sound I felt in my chest.

I don't know what made me go in after him. The valley teaches you not to. Strangers don't come to Emberhollow; that's not superstition, it's just true, like saying rain doesn't fall upward. In nineteen years I had seen maybe four outsiders, all traders who turned around at the pass because—Maren said—the roads beyond were bad. Nobody ever asked why the roads were always bad in the same direction.

But the book under my mother's forge was still burned into me. *The ember sleeps beneath the mountain's root.* And here was a stranger falling into my river like an answer being delivered.

I dragged him onto the shingle on our side, coughing and half-conscious, and rolled him flat. Up close he was younger than the scars suggested—thirty, maybe thirty-five—with a jaw like something broken and badly set, and a brand on the back of his right hand: a circle crossed by a line, seared deep enough that no years could fade it.

I knew that mark. Every child in Emberhollow knew it. It was stamped on the spine of every founding document in the elder's hall, inked in the margins of the festival hymnals, carved over the door of the granary. We were told it meant *harmony*. The mark of the valley's founding compact.

On this man's hand, it looked like a slave brand.

"Hey." I slapped his cheek, gently, then less gently. "Who's chasing you?"

His eyes opened. Grey, unfocused, then focused, and what I saw in them when they fixed on me stopped my hand mid-air. Not confusion. Recognition. The terrible, certain recognition of a man finding something he'd spent twenty years looking for.

"You have her chin," he said, and fainted.

* * *

I should have left him on the bank and run for Maren. That's what sense said. Instead I hauled him up the path to the forge—not inside, I wasn't ready for that yet—and propped him against the woodpile behind it, out of sight of the road, and sat across from him with my mother's skinning knife open on my knee while he slept off whatever was killing him.

It gave me three hours to look at him properly. The ribbons of sleeve weren't torn; they were *cut*, in long parallel lines, by something with edges. Under them his arm carried old scar tissue in patterns too regular to be accident—rows of short parallel strokes, like tally marks, running from wrist to elbow. Dozens. Hundreds. I counted forty-one before I made myself stop.

And on the inside of his left forearm, newer, angrier: a burn in the shape of a word. I leaned close enough to read it before I understood I shouldn't. One word, seared into the skin in a fine spidery script I recognized, God help me, from the pages under the forge.

*Ember.*

He woke at dusk. I knew because his breathing changed—went from unconscious to very carefully awake in one breath, the way soldiers wake, or animals. When I looked up from the knife, his eyes were already open and tracking me.

"If you scream," he rasped, "they'll hear you a valley away."

"They're not here."

"They're coming." He tried to sit up, failed, settled for turning his head to take stock—the forge, the smoke-shed, the darkening ridgeline. "Where are we? What's this place called?"

"Emberhollow."

Something moved through his face like weather. "Of course it is," he whispered. "She actually did it."

"Did what? Who?" I shifted forward. "You said something at the ford. You said I have her chin. Whose chin?"

He looked at me for a long time. I watched him decide things—one thing after another, each decision costing him visibly, like a man paying out rope he couldn't spare.

"Tamsin," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded. "My mother's name was Tamsin Galloway. She died when I was six. She was the village smith. She made hinges and horseshoes and she hummed when she worked and she never—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "She never once told me anything worth knowing, apparently. So you're going to tell me. Who chased you here, why does your arm say *Ember*, and how do you know her chin?"

The man closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet, which somehow frightened me more than anything else about him.

"My name is Kest," he said. "Twenty-two years ago, in a garrison chapel that doesn't exist anymore, I swore an oath standing beside your mother. There were nine of us. Swordspeakers. We bound our voices into steel so the blades would carry what we couldn't say aloud." He raised his branded hand, flexed the fingers like they hurt. "Your mother stood at my left. She held my wrist during the words, the way oath-partners do, so that if either of us faltered, the other would feel it."

"Swordspeech isn't real," I said automatically. It came out flat and strange. It's what I'd been taught, the way you're taught the names of clouds. *Old stories. Valley nonsense.* Except I'd stood at the ward-line two days ago and heard a rusted blade whisper my name, hadn't I. Except the ink under the forge had moved.

"No," Kest agreed quietly. "Not anymore. She saw to that." He touched the burn on his arm without looking at it. "The ones chasing me call themselves ash-priests. They were librarians once, keepers of the oaths, and when the swordspeakers broke their order apart, the priests kept the books instead of the blades. They've spent twenty years reading one particular prophecy and burning everyone who might make it come true—or everyone who might stop it. Your mother was both."

"The prophecy." My mouth had gone dry. "Under the forge. With my birthdate in it."

Kest's breath caught audibly. "You've read it."

"Some of it. The ink moves."

"It would." He struggled upright against the woodpile, and I let him, because suddenly I needed him vertical, needed him to be a person saying these things to my face. "Then I'll say it plainly, girl, because we may not get a plainer night than this one. The prophecy names an Ember—a child born of the last swordspeaker's line, in the last year the oaths held, who will either end the war-god's sleep or wake him screaming. The ash-priests have killed every child who fit even part of that description. Every one. For twenty years." His voice dropped. "Except you. Because your mother built you a cage and called it a valley, and hid you in the one place their books can't reach."

The world tilted slightly. I put my hand flat on the cold ground and waited for it to steady.

"That's not—" I started. "Emberhollow is a refuge. It was founded by settlers fleeing the border wars. Elder Maren says—"

"Maren." Kest laughed, and there was no humor in it at all, just exhaustion ground fine. "Is she still alive? Of course she is. Ask her who signed the founding compact. Ask her why the ward runs in a perfect ring, girl, and why nobody ever wanders in by accident, and why the roads are always bad in exactly one direction." He leaned toward me, and the brand on his hand caught the last light. "Refuges keep people out. What your mother helped build keeps everything *in*. Including you."

I wanted to hit him. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I wanted to hit him until the words rearranged themselves into something I could live inside. My whole life I had believed myself ordinary in a valley of ordinary people, safe in a valley built for safety, and this stranger with tally-marks cut into his arm was telling me in one evening that I was livestock—that the walls were a pen, and my mother had built them, and the men burning his arm had written my birthday down a century before I was born.

"How do I know any of this is true?" I whispered. "How do I know you're not one of them?"

He held out his branded hand, palm up, steady despite the shaking in it.

"Because they found me three days ago," he said, "and they didn't kill me. They marked me instead, and turned me loose as bait, because a deserter crawling home to his oath-partner's grave leads them straight to whatever she died protecting." His eyes were terribly calm. "I'm the lure, Yara. I've probably been the lure since the ford. Which means we have until they finish closing the ring—and if you're still standing in the valley they built to hold you when it closes, you will never leave it again. Neither choice. Neither life. Just the pen, forever, with Maren feeding you lies about bad roads."

Far off, up the eastern ridge, a light blinked once. Twice. A signal, patient and practiced, moving along the height of land like a slow thought.

Kest saw it too. He didn't flinch. He just watched it with the expression of a man checking a wound he already knew the depth of.

"They're early," he said. "They must want you more than I guessed." Then he looked back at me, and for the first time since the ford, he smiled—a thin, cracked, honest thing. "Your mother used to make that exact face. Right before she decided to do something unforgivable."

Above us, the first ash-priest's lantern crested the ridge.
