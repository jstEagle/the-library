## 4. Ash-Priests in the Orchard

They came at the hour when the orchard light goes gold and long, six riders in grey, moving at a walk, as if Emberhollow were a town they owned and had merely been away from for a while.

Yara saw them first from the forge roof, where she'd gone to think—or to hide; the two had become difficult to tell apart. Three days since Kest collapsed at the ford. Two since she'd hidden him in the old smoke-cellar beneath her mother's workroom, packed his wounds with moss and lies about how she felt about all of it. One since Elder Maren had looked at her across the council table with an expression Yara still hadn't found a name for—pity wearing duty's face—and said, *We should talk soon, child,* in the tone of someone postponing a confession they've rehearsed for decades.

Now the riders stopped at the orchard gate and did not enter. That was the first wrong thing. Armed men at a festival-eve gate should have been shouting, demanding, dismounting. These six simply sat their horses among the apple trees like grey stones, and waited, and the whole valley came out to look at them the way a body comes out to look at a storm building on the ridge.

Yara climbed down and walked into the crowd because hiding had stopped being possible somewhere around the moment the ink moved in the prophecy book.

Vessic dismounted last.

She was smaller than Yara expected—a narrow woman in ash-grey robes gone soft at the hems, silver hair cropped close, hands folded in front of her like a woman arriving at a neighbor's door with bread. No weapon that Yara could see. She stood in the trampled grass between the first rows of trees and let the crowd assemble itself around her, patient as weather.

"People of Emberhollow," she said, and her voice carried without effort, the way voices do in valleys built to hold sound. "I am Vessic, keeper of the Ash, reader of the Sealed Word. I come with no soldiers past your gate and no demands but one."

The crowd murmured. Old Tam Brackish spat into the dirt. Someone's toddler was shushed twice.

"You have lived behind your founders' ward for a hundred years," Vessic went on. "You have lived well. Your fields are kind to you. Your winters are mild. Your children are born without the gift that has burned up every generation before you." She paused, letting the silence do its work. "That peace is not free. It never was. It is paid for by something your elders keep from you, and that payment has come due."

Yara felt every eye in the valley begin its slow orbit toward her, and understood, with a lurch, that Vessic had not come to search for anything.

She had come to *name* it.

"There is a girl in this valley," Vessic said, "born on the night the stars made their crooked row. There is a word written in a book older than this town, and the word is *Ember*, and the ember has a face." Her gaze moved through the crowd—not searching, *delivering*—until it found Yara and settled there with something almost like gentleness. "Surrender her to us, and the ward holds. The valley keeps its walls. Your children keep their quiet blood. Refuse—" she spread her small hands, apologetic, "—and the ash comes over the ridge the way it did before your founders fled it, and no ward built by tired oathbreakers will stop what follows us."

Nobody moved.

That was the horror of it, Yara thought later. Not screaming, not torches. Just a hundred and forty people standing very still in an orchard, doing arithmetic.

* * *

They put it to the council within the hour, because Emberhollow was a town that believed problems could be voted on, and Yara sat outside the meetinghouse door because Maren wouldn't let her in—"for your own good," which Yara was beginning to understand was the phrase adults used when they meant *so we can discuss you more comfortably*.

Through the door she heard most of it anyway. Sound carries in valleys built to hold sound.

Tam Brackish: "—not saying I like it, I'm saying the ward's the only reason my grandchildren have never seen a burned field—"

Widow Corren, sharp and dry: "And you'll hand her off to *those* people on the strength of one speech? You saw them, Tam. Six of them. Where's the army? Where's the ash? It's a bluff dressed in scripture."

"Or it's an advance rider," Tam said, "and the rest are already on the ridge."

Then Maren's voice, low and level, and Yara pressed her ear against the wood until it ached. "I have watched that girl since the night she was born. I have watched her for reasons I will explain to this council when this stranger is gone, and I will say only this now: whatever we decide, we decide it knowing that what the ash-priests call surrender, the book calls *fulfillment*. Read your histories. Every hand that ever gave a swordspeaker to a priest thought it was buying peace. Ask them how that turned out."

A long silence. Then Corren again, quieter: "Elder. If what she says is true—if the ward fails either way—isn't it better to lose one than all?"

And there it was. Yara closed her eyes. One against all. The oldest arithmetic in the world, and she was the one being subtracted.

What hollowed her out wasn't the argument itself. It was that she loved these people. She'd shod Tam Brackish's mule every spring for six years. Widow Corren had taught her letters with a stick in the dirt behind the dye-house. She could walk the orchard rows and name who'd planted each tree, and half of them were dead now, and she'd grieved them, and now those same grieving neighbors were behind a door weighing her life against their comfort with the careful, reasonable tones of people discussing drainage.

She wasn't angry. That was the worst part. She kept waiting for anger and it wouldn't come. What came instead was a terrible clarity, cold and clean as a drawn blade: *this is what the prophecy wanted.* Not the priests. The book. A girl alone in a valley, unloved by circumstance if not by heart, so easy to give up—by others, or finally by herself—that walking to the spire would feel like mercy. Like relief. The ink didn't need to drag her anywhere. It just needed everyone around her to arrive, honestly and sadly, at the conclusion that she was the price.

Kest had told her, feverish in the smoke-cellar: *The oath doesn't need your hatred, girl. It needs your consent. Hate fights back. Tiredness walks.*

* * *

The council emerged at dusk without a decision, which in Emberhollow was a decision—the valley's way of hoping a problem would drown itself overnight. Maren found Yara on the meetinghouse steps, took her elbow, and steered her into the shadow of the bell tower, out of earshot of the dispersing crowd.

"Nothing will happen tonight," Maren said. "No one will touch you. I gave my word to the council and I give it to you."

"Your word," Yara said. "Like you gave your word to watch me?"

Maren flinched. Small, but Yara saw it, and filed it away next to the badly-told founding story and the way the elder's eyes had gone to the ridge line three times during Vessic's speech.

"I don't know what Kest has told you," Maren said carefully.

"That my mother broke an oath. That the valley is a cage. That I'm the thing in the cage the cage was built around."

"He should have kept his mouth shut and his wounds to himself." But there was no heat in it—only exhaustion, deep as a well. "Yara. Listen to me. Whatever you decide—and you *will* decide, I've known that since you were four years old and argued with me about the shape of the moon—do not go to Vessic. Do not negotiate. Do not offer yourself. The bargain she named is real, and it is also a lie, because the ward does not need an Ember surrendered to it. It needs an Ember *spent*. And there is more than one way to spend a person."

"What other way?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and Yara watched the old woman decide something, watch the decision cost her visibly, like pulling a splinter twenty years deep.

"Tomorrow," Maren said. "After the festival, when the valley's drunk enough to hear hard truths. Tonight—go home. Bar your door. And stay away from the smoke-cellar, because if Vessic's readers are half as good as I fear, they already know where he sleeps."

Yara went home. She barred the door. She sat in her mother's chair with the prophecy book open on her knees, because she had nowhere else to put her hands.

The page had changed again.

Where yesterday the ink had promised the Ember would be *given*—handed over, surrendered, delivered—it now read differently, the letters still faintly wet:

*On the eve of the Founding Festival, the valley will weigh her worth, and finding it heavy, will set her down.*

Set her down. Put her down. Like a tool after use. Like a burden too costly to carry.

Below that, in fresh ink, a new line was forming even as she watched, each letter surfacing through the paper like a drowned thing rising:

*And she will go willingly, for she will believe herself a mercy.*

Yara stared at it until the candle guttered, and the cold clarity from the meetinghouse door curdled into something hotter, something with edges. The book wasn't predicting her. It was *drafting* her—testing lines, discarding ones that didn't fit, revising her like a bad chapter.

Fine, then.

If it wanted to write her, she would give it something to revise.

She blew out the candle, and in the dark she began, very quietly, to plan how to be a disappointment.
