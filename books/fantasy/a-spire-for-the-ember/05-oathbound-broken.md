## 5. Oathbound, Broken

Kest had learned to sleep in fragments—twenty breaths at a time, one ear always open for the sound of ash on stone. So when the girl finally stopped asking questions and drifted off against the forge wall, he lay awake in the dark and let the memory come, because it always came, and fighting it only meant it arrived worse.

Twenty years ago tonight, give or take the turning of a season. The last night of the swordspeakers.

* * *

They had gathered at the Hollow Spire—all nine of them who still lived, which was to say all nine who still mattered—and the air above the mountain smelled like a struck flint that hadn't decided whether to spark. Sera Amberdale had stood at the center of the ring with her blade drawn and her daughter's bundle wrapped against her chest, and Kest remembered thinking, with the strange clarity that comes before catastrophe, that she looked like a woman holding a lantern out over a cliff.

"You've all felt it," Sera said. "The god is waking faster than we starved him. Another decade of this oath and there won't be enough of us left to keep the fast."

"There will be no fast," said Vessic—young then, still handsome in the way knives are handsome, his voice smooth as poured wax. "There will be a feeding. The Ember is born, Kest. You've seen her eyes when she cries. That is not a child's light. That is *his* light, waiting for a mouth."

Kest had stepped forward then. He'd sworn beside Sera at the first binding, back when they were both nineteen and certain that oaths were armor rather than rope. "Then we bind the child instead. We write her into the vow—her blood, our keeping. She grows up swordspeaker, and the ember serves us instead of him."

He could still feel the silence after he said it. Nine people not breathing.

Sera turned to him slowly, and there was something in her face he'd never seen before and would spend twenty years learning the name of: pity. Not for herself. For him.

"Kest," she said gently, "that's exactly what the book wants you to say."

* * *

The original oath was simple, the way all terrible things are simple. The swordspeakers had spoken their names into their blades—not the god's name, never his—and in exchange the blades drank the war's fire and gave back silence. Starve the god of voices, and the war starves with him. It had worked for two hundred years. It had cost them everything worth having: children born mute to steel, marriages dissolved by secrecy, whole lifetimes spent as jailers of a hunger none of them had ever chosen.

And now there was Yara. Born inside the oath's own shadow, carrying an ember bright enough that the prophecy book—one hundred years sealed, one hundred years silent—had begun writing again.

"We don't bind her," Sera told them that night. "We hide her."

"Where?" Vessic asked, and smiled. "There is nowhere magic cannot find an ember. You know this. The book knows this. The book wrote her into itself before she drew her first breath—"

"I know a place." Sera said it quietly, but the quiet carried. "A valley north of here. Dead ground. No ley runs through it, no blade sings in it. My grandmother called it Emberhollow and swore nothing grew there but forgetting."

"A ward," Vessic said. "You want to build a ward."

"I want to build a cage." Her voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was worse. "Around my daughter. Around the valley. Around every scrap of power that might reach her for as long as I can hold it together. And when she's grown, if the ember sleeps in her the way I pray it does, she lives her whole life as an ordinary woman in an ordinary valley, and the god goes on starving, and nobody's child burns."

"And the cost?" That was Aldric, old Aldric, whose sword arm had gone grey before his hair did. He asked it not cruelly but the way you ask a dying man if he's comfortable. "Sera. A ward that size doesn't run on good intentions. What does it eat?"

She looked down at the bundle in her arms for a long moment.

"It eats the oath," she said. "All of it. Every blade we've bound, every name we've spoken into steel. If I anchor the ward to our vows, it has fuel for fifty years. Maybe sixty. Long enough."

Kest understood before anyone else did. He watched it land in the others' faces one by one—the widening of the eyes, the involuntary step backward—and he heard himself say, "That kills the order. That ends the swordspeakers. There'll be nothing left to fight him if he wakes anyway."

"Yes," Sera said. "That's what it costs me. Do you want to know what it costs you?"

Nobody answered. She answered anyway.

"Everything. Because I can't build it alone, and the only hands strong enough to help me are yours, and helping me means breaking the very oath your names are written in. Shattering your own blade, Kest. Speaking your own unbinding. You'll be oathbreakers to every tradition we have. When the priests come—and they'll come, Vessic will see to it—they'll call us traitors, and they'll be right, and it will still be the correct thing to do."

Vessic laughed. Kest had heard men laugh in battle, in grief, in madness, and none of it sounded like that. "Or," Vessic said, "we do what the book says. The book has never been wrong, Sera. The Ember rises. The war reforges itself. All we control is whose hand holds the fire when it comes. Give her to the order. Let us raise her *right*."

"She's three weeks old."

"She's the end of the world wearing swaddling cloth."

Kest drew his sword.

Not at Vessic—at himself. At the oath. He remembered the weight of it, the way the steel seemed heavier than iron should be, heavy with two hundred years of whispered names. He remembered setting the point against the floor stones and speaking the unbinding words his own master had taught him for emergencies no one expected to survive, and he remembered the sound the blade made coming apart—not a crack, not a shatter, but a long low *exhale*, like something relieved to stop pretending.

Eight others followed him. Eight blades exhaled their oaths into the night. Sera caught each fragment of released power as it rose—caught it like rain in cupped hands—and fed it into a working so vast that the mountain itself seemed to lean away from her.

It took until dawn. It took everything.

* * *

What it cost him. People asked him that later, or tried to, before he stopped letting people get close enough to ask. They imagined the obvious things: the order broken, the brotherhood scattered, Vessic walking out of the spire with six followers and a promise of reckoning. Those were real losses. None of them were the cost.

The cost was smaller and sharper. The cost was this: Sera needed a courier. Someone to carry the baby north through country crawling with priests, someone the ash-cloaks wouldn't recognize because he'd already been declared traitor and had nothing left to lose. And when they reached the valley's edge, in that grey dead ground where even his sword-hand felt strangely empty, Sera had knelt in the mud with her daughter and wept without any sound at all, and then she'd handed Yara to a childless forge-widow named Brenna who owed the order a debt she'd never understand, and she'd kissed her daughter's forehead once, and she'd stood up, and she'd said:

"Kest. Swear me one more oath. Just one. Small one."

"Anything."

"Don't come back." She'd smiled while she said it, and it was the cruelest thing he'd ever seen anyone do. "Don't check on her. Don't watch from the ridgeline. Don't leave tokens, don't send word, don't stand outside her window at night like some story-book ghost. If the priests find one thread connecting her to the old world, they pull it, and the whole weave comes apart. She survives if and only if she is *nobody's*. Not the Ember's. Not the order's. Not even yours."

"That's not small."

"No," she agreed. "But it's one sentence, and you can hold it."

He held it for twenty years.

He wanted credit for that, sometimes, in the worst hours—he wanted someone to know what it had cost to walk away from a valley where a girl was growing up motherless and not once climb the ridge, not once buy a rumor from a peddler about a blacksmith's daughter with quick hands and her father's stubborn chin. Twenty years of holding a door shut with his whole body. And then, four days ago, standing in the ruins of a border shrine with Vessic's voice in his ear saying *the book has opened, the book has opened, the ember is found*—he had broken the only oath he'd managed to keep, and crossed the mountains, and started running toward the one person in the world he was forbidden to find.

Because the book had opened. Because if the prophecy was awake, then Sera's arithmetic was failing, and the ward was thinning, and somewhere in that valley a young woman was about to learn that her whole invisible life was a load-bearing lie.

Someone had to tell her the truth before the priests did. Someone had to tell her the part the priests would never say: that every terrible thing in her life had been bought and paid for by a woman who loved her enough to end the world's oldest order with her bare hands.

* * *

The forge was cold. In the corner, Yara slept badly, the way people sleep when their life has come apart in the last day—fists curled, brow furrowed, one hand near the hilt of her grandmother's rusted sword even unconscious, and Kest felt something twist in his chest at the sight, because that was Sera's sleeping posture exactly, learned or inherited he'd never know.

Tomorrow she'd ask more questions. Tomorrow he'd have to tell her the rest: that the woman who raised her wasn't random, that the debt Brenna paid had a shape, that the valley's kindly elders kept ledgers on every child born under that dead sky just in case one of them glowed. Tomorrow he'd watch her learn that her survival was purchased with a broken vow, and that the man telling her was the breaker.

But tonight—tonight there was a sound from outside. Soft. Rhythmic. Wrong.

Ash on stone. Many boots, walking in step, coming up the river road toward Emberhollow.

Kest sat up in the dark and reached for a sword that had been dust for twenty years, and listened to the future arrive.
