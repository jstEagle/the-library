## 6. The Book Rewrites Itself

Yara did not sleep.

She sat in the forge with the prophecy book open on her mother's anvil, a single lamp turned low, and she watched the ink. Kest had taken the cot by the door, his sword across his knees even in sleep, and Maren had gone back to the village with a face like wet clay, promising—promising—to keep the ash-priests talking until morning. Which meant Yara had one night alone with the thing that had decided her life before she was born.

She had tried to read it properly, the way you'd read any book. Front to back. It refused. The pages slid under her fingers like fish scales, opening always to the same spread: the founding of the valley, the oath of the first swordspeakers, and then—her name. *Yara Amberdale, born of the forge-line, on whom the ember settles.* Her birthdate. Today's date. And below that, in a hand that was not quite her mother's and not quite anyone's, a line that had been there when she first opened the book three days ago:

*The Ember will go up to the spire and speak the name.*

That was all. No name given. Just *the name*, sitting at the end of the sentence like a door left ajar.

Around midnight—or what she guessed was midnight; the valley kept its own hours and its own dark—the ink moved.

Not smudged. Not bleeding. It moved the way a worm moves under bark: a slow displacement, a purposeful shifting, and when it settled, the last line read:

*The Ember will go up to the spire and speak the name willingly.*

Yara's stomach dropped through the floor of herself.

"No," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in the forge, swallowed by the cold iron all around. "No, I haven't agreed to anything."

The ink shivered. Settled again.

*The Ember will go up to the spire and speak the name willingly, for the sake of her neighbors.*

"You're lying," Yara whispered. "You're a liar made out of ink."

But even as she said it, she felt the trap close around her ankle, soft as silt. Because she would do it for her neighbors. She already knew she would. Vessic had stood in the orchard that afternoon with his patient gray eyes and offered the valley a bargain, and she had watched Bram Tuller—Bram, who'd taught her to gut a fish, whose wife had mended every coat Yara had owned since she was six—look at his feet and say nothing. Say nothing. That silence had been worse than an answer.

If the book knew that—if it could write *for the sake of her neighbors* because it had counted the same silences she had—

Then it wasn't predicting her. It was reading her. And if it could read her, it could write her.

* * *

She tested it. That was the forge-girl in her; when a blade behaves strangely, you test it. You don't stand there admiring the strangeness.

"Here's what I'm going to do," she told the book, keeping her voice flat and steady, the way her mother had taught her to talk to hot metal. "Tomorrow I'm going to walk to the eastern ford and follow the river out of the valley. Away from the spire. Watch me."

She turned to the last page and waited.

Nothing. A full minute of nothing. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere in the walls, the old timbers ticked as they cooled.

Then, slowly, in that same not-quite-familiar hand, new words surfaced from the paper like drowned things rising:

*The Ember will turn back at the ford, for the roads beyond are watched, and the watched road is death.*

"I don't care if it's death," Yara said, and her voice cracked on the second word, which infuriated her. "I don't care."

*The Ember cares,* the ink wrote. *The Ember has always cared. That is why she was chosen.*

And there it was. The whole mechanism, laid bare as a gutted fish. It didn't need to force her anywhere. It only needed to know her better than she knew herself—which wasn't hard, since it had apparently been doing her knowing for twenty years while she thought she was living an unremarkable life in an unremarkable valley. Every choice she made, it had already written a reason she'd make it. Every reason was true. That was the horror of it. Not lies. Truths, arranged into a corridor so narrow that walking down it would feel exactly like freedom.

Her mother had died rather than let this happen. Had shattered an oath, had hidden her in the one place magic couldn't reach—and the book had simply waited, patient as rust, for the ward to thin enough to find her anyway.

Yara picked up the poker.

"Don't," said Kest, from the cot. He hadn't been asleep after all. He was watching her over the blade of his sword with those scarred, tired eyes. "It won't burn. We tried everything, twenty years ago. Your mother tried longer than anyone."

"It's writing me." She heard how young her voice sounded and hated it. "It's writing my life, Kest. It wrote *willingly*. It's going to make me want things and then call them mine."

Kest set the sword down and came to sit across the anvil from her, and for a moment he looked less like a deserter and more like what he claimed to be: a man who had loved her mother enough to break himself on the breaking of her oath. "Then don't give it true things to work with," he said.

"What?"

"A prophecy is a loom," he said. "It can only weave with thread that exists. Your mother figured that out too late—she gave it twenty years of true thread. Duty. Love of the valley. Love of her. All of it true, all of it usable." He tapped the anvil with one knuckle. "So become someone it hasn't read. Do something it can't explain."

"Like what?"

"I don't know. That's rather the point."

* * *

They worked at it until the small hours, and it was the strangest night of Yara's life, stranger even than the whispering sword at the valley's edge. Kest would suggest an act—a sudden departure, a destruction, a flight—and Yara would feel the truth of it slide into place, feel the corridor walls adjust around it, and know the book had already written the chapter. Stay: written. Flee: written. Fight: written. Even refusing the spire was written; there was a whole passage about the Ember who hides until the war finds her anyway, and it ended badly for everyone she loved, and the book knew she'd read it that way.

Every road out of herself had her footprints already printed on it.

Near dawn, hollow-eyed, she said, "My mother. Did she ever beat it? Even once?"

Kest was quiet a long time. "Once," he said finally. "The night you were born. Everything the book had written about that night—it had written your death, Yara. The ember passing to another line. And she—" His jaw worked. "She spoke your name into her blade before the book could finish the sentence. Named you herself. Out of order. Off the page." He looked at her. "The ink couldn't follow. For twenty years, it couldn't find you."

Yara looked down at the book, and the book seemed to look back, and she understood two things at once, the way you understand fire and burning in the same instant.

The book could be beaten. Her mother had proven it. But not by choosing differently within the story it had written—not by any cleverness inside the corridor. Only by stepping off the page entirely. By authoring something it hadn't read yet.

And the other thing: the book knew she now knew this. The ink was moving again.

She looked down. Fresh lines were surfacing, quick now, almost eager, and she read them aloud without meaning to, her voice going strange and distant in the cold forge:

*The Ember will learn what her mother learned. The Ember will try to escape the sentence. But the Ember is her mother's daughter, and her mother's daughter will do what mothers do—*

The line stopped. Incomplete. Hovering.

Waiting for her to finish it.

Yara stared at the unfinished sentence, her heart slamming, and felt the trap in its purest form: whatever she completed it with, the book would take. If she wrote defiance, defiance became part of the design. If she wrote surrender, surrender was sanctioned by her own hand. The blank space at the end of the line was not a gap. It was a mouth.

"Well," she said quietly. "No."

She took up the lamp and held the flame to the corner of the page.

The fire wouldn't catch—Kest had told her it wouldn't—but this time she wasn't trying to burn it. She was trying to interrupt it. And as the heat washed over the paper, the hovering ink trembled, and the sentence, denied its ending, began to dissolve backward into the fibers of the page, unwriting itself, and in the moment before it vanished Yara saw the next line already forming beneath it, hurried now, the first crack in the book's terrible patience:

*The Ember will come to the spire within three days, or the valley—*

Outside, far off toward the village, a bell began to ring. Not the festival bell. The alarm.

Kest was on his feet with his sword drawn. "Ash-priests," he said. "They're done bargaining."

Yara closed the book on its unfinished threat and picked up the rusted blade from beside the anvil—the one that whispered—and felt, for the first time, it whisper in words she recognized as her own.

Three days. Or the valley.

The book thought it was giving her a deadline.

It didn't yet understand that it had just taught her the shape of the cage—and that forge-girls, when they find the flaw in the metal, strike there.
