## 7. Maren's Confession

Maren's house smelled of dried lavender and old paper, the same as it had when Yara was small enough to be carried there with scraped knees. That sameness felt like a trick now. Everything in Emberhollow did.

The elder sat by her hearth, hands folded in her lap, and did not look up when Yara came through the door without knocking. Kest stayed outside, leaning against the doorframe where Maren could see him—a concession, not a kindness. Yara had insisted on that much. If this was an ambush, she wanted the ambushers to know she'd brought a sword.

"You read it," Maren said. Not a question. "The book under the forge."

"Every word." Yara didn't sit, though there was a chair waiting for her, angled toward the fire like it had been placed there years ago and never moved. Maybe it had been. Maybe everything had been. "Tell me about the founders."

Maren's hands tightened on each other. "You should sit."

"I've been sitting my whole life. In a valley I now understand is a cage. I'd rather stand."

That got the old woman to look up. Her eyes were wet, but steady—the eyes of someone who had rehearsed this confession for decades and dreaded every rehearsal. "Yes," Maren said. "A cage. You're not wrong, child. You're just wrong about who built it, and why."

"Then tell me who."

"The first swordspeakers." Maren rose, joints cracking, and crossed to a cabinet Yara had never seen opened. From inside she drew a bundle of oilcloth, unwrapped it on the table between them: a sword, plain-hilted, its blade gone grey as ash. "This belonged to Hessa Amberdale. Your mother's mother's mother. She was one of them—one of the nine who stood at the valley's mouth and spoke the ward into being."

Yara stared at the blade. It didn't whisper. Of course it didn't. Nothing whispered here; that was the entire point of the place. "Swordspeakers built the ward. The ward keeps out magic. Why would they—" And then it landed, cold and simple, the way true things always land. "They built it to keep magic *in*. Or rather—out of themselves."

"They were tired," Maren said quietly. "Nine of them, after thirty years of war fought through their own mouths. Do you know what swordspeech does to the speaker? Every name you speak into steel, you feel. Every death the blade deals, you carry. Hessa wrote that she could no longer sleep without hearing the war-god's name trying to climb out of her throat. So they found a dead place—a hollow where nothing answered—and they walled themselves inside it, and they starved the god of voices." She touched the grey blade almost tenderly. "It wasn't a refuge, Yara. It was a vow of silence. A prison they built around their own gift and then forgot they were prisoners, because forgetting was the only mercy left."

"And the prophecy book?"

"Kept by the wardens. One per generation. Passed down in secret." Maren met her eyes. "I am the ninth warden. My mother was the eighth. Hers the seventh, all the way back to Hessa herself."

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, festival lanterns were being lit, and the sound of children running past the window made Yara's chest ache in a way she couldn't afford.

"One child," Yara said. "Kest said one child per generation gets watched."

Maren flinched. "He would know. He was meant to be part of the watching, once."

"Then say it plainly. Say what you did to me."

"I watched you." No flinching this time. Maren straightened like a soldier accepting sentence. "From the day you were born. Your mother broke her oath to hide you here—she poured her gift into breaking the ward's hold on her own bloodline, and it nearly killed her, and it left a mark on you that we could not see but always feared. So I watched. I taught you your letters. I gave you bread when your mother worked late at the forge. I sat with you through the fever year. And every season, I checked the signs. Did the blades near you rust faster than they should. Did the forge-fire burn strange colors. Whether you dreamed in words you shouldn't know." Her voice frayed. "Twenty years, Yara. Twenty years of hoping the ember had died with the oath, and twenty years of being ready if it hadn't."

"You lied to me."

"I kept you alive."

"You let me grow up thinking I was *nothing*. Thinking my mother's stories about talking blades were grief-madness. You watched me mourn a gift I thought I'd invented, and you said nothing."

"Yes." Maren's voice cracked clean through. "And I would do it again, because the alternative was the ash-priests finding you at six years old and putting a blade in your hand before you knew it was a choice. Silence isn't only cruelty, child. Sometimes it's the last wall standing between someone and the thing that wants to use them."

Yara turned away. The lantern light through the window caught the frost on the glass, and she thought of the ward thinning at the valley's edge, the rusted blade whispering her mother's name, all the small cracks in a wall she'd been living inside without knowing it was a wall.

"My mother knew," she said finally. "She knew what I might be. And she stayed anyway. She forged swords for people who'd never hear them speak."

"She hoped love would be enough to smother it." Maren came to stand beside her, close enough that Yara could smell the lavender. "It nearly was. Then the book started writing again, three months ago, and I knew hope had run out. I sent word to no one. I burned my letters. I told myself I had until the festival." A bitter breath. "Vessic arrived two days early. Wardens are rarely granted such luck."

"Why confess now? Why not lie again? It's what you're good at."

The blow landed—Yara saw it land—and Maren took it without complaint. "Because Vessic will offer the valley a bargain tonight or tomorrow. Surrender the Ember, and the ward holds. Refuse, and he breaks it himself and takes you anyway. When that moment comes, I will stand before our neighbors and tell them the truth: that this valley was founded by cowards, that I am one of those cowards' keepers, and that no bargain struck with ash-priests has ever held longer than the priests found it convenient." She gripped Yara's shoulder, her hand light and trembling. "But whether they believe me will depend on whether you do. So I am asking you—not as your elder, not as your warden, but as the woman who taught you your letters—do you believe me?"

Yara looked at the grey sword on the table. At the chair angled toward the fire, kept ready for twenty years of visits. At the lavender, which her mother had grown too, from cuttings Maren must have given her—two women trading sweetness across a conspiracy.

Betrayal sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. But beneath it, another truth pressed upward: every kindness Maren had shown her had also been real. The bread had fed her. The letters had freed her. The fever-year vigils had saved her life. A jailer who loved her prisoner was still a jailer—but she was not only a jailer, and Yara was done pretending the world came in single colors.

"I believe you," Yara said. "And I hate that I believe you. Both things can be true. I'm learning that fast."

Maren exhaled, long and shaking. "Your mother said something like that once. About oaths. That two truths can share a mouth the way two names share a blade." She squeezed Yara's shoulder once and let go. "Then listen to the last of it, because there isn't much time. The ward isn't failing on its own. It's failing because you're near it—you're the ember, and embers wake what sleeps around them. Which means Vessic doesn't need to break anything. He only needs to keep you in the valley and wait. Every hour you stay, the walls come down faster. Every hour you stay, his prophecy comes true on its own."

"So I leave, and the valley stays safe."

"For a while. Until the next generation's child shows the signs, and the next warden takes up the watching, and the cage fills again." Maren's eyes were fierce now, wet and burning. "Or until someone ends it properly. Your mother tried to end it by hiding you. Hessa tried to end it by walling it up. Neither worked, because neither of them was willing to pay the whole price."

"What's the whole price?"

Maren looked at the grey sword, then at Yara, and Yara saw the answer arrive in the old woman's face before any words did—the answer Maren had carried for twenty years and prayed never to deliver.

"The ward doesn't open from the outside, child. It opens from within, and only by a speaker's voice. To unmake it, someone born under it has to stand at the valley's heart and call the magic home—every thread of it, all at once, into a world that has forgotten how to hold it. The founders wrote that cost into the founding itself." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Hessa's notes say the speaker who breaks the ward breaks with it. That's why they called it a spire, in the old tongue. A spire is what burns down to nothing so the fire can move on."

Outside, the festival bells began to ring—and then stopped, mid-peal, all of them at once, the silence ringing louder than the sound.

Down the lane, torchlight moved where no torchlight should be. Ash-priests, coming up the valley road, and behind them the sound of Vessic's voice, smooth as oil, calling for the elders of Emberhollow to come out and talk terms.

Maren closed her eyes. "They're early," she said. "Gods forgive us all—they're early, and the bargain starts now."
