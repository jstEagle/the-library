## 10. A Valley Without Walls

The morning after the world ended, Yara woke to birdsong.

That was the first strange thing. Emberhollow had never had so many birds—the ward had kept out more than magic. It had kept out weather that mattered, seasons that bit, the slow traffic of the wider world. Now the valley's rim stood open to the sky in a ragged crescent where the ward had been, and everything on the other side was pouring in: wind with teeth in it, light that came down at angles she'd never seen it come before, and sound—distant cart-wheels, a dog barking three ridges away, the enormous ordinary hum of a world that had been going on all this time without them.

She lay in her mother's old bed and listened to it and did not cry.

She'd done enough of that at the spire.

* * *

They found Vessic's ash-priests scattered along the mountain road like crows after a gunshot. Some had fled when the blade refused the war-god's name. Some had simply sat down where they stood, their whole lives' architecture collapsing around them, and Kest said to leave them be—"Men who've lost their god need a while to figure out what they are instead." So the valley fed them, because Emberhollow fed people; that much of itself it had kept. And by midsummer a few of them were hauling timber alongside everyone else, ash-gray robes traded for work shirts, looking perpetually surprised to be allowed.

The spire itself they left standing. Yara argued for pulling it down stone by stone, but Maren—old, half-lame now, walking with a stick she'd taken from the ruin of her own authority—shook her head.

"Leave it," she said. "A town that pretends its prison wasn't real will build another one without noticing."

So the spire stayed, hollowed out, its black books burned or carried down into the valley for anyone to read. Children climbed it in defiance of every adult who told them not to, which Yara privately thought was the healthiest thing that had happened there in a hundred years.

* * *

The rebuilding went slowly, which was its own kind of miracle.

For generations Emberhollow had built the way besieged people build: low, inward-facing, roofs sloped against nothing in particular, streets that curved back on themselves as if the valley might forget where its edges were if the town didn't remind it. Now the edges were gone, and the town didn't know how to stand in open country. The first new house—Bram Tullow's, raised on the south slope where his father had always said the soil was too exposed—faced outward toward the pass, windows wide, no wall on the valley side at all. Half the elders called it madness. The other half came to look at it, again and again, saying nothing, their faces doing something complicated.

Yara helped raise the beams herself. Her hands blistered. She found she liked the blisters—they were honest, they were hers, they had nothing to do with any book.

"You don't have to do this part," Kest said one afternoon, passing her up a roof-beam, sweat streaking the dust on his scarred face. "You're owed an easy year. Maybe ten."

"Owed by whom?"

He grinned. "Fair point."

He was staying. That had settled itself somewhere between the ford and the spire, without either of them announcing it, the way some things settle. He slept in the forge loft and argued with Maren about history and taught the Tullow children sword-forms that were really just games with sticks, and sometimes Yara caught him watching her across the yard with an expression she couldn't read—not grief exactly, not hope, but the place where grief has been worn smooth by years of handling. He had loved her mother. He had broken his oath to save her daughter. Some mornings he still looked at Yara like he was checking, quietly, whether the price had been worth paying.

She tried to give him good days to look at.

* * *

Magic, it turned out, was everywhere now, and belonged to no one.

It came up through the soil like water finding old channels. Bram Tullow's wife could coax the orchard into early blossom. A shepherd boy on the east ridge whistled and the wind answered him, embarrassed about it. Nothing like the old power—the war-god's hunger, the swordspeakers' terrible fluency—but small magics, domestic ones, unclaimed by any oath or order. When the ash-priests' remaining scholars came asking, carefully, whether these gifts should be "regulated," Yara told them what she'd learned at the top of the spire with the blade singing in her hand:

"Ask people what they choose. Not what they're chosen for."

The words got repeated around the valley until they sounded older than they were. She let them. An inheritance had to start somewhere, even one invented last Tuesday.

Her own gift had changed shape. Swordspeech remained, but quieter—a conversation rather than a command. The forge blades she made now seemed to want to know their owners, to fit a grip the way a glove fits, and folk came from beyond the pass to buy them, which meant coin, which meant the valley would eat next winter, which meant the future was a thing you could plan for instead of merely survive. Her mother had forged in silence for twenty years. Yara forged listening, and thought her mother would have understood the difference better than anyone.

Some nights she took her mother's oldest blade out past the last lamplight and spoke to it—not names, not oaths, just talk. *This is what happened today. This is what I'm afraid of. This is what I want.* The blade hummed back low and warm, holding the words the way a riverbed holds water: shaped by them, defined by none.

* * *

Maren died in the first snow.

Not dramatically. She'd earned the right not to. She went in her sleep in the house Bram Tullow built her facing the open pass, and they buried her on the south slope under a stone Yara cut herself, and on it, after long argument with herself, Yara carved only two lines:

*She watched over a prisoner.*
*She set her free.*

At the burial, Kest stood beside her and said nothing, which was the correct amount.

Afterward, sorting Maren's papers, Yara found a single sheet in the elder's cramped hand, dated the night of the Founding Festival—the night everything began. It read, in full:

*If the girl breaks the ward, I will have failed in my charge and succeeded in my purpose, and I no longer know which of those matters more. Let the record show I chose to stop choosing for her.*

Yara read it four times. Then she folded it away, and went outside, and looked up at a sky with no walls in front of it, and let herself feel the whole weight and strangeness of being someone's answer to a question they'd spent their life afraid to ask.

* * *

Autumn came hard that first year—the valley's first real autumn, frost silvering the orchards, the mountains wearing their crowns of white with no ward to soften them—and Emberhollow shivered and adapted and complained magnificently, which Yara counted as signs of life. The Founding Festival was not celebrated. Instead, on the anniversary of the night she'd found the prophecy beneath the forge, the town held something new: no name for it yet, just fires lit on the slopes where the ward used to stand, one fire for each family, so that from the ridge the valley looked like a constellation that had decided, after a century of hiding, to introduce itself.

Yara stood on the ridge that night with Kest, looking down at all those small defiant lights.

"They'll write books about this," he said. "Bad ones."

"Probably."

"Some of them will say you were chosen."

"I wasn't." She said it easily now, the way you say your own name. "I decided. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and if they get it wrong in the books I'll haunt whoever writes them."

Kest laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, rare enough that she stored it away like fuel.

Below them, a child's voice rose from the nearest fire, singing one of the old festival songs with the words changed, badly, hilariously, to fit the new story. The song would spread. Songs always did. Someday children would sing it who had never seen a ward or a spire or an ash-priest's gray robe, and they would think the valley had always been open to the sky, and they would be wrong, and it wouldn't matter, because what they'd inherit wasn't the history—it was the choice, renewed each generation, to keep the walls down.

Yara breathed the cold clean air and felt the ember in her chest—not the prophecy's ember, not the war-god's spark, just herself, warm and ordinary and entirely her own—banked low and burning steady, a fire kept for warmth instead of conquest.

Home, she thought. Not defended. Remade. Still becoming.

She turned toward the path down, toward the fires, toward the people waiting—

and stopped.

Because far off, beyond the pass, at the very edge of hearing, something vast turned over in its sleep. The war-god, unbound, dreaming on without an oath to feed it. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Yara felt—faintly, distantly, unmistakably—something else stirring too. Not hunger. Not war.

A question. Rising through the soil of the world like water finding old channels, moving toward every living thing that could hear it, and arriving, at last, at her:

*And what will you do with a magic that belongs to no one?*

Yara smiled into the dark, and began, at last, to answer.

*From the recovered pages of the Emberhollow prophecy-book, final entry, undated:*

*Here ends the book that wrote itself.*

*Let it be recorded plainly, since plainness was never this book's habit: the Ember was never a weapon. The Ember was a door, and doors do not choose which side they open onto. The girl opened hers onto morning.*

*The god sleeps unbound. The magic walks free, owned by no blood, bound by no name but the names freely given. We who wrote these pages in fear are forgiven nothing and forgotten everything, and this is right.*

*If any hand finds this book, burn it or keep it as suits you. It will write no more. It has learned what the girl already knew:*

*that a story belongs to the one who chooses it,*

*and a valley without walls*

*is not an ending.*
