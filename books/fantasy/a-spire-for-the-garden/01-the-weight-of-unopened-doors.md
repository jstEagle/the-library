## 1. The Weight of Unopened Doors

There are forty-one ways to be invisible in the Meridian Guild, and I have practiced all of them.

The first is posture. Walk like a man carrying nothing worth stealing—shoulders down, eyes on the middle distance, hands loose. The second is timing: never be first through a door or last through a gate, because those are the moments people remember faces. The third is the one nobody teaches you, because nobody has to: want to be unseen so badly that the world obliges out of sheer politeness.

I have been practicing that third way for nine years, and if you asked me—and no one does—I'd tell you it suits me fine.

It was the third Thursday of the month, which meant inventory, which meant the Gatehouse was mine until dawn. The Guild's other buildings had names with ambition in them: the Counting House, the Vault of Whispers, the Long Gallery where they kept the bottled years at a temperature just below forgiveness. The Gatehouse had no ambition at all. It sat at the eastern edge of the guildyard like a servant who'd stopped being noticed, all slate roof and shuttered windows and one door at its heart that hadn't opened in a hundred years and would not open for me, or you, or the Guildmaster herself, or God, presumably, if He came asking politely.

I unlocked the side entrance—the front door being ceremonial and stuck—and lit the lamp over the workbench. The flame came up green for a heartbeat before settling yellow, as it always did. Everything in this building was slightly haunted by something too polite to name itself.

"Right," I said to the room, because nine years alone in a building teaches you to narrate. "Let's see what impossible nonsense we're pretending to look after tonight."

The inventory ledger lay open on the bench, its spine cracked soft from handling, pages the color of weak tea. I dipped my pen and began.

Item one: *Six bottles, echoes, assorted.* The echoes lived on the second shelf, each bottle stoppered with wax and labeled in a hand long dead. I held one up to the lamplight—a sound of Vessany's rain, according to the tag, though nobody alive knew what Vessany's rain sounded like, which was either the joke or the tragedy, depending on your mood. The rain inside the bottle moved when you turned it. Not like water moves. Like weather remembering itself.

Item two: *One year, borrowed, unclaimed.* It sat in a lead-lined box the size of a hymnal, humming faintly, the way a cat hums when it's pretending to sleep. Someone had borrowed a year off their life sometime in the last century and never come back to return it or reclaim the original, and now it was mine to dust. That's the Meridian Guild for you. We trade in what can't be weighed, and then somebody has to keep the shelves.

That somebody was me. Juniper Brightwater, Keeper of the Gatehouse, keeper also of the ledgers, the oil, the candles, the keys that opened everything except the one door that mattered. Nine years. If the Guild were a body, I'd be the part of it that keeps the blood moving while everyone else gets to be the face.

I don't mind. I want that written somewhere, even if the only place is my own head: *I don't mind.*

The trouble with writing things in your own head is that heads are notorious liars' workshops.

* * *

The knock came at half past eleven—not at the door but at the window, three taps, arrhythmic, familiar as my own pulse. Tam's face appeared upside-down above the glass, framed by the frost creeping up the pane, his breath fogging the corner he'd rubbed clear.

"You're supposed to use the door," I said, letting him in anyway. "We have one. Several."

"The door's boring." Tam Wren was seventeen, all elbows and enthusiasm and hair that looked like it had been styled by a startled owl. He was the guild's apprentice ledger-keeper, which made him technically mine, though I'd have argued the point under oath. He shrugged off a coat too thin for the season and held up a cloth-wrapped bundle smelling of bread and woodsmoke. "Orla sent provisions. She says you skip supper when you do inventory, and she says it like an accusation."

"Orla says everything like an accusation. It's how she shows love."

"She also said—" Tam paused, savoring it, "—and I quote, 'Tell that stick insect of a clerk that if I find him eating raw oats over the sink again I'll season him myself.'"

"That woman threatens me more often than she feeds me, and yet here's bread."

"Here's bread," Tam agreed solemnly, setting it on the bench between the bottled echo and the borrowed year, which struck me as exactly the kind of company bread shouldn't keep, but the Guild had never been much for proper storage.

He settled onto the stool across from me, uninvited, unwelcome, and entirely necessary. This was the arrangement we had arrived at without ever discussing it: I performed solitude, and Tam ignored the performance so thoroughly that it collapsed into companionship every third Thursday whether I liked it or not.

I liked it. I'd deny it in court.

"What are we cataloguing tonight?" he asked, already reaching for a shelf.

"Careful—" Too late. He'd picked up the echo of the rain and tilted it, and the sound inside shifted, a low hush like weather crossing a country that no longer existed. We both went quiet. Even Tam, who treated silence as a personal enemy, went quiet.

"What does it sound like to you?" he asked finally.

"Rain."

"It sounds like more than rain."

"Everything in this building sounds like more than what it is," I said. "That's the whole business model. Put it back before it puts ideas in you."

He put it back, but carefully, the way you set down a sleeping animal. Then he helped me work, and I let him, because two pairs of hands make inventory less like a vigil and more like a job.

* * *

By one in the morning we had counted: fourteen bottles of echo; three names not yet earned (they sit in velvet boxes and whisper to each other when the lamps are low—I've heard them, and I've decided not to think about what they say); one borrowed year; a jar of someone's courage, donated voluntarily, contents cloudy; seven feet of ribbon that unspooled slightly further every time you measured it, so that it was either growing or lying; and a small tin that rattled, unlabeled, which I have catalogued every month for nine years as *one tin, rattling,* and which I intend to keep not opening until one of us dies.

Then there was the door.

The sealed door stood at the heart of the Gatehouse the way a heart stands in a chest: centrally, indispensably, and without doing anything visible. It was black oak banded in iron gone green with age, tall as two men, carved with a design I'd spent years refusing to study and could nonetheless draw from memory: a tree, a river, a spire, all braided into one continuous line that began nowhere and ended nowhere. There was no handle. There was no keyhole that any locksmith had ever found. There was only the seam where the two halves met, thin as a hair, running top to bottom.

Every third Thursday, after inventory, I oiled the seam.

Nobody had told me to. When I took the post, old Keeper Aldery—who'd held it forty years and left nothing behind but the habit and a note reading *"Mind the hinges"*—had simply been gone, and the oiling had seemed to come with the chair. So once a month I climbed the ladder with the lamp and the little clay pot of oil and I worked the brush along that impossibly thin line, and the wood drank it in like something living, and the door stayed shut, and the country on the other side—if there was a country, if there was a side—stayed wherever it was.

Tam watched me do it, as he always did, perched on the bottom rung with his chin in his hands.

"Do you ever wonder what's behind it?" he asked.

"No."

"You've wondered. You oil it like it's a patient."

"I oil it like it's a door. Doors dry out. Doors crack. And if this door cracks—" I climbed down and capped the oil pot, "—then whatever the Guildmaster keeps in her ledgers under 'contingencies' stops being theoretical."

"You've read the contingency ledgers?"

"I've read everything in this building. That's most of why they gave me the post. Nobody else wanted to read that much."

It wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth was that I had read everything because reading was how you became furniture. A person who knows every fact about a place stops being a stranger in it, and a person who is useful in a hundred small ways stops being examined in any large ones. Invisibility isn't a talent. It's a trade, like anything else, and I had apprenticed to it young, and I was very good at my work.

Tam yawned enormously, cracking his jaw. "Orla said to tell you she'll have breakfast ready whenever you surface. She said 'surface' specifically. She thinks you live down here like a newt."

"Orla thinks everyone lives like a newt. She measures all human flourishing in breakfasts."

"Well, she's right, isn't she?" He grinned at me, that wide open grin that had no armor anywhere on it, and something in my chest did the thing it always did around him—the thing I refused to name, which was hope, which was dangerous, because hopeful people get seen.

"Go home, Wren. It's past your bedtime and past mine, and one of us has a reputation as a responsible adult to maintain."

"You don't have a reputation as anything. That's sort of your brand."

* * *

He wasn't wrong, and hearing it said plainly, even as a joke, landed wrong in me, like a stair that isn't where your foot expected. I saw him out through the side door, watched his lantern bob away across the frozen yard toward the kitchens, and stood a moment breathing white air into the dark.

The guildyard at night was its own small country. Lamplight in the windows of the Counting House, where clerks worked late over trades in things that couldn't be touched. Smoke from Orla's kitchen chimney, rising straight in the still cold—she baked at midnight, claimed insomnia, fed the whole compound on the proceeds. Somewhere beyond the walls, the city of Meridian proper slept under snow, and beyond the city, provinces and borders and the ordinary geography of an ordinary world, none of which had ever had a place in it for Juniper Brightwater, who had arrived at the Guild's gates nine years ago at roughly seventeen years of age, half-starved, with no history anyone could find and no history, privately, that he cared to offer.

I had built my invisibility the way masons build arches: stone by stone, each one placed deliberately. No family mentioned. No hometown given. A surname chosen from a list in a naming book because it sounded forgettable. A face kept level in every meeting, opinions kept mild, laughter kept small. Nine years of craftsmanship. And the result was a life so quiet you could hear the bottled rain thinking.

I went back inside. I banked the lamp. And then, because the night was long and the routine demanded it, I sat down at the workbench to deal with the stack of ledgers waiting for rebinding—the ones too damaged to shelve and too important to discard.

Mostly it was tedium. Cracked spines, foxed pages, the Guild's century of bookkeeping in impossible commodities slowly crumbling the way everything crumbles. I worked with paste and linen thread and a bone folder worn smooth as a spoon, and the work steadied me the way it always did. There is a particular mercy in repair. Nothing is required of you but patience.

Near the bottom of the stack was a ledger I didn't recognize. Old—older than old, its boards swollen, its clasp fused with corrosion. The spine label had been scraped off, deliberately, by someone who wanted it anonymous, and the paper inside was heavy and cream-colored and ruled in a hand so precise it looked printed.

I should have shelved it for the Guildmaster. Unlabeled volumes went upstairs. That was procedure, and procedure was another of my stones in the arch.

Instead I opened it.

The first pages were inventories like my own—but of things no guild had ever stocked. *Sunlight, preserved, one summer's worth. Riversong, thirty feet. Doors, of which one.* The handwriting changed hands every few pages, generations of keepers passing the pen like a relay baton, and somewhere in that procession of strangers I felt the same vertigo I feel on high balconies, the floor suddenly negotiable.

And then, near the middle of the book, in a page ruled for prophecy rather than accounting—for dates, and events, and names—a line of that ancient careful script, written a hundred years before I was born:

*On the third Thursday of the eleventh month, the keeper of the door will read these words.*

Below it, a date. Tonight's date.

And below that, a name. My name. Not Brightwater—that name I'd chosen from a list like picking a coat off a peg. But *Juniper*, spelled out plain as rain, in ink older than the Guild's oldest ledger, in a hand that had been dust for a century.

The lamp flame leaned sideways though there was no draft. Somewhere in the walls, the tin rattled, once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.

I sat very still in my invisible life, holding a book that had known I was coming since before my grandmother was born, and that was when someone knocked at the guild's front door—three slow strikes, deliberate, nothing like Tam's tap at the window.

No one knocked at the front door. The front door hadn't opened in years.

The knocking came again. Patient. Certain. Like it already knew I was awake.
