## 3. The Inquisitor Who Smiles

I have found in my long career that there are exactly three ways to enter a room: as a threat, as a guest, and as a bore. The first makes people hide things quickly. The second makes them hide them badly. But the third—ah, the third is where the Crown pays its wages. Nobody guards a secret against a bored man. A bored man is furniture. Furniture hears everything.

So it was that on a grey morning smelling of river and yesterday's rain, I presented myself at the Meridian Guild's front gate with a ledger case, a tax warrant stamped twice for tedium, and the expression of a man who would rather be anywhere else, which required only moderate acting, because the morning was miserable and my boots had soaked through by the second bridge.

The Gatehouse sat at the guild's western edge like a stubborn tooth: narrow, iron-banded, older than the street it refused to align with. The door itself was unremarkable, which was my first clue that it was remarkable. Doors that matter are always made to look like they don't. It's a rule of my trade.

A boy answered my knock—young, freckled, vibrating slightly with the particular terror of apprentices confronting government. Tam, per the manifest I'd memorized on the ride over. Good. I like starting with the ones who sweat.

"Inquisitor Corvane," I said, offering the warrant with a smile I keep oiled for exactly this weather. "Crown Audit of Guild Revenues. Nothing alarming. Your guildmaster filed a deduction in the spring that has the tax office in fits, and I've been sent to make everyone miserable about it."

"Guildmaster Yew isn't—it's early," the boy said.

"Then I'll wait," I said pleasantly. "I'm very good at waiting. It's practically my profession."

It was not, in fact, practically my profession. My profession was doors. Rumors of doors, specifically—a living one, sealed, breathing, somewhere in this city of forty thousand souls who all swore they'd seen nothing unusual, which in my experience means forty thousand people have each seen something small and agreed not to stack their somethings into a whole.

* * *

They put me in a receiving room with tea I did not drink and a chair that had been dusted recently, which told me they'd been warned. Word travels ahead of an Inquisitor the way cold travels ahead of winter; someone at the harbor office had earned himself a very interesting evening explaining his correspondence habits.

The room was a masterclass in studied blandness. Shelves of ledgers, none older than twenty years. A map of the city with no marks on it whatsoever—no ink, no pins, no wear. Who keeps a map nobody consults? Someone hiding what the map was *for*. I committed the wall behind it to memory and sat down to wait for the interesting thing to walk through the door.

It did. Not the guildmaster—I'd catch her later, and she'd be prepared, and we'd have our little fencing match of account books—but a person I hadn't expected until much deeper into the audit.

"Juniper Brightwater," they said, in a voice like someone apologizing for existing in your vicinity. "Keeper of the Gatehouse. Guildmaster Yew asks me to sit in on any interview regarding the west wing. Inventory records come through me."

"And you came instead of her sending for you," I observed. "Diligent."

"Terrified," they said, and then, as if hearing themselves, added, "of arithmetic errors."

I laughed. Genuinely, actually laughed, which surprised us both. There is a species of person who tells you the truth sideways while lying to your face, and I find them delightful, like cats that bring you dead birds they clearly intend for you to admire rather than eat.

"Master Brightwater," I said, spreading the warrant across the table. "Sit. This will be dull beyond your worst imaginings, and if you survive it, you may consider yourself immune to all future suffering."

They sat. Thin hands, ink-stained in the manner of someone who writes all day and not in the manner of someone who wants you to notice. Eyes that catalogued my warrant, my cuffs, my left boot, in that order—and lingered half a heartbeat too long on nothing at all near the western wall. Interesting. People look where their fear lives.

* * *

We did honest work for two hours, or what passes for it in my line. Deductions. Tariff schedules. The guild's frankly heroic creative accounting regarding "perishable intangibles," which is either bottled echoes or the most audacious fraud in municipal history, and I genuinely did not care which. I let Juniper explain the inventory system in exhaustive detail because people reveal themselves in their systems, and theirs revealed a soul that had built invisibility like architecture. Every record cross-filed three ways. Every anomaly explained before it could be asked about. Nine years of ledgers without a single loose thread.

Nobody is that tidy. Tidiness on that scale is a confession wearing a neat coat.

"Tell me about the door," I said, mid-way through the third pot of tea, casually as asking after the weather.

"The Gatehouse door?" They didn't flinch. Credit to them; most flinch. "Iron-bound oak. Older than the guild. We oil it quarterly. It doesn't open."

"Doesn't?"

"Sealed before my time. Guild policy. The space behind it is used for storage of—" a pause so brief a hummingbird would miss it "—decommissioned stock. Nothing worth your attention."

"Everything in this building is worth my attention, Master Brightwater. That's what the attention is *for*." I smiled and turned a page. "There are stories, you know. Silly ones. That the Meridian sits on a door that goes somewhere else. That it breathes. That the guild keeps a country in a bottle and the cork is that ugly old door."

"That's very poetic," Juniper said. "Whoever tells those stories should write them down. There's money in fantasy. The guild would know—we'd tax it."

There it was. The joke deployed precisely where a stammer should go. I looked up from the warrant and let myself really look at them for the first time, and they let themselves be looked at, which was its own kind of performance—shoulders apologetic, gaze modest, a face built to be forgotten. And underneath the performance, something watching me back with the patience of a stone that has decided to be a stone for a very long time.

"You'd make a terrible liar," I said warmly, "if you ever tried to lie about anything that mattered."

"I don't," they said. "Try. Or matter."

"Mm," I said, and let it go, because a fish that knows the hook exists is best left swimming in shallow water where you can see it.

* * *

I interviewed the cook next, because kitchens are the confessional booths of any great house. Orla was a broad, flour-dusted fortress of a woman who answered every question with complete honesty about bread and total ignorance about everything else, and whose hands never stopped moving, and whose eyes went flat as pond ice when I asked whether anyone strange had come to the gate lately.

"Strange how?" she said. "You're strange. You came to the gate."

"A fair point." I complimented the smell of whatever she was murdering in that pot, and she informed me it was dinner and that I wouldn't be staying for it, and I believed her on both counts.

By late afternoon I had audited nine years of taxes, found three irregularities worth prosecuting if I were the sort of Inquisitor who prosecuted arithmetic, and learned precisely nothing that would survive in a report—which meant I had learned a great deal. Buildings that want to seem empty arrange themselves. This one arranged itself beautifully. And beneath the arrangement, in the pauses, in the map with no marks, in a keeper who joked instead of stammering, something hummed like a wire under tension.

I did not find the door. I want to be honest about that; my reports are admired for their honesty, mostly by people who never read them. What I found was the shape of a hole where a door would be. And a shape of a hole, in my experience, is simply a door you haven't knocked on yet.

* * *

I was shown out at dusk. The rain had started again, fine and needling, and the courtyard lamps were being lit by a boy whose hands shook more than Tam's had. Yew herself appeared at last for the goodbye—a dry, unhurried woman with eyes like filed documents—and we exchanged the courtesies of two professionals who each knew exactly what the other was and found it impolite to mention.

"A thorough audit, Inquisitor," she said.

"A pleasurable one," I lied, smiling. "Your keeper Brightwater is a treasure. So calm. You truly couldn't rattle them."

"No," Yew said. "We couldn't."

I do enjoy a guildmaster with a sense of humor. Hers was excellent. Mine, people tell me, is also excellent, usually right before I ruin their lives.

At the front gate I paused. And here I performed the small ceremony I had come to perform, the real business under the tax business, the message that mattered. From inside my coat I drew a length of black ribbon, silk, a hand's width, and a wax seal the color of dried blood bearing the sunburst of the Crown's Inquisition.

"What's that?" Tam asked, from the safety of the doorway.

"A receipt," I said cheerfully, and tied the ribbon to the iron bars of the gate in a neat bow, and pressed the seal into the knot. The wax caught the lamplight and went dark again immediately, like something swallowing. "Every audit ends with a seal. Yours says: closed pending review. Which is Inquisition language for"—and I gave them all my very best smile, the one from the portraits, the one mothers in the market warn children about without knowing why—"*we are not finished*."

The ribbon stirred in the rain. Nobody moved to take it down. Wise. Taking it down would have been an answer, and answers are expensive this season.

"Good evening, Keeper," I said to Juniper, who stood slightly apart, hands folded, face composed into polite blankness. "Oil your door. It sounded creaky when I arrived."

Something crossed their face then—quick, gone, buried under courtesy—but I saw it, because seeing is the entire job. It wasn't fear. Fear I know like my own handwriting. This was something older and sadder, grief's quiet cousin, and it told me more than nine years of ledgers had.

I walked out into the wet streets alone, whistling, because a man who whistles is a man with nothing to hide, and because I was in an excellent mood. Somewhere in this city, a door breathed. Somewhere behind a boring face, a keeper held keys they didn't know they had. And now, thanks to a length of silk and some theater, they would lie awake tonight wondering how much I knew.

Wondering is where mistakes are born. I collect mistakes. I keep them in albums, like pressed flowers.

Behind me the guild lamps burned, and the black ribbon on the gate hung dark against the iron, patient as a promise. I tipped my hat to no one in particular and let the rain have the streets.

Tomorrow I would begin pulling threads. One of them would unravel.

They always do.
