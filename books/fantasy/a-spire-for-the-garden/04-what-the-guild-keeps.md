## 4. What the Guild Keeps

I have kept two ledgers for forty years, and only one of them is written down.

The written one sits in the counting room behind a lock that has been picked by four generations of apprentices and therefore no longer qualifies as a lock. It records the trade in possible things: pepper, paper, the usual sins of commerce. The other ledger I keep in my head, and it records everything else. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. The names of children not yet born who will one day come to us needing to buy back their own courage.

And now it must also record this: I looked at Juniper Brightwater across the counting table tonight and told them the truth, or as much of it as a woman my age can say out loud without her voice cracking like old vellum.

I am getting ahead of myself. Old bookkeepers always do. We live in the past tense; it's where our margins are.

* * *

The knock came at my study door an hour after Corvane's carriage rattled away from the gate, and I knew it was Juniper before they spoke, because nobody else in this building knocks as though they're apologizing to the wood.

"Guildmaster." They stood in my doorway holding the Prophecy Codex like it was something dead that might still bite. Which, in fairness, it was. "This was under the floor of the Gatehouse. There's a name in it. My name. And today's date."

"Come in," I said, "and close the door. Not that it matters. This room hasn't kept a secret since before you were born."

They sat. I poured tea, because in my experience the end of the world goes down easier with chamomile, and because it gave me something to do with hands that wanted to shake. Juniper set the codex on my desk with the reverence people reserve for things they don't understand, which is most reverence, if we're honest about it.

"Before you ask," they said, "I want to know if you already knew."

There it is. Nine years I'd watched this child dust shelves and miscount stock and insist, with total sincerity, that they preferred being nobody, and nine years I'd thought: *you have your mother's stubbornness, and she'd be furious to hear you say that.* Nine years of knowing and saying nothing, all of it arriving at once in the shape of one direct question from someone who had never once asked me for anything but wages.

"Yes," I said. "I knew about the name. I've known for six years."

I would like to report that Juniper raged. They didn't. They went very still, the way people do when the floor turns out to be thinner than advertised, and their tea cooled in front of them untouched, and after a long moment they said, "Six years," in a voice flat enough to iron shirts on.

"You deserved better than what I could give you by telling," I said. "You were happy. Or you had built something that passed for happy, and I wasn't going to take it from you before you'd finished building it."

"You don't get to decide that."

"No," I agreed. "But I did. That's what guildmastership means, mostly. Deciding things badly on behalf of other people and then writing very neat entries about it."

* * *

Let me put down what I told them, properly, because it deserves a clean entry even if my hand isn't steady.

A hundred years ago there was a country called Vessany. You will not find it on any map, not because maps are liars—which they are—but because the country itself was unmade. Erased. Not conquered; conquest leaves ruins, and ruins leave names. Vessany was taken out of the world so thoroughly that the world healed over the wound and forgot it had ever bled. Rivers rerouted themselves. Treaties reorganized. The neighbors' grandchildren grew up certain the valley east of the mountains had always been a lake, and when you showed them the drowned orchard walls at low water they said, charmingly, "Interesting geology."

Nobody remembers who did it. That is the cruelest engineering of the whole affair: the weapon erased its own fingerprints. Some say a rival empire. Some say a bargain gone wrong at the level where bargains are made with things older than countries. I have spent forty years reading the surviving correspondence and I can tell you only that whatever did the erasing was thorough, patient, and—this detail haunts me—*polite*. It left receipts.

But an erasure, however thorough, has edges. And inside the edges, things can survive the way embers survive in a banked fire: covered, starved of air, not yet cold.

Vessany survived here. Inside these walls. The Meridian Guild was founded—not originally, mind you; we began as ordinary smugglers with excellent taste—by three people who discovered that the guildhouse stood on one of those edges, and that within our boundary stones, the erased country persisted. Not as memory. As *fact*. The orchards grow in the cellars. The rain sounds different in the east corridor. And the door in the Gatehouse—the door Juniper has oiled every week for nine years—is the last true door out of Vessany, sealed from the far side during the final hour, when a handful of the country's people were smuggled through ahead of the erasure like furniture out of a burning house.

Except they weren't furniture. They were people, and they are still here, and we call them the Remnant, and they are fading.

That is why we trade in impossibles. Every bottled echo, every borrowed year, every unearned name we sell puts coin and favor in our hands, yes—but more importantly, it feeds the boundary. Belief, attention, use. A thing that is traded is a thing that exists. The Remnant persist because the guild keeps the ledger open, keeps the doors turning, keeps a hundred small impossible transactions humming like a heartbeat someone forgot to stop. We are not merchants, whatever my tax filings say. We are life support wearing a merchant's coat.

"How many are left?" Juniper asked.

"When I was young? Four hundred, perhaps. Now?" I did the arithmetic I do every year and hate every digit of. "Perhaps ninety. Fewer each spring. They don't die, exactly. They thin. You'll see one standing in the cellar garden and realize you can count the bottles behind them. They apologize for it, Juniper. They apologize for fading, as though it were an imposition."

"And the door?"

"Sealed. Locked from both sides, and the keys scattered, and half the reason lost. Opening it was supposed to be temporary—a way out for those smuggled through, a way home once the world forgot to watch. But the world never stopped watching, and then the Crown decided that a door between worlds is either a threat to be destroyed or a resource to be owned, and neither option ends well for anyone standing nearby."

Juniper turned the codex toward me. Their own handwriting in the margin, fresh, next to ink a century old that spelled out *Juniper Brightwater* beside tonight's date.

"So tell me the rest," they said. "The part you've been dancing around for six years. Why my name is in a hundred-year-old book."

And here is where I must record the second entry in tonight's ledger, the worse one.

* * *

"I recognized your surname the day Orla dragged you in off the street," I said. "Brightwater. It's not a common name. It isn't any name, actually—it belongs nowhere else in the world. It's Vessany. There was a Brightwater family in the orchard districts. Beekeepers, mostly, and one notoriously bad poet. When a feral child appeared on our doorstep with that name and the Gatehouse took an immediate liking to you—I mean that literally, the lock stopped sticking the week you arrived—I did the sum."

"You knew I might be one of them. One of the smuggled children."

"There was one infant unaccounted for. Passed through the door in the last hour, into the arms of a smuggler who was supposed to carry it beyond the erasure's reach. The smuggler's trail goes cold. The child's trail goes cold. And nine years ago a half-starved child with a Vessany name walks out of the hills to my doorstep, and the codex says—" I touched the page, because I couldn't touch theirs. "The codex says the fourth key will be named Brightwater, and that the door will move on the night written there. Tonight, as it happens."

"The fourth key," Juniper repeated. "Tam thinks there are three keys. Three founders."

"Tam thinks what I let Tam think. Yes, three founders, three keys. But a lock needs a hinge, and the hinge isn't metal, Juniper. The hinge is blood. Your blood, if the codex is honest, and prophecy codices are honest the way rivers are wet."

Silence. Then, very quietly, with a control that frightened me more than screaming would have: "Six years. You watched me oil that door every week. You let me build my whole self around being nobody, knowing—"

"Yes."

"You could have told me. I could have hated it. I would have hated it. But it would have been *mine* to hate."

Every word landed where it was aimed, and I let it, because ducking would have been obscene. "You're right," I said. "I stole the choice. I'll carry that. Write it in whatever ledger you keep on me, and believe me, you should keep one. But Juniper—Corvane was here today asking about a living door, and he left a black ribbon on my gate, and black ribbons mean the Inquisition has moved from rumor to audit. Whatever you decide to do with the truth, you will not do it alone, because alone is the one way that gets everyone killed."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You walked here carrying a century-old book instead of running into the hills with it. That's a decision. It's just wearing work clothes."

* * *

We sat a while longer. I refilled their tea, and this time they drank it, which I choose to interpret as forgiveness, or at least a ceasefire. Outside, the city muttered along toward midnight, unaware that it was built partly on a hole in the world and entirely on not asking questions.

"Do you ever miss it?" Juniper asked finally. "Vessany. You talk about it like you've been."

I hadn't expected the question, which is careless of me; Juniper has been surprising me for nine years and I never learn.

"I was born in Harrowgate," I said. "Two hundred miles from anywhere Vessany ever was. I've never set foot in the country, and neither, in a strange way, have the Remnant—they live in its ghost, in the part that fits inside our walls. But my grandmother sang lullabies in a language she called 'the old market tongue,' and swore she'd learned it from her mother, and couldn't say where her mother got it. That's how erasure works, Juniper. It doesn't kill everything. It kills the *middle*. Leaves orphan fragments floating loose, looking for somewhere to land." I closed the codex. "The guild found some of those fragments and gave them walls to land against. That's all we are, in the end. A wall for orphaned things."

"And me?"

"You," I said, "are the most orphaned thing that ever landed here. And the least alone, if you'd only look up from your ledgers long enough to notice who's been keeping yours."

They almost smiled. It was a narrow, bruised, suspicious almost, but I banked it.

"The keys," they said. "The founders. If we're doing this—and I'm not saying we are—if we're even considering it, I need to know where they are."

"Maren Ostrow keeps a pawnshop on Candle Lane and doesn't remember why certain melodies make her cry. Aldous Finch collects tolls on the Ferrow bridge and believes he's lived beside that river his whole life. He has. Just not *this* river. And Sera Ilves—" I paused, because this entry is oldest and hardest. "Sera died thirty years ago. She's buried in the Marrowfield cemetery, and her key was buried with her, because she didn't trust the living with it."

"Then that key's lost."

"No," I said. "That key's *dug up*, if the reports from the sexton are accurate. Somebody's been at her grave. Something translucent, and terribly polite about it."

Juniper stood, tucked the codex under their arm, and got nearly to the door before stopping, back still turned.

"Why tell me now? After six years of silence. What changed?"

"Tonight changed," I said. "The date in the book arrived, and the Inquisitor arrived, and I ran out of the luxury of protecting you from your own life. Besides—" and here I confess I reached for the only comfort I had to offer, which is honesty, delivered late. "I've watched you guard that door for nine years, Juniper, and I've come to believe the door chose well, whoever chose. You keep things safe. It's the one talent you have that can't be bought, bottled, or borrowed."

They left without answering, which is their way. But at the threshold they paused and said, "Orla's making seed bread tomorrow. She'll expect you at table."

"I'll be there," I said, and meant it the way I mean every entry in the second ledger: completely, and with the full knowledge that keeping it may cost more than I've budgeted for.

After they'd gone, I sat with the lamp low and opened my private accounts, the ones even the second ledger doesn't hold, and I made a mark against my own name. Six years of silence. Interest accrued. Payment coming due.

And beneath my window, faint as breath, from the direction of the Gatehouse, I heard the sound I've heard only twice in forty years: the great lock turning over, all by itself, like something practicing.

Like a door remembering what it was for.
