## 5. Three Keys, One Lock

I want it on the record—my record, which is a leather-bound notebook I bought with my own wages and keep under my mattress—that I figured it out before anyone told me.

Nobody will ever confirm this. Juniper will say they suspected all along and simply let me work through it for pedagogical reasons, which is what they say whenever I do anything right, as though my competence were somehow their gift to me. Guildmaster Yew will say nothing at all, because Yew communicates primarily through eyebrow and invoice. But I have the notebook, and the notebook has dates, and dates don't lie even when guildmasters do.

Here is how it happened.

* * *

The morning after the codex came out from under the floorboards, nobody in the Gatehouse was acting normal, which for the Gatehouse is saying something. Orla burned the porridge. Orla has never once burned porridge; she claims she could make it blindfolded, upside down, during an earthquake, and I believe her, because I once saw her rescue a soufflé from a collapsing shelf without breaking her sentence about the price of saffron. But that morning the porridge came up black as tar, and she scraped it into the fire and said "new recipe" in a voice that dared anyone to ask questions.

Juniper didn't eat at all. They sat at the long table with the Prophecy Codex open in front of them like it was something dead on a dissecting table, and they turned pages with the tips of their fingers, the way you'd handle a snake you weren't sure was finished being venomous.

I, meanwhile, was doing my job. My job, formally, is apprentice of the Meridian Guild: I dust the bottled echoes so they don't settle (echoes settle, did you know that? leave one alone six months and the sound inside goes murky, like rain heard through a wall), I copy ledger entries, I learn the cataloguing system, which has eleven exceptions and every one of them is Yew's idea of a joke. And my job, informally—the job I gave myself the day I arrived, age twelve, with one bag and no surname worth mentioning—is to understand what this place actually *is*.

Nine years of informal employment had gotten me this far: the guild trades in impossible things, the impossible things come from somewhere, the somewhere is behind the sealed door in the Gatehouse cellar, and everyone senior to me has agreed, without ever saying it aloud, that we do not discuss the door.

Well. The codex was out of the floorboards now. The door was discussed whether we liked it or not.

"Juniper," I said, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving things alone, "read me the passage again."

"No."

"You read it aloud last night. To Yew. I heard it through the floor."

"That was a private conversation."

"It was at shouting volume."

Juniper closed their eyes briefly, the way they do when they're counting to some number only they know. "It was a stressful night, Tam."

"It was the most important night in the history of this guild, possibly the country."

"The country doesn't exist. That's rather the point." They said it flatly, but their hand had gone to the codex's edge, holding it shut, the way you'd hold a wound closed. "Eat your toast."

"My toast is charcoal."

"Then chew thoughtfully."

* * *

Here is what I'd heard through the floor, and I will transcribe it exactly, because exactness matters when everything else is coming apart:

*And on the night the keeper reads her own name, the lock shall ask for its teeth: three keys carried out of the burning, held by three who forgot, and a fourth that never left.*

Four. That word is what kept me up until the candle drowned itself. Three keys and a fourth thing—"a fourth that never left." A fourth key that never left. Never left *where*? The country? The guild? The sentence?

I did what I always do when a question won't leave me alone: I went to the archive.

The archive is two rooms behind the counting office, and I am the only person under sixty who enters them voluntarily. Everything the guild has ever recorded is in there, or was supposed to be, though whole decades are missing—gaps in the shelves shaped like someone's conscience. I'd noticed the gaps years ago. I'd assumed moths. It says something about this place that I assumed *moths*.

That morning I pulled the founding records. Charter documents, membership rolls, the original articles of trade—and here is where I admit my hands were shaking, because I'd already guessed, the way you guess at a stair in the dark, and guessing doesn't stop the drop.

Three founders. Everyone knows the guild was founded by three; it's carved over the front door in letters worn nearly smooth: *YEW AND OSTROW AND ILVES, WHO BARGAINED WITH THE DARK SO OTHERS MIGHT LIGHT A CANDLE.* We all walked under that carving every day and thought about taxes.

Guildmaster Yew—our Yew, the woman who signs my wage chit and confiscates my sweets—was one of them. Which made her either impossibly old or something else entirely, and given that the Erasure was a hundred years ago, "impossibly old" wasn't going to cover it.

Ostrow. Ilves. Two names, nowhere in the current rolls, nowhere in the city directories I'd memorized out of boredom. Scattered, then. Or hidden. Or—

Or keys. I sat down on the archive floor with the charter across my knees and thought it through properly, the way Juniper taught me to think through a ledger discrepancy: follow the numbers until they confess.

A door between worlds. Sealed. The codex says the lock shall ask for its teeth—teeth, plural, three—and that the teeth were *carried out of the burning*. Carried by whom? By people. By the founders themselves, presumably, when Vessany fell—or when Vessany was *erased*, which the codex insists is different, and after last night I was in no position to argue. You don't seal a door like that with iron. Iron is for doors between rooms. A door between existence and nonexistence would need a lock made of something that existed on both sides. Something alive. Something that could be scattered into ordinary lives and forgotten, because a key that knows it's a key can be stolen, but a key that thinks it's a pawnbroker—

I stopped. Sat very still. Somewhere upstairs Orla was hammering something, aggressively, possibly the porridge pot.

*A pawnbroker.* Because I knew a pawnbroker. Everyone in the Lamplight Quarter knew Maren Ostrow, who ran the shop with the brass scales and never haggled and never smiled and paid fair coin for things other shops wouldn't touch. I'd sold her a dead man's watch two winters ago—my father's, not that I'd said whose—and she'd looked at it a long time before paying, and she'd said, "You don't want this back?" and I'd said no, and she'd wrapped it in cloth like it was a bird with a broken wing.

Maren Ostrow. Ostrow. The name had been sitting in the city my whole apprenticeship, wearing an apron.

And Ilves? I went back through the directories, the tax rolls, the parish lists. Nothing. Dead, maybe. Or renamed. Or—

"Aldous Finch," I said aloud to the archive, because saying things aloud helps me, and the archive is the one room where nobody tells me to stop.

Finch keeps the toll bridge over the Ansel. He's been there forever; boatmen say he was there when their grandfathers were boys, and they say it the way you say things that don't quite add up but you've decided not to look at directly. Forty years on a bridge, give or take a century. He waved at me once, when I was fourteen, and I have thought about it more than any reasonable person should.

Three founders. Three keys. Yew, who stayed. Ostrow, who sells memories she doesn't remember selling. Finch, who holds a river nobody can find the source of—I'd checked the maps; the Ansel just *starts*, mid-country, like a sentence with no beginning.

Which left the fourth. *A fourth that never left.*

I wrote it all down in my notebook. I dated it. Then I sat on the archive floor and felt the strange double weight of it: terror underneath, and on top of the terror, shamelessly, irrepressibly, *joy*. Because I had done this. Me, Tam, no surname worth mentioning, apprentice, sweeper of echo-dust. I had taken a century-old riddle and a handful of city gossip and I had *solved* it, or near enough, and whatever was coming for this guild—for Juniper, who'd gone grey around the mouth since reading their own name in a dead book—whatever was coming, we were not going to sit here and wait for it. We were going to have a *quest*. Keys. Founders. A door out of an erased country. This was the kind of thing I'd read about smuggled under the covers, and now it was happening in my own house, and I was the one holding the map.

I want to be clear that I understood, even then, that people were frightened and grieving and that joy was unseemly. I understood it the way you understand a rule printed on a sign. Understanding and obeying are different trades.

* * *

I cornered Juniper in the stockroom at midday, where they were pretending to inventory the bottled years—a task they do when they want to be alone, because the bottled years require absolute silence and everyone knows it, including, I suspect, the bottles.

"I know who the keys are," I said.

The silence changed texture. Juniper set down the year they were holding—it was a small one, a child's summer by the look of the wax seal—and turned around slowly.

"Sit down, Tam."

"I'll stand."

"Sit *down*." They pointed at a crate. I sat. Juniper leaned against the shelving and rubbed their eyes, and up close they looked like someone who hadn't slept, which was new; Juniper is the most thoroughly rested person I know, rest being the natural condition of someone who has spent nine years making themselves invisible. Invisibility, I've learned, is exhausting only when it starts to crack.

"Tell me what you think you know," they said.

So I told them. Ostrow. Finch. Yew. The charter, the carving, the toll bridge, the pawnshop, the river with no source. I talked fast because I always talk fast when I'm right, and I watched Juniper's face while I did, and here is the thing I will put in the notebook in ink: their face never once showed surprise. Not at Ostrow, not at Finch, not at any of it. At the end they just nodded, slowly, like a doctor confirming symptoms.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Since last night. Yew told me some. I worked out the rest."

"And you weren't going to tell *me*?"

"You're fourteen."

"I'm sixteen."

"You're sixteen," Juniper conceded, in the tone of someone granting a border province. "You're sixteen, you're an apprentice, and the people hunting whoever opens that door burn houses, Tam. Corvane left a black ribbon on our gate yesterday. That ribbon means *audited*, and audited means he comes back, and next time he won't be smiling about taxes. Every person who knows what you just told me is a person who can be made to tell it. So no. I wasn't going to tell you. I was going to let you keep dusting echoes."

"For how long?"

"As long as possible."

"And now?"

Juniper looked at the shelf of bottled years—all those summers and deaths and Tuesdays in glass—and something in their jaw set, that stubborn line I've seen them use against Yew herself.

"Now you know. So now you work. But you work *inside these walls*, do you understand me? You decipher. You cross-reference. You stay where Orla can feed you and I can see you. You do not go near Maren Ostrow, you do not go near Aldous Finch, and you do not—" their voice caught, just slightly, on the next words, "—you do not go looking for what the fourth key is. That's mine. Whatever it is. It's mine to carry."

"But it might be you."

"*Thank you*, Tam, I had considered that."

They said it sharply, and then immediately looked sorry, because Juniper is incapable of sharpness lasting longer than a breath. They came and sat next to me on the crate, which they never do, close enough that I could see the codex-ink still faintly stained on the side of their hand, like they'd been gripping the book in their sleep.

"Can I say something terrible?" they asked.

"You usually do."

"When you were talking just now—you had your notebook out, and you had it all underlined, and you were—" they searched for the word, found it, winced at it, "*glowing*. And I wanted to shake you. Because this isn't a story, Tam. There's no version of this where we ride out at dawn and come home singing. If Maren Ostrow takes back her memories, it will hurt her worse than anything that's ever happened to her, and she'll hate us for it, and she'll be right to. If Aldous Finch learns his river isn't real, forty years of his life turn to smoke while he stands there watching. And if the fourth key is what I think it is, then the fourth key stops existing, more or less, and everyone will call it necessary, and they'll even be right." They exhaled. "You're glowing about a funeral with extra steps."

I looked at my notebook. The underlines suddenly looked childish, all those eager strokes of graphite.

"I'm allowed to hope," I said, and hated how young my voice came out.

"You're allowed," Juniper said. "Hope's free. It's everything it costs that's the problem." They stood, and offered me a hand up, and for a second their grip was warm and real and older-sibling-tired. "But since you're going to hope regardless—because you're you, and I've known you four years, and you have never once obeyed a rule you could climb over—hope *usefully*. Find me Sera Ilves. Ostrow and Finch I can find; they're alive and they have addresses. Ilves is a ghost in the records. If anyone can raise her, it's the boy who assumes moths ate fifty years of ledgers."

"They didn't?"

"Yew did," Juniper said, "during the bad decade. Ask her about it sometime and watch her eyebrows." And they almost smiled—almost, a weather system on the horizon of a face—and left me alone in the stockroom with the bottled years.

* * *

I worked until dark. I want that noted too: I obeyed. Mostly. I stayed inside the walls, I pulled records, I built Ilves out of scraps the way you build a fire from damp wood—one splinter at a time, cursing. A midwife's license, eighty years old, issued to a Sera Ilves in a district that no longer appears on maps. A burial receipt, forty years old, from the churchyard of Saint Ferren's-on-the-Hill, paid in full, plot number 114. A single line in a guild margin, in handwriting I recognized as Yew's but younger: *S. asks too many questions. Good.*

By lamplight I copied it all into the notebook, and beneath it I wrote the thing I'd been circling all day, the thing with teeth in it:

*Three keys, one lock, and a fourth that never left. If the fourth is Juniper, then the prophecy didn't choose them last night. It chose them a hundred years ago, before they were born, and everything since—the Gatehouse, the ledgers, the invisibility—wasn't a life. It was a waiting room.*

I didn't like writing it. I wrote it anyway, because somebody in this guild has to write down true things, and apparently it's the sixteen-year-old.

Then I went up to the kitchen, where Orla was baking bread at midnight for no reason she'd admit to, and Juniper was sitting at the table staring at nothing, and I laid my notebook down between them and opened it to the burial receipt.

"Saint Ferren's," I said. "Plot 114. She's buried on the hill above the city, and Juniper—"

I stopped, because Orla had gone still with her fists in the dough, and Juniper had gone white in a way I'd only ever seen in the codex chapter, and from the direction of the front gate, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the black ribbon snapping loose in the wind—which shouldn't be possible, because ribbons don't snap, ribbons flutter, unless something has torn them off the gate.

Unless something is coming up the path.
