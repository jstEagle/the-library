## 6. The Woman Who Sold Her Name

There is a particular kind of hangover that comes from learning your name was written in a book a hundred years before you were born. It isn't a headache, exactly. It's more like wearing someone else's coat—you keep reaching for pockets that aren't where your hands expect them to be.

I had slept perhaps three hours, most of them in a chair, because my bed felt like a stage set now. *Juniper Brightwater's bed.* Even my own furniture had started addressing me formally.

So I did what I always do when the world turns traitor: I made a list.

Yew had given me the name at dawn, sliding a slip of paper across her desk as though it were contraband, which, technically, everything in this building was.

"Maren Ostrow," she'd said. "Third shop past the Cooper's Arch, in Salt Row. She'll be cold to you. Don't take it personally."

"Why would I take it personally? I'm nobody."

"Juniper." Yew had looked at me over her spectacles with an expression I'd spent nine years cataloguing and still couldn't file. "You stopped being nobody the moment you read that page. The world just hasn't caught up."

The paper said:

*M. OSTROW — PAWNBROKER — SALT ROW — FOUNDER: THE KEEPER OF NAMES.*

And beneath that, in Yew's cramped hand, a single line she probably thought I wouldn't read upside down: *She paid more than we asked. Be gentle.*

I folded the note into my coat pocket, where it sat against my ribs like a coal.

Tam wanted to come. Of course Tam wanted to come; Tam wants to come to everything short of executions, and I suspect he's working on those. He stood in the doorway of my workroom holding a satchel of supplies he'd packed without being asked—bottles, wax, the good lockpicks—and radiated the specific hopefulness of a dog who has seen its lead.

"No," I said.

"But—"

"This isn't an adventure, Tam. It's a conversation with a woman who may not want to be found. If there are two of us, we're a delegation. One of me is just a customer."

"A customer buying what?"

I looked at the shelves. Bottled echoes, mostly—the guild's bread and butter, if your bread is other people's Tuesdays and your butter is sound. Rain on a roof you've never stood under. A market crowd from a city that no longer appears on maps.

"I don't know yet," I said, which was true, and pulled down the bottle I wasn't supposed to know how to find.

* *

Salt Row earns its name honestly. Everything there tastes faintly of brine—the air, the well water, the fishmongers' jokes—and the buildings lean together like old conspirators. Third shop past the Cooper's Arch turned out to be a pawnbroker's with a window display arranged with the loving cruelty of someone who knows exactly what broken things people will crawl back for: a wedding ring, a fiddle with one string, a child's boot, brass, immaculate, catching the light like it had somewhere better to be.

A bell above the door announced me. Behind the counter sat a woman in her sixties with iron-grey hair pinned up with what appeared to be two knitting needles and the posture of someone who has heard every sob story in a maritime language and invented responses in several others.

"Buying or selling?" she said, without looking up from her ledger.

Her ledger. I felt mine, the real one, humming in the satchel like a wasp in a jar.

"Selling," I said, because everyone in Salt Row is selling something, and it buys you thirty seconds of professional attention before the assessment begins.

She looked up then. Her eyes were the pale grey of weather coming in. They went over me the way a jeweler goes over a stone—weight, cut, flaws—and something flickered across her face too fast for me to name. Recognition would have been too much to hope for. But it wasn't nothing.

"You're not from here," she said.

"Neither is anyone."

"That's a city answer." She set down her pen. "Name?"

"Brightwater. Juniper Brightwater."

Something happened to her hand. Just a small thing—the pen slipping half an inch before she caught it—but I've spent nine years watching people's hands lie for their faces, and I know what a tremor looks like when it's been suppressed so hard it comes out sideways.

"Never heard of you," said Maren Ostrow.

"That's all right," I said. "Until about four days ago, neither had I."

* *

We did not speak of names again for some time. Instead I browsed, because browsing is what customers do and because I needed to understand her before I could risk understanding myself in her.

The shop was a museum of surrendered lives. Instruments no one could play anymore. Uniforms from regiments that had been disbanded or, in two cases, that I was fairly sure had never existed—which, given recent revelations, I filed under *questions I am not ready to open.* Books with pages pressed flat by decades of waiting. And everywhere, everywhere, small things: thimbles, buttons, a glass bird, a tin soldier missing his rifle arm but standing at attention anyway.

"People bring you their whole histories," I said, "and you give them coin."

"I give them fair value," she corrected. "What they do after is their affair."

"And when they don't come back? When they sell the ring and never redeem it?"

"Then it stays on my shelf until the world ends or I do." She shrugged, but her shoulders didn't agree with the shrug. "Everything in this shop was loved once. That's not my doing. I just don't throw love away because somebody ran out of money."

*Be gentle,* Yew had written. I was beginning to understand why.

I set the bottle on the counter between us. Plain glass, corked with grey wax, no label. Guild stock is unlabeled for the same reason poison is: so that no one helps themselves.

Maren looked at it the way a sailor looks at weather.

"I don't deal in curiosities. Glassware's out front."

"It's not for sale," I said. "It's a gift."

"Nothing's a gift."

"This one is."

She didn't touch it. She looked at me instead, long and level, and I watched her decide whether I was a con artist, a tax man, or worse. I tried to look like none of those things. It's difficult. My whole face has been trained to look like nothing, and apparently *nothing* reads as suspicious in Salt Row.

"What is it?" she asked finally.

"An echo. Bottled sound. The guild makes them—or finds them; honestly the distinction gets philosophical." I kept my voice light, tradesman's patter, because the alternative was a voice that shook. "This one is rain."

"Rain." Flat. A door closing. "It's been dry season, boy. Rain's free if you wait a fortnight."

"Not this rain."

The bell above the door jangled. A woman came in wanting to hock a necklace, and Maren rose to deal with her, and I stood at the counter with my heart going like a trapped moth while the transaction unspooled in front of me—haggling, sighing, the necklace vanishing into a drawer of other people's worst weeks. Normal commerce. Possible things only. I envied her the ledger.

When the door closed behind the customer, Maren didn't sit back down. She stood with her knuckles on the counter, leaning toward me like a cliff considering its options.

"You're from the Guild of Meridian," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Then you should know I settled my accounts with them twenty years ago. Paid in full. There's nothing owed either direction, and I'll thank you to carry that back through the Cooper's Arch and however many miles you crawled from."

"There's nothing owed," I agreed. "That's what makes it a gift."

"Gifts have hooks in them. Everyone knows that. You lot taught the whole city that rhyme."

"We didn't teach you this one." And then, because gentleness had got me exactly nowhere and honesty was the only currency she hadn't refused yet: "Maren. Twenty years ago, what did you pay the guild?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had grain, like wood.

"You know," she said quietly, "in twenty years, not one soul from that guild has walked in here. Not one. I used to leave the lamp burning late, first few years, in case. In case someone came to say *it was worth it*, or even to say *we took too much*. Nobody came. And now you stroll in with your nice coat and your bottled rain and you say my name like you know it." Her jaw worked. "Who told you my name?"

"A book," I said. "A very old book. It also says—" I stopped. Some confessions you ration. "It says my name too. Written down before either of us was born. I came here because I thought maybe you'd tell me what it means. Because I think you're the only person alive who might remember enough to try."

"I don't remember anything," said Maren Ostrow, and every word of it was true, which is the most exhausting kind of sentence to hear.

"Then the bottle can't hurt you," I said. "Can it?"

* *

She poured us tea, eventually. This took convincing—not of her, oddly, but performed for herself, a ritual of ordinary hostessing to prove that the morning was still under control. The tea was terrible, oversteeped and salty from the air, and we drank it standing up among the surrendered loves of strangers like two mourners at a wake with no corpse.

"My daughter was dying," she said at last, to the tea rather than to me. "Winter fever. Nineteen years old and turning blue at the edges, and the physicians had done everything physicians do, which is stand around looking learned while they compose your eulogy. And a friend of a friend said—I've never known who, I've hated them and thanked them in roughly equal measure ever since—that there was a place that traded in impossible things. That if you had something *worth enough*, truly worth enough, they could make a trade that wasn't a trade at all. More like a... correction."

"The guild doesn't do corrections," I said automatically.

"The guild did one." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine. "They took my memories. All of them. Of a place, a country, a home—they called it something, I wrote the name down afterward, I was so frightened of losing even the word, and then I lost the paper, or threw it away, or maybe I sold it, ha, to someone in this very shop—and what's left is a shape. A country-shaped hole. I know the shape of it the way your tongue knows a missing tooth. I know I had a mother there. I know there was a tree I climbed. I know the word for hello in a language that does not exist, and I cannot say it, because saying it would require remembering it, and there is nothing there to remember *with*."

She set the cup down. Her hands were steady now. Steady hands frighten me more than shaking ones; shaking means the feeling is getting out.

"They saved my daughter's life," she said, "and I have never once regretted it, and I have cried about it every year on a day I cannot name, in a month I have to count to find. That is the price, boy. That is what your guild charges. Not coin. Continuity. So you can take your little bottle and get out of my shop, because I survived this once and I will not be *unmade* twice for the comfort of—"

"Vessany," I said.

She stopped as though I'd slapped her.

"The country was called Vessany," I said softly. "Your mother's name, according to the founding rolls, was Ilse Ostrow née Varr. The tree was an ash, in the yard of a house on Candlemaker's Lane, and you fell out of it when you were eight and lied about it for a week because you were supposed to be minding your brother. Your brother's name was Dov."

"Don't." Barely a whisper.

"He lived. For what that's worth. He lived, he grew old, he named a son after the ash tree, and he never stopped setting two cups out at evening for forty years, and the second cup was yours."

"*Don't.*"

"I know because the guild keeps records of everything it takes. I read them last night. I couldn't sleep, so I read them, and I'm sorry, Maren, I am so sorry, but I needed to know if it was true, because someone wrote my name in a prophecy and I needed to know whether I was mad, and the price of my sanity turns out to be handing you yours, and I would give it back if I could, but I can't, so instead—" My voice cracked, damn it, trained-for-nothing face and all. "Instead I brought you this."

I unstopped the bottle.

* *

Sound, when the guild bottles it, doesn't pour. It unfolds. The cork came free with a sigh, and the room filled slowly with rain—not the fat indifferent drumming of city rain on slate, but a softer thing, patient, generous, rain falling on leaves. On orchard leaves. Underneath it, almost too quiet to catch: a wooden shutter knocking gently in wind, a dog two streets off, a woman's voice calling a name across a yard—a girl's name, warm with amusement, the tone of someone calling a child who has climbed something she shouldn't—

"—the ash tree," Maren whispered.

The echo played itself out and faded the way real rain fades, reluctantly, in stages. Then silence, of the ordinary kind, the cheap kind.

Maren Ostrow, founder of the Meridian Guild, Keeper of Names, the woman who had sold her entire country to save one life, stood among her shelves of abandoned loves and wept.

She didn't know why. That was the unbearable part. I watched her reach for the grief the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't there—one moment supported by composure, the next plunging—and she wept the way people weep at funerals for someone they were told about but never met. Her body remembered. Her body had been keeping the vigil all these years while her mind paid rent elsewhere.

I did not touch her. I wanted to. But some griefs bite, and she hadn't invited me past the counter of her life, and so I stood with my hands at my sides and let her cry, and I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this is what Yew meant. This is what survival costs. Somewhere out there is a version of me who paid this price without knowing, and I am the receipt.*

After a long time, the storm passed. Maren wiped her face with a shop rag, briskly, the way women of Salt Row do everything, and glared at me with red-rimmed eyes.

"If you ever tell anyone I cried," she said, "I will deny it, and then I will ruin you. I know people. I own their boots."

"Noted."

"And you—" She pointed a knitting-needle-sharp finger. "You'll come back. Tomorrow. And you'll bring whatever else is in that satchel, and you'll tell me about this book, and you will *not* pretend it's simple, because nothing the guild touches is simple, and if I'm to—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Started again, smaller. "If I'm to consider whatever it is you actually came here to ask me, I need to know all of it. Every piece. Even the parts that hurt."

"Especially those," I agreed. "Those are most of it."

She picked up the empty bottle, turned it in her fingers, and held it a moment too long before setting it down—as if she could hear the ghost of rain still in the glass.

"Go home, Juniper Brightwater," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded different than it ever had, older, like a key fitting a lock it was cut for. "Come back at dusk tomorrow. And walk carefully."

"Carefully?"

She nodded toward the window, toward the street outside, and I followed her gaze to the shop across the lane, where nailed to the doorpost of the cooper's, black ribbon stirred in the salt wind.

Someone had marked the row overnight. Someone was marking doors all over the city.

And every ribbon, I would learn too soon, pointed back to us.
