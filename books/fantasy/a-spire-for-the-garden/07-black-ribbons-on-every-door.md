## 7. Black Ribbons on Every Door

There is a particular pleasure in paperwork that I have never bothered to hide from my colleagues, mostly because they find it distasteful and I enjoy their discomfort. A ledger is a confession written by someone who doesn't know they're confessing. Every column balances toward truth eventually. You simply have to be patient enough to read the whole book.

I was patient. It is one of my few virtues, and I have made it do the work of all the others.

By morning of the third day after my visit to the Meridian Guild, I had spread across my office floor — because desks are for men with fewer ambitions — a map of Candlemere's lower quarters, and upon it, forty-one small black ribbons, each pinned to an address. Forty-one houses, shops, warehouses, and one suspiciously large bakery, all of which touched the guild in some way: supplied it, bought from it, sheltered its people, or merely smelled faintly of impossible things when the wind came off the canal.

The ribbons were not decoration. They were arithmetic.

"Explain it to me again," said Deputy Inquisitor Halloway, who stood in my doorway holding two cups of coffee as though he hoped one of them would turn out to be courage. "The ribbon system."

"It's simple." I crouched over the map, careful not to disturb the pins. "When you want to catch a fish, you don't chase the fish. You study the water. The guild is the fish. These—" I gestured at the ribbons— "are the currents."

"And the door?"

"Ah." I smiled up at him. He flinched slightly, which I noted for later. "The door is why we're fishing."

I will not bore you with the full taxonomy of my investigation, though I confess I was tempted to have it bound and gilded; some of my best work has lived only in case files, unthanked. Suffice it to say that over eleven years with the Crown's Inquisition, I had developed a nose for the impossible the way a sommelier develops a palate. Most people think magic announces itself — thunder, light, ravens speaking in dead languages. Nonsense. Real impossibility is quiet. It is a woman in Tallow Street who has not aged since the flood. It is a cellar where bread never molds. It is a guild that pays its taxes in coin minted with a king's face no living citizen recognizes.

That last one had brought me to the Meridian Guild in the first place, three years ago, and I had left empty-handed but not empty-headed. Guildmaster Yew was a locked room wearing spectacles. Her clerk — the small gray one, Brightwater, whose name I had looked up afterward and found to be a masterpiece of nothing: no family, no history before nine years ago, no debts, no lovers, no vices I could purchase — her clerk had lied to me with the smooth competence of someone who had been lying their whole life, which is itself a kind of biography.

And now there were rumors. There are always rumors, but these had *texture*. A living door. A country behind a wall. Whispers moving through the lower quarters like damp through plaster, and whispers with that much moisture in them always trace back to something real.

"You're smiling again," Halloway said.

"I'm always smiling. It disorients people. Take the eastern district today — start with the candlemaker on Wick Lane, then the apothecary. Mark anything odd. And Halloway—"

"Yes?"

"If anyone asks what we're looking for, tell them tax irregularities. People forgive the Crown almost anything except an interest in their souls."

* * *

We marked fourteen houses that day, and I learned more from the marking than from any interrogation, because a black ribbon does its own work. We did not nail them up ourselves, you understand. We simply asked permission to leave a seal pending audit, and permission was granted, because refusing an Inquisitor requires either innocence of a very loud variety or guilt of a very organized one, and most people are neither. They watch us pin the ribbon to their lintel with the face of someone swallowing a stone, and then they close their doors, and then — this is the important part — they talk.

By evening, the ribbons had done three days' work in six hours. A laundress mentioned that the guild's cook bought saffron no honest kitchen could afford. A ferryman remembered carrying crates of "glass bottles that clinked like they were full of weather" down to the guild's water-gate. A drunk on Coriander Row swore he'd once seen a man walk out of the guild's front door twice — same coat, same limp, four minutes apart — and hadn't reported it because he'd assumed he was drunk, which was fair, because he was.

The city was telling on itself. Cities always do, if you make them nervous enough. Fear loosens tongues the way heat loosens glue, and I had spent the day applying heat in small, polite increments: a question here, a seal there, a smile everywhere. By nightfall, Candlemere's lower quarters felt like a hand slowly closing — not clenched yet, just curling inward, finger by finger, around something it intended to keep.

I dined alone at a chop house near the canal and made notes by candlelight while my mutton went cold, which was fine, because I'd really ordered the candlelight.

Item: the cook. Orla Brannigan, sixty-one years in service, thirty-two of them at the Meridian Guild. No recorded family. Kitchen of unusual size for a staff of fewer than twenty. Purchases inconsistent with menu. Recommendation: interview personally.

Item: the apprentice. Tam Wrenn, sixteen, orphan, guild-raised. Too young to know anything useful, too young to lie well. Low priority, but children repeat what they overhear. Possibly useful later.

Item: Juniper Brightwater. Nine years of records and not one entry out of place. Either the most boring employee in Candlemere or the best-hidden person in Vessany—

I stopped writing. Not because the thought frightened me, but because it pleased me too much, and pleasure makes a sloppy investigator. Still. The name sat on the page looking at me. *Brightwater.* There was no river called the Brightwater anywhere in the Crown's dominions. I had checked. Names are like coins: they come from somewhere, even counterfeits.

I underlined it twice and finished my wine.

* * *

Orla Brannigan received me in her kitchen at nine o'clock the next morning, which she informed me was the only hour she had free between bread and lunch, and that if I wanted to talk during bread I would have to talk while she worked, because dough waits for no man, least of all a government man.

The kitchen was enormous, warm, and smelled so violently of cinnamon that I briefly suspected it was deployed as camouflage. Copper pots hung from rafters like a small metallic army. A cat regarded me from atop a flour barrel with an expression I have otherwise only seen on judges.

"Inquisitor," Orla said, not looking up. She was a broad woman built along the lines of her own oven, forearms like a stevedore's, hair iron-gray and tied back without ceremony. "You'll forgive me if I don't curtsy. My hands are spoken for."

"Perfectly forgiven. I'll try to be brief."

"Everyone says that. Nobody is. Knead or stand, but pick one."

I elected to stand. "I'm conducting interviews of guild-affiliated households. Routine audit."

"Mm." She punched the dough as though it owed her money. "My sister married a tax auditor. Worst ten years of her life, and she told me everything about the work. So ask your real questions, love, and we'll both save the pretense for someone who hasn't heard it before."

Well. I did warn Halloway that I enjoyed being disoriented.

"You've cooked for the guild for thirty-two years," I said. "In that time, have you ever prepared a meal that could not possibly exist?"

The kneading stopped. Just for a heartbeat. Then resumed, slower.

"That's a strange question for a tax man."

"I'm a strange tax man."

She turned then and looked at me properly, and I'll grant her this: she didn't flinch. Most people, when I look at them directly, do one of two things — they perform innocence, badly, or they perform anger, worse. Orla Brannigan did neither. She looked at me the way you look at weather coming in off the sea. Something to be endured, not argued with.

"I've fed that guild every day for thirty-two years," she said. "Stews and loaves and honey-cakes and funeral puddings. If any of it was impossible, nobody told me, and nobody sent it back."

"And the saffron?"

"The saffron," she said, "is none of your business, and it comes from my cousin in Sarrat, who owes me for keeping his children fed the winter his wife took ill, and if you want to audit gratitude, Inquisitor, you'll need a bigger ledger than the Crown prints."

It was a good answer. Structurally sound, emotionally true, difficult to prosecute. I found myself almost admiring it, the way a locksmith admires a lock that defeats him.

"There are stories," I said pleasantly, "going around the lower quarters. About the guild. About a door."

"Stories." She turned back to her dough. "There's always stories. Last year it was ghosts in the tannery. Year before that, a mermaid in the canal, turned out to be a drowned wig. Stories are cheaper than bread around here."

"Some stories are expensive."

"Then they're not mine to pay for." She slid the loaf into the oven, wiped her hands on her apron, and faced me with the finality of a woman closing a shop shutter. "Now. I've got lunch for nineteen due in three hours. Was there anything else?"

There was. What there was, specifically, was a search warrant, which I produced from inside my coat with the flourish of a card trick, because I am not above theater when theater works.

Her kitchen, I am sorry to report, yielded nothing. I say *sorry* with complete insincerity; I had expected nothing and come anyway, because showing a suspect you'll search them costs you nothing and teaches them everything about how far you'll go. My deputies went through the pantry, the cold cellar, the spice cabinets. Halloway opened a tall cupboard and found, stacked in careful rows, dozens of small notebooks — recipe books, decades of them, in a handwriting like storm-fence wire.

"What's this?" he asked, holding one up.

"Thirty years of receipts," Orla said. "Every dish I ever made. You want to read about stew, help yourself."

Halloway glanced at me. I nodded. He gathered the notebooks — all of them — into a canvas sack, and here is where I must record, for accuracy's sake, exactly what happened, because I have gone over it in my mind many times since and it has not gotten less strange.

Orla Brannigan watched him pack her life's work into a bag, and she did not protest, did not plead, did not threaten. She walked to the great hearth, took down a cast-iron pan with one hand as if lifting a teacup, pulled a notebook — the newest one, still soft-cornered — from her apron pocket where it had been all along, tore out a page covered in dense, furious handwriting, and held it in the coals until it caught.

"Those are evidence," Halloway said.

"This is dinner," she said, and fed another page to the fire.

What burned, I would learn later from the shape of the ash and the smell of it — I have a good nose — was not a recipe. Or rather, it was a recipe, but written in no alphabet my translators recognized, in ink that smoked green before it died. By the time we understood what we were watching, she had burned four pages, calm as a woman basting a goose, and the cat had relocated to the windowsill to observe our failure with professional detachment.

"Arrest her?" Halloway whispered.

"On what charge? Destroying her own property?" I closed my notebook. "No. Leave the rest. They're worthless now, aren't they, madam? Whatever was in those pages, the rest of the book is just bread."

"Everything in that book is bread," Orla said, patting the last embers flat with her palm, unhurried, unburned. "Some of it feeds the body. Some of it feeds other things. But it's all bread, love. You can take the whole shelf home to the Ministry and it'll do you less good than a crust, because bread only means something to the people it was made for."

I looked at her for a long moment. She looked back, patient as stone, and behind the patience — I saw it clearly, and it delighted me — was fear. Not of me. Of something else. Something she considered worse than me, which, given my profession, was genuinely flattering.

"Thank you for your time," I said. "The ribbon stays on the door."

"I'd expect nothing else." At the threshold I paused, because I had one more card, and it was a good one. "One last thing, Mrs. Brannigan. Your clerk. Brightwater. Tell me about them."

For the first time that morning, her face changed — not dramatically, nothing so generous. Just a tightening around the mouth, a flicker behind the eyes, the smallest possible crack in the stonework. And I understood, standing in that cinnamon-warm kitchen, that I had finally found the wall's load-bearing brick.

"They're my scullery boy," Orla said quietly. "Came to me half-dead nine years ago and ate like a wolf and never once gave me trouble. That's all there is to tell."

"Nine years ago," I repeated. "Before that?"

"Before that," she said, turning back to her oven, voice steady as a hearthstone, "there wasn't any before that. Now get out of my kitchen, Inquisitor. The rolls won't wait and neither will I."

* * *

That evening I sat in my office with the map and moved three pins and added five new ribbons, and then I sat for a long time doing nothing at all, which for me is the same as working.

The city was closing like a hand; I could feel it through the soles of my boots. Fourteen houses marked, forty-six total now. Yew's guild sealed under preliminary writ. The Remnant — for I had begun privately calling the whisper-people that, having pried the word out of a half-mad lamplighter who claimed to have seen "the faded ones" walking the guild wall at dusk — the Remnant were stirring, and stirring things make mistakes.

And beneath it all, one name kept surfacing in my notes like a cork in dark water.

*Brightwater.*

A clerk with no past. A name attached to no river. A date in an old rumor — the lamplighter had rambled about it, *the book says the name, the book says the day* — that matched, when I checked it against my calendar, tomorrow.

I poured myself a glass of the cheap wine I pretend not to prefer, and I allowed myself, at last, the smile I'd been rationing all day.

Everyone believes the door is hidden behind walls and wards and thirty years of bread. But doors are not hidden by walls. Doors are hidden by *keepers*. Find the keeper, find the door. And a keeper who appeared from nowhere nine years ago, with no history, no family, and a name that doesn't belong to any river in the world, is not a person at all.

A keeper like that is a key someone dressed up as a person.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would pay the Gatehouse a second visit. No warrants this time. Just a friendly call, a bottle of decent wine, and a long conversation about ledgers.

After all — I wrote it in my notes, underlined twice, next to that impossible name —

everyone confesses eventually. Some of us just write it down first.
