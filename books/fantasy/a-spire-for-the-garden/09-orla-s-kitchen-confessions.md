## 9. Orla's Kitchen Confessions

There are three kinds of silence in a kitchen, and I have spent forty years learning to tell them apart.

There's the silence of a kitchen that's done for the night—banked coals, covered dough, the pots hanging still as hanged men. There's the silence of a kitchen that's waiting, which is the same as the first except the bread rises faster in it, like it knows something's coming. And then there's the silence of somebody sitting at your table who has something to say and hasn't said it yet.

That third one was in my kitchen tonight, wearing Juniper Brightwater's face and pretending very hard to be inventory.

They'd been at the Gatehouse all evening doing whatever it is they do down there with their ledgers and their oilcloth and that door nobody talks about, and when they came up they had the look I've learned to read the way other folk read weather. Pale around the mouth. Hands too careful with things that don't need care. They'd found something in the floorboards—I don't know what, don't want to know what yet—and they were carrying it around inside them like a swallowed stone.

So I did what I always do when one of mine comes home carrying a stone.

I baked.

* * *

"You didn't have to stay up," Juniper said, when I set the loaf down between us. It was well past midnight. The guild above us had gone quiet in that particular way it does—not empty, never empty, but holding its breath. Somewhere overhead a shutter creaked. The stockrooms ticked and settled, bottles of borrowed years knocking gently against each other like teeth.

"Course I had to," I said. "Bread doesn't wait for convenient hours. Neither does hunger."

"I'm not hungry."

"Nobody's ever hungry at midnight. That's not what midnight bread is for."

They looked at the loaf the way they look at everything lately—like it might be a trap, or a prophecy, or both. Then they tore off a piece anyway, because I raised them better than to refuse food in my kitchen, prophecy or no prophecy, and ate it without tasting it. I could tell they weren't tasting it. Nine years of watching this child eat, and I know the difference between eating and fueling.

I let the quiet sit a while. That's another thing kitchens teach you: most truths need proofing time. You can't rush them out of people any more than you can rush dough. You just keep the room warm and wait.

It took half a loaf.

"Orla." They turned the crust over in their fingers. "How old was I? When you found me."

Ah, I thought. There it is.

And here is where I should tell you something about myself, since we're being honest tonight, which I suspect we are whether I like it or not. My name is Orla Brennan, though half the guild calls me Orla-of-the-Kitchen the way you'd name a landmark, and I have fed every soul under this roof from Guildmaster Yew on down to the rats, who get the heels and don't complain. I am not a sentimental woman. Sentiment spoils faster than cream in this trade. But I will tell you plainly, because plainness is the only currency I've ever trusted: there are exactly two things in this world I would burn the whole Meridian Guild down for, and one of them is sitting across my table tearing bread like the world might end before the next loaf.

The other is the recipe book, but we'll come to that.

"How old do you think?" I said.

"Feral," Juniper said. "Tam says feral. Tam says you told him feral."

"Tam talks too much. He gets it from me, so I can't rightly punish him for it." I wiped my hands on my apron, an old habit, hands already clean. "You were nine. Maybe ten. Skinny as a whippet and twice as mean, if a whippet can be mean, which I've since learned they can. You bit me."

"I did not bite you."

"You absolutely bit me. Drew blood. Right here." I held up my left hand, where—if you knew where to look, and only I know where to look—there's a scar no bigger than a fingernail moon. "I'd put out a bowl of stew and you came at it like I'd poisoned it and you were going to die fighting rather than poisoned. Took the skin off my thumb getting to it. Ate the whole bowl standing up, watching me the entire time, ready for me to change my mind about the arrangement."

Juniper stared at their own hands. "I don't remember that."

"No," I said. "You wouldn't."

* * *

Here's what I remember, since apparently tonight is for remembering, and someone in this kitchen ought to do it properly.

Nine years ago, in the autumn, Yew came down to my kitchen at an hour she knows I hate and said, "Orla, there's a child in the Gatehouse storeroom."

I said, "Then feed it."

She said, "It won't let anyone near it."

I said, "Then send someone who isn't anyone," and she gave me a look, and I gave her back the same look, and that's how the two of us communicate, mostly, looks that would take a paragraph apiece to write down.

Because here's the thing about the Gatehouse, and about me, and maybe about you too if you've lived long enough in a place like this: I knew what that child was the moment I saw them. Not the details. Not the hows or the whos. But I've worked in this guild long enough to know what children look like when they've been passed through something. They come out the far side of doors like that with a particular kind of emptiness—not hollow, mind you, hollow can be filled, but *scoured*. Like the inside of a pot after scouring. All the surface gone. Just the iron underneath, good iron, strong iron, but nothing soft left showing.

You were curled up behind the bottled echoes with your knees to your chest, and when I opened the door you looked at me with eyes that had already decided I was going to hurt you, and had already decided to survive it anyway, and I thought: *well. There's a keeper.*

Not a child. A keeper. Somebody whose whole self had been reduced to one job: hold the line, guard the door, trust nothing. And I thought, whoever sent you through that door sent you to the one place in all the world built for exactly that, and either they knew what they were doing or God does, and I've never had much use for distinguishing between the two.

I set down a bowl of stew. I sat down on the floor across from you, which my knees objected to then and object to now in retrospect, and I said, "Eat. I'm not going anywhere."

You ate. You watched me the whole time. And when the bowl was empty you said—the first words I ever heard from you, and I have kept them like a coin in a pocket ever since—you said:

"What do you want for it?"

And I said, "Nothing. First one's free. Kitchen rule."

And you said, "Nothing's free."

And I said, "In my kitchen it is," and got up, and left the door open behind me, and went back to my bread.

You came up two hours later. Stood in the doorway. Watched me work. Didn't say a word. I didn't say one either. I just pointed at the bench and kept kneading, and you sat down, and that was that. That was the whole negotiation. Nine years ago, and I have never once asked for terms, and neither have you, and that silence between us has been the soundest contract I've ever been party to, and I've seen contracts in this guild that trade in years and names and the last breaths of dying kings.

* * *

"So why didn't you ask?" Juniper said now, quiet, into the middle of the table. "Where I came from. Who I was. Everyone else asks questions. Yew asks questions. The whole guild runs on questions—it's a *trade*, questions are literally our stock-in-trade, and you—" Their voice did something complicated. "You never once asked me anything."

"Course I didn't."

"But why?"

I took my time answering, because the answer matters and because my hands needed something to do, so I cut two more slices and buttered them, which is a sacrament and not to be rushed.

"Because some questions unmake families," I said. "That's the whole of it. You want the long version?"

"I want the long version."

"The long version is this. I had a family before this one. Husband, Colm. Two boys. Fergal and little Denny." I said the names evenly, the way you learn to say names you've said ten thousand times until the saying of them stops costing. "Colm died of the lung-rot the year Denny turned six. The boys went in the fever winter three years after. And I'll tell you what everyone did afterward, everyone who loved me, all the good kind souls of this city—they asked questions. What was it like. How did they go. Did Denny say anything at the end. Was he frightened. Were you there. Every question a knife with a polite handle, and every one of them meant kindly, and every one of them opened me up again like I was a body on a table. Because here's what questions do, Juniper, when they're aimed at the tender parts: they make you answer. And answering means deciding what happened. And deciding what happened means the grief gets a shape, and the shape becomes permanent, and you carry that shape forever instead of the person."

I pushed the buttered bread toward them.

"A family isn't made of answers. A family is made of showing up. You showed up in my doorway at two in the morning looking like something a door spat out, and everything in me that still worked said *feed that one*, and I have spent nine years feeding that one, and not one day of it has required knowing your blood or your birthplace or your business. You want to tell me someday, you'll tell me. You don't, I'll still know everything worth knowing. You take your tea bitter. You hum when you think no one's listening, badly. You alphabetize the spice rack when you're frightened, which drove me mad for the first year until I understood what it was. You laugh at your own jokes a half-second before you finish telling them. You'd walk into fire for Tam and pretend afterward it was logistics." I shrugged. "That's who you are. That's the whole census. Where you were born is a fact, and facts are cheap. I deal in bread."

Juniper was crying by the end of it. Not loudly. Juniper doesn't do anything loudly; even their catastrophes are conducted in a whisper, which is going to be a problem for them eventually, mark me. But crying, yes, silent, furious at themselves for it, wiping their face with the heel of their hand like the tears were an administrative error.

"I found my name in a book," they said. "A hundred-year-old book. With today's date next to it."

"I know."

Their head came up sharp. "You *know*?"

"Yew told me. Weeks ago, when she first started pacing the way she paces when the ledgers won't balance." I met their eyes and didn't flinch, because I wasn't going to start flinching in year nine. "I told her to leave the book where it was. She didn't listen. Yew never listens, it's why she's guildmaster and why her office smells of burnt decisions."

"And you didn't tell *me*."

"No."

"Why not?" And there it was underneath, the real question, the one that had been sitting in them all night like a stone in a shoe: *why does everyone decide things about me without me.*

So I answered it straight, because they'd earned straight, and because lying to this child has never once worked and I'm too old to practice.

"Because the moment you read that book, you'd stop being my Juniper and start being the book's Juniper. Prophecy's a question too, love. The worst kind. It asks *who are you really* and then it doesn't wait for your answer, it writes its own. And I have buried enough people to know that the ones who survive are the ones who belong to somebody. Belonging to a book is no belonging at all." I reached across and took their hand, flour and all, and held it the way I used to when they were new and couldn't sleep for listening to the walls. "So hear me now, and hear me plain, because I'll not say it twice and I'll deny saying it at all if you repeat it to Tam. Whatever that book says you are—whatever the door says, whatever the Inquisitors say, whatever the ghosts say—you are first and always the child who bit me over a bowl of stew, and I have loved you since before you earned it, which is the only way love works, and if you come out the far side of whatever's coming missing pieces, you bring the pieces home and I'll bake them into something."

They laughed. Wet, cracked, but a laugh. "Bake them into something."

"That's right. Grief pastry. Family recipe."

"It's not a family recipe."

"Everything's a family recipe if a family eats it twice."

We sat there while the coals settled and the bread finished cooling and the guild ticked overhead, and for a while—for a few minutes that I would not trade against any treasure in the stockrooms, bottled years and all—we were just two people at a table at midnight, and the world outside the kitchen could go hang.

Then Juniper said, "Orla. If I find out where I came from. If it turns out I'm—from somewhere that isn't here. Somewhere that stopped existing." They couldn't finish it. They didn't need to.

I squeezed their hand once, hard, the way you check a knot.

"Then you'll still know where you're *going*," I said. "Home is a direction, not an origin. Now eat your crust."

They ate their crust.

* * *

It was near dawn when the banging started.

Not at the kitchen door—at the guild's front gate, heavy and arrhythmic, the knock of someone official or someone desperate, and in this city those have started sounding the same. Juniper was on their feet before I'd gotten my apron off, and I caught their sleeve.

"Whatever's out there," I said, "it knocks. Things that mean us harm don't knock."

But even as I said it, I heard the second sound underneath the knocking, fainter, coming from somewhere below—from the direction of the Gatehouse, from the direction of the door—and it wasn't knocking at all.

It was digging.

Soft, patient, unhurried, like earth being moved by hands that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Juniper heard it too. I watched the color leave their face by candlelight, and I watched them go to the stairs, and I called after them the only blessing my family ever had, the one my mother said over rising dough and open doors alike:

"Come back hungry!"

They didn't answer. But they paused at the top of the steps—one heartbeat, two—long enough for me to see that they'd heard me.

Then they were gone, down toward the digging, and I banked the coals, and covered the dough, and sat alone in the warm dark of my kitchen listening to the dead of Vessany shovel their way toward morning.
