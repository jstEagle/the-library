## 12. The Bargain in the Glass House

I have interrogated smugglers in wine cellars, forgers in their own parlors, and once a bishop in the crypt beneath his own cathedral, which he had been using to store contraband lightning. I am considered, within the Inquisition, a man of refined settings. So when I chose the Glass House on Vellum Street for my meeting with Juniper Brightwater, I did it with the same care a surgeon brings to choosing a blade.

The Glass House is a tea room that sells no tea. It is owned by the Meridian Guild—of course it is; everything on this street is—and its walls are panes of enchanted glass that show the drinker not their reflection but their intentions. Sit down at one of those tables and you will see yourself as you are about to be: the lie you're about to tell, the hand you're about to shake, the knife you've told yourself you won't need. It is meant to be a place of honest negotiation.

It is also, I will admit, beautiful. The glass catches the afternoon light and throws it around the room in long amber ribbons, so that everyone sitting there looks faintly gilded, faintly forgiven. I have watched hardened criminals walk into that room wearing guilt like a wet coat and walk out an hour later having signed confessions, weeping, grateful. People want to be seen clearly. It is the great weakness of our species, and I have built a career on it.

I arrived first. I always arrive first. There is a kind of power in being found already seated, already comfortable, a cup of something steaming at your elbow—as though the room itself had decided you belonged and everyone else was merely admitted. I ordered two of whatever the house served, because the house serves only one thing: a pale gold liquor that tastes, they say, like the moment before you say something you regret. I have never confirmed this. I regret nothing loudly enough for the taste to register.

Juniper Brightwater came in at four o'clock precisely, because of course they did. Gatekeepers are punctual the way gravestones are permanent. They stood in the doorway a moment, taking in the glass, the light, me, and I watched them do the arithmetic every careful person does upon entering a trap: exits, witnesses, weight of the furniture versus weight of the man.

"Sit," I said. "The chairs don't bite. Nothing in here bites. That's rather the point."

They sat. Up close, Juniper Brightwater was exactly what their file promised and nothing what their file suggested—the file said *clerk, unremarkable, nine years' service, no irregularities*, and the person across from me said *I have been counting the seconds since I walked in and I know how many there have been*. Their eyes went to the glass wall beside them, and I saw them flinch very slightly at whatever it showed them.

"Don't look too long," I said. "The glass is honest. Honesty is habit-forming."

"What is this place?"

"A tea room that doesn't sell tea. A confession booth without the theology. The Guild built it back when the Guild still believed in fair dealing." I slid the second cup toward them. "Drink or don't. It's not drugged. If I wanted you docile, Brightwater, I'd simply talk longer."

That earned me almost a smile. Almost. I noted it the way a lepidopterist notes a rare specimen: *specimen capable of humor, condition fragile.*

"You audited us last week," they said. "Taxes. You left a ribbon on the gate."

"I did audit you last week. Your ledgers are immaculate, by the way. Genuinely. Nine years of entries and not one rounding error. Do you know how rare that is? Most people's books are little autobiographies of their self-deceptions. Yours read like they were written by someone who wants to be forgotten by the paper itself." I turned my cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "And yes, I left a ribbon. Black silk. You'll notice I didn't leave a summons. That was courtesy. This invitation is also courtesy. I am, today, a very courteous man."

"Inquisitor Corvane." They said my name the way one identifies a stain. "Why am I here?"

"Because you read the codex."

I will give them this: they didn't move. Not a blink, not a breath, not the tiny withdrawal of the shoulders that betrays most people about half a second before they deny anything. They simply looked at me with the flat patience of a locked door, and I thought, *ah, there you are*—because I had spent three weeks reading depositions and tax rolls and a certain century-old book with pages like autumn leaves, and nowhere in any of it had I found a description of Juniper Brightwater that matched the person actually sitting in front of me. The file said unremarkable. The person said *load-bearing*.

"The Prophecy Codex," I went on, pleasantly. "A tedious document, mostly. Centuries of guild founders predicting grain prices and naming their enemies' horses. But near the end there's an entry—a date, and a name. Tomorrow's date, as it happens. Or yesterday's, depending on when you finally slept. And the name is yours." I let that sit in the amber light between us. "Written down one hundred years ago by people who could not possibly have known you. Unless, of course, they knew exactly what you were going to be."

"You're mistaken," Juniper said. "I dusted that ledger shelf for nine years. There's no codex under the floorboards."

"There it is," I said, delighted despite myself. "You know, most people deny things they've been accused of. You denied a thing I hadn't accused you of yet. Floorboards. I never mentioned floorboards."

The silence that followed was exquisite. I have dined out on lesser silences.

"I'm going to tell you a story," I said, "and then I'm going to make you an offer, and then you're going to leave, because you'll want to think, and thinking requires walking, and you strike me as a walker. Are you comfortable? Don't answer. Story time.

"A hundred years ago there was a country called Vessany. Orchards, rivers, a capital with eleven bridges, a language with forty words for rain. It had the misfortune to sit on top of something the Crown wanted—not gold, not oil, but a door. A door between what is and what might otherwise have been. The Crown erased Vessany. Not conquered—erased. Maps redrawn mid-stroke. Records burned and replaced with records saying they had always burned. Mothers who woke up remembering children they'd never had, and grieved anyway, because grief is loyal even when memory isn't. The country didn't die. Countries don't die. It was *unmade*, except for one seed of it, hidden inside a trading company's walls, where impossible things are kept the way other warehouses keep salt." I sipped. "Your warehouse, Brightwater. Your door. Your Remnant—those patient translucent gentlemen currently excavating a grave in the Marrowfield cemetery, though I'm sure you know nothing about that either."

Nothing moved in their face. But their hands, folded on the table, had gone white at the knuckles, and the glass beside them—I glanced at it, because I am not above cheating—showed not Juniper Brightwater the clerk but a figure standing in a doorway made of light, holding it open with both arms, and the light was full of people.

The glass never lies. That's why I hate it.

"So," I said, and set down my cup, and leaned forward, and let my voice drop into the register I save for the moment that matters. "Here is the offer.

"Destroy the door."

They blinked. Of all the things they'd braced for, I suspect that wasn't among them.

"You heard me. Burn it, seal it, unmake it—whatever the ritual requires, and I know you know what it requires, because you've been oiling that door for nine years like a priest tending an altar while telling everyone you're just a janitor. Destroy it, and Vessany finishes dying. The Remnant fade. The country goes from *erased* to *never*, which is a mercy, if you think about it—no more ghosts digging up graves, no more fading, just quiet. And in exchange—" I held up one finger. "In exchange, I bury you. Your name, your bloodline, your entry in that codex. I am the only man in the Crown's service with the authority to amend an Erasure record, and I am offering to write you out of the prophecy and into nothing. No Inquisitor will ever come for you again. No ribbon on your gate. No codex with your name in it. You go back to your ledgers, your cook, your apprentice, your small gray orderly life—and it becomes true. Permanently. Nobody. Forever. Which I understand," and here I smiled, and meant perhaps a third of it, "is what you always said you wanted."

The glass beside them had changed. It showed Juniper at a desk, old, content, a ledger open, dust motes in a shaft of window light, and the image was warm and still and utterly silent, like a painting of a life rather than a life.

"That's it?" they said quietly. "You want the door destroyed."

"I want the door destroyed. Yes."

"Not opened. Not controlled. Destroyed."

"The Crown's position on doors between what is and what might otherwise have been is well documented. We are against them. Enthusiastically."

"And the Remnant."

"Fade. Peacefully. I'm told it doesn't hurt. I'm told—" I paused, because this next part required care, and care is a muscle I keep well-exercised. "I'm told they're already halfway gone, Brightwater. A hundred years of thinning. What's left of them is less than what's missing. You wouldn't be killing anyone. You'd be letting a candle finish burning down instead of relighting it and watching it flare and gutter and hurt everyone standing close. Sometimes the kindest thing is to stop pretending a thing can be saved."

I watched that land. I am not proud of how carefully I'd prepared it, but I am honest with myself, which is more than can be said for the glass in this room.

And here is the thing I must confess, since the walls insist on honesty: I believed some of it. The Crown trained me to believe all of it, and I am good at my work, and good work leaves residue. There is a version of me that genuinely thinks erasure twice is mercy. There is another version that has started dreaming, lately, of a woman with my grandmother's jawline standing in a sunlit orchard, and that version has been very quiet in meetings and very loud at night.

Juniper looked at their hands for a long time.

"You said I'd want to think," they said finally. "I'm thinking."

"Take your time. The liquor helps. Tastes like the moment before a mistake, or so I'm told."

"It tastes like my mother's kitchen," Juniper said, and then went very still, as though the sentence had escaped without a permit.

I did not smile. I wanted to—it was the exact crack I'd been prying at—but a good interrogator knows when to hold the crowbar still. "Tell me about her," I said softly.

"No."

"She was Vessani."

"*No.*" Louder now, and the glass beside them shivered, literally shivered, showing for one flickering instant a woman's hands passing a bundle through a narrowing seam of light, and then blank amber again. Juniper stared at the glass. "What did it show you," they said. "Just now. Tell me exactly."

"A door closing," I lied, smoothly, and the glass showed me my own lie as a small black thread through the amber, and I drank to cover it. "Doors, bundles, mothers. The usual iconography of people who've just learned their whole life is a footnote."

"You knew." Their voice had gone low and dangerous, and I confess it did something to me, hearing a clerk use that voice. "Yew knew. Someone knew. Nine years I've been keeping those ledgers and everyone in that building knew what I was except me."

"Yes."

"Why not tell me?"

"Because knowledge is a key, Brightwater, and keys are how doors get opened, and my entire profession exists to keep that particular door shut. Yew didn't tell you because she loved you. I'm not telling you because I'm offering to save you. Same silence. Different architects." I spread my hands. "But you deserve to hear it plainly, so: you are a child of Vessany. Smuggled out as an infant through a closing door by someone who loved you enough to commit treason. The codex named you because the codex names hinges. You are not the gatekeeper, Juniper. You are the gate. And the Remnant know it, and Yew knows it, and Maren Ostrow is weeping over bottled rain in her pawnshop because her memories came back, and Aldous Finch is holding up a river with his bare stubborn soul and calling it a commute. The keys are gathering. The lock is turning. And in"—I consulted my watch, purely for theater—"perhaps six days, something is going to happen under that Gatehouse whether the Crown approves or not.

"Unless you destroy it first. And walk away clean. Nobody. Forever. Safe."

The glass showed them the desk again. The dust motes. The warm, silent, painted life. And I watched Juniper Brightwater look at it the way a drowning man looks at a shore—not with longing, exactly, but with the terrible arithmetic of someone calculating whether the swim is worth it.

"I could say yes," they said.

"You could."

"I could burn it tonight. Before anyone else gets hurt. Tam got a ribbon on his boarding house door this morning—he told me, he thought I didn't understand what it meant. Orla burned her recipes, Corvane. She stood in her own kitchen and fed thirty years of her life into the stove rather than let your people read them. These are *my people*. And you're telling me if I kill the last piece of my mother's country, you'll let me keep them."

"I'm telling you it's the only way you keep them."

"And the Remnant."

"Fade."

"And the orchards. The forty words for rain. The eleven bridges."

"Gone. Were always gone. Will have always been gone." I leaned back. "Yes."

Juniper picked up the cup I'd poured for them. Held it. The light came through it gold, and for a moment they were gilded like everyone else in that lying beautiful room, and I thought—I will record this honestly, because the glass would record it for me anyway—I thought, *they're going to say yes.* I had built the offer perfectly. Duty to self against duty to the forgotten, and self is so much closer, self is right here, self has a name and a cook and an apprentice with a bandaged arm and a bed that smells of bread. I have made this offer eleven times in my career. Eleven times, the person said yes. People choose their lives. It is the most reliable law I know.

Juniper raised the cup halfway. Stopped.

"You said it doesn't hurt," they said. "When the Remnant fade. Who told you?"

"A colleague."

"Has your colleague ever faded?"

"No."

"Have you ever watched it happen?"

I opened my mouth and the glass, treacherous honest glass, showed the room a memory I did not know I carried: a Remnant woman in a cell below the Bastion, three years ago, going transparent by degrees over eleven days, and on the eleventh day she had looked at me through the bars with my grandmother's jawline and said, *you have his chin, you know. My son's chin,* and then she was light, and then she was air, and then I signed the certificate that said *subject expired, natural causes, no further action required*, and my hand did not shake until it was over, and then it shook for a week.

The amber held the image for maybe two seconds. Long enough.

"Inquisitor," Juniper said gently, and there was no triumph in it, which was somehow worse than triumph, "who was she?"

"My grandmother," I said, before I could stop it, and then I stopped it, hard, and reached for the bottle, and poured, and my hand was steady, I made it steady, I have made worse things steady. "Irrelevant. Dead. Faded, as it happens. Peacefully. As advertised."

"She knew your face."

"She was delirious."

"At the end."

"They usually are."

Juniper set the cup down without drinking. Looked at me for a long moment with an expression I could not catalogue—pity, I realized later, much later, after everything; it was pity—and said:

"You're asking me to do to a whole country what was done to her. Slowly. With paperwork."

"I'm asking you to spare yourself."

"I know." They stood. Pushed the chair in neatly, because of course they did, gatekeepers push in chairs. "And I want you to know that I thought about it. Honestly. For about ten seconds it was the easiest yes of my life—you should know that, you should know how close it was. I have spent nine years building a nobody and I *like* her. Him. Me. I like the ledgers and the routine and Orla's terrible soup and Tam's terrible questions. You offered me the one thing I've wanted since I was a feral child eating out of your evidence bins, and I nearly took it, and I want you to sit with that, Inquisitor, because I think you offered it hoping I would, and I think part of you hoped I wouldn't, and I think the glass in this room has been showing you which part is winning."

The glass behind me, I did not turn to check. I did not need to. I could feel it looking at me the way my grandmother had looked at me through the bars.

"For what it's worth," Juniper said, from the doorway, backlit by the ordinary street, "your grandmother had forty words for rain. I don't remember them. But I remember that someone does. Good evening, Corvane."

And they walked out mid-sentence—my sentence, the one I was still constructing, the one about consequences and six days and the full weight of the Crown—walked out of the Glass House leaving the offer on the table like an unpaid bill, and the door chimed, and the amber light settled, and I sat alone in the honest room with two untouched cups.

The glass beside me showed a man in an Inquisitor's coat standing in a sunlit orchard, and the man was crying, and I stayed until the light went out of the walls, and then I went back to the Bastion and wrote in my report: *Subject unpersuaded. Recommend escalation.*

Six days.

Underneath the report, in a hand I did not recognize as mine, I wrote a single word and then struck it through so thoroughly that no one will ever read it.

It was *Grandmother*.
