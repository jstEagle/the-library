## 13. Running Out of Country

I want it on the record that I was doing everything right.

I had catalogued the west storeroom twice, alphabetized the bottled echoes by pitch instead of date because Juniper said that made more sense and I would follow Juniper into a fire, and I had sharpened every pencil in the counting room to a point so fine they could have signed treaties. This is what I did when I was scared, which was often, which was why my ledgers were the tidiest in guild history and possibly in the history of tidiness itself.

The flickering started at half past four in the afternoon, while I was holding a jar of borrowed years up to the light to check the sediment.

One moment the jar was there. The next moment the jar was not there, and neither was the shelf, and neither was the wall behind the shelf — I was looking straight through a hole in the world at an empty lot, weeds shoulder-high, a broken fence, gray sky where the roof should have been. Then the wall came back, and the shelf came back, and the jar came back, and I was standing very still with my heart going like a trapped bird and the jar of years sweating in my hand.

"Juniper," I said. My voice came out about an octave higher than intended. "Juniper, the building blinked."

They came around the corner at a walk, because Juniper never ran anywhere, on the theory — they told me once — that running tells everyone exactly what you're afraid of. They looked at the shelf. They looked at me. They took the jar out of my hands and held it up to the light themselves, and for three full seconds nothing happened, and I started composing an apology for being hysterical.

Then the wall went away again, and this time it stayed gone long enough that I could count the weeds.

"Huh," said Juniper.

That's it. That's all they said. *Huh.* Nine years I've worked for this person, and I can tell you their entire emotional vocabulary is some kind of secret code where *huh* means the sky is falling.

"The codex," they said, still looking through the hole at the empty lot. "Yew read from it. Out loud. Last night."

"To show us the passage," I said. "About you."

"About me." Juniper set the jar down with exaggerated care, the way you'd set down something that might be listening. "Reading it aloud restarts the clock. The Erasure doesn't like being remembered. It's coming back to finish the job, and it's starting with the rooms nobody's standing in."

As if to prove them right, the storeroom door — which I could see perfectly well, oak, iron-banded, older than both of us put together — dissolved into a suggestion of a doorway, then into nothing, and beyond it the corridor did the same, and for one horrible moment the whole back half of the guild was just Vessany-shaped absence with weeds growing in it. Then it reassembled, slightly wrong. The door hinges were on the other side now. I checked twice.

"We need Yew," I said.

"Yew's in the city trying to buy us time from people who don't sell time anymore." Juniper was already moving, touching walls as they went, and I noticed — I notice things, it's my whole function — that wherever their fingers passed, the plaster stayed solid a beat longer than it should have. Like the building knew whose name was written in the book. Like it was leaning on them.

"Then we need Orla," I said, "because if the building eats itself, I refuse to be hungry when it happens."

And that, as it turned out, was the single most strategic decision anyone made all day.

* * *

Here is what I learned over the next six hours, and I am writing it down partly for the record and partly because writing it down makes it feel less like drowning:

The flickering isn't random. It follows the ledger order. The oldest entries go first. The east wing, which holds stock acquired before the Erasure — the original inventory, the founding trade, the things the guild was built to keep — is dissolving fastest. By sundown the Echo Gallery was a field. By full dark the room where we kept the names-not-yet-earned was a hedgerow, and I stood in the middle of it watching nightingales that didn't exist sing in a hedge that didn't exist, and I cried a little, and I'm not ashamed, or I'm only a little ashamed.

Juniper organized us like a general who'd read one book about war and mostly remembered the parts about snacks.

"Maren's echo comes with us," they said, pulling bottles off shelves two seconds ahead of the emptiness. "Aldous's river-water, obviously. Anything tied to a founder stays. Everything else—" they looked at a shelf of bottled laughter, forty years of someone's joy, and their jaw did the thing it does when they're deciding not to feel something yet. "Everything else, we let go."

"You can't save it?"

"It's not *in* the bottles, Tam. It's in the country. The bottles are just cups. When the country goes, the cups stay full of nothing." They handed me an armload anyway. "We carry them out regardless. Because if any of this survives, someone will want proof it was real."

So we carried. Back and forth, through corridors that kept forgetting to exist, Juniper walking point with their palm flat against the wall like they were steadying a spooked horse, me behind with my arms full of impossible things, and the strange part — the part I keep circling back to — is that the flickering slowed wherever Juniper touched. Not stopped. Slowed. The weeds came up more politely. The absence hesitated at the threshold of whatever room they were in, like a debt collector who's found out the debtor is somebody's cousin.

"You're doing that on purpose," I said.

"I'm doing nothing on purpose. I don't know how to do anything on purpose. I oil a door and file receipts." But they said it fast, and their hand never left the wall.

By the time Orla found us, we had rescued maybe a third of the pre-Erasure stock and stacked it in the front hall, which was holding — barely, grudgingly, the way old people hold onto a grudge — and I was sitting on the steps feeling sorry for myself in a thorough, professional manner. Orla looked at the stacks. She looked at the hole in the east wall where the Echo Gallery used to be, all weeds and gray light. She looked at the two of us, dusty and hollow-eyed.

"Well," she said. "Nobody's eaten since noon, and I'll tell you what my mother told me: a country can end, but dinner is a decision."

"Orla," Juniper said, "half the kitchen might not exist."

"Then I'll cook in the half that does." And she rolled up her sleeves and marched toward the flicker like she owned it, which, knowing Orla, she intended to.

* * *

I helped her. It seemed safer than thinking.

The kitchen was the strangest battlefield I've ever stood in. Half of it was our kitchen — copper pots, the scarred table, the window over the sink where Orla grows herbs in tins — and the other half was an empty lot at dusk, and the border between them moved. While we worked, it moved toward us. I watched the bread oven flicker out of being while the dough was still inside it, and flicker back, and flicker out again, each time returning a little less certain of itself, until the last time it came back it was a clay pit with no door and no chimney and Orla swore under her breath in three languages and built a fire in the hearth instead.

"Chop," she said, putting a knife in my hand and onions in front of me. "Don't watch the walls. Walls know when you're watching. It emboldens them."

"That's not how walls work."

"Neither is a country surviving a hundred years inside a tax office, and yet." She had a point. Orla usually does; it's her worst quality.

So I chopped, and she cooked, and the thing about Orla's cooking is that it isn't cooking so much as siegecraft. She pulled everything from the deep cellar, the cold stores, the places that predate the flickering's reach — root vegetables, salted pork, the black loaves she bakes when she's angry at someone specific. The smell filled the kitchen and pushed outward, down the corridor, into the hall, and I swear on every ledger I've ever balanced that the flickering flinched from it. The empty lot stopped advancing at the kitchen doorway. It stood there, weeds waving in wind that wasn't ours, like a dog that has followed you home and isn't sure it's allowed in.

"See," Orla said, stirring, not even looking at it. "Onions. Works on ghosts, Inquisitors, and ontological collapse. My mother knew things."

"Your mother sounds terrifying."

"She was. She'd have loved this place." Something crossed her face then, quick as the flickers, and was gone, and I had the sense — not for the first time — that Orla's past is a locked box she keeps the key to in her mouth.

Juniper came in when the bread came out. They stood in the doorway with dust in their hair and a bottle of someone's laughter under each arm, and they breathed in, and their shoulders came down maybe half an inch, which for Juniper is a standing ovation.

"Dinner," Orla announced, setting down bowls, "of everything I could defend."

We sat at the scarred table. Three bowls of stew, black bread, tea that Orla made from the herbs in the tins on the windowsill because those, at least, were hers, planted by her hands, and apparently reality respects squatter's rights. And here is the thing I will remember until I die, whenever and however that happens:

None of it tasted like anything.

Not bad. Not spoiled. Just — absent. The stew was warm and the bread was warm and the texture was right, and there was simply nothing where the flavor should have been, a hole shaped exactly like taste. I watched Juniper take a bite and stop chewing, very slowly, the way you stop when your body understands something before your mind consents to.

"The country's going," they said quietly. "It's taking its flavors with it. We're eating the memory of food."

"Eat it anyway," Orla said. "Flavor's the least of what food is for. Food is for sitting down. Food is for *here, you look thin.* Flavor is decoration." She pointed her spoon at the advancing darkness visible through the doorway. "That thing out there can take the salt out of my bread. It cannot take the bread out of my hand or make me stop handing it to you. So eat."

So we ate. Tasteless, warm, perfect. And somewhere in the middle of the meal I realized the flickering had stopped entirely — not slowed, stopped — within earshot of the table, and that the empty lot at the kitchen door had gone quiet, and that the three of us sitting down together in a doomed kitchen was somehow the strongest wall in the building.

I said so. Orla snorted. Juniper looked at their bowl for a long time and then said, "Yew needs to hear that. When she gets back. If the Remnant are fading because they're forgotten, and the country's fading because it's unremembered—"

"Then remembering out loud is a weapon," I finished. "Dinner as counter-magic."

"Don't get mystical about my stew," Orla said. "You'll ruin it, and it's ruined enough."

But she was smiling. It was a small smile, the kind she denies later, but I saw it. I see everything. It's my whole function.

* * *

We worked through the night by that logic. If the Erasure advanced on absence, we would fill the guild with presence: voices, noise, names. Juniper walked the corridors reading ledger entries aloud — item, date, provenance — in their flat gatekeeper's voice, and the walls held where they walked. I copied the oldest records into fresh books, saying each line aloud as I wrote it, feeling faintly ridiculous and refusing to stop. Orla baked through the small hours, and the smell of it — even flavorless, even ghostbread — pushed the dark back another room, then another.

At some point near dawn I found Juniper standing in the ruins of the Echo Gallery, which was now mostly sky and nettles, holding Maren Ostrow's bottle of rain against their chest.

"It's quieter in here," they said. "The rain. Listen."

I listened. Under the hiss of the bottled storm there was something else, faint, getting fainter: a sound like a crowd heard through a wall, like a market square, like a country talking in its sleep.

"They're still in there," Juniper said. "The Remnant. The ones who couldn't hold a shape. The country's going and they're going with it, and we're down here eating tasteless bread."

"So we open the door faster," I said. "Two keys. We have two keys. Maren said yes, Aldous said yes—"

"Aldous said yes to a river he doesn't understand yet. And the third key is in a grave the Remnant are digging up without us." Juniper turned the bottle in their hands. Rain hissed. Somewhere far inside it, the sleeping country murmured. "Tam, if the Erasure reaches the Gatehouse before we do—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that."

"I know *you*," I said, and meant it so hard it embarrassed us both. "You've oiled that door every week for nine years. It knows your hands. Nothing gets erased that you've touched. I've watched you prove it all night."

Juniper looked at me for a moment with an expression I'd never seen on them before — something young, almost startled, like a person hearing their own name called in a language they thought they'd forgotten.

Then the floor shuddered.

Not flickered — *shuddered*, a deep structural groan, stone settling the way it does before it stops bothering to stand, and every bottle on every surviving shelf rang once, softly, like struck glass, like bells, like a hundred small alarms. From the direction of the Gatehouse stairs came a sound I felt in my teeth: the great sealed door, nine years silent, groaning in its frame.

And beneath the groan, threading up through the foundations, thin and clear and unmistakable, came knocking.

Three knocks. A pause. Three knocks.

From the *other side*.
