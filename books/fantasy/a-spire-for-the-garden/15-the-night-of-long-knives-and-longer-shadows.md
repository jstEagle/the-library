## 15. The Night of Long Knives and Longer Shadows

I have burned a great many things in my career as an Inquisitor of the Crown. Ledgers, mostly. Letters, occasionally. Once, memorably, a man's entire collection of taxidermied songbirds, which he had stuffed with contraband saffron and which made the most extraordinary popping sounds as they went.

But I had never burned a country before tonight.

Let me be precise, because precision is the one virtue they cannot take from me: we did not burn Vessany. You cannot burn what does not exist. We burned its effigy—a scaffold-and-canvas thing my clerks assembled in the Gatehouse courtyard from guild records, guild furniture, guild pride—and we burned it while the people who kept that impossible country alive stood in rows and watched.

I want it noted that I gave them the courtesy of watching. Some of my colleagues would have blindfolded them. I find that wasteful. Fear you can see is fear that teaches.

* * *

The raid began, as all my best work does, with paperwork.

Three weeks of it, to be honest. Warrants drawn in triplicate, each one duller than the last, because the Crown's bureaucracy has an unshakable belief that tyranny should be legible. *Writ of Seizure, premises known as the Meridian Guild Gatehouse, on suspicion of trafficking in proscribed phenomena.* I signed it with the flourish I reserve for occasions when I am about to ruin someone's life before supper.

I brought forty men. That was vanity. Thirty would have sufficed for the arrests; the extra ten were for the courtyard, for torches, for theater. I have learned that an Inquisitor who neglects theater is an Inquisitor whose warnings are forgotten by Thursday. The black ribbon on the gate had been a whisper. Tonight would be a sentence.

We came at the hour when the kitchen fires die down and the watchmen yawn—half past eleven, by the guild's own clocks, which I had verified ran four minutes fast, because if you're going to destroy a place you ought at least to know what time it thinks it is.

The door was unlocked.

That detail still bothers me. Guildmaster Yew was many things—evasive, complicit, possessed of a smile like a locked drawer—but she was not careless. An unlocked door at half past eleven, on the night after her apprentice was seen buying lamp oil in quantities suitable for a siege, meant one of two things. Either she had given up.

Or she wanted us inside quickly, so that something else could happen outside.

I chose, at the time, to believe the former. I record here, privately, in the ledger I keep behind my own ribs where no auditor will ever find it, that I suspected the latter, and proceeded anyway, because a trap you walk into knowingly is called a decision.

* * *

Yew received us in the front hall wearing her good coat, the one with the silver thread at the collar, as though we were expected guests rather than the end of everything she'd built.

"Inquisitor Corvane," she said. "You've brought friends."

"I've brought the law. Friends are extra."

"Will there be reading?"

"There is always reading." I unfolded the writ and read it aloud, every clause, including the ones about penalties for obstruction, which I delivered with particular relish because Yew's eyes never left mine and I wanted her to understand that I understood that she understood. This is the true grammar of my profession. The words are almost incidental.

Her clerk—a round, frightened boy named Pellworth who smelled of ink and terror—took notes as though the seizure of his life's workplace were a particularly interesting sermon.

"The codex," I said, when the formalities were done. "You know the one. Old. Bound in something that isn't leather. Contains a country."

"Contains a country," Yew repeated. "What a phrase. You say it as though it were normal."

"Nothing about your establishment is normal, Guildmaster. That is rather the charge."

She gestured to her office with the weary grace of a woman opening her own veins. And there it was, exactly where my informants said it would be, beneath the floorboard under the third shelf—the Prophecy Codex, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling of dust and older air. My hands shook slightly when I lifted it. I'll admit that. Not from awe. From the specific vertigo of holding a book that should not exist and knowing, absolutely, that it did—that the world had a hole in it shaped like Vessany, and this book was the stitching around the wound.

"Careful," Yew said quietly. "It bites."

"Books don't bite."

"This one remembers being read aloud. It resents interruptions."

I looked at her then, really looked, and saw what I had been refusing to see for three weeks: she wasn't afraid. A woman watching her guild fall should be afraid. She was *waiting*, the way one waits for a kettle, and I felt the first cold finger of the evening touch the back of my neck.

"Where is Brightwater?" I asked.

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Gone," she said, "is a direction I don't trade in anymore."

* * *

They found Tam in the cellar corridor.

Not hiding—covering. The boy had wedged himself into the narrow passage behind the wine racks, the one that runs toward the old cistern stairs, and he'd done something clever and stupid with a tripwire, a shelf of bottled echoes, and a lantern. When Sergeant Halloway's squad came through, the shelf came down, and a dozen echoes broke at once.

I heard it from upstairs. Everyone heard it. Imagine a dozen small glass deaths all screaming their contents simultaneously—one echo of a wedding, one of a storm, one of someone laughing so hard they couldn't breathe, one that might have been a lullaby in a language none of us knew but all of us, briefly, grieved. The sound went through my men like a spear through wet paper. Hardened soldiers sat down on the cellar steps and put their heads in their hands.

And in the middle of it, Tam—sixteen, freckled, armed with nothing but a broken bottle and poor judgment—tried to run for the cistern door.

Halloway caught him. Halloway is gentle for an Inquisitorial sergeant, which is to say he only broke the boy's arm instead of his neck, and even that I suspect was more force than intention. I arrived to find Tam pinned against the stones, white-faced, cradling his left arm at an angle arms do not prefer, and still—this is the part I keep returning to—still positioning himself between us and the cistern stair.

"You're too late anyway," he said. His voice cracked on *anyway*. "You're too late, you're too late, you're too late."

"Am I?" I crouched to his level. I have found that children lie better standing and confess better sitting; something about the change in altitude rearranges their courage. "Too late for what, Master Tam?"

He shut his mouth with an audible click and glared at me with a hatred so pure and so young it was nearly a pleasure to receive it. Nobody looks at you like that unless they believe you can be defeated. Adults have learned better. I envied him the lesson he hadn't learned yet.

"Bind the arm," I told Halloway. "Properly. He comes with us alive."

"You're taking him?" Yew had appeared in the cellar doorway, and for the first time all night her composure showed a seam. "He's a child. He's an apprentice. He knows nothing worth knowing."

"He knows where Brightwater went."

"He knows nothing," she repeated, too fast, too flat.

And there it was. The seam widened. Yew, who had lied to me beautifully for three weeks about taxes and inventories and the provenance of certain bottles, could not lie about a bleeding boy, and I filed the tell away the way I file everything: carefully, guiltily, and forever.

* * *

The effigy burning was my idea, and I stand by it, and I am aware that standing by it may be the worst thing I ever do.

Here is the reasoning I presented to myself, in the tidy courtroom of my skull: the guild's power is secrecy. Secrecy dies in daylight. Therefore drag the guild's secrets into a courtyard, build them into a shape, and set them alight where the whole quarter can see. Let the neighbors watch the impossible things go up in smoke—let them see that the Meridian Guild is only wood and canvas and paperwork after all. Fear of the Crown fades. Fear of fire spreads.

It was excellent reasoning. It was also, I now understand, a mistake of the kind that reshapes careers and countries, because I had failed to account for one variable:

Juniper Brightwater.

* * *

They brought the prisoners out to watch—Yew in irons, Tam with his splinted arm bound across his chest, Orla the cook, who had been taken in the kitchens without incident and without a word, though she did pause at the courtyard door to look back at her ovens with an expression I can only describe as a mother leaving a sleeping child. The other staff, the ones who weren't essential, I released with warnings. Mercy is cheap and buys goodwill I may need later. Also, someone has to spread the story, and frightened employees spread it best.

The effigy stood twelve feet tall in the center of the courtyard. My clerks had built it from the guild's own confiscated furnishings: Yew's desk for the torso, shelving for limbs, and for the head—this was Clerk Fenwick's inspiration, and I permitted it, and I regret permitting it—a great round bottle-glass window frame, so that the firelight would shine through it like a face.

Around its neck we hung a placard: *HERE STOOD THE MERIDIAN GUILD, TRADER IN LIES.*

Torches went in at midnight.

It caught beautifully. Of course it did; we'd oiled it. The flames climbed the shelving limbs and swallowed the desk-torso and roared up into the bottle-glass face until the whole head was a lantern, and the heat pressed against our faces like a hand, and my men cheered, because men always cheer, because cheering is easier than thinking.

And Juniper Brightwater stood at the edge of the crowd and watched.

I don't know how long they'd been there. They hadn't been in the building—we searched it twice, floorboard by floorboard, and found nothing but absence shaped exactly like them. But there they were at the courtyard wall, in that grey coat of theirs, half-lost in shadow, and they did not run when the light found them. They did not weep or curse or beg. They watched their whole world burn in miniature with the stillness of someone counting.

I know that stillness. I wore it for years, early in my career, before I learned to perform feeling instead of suppressing it. It is not calm. It is arithmetic. It is a person calculating exactly what has been taken from them and arriving, line by line, at a total.

Our eyes met across the fire.

I raised my hand—not a threat, I want that recorded, something more like a salute, or perhaps an apology wearing a salute's clothing—and mouthed, *Where is the door?*

Juniper Brightwater looked at me across the burning shape of everything they loved, and their expression did something I had not seen it do in all our interviews, all our careful dances, all the lies they told me so well I'd half believed them.

They smiled.

It was not a kind smile. It was not afraid. It was the smile of someone who has stopped needing to convince you of anything, and it said, as clearly as words: *You're late. You're too late. You just haven't counted yet.*

Then they stepped backward into the dark between two houses, and by the time my men reached the wall there was nothing there but a smell of rain that hadn't fallen.

* * *

I could not sleep afterward. This is rare enough to be worth recording; I sleep the way I audit, thoroughly and without sentiment.

I sat in my office with the codex on my desk and a decanter at my elbow and I told myself the night had gone well. The guild was broken. Yew was in a cell. The codex was seized. The boy was patched and talking—or would be, once Halloway applied the gentler arts. The city would wake to ash and rumor and the certain knowledge that the Crown sees everything.

And yet.

The unlocked door. Yew waiting for the kettle. Tam guarding a stair to nowhere with a broken bottle. Brightwater smiling at me over a fire like a banker watching a rival's note come due.

They had wanted us inside. Every piece of it—the open door, the empty building, the prisoners who surrendered too easily—had been arranged so that we would be *here*, occupied, triumphant, burning furniture, while somewhere beneath or beyond or through the Gatehouse, the actual work went forward without us. I had raided a shell. I had arrested the scenery. Somewhere in the dark, Juniper Brightwater was doing whatever it is that a keeper of doors does when they finally decide to open one.

I pulled the codex toward me and opened it to the page my informants described, the page with the name and the date, and I read it again by candlelight, and this time I noticed what I had missed in my triumph: the prophecy did not end with the name. There was a line beneath it, in a different hand, newer, smaller, pressed so lightly into the vellum it was nearly breath:

*The hinge turns both ways.*

Both ways. A door that opens is a door that lets things out. But a door that opens is also a door that lets things *in*.

I sat with that for a long time while the decanter emptied, and somewhere past two in the morning I set down my glass and admitted, to the candle, to the codex, to whatever portion of me still kept honest books: I did not want to destroy the door.

I wanted to see what was on the other side.

God help me. I wanted to see Vessany.

I rang for my clerk and began, by candlelight, to write new orders. Not for destruction. For pursuit.
