## 16. The Door Out of Nowhere

The stairs beneath the Gatehouse had never existed until tonight. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the last thing I own that still works.

For nine years I catalogued the cellar below the guild's counting room: forty-one shelves, a damp corner where the bottled echoes sulked, a rat I named Chancellor because he had the temperament for it. There was no door in that cellar. There was no stair going down from it. And yet here we were, Tam bleeding through my shirt into my ribs, Maren Ostrow's key warm in my left pocket like a coin fresh from a hand, and a staircase unspooling ahead of us into dark that smelled—impossibly, absurdly—of cut grass and rain on hot stone.

"Chancellor's gone," Tam said, which was not what I expected them to say.

"What?"

"The rat. His shelf dissolved an hour ago. He just—" They made a small gesture with their good arm, a kind of opening of fingers, like a magician showing the trick had no trick in it. "He was there and then the shelf wasn't and he wasn't either. Juniper, I'm sorry. I know he was yours."

"He was nobody's. He was a rat." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Rats don't get eulogies."

"Everything gets a eulogy now," Tam said. "That's sort of the whole situation."

I couldn't argue. The whole situation, as Tam put it, was this: three days ago the codex had been read aloud by Inquisitor Corvane in a voice like honey poured over a knife, and ever since, Vessany—the erased country, the impossible country, the country that lived inside these walls the way a song lives inside a throat—had been coming apart at its seams. Rooms flickering back to empty lots. Stock dissolving mid-inventory. Orla's bread coming out of the oven tasting of nothing at all, which for Orla was worse than burning.

And Yew was gone. Taken. The codex seized. Tam's shoulder opened up by an Inquisitorial blade meant for my throat.

So: down the stairs that shouldn't exist, toward the true door, carrying two keys out of three and one apprentice who kept insisting they were fine in the tone of voice people use when they are actively not fine.

"You're dripping," I said.

"I'm *dripping*," Tam agreed cheerfully. "It's very dramatic. If I die down here you have to put it in a book."

"I don't write books. I write inventory."

"Then list me. *One apprentice, slightly used, heroic.*"

"*Slightly*," I said, "is doing a lot of work in that sentence," and Tam laughed, and then hissed, and then went quiet, and the quiet was worse than the joke, so I put my hand on their back and kept us both moving.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the building did. I want you to understand how much that sentence costs me to say. I am a keeper of ledgers. I believe in floors resting on foundations resting on bedrock resting on the patient indifference of the earth. But the staircase descended past the depth of the Gatehouse, past the depth of the city, past any depth the city had ever agreed to have, and the walls changed as we went—from guild brick, to older brick, to stone, to something that wasn't stone at all but pressed earth with roots running through it, roots that belonged to trees I had never seen and somehow knew the names of.

"Juniper." Tam's voice had gone strange. "I know those roots."

"You can't know those roots."

"They're plum trees. The ones behind—" They stopped on the step. Their face, in the light of the lamp I carried, was doing something complicated. "Behind my grandmother's house. Except I've never had a grandmother's house. I made her up. I told everyone she lived in the hills and made plum brandy and I used to describe it so much I half believed it, and Juniper, I think I was describing *this*. I think all of us were. I think every invented grandmother in this city was Vessany."

We stood there a moment with that. Somewhere above us, the city that wasn't real continued not being real, four hundred thousand people living inside a lie none of them knew they were telling, and every one of those lies leaking downward through roots toward a door.

"Keep moving," I said gently. "You can grieve your fictional grandmother at the bottom."

"She'd have wanted it that way," Tam said, wet-eyed and grinning, and we went on.

* * *

The door was smaller than I expected.

That sounds wrong, I know. You spend your whole life tending a sealed gate, oiling its hinges, keeping its ledgers, learning its moods the way you learn a horse's—and then you find the true door and it's *small*. Waist-high if you're generous. Set into the earthen wall like a door into a root cellar, wooden, iron-banded, with a latch instead of a lock. No grandeur. No spire. Just a door, the kind you'd find at the back of a garden, the kind you prop open in summer so the smell of the beans can get in.

Two keys fit into two keyholes I hadn't noticed until Maren's brass key and Aldous's river-iron key were in my hands. The wood around the keyholes was worn smooth, polished by centuries of hands, and I understood suddenly that this door had been opened and closed ten thousand times before anyone decided it needed to be shut forever.

"Are we doing this?" Tam whispered.

"No," I said. "We are standing here breathing for another minute, because once we do this there's no minute after it that's the same." I looked at their shoulder, at the blood soaking through the bandage Orla had tied with the fierce competence of a woman who has stitched worse wounds on worse nights. "How bad?"

"It's fine."

"Tam."

"It's *fine*, Juniper. It's a scratch with ambitions." They tried to smile. "Open the door. Please. I need to see it before—" They didn't finish. Before the country finished dissolving. Before the flickering reached the stairs. Before whatever was going to happen to them happened. I didn't make them say it. Some mercies are just knowing when to stop asking questions.

I turned the keys.

They turned together, the way keys do in dreams, without resistance, without ceremony, and the latch clicked—a small sound, a kitchen sound—and the door swung inward, and light came out.

Not lamplight. Not the gray nothing-light of Vessany-that-wasn't. *Sunlight.* Actual sunlight, gold and slanting, the late-afternoon light of a day in early autumn, and it fell across my boots on the packed-earth floor and I swear to you the dust in it moved the way dust moves in a room where someone is happy.

Beyond the doorframe: orchards. Rows of plum trees heavy with fruit, grass between them gone long and seed-headed, a low stone wall, and beyond the wall a town with red roofs and a church spire and laundry strung between windows, snapping gently in a wind that had waited a hundred years to touch anything.

Vessany. Not the memory of it. Not the echo of it. Vessany *as it was*, preserved behind a door the size of a garden gate, sunlit and intact and utterly indifferent to the fact that it had been erased from every map and every mind outside these walls.

Tam made a sound I will never be able to describe properly. Something between a laugh and the noise a person makes when a wound stops hurting. "The plums," they said. "The plums are *real*."

And on the far side, among the trees, waiting—

People.

Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe; the orchard light made it hard to count. They stood in loose rows like travelers on a platform watching for a train that is finally, finally announced. Translucent. That was the word Yew had used and it was right: you could see the plum branches through some of them, faintly, the way you see a candle flame through a cupped hand. They were patient. They were polite even in their waiting—you could feel the politeness, the way you feel weather. And every single one of them was looking at the door.

At me.

An old man in the front row took off his hat. That undid me more than anything else could have. A hundred years erased, a century of fading, and his first act upon seeing the door open was *manners*.

"We didn't dig up Sera Ilves," he said, and his voice came through clear as a bell over clean water. "I want you to know that. We stopped. When we heard the girl was hurt. We put everything back." He held up soil-darkened hands as if to show they were empty. "We would have asked first. We always meant to ask first. We only ever wanted to go home, and home was *here*, and the key was *there*, and we are sorry. We are so sorry about the girl."

"She's not—" Tam started, and I put a hand on their arm.

"She'll live," I said. "She's stubborn. She takes after people who aren't her family, apparently, except maybe they all are."

The old man smiled, and behind him the rows shifted, and I saw faces I had seen in the codex illustrations, faces I had seen in dreams I'd spent nine years refusing to examine. A woman holding a baby wrapped in cloth that was more memory than cloth. A boy who looked like Tam would look if Tam had been allowed to grow up somewhere with sun.

"Gatekeeper," the old man said.

"Don't call me that."

"What should we call you?"

And I stood there in the doorway with the smell of ripe plums rolling over me and the truth sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal—that I was born on one side of this door and smuggled through the other side of it; that the mother whose voice I remembered in fragments was probably standing in one of these rows or buried under one of these trees; that I was not the keeper of the gate but the hinge of it, the fourth key, the thing the door actually swung on—and I said:

"Nobody. For now. Nobody works."

The old man nodded as if that were a perfectly reasonable name. Maybe in Vessany it was.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about awe: it has a time limit. You get perhaps a minute of pure wonder, the world cracked open and the light pouring in, and then the ledger part of your brain clears its throat and starts itemizing the bill.

Because the Remnant were fading. Even now, even in the doorway's light, I watched it happen—an old woman in the third row went briefly transparent to the wrists, caught herself, solidified, and pretended it hadn't happened, the way people pretend not to stumble. The door being open helped them; I could see that, see them drinking the nearness of home like water. But the door alone wasn't enough. Two keys turned. The mechanism needed three. And the third key was in a grave that the Remnant had very politely started digging and then very politely stopped, and Yew—who knew where, who had always known everything and told me almost none of it—was in an Inquisitor's cell somewhere across a city that was currently busy forgetting itself.

"Juniper." Tam's voice. Wrong. Flat and small.

I turned. Tam was leaning against the earthen wall, and their hand—their *left* hand, the one farthest from the door—was translucent at the fingertips.

No. No, that wasn't—Tam wasn't Vessany-born, Tam was guild-raised, Tam was a made-up grandmother and plum brandy and a hundred small inventions, and I realized even as I thought it that that was exactly the problem. Tam was *made of Vessany*. All of them were. Every invented grandmother, every half-remembered lullaby, every dish Orla cooked from recipes she claimed came from her mother and had actually leaked up through the floorboards of a country that didn't exist. The Erasure wasn't just taking the walls. It was taking the people the walls had held.

"Hey," Tam said, looking at their own fading fingers with an expression of scholarly interest that fooled neither of us. "Neat."

"Sit down."

"Sitting down doesn't fix erasure, Juniper."

"Sit down anyway." I got them onto the floor with their back against the wall, facing the door, facing the light. "Listen to me. We need the third key. Yew knows where it is. Yew is in Corvane's custody, which means getting to her means walking into the Inquisition's front parlor, which means—" I stopped, because the next part of the sentence was *which means someone has to distract the most dangerous man in the city while we pull our guildmaster out*, and we both knew who the distraction was going to be, and we both knew I was the only one left to be it.

"Orla," Tam said. "Someone has to tell Orla."

"I know."

"And the Remnant can't cross yet. Right? The door's not—it needs all three?"

"Needs all three," the old man confirmed quietly, from the other side. He had come closer, close enough that I could see the stitching on his hatband, the specific wear of a specific life. "And it needs the hinge. It needs you, child. But not yet. Not until the key comes home." He looked at Tam with an expression of such gentleness that I had to look away. "The young one should come away from the door. The door takes, when it's incomplete. It can't help it. It's starving."

"Come on," I said, and hauled Tam up, and we retreated up the stairs with the sunlight spilling after us like something reluctant to let go, and at the top I looked back once.

The old man raised his hat again. Behind him, the whole orchard of the dead-and-fading stood in their rows, patient, polite, watching their door begin to swing shut on them for the second time in a century.

"Hurry," he said. Not a command. A request. From a man who had spent a hundred years being sorry for wanting the one thing anyone would want.

The door closed. The stairs began, slowly, to forget themselves—the topmost step going soft and lot-like under my boot—and I climbed faster, with Tam's breath ragged against my shoulder and one thought repeating in my skull with the dull rhythm of a stamp on a ledger page:

Corvane has Yew. Corvane has the codex. Corvane wants the door destroyed, and I am about to walk into his glass house and ask him for a favor.

Somewhere above us, faintly, the dinner bell in Orla's kitchen rang once—though there was no one left in the kitchen to ring it—and stopped.
