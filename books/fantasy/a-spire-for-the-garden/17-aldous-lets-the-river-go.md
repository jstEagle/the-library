## 17. Aldous Lets the River Go

I have kept the toll on the Vell for forty years, and in all that time I have never once seen the river freeze.

Other rivers freeze. The Marrow upriver freezes so hard the ice-sellers come out with their saws and their sledges and their blue-lipped laughter. The canal by the tannery freezes thick enough that boys skate on it and fall through it and are fished out and scolded. But the Vell runs green and unhurried under my bridge summer and winter alike, and I have long since stopped remarking on it, the way you stop remarking on your own heartbeat.

You can get used to anything. That is the whole of my philosophy, if I have one. You can get used to a wife who hums off-key. You can get used to grief. You can get used to waking at dawn because the dawn bell has rung every morning of your life, even though you cannot remember anyone ever being hired to ring it.

My name is Aldous Finch. I am sixty-one years old. I collect two coppers from carts, one from riders, nothing from children or priests or soldiers, and I have never once wondered why the toll schedule was posted in a language I could not read until someone told me it said what I already knew it said.

That last part is new. That last part arrived three days ago with a young person named Juniper Brightwater, who stood at the foot of my bridge with rain in their hair and an apology already loaded like a crossbow bolt, and asked me—politely, terribly politely—whether I had ever wondered where the water went.

* * *

Here is what I knew before Juniper came:

I knew the bridge. Stone, seven arches, moss on the northern balustrade that I scraped away every spring and which always came back by midsummer, patient as sin. I built it—or rather, I had always been building it; there was no day I could point to and say, there, that is when the first stone went down. It was simply mine, the way a scar is yours.

I knew Elowen. Forty-one years married before the fever took her, and I buried her on the rise above the eastern bank, under the rowan, because she liked rowans and disliked crowds and the churchyard was always crowded. I visited her every Sunday with a hip flask of plum brandy I poured into the roots, and I talked to her about the weather and the tolls and the boy who fell in the river in my thirty-second year and would have drowned if the current hadn't, quite gently, set him back against the pilings where I could reach him.

I knew the river. Green, slow, deep in the middle, shallow over the gravel shoals where the herons fished. It smelled of stone and weed and, faintly, of apples. I never thought much about the apples. Rivers don't smell of apples. Ours did. I noticed this the way you notice a picture hanging crooked in a room you've lived in forever: eventually your eye slides past it, and then one day somebody new comes into the room and says, "Why is that crooked?" and you can never unsee it again.

Juniper Brightwater was that somebody.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the light does that thing on the water—the gold going long and thin, the way it does, the way it has always done—and they didn't ask for passage. They just leaned on the balustrade beside me and looked at the river the way I look at ledgers, which is to say like something that might lie to them.

"You're the toll-keeper," they said.

"Forty years."

"Do you remember your first day?"

And here is the thing I want written down plainly, because whatever else happens, I want someone to know I am not a fool: I heard the trap in the question. I felt it the way you feel a step missing on a stair you've climbed ten thousand times. My whole life, I realized, was a staircase with steps missing, and I had learned to climb it in the dark without stumbling, and this child with rain in their hair had just turned on a lamp.

"No," I said. "I don't."

Most people would have let it go. Juniper Brightwater sat down on the cold stone next to me, which no one does, the stone is wet and nobody ever sits on my bridge, and said, "Neither do I. My first day, I mean. At the Gatehouse. There's a hole where it should be, and I've spent nine years not looking at it, because looking felt dangerous." They turned and looked at me with eyes the color of the river in November. "But it turns out the danger was in the not-looking. Sir—I'm sorry—I have to show you something, and it's going to hurt, and I will sit here as long as you need after."

I should have sent them off. I was sixty-one years old and I had earned my quiet the way you earn anything, by paying for it daily. Instead I said, "Show me, then," because some part of me—the part that smelled apples in the river and never asked why—had been waiting forty years for exactly this conversation.

They took a bottle out of their coat. Small, green glass, corked with wax. Inside was rain. Not water—rain. You could hear it before they even uncorked it, a sound like a roof over your head in a country you've never seen, and when they pulled the wax free the sound came out and filled the whole arch of the bridge.

It wasn't our rain. Our rain comes off the hills, all business, in and out in an hour. This rain took its time. This rain fell on leaves—broad leaves, orchard leaves—and on tiles, and on something wooden and old, and underneath it, very far under, someone was singing. Off-key.

Elowen used to sing off-key.

I don't remember standing up. I remember the bottle in my hands, both hands, the way you hold a bird. I remember saying, "Where is this," and my voice coming out in a register I hadn't used since her funeral, and Juniper saying, "Vessany," and the word going through me like a hook through cloth.

Because I knew it. Not the name—I'd never heard the name—but the sound. The rain knew me. That's the only way I can put it. The rain fell the way it falls in a place that has been waiting for you specifically, and I stood on my own bridge in my own evening and wept like a man being greeted.

* * *

They told me the rest of it gently, over the next hour, sitting on the wet stone while the light died. About the guild. About the door. About a country erased a hundred years ago—not destroyed, erased, unwritten, folded up like a letter and tucked inside four walls—and about the people still in it, fading, patient, polite. About how the guild's founders scattered themselves into ordinary lives to become keys, and how one of those founders was a man who became a bridge-keeper on a river he believed he'd always known.

"The river isn't real," Juniper said, and flinched at their own words. "I'm sorry. That's cruel. What I mean is—it's real, but it isn't ours. It's a piece of the Vessany you carried out. You've been holding it for forty years, Mr. Finch. Holding it up. That's why it never freezes. That's why it smells of apples. That's why the current set that boy back against the pilings. It's not a river. It's a memory keeping itself alive, and you're the hinge it hangs on."

I looked at the water for a long time.

Forty years. Two coppers from carts, one from riders, nothing from children. The moss that always came back. The dawn bell with no ringer. Elowen, humming off-key, planting rowans. Every Sunday, brandy into the roots, talking to her about weather and tolls, and her grave on the rise, and—

"Her grave," I said.

Juniper was quiet.

"Is she real? Is she—" My voice did something disgraceful. "Is she part of it? Did I make her? Is my whole marriage a—a prop?"

"I don't know," they said, and I believed them, because it was the first thing they'd said that didn't sound rehearsed. "The codex doesn't say. Yew doesn't know. Some of what you built might be truly yours. Grief is real wherever it grows, Mr. Finch. But I won't lie to you: I don't know, and I'm sorry, and asking you to do this anyway is the cruelest thing I've ever done to another person, and I'll carry that."

Well. There it is, I thought. There's the bill, presented at last, after forty years of credit.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat in my toll-house with the lamp lit and the rain-bottle uncorked on the table, letting the sound of that other country fill the little room, and I took inventory.

That is what we keepers do, apparently. Juniper keeps ledgers for impossible goods; I suppose I keep one too, the kind you carry behind your eyes. So I counted.

One bridge, seven arches, mine beyond any deed. One river, unfreezing, apple-scented, possibly borrowed. One wife, buried under a rowan, beloved beyond the reach of any erasure—of that I am certain, whatever else is true. One flask, half full of plum brandy. One life, sixty-one years, of which perhaps twenty were mine entire and the rest were held in trust for people I'd never met.

And here is the entry I keep coming back to, the one I wrote deepest: somewhere on the far side of a door, there are people fading. Polite ghosts, Juniper called them, terrifyingly courteous, waiting at a station with their luggage packed for a hundred years. And the only reason they're still waiting—still anything at all—is that an old fool on a bridge kept a river running for them without knowing it.

You can get used to anything. But I found, sitting there, that I did not want to get used to being a wall. I had been a husband. I had been a keeper. I could be a key.

* * *

In the morning I did what needed doing first, and that was Elowen.

I walked up the rise with the flask and the rain-bottle and I sat with her under the rowan, which was in berry, red as a warning. I told her everything. All of it—the guild, the door, the erased country, the child with rain in their hair, the river that wasn't a river. I told her the way I told her everything, plainly, because she had no patience for a man circling a hard thing.

"And here's the question, love," I said. "If you weren't real—if you were something I built to keep myself company in a stolen life—then none of it matters less than it seems to matter. Because I loved you. Whatever I am, whoever made me, the loving happened. That's mine. Nobody can erase that; I've decided. I'm deciding it right now, sitting here, and I dare the universe to object."

The wind moved in the rowan. Berries clicked together. It sounded almost like her laugh, which had always been more breath than sound.

"But if you were real," I said, "truly real, then maybe you were Vessany too. Maybe we were both smuggled out in different pockets of the same theft. In which case, love, I'll see you on the other side of the door, and you can tell me off properly for taking forty years to find my way home."

I poured the brandy into the roots. Then, because it seemed right, I uncorked the rain-bottle and set it on the grass between us, and we listened to Vessany's rain fall on its orchards together, the two of us, for the last time on this side of anything.

When the sound ran out I corked the bottle and stood, and my knees cracked the way sixty-one-year-old knees crack, and I said, "Right then," to her, and, "Right then," to myself, and went down to the bridge to give it back.

* * *

Juniper was there at dawn, with the apprentice—Tam, all elbows and earnestness, carrying a satchel like it contained the sun—and a woman I hadn't met, gray-haired, arms crossed, introduced only as Maren, who looked at the river with an expression I recognized: the look of someone seeing a debt.

"You don't have to watch this," I told them.

"Yes, we do," said Maren, shortly. "We owe you."

So they stood at the foot of the bridge, and I walked out onto it, alone, the way I had walked it ten thousand mornings, except this time I was counting the arches the way you count a friend's face before a long journey.

Seven arches. Moss on the north balustrade. The gravel shoals where the herons stood, patient as clerks. The middle deeps, green and slow, smelling faintly of apples. Forty years of dawns. The boy set gently against the pilings. Elowen's ashes-warm ghost on the eastern bank.

"I carried you," I said to the river. "I didn't know it, but I did, and I'd do it again, and I'm not sorry, and that's the truth of it. But you belong to them. You've always belonged to them. So go home, love. Go home and be a river again, a whole one, with a source and a mouth and fish in it and winters that freeze it proper. Go home and be somebody's whole country instead of one old man's hiding place."

I put my hands on the balustrade. The stone was warm. It had always been warm; I'd blamed the sun.

"Now," I said, "and thank you for everything."

I don't know what I expected—an earthquake, a flood, some grand unspooling. What happened was quieter and worse and better. The river sighed. There's no other word for it. The whole length of it, bank to bank, drew one long breath like a tired man finally sitting down, and the green went out of the water, and the apple-smell rose up once, strong, like a hand waving from a departing coach, and then the Vell simply—left. Not drained. Left. The water went the way a crowd goes when a festival ends, gradually and then all at once, and what remained was the bed of it: stones, and gravel, and, here and there, small things glinting that I chose not to examine. Coins, maybe. Or teeth. Or seeds.

And under everything, faint as breath on glass, the shape of orchard rows, laid out along the valley floor like a sleeping body, waiting to be remembered back into bloom.

I stood on my bridge over no river at all, and I laughed. I couldn't help it. Forty years of solemnity and it ended with me giggling like Tam there, because the bridge still stood, and the toll-house still stood, and the sun came up the same as ever, and it turns out you can lose an entire river and the world keeps its appointments.

"Well," I said, turning around. My voice held. I was proud of that. "That's that, then. Someone owes me two coppers. There was a cart due at noon."

Maren was crying and pretending not to. Tam was staring at the empty riverbed with their mouth open. And Juniper Brightwater came out across the stones and took my arm, careful, the way you take the arm of something valuable, and said, "Mr. Finch—are you—"

"Aldous," I said. "If we're doing this, it's Aldous. And I'm better than I've been in forty years, though I grant you that's a low bar, given I now appear to be a bridge-keeper with no river, which is a profession somewhere between priest and fraud." I patted their hand. "Come on, then. Where's this door of yours? I've a wife to find, or a grave to finish grieving, and either way I mean to be smiling when I get there."

We started walking. Behind us the empty valley lay open under the morning, all its secrets showing, and ahead of us the road bent toward the city, toward the Gatehouse, toward whatever was burning there now—I had heard the bells, everyone had heard the bells, and Juniper's face when I mentioned them was answer enough.

They walked fast, that one, with the apprentice limping and Maren setting a pace like a woman collecting on a loan, and I matched them step for step, an old man with nothing left to carry but himself, which is, I have found, the lightest luggage there is.

At the top of the hill I stopped once and looked back. Just once. The bridge stood black and beautiful over its absence, and on the rise the rowan burned red, and I raised my hand to both of them—to the stone and the tree, to the life and the love—and then I turned my back on forty years and walked toward the door, smiling, as promised.

Somewhere ahead, the bells had stopped ringing.

That frightened me more than anything.
