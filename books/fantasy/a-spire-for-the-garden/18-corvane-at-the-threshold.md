## 18. Corvane at the Threshold

I have burned a great many things in my career. Ledgers, mostly. A chapel once, though the paperwork on that was so tedious I've tried to forget it. You learn, as an Inquisitor, that fire is the honest tool: it doesn't negotiate, it doesn't weep, it doesn't ask you to reconsider. Fire simply agrees with you.

I had come to the Gatehouse with a cask of alchemist's fire and a warrant signed in triplicate, and I want it on the record that my hand did not shake when I signed the requisition for the cask. My hand was perfectly steady. It was the rest of me that had begun, quietly and without authorization, to come apart.

The courtyard was still smoking from the raid. Two nights ago my people had taken Guildmaster Yew in irons, seized the codex, and put a crossbow bolt through an apprentice who had the poor judgment to be brave. Standard work. Clean work. I'd stood in that same courtyard and watched the effigy of the guild burn—straw and painted canvas, a guildmaster with a smile stitched on wrong—and I had felt the particular satisfaction of a job approaching its end.

So why, you ask, was I standing alone at the top of a staircase that hadn't existed two nights ago, beneath the ruins of a Gatehouse that was supposed to be rubble, holding a lantern instead of a torch?

Because the rubble had opened. That was the trouble. We had pulled the Gatehouse down to its bones, and the bones had turned out to be a throat. The floor of the cellar had split along a seam no survey had ever recorded, and the stairs went down and down into a dark that my lantern didn't so much illuminate as apologize to. And from the bottom of those stairs came light—warm, gold, unhurried light, the kind that belongs to late afternoons in countries that no longer exist.

I am not a superstitious man. Superstition is what the Crown calls religion when it can't tax it. But I know architecture, and architecture was telling me something impossible: this door had been here longer than the guild above it. Longer than the city. The stone of the threshold was worn into a shallow crescent by feet, millions of feet, all of them passing through in the same direction.

I stood there a long moment, being a reasonable man. I recited the statute. *Any aperture, gate, or way between the world and that which has been excised from the world is hereby declared a wound in the Crown's body, and the closing of such wounds is the sacred office of the Inquisition.* I had helped write the modern phrasing, actually. I'd been rather proud of the word *excised*. It has a surgical cleanliness to it. It lets you pretend you're a physician.

Then I went down the stairs, because I am, whatever else may be said of me, thorough.

* * *

The door was open.

I want you to understand what that meant, mechanically, because I didn't, not yet. Nine years a keeper had oiled that door and kept it shut, and two founders' keys had turned in it, and a third had been dug out of a grave by the patient dead, and a child of Vessany had put their hand to the wood and asked it, politely, to remember. All of that had happened in the days I'd spent chasing paperwork and burning effigies. While I was being thorough, the world had gone ahead and changed.

The door stood open, and through it came a country.

Not a view of a country. A country. The light spilled through and it had weight and smell—cut grass, woodsmoke, apples ripening past their moment, the green mineral breath of a river in summer. Through the doorway I could see an orchard going up a hillside in rows, and beyond the orchard a town with slate roofs the color of storm clouds, and a spire—of course a spire, they always build the spire first, it's how a country announces itself—and the whole of it lit by a sun that hung at four in the afternoon and showed no interest in moving.

And the people. They stood on the far side of the threshold in a loose crowd, the way travelers stand at a station when the train is late and no one wants to say so. Translucent. That was the word the reports used, and it was accurate and useless. They were translucent the way a window is translucent—you see through them and what you mostly see is what they're looking at. And they were looking at me.

I have faced down chancellors, heretics, a wyrm in the Fenmarshes that the local priests insisted was a saint. I have never been looked at the way they looked at me. There was no hatred in it. There was no fear. It was the look you give a door when you have been standing outside in the cold for a hundred years, and someone has finally, finally turned the handle.

"Master Corvane."

The gatekeeper was there—Brightwater, the ledger-keeper, the nobody who had turned out to be the hinge of the whole century. They stood beside the door with a bandage on their arm and an expression I couldn't file under any of my usual headings. Not defiance. Not pleading. Something worse. Recognition.

"You shouldn't be here," they said.

"I have a warrant," I said, and heard how it sounded, and pressed on anyway, because a man must have something to hold. "The door is to be destroyed. The wound closed. You know the law. You've kept the ledgers; you've read the law."

"I've kept the ledgers," Brightwater said. "That's rather different."

I set the lantern down. I put my hand on the cask of alchemist's fire. This is the part of the story where, in the reports I would eventually be required to write, I performed my duty. I want to be honest with you, whoever you are, reading this in some archive after I'm dust: I tried. I stood in front of the open door of Vessany with enough fire to unmake it, and I tried to be the instrument the Crown had spent forty years honing.

I looked at the orchard. I thought: *one cask, well-placed, and the hillside burns. The town follows. The Remnant scatter like smoke and the wound closes and the statute is satisfied and my name goes in the annals next to the word excised.*

And an old woman in the crowd stepped forward.

* * *

She moved the way old women move when they have decided that dignity is a luxury for people with more time. She came right up to the threshold, close enough that the light of my lantern and the light of her impossible afternoon met on the worn stone between us, and she looked at me, and she smiled.

I knew that smile.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last discipline I had left. I did not know her face—not the whole of it, not the way you know a face you see at market. I knew the smile. I knew the particular asymmetry of it, the way the left corner rose higher, the way it crinkled not the eyes but the space just under the brows, the way it arrived a half-second before she seemed to decide to give it. I had seen that smile over a kitchen table in a house that smelled of caraway and coal smoke. I had seen it turned on a boy who was trying very hard to look serious in his first uniform.

"Your grandmother made bread the way the old country made it," she said. Her voice was thin as paper but it carried, the way voices carry in places where there has been long practice of listening. "With a little rye in it, and a cross scored on the bottom where no one would see. She learned that from her mother, who learned it in Vessany, in a town called Ilves' Rest, before the Erasure took the town and left the recipe in her hands like a stone from a demolished house."

The courtyard of my childhood opened inside me like a trapdoor.

My grandmother, Ansa. Dead thirty years. A woman who never spoke of where she came from, who went quiet as a held breath whenever the Erasure was mentioned, who taught me to read from a book with no publication line, who sang songs in a language she claimed was "just nonsense, a nonsense she'd picked up," nonsense that made her cry, gently, every time, without her ever appearing to notice.

"You're lying," I said. It came out at the volume of a boy, not an Inquisitor.

"Ask me what she called you," the old woman said.

I didn't ask. I stood there with my hand on a cask of fire and my whole life rearranging itself behind my eyes, and I didn't ask, because I was afraid—and I was right to be afraid—that she would say *Koru*. My grandmother's word, her private nonsense-word, the name she used when I fell and skinned my knee, the name she used the last time I saw her, when I was nineteen and in uniform and too important to sit with an old woman's hands folded in her lap. It means, in the language that is just nonsense, *little hinge*. I learned that much, later, from Brightwater's codex. A small door-fitting. The piece that lets a thing swing open.

She said it anyway. Of course she said it. I had walked into the one room in the world where my name was still remembered, and I had brought fire.

"Ansa was my cousin," the old woman said. "We were girls together in Ilves' Rest. I stood in the crowd the day the door was sealed and I watched her go through with a baby on her hip, and the door closed, and a hundred years went by, and I thought—I thought, all that time, that she had made it out. That somewhere on the other side, the line went on. And then you walked through that door wearing her smile like a borrowed coat, and holding fire." She looked at the cask. She did not flinch. None of them flinched. That was the worst of it, the thing I still cannot make peace with: not one of them begged. "So," she said. "Little hinge. What will you do?"

* * *

Here is what I did. I did nothing. I stood in the doorway of the erased country with my grandmother's name in my mouth and my grandmother's smile six feet away wearing a stranger's face, and I did nothing, and it was the longest nothing of my life.

I thought about the statute. The statute is a good statute. I have defended it in the chancellery with my actual voice, which I have never once raised, because a raised voice is a concession. The statute exists because a hundred years ago a country died—not conquered, not ceded, *erased*, unwritten by powers the histories no longer name, and the unwriting did not stop at the border. It kept reaching. It would have kept reaching into the world like water into a cracked hull, had not someone sealed the last door and paid whatever price sealing costs. Every year the Crown spends blood and gold maintaining the seals, the quiet ones, the ones nobody knows about, and every Inquisitor learns early that the alternative to our work is not peace. It is the slow leak of nowhere into everywhere.

That is what I believe. I want that on the record too, because I know what comes next in this account, and I don't want anyone thinking I was always this.

But the statute was written by men who had never stood in the doorway. The statute says *wound*, and I looked through the door and saw an orchard. The statute says *excised*, and I heard—God help me, I heard—someone on the far side of the crowd, a child's voice, thin and translucent like the rest of them, humming a nonsense song. My grandmother's song. The one she claimed she'd picked up. The one she hummed while she scored the cross into the bottom of the loaf where no one would see.

The Remnant, the reports call them. I had read the reports. *Remnant* is a good Inquisitorial word. It means leftover. It means scrap. Standing in the doorway, I counted forty-one of them, and every one was somebody's grandmother, somebody's cousin, somebody's whole town that the world had agreed to forget because forgetting was cheaper than grieving.

"Corvane." Brightwater's voice, careful, the voice you use near a frightened animal. "The door is failing. It was never meant to stay open this long. Whatever you're going to do—"

"I know what I'm going to do," I said.

I didn't. I picked up the cask. That much I can tell you: I picked it up. My hands knew their work. Forty years of knowing their work. And the old woman watched me, and did not step back, and behind her the forty-one did not step back, and the child hummed, and the sun hung at four in the afternoon in a country that had been dead for a century and was somehow, impossibly, still keeping its appointments.

I thought about my grandmother's hands. That's the truth of it, the whole ugly tender truth. Not the statute, not the seal, not the leak of nowhere into everywhere. Her hands, floured to the wrist, scoring the cross into the bottom of the loaf. *So the bread knows where it came from,* she told me once, when I was small enough to ask questions. *Even if nobody else does.*

The door shuddered. Not a sound—a feeling, in the teeth, in the worn stone under my boots, the whole threshold groaning like a bridge in wind. Brightwater cried out, staggered, caught the doorframe with their bandaged arm. The orchard light flickered, the way a lamp flickers when the oil runs low, and for one horrible half-second I saw through the country to the nothing behind it, and the forty-one flickered with it, and the child's song skipped like a scratched record.

And I understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that the door was not going to hold. That it didn't matter what I did with the cask. That the real choice was not fire or no fire. The real choice was standing in front of me, swaying in the doorframe, holding a country open with a bandaged arm and a borrowed name—because that was the last piece, the piece the codex had whispered about and my reports had dismissed as heretical poetry. The hinge. The door needed a living hinge, and the living hinge was Brightwater, and the price of a hinge is everything the hinge is.

"No," I said. Aloud. To a gatekeeper. To a country. To a statute.

I set the cask down on the worn stone, on the far side of the threshold line, on Vessany's side, where it could burn nothing but afternoon light. And I stepped through the doorway—onto the grass, into the smell of apples, into the gaze of forty-one ghosts who were not ghosts—and the old woman took my arm the way my grandmother used to, firmly, as if I were a child who might otherwise wander into traffic.

"Koru," she said.

The door began, behind us, to fail.
