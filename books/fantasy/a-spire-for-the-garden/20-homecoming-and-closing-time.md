## 20. Homecoming and Closing Time

### Yew

I have kept ledgers for sixty years, and I will tell you what nobody tells you about the trade: the hardest entries are not the losses. Losses you can total. The hardest entries are the departures—the columns where you write a name, a date, and then nothing, because there is no column for *where they went*, only the faith that *went* means something better than *gone*.

Tonight I filled forty-one such columns before the ink dried on the first.

The chaos upstairs had been Corvane's doing, and Corvane's undoing, in the same hour. His men had dragged me out of my own office in irons, and then his men had come back without him, and then Corvane himself had come down the Gatehouse stairs like a man walking in his sleep, keys in his hand, face the color of old paper. He unlocked my irons himself. He did not apologize. Inquisitors never do; it would be an admission that they were wrong, and the whole architecture of the Crown rests on the belief that they are never wrong. But he stood aside from the door, and he said, in a voice I will charitably call human, "The ledger. Someone should keep the ledger."

So that was my post. Not the threshold—that belonged to Juniper, poor lamb—but the little table beside it, with the great departure book open, and my pen, and my hand, which did not shake until the third hour and then would not stop, so that my entries from that point onward are a disgrace to the guild and I have decided to be proud of them.

They came up from below in ones and twos, the Remnant. Translucent as candle-flame, patient as rain. The polite ones bowed. The frightened ones held each other. A woman in a dress a century out of fashion stopped at my table and asked, "Do we sign?"

"Name only," I said. "The guild asks no toll tonight."

"Tonight," she repeated, tasting it. "What is tonight?"

"Thursday," I said, because someone had to say something true and small.

She wrote her name in a hand like copperplate, pressed her fingers to the page to be sure the ink took—though I do not know what a ghost's ink takes *to*—and walked through the door Juniper was holding open, and the door took her the way the sea takes a swimmer, gently, entirely.

I wrote: *Ilma Voss. Thursday. Went home.*

There is no column for what it cost. There never was. I am adding one.

### Maren

I told my daughter I was going out to settle an account.

Which was true, in the way that most lies are true if you sand them down. I had one account outstanding, forty years past due, and the creditor was myself.

The rain was the thing. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in that room was being honest and it seemed rude to be the only one arranging my face. Juniper had played me the bottled rain weeks ago—a sound of Vessany, they said, though I had no country by that name—and I had wept like a gutted fish and gone home and not slept and told myself it was the weather.

But a woman who weeps at weather is a woman with a hole in her, and I had lived with the hole so long I'd furnished it. I knew its shape. I knew where the drafts came in. What I had never known was what the hole was *for*, and then Juniper told me: it was the exact shape of an orchard, and a river, and a language I had traded away so my daughter could be born breathing.

You would do it again. That is the terrible arithmetic of it. I would do it again. But I had thought the trade was permanent, and it was not, and here was the rain, here was the rain, here was my mother's voice saying a word I suddenly understood to mean *come in from the cold*.

So I signed Yew's book. My hand shook worse than hers, and she is seventy-something and had recently been in irons.

"Your daughter," Juniper said. They stood at the threshold with the light of another country on their face, and they looked so tired, and so young, and so much like someone I might have known in a life I couldn't remember.

"Will you tell her?" I asked. "When she's grown. That her mother didn't abandon her. That her mother *went*."

"I'll tell her myself," Juniper said. "She'll inherit a true story. That was the price. I keep my prices."

I believed them. I have been a pawnbroker for forty years; believing people is not my habit. But I looked at the door, and through it I could see the orchards, and the light coming through the leaves was the exact color of the light in the one dream I'd had my whole life, the recurring one I'd never told anyone about, and I thought: *there it is. There is the thing the hole was for.*

I stepped through. It did not hurt. That surprised me. I had braced for it to hurt.

It felt like putting down a load I'd forgotten I was carrying, and then, a moment later, like picking up a different one, lighter, that I would carry gladly: the whole weight of a country, and my mother's voice, and rain.

### Aldous

I stayed.

Everyone expected me to go, I think. Even me, a little. The door was open, and home was through it, and I had spent forty years being a key without knowing what I unlocked. A man could be forgiven for walking through.

But here is the thing about the river.

They told me it wasn't real. Juniper sat with me on my own bridge—my bridge, the one I'd swept every morning since before my hair went grey—and explained it kindly, the way you'd tell a man his father wasn't his father. The river was a piece of Vessany, carried out in Aldous Finch's chest like a coal in a pocket, and the bridge was built on it, and the tolls I'd collected were collected on a road that led nowhere, and the village on the far bank existed because he believed in it, and when he let go, it would all fold up like a fan.

I asked him—asked Juniper, I mean, though I half meant the river—"And my wife's grave?"

Juniper went quiet. That child is honest to the bone; it's their worst quality and their best.

"The grave is real," they said. "She was real. She's buried in ground that will still be here. But the river will change, Aldous. It'll become whatever river was always supposed to be there. It won't be yours anymore."

I sat on the railing a long while. The water went by underneath, doing what water does, which is leave.

And I thought about going through. I thought about my mother's kitchen, which I do not remember but which Juniper swore was waiting. I thought about being a key instead of a man.

Then I thought about Elspeth. Not her grave—her. How she used to say I was the only man she knew who could sit still as a post. How she'd married me because, she claimed, I made her feel like the moving one. How she was buried under a willow on the near bank, and the willow was real, and if I left, I would leave her on the wrong side of a door that would never open again.

"I'll stay with the river's memory," I told Juniper. "Somebody has to remember what it sounded like. You've got your bottled echoes, but a bottle's not the same as a man who stood on it every morning for forty years and complained about the damp."

Juniper laughed. It cracked in the middle, the way a laugh does when it's doing the work of crying.

The letting-go did not look like anything, from the outside. I just stopped holding it. Forty years of holding, and it turns out the trick was only to open your hand.

The river shivered. The far bank went soft, then strange, then simply *other*—and then there was a river there, an ordinary river, with an ordinary far bank, and it was not mine, and it never would be, and I sat down on my bridge—my bridge, still mine, the stones of it, the mortar, the sweep of my own broom—and I wept for a river like it was a wife.

Which, I suppose, in the end, it was.

### Tam

I was not supposed to be on my feet. Orla had said so with the full authority of a woman who has raised feral children and buried two husbands, and I had agreed with the full sincerity of an apprentice who intended to do exactly as he pleased the moment she looked away.

My shoulder was bandaged. My ribs were a symphony. The Inquisitor's blade had found me on the stairs and I had decided, in the moment, that a wound was a small price for Juniper's escape route staying open, and I still think that, and I will be milking the story for decades.

So I leaned on the doorframe at the top of the under-stairs and I watched the crossing, because someone young should witness it. That's what I told myself. Someone has to remember it who will still be alive in sixty years to be insufferable about it.

The Remnant went through in ones and twos and then, as the night deepened and the door held, in families. A man carried an old woman who might have been his mother or his grandmother; time had gone strange in them and you couldn't tell. Children ran through laughing, which undid me entirely, and I had to pretend to check my bandages.

And Juniper stood at the threshold the whole night, holding the door, and I watched them getting thinner. Not thinner in the body—thinner in the *self*, if that makes sense, the way a candle is thinner toward morning. The door was drinking them. Everyone could see it. Orla could see it, and Orla does not see things she cannot fix, so she had planted herself at the bottom of the stairs with a tray of bread no one could taste anymore, feeding the crossing like it was a hearth fire, and every Remnant who passed took a piece, because refusing Orla is not possible in this life or any other.

"Go through," I heard Juniper tell one of them, gently, over and over. "Go through. It's all right. It's all right."

And not once—not once—did they say *and then I will close it and be done*. They just held the door, and thinned, and held it.

I have thought a great deal since about what courage is. I used to think it was the raid, the blade, the wound. It isn't. It's a person standing in a doorway for six hours, giving themselves away by the spoonful, and never once asking anyone to notice.

I noticed. I'm telling you. Someone had to.

### Juniper

Here is what nobody tells you about holding open a door between worlds: it is mostly waiting.

The heroics were over. The keys had turned—Maren's grief, Aldous's river, Sera Ilves's grave-given iron, and me, the hinge, the living fourth, the child passed through a closing door forty-odd years ago by a mother whose voice I had finally heard again on the far side, saying the word that meant *come in from the cold*, and meaning, this time, *stay*.

I did not stay. I held the door, and the door held me, and between the two of us we managed the night.

They came through. That was the whole of it, the whole grand destiny the codex had promised: a door, and people coming through it, and my name written in dust a century ago turning out to mean nothing more and nothing less than *be the one who stands here*. Ilma Voss. The family with the copperplate hands. The children. An old soldier who saluted me, which I did not earn and could not refuse. A woman who pressed a dried apple-blossom into my palm—real, solid, from a country that had been erased and was now, leaf by leaf, refusing to be.

And through the door I could see it all: the orchards, the spire, the streets with their crooked chimneys, the rain that Maren had wept at, the light that was the exact color of a thing I'd been homesick for my whole life without knowing the word for it.

My mother was there. She did not cross. She stood on the far side, in the orchard light, and she looked at me the way you look at a fire you have carried a long way in the wind, and she said, "You grew," which is what mothers say, in any country, erased or not.

"I had help," I said.

"Then you were rich," she said, "whatever else you were," and I could not answer, because it was true, and because the door was pulling at me, and because some sentences you have to carry rather than finish.

Orla stood at the bottom of the stairs the whole night. She did not ask me to come away. She did not say *you owe me*, though she could have, nine years of bread and bandages and fierce unsentimental love. She just stood there, arms folded, and every time I wavered she would catch my eye and mouth something—usually an insult, once, memorably, the word *biscuits*—and I would steady.

That is what an anchor is. Not a rope. A woman mouthing *biscuits* at you from the bottom of a staircase while you come apart.

Tam leaned in the doorframe, bandaged and pale and refusing all sensible instructions, and Corvane—Corvane stood at the top of the stairs with his black ribbons hanging limp in his hand, and he did not burn anything, and he did not arrest anyone, and when his own grandmother came up out of the dark below and paused in front of him, he said something to her in a language I did not know and she answered, and whatever passed between them, he stepped aside after, and he stayed aside.

Near dawn, the last of them came through. A child, last of all, because it is always a child last of all; the Remnant had organized their exodus the way the polite organize everything, worst off first, smallest last. The child stopped in front of me, looked up, and asked, "Aren't you coming?"

And there it was. The whole question, in a mouth the size of a teacup.

"I live here," I said.

"Where's here?"

I looked back at the stairs. Orla, mouthing *biscuits*. Tam, bleeding and beaming. Yew at her table, pen scratching, filling in the last columns of the last ledger of the erased world. Aldous, who had chosen a grave over an orchard and called it love. A guild that traded in impossibles and had somehow, in nine years of my keeping its smallest door, made me possible.

"Here," I said, "is where my people are."

The child considered this with the seriousness children bring to border crossings, nodded once, and went through.

And then it was time, and I knew it the way you know a song has ended—not by the silence but by the shape of it. The door was failing. My arms were failing. The hinge had turned as far as hinges turn.

I looked through one last time. The orchards. The light. My mother, raising her hand—not waving, exactly. The word she mouthed was the one that meant *come in from the cold*, and the word she said after it, aloud, in Vessany's tongue, was the one that meant *but not from me*.

"Close it," Yew said quietly, from her table. "Juniper. It's time to close the shop."

I let go.

It did not slam, which I had expected. It closed the way a book closes—gently, at the end of its page, with a sound like a breath let out—and the light of another country narrowed to a line, to a thread, to nothing, and the stone of the under-door went cold and ordinary and final, and I was on this side of it, kneeling on flagstones in a cellar under a ruined gatehouse, with apple-blossom in my fist and rain in my ears that was only rain.

Orla's hand came down on my shoulder like a roof beam. Warm. Real. Weighing something.

"Well," she said. "That's the last of the impossible stock, then."

I laughed, or sobbed, or both; the distinction had stopped mattering somewhere around the third hour. "Closing time," I managed.

"Closing time," she agreed. "Upstairs. There's bread."

There was bread. There was no taste in it, and it was the best thing I have ever eaten, and we ate it together in the grey morning in a courtyard that smelled of smoke and ash and, faintly, impossibly, apple-blossom.

### Yew

The final entry I wrote that night—or morning, by then—was not a departure.

*Gatehouse door: closed. Permanently. By the hand of Juniper Brightwater, keeper, key, and hinge, who was ours before we knew it and who chose us after.*

Then, because I am an honest bookkeeper if nothing else, I added a line the guild's charter has no provision for:

*Inventory of the impossible, remaining: one found family. Condition: worn, grieving, intact. Value: not for sale.*

I blotted the page. Outside, the sun came up on a city with no more ribbons on its doors, and Corvane stood in my doorway looking like a man whose whole religion had been repossessed, and I had the sudden, uncharitable, entirely correct suspicion that he was about to ask for work.
