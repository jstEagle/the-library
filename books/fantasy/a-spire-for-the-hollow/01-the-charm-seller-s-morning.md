## 1. The Charm Seller's Morning

The bells of Vess rang the hour before dawn, and Freya Nakamura was already awake, listening to them the way a man listens to a dog that might turn on him.

Seven bells. The city's oldest tower counted seven before the sun cleared the eastern hills, and in three years he had never once slept through them. Not because they were loud — the sound came muffled through the shutters of his rented room above the chandler's shop, softened by plaster and old wool — but because some part of him had learned to keep watch even in sleep. Some part of him had been keeping watch for longer than he could remember.

He lay still beneath the blanket and let the hum find him.

It was always there, the hum. Beneath his skin, beneath the marrow of his wrists, a faint vibration like a struck tuning fork that never quite finished ringing. On quiet nights it was almost pleasant, a warmth in the blood, a reminder of what he was. On mornings like this one — when the wind off the harbor carried the smell of tar and cold iron, when the sky over Vess hung low and gray as unwashed linen — it felt less like a gift and more like a brand. Something the wrong eyes might see if he let it rise too close to the surface.

So he did what he did every morning. He breathed out slow, and he pressed the hum down.

It went grudgingly, the way a cat goes into a basket. He felt it settle somewhere behind his navel, coiled and patient, and he felt the familiar ache follow it down — a dull pressure at the base of his skull, like a headache waiting just out of reach. Three years of practice had taught him to hold it there all day without flinching. Three years ago, holding it had made his hands shake. In another three years, perhaps, it would be as easy as breathing.

Perhaps. But he doubted it. Magic, in Freya's experience, did not like being told to wait.

He rose in the dark and dressed by touch: the rough gray tunic, the apron with its singed hem, the plain wooden beads he wore at his throat because a charm-seller who wore no charms invited questions. His stall-goods waited in the crate beneath the window — luck-knots braided from harbor grass, river-stones polished smooth and drilled for cord, little clay bells fired in a borrowed kiln. None of them held anything but string and stone and clay. That was the point. That had always been the point. A man could sell hope honestly enough, so long as the hope was only hope.

He touched the door latch and paused, as he always paused, feeling for the thread of attention he kept knotted there — not magic, nothing the wardens could name, just habit sharpened into instinct. The street below was empty except for a gull arguing with something dead on the cobbles. No boots. No red-and-black sashes. No watchers at the corner where the lamplighter's post leaned crooked against the wall.

He went down.

* * *

The Saltmarket opened its stalls an hour after sunrise, and by then the fog had burned back to the waterline, leaving the square washed in pale gold light that made even the fishmongers' tables look almost kind. Freya claimed his usual spot between the rope-seller and the woman who sold candied ginger, and he set out his wares with the small rituals of a man arranging an alibi: knots here, stones there, bells in their straw nest, each item priced in chalk on a slate he wiped clean every night.

"Morning, Nakamura," called the rope-seller, a broad woman named Hesta with forearms like mooring cables. "You look tired."

"I always look tired."

"You always do." She laughed and turned back to her coils of hemp, and Freya smiled the smile he kept for neighbors — warm, shallow, and exactly deep enough.

He had been Hesta's neighbor for three years now, and she knew nothing about him worth knowing. That was not carelessness; that was craft. He had built himself a life the way a mason builds a wall around a well — solid on the outside, hiding the drop within. He sold charms that were not charms. He lived above a shop whose owner asked no questions because Freya paid early and swept her step. He ate at Tomas's bakery twice a week and let the baker talk enough for both of them. And underneath all of it, under the chalk prices and the polite laughter and the careful, practiced ordinariness, he was the last keeper of the Hollow Gate, and the Gate was sleeping beneath the city, and he was the only thing standing between it and everything that wanted it open.

He did not think about this during market hours. Thinking about it made the hum stir.

The morning passed the way good mornings passed: slowly, unremarkably. A dockhand bought a luck-knot for his brother's first voyage. Two children spent a stolen copper on a river-stone apiece and argued over which one was shinier. An old soldier haggled Freya down two coppers on a bell "for my granddaughter's christening," then paid the full price anyway when he thought Freya wasn't looking. Small commerce, small kindnesses, the ordinary machinery of a city that did not know what slept beneath its foundations.

And then, near midday, the young woman came.

He noticed her before she reached the stall, the way he noticed everyone now — cataloguing, assessing, the old keeper's reflex that no amount of ordinary living had worn away. She was perhaps nineteen, thin in the way of someone who forgot meals rather than skipped them, with brown hair escaping a braid and eyes that moved too fast, touching everything and resting on nothing. She wore a housemaid's dress gone gray at the seams, and she walked through the market the way a rabbit crosses open ground: quick starts, long pauses, ears up.

She stopped at his stall. She looked at the luck-knots. She did not pick one up.

"You're the charm seller," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"For certain values of charm," Freya said, and gave her the smile. "Looking for anything particular?"

Her hands were twisting in her apron. "My sister's having a baby. Next month. There are complications." Her voice caught on the word and steadied itself. "The midwife said — she didn't say much. They never say much. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That I'd buy something. Something lucky. For her."

Freya's chest tightened, and he hated how easily it tightened, how after three years he still hadn't learned to hear grief without reaching for it.

He picked out a knot of harbor grass, the tightest braid in the basket, worked with a pattern his grandmother had called the cradle-weave. He held it out across the stall.

"This one," he said. "Braided for safe passage. It won't work miracles — I want to be honest with you about that, because you'll get enough lies from people selling you miracles. What it will do is give your sister something to hold. Something to grip when the pain comes. And sometimes—" he turned the knot in the light, letting her see the care in it, the hours in it, because there had been hours, real ones, "—sometimes that's half the battle. A thing to hold onto. Do you understand?"

She nodded. Her eyes had gone bright.

"Two coppers," he said, and when she fumbled in her purse and came up short, he closed her fingers over the knot and shook his head. "One, then. Call it a christening discount. You can owe me the rest when the baby's born fat and loud."

She laughed — wet, startled, grateful — and took the knot like it was made of glass. "Thank you," she whispered. "You're kind. Most people in this city aren't, anymore."

"No," Freya agreed quietly. "They're frightened. It's different."

She lingered a moment, turning the knot over, and Freya watched her face do something complicated — watched hope and fear pass through it like weather. Then she thanked him again and hurried off toward the cloth stalls, clutching the braid, and Freya stood at his stall in the gold light and felt the hum shift beneath his skin, restless, wanting to follow her, wanting to slip into the knot and make it true.

He pressed it down. Harder than usual.

Because that was the cruelty of it, the joke the world had played on him: the charms would have worked, if he let them. A thread of the Gate's power, a breath of intention, and the cradle-weave would become what it pretended to be. He could feel the possibility sitting in his palms like heat from a stove, close and easy and forbidden. Every day he stood in the market and sold people nothing, and every day he could have given them something, and every day he chose the law and the seal and the slow, grinding discipline of doing nothing at all.

Three years since the edicts. Three years since the last king died and the regent — no, he corrected himself, the *king*, Aldous was king now, had been for a year, though Vess still spoke of him carefully, the way one speaks of a storm that has passed but not gone far — three years since magic had been named a crime against the crown. Not taxed. Not licensed. *Criminal.* The gifted were to be registered, collared, put to work for the throne or put to death for refusing. The wardens had new sashes and new powers and a new motto stamped on their writs: *No Gift Above the King.*

Freya had not registered. Registration meant testing, and testing meant they would find what he was, and what he was was not a hedge-witch or a charm-monger or any of the small magics the crown thought it understood. What he was, the crown must never learn.

So he sold string and stone and clay, and he pressed the hum down, and he went home at dusk along streets he varied nightly, and he slept badly, and he woke to seven bells, and he did it all again.

That evening, after the stalls were struck and the fog rolled back in off the harbor, he did the other thing. The real thing.

* * *

The descent began behind the chandler's shop, down a stair most tenants believed led to a flooded cellar. It did not. It led down, and down, and further down than any cellar in Vess had business going, through stone that grew older with every step — older in the way stone gets old, not cracked but *deepened*, as if the weight of centuries had been pressed into it like flowers into a book.

Freya carried a shuttered lantern and a satchel of tools he had not needed in months, and he counted the stairs as he always counted them, because counting was a ritual and rituals were armor. One hundred and forty steps to the first landing, where the air changed. Ninety more to the second, where the walls began to sweat and the lantern flame bent sideways, drawn by a current that had no source. Sixty more after that, spiraling, to the threshold of the Hollow.

The Hollow was not a cave. Caves ended. The Hollow was a wound in the world's skin, a vast irregular chamber where the rock fell away into dark on every side, and at its center, sunk into the floor like a coin pressed into wax, lay the Gate.

It did not look like much. That was the first lesson every keeper learned, and the last lesson kings ever grasped: the things that matter most wear the smallest faces. The Gate was a ring of black stone, wide as a wagon wheel, set flush with the floor, carved with script so old that no living scholar could read it — script that predated the kingdom, predated the free cities, predated the word *Vess*. Around it, driven into the rock at the four quarters, stood the seals: four pillars of veined white stone, each taller than a man, each humming.

Freya felt them hum before he heard them. He felt everything here. Down in the Hollow, stripped of the market and the chalk prices and the careful smile, he did not have to press the magic down. Here it rose in him like water finding its level, and the Gate answered it, and for a few minutes each night he was allowed to be, entirely and terribly, what he was.

He knelt at the northern pillar first, laid both palms flat against the white stone, and listened.

Listening was not hearing, exactly. It was more like pressing an ear to a door and reading the room beyond by vibration alone. The pillar sang to him in a language older than words: a long, steady note, the note of a seal holding, of a boundary intact, of ancient things on the far side of the stone staying anciently asleep. He moved to the eastern pillar. The same note, steady, true. Southern. True. He saved the western pillar for last, as he always did, because the western pillar was the one that worried him.

It had been worrying him for a year.

He knelt. He pressed his palms to the stone. And there it was — faint, so faint that anyone else would have missed it, but Freya had been listening to these pillars since he was six years old, kneeling beside his grandmother in this same dark while she taught him the notes the way other grandmothers taught lullabies. Faint, but present: a flutter in the western pillar's song. A hitch, like a heartbeat skipping. Like a lock beginning, very slowly, very patiently, to rust.

His stomach dropped, the way it dropped every night now, and he made himself stay calm, because panic fed the wrong things.

He took out his tools — a rod of cold iron, a vial of blessed salt, a spool of silvered wire — and he performed the maintenance. It was not repair; nothing was broken yet. It was tending, the way one tends a fire banked for the night, feeding the seal's strength with his own, pouring a thin stream of the hum through his palms and into the stone until the flutter smoothed and the long note steadied. It cost him. It always cost him. When he rose, his legs were heavy and his vision swam at the edges, and he stood a moment in the dark breathing until the world came back.

Four pillars. Four songs. Three true, one mended.

He allowed himself, as he always allowed himself, one minute at the Gate's edge. He stood looking down at the black ring and the unreadable script, and he let himself remember — not the histories, not the dry chronicles of the first kings binding the Old Powers beneath the earth, but his grandmother's voice, low in this dark, telling him what the Powers were. Not demons. Not gods. *Neighbors,* she had called them. Old neighbors, from before humanity's tenancy, who had lost a war and been locked in the cellar, and who had nothing but time and hunger and an infinite capacity for patience. The seals were not a wall, she said. Walls break. The seals were a promise, renewed nightly by keepers' hands, that the war stayed finished.

"And what happens when there are no more keepers?" he had asked, aged nine, terrified.

"Then the promise runs out," she had said, "the way all promises run out. That is why there is always one more keeper. Until there isn't."

He was the last. He had known it for eleven years, since he buried her above the treeline and came down alone into the dark. Eleven years of being the only hand renewing the promise. Three years of doing it while pretending, in the daylight world, to be nobody at all.

The flutter in the western pillar was small. Seals had fluttered before and settled. Grandmother had spoken of flutters lasting decades, worn smooth by generations of tending.

But the flutter was also growing. Slowly. Patiently. The way everything on the other side of that stone was patient.

Freya banked the lantern, climbed the two hundred and ninety steps, and emerged into the night behind the chandler's shop with the ache behind his eyes and the hum pressed down and the ordinary city lying all around him, ten thousand hearthfires burning, ten thousand citizens going to bed believing the world was solid.

He stood in the alley a moment, breathing the fog, and told himself what he told himself every night: *it holds. It holds tonight. Tend it tomorrow.*

Then he saw the paper pasted to the chandler's door.

It had not been there at dusk. It was a broadsheet, printed in the crown's crisp black type, and even by lantern-light he could read the heading before his heart finished its first hard beat:

*BY ORDER OF THE KING: Let it be known throughout the realm that all persons possessing the uncanny gift, of whatever degree...*

He read it twice. He read it a third time with his finger on the words, as if touching them might change them. All gifted persons to present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who concealed their gift would be hunted. Informants would be compensated.

Below, smaller, almost an afterthought: *Reward offered for information leading to the identification of unregistered gifted.*

Freya looked up the empty street. Somewhere a dog barked. The fog moved between the houses like something alive.

Thirty days, the paper promised him. Thirty days of selling string and stone and clay. Thirty days of pressing the hum down.

He peeled the broadsheet off the door, folded it small, and put it in his pocket, and went upstairs to lie awake and count what thirty days might hold.
