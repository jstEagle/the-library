## 2. Proclamations on Every Door

Freya woke to the sound of hammers.

Not the friendly kind — not the cobbler's tap or the shipwright's mallet ringing up from the river yards. This was iron on wood, quick and rhythmic and everywhere, as if the whole city of Vess had grown hands overnight and was knocking on every door at once. He lay in his narrow bed above the shop and listened, and the hum beneath his skin — the one he kept pressed down like a hand over a singing bowl — answered the sound with a low, uneasy tremor.

He had slept perhaps two hours. The broadsheet was still folded in his pocket, small as a prayer, and he could feel its weight there as though paper had mass enough to bruise.

He dressed in the dark. He always dressed in the dark; lamplight made his shadow do things shadows shouldn't, if you knew how to look. Down the stairs, past the shelves of his harmless wares — luck-knots of red string, river-stones drilled and hung on leather, clay bells fired in a neighbor's kiln and painted with eyes against envy — and out into the street.

The fog had thinned but not lifted. It hung in the mouths of the alleys and pooled in the gutters, and through it Freya watched the criers' men work their way down Candlemaker's Row. They moved in pairs, one to nail, one to smooth the paper flat with a wooden paddle, and they did not speak to each other, and they did not look at the people who stood in their doorways watching. There were more watchers than Freya would have expected at this hour. Bakers with flour still on their forearms. A fishwife with her baskets set down beside her, forgotten. An old man in a nightshirt, holding his shutter half-closed as though the paper might see him back.

The broadsheets went up on doors, on shutters, on the posts at the corners where the lamplighters tied their ladders. By the time the fog burned off, Vess wore them like a rash.

Freya read one properly for the first time in full daylight, standing before the post outside the Tanner's Rest, and read it again because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense the first time.

*By decree of His Majesty Aldous, First of That Name, rightful King of Vess and all its territories:* the letters marched in black ranks across the page. *All persons possessed of unlicensed arts — being witchcraft, hedge-craft, charm-work, scrying, mending, whispering, or any gift of the old blood — shall present themselves for registration within thirty days at any wardenhouse. Registered persons will be assessed, catalogued, and bound to lawful service. Unregistered persons found after the term will be taken. Taken persons will be judged.*

*Judged.* The word sat there doing the work of three worse words.

Below it, smaller: *Reward offered for information leading to the identification of unregistered gifted.*

He had read that part last night by the light of his own doorway, and last night it had been an abstraction, a thing that happened to other streets. In daylight it was arithmetic. Vess held forty thousand souls and perhaps four hundred of the gifted, most of them hiding gifts no larger than a candleflame — a woman who could keep milk sweet a day longer, a boy who could find lost buttons. Four hundred people who now had thirty days to decide what they were willing to lose.

And one of them, Freya Nakamura, who sold string and stone and clay, who pressed the hum down every waking hour of every waking day, who carried beneath the floor of his own cellar a door older than the kingdom and the only reason the kingdom still stood.

"Bad business," said a voice at his shoulder.

Tomas. The baker from two doors down, big as a barrel and shaped much the same, his apron dusted white and his breath smelling of the first batch. He was looking at the broadsheet the way he looked at bread that had come out wrong — not angry, exactly. Grieved.

"It is," Freya said.

"They posted one on my door." Tomas jerked his chin back toward his bakery, where the paper flapped gently in the river wind. "On *my* door, Frey. I've lived on this row nineteen years. I pay my tithes. My father paid his. And some clerk with a hammer treats my threshold like a fence post."

"That's not what it means, though. It's on everyone's door."

"I know what it means." Tomas lowered his voice, which for Tomas meant only that the fishwife three stalls away could no longer hear him. "It means they want us reading it. It means they want us *talking* about it. You know what happens next. Someone reads 'reward,' and someone looks at their neighbor, and someone remembers their neighbor does something odd." He paused. His eyes, small and brown and honest as a dog's, came around to Freya and rested there a moment too long.

Freya kept his face pleasant and empty, the face he wore for customers. "I sell charms, Tom. String and stone. Half of it doesn't even hold together in the rain."

"I know." But Tomas said it strangely, and then clapped Freya's shoulder hard enough to stagger him, and turned back toward the smell of his ovens, and did not say anything else.

* * *

The market that morning was wrong in a way Freya had never felt it wrong before.

Vess's markets ran on noise — the bawling of fishmongers, the quarrels over pennies, the carters swearing with real artistry. Today the noise was all still there, but underneath it ran a new current, a hush that moved through the crowd like oil through water. People were talking with their heads close together. People were looking away from each other. At the corner by the well, a woman Freya had known for years — Marta, who sold apples, whose daughter he'd once quietly cured of night-terrors with a knot of braided thread and no magic at all, just patience — caught his eye across the square and then deliberately, carefully, looked elsewhere.

He sold four charms before noon. All of them to strangers. None of them met his eye.

A young man bought a luck-knot without haggling, which in Vess was nearly a confession of something. An old soldier fingered a river-stone and asked, too casually, whether the stones "did anything." Freya gave him the answer he'd given a thousand times — *they're just stones, friend, but they're good ones* — and the soldier bought two, and Freya watched him walk away turning them over in his scarred hands, and wondered if he'd just sold protection or evidence.

Because that was the trap inside the edict, wasn't it. Every charm he'd ever sold was now a confession waiting to be remembered. Every customer who had ever whispered *my mother swears your knots work* was now holding a coin purse full of testimony. Three years of harmless trade, three years of building a life out of small kindnesses and small profits, and the crown had found the one lever that could pull it all apart: not force, but memory. Not wardens, but neighbors.

He closed the stall early. His hands were steady, which surprised him. It was everything under his hands that shook — the hum rising like floodwater behind the wall he kept it behind, wanting out, wanting to *do* something, and there was nothing to do. Magic couldn't fix this. Magic was the thing being hunted.

In the afternoon he did what he always did when the world grew teeth. He went down.

* * *

The cellar stairs were steep and the dark at the bottom was total, and Freya navigated it without a lamp because he didn't need one — the seals gave off a faint grey luminescence, like moonlight soaked into stone, and always had. He'd told the previous tenant, years ago, that the cellar flooded. Nobody wanted a flooding cellar. Nobody asked questions about a flooding cellar.

At the bottom, behind a false wall of brick and mildew, the Hollow Gate waited.

It did not look like much. That was the joke of it, the terrible private joke he'd carried alone for three years: the fate of the world, and it was a door. Old grey stone in a frame of older stone, set into the living rock beneath Vess, tall as two men and carved over every inch with script that predated the kingdom, predated the language, predated maybe the ground itself. And across its face, laid in lines of silvered metal gone green with age, the seals — nine of them, great interlocking bands of wrought sigil-work, each one humming at a pitch below hearing.

Except Freya could hear it. Had always been able to hear it, since the night in his nineteenth year when old Keeper Wren had put his palm against Freya's chest and said, *oh, child, you're loud.*

He knelt at the Gate's base the way he knelt every third night, and laid both palms flat against the lowest seal, and let himself listen.

The hum rose through his arms. It was not unpleasant — it was like standing near a cathedral organ when someone plays the lowest pipe, a vibration more felt than heard, a vast patience. The seals were sound. All nine of them, whole and tight and singing their long note into the stone. Beyond them, on the other side of a door that had not opened in six hundred years, something waited that did not have a name in any tongue Freya knew, and the seals were the only reason it was still a *something behind a door* instead of a *something in the world.*

His gift flowed along the bands the way a householder walks his walls at night, touching each latch, checking each window. First seal, sound. Second, sound. Third — he paused. Third had a hairline roughness to it, a burr in the note, and his heart stumbled before he found the flaw's true shape: not damage. Wear. Six centuries of wear, a thread-thin thinning no keeper had been able to prevent, because the seals were mortal work holding shut an immortal door, and mortal work tired.

Wren had told him once, in the last winter of the old man's life: *They'll ask more of you than you think you have. They always do. That's what keeping is.*

Freya smoothed the burr as best he could — a trickle of the hum, guided careful as water into a crack — and worked his way up through the remaining six, and they sang clean, and he sat back on his heels in the grey light and let the silence of the deep rock settle over him.

Thirty days. The edict upstairs, nailed to the world. And somewhere beneath all the crown's fear and greed and paperwork, did anyone in that marble palace even know what sat under their city? Wren had sworn the royal line had forgotten — that the last king who'd known had died two hundred years ago and taken the secret into a sealed tomb. But kings dug tombs open. Kings got curious. And if Aldous learned what the Gate was, and what opening it might buy him—

Freya pressed his palms to the cold floor and breathed until the thought went quiet.

Then he climbed back up into the world of bread and fog and hammers, and locked the false wall behind him, and lit a lamp he didn't need, because tonight he wanted light.

* * *

He smelled the burning before he understood it.

Evening had come down blue and cold over Candlemaker's Row, and Freya was banking his stall fire when the wind shifted and brought him scorched wood and hot iron. For one blind instant he thought: *wardens, they've come for me,* and the hum surged so hard against its walls that the lamp flames in three windows guttered — and then he saw Tomas.

The baker was kneeling on his own doorstep with a branding iron in one fist, the kind used to mark loaves, its tip glowing orange in the dusk. On the stone step before him, black and smoking, letters were taking shape. Tomas wrote slowly, with the concentration of a man who rarely wrote anything longer than a ledger line, and the smell rolled off the step in waves.

"Tomas." Freya crossed the street fast. "What in the name of — put it down, you'll ruin the—"

"Ruin nothing." Tomas didn't look up. "Stone takes it fine. Watched a sign-painter burn a shop name once, lasted twenty years." He finished the last stroke, sat back on his heels, and blew across the step like a man cooling soup.

In the blue dusk, by the light of the dying iron, Freya read it:

**NO GIFT UNDER THIS ROOF**

For a moment he could not speak. The words seemed to move, seeming one moment a boast and the next a plea and the next a shield, and none of those were right either.

"Tomas—"

"Don't." The baker heaved himself upright with a groan, tucking the iron into his apron. "Don't thank me and don't argue with me, I haven't the patience for either. Word'll be all down the row by morning. Good. Let it be." He looked at the burnt letters, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "My grandmother was a mender, Frey. Little gift. Stitched wounds closed with her thumb, that's all, never hurt a flea. When the purges came under the old regent, our neighbors — people she'd delivered, people she'd fed — they came for her anyway. My father watched it. He never spoke her name again." Tomas's jaw worked. "I was seven when he told me why. Said, *if it ever comes again, you make sure everyone knows where you stand before they can guess for you.*"

The fog was coming back off the river, threading between the houses. Somewhere down the row, a child was being called in to supper, the call hanging in the cold air like something from another century.

"You don't know what you're marking yourself with," Freya said quietly. "If they come asking questions about this street, about me—"

"About you?" Tomas frowned, genuinely puzzled, and Freya saw with a lurch that the baker had built his shield for the row itself, for the principle of it, and had not once aimed it at Freya specifically. Or — worse, kinder — that he had aimed it there all along and simply never said so. "Frey. Nineteen years I've lived next to you. You helped carry my wife's coffin when my brothers wouldn't cross the river in winter. You leave bread money in my till when you think I'm not looking, and you overpay, which is its own kind of insult." A ghost of a smile. "Whatever you are or aren't, you're mine to stand for. That's how rows work. That's how *people* work."

Freya's throat was tight. The hum stirred, wanting to answer, wanting to give the man something true, and he pressed it down and gave him only words. "Thank you, Tom."

"Aye, well." Tomas cleared his gruffly and nodded at the broadsheet still nailed to Freya's own door, pale in the gloom. "You should take that down. Bad luck leaving crown paper on your threshold overnight."

"I already did."

"Mm." Tomas gathered his iron and turned toward the warmth of his ovens, then stopped, half in shadow. "Thirty days, the paper says. You know what I keep thinking? Thirty days is a long time. Long enough for a man to finish his accounts. Long enough to visit family." He glanced back, and the lamplight from his doorway caught his eyes. "Long enough to get somewhere else, if a man had somewhere else to be. Just saying it's a long time. That's all."

He went in. The door closed. The smell of evening bread followed him, warm and ordinary and impossibly far away.

Freya stood alone in the middle of Candlemaker's Row with the fog closing in and the burnt letters smoking faintly on the step across the way. *No gift under this roof.* A lie, if the crown ever tested it — but a lie Tomas had chosen, knowing the price of lies these days.

Three quiet years. He had thought, some nights, that he might have thirty of them. That the hum and the Gate and the grey stone dark could stay secret until he was an old man kneeling at an old door, and some younger keeper after him would inherit the vigil the way he'd inherited it, and the world would go on not knowing how thin the floor beneath it really was.

But the hammers had come at dawn, and the paper was on every door, and the reward was printed in black ink, and Marta had looked away from him across the square, and somewhere in this city of forty thousand there was already someone counting coins and remembering a charm.

Freya looked up the empty street. Somewhere a dog barked. The fog moved between the houses like something alive.

Thirty days, the paper promised him. Thirty days of selling string and stone and clay. Thirty days of pressing the hum down.

He peeled the broadsheet off the door, folded it small, and put it in his pocket — and froze, because beneath his fingers, through the paper, through the wood of the door itself, he felt it: a second heartbeat in the deep rock, faint and wrong, the lowest seal's long note shivering out of tune.

Something, far below, had noticed the world changing too.
