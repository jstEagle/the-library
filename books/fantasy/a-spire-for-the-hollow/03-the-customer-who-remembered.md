## 3. The Customer Who Remembered

The rain came to Vess three days after the edicts, as if the sky itself had decided to weep for the city's good sense. It fell in fine grey sheets that turned the cobbles to slate and drove the market crowds under awnings, where they huddled and muttered and pretended not to look at the parchment nailed above the fountain — the one with the king's seal, the one with the word *gifted* written in ink the color of dried blood.

Freya watched the rain from the shelter of his stall and counted the ways his life was ending.

He had gotten good at counting. It was a keeper's habit, old training worn smooth by three years of practice: count the exits, count the wardens, count the seconds between the bell at the Temple of the Nine and the moment the market watch made its slow circuit past the fountain. Counting kept the hands steady. Counting kept the hum beneath his skin folded down small, small as a coal banked in ash, so that no warden's senses — dull as most of them were — would ever catch its warmth.

The stall itself was modest and honest-looking, which had taken work. A strip of oiled canvas against the drizzle. A folding table with its wares laid out in careful rows: river-stones drilled and threaded on leather cords, sprigs of dried rowan, knotted red thread, little clay beads glazed the green of pond water. Nothing that *worked*, nothing that would interest a warden with a testing-iron. Just charms — the harmless kind, the kind every grandmother in Vess had sold before the outlawry, luck and love and safe-childbirth, the small superstitions of small lives. Freya priced them fairly and let the buyers believe what they wanted to believe. The magic, when he used it at all — and he almost never did — went elsewhere. Downward. Into the dark.

The bell at the Temple of the Nine rang the third hour, and with it, she came.

He had known she would. He had been counting that, too — three days since the edicts, three days since the nervous young woman with the chapped hands had bought a luck charm and looked over her shoulder twice on her way out of the market. Three days was how long fear took to ripen into either courage or hunger. He had hoped for courage. He had hoped she'd come back to tell him she'd burned the charm, or thrown it in the canal, or simply to stand at the edge of his stall and look at him with the particular shame of someone who wants to apologize without knowing how.

She came back with hunger. He saw it in the way she walked — too straight, too deliberate, a woman rehearsing a part on the way to the stage.

"Master charm-seller." She stopped at the table and pushed back her hood. Rain beaded in the brown curls at her temples. Up close she looked even younger than he'd remembered — nineteen, perhaps twenty, with the kind of face that markets were full of in Vess: pretty in a tired way, worn at the edges by rent and bread prices and the general weight of being poor in a kingdom that had stopped pretending to care. "You sold me a charm. Three days ago. For luck."

"I sell many charms." Freya kept his voice mild, his hands busy, straightening a row of rowan sprigs that did not need straightening. "I don't always remember which."

"You remember this one." Her eyes came up and held his, and there it was — not courage, not hunger exactly, but something worse than both. Certainty. The terrible calm of a person who has already done the worst thing they're going to do and is only now arriving at the scene of it. "You remember because you touched my hands when you gave it to me. And your hands were *warm*, master. Warm in a way hands aren't, in this weather. Warm like something was in them."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere across the square, a fishmonger argued with a cart-driver, their voices thin and sharp through the water-hiss.

"Chilblains," Freya said pleasantly. "You should try the apothecary on Tanner's Row. Tell him Freya sent you, he'll give you a fair price on the salve."

"My name is Merrin," she said, as if he hadn't spoken. "Merrin of Cooper's Lane. And three days ago I was so frightened I couldn't think, and I took your charm, and I went home, and I sat with it all night. And then I went to the warden-house on Bell Street." She said it fast, the way you pull a tooth — one motion, no stopping, because stopping was unthinkable. "There's a bounty. You've seen the edicts. Five crowns for a name, twenty for a capture. My father's a cooper and his lungs are bad and we owe the guild-house two seasons' rent, and the edicts say — the edicts *say* —"

"The edicts say a great many things," Freya said. He was aware of his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like a drum in a deep room. He was aware of the hum beneath his skin, banked and small, and of how much he wanted it to stay banked. "Merrin of Cooper's Lane. If you've come to confess something, confess it to the Nine, not to me. I'm a seller of trinkets. My magic is the magic of a fool and his money, soon parted."

"That's what I told them," she whispered. "I told them I wasn't sure. I told them maybe I'd dreamed the warmth. And they gave me this." From her sleeve she drew a small disc of dull iron, no larger than a coin, and set it on the table between them with a click that Freya felt in his teeth. A testing-iron. Cheap issue, the kind they pressed into informants' palms — it did nothing on its own, nothing at all, until a keeper's gift stirred the air around it. Then it sang. "They said if I brought them proof, it'd be ten crowns instead of five. And if I didn't come back by the fourth bell, they'd come find *me*."

She turned the iron over with one chapped finger. It lay there, inert, grey as the sky.

"They're watching from the cloth-seller's awning," she said. "Two of them. Grey coats. They've been there since the second bell. I'm sorry." The words came out of her like blood from a wound, all at once, unstoppable. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, my father can't breathe, they say there's a physician in the warden-house that gifted folk are taken to, they say it's not — they say it's just registration, just a ledger, just —"

"Merrin." Freya said her name quietly, and something in his voice made her stop. He looked at her — really looked, the way he had looked at a hundred customers, a thousand, seeing the fear and the want and the small human machinery underneath — and he found that he could not hate her. That was the cruelest part. He could see the whole shape of it: a girl in a drowning kingdom, handed a rope made of someone else's neck. "Look at me. Whatever happens in the next few minutes — you did what you did to keep your father breathing. I understand that better than you could know. I need you to know that I understand it."

Her face crumpled. "Then don't make me —"

"Pick up the iron, Merrin."

Her hand trembled toward it. And Freya — because three years of hiding had not burned the keeper out of him, because the same instinct that had sealed the Hollow Gate a thousand times in the dark still lived in his hands — did the one thing he had sworn he would never do in the open market of Vess.

He let the hum rise.

Just a breath of it. A thread. He let it climb from the banked coal in his chest up through his arms and out through his fingertips, warm as summer pavement, and he watched Merrin's eyes go wide as the testing-iron began — softly, sweetly, horribly — to sing.

It was a high, clear note, like a wet finger on a glass rim, and it cut through the rain-noise of the market like a blade. Heads turned under the awnings. Across the square, by the cloth-seller's stall, two figures in grey coats went very still, and then very much not still.

"Why," Merrin breathed. The iron was shivering against the wood. "Why would you — they're *here* —"

"Because if you'd gone back without proof," Freya said, already sweeping his satchel closed, "they'd have taken you next. A girl who names a charm-seller and can't produce him. They'd have wondered why. Do you understand? You're not my betrayer, Merrin. You're my witness. Sing, little iron. Sing loud."

The warden on the left shouted something. The market came apart the way markets do — not all at once but in a spreading wave, stalls folding, bodies flowing, a general animal consensus that grey coats meant *run*. Freya was already moving. He flipped the table's edge and sent his harmless charms scattering — river-stones and red thread skidding across wet cobbles, a small bright chaos to tangle the first warden's boots — and then he was through the gap behind the awning and into the mouth of Tanner's Row, and Vess closed around him like water around a dropped stone.

* * *

He had run these roofs in his mind a hundred times. That was the keeper's discipline: know every exit, count every path, and never — *never* — need them. Now he needed them, and his body remembered what his mind had rehearsed, and the two moved together through the rain with a desperate, singing clarity.

Tanner's Row was a funnel of awnings and shouting; he went through the dyer's yard instead, over a wall slick with rain-rot, dropping into the alley behind the chandler's where the smells of tallow and lye hit him like a wall. Behind him, boots. A whistle — the warden's two-note signal, *prey sighted*, repeated by another voice further off, and then a third, and Freya understood with cold clarity that this was not two men with a testing-iron. This was a net. They had seeded the whole market. They had known since the second bell. Perhaps since the edicts. Perhaps — and this thought arrived like ice water — since long before.

He went up.

The chandler's lean-to gave him the first handhold, then a drainpipe, then the low roof of the cooperage where tar-pots steamed in the rain and his boots slipped on shingles gone black with weather. Vess unrolled beneath him: the grey sprawl of tiled roofs, the canal cutting its silver seam, the Temple of the Nine with its nine bells hanging silent in the rain. He ran the ridgeline, low and fast, breath tearing, and below and behind him the whistles multiplied and wove together into a pattern he recognized from three years ago, from another life: the closing circle. The wardens' hunt-craft. They weren't chasing him. They were *herding* him, funnels of grey coats narrowing the streets, driving him — where?

Toward the canal bridge. Toward the open square where a wagon waited with a canvas top and four men around it.

No. He cut left, off the ridge, down onto the roof of the fulling mill where great wet bolts of cloth steamed in the yard, and the drop jarred his knees and sent a bolt of pain up his thighs that he welcomed, because pain was simple, pain was *now*. The canal. If he could reach the canal —

"Freya!"

The voice came from below and to the left, from the bakery, from the one place in all of Vess that had ever felt like a door left open for him, and his heart went down into his boots.

Tomas stood in the bakery's yard with a peel in one hand and flour to his elbows, a big soft mountain of a man with a beard like storm-cloud and the only friend Freya had let himself have in three careful years. Rain plastered his hair. Behind him the bakery's ovens glowed, and the smell of morning bread — Freya would remember this, he would remember it all his life, the smell of *bread*, of rye and honey and the woodsmoke of the morning's first firing — rose up through the rain like something holy.

"Back door," Tomas said, low and fast, as Freya dropped into the yard. "Through the cooling room, out the alley, I've the cart there, the flour cart, you can be at the east gate before —"

"Tomas." Freya grabbed his forearm. Flour and rain and the steady drumbeat of the man's pulse under his grip. "The edicts. They'll come here. They'll know we're friends."

"I burned the mark on my own doorstep, didn't I?" Tomas said. His grin was the same one he'd worn the day they'd met, when Freya had been a stranger buying a loaf and Tomas had given him two. "Old hearth-sign. Means *shelter*. Let them read it. Go, you great fool, *go* —"

The yard gate burst open.

They came through it the way water comes through a cracked dam — first one warden in a grey coat, then three, then six, iron-tipped staves in their hands and the rain streaming off their cowls. Freya knew the lead one. Everyone in Vess knew Warden-Sergeant Hesk by his missing left ear and his voice like a hinge that had never once been oiled.

"There he is." Hesk's eyes, small and wet as currants, went from Freya to Tomas and back. "And the baker. Well. The baker with the hearth-sign burned on his own step. That's aiding and harboring, that is. That's a cell at Bell Street, that is."

"Tomas, *run*," Freya said.

But Tomas was already setting down the bread peel. Setting it down gently, the way he did everything, as if the world were made of things that deserved care. He stepped sideways, that enormous body unfolding to its full height, and planted himself between Freya and the wardens like a wall that had decided, after long consideration, to be a wall.

"Boy's done nothing," Tomas said. "Sold charms. Little knots and stones. You want to arrest a man for knots, Hesk, you'd best arrest every grandmother in Vess."

"Step aside, baker."

"No," said Tomas, pleasantly.

What happened next happened fast, and Freya lived the rest of his life inside those few seconds. Hesk's stave came up. Tomas swung — not at the warden, never at the warden, just a great sweeping blow of his arm that caught two of them and sent them sprawling into the mud — and the yard exploded. Freya was moving, through the cooling room, past the ovens whose heat licked his face like a living thing, through the back door into the alley where the flour cart stood with its canvas cover — and behind him he heard the crack of a stave on bone, heard Tomas *roar*, heard the roar break off into something else, something that stopped Freya's feet for one terrible half-second at the mouth of the alley.

He ran anyway.

That was the thing he could never explain afterward, not to Isolde on the Salt Road, not to himself in the dark hours of a hundred campsites: he ran anyway. Because Tomas had spent himself — Freya heard it, knew it with a keeper's terrible clarity, the way you know a bell has cracked by its tone — so that Freya could run, and to stop running would be to spend Tomas for nothing. Survival began there, in that alley, in the rain, and it began already costing. It would never stop costing.

The flour cart took him two streets, crouched under canvas in the dark smelling of milled wheat while boots pounded past and whistles crossed overhead like gulls. Then the east gate, where the queue of carts was long and the guards were checking faces against a parchment list, and the cart was waved through on the strength of the driver's boredom before the guards reached the back. Then the fields. Then the grey rain, and the road, and the city of Vess shrinking behind him into the murk, its nine bells ringing the fourth hour, small and clear and impossibly far away.

Freya walked until the light failed. Then he sat down under a hedgerow with the rain coming through the branches, and he let himself feel it — all of it, all at once, the way he had never let himself feel anything in three careful years.

Merrin's face, crumpling. *My father can't breathe.*

Tomas's arm sweeping the wardens into the mud. *No,* said pleasantly.

The sound the roar had broken off into.

He put his face in his hands. Beneath his skin, the hum still burned, banked and patient and useless — a gift that could seal the Hollow Gate against the end of the world and could not buy back one baker's freedom, could not unname one frightened girl, could not do a single small human thing that mattered. He had spent three years being careful, and careful had gotten Tomas taken, and the edicts had gotten Merrin hungry, and the king — the *usurper*, the man who had taken a throne and was now taking a kingdom apart gift by gift, name by name — sat warm and crowned behind walls Freya would never reach.

Five crowns for a name. Twenty for a capture.

He thought: *they'll torture him.* He thought: *he'll give them nothing, because he's Tomas, and then they'll give him something worse than questions.* He thought: *I could go back. I could go back and turn myself in and —* and the thought was a door, and behind the door was the Hollow Gate, and behind the Gate was everything the first kings had bound, and the door did not open for love. It did not open for guilt. It opened for keeper's blood, and that was the whole of his life's terrible arithmetic.

Somewhere out in the dark, an owl called, once. The rain went on falling, patient as the kingdom's new hunger.

Freya raised his head. East, the road ran pale through the fields toward the hills and the free cities and a life he could not yet imagine — and behind him, faint on the night wind, Vess's nine bells rang the midnight, and beneath their ringing, so faint he might have dreamed it, he heard the other sound. The one only a Gatekeeper could hear. The deep, slow pulse of the Hollow Gate beneath the city, steady as a sleeping heart.

It had never sounded like that before.

It sounded, he thought as he stood and turned his back on everything he had lost — it sounded *uneasy*.
