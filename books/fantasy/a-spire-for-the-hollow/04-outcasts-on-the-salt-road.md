## 4. Outcasts on the Salt Road

The Salt Road began where Vess's cobbles ended, and for the first mile it was all I could do not to look back.

I had told myself I would not look back. I had made a whole speech of it in my head while climbing down the drainpipe behind Tomas's bakery, my satchel of charms digging into my shoulder blade, the smoke of the burning quarter rising behind me like a black flag. *Don't look back*, I'd said to myself. *Looking back is how they catch you.*

I looked back anyway.

Vess lay in its bowl of hills with its spires catching the last grey light, and even from this distance I could see the wardens' lanterns moving through the streets in patient lines, the way a comb moves through hair, looking for lice. Somewhere down there was Tomas's bakery, and Tomas himself, who had pressed a warm loaf into my hands not six hours before the edict went up and said, *Go east, you fool, and don't write to me.* Somewhere down there was the Hollow Gate beneath the city, sealed under forty feet of stone and three years of my careful, unremarkable life.

And somewhere down there was the girl with the nervous hands who had bought a luck charm from me twice, and whose face I would be seeing every time I closed my eyes for a long time to come.

I turned east and walked.

* * *

The Salt Road earned its name honestly. It ran white through the brown country, packed salt and crushed shell from some ancient trade that had died before anyone's grandmother was born, and it glittered faintly even at dusk, so that walking it felt like walking along the spine of something long dead. There were no trees near it — nothing grew within thirty paces of the salt — which meant no cover, no shadow, nowhere to hide if hoofbeats came up behind you. The road's makers had wanted travelers to see each other coming. In the old days, I supposed, that had been a courtesy.

Now it was a gallows.

I walked through the night because stopping felt like dying, and by the time the sun came up sick and yellow over the eastern hills, my feet had gone past pain into a kind of numb buzzing, and my mouth tasted like copper and fear. I had passed two farmsteads, both shuttered, both with fresh edicts nailed to their gates — the crown's seal, Aldous's faceless crest, the same words in the same terrible tidy hand: *All persons harboring or concealing the gift of sorcery shall be held accessory to treason.* One of the edicts had been torn halfway down. Someone had tried to rip it off and been interrupted, or had lost their nerve, or had simply run out of strength. The torn half flapped in the morning wind like a small desperate bird.

That was when I saw the cart.

It sat in the ditch where the road crossed a dry streambed, tilted at an angle that suggested it had gotten there quickly and unhappily. A mule stood nearby, grazing the salt-starved grass with the profound indifference of its kind. And beside the cart, arguing in fierce whispers, were three people who looked, to my practiced eye, exactly as I felt: like people running.

The first was a big man, broad through the shoulders, with a warden's travel cloak — the grey one, the plain one, without the white trim of rank — worn the way an honest man wears stolen goods, constantly aware of it. He had a short beard going grey at the chin and a face that might have been handsome before someone had rearranged part of it around the left eye. He was standing between the other two and the road, arms folded, doing the thing wardens do with their bodies that makes doorways feel smaller.

The second was a woman perhaps fifty, weathered as an old fence post, with iron-grey hair escaping its braid and ink stains on her fingers. She was holding the cart's broken axle pin in her cupped hands and looking at it with an expression of such tender concentration that I knew — knew before anything else about her — what she was doing. She wasn't examining the pin. She was *mending* it. I could feel it from thirty paces, a faint warmth in the air like the memory of a hearth, the smallest possible working, the kind hedge-witches did for eggs and hinges and feverish children. A child's gift, they called it. Useless. Harmless. Worth the rope, now.

The third was a boy, fourteen maybe, all elbows and dirty blond hair, sitting cross-legged on the cart's bench turning a lock over in his fingers — a padlock, brass, expensive-looking, and very much not attached to anything. He was whispering to it. Not muttering. Whispering, close, the way you'd speak to a shy animal, and the lock's shackle popped open with a soft click like a sigh.

I should have walked on. Every survival instinct I owned — and three years of double living had bred a whole stable of them — said walk on, keep your head down, strangers are just dangers you haven't been introduced to yet.

But the woman looked up and saw me seeing her, and her hands closed over the mended pin, and her face went white, and the big man turned, and I watched him take in my satchel, my city boots, my road-dusted cloak, and arrive at his conclusions with the speed of a man who'd spent his life arriving at conclusions.

"Road's wide," he said. His voice was gravel over deep water. "Walk it."

"I intend to," I said. "East."

"Everyone's going east." He shifted his weight, and I saw his hand rest easy near the knife at his belt — not threatening, just *there*, the way weather is there. "Doesn't mean we're traveling companions."

"We're not," I agreed. "I'm passing through your argument."

The boy snorted. "Argument. Brannic, he thinks it's an *argument*. Isolde's been calling you a mule for an hour."

"A mule," the woman — Isolde — said mildly, not taking her eyes off me, "who broke our axle pin jumping a rut at speed because he refuses to believe the road has ruts." She opened her hands then, showing me the pin, and I saw the hairline crack that had split it was gone, healed smooth, the metal bright along the seam like new-scarred skin. Her eyes met mine over her cupped hands, and there was a question in them, sharp and quiet: *What did you see? What will you do?*

I could have lied. I was good at lying; it had kept me alive. I could have said, *Nice repair work, must have a spare in the cart,* and walked east and never thought of them again except at three in the morning.

Instead I heard myself say, "You shouldn't do workings in the open. Even small ones. The salt carries sound, and feeling farther."

Silence. The wind moved the torn edict on the far gatepost. The boy stopped turning the lock.

Brannic's hand came off his knife slowly, which from a warden — even a disgraced one, even a former one — was practically an embrace. "You felt that," he said. "From thirty paces."

"I felt it from the top of the hill," I admitted, because apparently three years of caution had burned up somewhere between Vess and this ditch, and what was left in me was tired enough to be honest. "You're leaking, mistress. Like a bucket with a bad seam."

Isolde laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her. "Thirty years I've done smallwork," she said. "Never once been caught. Three days on this road and I'm a leaky bucket."

"The road's different now," I said. "Everyone's listening differently."

That landed on all three of them, I could see it land, and for a moment we four stood in the ditch among the salt grass and understood each other completely, which is a rare and terrible thing between strangers.

"Well," said the boy, breaking it, hopping down off the bench with the padlock still in his fist. "He knows things. I vote he fixes the wheel, since Isolde's used up her daily miracle on the pin and Brannic's useless with his hands. Big hands. No finesse." He stuck out a grimy palm toward me. "Pip."

"Pip," Brannic said warningly.

"What? He's got keeper's scars on his right palm, I saw them when he waved. Anyone with keeper's scars can't be worse company than you two." Pip grinned at me, quick and crooked, and my stomach dropped through the salt into the cold earth below, because the boy was right — the old seal-scars, the fine silvered lines across my right palm where the binding rites had marked me at nineteen, were showing clear as writing in the morning light.

I had covered them for three years. Gloves, grease, a story about a kettle. And I had been out here one night and one morning and already I was leaking too.

Isolde was looking at my hand now, and her expression had changed — not fear, not greed, but something older and gentler and much harder to bear. Recognition. Pity, almost. "Ah," she said softly. "Ah, lad."

"Don't," I said.

She raised her hands, palms out. "I said nothing."

"You said it *loudly*."

Brannic stepped closer, and up close I could see the warden's brand on his wrist, half burned away — removed, deliberately, painfully, by someone who had decided the order was worth more than the skin. "Gatekeeper," he said. It wasn't a question.

"There's no Gate to keep," I said. "There hasn't been for three hundred years. It's an old word for an old job, and the job is minding seals nobody's ever seen, and right now the job is *walking east*, same as yours." I looked at the broken cart, the tired mule, the three of them — a disgraced warden, a hedge-witch, a thief-child, the whole ragged census of Aldous's new kingdom — and I heard myself make the mistake I would spend chapters of my life explaining. "Your axle's cracked in two places, not one. The pin she mended won't hold the load. You'll break down again by midday, and midday is when patrols ride this stretch. I've seen their schedules. I sold charms to half the garrison."

More silence. Then Pip said, delighted, "He *scryed* the patrol routes off garrison customers. Oh, I like him."

"I didn't scry anything, I listened—"

"He listens," Pip told the others, as if reporting a marvel. "To soldiers. About axles."

Brannic rubbed his ruined eye socket, a gesture I would learn meant he was thinking hard and hated it. "We're not a caravan," he said finally, to me, to all of us, laying down law. "We're not a fellowship. We're four people whose roads happen to overlap until they don't. Nobody asks questions they don't want answered. Nobody works magic in the open—" a glance at Isolde, who had the grace to look abashed, "—and nobody dies for anybody else. Agreed?"

"Cheerful," said Pip.

"Honest," said Brannic.

I looked east, where the road ran white and empty toward the free cities and whatever thin hope lived there, and west, where the lanterns of Vess were still combing the streets, and I thought about Tomas saying *go east, you fool,* and about how the safest thing in the world was to be alone, and how alone had gotten a baker taken in my place, and how alone was starting to feel less like safety and more like the shape the hunt preferred its prey.

"Agreed," I said. "My name's Freya."

* * *

We fixed the wheel by midmorning. That is to say, I found the second crack, Isolde coaxed the wood true with a working so small and so controlled it barely stirred the air — she was better than she claimed, far better, a surgeon of small things — and Brannic lifted the cart bed one-handed while Pip, hanging underneath like a cheerful parasite, whispered the rusted nut loose in a way I politely did not watch.

By noon we had a system none of us acknowledged: Brannic walked point with his hood up, reading the road like the ex-warden he insisted he wasn't; Isolde rode the cart and dozed and pretended not to listen to everything; Pip ranged ahead and behind in loops, appearing and vanishing like a thought you couldn't quite finish; and I walked beside the mule, because the mule asked no questions.

Her name was Turnip. This was Pip's information, delivered unprompted.

The salt country rolled on, treeless and bright, and the edicts appeared at every crossroads, every wellhead, every shrine — and there were more shrines than I'd expected, little stone cairns hung with ribbon and bone charms, the folk-religion of people who had never trusted the crown's temples. Someone had defaced most of them. Someone else, at the third shrine, had nailed up a response: a strip of linen with charcoal letters, crude but determined. *THE OLD KINGS BOUND WHAT SLEEPS. WAKE NOTHING. TRUST NO CROWN.*

I read it twice and said nothing, and felt the seal-scars on my palm itch, which they only ever did when I thought too hard about what slept beneath Vess.

We camped that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, barley tea, half a loaf Isolde produced from the cart with the mysterious abundance of grandmothers everywhere. Nobody told stories. But stories leaked anyway, the way Isolde's workings leaked, in seams and fragments.

Brannic, staring into the fire: "Twenty-two years in the whites. I trained half the captains now hunting us. I taught Serah Vane to track in snow." Then silence, as if he'd said more than he meant, which he had, and I filed the name away.

Isolde, mending a tear in Pip's sleeve without being asked, thread pulling itself: "My daughter had the gift. Brighter than mine. She registered, year one, because the edicts seemed reasonable then." The thread stopped. Started again. "They publish the lists, you know. Monthly. I read them at every town. She isn't on any of them, which means either she's hidden well or—" The thread knotted itself and stayed knotted.

Pip, upside down against the waystone, tossing the brass padlock and catching it: "Locks talk. Always have, since I was small. They tell you what they're afraid of." A grin at the fire. "Mostly they're afraid of me."

And me, I gave them the least dangerous true thing I owned: "I sold charms. Luck, sleep, safe childbirth. Nine parts superstition, one part smallwork. Three years in Vess and nobody ever looked at me twice." I turned my scarred palm toward the firelight, let them see it plainly, because hiding it had lasted exactly one day and honesty, I was learning, was a currency among fugitives. "Until somebody did."

None of them asked what I'd really been keeping. Later — much later — I would understand that this restraint was the first gift they gave me, though none of them knew they were giving it.

* * *

On the third morning, the hounds started.

Not close. Distant, a threading of baying through the eastern hills, thin as wire. But Brannic went rigid at the sound like a man hearing his own name called from a grave, and Isolde's hands stilled over her mending, and Pip dropped out of the rocks above us silent as falling dust, all grin gone.

"Two parties," he said quietly. "One on the road behind, one cutting across the flats ahead. Moving like they know where we'll be."

"Wardens don't guess," Brannic said. His voice had gone flat and cold, the professional voice, and it frightened me more than the hounds. "Somebody's tracking. Something of ours. Blood, hair, a worked charm — they'll have a scent-reader with them." He looked at each of us, and last at me, longest at me. "Anything in that satchel carry your feeling, Gatekeeper? Anything you've handled long?"

I thought of the luck charms. Dozens of them, made over three years, each one soaked through with hours of my hands, my hum, my careful hidden warmth. Sold, scattered, gone. Except—

Except the girl had bought two. And the second one, the one she'd never paid for, the one I'd pressed into her hands when she cried and said her brother had been taken — that one I had made strong. Made *well*. Made full of me.

"They'll have a charm of mine," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "A strong one. From Vess."

Brannic closed his eyes. When he opened them, all the wariness of the first day was still there — but underneath it now, unmistakable, was something else. Something that looked, God help me, like decision.

"Then we stop being four roads overlapping," he said, and turned the mule east, off the salt, into the hills. "Keep up."

Behind us, faint and getting less faint, the hounds sang — and ahead, in the hill country where no maps I owned went, our real road began.
