## 6. The Usurper's Hounds

The hounds found the trail on the third morning out of Vess, and Brannic knew it before the handlers said a word — knew it by the way the big brindled bitch swung her head east and held it there, ears flat, throat working around a sound that was half whine and half hunger.

He had trained that dog. He had trained her handler too, back when the wardens were something other than what they had become.

"Fresh sign, Captain," said Wren, the youngest of the six riders Serah Vane had brought down from the citadel, his voice bright with the particular eagerness of a man who has never yet had to look at what he catches. "Boot prints by the drainage culvert. Four sets, maybe five. One of them small."

Small. That would be the boy — Pip, the charm-seller had called him, though Brannic doubted it was the child's true name any more than *Freya Nakamura* had been the whole truth of the man who sold luck in paper packets. Brannic crouched at the culvert's mouth and studied the prints without touching them. Mud still glistened in their hollows. The fugitives had passed in the night, walking fast but not running, which meant they did not yet know they were hunted. Or they hoped they weren't.

"They'll make for the Alden ford," he said. "It's the only crossing inside two days' walk, and the ferry at Marsh Hollow is burned." He stood, brushed the mud from his knee, and did not look at Serah Vane as he said it. "You could take them there. Or you could let me ride ahead alone and talk to them first. The charm-seller isn't dangerous. None of them are. They're frightened people running from—"

"From the law, Captain Galloway." Serah's voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. She sat her grey mare with the stillness of a drawn bow, gloved hands folded on the pommel, her face composed into an expression Brannic recognized from ten years of watching her learn it: nothing wasted, nothing shown. "There is no law anymore except the king's word, and the king's word says these people belong to him. You may find that distasteful. I confess I have stopped finding anything distasteful. It saves time."

"You were better than this." The words came out of him before he could weigh them, rough as gravel. "At the academy you argued a case for three hours to keep a hedge-witch from the branding iron because the evidence was thin. I stood in the gallery and thought, *there is the finest officer the wardens will ever produce.*"

Something moved behind Serah's eyes — quick, deep, immediately drowned, like a fish under ice. "And then you disgraced yourself at Cael Rowan," she said, "and the finest officer the wardens will ever produce learned that evidence is a luxury the crown no longer stocks. Ride ahead if you like, Captain. Talk to your frightened people. But know that when you fail to bring them in — and you will fail, because mercy is a language they've stopped trusting — my hounds will do what hounds do." She turned the mare east. "Wren. Give Captain Galloway the slow horse. We wouldn't want him arriving before his conscience does."

The column laughed. It was a young laughter, unburdened, and Brannic rode at its rear all that day carrying it like a stone in his chest.

* * *

They made camp that night on a ridge above the Alden water, and Brannic lay awake listening to the river talk to itself in the dark.

He had served the wardens for nineteen years. He had believed in them the way some men believe in saints: imperfect, yes, occasionally cruel, but aimed at something holy. The old oath had been *to shield the many from the few whose gifts run wild* — and Brannic had lived it gladly, had pulled children from burning houses alongside fire-gifted healers who worked under warden license, had shaken the hands of registered mages at midwinter festivals. There had been a time when the brand on a gifted criminal's wrist was a last resort, applied after trial, after appeal, after every gentler door had been tried and locked.

Then Aldous had taken the throne over his brother's body, and the doors had begun locking from the outside.

Brannic's own fall had come early — a year ago now, though some nights it felt like yesterday and others like another life. A registered weather-worker named Maren Ostrey, sixty years old, licensed since girlhood, accused of hexing a granary. The evidence was a dead pigeon and a jealous neighbor. The order came down from the new Lord Warden: branding and confiscation, no trial, the king's edict superseding statute. And Brannic — fool, honest fool — had refused the order aloud, in the yard, in front of forty men. He had spent three months in his own cells for it. When they released him it was not forgiveness; it was arithmetic. They were short-handed, and a disgraced captain who hated himself was more useful than no captain at all. So they put him back in uniform with his rank stripped to a courtesy title and sent him to hunt the very people he had once sworn to shield, knowing — they must have known — that each capture would grind another layer off whatever remained of his soul.

And here was the thing he could not say to anyone, the thing that woke him on ridges above dark rivers: some mornings, grinding felt like relief. Some mornings it was easier to be the hammer than to lie awake being the hand that should have stopped it.

Below him, the hounds stirred in their pickets, and one of them — the brindled bitch, Bess, whom he'd raised from a pup on scraps and patience — lifted her head and looked at him across the dark with yellow eyes, and Brannic thought: *she remembers who fed her.* Dogs remembered. It was men who had learned to forget.

* * *

They struck the ford at dawn, or rather Serah struck it, because she had split the column in the night — Brannic realized it only when he crested the ridge and saw her banners already deployed along the far bank, riders spaced in the reeds, the hounds held in close at the waterline where the mist hung like unwashed wool.

"She moved without telling you," Wren said beside him, and there was no mockery in it now, only a kind of awe. "She said you'd warn them off otherwise."

"I would have," Brannic said. There was no point lying to the boy. "Watch the near bank. If anyone breaks cover toward you, shout *hold* and mean it. If Serah gives the order to loose, count to five before you draw. Five seconds is long enough to miss a heart."

Wren looked at him strangely. Brannic kept his eyes on the ford.

It was a poor crossing even in good weather — the Alden spread wide and shallow there over a shelf of gravel, knee-deep at the center, but the banks rose steep and rooty on either side and the old toll-house squatted at the near approach with its gate chained shut. The toll-house was why Brannic's stomach had been tight since midnight. He had crossed here a hundred times in his service years. He knew the toll-house had a cellar, and the cellar had a grate, and the grate had a lock — an old one, a good one, forged by a locksmith in Marsh Hollow who had died owing Brannic a favor and paid it in ironwork instead. A lock like that would stop a fugitive cold.

Unless the fugitive could speak to locks.

He saw them come out of the trees at first light exactly as he'd predicted: four figures moving low along the near bank, the tall charm-seller in front, the hedge-witch with her satchel clutched like a shield, the boy darting ahead and back like a bird testing a cat's reach. They saw the far bank a heartbeat later. Brannic watched the whole shape of their hope collapse from two hundred yards — watched Freya Nakamura go rigid, watched him turn to say something to the others, watched the small group huddle in the roots of an alder like animals deciding whether to bolt.

Serah's horn sounded. Clean, clear, terrible. The hounds went into the water.

What followed was not a battle; Brannic had been in battles, and battles had lines and noise and a certain honesty of violence. This was a hunt. The hounds hit the shallows in a white spray, baying that sound they made — the one the trainers called *the announcement*, designed to freeze prey in place — and the fugitives scattered along the bank like leaves. Arrows hissed past Brannic's shoulder from the far side, pinning the alder tree, pinning the path south. Serah had planned it well. She always planned well. That had never been the problem.

The problem was the toll-house.

Brannic saw it happen almost too late to matter: the boy — Pip, small and quick, face white as bone — broke away from the others and ran *toward* the toll-house, straight at the chained gate, while the hounds wheeled after the taller figures. For one absurd moment Brannic thought the child had gone mad with fear, was simply throwing himself at the nearest wall. Then Pip dropped to his knees at the gate's padlock, pressed both hands against the iron, and *whispered.*

Brannic had heard of such gifts. Lock-whisperers appeared in the old registers once a generation, listed between coin-singers and candle-hearths, classed as trivial, harmless, barely worth the ink. He had never seen one work. He had certainly never seen one work under the announcement-baying of six scent-hounds with arrows in the air.

The chain slid off the gate like a snake shedding its skin. The padlock's shackle popped open with a click Brannic heard across fifty yards of river-mist, a small domestic sound, obscene in all that chaos. The gate groaned wide. Pip vanished into the dark of the toll-house, and a moment later the others followed — the witch hauling the charm-seller by the arm, all four of them swallowed by the doorway just as Serah's riders reached the near bank.

"Hold!" Brannic shouted, and this time he meant it with his whole chest, throwing himself between the reeds and the riders coming up the path. "Hold, damn you — the building's got a cellar and a second door, we'll have them boxed in an hour! Nobody looses an arrow into a stone room full of prisoners!"

It was even true. It was mostly true. It was also, he knew, buying seconds, and seconds were all the fugitives needed, because he had built that cellar's second door into his report of the terrain and Serah had waved it off — *a rusted hatch in a ditch, Captain, hardly worth posting a man* — and so no rider stood at the ditch, and when the four of them came up out of the ground thirty minutes later, wet to the waist and shaking, the road north lay open and empty before them.

Brannic was the one who found the abandoned padlock in the mud by the gate. He turned it over in his palm. The mechanism was untouched — not picked, not forced, not broken. Every tumbler simply *open*, aligned as neatly as a choir. As if the lock had been asked politely, and had wanted, very much, to say yes.

"Captain." Serah stood behind him, and when he turned, her face was unreadable as ever, but her eyes had gone somewhere cold and calculating. "That boy opened a warded gate-lock in under ten breaths. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means he's a child with a small gift and no training, and you nearly put an arrow through him for it."

"It means," she said, ignoring him with the ease of long practice, "that the king's list of the gifted is incomplete. It means there are more of them hiding than the registries ever caught. It means—" and here her voice dropped, so that only Brannic heard it, and something in the dropping made the hair rise on his neck, "—that the charm-seller is traveling with people worth having. Which means the charm-seller is worth more than we were told. Which means, Captain Galloway, that when I write my report tonight, I am going to recommend we stop treating this as a rounding-up and start treating it as what it is." She took the padlock from his hand, weighed it, closed her fist around it. "A pursuit. With a quarry worth the crown's whole attention."

"And what is it, Serah?" Brannic asked quietly. "What is he? Because I rode with those people for a day before you caught our trail, back when I still pretended to be hunting them honestly, and I'll tell you what I saw. A man who sold luck-charms to washerwomen. A witch who mends cloth. A thief who talks to hinges. Whatever the king thinks he's chasing, it isn't an army."

For a moment — one moment, brief as a fish under ice again — Serah Vane looked tired. Deeply, humanly tired, in a way that made her suddenly, unbearably young to him, the academy girl who had argued three hours for a hedge-witch's wrists.

"No," she said. "It isn't an army. But the man who wrote my orders used the word *Gatekeeper* this morning, Captain, and he didn't use it idly, and until I know what it means, I'm going to keep riding." She turned away, then paused, her back to him, the mist beading on her shoulders. "You counted to five today. Before drawing. I saw you do it twice." Her voice was very level. "The next time you do it, I will have to decide whether it was slowness or treason. I would prefer not to decide. Try to be slow about something else."

She walked back to her horse, and Brannic stood alone among the reeds with the river running cold and constant behind him, and understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound opening, that he had perhaps a day left — a day, maybe two — before Serah Vane decided what he was, and before he had to decide it faster than she did.

North, over the hills, four frightened people were running for their lives, and one of them was the last of something old and terrible, and the woman he had raised to be the best of the wardens was riding to drag them back into the dark.

Brannic tightened his saddle girth, checked the slow horse's hooves, and began, quietly, deliberately, to think about how a man loses a trail on purpose — and what it would cost him when she noticed.
