## 7. A Family Made of Necessity

The waystation had been dead longer than Pip had been alive. He could tell by the way the ruins sat in the landscape — not broken the way a thing is broken when it dies fighting, but settled, the way a thing dies when everyone simply stops coming. The roof had folded inward like a cupped hand. Moss owned the eastern wall. Someone had carved a blessing into the lintel once, in old Vessian script, and the rain had spent fifty years wearing it down to a suggestion: *rest, traveler, and be*.

Pip had never been anywhere that felt like it wanted him to rest.

"Mind the third step," Isolde said behind him, and he looked down to see the flagstone tilted on a bed of shattered timber, waiting to drop an ankle into darkness. He stepped over it. She'd only been inside for ten heartbeats and she already knew the floor better than he did. Hedge-witches, he decided, were strange people.

Brannic came in last, as always, with his back to the road and his hand on the knife he wasn't supposed to have anymore. A disgraced warden carried no badge, but Brannic's hands hadn't learned that yet. They kept reaching for things that weren't there — a hilt, a whistle, a wall of brothers in grey who would never stand beside him again.

"We're two days from Mereth," Freya said. He set down the satchel of charms he guarded like other men guarded children, and stood a moment in the ruined doorway with the last grey light behind him, so that Pip could see the shape of him against the drowned sky — narrow shoulders, ink-stained fingers, a man built entirely out of quiet. "If we don't light a fire, we'll freeze. If we do, they might see the smoke."

"They might," Brannic agreed.

They lit the fire. That was the whole argument, and it told Pip more about these three strangers than any of them would ever say out loud: they had decided, somewhere on the Salt Road without discussing it, that warmth was worth the risk. Pip crouched by the hearthstones and fed kindling into the first flame with hands that wanted to shake. He'd stolen candles before. He'd stolen lamps, lanterns, a chandelier once, off a cart, which had gone very badly. But he had never in his eleven years stood in a room where someone else was also cold, and watched four people choose the same small fire.

He was used to being alone in rooms. It startled him, every time, that he wasn't anymore.

* * *

Isolde made tea from pine needles and something dried she kept in a twist of cloth, and it tasted like bark and bitterness and, somehow, like kindness anyway. Wren — her gift was called mending, but Pip had watched her lay two fingers on Brannic's split knuckles on the second night of their acquaintance and seen the skin close like water smoothing over a stone — Wren sat close to the flames and pulled her shawl down over her wrists and said, without preamble, because that was how she did everything:

"My husband sold me for a horse."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke. Pip wrapped both hands around his tin cup and waited, the way you wait outside a door you've just picked, listening for whether anyone's home.

"Not a good horse," she went on, staring into the flame. "A lame one. A bay mare with a bad hip, twenty years old if she was a day. The magistrate in Corren's Ford needed a mount and Tam needed coin for dice, and I — " she smiled, a small terrible smile, "— I had mended the neighbor's fence and the miller's roof-beam and the reeve's daughter's crooked spine, all for nothing, all because I couldn't bear to watch a thing stay broken. Word gets around, in a village. Word got around. Tam traded me before they came for me, and took the mare, and I hope to every god listening that she threw him into the ravine at Wolf's Bend."

"Did she?" Pip asked.

"I'll never know." She drank her bitter tea. "I ran east for six weeks. Slept in ditches. Ate things I won't describe while you're drinking that. And then I stopped running, because I realized nobody was chasing me. I wasn't worth chasing." Her eyes went around the fire, resting on each of them a half-second longer than was comfortable, which was Isolde's way of touching people. "Until now. Until I fell in with a Gatekeeper, a warden, and a thief, and became worth chasing after all."

"I'm not a warden," Brannic said, too fast.

"You wear your shame like a badge, love. It's shinier than the real ones."

* * *

Pip had thought about what he would tell them, in the hours he pretended to sleep with his back against the wall and his ears doing the work his eyes couldn't. He had a story prepared. Everyone who survives on the streets has a story prepared; it's the currency you pay grown-ups with, the sad-eyed tale that opens purses and closes questions. His prepared story had a mother who died of fever and a father lost at sea and a grandmother who taught him cards. All lies. All smooth as river stone from handling.

But Isolde had said *sold for a horse*, plain as bread, and Freya was sitting with his cup forgotten going cold in his hands, and Brannic was looking into the fire like it owed him money or forgiveness, and Pip heard himself say:

"I had a lock."

They turned. He didn't look up. There was a splinter in the floorboard by his knee shaped like a little bird, and he fixed his eyes on it and talked to it instead of them.

"When I was small. Six, maybe seven. Somebody threw out a cabinet from the tally-house — big thing, iron-banded, and the locksmith's guild keeps its discards in the alley behind the workshop on Candle Street, and I slept in the alley sometimes because the forge-warmth comes through the wall there, in winter. And this cabinet had a lock on it, a proper one, brass and steel, and I — " he shrugged, feeling his ears go hot. "I talked to it. Not words. You can't talk words to a lock, they don't have ears for words. But you sort of... hum at it. Feel for where it's *stuck*. Everything that's locked is stuck somewhere, and everything stuck wants to come unstuck. That's all picking is. Being polite about it."

"What was in the cabinet?" Isolde asked softly.

"That's the thing." He laughed, one note, ugly. "Nothing. Empty. Rusted through on the inside, floor fallen out. It was never keeping anything *in*. And I sat there in the alley with my hands on it and my gift humming, and I understood that nobody had ever locked anything away *for* me. Not a treasure. Not a secret. Nothing. The world locks its doors and I am what's on the wrong side of them." He picked at the bird-shaped splinter. It wouldn't come loose. "I lived in the alleys of Vess nine years. I know every lock in that city by feel. Four hundred and sixty-one, and not one of them ever opened onto anywhere I was allowed to be."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the dead pines like a long breath drawn and held.

"Four hundred and sixty-two," Freya said.

Pip looked up. The charm-seller was watching him with those grey eyes that always seemed to be apologizing for seeing so much.

"The Gate," Freya said. "It's a lock. The biggest one there is. And I'm going to need somebody who can talk to locks, before this is finished."

Pip's chest did something complicated and painful, and he understood, with the clarity that only comes at the worst possible moment, that he would follow this tired charm-seller into anything. He looked back down at the splinter-bird and worked it loose at last and held it in his palm, small and light as a stolen coin, and didn't say any of what he was thinking, because street kids learn early that saying a thing out loud is how you lose it.

* * *

Brannic talked last, and least. That was his way; he gave words the way a miser gives coins, one at a time, counting each.

"Serah Vane was mine," he said. "My recruit, then my second. Twelve years I trained her. Best I ever made — better than me, before the end. She could read a trail like scripture." He turned his cup slowly, watching the dregs swirl. "When Aldous took the throne and the wardens stopped catching criminals and started catching *gifted*, I wrote letters. Up the whole chain. Eleven of them. Do you know what happens to a warden-captain who writes eleven letters?" He didn't wait. "You get twelve chances to stop writing, and then you get stripped in the yard in front of the men you trained, and Serah stands in the front row and doesn't look at you, and that's how you learn you've already died and just haven't lain down yet."

"She hunts you now," Isolde said. Not a question.

"She hunts us. And she'll find us, because she's the best there is at finding, and I taught her everything I know." He set the empty cup down with great care, as though it might break, as though it mattered. "Which means everything I know is worthless. Remember that, when the hounds come. Don't trust my tricks. She grew up inside them."

"Then we'll need new tricks," Pip said, and Brannic looked at him — really looked, for the first time since the river ford — and something in the big man's face shifted a degree, like a lock turning one tumbler closer to open.

* * *

Freya went last of all, and smallest. He told them about the market stall, the luck charms, three years of selling harmless copper and hiding the hum under his skin. He told them about Tomas, the baker, who burned a warning into his own doorstep and paid for it with his freedom, and his voice broke on the name and mended itself the way Isolde mended things — quietly, leaving only the faintest seam. He told them about the Gate beneath Vess without telling them what it held, not yet, holding that card close the way careful men do, and Pip let him keep it. Everyone at this fire had earned one sealed cabinet.

But at the end Freya said, looking around at the three of them with the firelight making shadows of his face, "I ran to save myself. That's the truth. I ran because they named me and I was afraid. And now —" he stopped, started again. "Now there are four of us. And I find I'm not afraid of the same things anymore."

That was all. It was not much. It was, Pip thought, the most anyone had given him in his entire life.

* * *

Later, when Isolde slept with her back to the warm stones and Brannic took first watch at the collapsed doorway, a silhouette against the stars, Pip lay on his bedroll and listened to the ruin settle around him. Old houses talked. This one talked in the language of rain finding holes and beams giving up slowly, and underneath it he could hear Freya breathing, slow and even, three feet away.

Pip had never shared a room with anyone who wasn't trying to rob him or sell him.

He thought about locks. Four hundred and sixty-one of them, and the feel of each one's particular sticking place, the little stubborn heart of them. He thought about how a lock, hummed at gently enough, will confess anything. And he thought: *these three are stuck*. Stuck and running and hunted, each of them pried loose from a life that threw them away — a husband with dice, a yard full of turned backs, a city full of wardens — and here they lay in a dead building in the middle of nowhere, sharing a fire they couldn't afford.

Nobody had ever been locked *in* with him before.

*I pick my family,* he decided, lying still so no one would notice the decision happening. *Same as anything else. Only this time I'm keeping it.*

Worth dying for, the thought came, unbidden and calm, and it didn't frighten him the way it should have. It fit him like a key fits a lock: not comfortable exactly, but *true*, the tumblers lining up one by one in the dark of him. Isolde's bitter tea. Brannic's counted words. Freya's breaking-mending voice. His. His to guard, his to unstick if they were ever stuck, his to spend himself on.

Out at the doorway, Brannic shifted, and then went rigid, head tilting — the way he did when his old instincts woke.

"Pip," he said, very low. "Come listen."

Pip rolled to his feet silent as smoke and crossed the rubble, and Brannic pointed his chin at the night beyond the broken walls. Far off to the west, back along the Salt Road they had walked all day, a sound threaded through the pines.

Dogs. Many dogs. And under the baying, thin as wire, the high sweet note of a hunting horn.

Brannic closed his eyes. When he opened them, Pip saw a man hearing his own student calling his name across the dark.

"She found the ford," Brannic breathed. "Two days' trail, eaten in one night. Wake the others."
