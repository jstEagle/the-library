## 8. The Free Cities' Door

They saw Mereth at dawn, and Freya understood why the old songs called it the Door of the World.

The city rose from a shoulder of white cliffs where the Salt Road finally gave up its grudge and became something gentler — a paved way lined with cypress, then with lamps, then with people. Mereth did not have walls so much as it had terraces, tier upon tier of pale stone climbing toward a citadel crowned in copper that caught the early light and burned like a held breath. Below it, the harbor spread wide and glittering, crowded with masts from a dozen nations: black-hulled traders out of the Ashen Isles, slender riverboats, even a fat merchant cog flying the colors of Vess itself. Gulls wheeled and screamed over the fish-market terraces. The air smelled of brine and bread and tar, and beneath it all, faint as a memory, Freya caught the sea-salt tang of old magic worked into the foundations — wards laid so long ago they had become indistinguishable from stone.

"Gods," Pip breathed beside him. The thief stood on the crest of the last hill with his hands jammed in his pockets, as if he didn't trust himself not to reach for something. "It's *big*. I've seen big. I grew up in big. That's—" He shook his head. "That's different."

"It's free," Isolde said quietly.

Brannic said nothing at all. He had been quiet for two days, since the ford, since the hounds, since the moment he'd put a warden's blade into a warden's chest to buy them their escape. He walked like a man carrying something heavy on his shoulders that no one else could see, and when he looked at Mereth he looked at it the way a man looks at a door he isn't sure will open.

Freya felt the same. Three weeks on the road had worn them down to sinew and suspicion. His boots were ruined; his charm-bag was empty except for three luck-knots he hadn't had the heart to sell; and under his skin the Gate hummed its low, patient note, louder now than it had ever been in Vess, as if distance had made it anxious. He had learned to sleep through it. He had not learned to ignore it.

"They'll ask questions," Brannic said at last, his voice rough from disuse. "At the gates. They'll ask who you are and why you came."

"We tell them the truth," Isolde said.

"The truth is that three of us are gifted and one of us used to hunt the gifted." Brannic's mouth twisted. "The truth got Tomas taken."

Freya flinched. He'd done nothing but flinch for days whenever Tomas's name surfaced, which was often, because guilt has excellent aim. "Then we tell them most of it," he said. "Fugitives from the king's hunt. Seeking sanctuary. That much is true, and it should be enough."

*It should be enough.* He heard the words in his own voice and hated how young they sounded. But hope is a stubborn thing, and Mereth was built entirely out of it — a city that had thrown off its kings four hundred years ago and never invited another one back. Somewhere in those pale tiers there had to be room for four tired people. There had to be.

They joined the river of travelers flowing up the switchback road — farmers with carts, pilgrims, a troupe of mummers arguing about a broken wagon-axle — and let the crowd carry them. At the first terrace gate, a bored official in a slate-blue sash glanced at their faces, waved them through with a stamp of his lead seal, and turned to the next traveler before they'd finished thanking him.

Pip stared back at him, delighted. "That's it? No questions?"

"It's a trading city," Isolde said. "Questions are bad for business."

Freya kept waiting for the other shoe to fall, and it kept not falling. They climbed through the morning city — past spice stalls and dye-vats and a temple where a priestess was sweeping the steps with a broom of bundled reeds, past children running hoops along the balustrades — and no one looked at them twice. No wardens. No proclamations nailed to doors. No neighbors burning warnings into thresholds. It took him until the third terrace to realize why his shoulders ached: he had stopped expecting kindness, and here it was simply lying around in the streets like loose coin.

He almost wept over a loaf of bread. A woman sold it to him warm, wrapped in cloth, and told him where the Council Hall stood, and asked after his health, and meant it.

*This,* he thought, biting into the crust while the others argued amiably about lodging, *this is what I was protecting. This ordinary morning. This is what Aldous wants to burn.*

The Council Hall occupied the summit of the citadel, a broad crescent of marble open to the sky, ringed with columns carved into the shapes of the first free speakers — men and women with their mouths open mid-word, hands raised mid-gesture, frozen forever in the act of saying what they thought. Freya had read about it as a boy in Vess, in smuggled books his mother had hidden inside flour sacks. He had imagined it as a fortress of liberty.

It was, in fact, mostly a waiting room.

They petitioned for audience at midmorning and were given a number — an actual stamped clay token, like a butcher's ticket — and told to wait in the colonnade with perhaps forty other petitioners: a shipping consortium disputing tariffs, a widow suing a shipwright, a delegation from some inland town complaining about wolves. Sunlight crawled across the marble. Pip fell asleep sitting up. Isolde mended a stranger's torn sleeve without being asked and had to be gently warned by Brannic to stop doing magic in public, even harmless magic, even here — and the stranger thanked her and moved away, and Freya watched Isolde's face fall and knew she'd been hoping, just a little, that this would be the place where her gift wasn't a crime.

In the middle of the afternoon, a clerk finally called their token.

The council chamber was smaller than Freya expected, and rounder — a sunken floor ringed by nine high seats, nine councillors above and petitioners below, so that speaking to power felt like standing at the bottom of a well. Most of the seats were empty; three were filled. In the center sat a heavyset man with a merchant's rings and a merchant's patience, introduced as Councillor Doss. To his left, a thin woman with iron-grey hair and a soldier's posture, Councillor Veyra. To his right, an old man with a scholar's stoop, Councillor Ombas, who watched Freya with eyes like a man reading a difficult text.

"State your business," Doss said, "briefly."

Freya had rehearsed this. On the road, around fires, he had rehearsed it a dozen times — the fugitives' speech, plain and dignified. It came out anyway sounding smaller than he'd hoped.

"We come from Vess. From the east. King Aldous has begun a hunt for the gifted — registration edicts, warden sweeps, arrests. We fled rather than be chained or worse. We ask Mereth for sanctuary."

Silence, of the particular quality that precedes politics.

"You are four," Veyra said. It was not a question.

"I am a hedge-witch," Isolde said, lifting her chin. "My gift is mending. Broken things only. I have never harmed anyone."

"I whisper to locks," Pip said, before anyone could stop him, because Pip had never met a secret he trusted more than his own honesty. "Doors. Chests. They talk to me. Sort of."

Veyra's gaze moved to Brannic and sharpened. "And you wear a warden's sword-belt under that cloak. I know the harness. I signed off on enough of them."

"Brannic Hale," Brannic said. "Warden, third company, eleven years. Discharged myself the day the hunt began." He paused. "I killed two of my former brothers at the river ford three weeks back. They'd have taken these three to Aldous's cells. I'd do it again."

That landed in the chamber like a dropped stone. Doss's rings clicked against the armrest. And then, slowly, all three councillors looked at Freya — because he was the one who hadn't spoken, and silence, in a chamber like this, is itself a statement.

"And you?" Ombas asked softly.

Freya weighed everything in the space of a breath. *Tell them most of it.* "I'm a charm-seller," he said. "Luck knots, fever-charms, small things. Small magic."

Ombas went on looking at him for a moment too long, the way a man reads a difficult text, and Freya could not tell whether the old councillor had finished the page or merely marked it for later.

Doss leaned forward. "Here is Mereth's difficulty, charm-seller, and I'll give it to you plainly, because you seem tired of pretty words. Aldous has sent envoys. Twice. He offers us continued trade — grain, iron, our ships in his ports unmolested — in exchange for certain courtesies. Return of fugitives. Cooperation with his hunt." He spread his thick hands. "Mereth lives by trade. Four hundred years of freedom rests on ships that must keep sailing. If we harbor the gifted, we hand him a cause for war. If we refuse him, we may lose the peace that freedom is *for*. Do you see the shape of it?"

"I see," Freya said, and his voice came out flat, "that you're deciding whether we're people or cargo."

"Freya—" Isolde began.

"No, I want to hear it." He turned back to Doss, and the hum under his skin rose with his pulse, and he pressed it down, down, the way he'd pressed it down for three years in the market at Vess. "Say the rest. Say what you're considering."

Doss had the grace to look uncomfortable. Veyra did not.

"The rest," Veyra said, "is arithmetic. Four fugitives are worth little. Aldous's goodwill is worth much. There is a faction on this council — I won't pretend otherwise — that would put you on a ship east within the week and call it diplomacy."

"There is also a faction," Ombas said mildly, "that remembers what the free cities were founded *for*, and notes that a king who chains magicians today will find reasons to chain merchants tomorrow. Aldous does not make modest appetites. Give him a finger and he invents a hand."

"So we are to be saved by philosophy," Brannic said dryly, "while his army walks down the Salt Road."

"His army," Veyra said, "is six hundred miles east and busy digesting Vess. Which gives us time. Time is the whole of my argument, warden. Buy time, spend it wisely, and perhaps the usurper chokes on his own kingdom before he reaches our cliffs."

"And meanwhile we hide us in your cellars like contraband," Pip said. There was no flippancy in it now. He looked very young, suddenly, and very tired. "Meanwhile we're the thing everyone's polite about."

No one answered him. That was answer enough.

The councillors conferred in murmurs — actually conferred, actually deliberated, and Freya stood at the bottom of the well and listened to his fate being discussed in the language of tariffs and envoy schedules and *the autumn grain contracts*, and something in him curdled slowly, like milk left out in the sun. He had spent three weeks believing in this place. He had carried the belief like a coal in his chest through the ford and the hounds and the nights of cold rain: *get to Mereth, get behind the free walls, and breathe.* He had imagined the free cities as the opposite of Vess — as the place where the hunt's logic could not follow.

But the hunt's logic had followed. Of course it had. Fear travels faster than any army. It had gotten here first and taken seats on the council.

The murmuring stopped. Doss cleared his throat.

"Provisional sanctuary," he said. "You'll be lodged in the Tidequarter — the old customs house, disused, adequate. You'll not work magic publicly. You'll not leave the lower terraces without leave. And you'll present yourselves again when the full council convenes after the harvest fair, at which time your status will be decided properly." He hesitated, and added, not unkindly: "I'd counsel you to give the council reasons to be brave. Reasons are in short supply this season."

"That's it?" Pip said. "We're— what, guests? Prisoners?"

"You're *pending*," Doss said, with a merchant's precision, "which in Mereth is both."

They were dismissed. A clerk led them down through the citadel's service stairs to the Tidequarter — a warren of salt-stained streets below the fish-market where the air hung thick with brine and the buildings leaned together like conspirators. The old customs house was a long, low hall of black timber, its great doors sealed with wax and official ribbon, its windows fogged with sea-damp. Inside: dust, rope coils, a scale for weighing cargo large enough to weigh a horse, and bunks left over from some earlier emergency. It smelled of tar and forgotten things.

Isolde set her pack down and looked around at the gloom and said, brightly, in the voice of a woman refusing to grieve in front of children, "Well. It has a roof."

"It has rats," Pip said, already climbing into the rafters to verify.

Brannic checked the doors and windows the way he checked everything, twice, and then stood in the middle of the hall with his arms folded, looking like a man guarding a warehouse full of nothing.

Freya walked to the tall seaward window. Below, the harbor lay gold in the late light, the masts swaying, the water moving in that slow patient breathing the sea does regardless of kings and councils. Somewhere out there, ships flew Vess's colors, carrying grain and iron and letters between a usurper and the men who might sell him four lives for a season's peace.

*Give the council reasons to be brave.*

He pressed his palm flat against the cold glass, and beneath his skin the Hollow Gate hummed its low note, faint across all that distance, patient as tide. They wanted reasons. They wanted arithmetic that came out in their favor. And Freya stood alone with the oldest reason in the world coiled under his skin like a sleeping serpent, and could not say a word of it — because the moment he said *Gate*, he stopped being a fugitive asking for shelter and became a weapon every crown in the world would want to hold.

He had traded one secret for safety once already, in a market stall in Vess, and it had cost him Tomas.

The door creaked. Isolde came and stood beside him, following his gaze out to the water.

"They'll do the right thing," she said. "Eventually. When they've finished being afraid."

"Fear keeps good books," Freya said. "It always balances."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "You didn't tell them. What you really are."

It wasn't a question either. He looked at her — at the woman who had deduced his seal-scars on the road, who knew exactly what slept beneath the city of his birth, who had kept his secret without being asked — and found he couldn't lie to her, not fully.

"Not yet," he said. "Maybe not ever. You heard them, Isolde. If I tell them what I am, I stop being a man at their door and start being a door myself."

Below, a bell rang the dusk hour, and lamplighters began their rounds along the terraces, kindling the city light by light, top to bottom, like a slow fire of stars descending toward the sea. It was beautiful. It was beautiful the way all besieged things are beautiful, and Freya watched it and understood, at last, that Mereth was not a fortress of liberty at all. It was a ledger. And somewhere in its columns, in ink not yet dry, someone was weighing the price of four lives against the price of grain.

Three days later, the envoys from Vess arrived.

Freya saw the procession from the customs house window — a dozen riders in the king's black and silver coming up the cliff road, banners snapping in the sea wind, and the crowds on the terraces pressing close to watch, cheering some of them, cursing others, and above it all the copper crown of the citadel catching the light like a held breath.

Among the riders, wrapped in a warden's grey cloak, sat a woman with iron-grey eyes that swept the crowd below with the patience of a fisherman reading water.

Serah Vane had found the door of the world.
