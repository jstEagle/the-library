## 9. Serah Vane's Ledger

The warden cells beneath the Citadel of Vess smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Serah Vane had long ago stopped noticing either. What she noticed now was the sound the baker made when he thought no one was listening — a small, rhythmic clicking of his tongue against his teeth, like a man counting something over and over to keep himself from screaming.

She stood outside his cell with a lamp in one hand and her ledger in the other, and for a moment she simply watched him through the barred window in the door.

Tomas Bell was a large man gone soft around the middle, the kind of body built by decades of rising before dawn to feed an oven. They had taken his apron but left him his boots, which she counted as a small mercy; the stone floor drank heat, and the cells were cold enough that breath showed. His hands lay open on his knees, palms up, as if he were still waiting on bread to cool. There were bruises on his knuckles where he had struck the bars, and darker ones on his forearms where the binding straps had bitten. Three days in the cells. He had not given them the name they wanted.

Or so Captain Dreeve believed. Serah had read the interrogation reports twice, and then read between their lines, because Dreeve wrote reports the way a butcher wrote receipts — everything measured by what could be sold upward to the Crown. *Subject remains obstinate. Subject invoked no names. Recommend continued isolation.* Dreeve heard nothing in silence. Serah had been trained by a different school, one that no longer existed, and she knew that men like Tomas Bell did not stay silent out of stubbornness. They stayed silent out of love, and love had a texture all its own. It leaked. It showed in what a man *didn't* say — the shape of the hole where a name should have been.

She unlocked the door herself and went in without ceremony, setting the lamp on the floor between them so its light fell upward and made her face unreadable shadow. An old trick. Her grandmother had taught it to her in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary, before the word *warden* had ever touched her life. *If they cannot see your eyes, girl, they will fill the dark with whatever frightens them most.*

Tomas lifted his head. His eyes were swollen, rheumy, but steady.

"Water," she said, and held out the flask before he asked. She watched him drink — watched the way his throat worked, greedy at first and then controlled, rationing himself even now. A careful man. A man accustomed to measuring things out: flour, coin, kindness.

"I'm told you've said nothing in three days," she said. "Not your name. Not your trade. Nothing."

His voice came out scraped raw. "They know my name. It's on the door."

"On the door," Serah repeated. She crouched, unhurried, until she was below his eyeline — another old habit, making yourself smaller than the prisoner, less threat than presence. "Yes. I saw it. Burned into the threshold of your shop. A warding mark, old style, the kind bakers' grandmothers used to chalk above their ovens against fire." She paused. "Except yours wasn't chalk, Master Bell. And it wasn't against fire. It was a warning. *He is known here. Flee.* Written in char, laid in haste, the night before the edicts went up. You burned a fugitive's road into your own doorstep."

Tomas said nothing. The clicking of his tongue had stopped. That, too, was information.

"You understand my difficulty," Serah went on, conversational, almost gentle. "The edict says every gifted soul must register or be hunted. The hunt has already begun in the low quarters — you've heard, surely, even down here. Warden-Captain Dreeve took four souls off Tanner's Row yesterday. One of them was nine years old." She let that sit in the cold air between them. "I am not Dreeve. I don't take pleasure in the work. But I am very good at it, and I would rather do it cleanly than watch it done badly. So. Tell me about the charm-seller."

Something moved behind the baker's eyes — not fear. Grief, arriving ahead of schedule, grief for a thing that hadn't happened yet.

"There's no charm-seller," Tomas rasped.

"No. Of course not. Just a man who sold luck charms in the market for three years, harmless trinkets, knotted thread and river stones, and never once drew the eye of any warden in a city crawling with them." She opened her ledger across her knee. "Just a man whose charms *worked*, though — because I have spoken to the young woman who bought one. The informant. She wore hers for six days and found coins in gutters and doors held open and her brother's fever breaking, and when the edicts came she did the arithmetic that frightened people always do, and she sold him for her own name on the register." Serah turned a page she didn't need to turn. "Her name is Maren Cotch. She cried while she gave her statement. Do you know why she cried?"

"She's a coward," Tomas said, and there was loathing in it, thick and human.

"No. She cried because the charm worked. Because she knew, holding it, that she was handing over something good, and she did it anyway." Serah closed the ledger. "Fear makes cowards of everyone, Master Bell. I include myself. Now — I'm going to ask you plainly, once, and then I'm going to tell you something true, and you can decide which of us lies better."

She leaned forward. The lamp guttered, throwing their two shadows huge against the damp wall, tangled together like wrestlers.

"The charm-seller. His name."

* * *

He lasted, in the end, longer than she expected and shorter than she feared. It was not the threats — she made none, and Dreeve's earlier threats had only taught the baker to brace. It was the true thing she had promised him. She told him that Freya Nakamura had escaped the market quarter clean, that the rooftop pursuit had lost him above the tanneries, that as far as her information ran he was alive and beyond the walls and moving east. She watched the relief move through Tomas's big body like weather, and she hated herself with a quiet professionalism even as she spent it.

"He's not what you think," Tomas whispered, when his voice finally came. His eyes were wet and fierce. "You think you're hunting a hedge-charmer. A mender of pots. He's—" The baker's jaw worked. "You don't know what he keeps."

Serah went very still.

"Keeps," she said.

And Tomas Bell, three days beaten and half-frozen, looked at her with the terrible clarity of a man past caring, and gave up the only secret he had left — not a name, not a street, but a word, a single word dropped into the dark between them like a stone down a well:

"The Gate."

Then he shut his mouth and would say nothing more, not that night, not under anything Dreeve could devise. But it was enough. More than enough. Serah walked back up the cell stairs with her lantern held level and her heart beating like a trapped bird, and behind her composed face her mind was running, running, down corridors twenty years dead, to a kitchen smelling of rosemary, to her grandmother's hands tracing a spiral in flour on the tabletop and saying, *remember this shape, child, remember what sleeps underneath the city, because someday there will be no one else who remembers.*

Serah Vane had thought those the ramblings of an old woman going soft. She had buried her grandmother's papers in a chest beneath her bed and never opened it, because opening it meant believing, and believing meant being the sort of person the new kingdom burned.

Now she went home and broke the chest's lock with her boot heel.

* * *

The papers smelled of dust and lavender and time. Serah spread them across her narrow bed by candlelight: her grandmother's hand, cramped and sure, filling page after page with diagrams — spirals within spirals, a map of Vess's foundations with lines descending beneath the oldest street levels to a chamber marked in ink gone brown as dried blood. Notes in the margins. Rites. Half of it was cipher, but half was plain, and the plain half undid her.

*A keeper holds the Hollow Gate. The Gate holds back what the first kings bound. When the last keeper fails, the seals fail, and the Old Powers wake to a world that has forgotten how to fight them.*

There was more. There was a list — a lineage, keepers named across centuries, each struck through upon their death in a hand that grew shakier with each generation. The final unstruck name was written in fresher ink, perhaps five years ago, added in a rush:

*Nakamura. Freya. The last.*

Her grandmother had known. Had known the charm-seller in the market for what he was — or guessed, and reached toward him, and died before she could finish reaching. Serah sat among the scattered papers until her candle drowned in its own wax, and she understood at last the full shape of what she had stumbled into, and it was so much larger and colder than a hunt for hedge-witches that she laughed once, softly, in the empty room, and the laugh sounded nothing like laughter.

Because she also understood — reading Aldous's edicts again in memory, reading the king's hungry face at court, reading the way his sorcerer-advisors spoke of *old power* with longing instead of dread — that the usurper did not merely want the gifted registered. Registration was the sieve. Somewhere in the palace's ambitions, past the proclamations and the hounds and Dreeve's brutish sweepings of the low quarters, there was a purpose that needed a Gatekeeper found, and needed him alive.

She could not prove it yet. But she knew the smell of a hidden agenda; she'd been raised on secrets in a rosemary-scented kitchen. And she knew this: whatever Aldous wanted with the Gate, it was not to keep it shut.

* * *

The throne room of King Aldous had been the Council Hall of old King Edmun, and the change was all in the details. The tapestries of the old line had come down; in their places hung black banners worked with the boar's-head sigil of House Marrow, Aldous's house, stitched in silver thread that caught the light like frost. The throne itself remained, oak and iron, too heavy and too ugly to replace, but Aldous had draped it in furs, as if the seat of a seven-hundred-year kingdom were a hunting blind.

Aldous received petitioners in the mornings, and Serah requested audience on a grey morning when rain lashed the high windows and the braziers fought a losing war against the drafts.

She knelt, as required. Rose, as required. Kept her eyes correctly lowered, as required, and catalogued him in glances: broader than rumor made him, grey at the temples though barely forty, with a soldier's stillness and a merchant's patience and eyes the color of a frozen pond — pleasant at a distance, lethal at depth. At his shoulder stood Master Corvane, the royal sorcerer, a thin man wrapped in grey whose presence always made Serah's teeth ache, the way a coming storm made old scars ache.

"Warden-Serah Vane." Aldous's voice was warm gravel. "The woman who took the Hollow Quarter without losing a man. Your reputation precedes you, as does your request. Leave to pursue the fugitive Nakamura personally." He smiled. "The charm-seller. I confess, he seems a small fish for someone of your talents."

"Small fish, sire, are how the net learns its trade," Serah said. "But he is not small. He evaded a full pursuit through his own streets, streets he clearly knows better than we do. He had a confederate inside the city — the baker — placed years in advance. This is infrastructure, not accident. And my record speaks to what I can do with such threads." She bowed her head fractionally. "Give me leave and a warrant, and I will bring you the man who slipped your wardens."

Corvane stirred. "Other wardens—"

"Other wardens have failed," Aldous said mildly, and Corvane subsided. The king descended one step from the dais, close enough that Serah caught his scent — oiled leather and, faintly, something mineral, like the air inside a cave. His frozen-pond eyes studied her. "You want him alive, I trust. Dead men make poor examples."

"Alive, sire." Carefully. "For questioning."

"Questioning." Aldous turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. For a long moment he said nothing, and Serah felt the weight of him the way one feels a change in pressure before snow. Then: "Granted. A warrant, six riders of your choosing, and access to the hounds. Bring me the charm-seller." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "There are those at court who believe Nakamura may be more than he appears. If you find evidence of that — evidence of *anything* old attached to him — you will report it first to Master Corvane. Before me, even. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly, sire."

It was a lie. She delivered it flawlessly, and Aldous dismissed her with the satisfied nod of a king who believed he had just leashed his best hound, and Serah Vane walked backward three steps, bowed, and left the throne room with her grandmother's secret burning in her chest like swallowed coal.

Let Corvane be the king's instrument. Let Aldous chase his opening. She would ride east with a warrant and a hidden ledger and a lineage of dead women whispering in her blood, and she would find Freya Nakamura first — not to deliver him to a throne, and not, whatever the warrant said, to drag him back to Vess.

To warn him, if it came to that. To stand beside him, if it came to that. She did not yet know which. She only knew that the Gate must stay shut, that she was the last person in Vess who remembered why, and that the hunt had just become a race.

By nightfall her six riders were chosen, her warrant sealed, and the chest of her grandmother's papers packed into a saddlebag wrapped in oilcloth. At the east gate, in the last grey light, she looked back once at the city stacked on its hill — the spires, the smoke, the streets above the sleeping hollow — and then turned her horse onto the Salt Road, into the rain, after a family of strangers who did not yet know what hunted them, or what rode behind it.
