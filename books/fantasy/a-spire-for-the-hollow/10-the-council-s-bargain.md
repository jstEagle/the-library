## 10. The Council's Bargain

The council chamber of Mereth smelled of salt and old cedar, and Isolde thought it looked less like a seat of power than like a ship's belly turned inside out — ribbed timbers overhead, walls paneled in driftwood gray with age, windows cut high and narrow so that the light came down in pale blades rather than falling warm. Nine councillors sat behind a crescent table of black stone worn smooth by a century of elbows. Nine faces, and not one of them kind.

She stood at Freya's left shoulder, close enough to feel the tremor he was trying to hide. He was good at stillness — three years of selling charms in a city that had outlawed his gift had taught him that — but she had spent a week watching the way magic moved under people's skin, back when such watching had been safe, and she could see the effort of it now. The tightness at his jaw. The way his fingers kept finding the seam of his sleeve and pressing there, as though he could pin himself to himself.

Pip stood on his other side, uncharacteristically quiet, hands tucked into his armpits like a boy caught stealing apples. Brannic filled the space behind them all, arms crossed, warden's bearing stripped of its warden's authority — an empty scabbard of a man, standing guard over nothing he had any right to protect.

"Freya Nakamura." The speaker of the council was a woman named Odile Marchand, silver-haired, sharp-faced, with rings enough on her fingers to buy a street in Vess. "You petition this council for sanctuary. For yourself and your companions. You will understand that we do not grant such petitions lightly."

"We ask for nothing but leave to stay," Freya said. His voice was steady. Isolde marveled at it. "We work. We keep our heads down. We cause no trouble."

"No trouble." A councillor to Marchand's left, a fat man with merchant's jowls, made the words sound like something foul on his tongue. "You bring trouble the way a drowned man brings water, Nakamura. There is a usurper king marching west with four thousand soldiers and a hunt for the gifted, and you walk into my city carrying exactly what he is hunting."

Isolde watched Freya take that blow without flinching. She wanted to speak — her gift was small, mending, mending, always mending, but she had spent her whole life patching what was torn, and she could feel the tear in this room as clearly as she felt a split seam. She said nothing. This was his fight, and Pip's hand had drifted near hers in warning: *let him*.

"The free cities were founded by the gifted," Brannic said from behind them. His voice came out low and rough, gravel dragged over old regret. "I stood warden's watch on these walls when I was young, Councillor Verre. I remember the oaths. *No hand shall be raised against a keeper of gifts within the free stones.*"

"The free stones," Verre said, "have never been threatened by an army before."

"And they are not now," Freya said quietly. "Aldous marches west for us. Four fugitives. He does not break a city for four."

"He breaks cities because he can," said a third councillor, older than the rest, her voice dry as paper. "That is what kings do. Especially new ones, who must teach their kingdoms to fear them."

Marchand raised a hand, and the chamber stilled. She studied Freya the way Isolde had seen fishwives study a catch — turning him over, checking for rot beneath the shine.

"There is another matter," she said. "Word came to us from Vess. Slowly, and through channels I will not name, but it came. It says that the charm-seller of the Lantern Market was no hedge-witch selling luck in bottles. It says that he was the last Gatekeeper of Vess, and that beneath the city he kept sealed a door that has been shut since the first kings bound the Old Powers away." She paused. "It says that King Aldous knows what you are, Nakamura. And it says he wants the Gate opened."

The silence that followed had weight. Isolde felt it settle on her chest like wet wool. Beside her, Freya did not move, but she saw the color leave his face in stages — first his lips, then his cheeks, then everything below the eyes, so that what remained was a man carved out of candle wax.

"That is not true," he said.

It was beautifully done. Isolde almost believed him herself. But Marchand smiled, thin as a knife's edge.

"I did not ask if it were true," the Speaker said. "I asked nothing at all. I told you what I know, and I watched you lie about it, which tells me everything the report claimed and more." She folded her ringed hands upon the black stone. "So let us stop pretending, Keeper. Let us talk about what Mereth is willing to offer."

* * *

They gave the four of them a small room off the antechamber while the council conferred — a courtesy, a guard told them, that would look very different by evening. It was a square of grey light and salt-stained plaster with a bench along one wall, and they sat in it the way survivors sit in lifeboats: close together, not touching, each alone with the water.

Isolde mended while she waited. She always mended while she waited; her hands needed it the way other hands needed prayer. She had taken Pip's coat earlier that morning — a ragged thing, more patches than cloth, every patch sewn by a different hand across a different city — and she worked now at the last of its holes, drawing the threads together with the faintest brush of her gift, warmth moving through her fingertips like water through a cracked cup. Small magic. Useless magic, the wardens had called it, once upon a time, before usefulness became a death sentence.

Pip watched her work. He was fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and wariness, hair cropped short against lice and a face that had learned to be forgettable so early that forgetting it had become second nature even to those who loved him. Isolde thought — not for the first time — that he was the most dangerous kind of survivor, the kind who had survived alone.

"She knows," Pip said softly. "The Speaker. About the Gate. How does she know?"

"Someone talked," Brannic said. He had not sat down. He never sat down anymore; sitting, Isolde suspected, reminded him of the warden barracks, of a life he had burned behind him. "Vess is full of clerks and clerks have tongues. Someone in Aldous's chancery sold a secret, or someone in ours did. Doesn't matter which."

"It matters," Freya said. He was standing at the narrow window, looking down into the council yard, where gulls argued over fish guts on the cobbles. His reflection in the glass was ghost-pale and doubled, one Freya above the harbor and one below, and Isolde thought he looked like a man standing between two worlds and belonging to neither. "It matters because if the council knows, then others know. And if Aldous knows what I am—" He stopped. His hand went again to his sleeve seam, pressing, pressing. "Then everything I've done for three years has been for nothing. Every charm I didn't sell. Every seal I renewed in the dark. All of it, undone by a clerk with a loose tongue."

"You kept a door shut against the end of the world for three years," Isolde said. "Don't you dare call that nothing."

He looked at her then, and something in his face cracked — just a hairline fracture, quickly hidden, but she saw it. She had gotten good at seeing it. Freya carried guilt the way other men carried knives, always in reach, always ready to be turned inward.

"They'll offer me something terrible," he said. "You all heard her. *What Mereth is willing to offer.* Councils don't offer sanctuary. They trade it."

"Then we refuse whatever it is," Pip said flatly, "and we go."

"Go where?" Brannic asked, not unkindly. "Back to the Salt Road? Serah Vane is on it. Or ahead of it. We ran east because east was the only direction the hounds weren't."

Pip had no answer to that. Isolde tied off the final thread on his coat, bit the cotton clean, and handed it back to him without looking up, because looking up meant showing him her face, and her face was afraid, and fear was a contagion she refused to pass to a child.

When the door opened and a clerk in sea-grey robes summoned them back, Isolde folded her needle into its case and felt the small click of it like a verdict.

* * *

The council chamber had changed while they were gone. The nine chairs were occupied as before, but the quality of the silence had shifted; it had teeth now. On the black stone before Marchand lay a single object, and Isolde knew what it was before her mind finished naming it, because she had grown up on her grandmother's stories and her grandmother had never tired of the old wars.

A shard of black glass, long as a forearm, veined with faint red light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart.

"What is that?" Brannic demanded.

"This," Marchand said, "came out of the Hollow of Vess two hundred years ago, in the time of the last attempted opening. My predecessors kept it as evidence and as warning. It is a splinter of the Old Powers' substance, drawn through the Gate's seam during the three days the seals failed. Three days, Keeper. In three days, this much came through." She lifted the shard and held it up to the window-light, and the red veins pulsed, and Isolde felt her stomach turn over, because the pulse was wrong — too slow, too patient, the rhythm of something that had waited centuries and could wait centuries more. "Imagine what forty years of freedom might have done."

"Put it down," Freya said. His voice had changed. All the softness was gone out of it, all the market-day pleasantness, and what remained was cold and old and absolutely certain. "Speaker. Put it down. Do not hold it longer than you must. It listens."

Marchand set it down. Quickly. Her composure held, mostly, but Isolde saw her wipe her palm on her robe beneath the table's edge, and saw the flicker of revulsion she tried to swallow. Good, Isolde thought. Be afraid. Fear of that thing is the only honest feeling in this room.

"Now," Marchand said, recovering, "to the matter of terms."

And she laid them out plainly, as merchants do, as though she were selling cloth. Sanctuary for all four of them — permanent residence, protection, legal papers, places in the city's rolls. And in exchange, Freya Nakamura would open the Hollow Gate. Not wide. Not fully. A controlled breach, supervised by the council's own battle-mages — hedge-wizards and storm-callers who had fled the kingdom over the years and drilled in secret in Mereth's cellars — through which the free cities could draw what power they needed.

"Aldous wants the Gate opened with keeper's blood so the Old Powers crown him," Marchand said. "Let him come. Let him stand before a wall of Mereth steel with the Powers already loosed on *our* terms, answering *our* call. He believes the ancient things will make him a war-king. Very well. We will give him a war, and we will win it, and the gifted of this kingdom will never again need hide in cellars and sell luck in bottles." Her voice rose, and for a moment — only a moment — Isolde glimpsed the believer underneath the merchant, the woman who remembered the oaths Brannic had spoken of, who had watched her own kin hunted and burned and had dreamed, all these years, of a day the hiding ended. "This is not betrayal, Keeper. This is the only chance our people will ever get. You are not being asked to damn the world. You are being asked to arm it."

The room waited. Isolde realized she had stopped breathing and made herself breathe again.

"No," Freya said.

Not loudly. Not angrily. Just *no*, the way one says no to a child reaching toward a fire, and Isolde understood in that instant how completely he meant it — not as negotiation, not as a first offer awaiting a counter, but as bedrock.

Marchand's face hardened. "Keeper—"

"You think you can control it." Freya stepped forward, out of the shelter of his companions, alone, the way he had descended alone into the Hollow three hundred times in the dark. "You have a shard the size of your arm that leaked through in three days, and you think you can open the door a measured crack and pour out exactly what serves you. Speaker, I have knelt on that Gate's seals with my blood in the grooves. I have felt what presses against them. It is not power waiting to be borrowed. It is hunger that has had four hundred years to refine itself. Your battle-mages would not command it. They would be the first thing it ate, and it would wear their shapes like coats while it walked up out of your cellar."

"Then help us ward against it—"

"There is no warding against it. That is why the first kings *bound* it instead of fighting it. They fought it first. Do you understand? They fought it, and lost, and binding it cost them everything they had, and they built kingdoms on top of the wound so that no one would ever dig there again." His voice cracked, finally, the steadiness failing at last, and Isolde heard what lived underneath it: three years of solitary terror, spoken aloud at last in a room full of strangers. "I will not open that Gate. Not for sanctuary. Not for the gifted. Not for anything you can put on that table."

Verre, the fat merchant, leaned forward. "Then hear what refusal costs, Nakamura." His voice was almost gentle, which made it worse. "At sundown tomorrow, if you have not agreed, you and your companions will be escorted to the east gate and released beyond our walls. We will send word to no one. We will simply... decline to protect you. What happens to four unarmed fugitives on the road, with the king's hounds behind them, is no business of Mereth's."

"You'd feed us to Serah Vane," Pip said, and his voice was very small and very hard.

"We'd decline to interfere," Verre corrected. "There is a difference. It is the difference that has kept this city free for two hundred years."

Brannic moved then — one step forward, and even without a sword he filled the chamber like a storm filling a valley. "I have stood watch on walls in the rain for men who despised me," he said, "because the oath mattered more than my comfort. You sit behind timber your founders bled for, and you sell the oath for a quieter winter. I am ashamed, Councillor. Ashamed that I ever wore the same colors as the men hunting these people, and ashamed tonight to learn that the famous freedom of the free cities smells the same as Vess. It smells like a locked door and everyone pretending not to hear the knocking."

"Master Brannic." Marchand's voice cut like a cleaver. "You are in no position—"

"No," Brannic agreed. "None of us are. That's rather the point."

* * *

They were escorted out — politely, firmly, between guards who would not meet their eyes — to the same small grey room, and the door closed, and this time Isolde was fairly sure it locked.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Below the window, the gulls still wheeled and screamed over the harbor. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, bronze and indifferent.

"Well," Pip said at last, dropping onto the bench. "That went well."

"Pip." Isolde's voice came out sharper than she intended.

"No, it's fine, it's fine. I'm just saying. We came here for sanctuary and got an eviction notice with a sunset deadline. Very traditional. Very free-city." He pulled his newly mended coat tighter around himself, and Isolde saw his fingers find the patch she'd sewn and worry at its edge, the way children worry at talismans. "What do we do?"

Freya stood at the window with his back to them. When he answered, his voice was hollowed out, scraped clean.

"I should have lied better in there. I should have bought us time. Promised to consider it, negotiated, done *something* besides plant my feet and say no." He laughed once, without any humor in it. "Three years of keeping my face smooth, and the moment it matters, I lose my temper in front of nine councillors."

"You didn't lose your temper," Isolde said. "You told the truth. There's a difference, and it's the difference that keeps doors shut that need to be shut." She rose and crossed to him, and after a moment's hesitation put her hand on his arm — the arm with the seal-scars beneath the sleeve, the ones she had noticed on the Salt Road and understood before he ever confessed. "Listen to me, Freya Nakamura. I mend things. Broken cups, torn coats, cracked bones, once a broken roof beam with a family under it. It's small magic. I've spent my whole life being told it doesn't count. But I know what I know, and here is what I know: some broken things should stay broken. Some tears are the only way out of a trap. Whatever is behind that Gate — whatever your blood has held back all these years — it is not a weapon waiting for a worthy hand. It is a wound pretending to be a door. And no one — not Aldous, not this council, not the desperate and the righteous together — gets to open it and call it salvation."

He looked down at her. His eyes were wet, though he would never have allowed it in the chamber.

"If I refuse," he said quietly, "they put you all out at sundown tomorrow. Into Vane's path. Into the hunt. Your safety is the price of my silence, Isolde. Don't you see? They've found the one coin I can't refuse to pay."

"Then we don't pay it," Brannic rumbled from the bench, "and we don't wait for sundown either. Walls have gates, and gates have guards, and I've been a guard. I know which hours they yawn."

"You're suggesting we break *out*?" Pip's face lit with something between horror and delight.

"I'm suggesting," Brannic said, "that a council which sells its oaths has no claim on our obedience. We came asking for shelter. We won't beg twice."

Isolde felt it move through the little room then — not her gift, nothing so gentle, but something older and rougher: the shape of a decision forming among four people who had been strangers six weeks ago. She looked around at them. The disgraced warden rolling his shoulders like a man taking up an old weight. The thief already eyeing the door's lock with professional affection. The Gatekeeper by the window, pale and exhausted and stubborn as the stone he guarded.

*These are mine now,* she thought, and the thought frightened her more than the council's threat, because she had lost a family once already — to the hunt, to the fire, to the ordinary cruelty of frightened neighbors — and she knew precisely what it cost to love people the world had decided to burn.

"Sundown tomorrow," she said aloud. "Which means we have until dark today to find the gaps in their watch rotations. Pip—"

"Already thinking about it," Pip said, and grinned, and the grin was fierce and scared and young. "Warden Brannic, sir, I'm going to need you to distract a guard. Try looking disappointed in him. You're very good at it."

But Freya had not moved from the window. Isolde followed his gaze down into the council yard, past the gulls and the gutted fish, to the harbor road beyond the wall — where, far off at the edge of sight, dust hung above the eastern hills. Too much dust for carts. Too steady for weather.

She felt him go rigid under her hand.

"That's not possible," he whispered. "She can't have moved that fast. The ford—the river—we bought ourselves weeks—"

The bell in the harbor tower began to ring. Not the hour-bell. The other one. The one Isolde had heard described in every port town from here to the Salt Road and hoped never to hear for herself.

Alarm. Enemy sighted.

Below in the streets of Mereth, doors began to slam, and the gulls rose screaming off the cobbles like a sheet torn out of the sky.
