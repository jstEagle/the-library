## 11. Night of Broken Seals

The bells of Mereth did not ring for fire that night.

Freya learned this later, in the way one learns everything after a catastrophe — pieced together from fragments, from shouting, from the faces of strangers lit orange by a sky that should have been dark. At the time, all he knew was that he woke with his heart already hammering, before any sound reached him, because something older than sound had touched him.

It came through the scars.

He had carried them for eleven years — thin white lines across both palms, crossing at the wrists like laces drawn too tight, the mark every Gatekeeper took when they bound themselves to their gate. Most nights he felt nothing from them. They were old scars, quiet scars, the kind a man could almost forget he'd earned. But tonight they woke him as if someone had pressed a hot iron to the inside of his wrists, and he sat up in the narrow room above the chandler's shop where the four of them had been given lodging, and he knew — the way a sailor knows a storm before the clouds gather — that something far away had broken.

"Freya?" Isolde's voice, soft, from the next room. She slept badly these days; they all did. "What is it?"

He didn't answer. He was listening — not with his ears but with whatever lived beneath his skin, the gift he had spent three years smothering under charm-work and small lies. The Hollow Gate lay five hundred miles west, beneath the city of Vess, behind seven seals of woven light that he himself had renewed each season since old Master Corvin died. And across all that distance, faint as a heartbeat heard through a stone wall, he could feel them.

One seal had frayed.

Not broken. Frayed — like rope strands parting one by one, like a stitch pulled loose from the hem of the world. And beneath it, pressing up against the gap with the patience of centuries, something stirred that should not have been stirring yet.

*Someone is unbinding it.*

The thought arrived whole and terrible, and with it the first bell began to ring — not the measured tolling of an alarm Freya might have imagined, but a wild, arrhythmic clamor, every tower in Mereth at once, as though the city itself had begun to scream.

* * *

They met on the stairs, all four of them half-dressed, and for a moment nobody spoke. Pip stood barefoot with a knife in each hand, which was very Pip — armed before awake. Brannic had his warden's blade already belted on, the disgraced crest still scratched from its face, and Isolde held little more than a shawl around her shoulders, her gray braid unbound, her eyes already doing what her eyes always did: looking for what was broken, so she could mend it.

"The wall," Brannic said. He had gone to the window and come back pale. "The outer wall. There are fires on the western quarter."

"Aldous can't have marched that fast," Isolde said. "The council said we had weeks. They said—"

"The council said a great many things." Brannic's voice was flat, but Freya heard the anger banked underneath it, the way you hear coals under ash. Three days ago the Council of Mereth had voted — narrowly, bitterly — to grant the fugitives sanctuary until such time as their presence became inconvenient. Brannic had called it *a rope long enough to hang us slowly*. "Vanguard riders, maybe. Or ships out of Salt Harbor. It doesn't matter how. It matters that they're here."

Down in the street, people were pouring from their houses in nightclothes, some running toward the wall with buckets and billhooks, some simply standing in the middle of the cobbles with their faces turned upward, watching the glow on the underside of the smoke. A child went past crying for its mother. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse.

"We should go to the harbor district," Isolde said. "Get off the streets. If there's fighting—"

"I need to see the wall," Freya said.

They looked at him. He realized his hands were shaking, and he closed them into fists so the scars hid against his palms, though there was no hiding anything from these three anymore. Not since the waystation. Not since the fire and the stories and the night Pip had declared, with the gravity only a fourteen-year-old thief could muster, that they were *stuck with each other now, so everyone stop pretending otherwise.*

"Why?" Brannic asked. Not suspicious — Brannic's suspicion had burned down to something quieter and harder weeks ago — but careful. "What aren't you telling us?"

Freya hesitated. Then he opened his hands and let them see the tremor.

"The Gate," he said. "Something's happening to the Gate."

* * *

They could not reach the wall. That was the thing none of the histories mentioned, Freya thought wildly as they were swept along — that in a city under attack, the direction everyone flees from is the direction you cannot go. The streets funneled them eastward with the flood of refugees, past shuttered shops and a temple whose doors had been thrown open to spill lamplight and prayer onto the steps, past a wine merchant dragging barrels into his cellar as if glass and vintage mattered tonight.

But from the high street, where the ground rose toward the old watchtower, they could see it.

Mereth's outer wall was a gray ribbon in the darkness, crowned with torchlight — and beyond it, on the plain, a scatter of fires that had not been there at dusk. Too few for an army. Too many for a raid. Siege engines were silhouetted against the burning brush the defenders had fired on the approaches, great jointed shapes like sleeping insects, and even as Freya watched, one of them lurched forward and spat a stone that struck the wall with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard.

"They're testing it," Brannic murmured. "Probing for weakness. This isn't the assault — this is Aldous telling the council he can knock when he pleases." His knuckles were white on the parapet of the watchtower stair where they'd wedged themselves. "Three days. We've been here three days, and he's already at the door. Someone told him exactly where we went."

Pip made a small sound. When Freya glanced over, the boy was hunched inside his oversized coat, arms wrapped around himself, staring at the fires with an expression Freya had never seen on him before. Pip's face was built for mischief; it had a whole vocabulary of grins and smirks and theatrical wounded dignity. None of that was present now. What was present was a boy from the gutters of Vess who knew precisely what happened to cities that lost sieges, because he had picked pockets in the aftermath of one.

"Hey." Freya put a hand on his shoulder. "Look at me, not the wall."

"They'll breach," Pip said. His voice was very steady, which was worse than shaking. "That's a ram under the tarp on the left one. I know rams. They'll breach the west postern within two days, maybe three, and then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Then it's Vess all over again. The lists. The wardens going door to door."

"No," Isolde said, with a fierceness that surprised them all. Her hand found Pip's collar and gripped it, gentle but immovable, the way she gripped torn cloth before she mended it. "No one is making lists tonight. Listen to me, Pip Wren — I have mended kettles and broken arms and one time a roof, an entire roof, and I am telling you that nothing that happens on that wall is going to happen to you. Do you hear me?"

Pip looked at her. Something in his face cracked, just slightly, and he nodded.

And it was then — in the space of that nod, in the breath between the distant boom of the ram and the next — that the second seal frayed.

Freya doubled over.

The pain was not like the first warning. The first had been heat; this was cold, a cold that started in the scars and climbed his forearms and seized his chest, and for one vertiginous moment he was not standing on a watchtower stair in Mereth at all. He was *beneath* — beneath stone and earth and the roots of the Spire, in the dark of the Hollow where no light had ever fallen, and he could feel the Gate the way a man feels his own heartbeat when he presses fingers to his throat. Seven seals. Seven interlocking rings of binding, laid down by the first kings with blood and oath and the deaths of nine keepers, each one a knot in a cord that held shut a door older than kingdoms.

Two knots, loosening. Not failing. *Being unpicked.* There was a difference, and the difference had a shape, and the shape was patient hands working in the dark.

"—Freya. *Freya.*"

Isolde's face swam back into focus above him. She had both hands on his shoulders, and he realized distantly that warmth was flowing into him from her palms — her small gift, the child's trick of mending, reaching instinctively for what was torn in him. It couldn't touch what was actually wrong. Nothing could, from five hundred miles away. But it anchored him, and he dragged air into his lungs and let the Hollow recede.

"He's white as milk," Pip was saying, high and frightened. "Isolde, he's—"

"I'm here." Freya straightened, leaning on the rail. His teeth wanted to chatter; he refused them. "I'm here. I'm all right."

"You're lying," Brannic said, "but go on."

Freya laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. Below them the ram struck again, and the crowd in the streets cried out as one, a single animal voice of fear. He waited for it to pass.

"The Hollow Gate holds seven seals," he said quietly. "I've told you what's behind it. The Old Powers. The things the first kings bound when the world was young enough to be worth saving. As long as the seals hold, those things sleep, and the war that nearly ended everything stays finished." He turned his hands over, let them see the scars crossed by the torchlight from below. "I'm bound to those seals. Every keeper is, while he lives. I feel them the way you'd feel your own heartbeat. And tonight—" His voice caught. He hated that it caught. "Tonight, two of them are unraveling. Not weakening. Being *unpicked*, thread by thread, by someone who knows exactly what they're doing."

Silence, except for the bells and the boom of the ram and the far-off crying.

"That's not possible," Brannic said at last. "You sealed it yourself. You told us — last season, before we fled Vess. You said it would hold for years."

"It would have." Freya stared out at the fires on the plain, and felt the truth settle over him like snowfall, cold and total. "Unless someone else is working at it. Someone with keeper's knowledge. Or—" and here was the thought he had been circling since he woke, the thought he had hoped saying aloud would make smaller, "—or someone who wants it open. Aldous didn't march on Mereth because we came here. We're a pretext. A hunt for the gifted is useful to a usurper — it gives him enemies, it gives him confiscations, it gives the fearful something to cheer. But that's not why the edicts went up. That's not why he needs every gifted person in the kingdom registered or dead."

Isolde went very still. "He's looking for a keeper."

"He's looking for *me*." Freya heard his own voice as if from a distance, calm and terrible. "Keeper's blood opens the Gate. It's the only thing that does. And I ran — I led him halfway across the kingdom, and now I'm standing in the one free city he intends to take, wrapped in a sanctuary the council will trade away the moment it becomes expensive." He looked at each of them in turn: Brannic with his scratched-out crest, Isolde with her mending hands, Pip with his knives and his gutter-quick eyes. His family, made of necessity, worth dying for. "I brought the war to your door. All of yours."

"Don't," Pip said sharply.

Freya blinked. "Don't what?"

"Do that." The boy's chin was up, defiant, terrified, magnificent. "The thing where you decide it's all your fault and start arranging yourself to be sacrificed about it. I've watched you do it since the ford. Well, I stole bread from a warden captain once and got my friend flogged for it, so by your arithmetic we're all monsters, and none of us are leaving, so *stop*."

For a moment, absurdly, Freya wanted to laugh. Then the third seal frayed, and the laugh died unborn.

This time he did not fall. He had braced against the rail, and he rode it out — the cold, the pull, the sense of vast attention turning over in its sleep beneath the world, like a leviathan feeling the harpoon lines part one by one. Three of seven. Whoever worked at the Gate was fast now, faster than any rite he knew, and the horrible mathematics of it assembled itself in his mind without permission: at this pace, the remaining seals would fail within days. Perhaps sooner. Perhaps before the ram even breached the wall.

Perhaps before he could do anything at all.

When he surfaced, all three of them were holding onto him, and the city was screaming below, and Serah Vane's face rose unbidden in his memory — cold-eyed, ledger-precious, asking him in a dream or a memory or a prophecy of guilt: *Did you really think you could hide a thing like you forever?*

"The choice," Freya said, and his voice came out scraped raw. "I was supposed to have a choice. Seal it or open it, save the family or save the world — I was supposed to get to *choose*. But someone is opening it whether I choose or not, and every hour I stand here is an hour closer to the Old Powers walking free under Aldous's banner." He pushed himself upright. His legs held. That was something. "We have to go back. Back to Vess. Back to the Hollow."

Brannic studied him for a long moment, torchlight and shadow moving on his scarred face. Then he nodded once, slow, the nod of a man accepting a sentence he'd known was coming.

"Pip," he said. "How quickly can you steal us horses?"

Pip grinned — thin, feral, alive. "Depends how quickly you can all look innocent."

Below them, the ram struck again, and somewhere in the dark between Mereth and the mountains, five hundred miles away, a fourth knot in the oldest cord in the world slipped loose — and Freya felt it, and began to run.
