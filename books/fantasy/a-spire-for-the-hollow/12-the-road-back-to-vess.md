## 12. The Road Back to Vess

Brannic had stood before war councils in three kingdoms. He had watched grey-bearded marshals argue over grain carts and river fords, had seen men weigh the lives of a thousand soldiers against the fall of a single bridge. He had thought, in those years when he still wore the warden's grey-and-silver and believed the badge meant something, that he understood what it cost to send people toward danger.

He had never understood anything at all.

Because the council that convened in the ruined waystation's common room on the fourth night after Mereth turned them away was four people and a guttering fire, and the decision they were weighing was not how many soldiers to spend but whether to spend themselves — all of them, everything they had, on a road that led straight back into the hunt's black heart.

"The vote is simple," Freya said. He stood with his back to the fire, and the light made a thin gold rim of him, as if he were already becoming something less than flesh. "The Gate is weakening. I can feel it — the seals fraying like old rope, one strand at a time. Someone is unbinding them, and every hour we sit here arguing, they get closer to whatever it is they want. If we go back to Vess, we can reseal it. If we don't—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. They had all heard what lay behind the Hollow Gate, or enough of it. Isolde had gone white when he first told them, her hands pressed flat against her belly as though the Old Powers were something she could feel moving in her own blood. Pip had laughed — Pip laughed at everything — but the laugh had died halfway out of his throat and never come back.

"And if we go," Isolde said quietly, "we walk into Vess. Into the city where every door bears the usurper's edict. Where the wardens know your face now, Freya. Where *they* know all our faces." She looked around the circle. "Brannic. Tell them what the hunt is like now. You were one of them."

The words landed like a slap, and she knew it, and she did not take them back. That was Isolde. She mended broken things — cups, cloaks, bones, hearts — but she never pretended a thing wasn't broken in the first place.

Brannic turned his hands over in the firelight. The burn scar across his left palm, from the night he'd torn his badge off and thrown it into the Wardenhouse brazier, had faded to a pale seam. He still caught himself rubbing it when he thought too hard.

"The hunt is thorough," he said. "Vane is thorough. She doesn't sweep a city; she *reads* it. She'll know we're coming before we're through the south gate. She'll have the roads watched, the inns watched, the churches watched. She'll have informants on every corner, and half of them will be people who don't even think of themselves as informants — bakers, barkeeps, children paid in coppers to remember faces." He paused. "And she isn't doing it for Aldous. Not entirely. There's something in it for her, something she's not sharing with her own wardens. I've seen her lie to superiors before. She's doing it again. That makes her more dangerous, not less."

"Then we don't walk in the front," Pip said.

They all turned to him. He was sitting cross-legged on the table itself, because Pip had never in his life sat in a chair when a table, a windowsill, or a rafter would do. He was fifteen and all elbows and quick eyes, and there was ash from the fire smeared on his cheek where he'd scratched himself, and Brannic had realized some days ago that he would die for this boy without a second thought, which was a dangerous thing for a man who had already lost everyone he'd ever been sworn to protect.

"Nobody walks in the front," Pip went on. "Vess has a back door. Well. Not a door. A drain. The old sewer gate under Tanner's Row — the wardens sealed it eight years back because smugglers were using it, but sealed doors are just locks that haven't been talked to yet." He grinned, and the grin was all teeth and no fear, which was Pip's way of being terrified. "Locks like me. I can get us under the city before Vane's hounds finish their breakfast."

"You've never been to Vess," Brannic said.

"I've never been to *Mereth* either, and I stole the council's seal off the chamberlain's belt while you were all groveling." He fished in his coat and produced, with a flourish, a rolled tube of parchment. "Which is why I've been busy."

He unrolled it on the table, pinning the corners with a knife, a spoon, and Isolde's thimble, which she would notice missing later and for which he would apologize with a sincerity that fooled no one. It was a map of Vess — a real one, surveyor's ink, the kind the Wardenhouse kept in its planning rooms. Brannic leaned over it and felt his stomach drop.

"Where did you get this?"

"Stole it," Pip said, as if that were an answer.

"*Where.*"

"Off a courier on the Salt Road, two nights ago, while you were all asleep and Isolde was pretending to sleep and Freya was actually asleep for the first time in three days. The courier was drunk on cheap wine and carrying a whole satchel — dispatches, this, a letter with the king's seal on it that I couldn't read but Isolde could." He glanced at her. "Go on. Tell them."

Isolde's face had gone the color of tallow. She took the map's edge in her fingers, careful, the way she handled everything — as though the world were made of things that could break.

"It's orders," she said. "From Aldous's chancellery to the warden-garrisons along the coast. Reinforcements to Vess. And a requisition—" her finger traced a line of cramped script "—for *keeper's instruments*. Chains of cold iron. A binding circle, portable. And bloodletting tools, surgical grade." She looked up at Freya. "They're not hunting you to kill you, Gatekeeper. They're hunting you to *use* you."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a night bird called three times and fell silent, as if it too were listening.

Freya didn't flinch. Brannic watched him — watched the way the charm-seller's soft hands curled slowly into fists, watched the exhaustion and the fear move behind his eyes and be *set aside*, deliberately, like a man setting down a burden he intended to pick up again later. Brannic had seen soldiers learn to do that. It took most of them years. Freya had learned it in a season, and it was the saddest kind of education.

"Then we know what they know," Freya said. "And they don't know we know it. That's worth more than any map." He looked around the circle, at each of them in turn. "So. The vote. We go back to Vess, under the city, find the Gate, and reseal it before Aldous's people can unbind it. We go knowing what it is — the heart of the hunt, the one city in the world where every wall has our names on it. We go knowing some of us might not come out. Say your piece now, or hold your peace forever after."

"I'll say mine," Brannic said.

He stood. His knees cracked — he was forty-one and the road had been hard on them — and he looked at the three of them, these people who had become, without his permission or his notice, the whole of his world.

"I was a warden for nineteen years," he said. "I hunted people like you. Like us. I told myself it was the law, and the law was the kingdom, and the kingdom was the people, and so long as I climbed that ladder of maybes I never had to look at what was at the bottom of it." His voice went rough. "I looked, in the end. Too late for some. There's a woman in the warden cells in Vess right now — a mender, like you, Isolde, a good woman who fixed children's crooked spines for free — and I signed the order that put her there, and I don't know if she's alive, and I will carry that whether or not I ever learn. So here is my piece: I'm going back to Vess because it's the right thing, and because I owe the city a debt I can only pay in the dark, and because—" he faltered, and hated himself for faltering, and pushed through "—because you three are the first thing in nineteen years I've believed in more than the badge. I'd walk into the Hollow itself for you. Vess is nothing."

Silence. Pip, for once, had nothing clever to say. Isolde's eyes were wet and she didn't hide it.

"Right," Freya said, hoarse. "Isolde?"

"I was going to say yes anyway," she said, "but now Brannic's made it awkward, so I have to say something better." A wet, tired laugh went around the circle. She straightened. "I mended a cracked bowl this morning. Just a bowl. And I thought — the Gate's a bowl too, isn't it? A bowl holding back the sea. And someone's chipping at it. And I am *not* the woman who stands in a dry room and lets the sea come in because the walk was long." She lifted her chin. "Yes. Vess. And I'll have draughts ready by dawn."

"Pip?"

The boy was quiet a moment, picking at the map's edge. When he spoke, the flippancy had drained out of him and what was left was young and bare.

"I never had a vote before," he said. "In anything. Nobody ever asked Pip what Pip wanted — they told him where to crawl and what to steal and when to run." He looked up. "You asked. So, yeah. Vess. And I'm going first through the drain, because it's my door, and nobody argues with a man in his own doorway."

Freya nodded, and Brannic saw him swallow something enormous.

"Then it's settled. Dawn. We sleep in shifts."

* * *

They did not sleep, much.

Brannic took the first watch and stood in the waystation's broken doorway, looking east, where the land fell away toward the low hills and beyond them, two hundred miles of bad road away, the dark mass of Vess crouched under its smoke. The moon was a paring of bone. Frost was coming early this year; the grass glittered like crushed glass, and his breath hung in the air and drifted apart.

He heard Isolde before he saw her — she moved quietly for a woman her age, but the night had a way of announcing the people you loved. She came to stand beside him with two cups of something steaming, and handed him one without asking. It was willowbark and honey and something sharper underneath.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"One of us should."

"We'll both be useless at dawn and Freya will make that face at us." She sipped. For a while they just stood, shoulder to shoulder, watching the frost creep across the yard. Then she said, without preamble: "The woman in the cells. The mender. What was her name?"

Brannic's cup stilled halfway to his mouth. "Maren. Maren Ostwick."

"Then we get her out too." Isolde said it the way she said everything — plainly, as a fact of the world, like *water is wet* and *bread needs salt*. "While we're under the city. You don't leave a name behind in a place like that. I know what the cells do to people. My sister was in the cells in Corvane for eleven days before they released her, and she came out with her body whole and the rest of her—" She stopped. Started again. "We get her out. That's all. I'm not asking for a plan. I'm telling you what the plan includes now."

Brannic looked at her — this small, grey-streaked woman who had lost a sister to the cells and a husband to the plague and a village to the edict, and who had somehow come out of all of it with her heart not merely intact but *open*, wide as a gate herself. He thought about how he had spent nineteen years learning to close, and how these three had spent three weeks teaching him, without ever once trying, to open again.

"All right," he said. "We get her out."

"Good." She finished her tea. "Now stand there and watch the dark, and I'll go make sure Pip hasn't stolen the thimble again."

* * *

Dawn came up grey and cold, and they made the road.

Isolde's draughts were bitter as grave-soil and worked like a miracle: a swallow each, and the world's attention slid off them like rain off oiled cloth. Not invisibility — nothing so kind — but a dullness, a forgettability. Brannic tested it at the first crossroads, where a warden picket stood around a brazier checking travelers' papers. The soldiers' eyes passed over the four of them the way eyes pass over a fencepost. One of them yawned.

"It won't hold past three days," Isolde murmured as they walked on, her voice pitched under the wind. "And it thins if we're frightened. Fear burns it fast. So whatever happens — don't be frightened."

"Easy," Pip whispered.

"I wasn't talking to you. You're frightened constantly. It's practically your resting state."

Pip stole maps. That became the shape of the journey. At a toll-bridge he lifted a garrison dispatch case off a mule's flank while Brannic stood talking to the toll-keeper about the weather. In a market town he came back from an hour alone with a warden patrol route scratched on slate, copied from a slate he swore he'd returned to its proper wall before anyone missed it. He brought them intelligence the way other boys brought home stray dogs — proudly, and expecting to be told he could keep it.

And each night, around whatever fire was small enough and sheltered enough, they planned. The drain under Tanner's Row. The warden cells and Maren Ostwick. The descent to the Hollow, whose entrance Brannic knew from his warden years — a sealed stair beneath the old chapel of Saint Verene, known to fewer than a dozen living men, and he had reason to hope he was one of the few still breathing. Freya drew the Gate from memory: the ring of standing seals, the ninefold binding, the words of the closing rite that he spoke only in a whisper, as if the words themselves had weight enough to bruise.

On the third night, the draughts thinning and the hills of Vess a black serration on the horizon, Freya sat apart from the fire with his scarred palms open on his knees, and Brannic came and sat beside him.

"How bad?" he asked. He'd learned to ask it plainly. Freya lied about small things — his hunger, his tiredness — but never about the Gate.

"Worse." Freya's voice was flat. "This morning a seal broke. Not frayed — *broke*. I felt it go like a tooth cracking. Something on the other side noticed us noticing it." He closed his hands. "Brannic. If we're too late — if we get there and it's already open —"

"Then we close it," Brannic said. "However late. However much it costs. That's the whole of the plan, and it's the only plan I've ever believed in completely."

Freya looked at him, and in the firelight his eyes were the eyes of a man standing on the edge of a very deep place.

"You know the worst part?" Freya said. "A year ago I would have given anything for someone to make this decision for me. And now I have all of you, and you *have* made it with me, and I'd give anything for you not to have to."

"Too late," said Pip's voice from the darkness, where the boy was supposedly asleep. "You're stuck with us, charm-seller. All the way down."

Isolde's soft laugh. Freya's quieter one, cracked at the edges. The fire burning low and gold against the coming dark of Vess.

Brannic watched the hills and thought of Maren Ostwick's name, and of the drain under Tanner's Row, and of the stair beneath Saint Verene's chapel, and of what waited at the bottom of it, growing less patient by the hour.

Two days, he thought. Two days, and we walk into the heart of the hunt.

Above the black hills, far to the east, a single red spark rose over the walls of Vess — a signal fire, or a beacon, or a warning — and Brannic felt, with a warden's old instinct he could never quite kill, that the city already knew they were coming.
