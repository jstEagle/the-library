## 13. Tomas in the Warden Cells

Vess at night had always been a city of small lights — candle-glow in bakery windows, the amber smear of taverns, the lanterns bobbing over the canal like patient fireflies. Freya remembered it that way even now, standing on the ridge of the granary roof with the wind pulling at his hood, and what he saw instead made something in his chest go cold and quiet.

The lights were gone. In their place: bonfires in the market square, great orange wounds in the dark, and around each one the shapes of men feeding it with furniture, with books, with doorframes pried from houses whose owners had either fled or been taken. Smoke rolled east on the wind, thick with the smell of burning oak and something underneath it, sweeter and wronger, that Freya chose not to name. He had seen enough pyres in the last three weeks to know.

"They're not even pretending anymore," Brannic said beside him. The disgraced warden lay flat against the slate tiles, his scarred hands folded, watching the warden barracks across the square with the stillness of a man who had learned patience in places where impatience meant dying. "Curfew bells rang at sundown. Anyone on the streets after dark is fair arrest. No warrant, no questions."

"Then we'd better not be anyone," Pip whispered from Freya's other side. The thief was a thin shadow among shadows, his eyes catching the firelight like a fox's. "I counted six patrols between us and the cells. Rotating. They cross at the fountain every quarter hour, which means we've got—" he squinted, lips moving, "—about as long as it takes Isolde to worry herself into a fit."

Below them, at the mouth of the alley where they'd left her, Isolde was doing exactly that. She had wanted to come. Freya had refused, and the refusal had cost him more than he'd expected — she'd looked at him with those steady hedge-witch eyes and said, *You'll want someone to carry you home after*, and he hadn't understood her then. He suspected he would soon.

"Tomas is in the undercells of the warden keep," Brannic said. "That's where they hold anyone worth questioning. I know the way in — there's an old drainage culvert from when the keep was a customs house, before Aldous's grandfather put walls up. It's grated now, but grates have never been a problem for our friend here." He nodded at Pip without looking at him.

"Grates are the *easy* part," Pip muttered, but he was already checking the little kit of picks and wires he kept wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs. "It's what's behind them I don't like. Wardens. Chains. The smell."

Freya said nothing. He had been silent most of the day, since the plan had settled into its final shape around the cold ashes of their morning fire. They had come back to Vess for the Gate — for the seals he could feel fraying in his sleep like stitches pulled loose from a hem — but the Gate sat beneath the city like a stone at the bottom of a well, and above it, in a cell he could not stop imagining, was a baker who had burned a warning into his own doorstep rather than let the wardens take Freya by surprise.

*Run, boy. Don't look back.*

He had looked back. That was the whole of his life's trouble, maybe. He looked back, and here he was, three hundred miles of road behind him, kneeling on a rooftop above the smoke of everything he'd spent three years building.

"We go down at the change of patrol," Brannic said. "In and out. We get him, we take him to Isolde at the tannery gate, and the four of us are under the Hollow before dawn."

"The four of us plus one," Freya said quietly.

Brannic glanced at him then, and there was something unreadable in the old warden's face — pity, or the memory of pity, which is sometimes worse. "Aye," he said. "Plus one."

* * *

The culvert smelled of a century of Vess's refuse, and Freya breathed through his mouth as they waded the last stretch of thigh-deep water. Above them the grate was a lattice of iron bars furred with rust, and beyond it, faintly, the sound of boots on stone.

Pip went up the culvert wall like something born in it — toes finding purchase on seams Freya couldn't see, one hand free, the other holding a bent wire he'd shaped against his knee. He pressed his face close to the bars and made a sound low in his throat, barely a breath, almost a note. Freya had heard him do it twice before, whispering to locks, and had never quite decided whether it was magic or madness or just a thief's superstition worn so long it had become true. The lock above clicked. The grate swung on hinges no one had oiled in years, silent as an apology.

"Clear," Pip breathed down. "Two guards, both looking the other way. Warden keeps never post their good men at the drains. Nobody respectable thinks about where the filth goes."

"That," Brannic said, hauling himself up through the gap, "is the single wisest thing anyone has ever said about institutions."

They came up into a cellar stacked with confiscated goods — chairs, chests, a harp with its strings cut, all of it tagged with wax seals and dust. From there a stair rose toward the sounds of the keep proper, and Brannic took the lead, moving with a certainty that still startled Freya. Three years ago this man had walked these halls wearing the grey cloak; he knew which stairs groaned, which torches left pools of shadow between them, which doors the night clerks propped open because the hinges squealed and no one had fixed them since Aldous took the throne.

Twice they pressed themselves into alcoves while boots passed close enough to touch. Once a door opened directly ahead of them and a young warden stepped out, yawning, and Brannic simply stood in the middle of the corridor with the weary authority of a man who belonged there, and the boy nodded to him and walked on. Freya's heart hammered so hard he was sure the whole keep could hear it. Brannic didn't even breathe differently.

The undercells were below the waterline, down a spiral stair where the air turned cold and mineral, where the walls wept and the torches burned blue at their tips. The smell hit Freya first — wet stone, old straw, and beneath it the sweet-wrong smell from the fires above, concentrated, intimate. Then the sounds. Not screaming. Screaming would have been easier somehow. What he heard was breathing: dozens of people breathing in the dark, carefully, the way you breathe when you've learned that noise brings attention and attention brings pain.

There were more prisoners than he had imagined. More than the edicts had promised. Rows of doors, iron-barred windows set at ankle height, and behind each one a shape, or several shapes, huddled.

"Gods," Pip whispered. His voice had gone very young. "There's kids down here."

"We can't," Freya started, and stopped, because he didn't know how to finish the sentence. *We can't help them all.* It was true. It was also the kind of truth that curdles in the mouth.

"One door," Brannic said softly. "One. That's the mission. Say it with me if you have to."

They found Tomas by asking the dark. A woman in the third cell — her voice cracked to a rasp, her wrists ringed with healing burns — told them the baker was two rows down, the big one, the one who wouldn't stop singing. *Singing?* Freya had asked. *What does he sing?* And she had laughed, a terrible dry sound, and said, *Bread songs. Kneading songs. Drives the questioners mad. They moved him three times to get away from it.*

Freya followed the sound before he followed the directions. Down the row, past cell after cell, a low rumble rose and fell, tuneless and stubborn, a man humming to himself in the dark the way he must have hummed over his dough for thirty years. Freya reached the barred window, dropped to his knees on the wet stone, and put his eye to it.

Tomas sat against the far wall, and Freya's first thought — his shameful, instant, human thought — was that it wasn't so bad, that Tomas was sitting up, that he was whole. Then the man turned his head toward the window, toward the faint gray suggestion of light, and Freya saw what the questioners had left of him.

His beard was half gone, torn out in patches. One eye had swollen shut days ago and healed crooked. His hands — those great flour-white hands Freya had watched fold loaves a thousand mornings, hands gentle enough to proof dough and strong enough to carry a sack of meal up three flights — his hands rested in his lap at angles hands should not rest. When he tried to rise, the motion stuttered, broke apart, and he laughed at himself, a soft wheeze, and stayed where he was.

"Well," Tomas said. His voice was gravel and honey gone to tar. "Either I've finally cracked, or that's Freya Nakamura's face at my window, and I'd bet my oven on the second."

"Tomas." Freya's voice came out broken into pieces. He gripped the bars. Cold iron, cold stone, and between them a distance of six feet that felt like the width of the world. "I'm here. We're getting you out. There's a grate, a drain — Pip can—"

"No." Said gently. Terribly gently. The way you say no to a child. "Come closer to the bars. Let me see you properly. My eyes aren't what they were, thanks to our hosts."

Freya pressed his face to the iron. This close he could see the rest: the burn scars tracing up Tomas's forearms in neat deliberate lines, the marks of questions asked with hot iron and answered, apparently, with bread songs.

"They asked about you," Tomas said. "Every day. Where did the charm-seller buy his supplies. Who else bought charms. Did he ever show you anything — anything at all. I told them you were boring, Freya. I told them you were the dullest man in Vess and your charms were beads and glue and wishful thinking." He smiled, and the smile cost him visibly, dragging at things that didn't want to move. "I think they believed me about the dullness. Nothing else."

"I'm going to get you out of here." Freya heard his own voice shake. Behind him he could feel Brannic and Pip waiting in the dark, patient, letting him have this. "Isolde's outside the walls. Her gift — mending, Tomas, she can mend *anything* — bones, flesh—"

"Boy." Tomas reached out and laid one ruined hand flat against the bars, palm to palm with Freya's, six inches of iron between them. "Listen to me, because I haven't got many words left in me today and I mean to spend them well. How did you find me?"

"A woman in the cells. Third row."

"And how did you get into the keep?"

"A drain. An unlocked—"

"How," Tomas said, and his voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow louder than shouting, "did a charm-seller, a thief, and a disgraced warden walk into the heart of the warden keep of Vess on the same week that the crown's own hunt-captain arrives with orders from the king himself? Think, Freya. You're cleverer than the dull man I loved."

The cold came up through the floor then, or through Freya's spine; he couldn't have said which.

"She let me live," Freya said slowly. "The wardens took me and I'm still alive. They don't leave witnesses. They don't—"

"They don't leave bait, either," Tomas said. "Unless they want something to come to the hook." He drew a long breath, and Freya watched him gather himself, watched him decide to spend his words exactly where he'd said he would. "The captain came to my cell yesterday. Serah Vane. Younger than I expected. Colder, too. She sat where you're sitting and she asked me what I knew about the Hollow. About gates." His good eye found Freya's and held it, and in it Freya saw the terrible arithmetic the baker had been doing alone in the dark. "She knows what you are, boy. She doesn't want your head. She wants you to lead her somewhere. And she wanted me to tell you — she said these exact words, and I've chewed on them all night trying to find another meaning and there isn't one — she said, *tell the keeper the door remembers his hand.*"

Silence, except for the dripping of the weeping walls and the careful breathing of the hidden prisoners and Freya's own heartbeat, loud and stupid in his ears.

"She let you live," he said, "to be a message."

"To be a message and a hook," Tomas agreed. "Both. Efficient woman. I expect she's very good at her work." He leaned back against the wall, and for a moment the baker's humor surfaced through the ruin of him, familiar as the smell of a warm loaf. "You should have seen her face when I sang, though. Cracked, just a little. There's a person under all that frost. Remember that. Might matter later."

"Tomas, I can't leave you."

"You can. You will." He raised the ruined hand, and Freya helped him press it again to the bars, palm to palm. "Listen. If you try to take me out of here tonight, one of two things happens. Either we're caught, and everything she wants comes true. Or we're not caught, and she learns her bait ran off the hook, and next time there won't be a message — there'll just be fire, everywhere you love, until you stop running. I'm an old man with bad hands. You're the last of whatever you are. That trade isn't a hard sum, Freya. Even a baker can do it."

"I owe you my life."

"You owe me nothing. I burned that warning because it was mine to burn." His breath caught, steadied. "But I'll tell you what you can give me, since you insist on owing. Go seal your door. Whatever's under this city — I've seen her face when she talks about it, and under the frost she is *afraid*, and that frightens me more than any iron ever has. Go do the thing only you can do. And live. That's the payment. Live, and open a shop somewhere, and name a loaf after me, and grow old enough to complain about your knees. Can you do that?"

Freya could not speak. He pressed his forehead against the iron bars, and Tomas's fingers, crooked and burned, curled as best they could around his.

Behind him, softly, Pip made a sound that might have been a swallowed sob, and Brannic's hand landed heavy and warm on Freya's shoulder.

"Time," Brannic murmured. "We're past time."

"I'll come back," Freya whispered. It was a promise, and a lie, and he didn't know which of them Tomas believed, and hated himself for not knowing.

"No," Tomas said, kind and clear and final. "You'll win. That's better than coming back." He released Freya's hand and settled himself against the wall, and lifted his chin, and began, low and cracked and utterly unbroken, to sing the kneading song.

They left him singing. Freya's legs shook on the spiral stair; Pip had to steer him twice by the elbow. At the cellar of confiscated goods the thief stopped, pressed his sleeve hard against his eyes, and said, furiously, "I hate this city," and Brannic said, "So do I," and pushed the grate open onto the black water and the long climb back toward the smoke-red sky.

* * *

They did not speak on the roofs. They crossed Vess like ghosts with a debt, dropping through a cooper's yard, along a wall-top where the mortar crumbled, over the tannery fence into the reeking dark where Isolde waited with the horses and a flask of willow-bark tea and a face that read their failure before a word was spoken.

"He wouldn't come," she said. Not a question.

"He couldn't come." Freya swung down from the wall and stood in front of her, and found he could not make his hands stop shaking, so he held them together and pretended it was the cold. "She kept him alive as bait. She sent a message through him, Isolde. She knows what I am. She knows about the Gate, and she *wanted* me to hear it — she wanted me here, in this city, right now, feeling exactly this." He heard his voice crack and despised it. "And the worst part — the truly unforgivable part — is that it worked. Because I'm here. And because some cowardly piece of me is glad of the excuse, glad there was a reason, glad the choice was made for me, because I could not have made it myself."

Isolde looked at him for a long moment. Then she stepped forward and did the thing no one else had done: she put her arms around him, and held on, flour-smell and tallow-smell and horse-sweat, her chin against his shoulder.

"Grief isn't guilt," she said into his ear. "You'll spend the next month confusing them. Everyone does. But he's alive, Freya. Alive and singing. There are men in those cells who weren't left alive for anything." She drew back and held his face in both hands, the way she probably once held her children, years ago, in another life. "Now tell me the message. All of it. Every word."

"*The door remembers his hand,*" Freya said. "Her exact words, through a broken baker, delivered to the one man in Vess who'd understand them. Which means she's not hunting me anymore. She's inviting me." He looked up at the sky above the warden keep, where the smoke blurred the stars into smears, and the cold thing in his chest that had been there since the river ford shifted, and sharpened, and became certainty. "She's not trying to catch me before I reach the Gate, Isolde. She's clearing the road."

Brannic had gone very still by the horses. "Why would a hunt-captain clear the road to the one sealed door in the kingdom?" he said slowly. "Unless she wasn't guarding it for the king. Unless she was keeping everyone away from it *for her own reasons* — and now she needs the keeper's help to hold what she's been holding alone."

Pip, red-eyed, sniffling, stared at the older man. "You think she's a keeper too?"

"I think," Brannic said, swinging up into the saddle, "that tomorrow night we go down into the Hollow, and I think we'd best assume we're walking into a conversation, not an ambush. And I think—" he paused, listening, and his face changed.

From the direction of the keep, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring. Not the curfew bell. The alarm — fast, urgent, doubled. And beneath it, rolling up out of the ground itself, up through the cobbles and the rooftops and the soles of Freya's feet, came a vibration he had felt only once before, three years ago, kneeling alone in the dark before the Hollow Gate:

the sound of a seal, very deep down, beginning to break.

"It's starting," Freya breathed. "Whoever's unbinding it — it's *starting*. We have to go. Now. Tonight."

They rode for the eastern wall with Vess ringing behind them like a struck bell, and the taste of a promise he couldn't keep still bitter on Freya's tongue, and the door beneath the city remembering his hand.
