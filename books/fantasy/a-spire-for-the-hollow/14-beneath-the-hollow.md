## 14. Beneath the Hollow

The dark beneath Vess did not feel like darkness. It felt like held breath.

Freya went down through the mouth of the old cistern behind the tanner's row, where the bricks had crumbled into a slope of soft rubble and the smell of wet lime covered the smell of men. He had left Isolde and Brannic in the cellar of the shuttered dyehouse with a lamp and a knife between them, and he had not told Pip where he was going because Pip would have insisted on coming, and because some doors a man had to open alone. The rope was old but true. He paid it out hand over hand and let his boots find the rungs worn smooth into the stone — rungs no mason had cut, if the histories were honest, but something older, patient, waiting for hands like his.

Three years since he had made this descent. Three years of checking seals from the surface, reading the hum through floorboards and cellar stone like a man reading a letter through a sealed envelope. He had told himself it was enough. He had told himself a lot of things.

Now, as his soles touched the first gallery and the dark closed over him like water, he felt the Gate's condition plainly for the first time in three years, and it was like plunging his arm into winter.

*Thinning.* Not broken — the seven seals still stood, seven knots of silence strung down the deep places like prayer beads — but thin, stretched, the way cloth thins before it parts. He could feel each one the way you feel your own heartbeat when you lie awake at night afraid. And beneath them, under all that careful quiet, something vast turning over in its sleep, slow as a whale under ice.

"Oh," Freya said aloud, to no one. His voice came back flattened, swallowed. "Oh, you're awake."

The gallery took him east and down. He knew the way the way he knew his own pulse: past the cistern channels the city had drunk from for four hundred years without knowing what they drained around; past the pillar-carvings worn to suggestion — figures with raised arms, figures bound; past the niche where the last keeper before him had scratched a tally of visits that stopped mid-stroke, one mark deeper than the rest, unfinished. He walked with his hand on the wall because the wall hummed, and the humming told him more than his eyes could have.

The seals were being *worked*. That was the horror of it, the thing he had understood on the road from Mereth and refused to look at straight: this was not decay. Decay was slow and aimless. This had direction. Someone had been unbinding the knots one strand at a time, gently, over months, the way you might loosen a knot in another man's bootlace while he slept — patiently, and with intent.

And now, as he came down the last stair into the antechamber of the Hollow, intent met intent, because there was light below him. A small, steady, chemical light, the kind wardens carried. And a voice, low, counting.

Freya stopped on the stair with his heart slamming and his gift rising unbidden along his arms, seal-scars prickling hot beneath both sleeves — the seven pale marks the Gate had pressed into his skin at nineteen, one for each knot, the marks he had spent years hiding under charcoal sleeves and lies about scalding accidents. He should go back. He should climb until his lungs burned and fetch Brannic, whose sword-arm was still good, whose hatred of the hunt would carry him down any stair in the world.

Instead he looked down past the edge of the last step, and forgot every sensible thought he owned.

There was a lamp set on the floor. There were chalk lines — no, not chalk, ground bone and ash, the old medium — laid out in a ring around the Gate's threshold, precise as surgery. And kneeling inside the ring, sleeves rolled, palms bloody to the wrist, working the sixth seal's anchor-point with her bare hands and her voice, was Warden-Captain Serah Vane.

She was not unbinding it.

He saw that before he saw anything else, before terror even finished arriving. Unbinding had a sound he knew now from three years of listening — a loosening, a sigh, threads sliding free. What she was doing had the opposite grain entirely. Her voice moved in the flat cadence of a rite he had learned at his own grandmother's knee, syllable for syllable, and where her bloodied fingers pressed the anchor-stone, the seal was *tightening*, drawing in like a wound closing by force of will alone. Badly. Crude, half-taught, missing two of the nine turns — but tightening.

She was trying to save it. She was trying to do his work.

Freya's foot shifted on the stair, and a grain of grit clicked once against stone, and in that silence it rang like a dropped coin.

Serah Vane came up off her knees faster than anything her size had a right to move, lamp snuffed with a pinch of her fingers, knife already out and leveled at the dark. "Whoever you are," she said, and her voice was perfectly level too, which frightened him more than shouting would have, "you've walked a long way to die somewhere interesting."

"It's me." His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat. "Vane. It's Galloway."

Stillness. Long enough that he counted his own breaths. Then, quietly: "Strike a light, then. If I'm wrong about what walks out of that dark, I'd rather know before I bleed any more."

He struck the wick of his own small flame — the little charmless fire-spark his gift could coax from almost nothing, the trick that had sold ten thousand luck-charms — and held it up between them.

She looked exactly as she had at the river ford, seen across forty yards of reeds and panic: tall, spare, grey-eyed, her warden's braid coming loose down her back, a bruise gone green-yellow along her jaw from Brannic's elbow. But there were new lines carved around her mouth, and her hands — her hands were ruined, the fingertips split and weeping, the nails cracked to the quick. Days of this work. Maybe weeks.

"You're bleeding," Freya said stupidly.

"You're observant," said Serah Vane. She did not lower the knife. "You're also standing between me and my light, above a door that opens onto the end of the world, and you're going to tell me how you know the keeper's cadence, charm-seller. Because only one kind of person knows it, and I watched that kind die out with my grandmother."

The word fell into the chamber and kept falling. *Keeper.*

Freya found that after three years of lying — to customers, to neighbors, to Tomas across the warm counter of the bakery, to himself most of all — the truth came out of him strangely light, almost effortless, like a stone finally set down.

"I'm the other kind," he said. He pushed up his sleeve and turned his forearm to the lamplight, and let her see the seven marks pale against his skin, arranged in their old constellation. "My name isn't really Galloway. Well. It is now. It wasn't first."

For a long moment Serah Vane did not move at all. Then she laughed — one short, harsh, disbelieving breath that was not quite laughter — and the knife lowered, though it did not vanish. She sat back on her heels among her ash-lines like a soldier granted an unexpected rest, and rubbed her ruined hand across her eyes.

"Seven years," she said. "Seven years I served the hunt. I burned hedge-witches out of attics. I put a boy of fifteen in irons for lighting a candle with a whisper. And the whole time, the man who sold charms two streets from my barracks was the last keeper of the Hollow Gate, and I never—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "My grandmother told me stories. She called herself a seamstress. She taught me the cadence when I was eight, made me swear to use it only if the hum ever woke. She said our family had kept a door once, long ago, and that the keeping had passed to other bloodlines, and that if I ever heard humming under a city's floors I would know what it meant. I thought she was senile." Her jaw worked. "Then Aldous started pulling wardens off every post in the kingdom and sending them here, to Vess, to stand guard over cisterns. And I came down to see why."

"And found the seals loosening."

"Someone's been at them since midsummer. Not a keeper — no keeper would leave it this ragged. They're unbinding it blind, following some stolen fragment of the rite, tearing where they should turn. Every week another thread gives way." She gestured at the ring of ash, at her split hands. "I've been slowing them. Putting back what I can. I'm a fool with a borrowed needle — I know perhaps a tenth of what this work needs — but it buys days. Maybe a fortnight."

"Why?" Freya came down the last steps at last, slowly, into the antechamber proper. Up close the Gate itself showed at the far end of the hollow: not a door as anyone would know it, but a seam in the world, a vertical line of wrongness where the dark grew darker, floor and ceiling and wall all bending subtly toward it like water toward a drain. Even dimmed under six strained seals it pulled at the eye. He made himself look away from it. "Why is Aldous doing this, Vane? Why would a usurper with a whole kingdom to hold want to crack open the one thing his ancestors built their crowns on top of? The first kings bound those Powers. Every royal house since has known the bargain. Opening the Gate doesn't win him a war. It ends the world."

"That's what I thought." She looked up at him, and in the lamplight her grey eyes had something in them he had never imagined finding there: fear. Real fear, worn openly, the fear of a woman who had audited her own convictions and found them wanting. "So I asked questions I shouldn't have. Read dispatches addressed to the Grand Warden alone. Sat outside doors." She drew a folded paper from inside her breastplate and held it out without letting go of it, so that he had to take it from her hand, their ruined fingers nearly touching. "Aldous doesn't believe the Powers will destroy him, Galloway. That's the part I couldn't make myself understand until I read his own hand in it. He believes they'll *crown* him."

Freya unfolded the page. The script was cramped, urgent, a king's private madness leaking through courtly phrasing. He read it twice because the first time his eyes slid off the words.

*…the Old Powers are not beasts but a court, and a court requires a king. The binding of the first kings was theft — the theft of a crown that waits. Let it be opened rightly. Let it be opened with keeper's blood, freely spilled at the threshold, and what sleeps will wake owing its crown to the hand that freed it. I will be that hand. I will be war-king over a court of gods…*

"He needs a keeper," Serah said, into the silence. "The opening rite fails without it. Keeper's blood at the seam, freely given — that's the keyhole, my grandmother's stories agree with his scholars on that much. For months he's been hunting the gifted kingdom-wide, and everyone thought it was ideology, cleansing, the usual theater of tyrants." Her mouth twisted. "It was a net. He's been trawling for you specifically. Tomas didn't betray you by accident, Galloway. The informant who named you was sent to your stall with a list of eleven names, and yours was the only one that mattered."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly. Freya sat down — not gracefully, just down, onto the cold stone beside her ash-ring, the paper crinkling in his fist. He thought of Tomas's face in the warden cell two nights ago, swollen, barely recognizable, alive because someone had decided it should be. Bait for a charm-seller. Bait for the last keeper of the Hollow Gate.

"Isolde," he said suddenly. "Pip. Brannic. Everyone we left on the road, everyone the hunt has taken since midsummer — he's been collecting us. Testing bloodlines."

"Likely. And discarding failures." She said it without flinching, because she had been the instrument and knew. "I requested the duty of hunting you personally. I told Aldous it was pride — a captain retrieving her own escaped prisoner." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "I didn't tell him I'd worked out what he intends. Or that my grandmother's blood runs in me, thin as it is, and might serve his rite nearly as well as yours."

Freya looked at her sharply. "You offered yourself as bait to a tyrant."

"I offered myself as a hunting hound to a madman," Serah corrected. "There's a difference. Hounds get close enough to bite." She reached into her collar and drew out a small object on a cord: a tablet of grey ward-stone, older than the kingdom, carved dense with the same nine-turn script that spiraled through the seals. "This was hers. My grandmother's. She died when I was sixteen and left me this and a cadence and a story about a door. I've spent twenty years wishing I'd listened harder." She held it out to him properly this time, open-palmed. "Her sealing rites are complete. All nine turns, all seven knots, everything the fragment Aldous stole leaves out. Take it."

Freya didn't take it. Not yet. "You understand what you're handing me. You're defecting. Against your own oath, your own wardens, your king."

"Aldous isn't a king." Her voice came out low and absolutely certain, and for a moment she sounded so much like Brannic that Freya's chest ached. "Kings keep things. He means to sell the world for a crown that doesn't exist. I've made worse mistakes than choosing against that." She glanced sideways at him, and something dry flickered through the fear in her face. "Besides. You need me. You're one keeper with a fortnight of head start to erase, and I'm the only person alive who knows the layout of his garrison, the rotation of his seals-detail, and which cellar in this city currently holds a baker I'm given to understand matters to you."

"Tomas." His voice caught on it. "You let him live."

"I let him live because dead bait catches nothing." She said it plainly, refusing to dress it. Then, quieter, looking down at her ruined hands: "And because he wouldn't stop saying your name. Even with his teeth loose. Even when they explained what saying it cost him. I've broken a lot of people, Galloway. I've never heard one be that loyal. It did something to me I'm still paying for."

Above them — faint, filtered through four hundred feet of city and stone — a bell began to ring. One stroke, pause, two strokes. The night-watch pattern. Ordinary. Except that it was followed, seconds later, by another bell, and then a third, out of sequence, overlapping, wrong.

Serah was on her feet before the third bell finished, lamp relit, knife out again — but pointed now toward the stair, guarding his back, the geometry of her body changed entirely, enemy become shield. "That's mustering," she said. "Not fire, not riot. *Mustering.* Somebody gave an order tonight, hours early, and the garrison's moving."

Freya felt it then — not heard, *felt*, through the seven scars on his arm, through the marrow of him — the sixth seal give a shudder like a struck harp string, one thread slacker than it had been an hour ago. Not Serah's work. Not decay.

Work. Deliberate, resumed work, from a direction he couldn't name, in hands he suddenly understood had never stopped.

"They know you're here," he breathed. "Or they don't need to wait anymore. Either way—"

"Either way," Serah Vane said, pulling him toward the stair, "the man who wants your blood at that threshold now knows exactly where you're standing. Move, keeper. We'll argue about trusting each other somewhere with fewer gods behind the walls."
