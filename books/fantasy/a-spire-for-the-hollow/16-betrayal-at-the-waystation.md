## 16. Betrayal at the Waystation

Pip had always known the dark was a country with borders, and that he'd been born on the wrong side of them.

He'd learned its geography young — the cold seams between warehouses in Vess where a boy could fold himself small enough to sleep, the warm dark behind bakery ovens, the black water under the tannery bridges where things floated that you didn't look at twice. Dark was just another street. You learned its corners, its dead ends, which shadows were safe and which had teeth. He'd thought he knew all of it.

Then they put the hood on him, and he learned there was a kind of dark that wasn't a place at all. It was a mouth.

* * *

It had gone wrong so fast he still couldn't make the pieces sit together in his head. They'd been three days out of Mereth, following the old salt road back toward Vess, moving at night, sleeping in ditches and culverts like animals. Isolde's concealment draughts made them smell of wet stone and rot, wrong to hounds, and for two days it worked. Pip had even started to feel clever again. Clever was his natural state. Clever was how a gutter-rat survived when bigger rats wanted what he'd lifted.

The waystation had been Brannic's idea — a ruined coaching inn half-swallowed by gorse, walls still standing to shoulder height, a cellar below with one entrance and good sightlines. "We rest till moonset," Brannic said, "then move." And Pip, who should have been watching the road because watching roads was the one thing he was actually good at, had let himself do something worse than sleep.

He'd started to feel safe.

That was the poison in it, he understood now. Not the draught going stale, not the wind turning, not even the dogs. It was the feeling — sitting in the cellar with Isolde mending the hem of his coat without being asked, humming some lullaby from her village, Freya dozing against the wall with his scarred hands loose in his lap, Brannic keeping watch with the patient stillness of a man who had decided these people were worth dying for — it was the feeling of *home*, and Pip had never once in his life been allowed to keep anything like home, and so of course, of *course*, the moment he reached for it, the world took it back.

The vanguard came an hour before dawn. No horns, no challenge. Just the dogs first — he heard them too late, their voices wrong, not barking but that low chuffing eagerness — and then the cellar door burst inward and the world became boots and torchlight and someone's knee driving into his spine, and a voice saying *the little one, take the little one alive.*

He fought. He wanted to remember that he'd fought. He bit someone's hand hard enough to taste leather and blood, and he screamed Isolde's name until something struck the back of his head and the dark mouth closed over him.

* * *

When the hood came off, the light hurt worse than the beating had.

They'd taken him to a farmhouse — or what had been a farmhouse. The vanguard had gutted it for a camp: furniture burned in the yard, the family nowhere visible, which was its own kind of answer. Pip sat bound to a chair in what had been the parlor, in front of a fire built from somebody's table, and tried to be small. Small was a skill. He made himself a stone, a sack, a nothing.

The officer who came to him didn't look like a warden. Wardens wore grey and moved like priests. This man wore the king's new black and gold, and he had the cheerful, well-fed face of a man who enjoyed his work the way other men enjoyed fishing.

"Well," the officer said, crouching to Pip's eye level. "The lock-whisperer."

Pip said nothing. His head was ringing. There was blood drying stiff on his collar.

"Brannic's little crew. We've been reading your trail for a week — you're not subtle, did you know that? A disgraced warden, a hedge-witch, the last Gatekeeper, and a thief." He smiled. "You're famous, boy. Do you understand? There are officers south of here who would pay a month's wages for a sketch of your face."

"I'm prettier in person," Pip managed. His voice came out thinner than he wanted.

The officer laughed like Pip had said something delightful. Then he took hold of Pip's broken-left-hand — when had that happened? during the taking, he supposed; he'd been clenching it into a fist and someone had stamped on it — and squeezed, gently, almost tenderly, and Pip's vision went white and he heard himself make a sound he'd never made before, high and animal, and hated it instantly and forever.

"There it is," the officer said softly. "There's the truth of you. All this talk of found family, all this loyalty — I've broken a hundred of you lot, boy, and I'll tell you what I've learned. Everyone has a price. Yours isn't coin. Yours is simpler." He leaned close. His breath smelled of wine and roast meat. "Yours is fear."

"I don't know anything," Pip said. "I'm the thief. Nobody tells the thief—"

"You know where they're going."

Everyone has a price. Pip stared at the fire made of somebody's kitchen table and thought about the first time he'd ever stolen food for someone else instead of himself — day-old rolls for Tomas's stepdaughter, back when Pip had still lived in Vess, back when kindness had been a thing that happened to other people. He thought about Isolde humming. About Freya saying, once, around a fire, *you're not cargo, Pip, you're ours* — embarrassed as soon as he said it, ears going red, and Pip had carried those words around inside him for weeks like a coal he cupped in both hands.

"Here's how tonight goes," the officer said, straightening up, all business now. "You tell me where the Gatekeeper is making for, and my lord Aldous's men ride light and fast, and your friends die quick — clean, even. I give you my word as an officer." He smiled again. "Or you say nothing, and we go get them anyway, because we have the dogs and the trail and time, and then I spend the whole night showing you what slow looks like. And afterward — afterward doesn't matter to you, does it? Because either way, boy, they die. The only question is whether you suffer first."

That was the trap of it, Pip saw dimly through the ringing in his skull. The officer wasn't even lying, probably. That was the worst part. The trail *was* there. The dogs *were* coming. His silence bought nothing except pain, and pain was the one currency Pip had spent his whole life learning never to pay.

He was a thief. Thieves survived. That was the whole of his creed, carved into him before he could write: run, hide, give up whatever you're carrying if they catch you, because a dead thief keeps nothing and saves no one. He'd told himself the creed was wisdom. Sitting in the burning warmth of a stranger's ruined parlor, he finally understood it had always just been fear wearing wisdom's coat.

"I want—" His voice cracked. He hated that too. "I want your word. Quick. Clean. Like you said."

"You have it."

And Pip, fourteen years old and shaking, with his broken hand cradled against his chest and tears he couldn't stop running hot down his face, told them.

"The Hollow," he whispered. "Under Vess. Beneath the old city, past the warden cells, down the keeper's stair. That's where the Gate is. That's where they're going — back to Vess, to seal it. That's everything. That's all of it. Please."

The officer wrote it down, unhurried, savoring it. Then he patted Pip's head like a dog's.

"You did the sensible thing," he said. "Truly. Most people never learn to be sensible."

They dragged him out to a supply wagon after that, and threw him in among the crates, and left him alone with the swaying dark and the sound of hooves and the distant voices of men preparing to ride.

* * *

He cried for a while. He wasn't proud of it, but there was nobody to see, and crying was at least honest.

Then he lay in the dark of the wagon and did the arithmetic of what he'd done, and the arithmetic was merciless.

Quick and clean. The officer's word. Pip had given up the only thing he'd ever been trusted with — not gold, not secrets about locks, but *people*. Four people who had looked at a gutter-rat and seen something worth keeping. And he'd handed them over in under a hundred heartbeats, because a man squeezed his hand and promised to be merciful, and Pip had believed him, or worse, hadn't cared whether it was true so long as the pain stopped.

He thought of Brannic holding the stair at some ford, bleeding, buying them time with his body. He thought of Isolde's gift, that small warm mending magic, and how she used it on other people's hurts before her own. He thought of Freya — quiet Freya, who carried the weight of the whole world under the city and had never once made Pip feel like baggage.

*You're ours.*

A coal in his hands. And he'd dropped it. He'd dropped it the moment it got hot.

The wagon jolted over a rut. Somewhere ahead, riders were moving toward the Hollow, toward Vess, toward the stair beneath the warden cells, carrying his words like a key.

Pip lay in the dark and felt something happen in his chest that he didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't courage — he knew courage when he felt it, and this wasn't it; this was colder and harder and it tasted of ash. It was the understanding that he had done the unforgivable thing, and that his friends might still live anyway, and that if they did — if by some grace they survived the men riding toward them with his betrayal in their ears — then he would spend the rest of his life owing them a debt that no amount of lock-whispering could pay.

Unless.

His broken hand throbbed. He flexed the fingers, wincing, testing. Two of them still worked.

He was, after all, still a thief. Still the boy who could whisper to locks. Still the smallest, quickest, most overlooked creature in any room — including, perhaps, a supply wagon guarded by men who considered him already broken and bagged.

The officer had asked where the Gatekeeper was going. Pip had answered. But there was one thing Pip hadn't told them, one thing he'd kept shut behind his teeth without even deciding to: the waystation. Where the four of them had split — because they *had* split, hours before the ambush, Brannic insisting on a decoy route, Isolde and Freya peeling off north while Pip and Brannic played the lure. The vanguard had taken Pip and lost the others. Which meant somewhere out in the dark, right now, Freya and Isolde might still be free, might still be moving toward Vess, might still—

Might still hear, someday, what he'd done.

Pip pressed his good hand over his eyes. In his mind he saw the cellar again, the fire, the humming, the word *ours*. Then he saw the officers' faces when they read their orders. *Beneath the old city, past the warden cells, down the keeper's stair.* They'd ride for Vess. They'd ride fast.

And Pip was going to escape this wagon if it killed him, follow them into the heart of the hunt, and stand between his betrayal and his family with whatever he had left — which was two working fingers, a whisper that locks listened to, and a shame the size of a kingdom.

He began, silently, to work at the knots.

The dark was still a mouth. But Pip had been born in the dark, and he knew its teeth, and he was done letting it chew on anyone but him.
