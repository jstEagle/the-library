## 17. The Army at the Gate

They came down into the Hollow in the hour before dawn, when the dark is thickest and the soul most willing to believe it will last.

Freya felt them before he heard them — a pressure against the seals like a thumb pressed slowly against the skin of a drum. He had lived with that drum for three years. He knew its every tremor: the settling of old stone, the slow breathing of whatever slept beyond the Gate, the small honest noises of a thing holding. This was none of those. This was iron and torchlight and two thousand boots finding the switchback stairs that wound down the Hollow's throat, and it came through his bones like a fever.

"They're in," Pip said from the ledge above him, and his voice was very small. "All of them. I did this."

Freya turned. In the grey half-light the boy looked younger than he ever had — thirteen going on fourteen, all elbows and hollow cheeks, his thief's fingers wrapped around a stolen spyglass as though it were the only warm thing left in the world. Behind him, Isolde was already moving among their small camp, kicking dirt over the fire, gathering her jars with hands that shook only a little. Brannic stood at the lip of the descent with his sword drawn, watching the torches below bloom one after another down the spiral, a garland of orange flowers being strung through the dark.

"Stop," Freya said to Pip. He crossed the rock and took the boy's shoulders. "Look at me. You gave them a road because they were going to take you apart piece by piece to get it. I know what fear does. I have spent my whole life being afraid." He tightened his grip. "What matters now is not how they learned the way. What matters is whether we are standing between them and the Gate when they arrive. So stand up, Pip. Stand up and be where I need you."

Pip's chin came up. His eyes were wet and furious and alive. "Where do you need me?"

"The upper gallery. The locks on the floodgates — can you whisper them open?"

The boy swallowed. Nodded. Went.

* * *

The Hollow had been named for its emptiness, but it had never truly been empty. It was a throat of stone beneath Vess, older than the city whose weight it carried, ribbed with galleries and buttressed arches raised by masons whose names the first kings had ordered struck from every record. At its bottom lay the Gate: not a door, exactly, though it had a door's shape — a seam in the world forty feet high, framed in black basalt carved with spirals that hurt to look at too long, sealed with nine wards laid down in blood by keepers across nine generations. Freya was the ninth. The scars on his palms, thin white lines like the creases of a glove, ached whenever the wards were touched.

He could feel them being touched now. Somewhere below, Aldous's engineers were working at the outermost seal with chisels and ward-irons and, if Serah's intelligence held, a captured keeper's rite-book. The first ward had been designed to die loudly. When it went, the whole Hollow would know.

"We can't hold them," Brannic said. He had come back from the ledge, and he said it plainly, the way he said everything, as if plainness were a kindness. "Two thousand, maybe more. We're four, plus Serah and six wardens who remembered their oaths. We don't hold an army. We buy minutes."

"Minutes are enough," Freya said. "If I reach the inner seals before they break the fourth, I can re-lay them. After the fourth—" He stopped. He had promised himself he would not think past the fourth.

"After the fourth, it costs a keeper everything," Isolde finished quietly. She stood with her satchel over her shoulder, a small round woman gone grey early, with flour still — absurdly, heartbreakingly — under her fingernails from baking for the camp the night before. Her gift of mending was a child's gift; she had said so herself a hundred times. She could close a tear in cloth, knit a cracked bowl whole, stop a small wound from bleeding. Nothing grand. Nothing that sang.

But she looked at the descending torches now and set her jaw the way she did when a loaf refused to rise.

"Then we make sure you never have to pay it," she said. "Go down. Mend your seals. We'll mend the road behind you."

* * *

The first ward broke while Freya was still three galleries above the Gate.

It did not go quietly. The sound was not a sound so much as an absence — a vast intake of breath through the stone, a moment when the Hollow forgot how to echo — and then the pressure came back doubled, trebled, a cold wind blowing out of nowhere that smelled of deep water and old iron. Every torch on the stairs guttered blue. Men screamed, far above, and Freya understood that the wind had touched them too, had shown each of them, for one heartbeat, something from the other side of the seam. He put his scarred palm flat against the gallery wall and poured himself into the remaining eight wards, feeling them like teeth gritted against pain.

Below him, the Gate's black frame had begun to sweat. Beads of moisture crawled along the basalt spirals, and where they gathered they were not water but something darker, something that moved against gravity, climbing toward the broken first seal like blood returning to a wound.

"Not yet," Freya whispered to it, in the language of the old rites, the words worn smooth in his mouth from three years of nightly tending. "Not yet. Sleep. Sleep."

The dark moisture slowed. Held. And Freya knelt on the cold stone with his hand on the second seal and began the long work of binding, and behind him, far up the throat of the world, the battle began.

* * *

He heard it in fragments, the way one hears a storm through a roof.

Brannic had chosen the third gallery for the defense — a narrow span where the switchback doubled back on itself, so that any force coming down had to pass within a spear's length of the wall, and where six determined men could fight in a corridor of their own making. Freya felt the first clash as a shudder through the stone. Then shouting. Then the particular terrible music of steel on steel, multiplied, echoing, until the whole Hollow rang like a struck bell.

Isolde was up there. That was the thought that kept dragging his attention away from the wards, that made his fingers fumble the knotwork of the binding. Isolde, who had never in her life done anything grander than hem a soldier's cloak and set a child's broken arm, standing in a battle corridor with her little gift and her grey hair pinned back, doing what she did.

He saw it once, later — she would tell it plainly, the way she told everything, and he would carry the image the rest of his life. A warden went down beside her with his thigh opened to the bone, and she dropped to her knees in the blood and put both hands into the wound, and *mended*. Not healed — she was always careful about that distinction, even now, even screaming with effort. Mended. As if flesh were cloth and she the finest seamstress in Vess, drawing the torn edges together, closing the gap, holding a man's life shut the way you hold a sack of grain shut with your whole body wrapped around it. The warden lived. She moved to the next one. There was always a next one.

And the men who fought beside her fought differently because of it. Freya heard it afterward in Brannic's flat recitation: they stopped retreating cleanly and started holding, absolutely, because behind them was a small grey woman kneeling in blood who would not leave, and no man runs past a woman like that. They held the third gallery for the better part of an hour. An hour. Against a vanguard of five hundred.

When the line finally bent — when there were simply too many bodies pressing down the stair, when two of the six oath-wardens were dead and a third dying and Brannic bleeding from a gash along his jaw that Isolde had no time to touch — it was Isolde who bought the retreat. She tore the hem from her own skirt, whispered the mending-word over it, and cast it out across the stair like a fisherman casting a net. Cloth became snare. The leading rank of soldiers found their greaves stitched to the stone, seams of thread harder than iron running boot to boot, twenty men suddenly sewn to the floor of the world. They cursed and hacked at it. It bought another ten minutes. It let the living fall back to the fifth gallery, where the corridor narrowed again, where Brannic planted himself in the center of the stair with his shield up and his sword low and made himself the hinge on which the whole night turned.

* * *

Freya finished the second seal as the sun would have been rising, if sun reached this far under the earth. His hands were cramping. Blood had run freely from his nose for some time without his noticing; he wiped it on his sleeve and climbed.

He found Brannic at the fifth gallery, and for a moment he could not move.

The big man stood alone at the narrowest point, feet planted wide, shield strapped and dented, sword trailing red. The bodies of the fallen lay around him in a drift, friend and foe tangled together, and the torchlight caught the wet shine of the stone and the steam of breath and blood. Above him on the stair, packed shoulder to shoulder, the vanguard of Aldous's army waited — hundreds of faces in the smoky light, and among them, Freya knew, men who had served under Brannic for years, who had eaten at his table, who had called him *Captain* before the word *disgraced* got hung around his neck.

"Brannic!" a voice called from the ranks. Young, strained. "Brannic, step aside! The king's pardon — he's offering pardon! You don't have to die for a charm-seller!"

Freya started forward. Brannic's free hand came up and stopped him without the big man even turning his head.

"I died three years ago," Brannic called back, and his voice filled the gallery like a landslide filling a valley. "In the court of King Aldous, when I was asked to put irons on a hedge-witch and her daughter, and I said no. Everything since has been extra. You want to give me my life back?" He rolled his shoulders. The shield came up. "Come and take the extra."

They came.

Freya had seen battles in stories, in the tapestries of the free cities, in the boasts of merchants. None of it was this. This was noise that lived inside the skull. This was Brannic meeting the first rush head-on and *stopping* it — literally stopping it, the press of fifty men checked by one, because a stair is narrow and courage is heavy and the man at the front cannot see a way past the shield. This was the sound of Isolde somewhere behind, chanting the mending-word over and over until it frayed at the edges, stitching wounds closed faster than the line could open them. This was Pip, ghosting along the upper ledges where no soldier thought to look, dropping a floodgate's rusted counterweight onto the stair behind the attackers so that reinforcements arriving found their own road sewn shut — the boy's lock-whisper turned, for once, against doors instead of for them.

And this, worst of all, was Brannic killing men he loved.

Freya watched him do it. Watched him meet the eyes of a young warden named Corren — Freya had learned the names during the weeks of preparation, Brannic insisting that if they might die by these men he should know whom he killed — watched Brannic say, almost gently, "*Go home, lad*" and then, when the boy came on anyway because the man behind him had a whip and the law had a rope, put his sword through him with a precision that was itself a kind of mercy. Watched him do it again. And again. Watched the grief build in the big man's face like water rising behind a dam, and watched him fight on anyway, because that was what Brannic was: a wall that grieves while it stands.

The line broke at midmorning. Not because Brannic fell — he didn't, though later Isolde would count eleven wounds on him, four of them serious — but because the army stopped trying to go through him and started trying to go around him, rappelling down the outer face of the Hollow on ropes, swarming the ledges, pouring into the lower galleries like water finding cracks in a dike. Brannic took one look at the ropes blooming on the cliff walls, spat blood, and roared the retreat.

"Down! Down to the Gate! All of you — GO!"

* * *

They ran. There was no dignity in it. They ran down the last galleries with the army thundering behind them, Isolde hauling a wounded warden over her shoulder with strength that her small frame should not have owned, Pip skimming ahead through gaps in the stone no grown body could have used, calling the turns. Serah Vane and her six oath-wardens formed the rear guard, fighting backward, giving ground foot by foot, and Freya ran past Serah and saw her face and understood that she had known this arithmetic from the beginning: that every minute purchased on the stair was a minute spent, and the purse was emptying.

They reached the antechamber of the Gate as the fourth seal began to die.

This time Freya felt it happen rather than heard it — felt the fourth knot of his own soul loosen, felt something on the far side of the seam turn over in its sleep and begin, slowly, with the patience of things that have waited nine hundred years, to wake. The antechamber grew cold. Breath fogged. The dark moisture on the basalt frame was running now, streaming upward off the Gate in ropes, coiling in the air like eels tasting water.

"How many remain?" Serah shouted, backing into the chamber, sword still working.

"Five," Freya gasped. "I've re-laid two. Five left, and they're breaking the third right now—"

"Then we hold this room," Brannic said. He planted himself at the antechamber's single entrance, and Isolde took her place behind him with her hands already glowing faintly, that soft candle-flame shimmer that was all her gift showed of itself, and Pip vanished into the shadows along the wall with his knives drawn, and Serah and her wardens ranged themselves in a line that had no right to be as steady as it was.

The first soldiers came through the entrance and died there. The second wave came over the bodies. The third wave came over those.

It lasted less than an hour, and it cost more than Freya would ever be able to reckon. Two more of Serah's wardens fell. Isolde collapsed twice from the spending of her gift and rose twice, grey-faced, hands still reaching for the wounded. Brannic's shield finally split down its face and he fought on with the broken half of it, and when a spear took him through the meat of his shoulder he pulled it out and kept swinging, and the soldiers began — Freya saw it, saw it clearly — the soldiers began to hesitate. Because the stair above was choked with their own dead, and the small woman in the corner would not stop mending, and the giant at the door would not stop standing, and somewhere in the smoke a boy's voice was whispering and men's weapons were coming apart in their hands, hilts unscrewing, buckles unsewing, armor unlacing itself like a lie coming undone.

And through all of it Freya knelt at the Gate with his blood on the basalt and his gift pouring out of him like heat from an open forge, laying the fifth seal, the sixth, racing the engineers' chisels and the keeper's rite-book they had stolen, racing the slow waking of the Old Powers, racing the simple arithmetic of his own exhaustion.

He laid the seventh seal as the antechamber's defenders were pushed back to the very walls.

He was reaching for the eighth when the soldiers parted, and the man they had been protecting came down the stair.

King Aldous wore no crown. He wore campaign leathers and a plain sword, and he walked through the carnage of his own vanguard as though it were a garden, and his eyes — pale, patient, terribly calm — went past Brannic, past Isolde, past all of them, and fixed on Freya kneeling at the Gate with his hands full of light.

"There he is," Aldous said softly. "The last keeper. Take him. Kill the rest."

The wave that came then was not a wave Freya's family could break. He felt Brannic go down under it — heard Isolde scream his name — felt hands seize his arms, his hair, wrenching him back from the Gate with the eighth seal half-laid and the ninth untouched, and the last thing he saw before they dragged him into the dark was Pip, pinned beneath a soldier's knee, watching him with an expression Freya knew he would dream about for the rest of his life, whatever length of it remained:

the expression of a boy watching his family be taken apart, and knowing whose fault it was.

The Old Powers, behind the seam, began to laugh.
