## 19. The Choice of Truth

The world above the Hollow was burning.

Freya saw it from the mouth of the stair as they dragged each other upward — the sky over Vess stained orange with signal-fires, the army's camp a scatter of embers across the ridge like spilled coals. Below them, in the dark of the earth, the Gate breathed. He could feel it against his spine even now, even bleeding, even half-carried: that slow tidal pull, the seam in the world widening one hair's width at a time, patient as rot.

Isolde had his arm over her shoulders. Her mending gift worked through him in small warm pulses, knitting torn muscle, closing what could be closed. It could not close everything. Brannic limped on his sword like a walking stick, his left side black with blood that was only half his own. Pip came last, silent for the first time Freya could remember, his eyes fixed on the ground as if it might open and swallow him as a kindness.

They made the ruined shrine at the stair's head — an old waystone chapel, roofless, its saint's face worn to a suggestion of features. Wind moved through it off the ridge, carrying smoke and the distant baying of hounds. Serah Vane stood watch at the broken doorway with her grandmother's rites-book open against her forearm, reading by moonlight, her face lit gray.

"An hour," she said without turning. "Maybe two before they regroup and come down the stair proper. The rites need preparation. Circle, chalk, keeper's blood freely given." She looked back at Freya then, and her eyes were not cold anymore; they were something worse. They were sorry. "You know what *freely given* means. You've known since you were old enough to read."

"I've known," Freya said.

His friends stopped moving. It was very quiet in the ruined chapel, the kind of quiet that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water. Somewhere below them the Gate exhaled, and the wind through the broken roof carried a smell that did not belong to any living season — wet stone and iron and something older than iron, older than names.

"No," Isolde said.

It was not a question or an argument yet. It was simply the word itself, set down between them like a stone she would build the rest of the night upon.

"Isolde—"

"*No.*" She lowered him onto the fallen altar slab more gently than her voice allowed, and then she rounded on Serah with all the fury of a woman who spent her life putting broken things right being told that one thing must stay broken forever. "Explain it. Explain it plainly, because I have been mending this man for two hours and I will not stand here and be lied to with rite-speak."

Serah's jaw tightened. "The seals are woven from a keeper's gift. Not his blood alone — his *gift*, the living root of it. That's why the first kings needed keepers at all. A seal made from anything lesser is a knot; a seal made from a keeper is a root, and roots hold." She turned the page with deliberate care. "To pour a gift into the Hollow permanently — to bind it so nothing can unbind it again, not king's blood, not warden-steel, not centuries — the gift must burn out entirely. Every thread of it. What remains afterward is—" She hesitated.

"Say it," Freya said quietly. "They deserve plain words. You're right about that."

"A husk," Serah said. "A keeper who cannot keep. He would live. He would live a long time, probably. But he would never touch magic again. Not a charm, not a whisper, not a candle-spark. And because the Gate would know his gift's shape until he dies, he would feel it every day of his life — the thing he gave away, sitting just behind a door he can never open." She closed the book. "My grandmother described it once. She said it was like going deaf in a house full of singing."

The silence after that was terrible.

Brannic sat down heavily on a broken pew, his sword across his knees. "There's another way," he said, and it was not quite a question either, but it was closer than Isolde's word had been. He was a warden; wardens believed every wall had a second gate. "Another keeper. Another line. The grandmother you mentioned—"

"Dead thirty years. And her gift went with her, unwoven, into the ground." Serah shook her head once, sharply. "I've read every page she left me. I hoped, when I came down into the Hollow, that there was some other answer hidden in the margins. There isn't. There is one keeper left in the world, and the Gate is opening, and the only permanent seal is the one that costs him everything he is."

"Then we don't do permanent," Isolde said. "We do temporary. We buy time. We find another way—"

"There is no time!" For the first time, Serah's composure cracked, and beneath it Freya glimpsed the girl who had learned sealing rites in secret while wearing a warden's coat. "You felt what came through the seam tonight. That was a *fingertip*. Aldous has keeper's blood on his blade and a rite in his hand, and when he comes back down — and he will come back down, tomorrow, with his whole army if he must — the Gate opens wide enough for things that make his cruelty look like a child's temper. Temporary seals are how we got here. Weak knots. Half-measures. Generations of keepers pouring a little of themselves in and hoping the next one would pour a little more, until the last keeper alive is holding shut a war with what's left of himself." Her voice dropped. "He's been doing that for three years. Did none of you wonder why he looked tired?"

They looked at Freya.

He had expected this moment for days — had rehearsed it in the dark stretches of the road, walking east toward Mereth, walking west again toward Vess. In the rehearsals he had been eloquent. He had spoken calmly, reasonably, the way he spoke to customers about charms: here is the price, here is the value, no refunds. Now, with four faces turned toward him in the moonlight — faces he had chosen, or who had chosen him, which amounted to the same thing — every prepared word died in his throat, and what came out instead was the truth underneath the truth.

"I'm tired," he said. "That's the first thing. I want to say all of this bravely and I can't, because I'm so tired, and I'm frightened, and I don't want to lose it. I love my gift. I have loved it my whole life. It's the hum under my skin, it's the luck I sold in the market, it's the warmth when Isolde mends me and I can feel her working. It's the only part of myself I ever thought was worth anything." His hands were shaking. He let them. "And I would give it up ten thousand times rather than let what's down there out. That's the second thing. That's the whole truth, and I'm sorry it took me this long to say it aloud, because I've known since the Salt Road. Since Mereth. Since the council put your safety on the table and asked me to trade my silence for it. I chose the world over you then, quietly, without asking. I owe you better than quiet."

"You owe us nothing," Brannic started.

"I owe you the truth," Freya said. "All of it. So here is all of it." He made himself look at each of them. "If I seal the Gate tomorrow, I come out the other side unable to work so much as a warming-charm. Ever. And I need you to hear the rest too, because it matters: I am not doing this *for* you. Please understand that. I'm not buying your lives with mine like a merchant striking a bargain. I'm doing it because the alternative is a world where Pip's grandchildren are born into a war the first kings already fought and lost once. Because someone has to pay, and I am the only one holding the coin." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Tomas used to say I was the most stubborn man in Vess. He was right. Don't try to talk me out of it. You'll only waste the hour we have."

"No," Isolde said again, softer now, and that was worse. She knelt in front of him and took his shaking hands in both of hers, and her gift rose in her palms, that small warm child-magic of mending, useless against seals and armies and everything that mattered. "You told us the truth. Here's ours. We refuse."

"You can't refuse—"

"We can," Brannic said. "Watch us." He leaned forward on the pew, and in the moonlight his scarred face was carved from something older than the chapel stones. "I wore the warden gray for nineteen years. I hunted people like you, Freya. People with a hum under their skin. I told myself it was law, it was order, it kept the small cruelties from becoming large ones. Then I watched what order became, and I ran, and I have spent every mile since trying to decide whether a man like me deserves a family at all." His voice roughened. "You gave me one anyway. Without asking. Without conditions. So no — I won't stand in a circle and watch you burn yourself hollow while I guard the door. I'll drag you out of it myself. I'll take the punishment of it gladly."

"The rites require keeper's blood," Serah said quietly. "No blood, no seal. If he refuses—"

"He isn't refusing," Isolde said. "*We* are refusing for him. Find the difference, warden."

And then Pip stepped forward.

He had been silent so long that the sound of his voice startled them all. He stood at the edge of the moonlight with his cap wrung in his hands, twisted into a rope of itself, and his face — that quick, clever, guarded face that had grinned its way through locks and ledgers and lie-filled market squares — was wrecked.

"It should be me," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"Let me say it. Let me get through all of it, because if I stop I won't start again." He swallowed hard. Freya realized the boy was crying and had been for some time, silently, the way street children cry — trained out of noise, trained to spend tears where no one can tax them. "You all gave your truths tonight. Here's mine. The waystation, on the road back. When the vanguard took me." His voice broke and he forced it flat. "They didn't even hurt me. That's the worst part. A sergeant sat me down, offered me hot food, and asked where the Gate was. And I was cold, and hungry, and scared, and I thought — I thought, they'll find it anyway, they'll torture it out of someone else, what difference does one lock whispered open make. I told them. Stairs behind the tanner's row. The Hollow. Everything." He looked up, and his eyes were huge and wet and unbearable. "Aldous's army is down there because of me. Brannic's wounds are because of me. You nearly died on that stair because of me."

"Pip." Freya reached for him.

"Don't." The boy flinched back. "Don't forgive me yet, please, I haven't earned it, and if you forgive me now I'll never be able to carry it right." He drew a shuddering breath. "Here's what I came to say. The sergeant said the Gate needs keeper's blood. Keeper's *gift*. Fine. Fine! But I've been thinking about locks my whole life, yeah? Every lock's just a seal somebody made, and every seal's got a weakness, and I've never met one I couldn't talk open — or talk *shut*. Let me go down there. Let me whisper the Gate sealed. Use my blood, use whatever's in me, use anything. I'm nobody. I'm a thief with dirty fingernails and no family and no name worth keeping. If it costs a gift, take *mine* — I'll give it, freely, gladly, I swear it on every god whose temples I've burgled—"

"You'd die," Serah said. "The rite doesn't work on ungifted blood. It would take your gift, find nothing, and take your life to balance the ledger. That's what *balance* means."

"Then I'll die," Pip said simply, "paying a debt. That's more than I ever thought I'd get to be — a debt worth paying. To *people*." His voice finally cracked all the way through. "I never had a family. I decided on you lot in a burned-out waystation over burnt rabbit and Isolde's terrible tea. First home I ever had. And homes are supposed to cost something, aren't they? Everyone always leaves them or loses them or burns them down. Let me be the one who pays for mine. Let me matter that much, just once."

The wind came through the broken roof and stirred the ash on the floor, and far below them the Gate sighed against its weakening bonds, and Freya felt the hum of his own gift rise in answer like a tide answering the moon — his whole life's warmth gathering, ready, waiting to be spent.

"No," he said.

Pip's face crumpled. "You don't get to—"

"No," Freya said again, gentler, and rose from the altar slab despite Isolde's hands, despite his wounds, and crossed to the boy, and took his dirty face in both palms the way Tomas used to take loaves out of the oven — careful, like something that had risen against all odds and deserved to survive. "Listen to me, Pip of nowhere, thief of Vess, whisperer of locks. Your betrayal bought an army an hour. Your courage is going to help me take it back. Both things are true, and I forgive the first because I've seen the second — I've seen you pick locks for strangers' children and steal bread for people hungrier than you and give away half of everything you ever thieved. Fear made you small for one bad hour. Love has made you enormous for months. I know which one you are."

"But—"

"But you're right about one thing." Freya looked past him, at Serah, at the rites-book, at the dark stair descending into the breathing earth. "Nobody carries this alone. Not me, not you. So here is my truth, the last piece of it, and then we choose together or not at all." He drew a breath that tasted of smoke and iron. "I will seal the Gate. My gift goes into the Hollow, and I come out the other side a husk, and I have made my peace with it. But I'm not walking down those stairs to spend it in secret, and I'm not letting any of you throw yourselves into the fire in my place, and if anyone tries — Pip, Brannic, I mean it — I will haunt you with the most irritating charms a husk can manage, which admittedly won't be many, but I'll be *creative*."

Someone laughed — wet, ragged, Isolde, still kneeling, wiping her face with her sleeve.

"What I need," Freya said, "is for you to walk down with me. All of you. Serah's rites, Isolde's mending to hold my body together while the gift pours out, Brannic's steel on the stair, and Pip—" he squeezed the boy's cheeks once, then let go, "—Pip's whispers, because Aldous's ward-stone is the finest lock he'll ever meet, and I want the best thief in Vess working it while I work the seals. Nobody dies in my place. Everybody stands beside me. That's the only bargain I'll strike."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Brannic rose from the pew, groaning, and picked up his sword, and the sound of it clearing the scabbard was the oldest sound in the world. Isolde stood and began unpacking her herbs with brisk, furious hands, daring anyone to call it consent. Serah opened the rites-book to its final page.

Pip wiped his nose with his wrist. "Beside you," he said hoarsely. "Fine. Beside you. But when this is done, when you're a husk — you're still coming to supper. Midwinter. Every year. That's the price *you* pay me. Swear it."

"I swear it," Freya said, and meant it with everything in him, everything that would soon be gone.

Then Serah lifted her head from the book, and the blood drained slowly out of her face.

"Freya," she said. "The seals just changed. Something's touching them from the outside — something with keeper's knowledge." Her voice went thin. "Aldous isn't waiting for dawn. He's opening the Gate *now*."

Below them, in the deep of the Hollow, the ancient dark began — softly, terribly — to sing.
