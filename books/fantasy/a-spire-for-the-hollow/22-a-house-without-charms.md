## 22. A House Without Charms

The ovens woke before the sun did, as they always did, and Freya rose with them.

He had learned the bakery's moods the way he had once learned the Gate's: by listening. The great brick mouths in the back room breathed heat through the dark, and the smell of the first loaves — Tomas's dark rye, sour and honest — came up through the floorboards of his room above like a hand on his shoulder, saying *up, up, the day is starting without you.* There was no hum beneath it. No whisper of power coiled in the yeast and the flour and the fire, no charm to be whispered over the proofing dough to make it rise twice as high. Just bread. Just work. Just the ordinary miracle of grain and water becoming something that fed people.

He had expected the silence to be a wound. It had been, for a while — those first weeks in Mereth when he'd reach for the small bright thread that had lived under his skin since childhood and find nothing there, like a man groping for a missing finger in the dark. He had grieved it the way you grieve anything buried: badly at first, then honestly, then gently.

Now, on the cusp of midwinter, one year and some spare months after the Hollow went quiet forever, he tied his apron in the grey light and found he was simply, plainly, content.

"You're late," Tomas called from the kitchen, though Freya was early. It was their joke. It had been their joke for eleven months.

"I'm punctual," Freya said, tying the apron. "You're greedy."

Tomas stood at the long bench with flour to his elbows, a broad man gone broader from good living, his forearms still roped with the strength of a Vess dockside baker who had once carried hundredweight sacks up four flights of stairs before dawn. There were new lines carved around his eyes — the warden cells had put them there, and no year of peace would sand them out entirely — but when he smiled at Freya across the bench, the lines arranged themselves into something like forgiveness made habit.

"The Hollow Street crowd will be here before the bell," Tomas said. "Cold snap coming. I feel it in my knees, and cold snaps mean they want the heavy loaves."

"Then we'll give them the heavy loaves." Freya took up his own station — the sweet doughs, the spiced buns studded with dried plum and candied peel that Mereth's midwinter market demanded, the honey-twists he had learned to braid with nothing but his two hands and a patience he had never needed before. His first attempts had been disasters, lopsided and weeping butter. Now his braids came out even. It was, he suspected, the only kind of magic he had ever genuinely possessed: the slow kind, the kind you earned.

Outside the frosted windows, Mereth was waking. Lamplighters moved down the street with their long poles, snuffing the night's flames one by one. A fish-cart rattled past toward the harbor, gulls wheeling after it like a torn banner. Somewhere beyond the terracotta roofs, the sea said what it always said, patient and enormous.

Freya slid the first tray into the oven and let the heat wash over his face, and thought: *a year ago today, I was underground.*

Not precisely today. But near enough. Near enough that his body kept its own calendar, the way bodies do, and every midwinter he woke with his heart going quick and thin in his chest, listening for a sound that was no longer anywhere in the world.

* * *

They came at dusk, as promised, and the little bakery on Hollow Street filled up the way a hearth fills with fire.

Pip arrived first, because Pip always arrived first and claimed it was coincidence. He came in through the door like weather, all elbows and noise, twenty years old now and taller than any of them had believed possible — "you were malnourished," Isolde said whenever he mentioned it, and he said it anyway — with a thief's quick grin sitting oddly on the face of a young man who now held an honest commission with the Mereth harbor watch. He had turned out to have a gift for finding things out, which was thievery wearing a uniform, and everyone pretended not to notice how often the watch's cases closed.

"Brought wine," Pip announced, slapping a dusty bottle on the counter. "Stole it — bought it. Bought it."

"That's what the last one said too," Freya said.

"The last one *was* stolen. This one's different." Pip leaned over the counter and lowered his voice to a stage whisper. "Serah's coming. Actual Serah. Warden-Commander Serah. I saw her seal on the message she sent the council. She's got her own wax now, Freya. Her own *seal*. We know someone with a seal."

"We've known her for a year."

"And every day she gets more official. It's beautiful. Like watching a knife get sharpened into a *document*."

Brannic came in second, ducking under the lintel out of old habit though he fit now; the doorway had not shrunk, only his expectation of doorways. He was greyer at the temples, and he walked with the faint favoring of the left knee that the stair below the Hollow had given him and would keep until he died — the stair where he had stood alone against thirty men of his former order and, impossibly, been relieved of duty rather than of life. He carried a wrapped parcel under one arm and set it down with the exaggerated care of a man pretending he had not spent three days worrying about a gift.

"For the table," he said gruffly. "From the north country. Don't ask what it cost."

"He'll tell us anyway," Pip said.

"It cost," Brannic began, and Isolde came through the door laughing, and whatever the number was, it drowned.

She had little Wren with her, bundled in blue wool, five years old now and solemn as a small owl, whose gift of mending — inherited, everyone agreed, from a mother who could make a torn sleeve whole with a touch — had recently been applied to a cracked teacup, a broken sparrow's wing, and, once, memorably, a shattered window that Isolde had then had a long conversation about. The gifted children of Mereth went, these days, to a schoolhouse with a garden behind it, where Serah's reformed wardens stood guard not with chains but with chalkboards, and where the curriculum included, prominently, *how to be careful.* Wren waved at Freya with the grave courtesy of the very young and went immediately to inspect the honey-twists.

Isolde herself looked tired in the way of women who ran schoolhouses and healed things and refused to stop doing either, and beautiful in the way of people whose faces had settled at last into their truth. She kissed Freya's cheek — flour and all — and held his shoulders and looked at him the look she always gave him at midwinter, the one that checked him over the way she checked a mended bone, asking silently: *whole? Whole.*

"Whole," he said.

"Mm," she said, unconvinced, and let go.

Serah arrived last, as commanders do, stamping snow from her boots. Out of uniform she was just a tall, hard-faced woman with wind-burned cheeks and a sword she checked at the door now, a habit nearly a year old and still deliberate every time. The reform of the wardens had cost her more than anyone knew except Brannic, who did know and never said — friends lost, an order gutted and rebuilt from shame upward, letters written to families of the hunted that no answer ever softened. But she smiled now, occasionally, and the smile had stopped looking borrowed.

"Something smells criminal," she said.

"Tomas's rye," said Freya.

"I'll want the recipe. As evidence."

They crowded the big table Tomas had built himself — he was better with wood than he admitted — and the supper went the way their suppers went: too much food, overlapping arguments, Pip and Wren conducting a coordinated campaign for the last honey-twist that ended in a negotiated settlement brokered by Isolde, and Brannic telling the story of the river ford again with slightly different details each time, which no one corrected anymore because the corrections were part of the ritual.

Tomas sat at the head and mostly listened, as he mostly listened, and laughed at the right moments in his deep quiet way, and once — when Pip described the look on a lock's face, as if locks had faces, when you whispered to them — Tomas caught Freya's eye across the table, and something passed between them that needed no passing. *You left me in a cell,* that look said, and *I know, and I'm sorry,* and *I know that too, and here we are anyway, with bread.* Some debts are not forgiven so much as converted — into flour, into shared mornings, into a life built side by side on top of the scar. It was the best either of them could do, and it had turned out, against all odds, to be enough.

Afterward, when Wren had gone sleepy-heavy against Isolde's shoulder and the wine bottle stood empty and Pip was arguing geography with Serah purely to stay awake, Freya stood at the window with his cup and watched the midwinter dark come down over Mereth, and felt the old pull in his chest.

Not magic. That was gone, truly gone; he had checked the way an amputee checks, out of hope and habit, and found only smooth quiet. What pulled was simpler. An anniversary. A door he wanted to look at one last time.

"I'll be back before the second bell," he said, and got his coat.

* * *

The Hollow beneath Vess had been sealed with masonry and law both — Aldous's death had collapsed the usurper's claim along with his ambitions, and the new council of Vess, such as it was, had bricked the descent at Serah's recommendation and posted a standing prohibition enforced by actual wardens, reformed ones, who took the duty seriously. Getting down required papers. Freya had papers. Serah had made sure of it years ago, sliding the writ across a tavern table without meeting his eyes, saying only, "In case you ever need to say goodbye properly."

The writ was soft at the folds now. He had never used it until tonight.

Vess in midwinter smelled as it always had — coal smoke and river damp and roasting chestnuts, the bells of the lower city counting out the hour in their cracked bronze voices. He walked the streets of his old life like a ghost taking inventory: the shuttered stall where he had sold his charms; the corner where the proclamation boards had once sprouted edicts like poisonous flowers; the bakery that had been Tomas's, sold long since to a widow who made passable rolls and nothing worth writing home about. People passed him and did not know him. Three years of hiding had taught him to be unremarkable; it seemed the skill survived its necessity.

The sealed entrance to the Hollow lay behind a plain iron door in a cellar street that no one visited, and the writ opened it, and the wardens on duty — young, bored, respectful of documents — waved him down the stairs without a second glance.

He had expected the descent to hurt.

It did not. That was the strange thing. He counted the steps out of memory — ninety-two to the first landing, forty more to the antechamber — and his body remembered each one the way it remembered bread-making, hands knowing shapes the mind had released. The air grew cold and mineral, smelling of deep stone and old water, and the lamplight of the wardens' station fell away behind him until his little lantern swung alone in the dark, and still there was nothing. No pressure. No presence. No vast sleeping attention turning toward him beneath the world like an eye beneath an eyelid.

He reached the last landing and stood before the wall.

They had built it well: dressed stone and iron and lead, mortar blessed by whatever priests would bless it, and across its face, worked into the pattern of the blocks like a signature hidden in a letter, the seals — dead now, inert, mere carvings. Freya raised his lantern. The old lines he had poured himself into, burning out everything he was in a single white instant while Aldous screamed and the Powers recoiled and Pip's whisper ate the usurper's ward-stone like rot through cloth — those lines were only lines. Scratches in rock. The door they marked led nowhere anymore. Whatever waited beyond had been shut away so thoroughly that even the waiting had ceased.

He pressed his palm flat against the stone.

Nothing.

No hum. No cold fire. No voice like the first kings' voices, no hunger pressing at the seam of the world. Just winter stone, cold as any cellar, dumb as any wall. The most dangerous door in creation, and it had become exactly what it appeared to be: a wall.

Freya stood there a long time, longer than he meant to, listening to his own breath and the drip of far water. He searched himself, one final inventory, the annual audit: *anything? Anything at all?*

And found — gladness.

Not relief, even. Relief implied he had been dreading the alternative. This was quieter than relief, deeper down, where the year's ordinary days had piled up like sediment over the wound: mornings of flour and Tomas's terrible humming; Pip turning up every Sunday with allegedly purchased wine; Isolde's schoolhouse garden going riotous each summer; Brannic teaching the neighborhood boys staff-drill in the yard and pretending not to enjoy it; Serah's letters, short and stiff and secretly funny. A life. Not a disguise worn over a double existence, not a fragile charm-shop fronting an abyss — a real one, load-bearing, his.

He had traded the gift for this, and the trade, he understood at last, pressing his hand to the silent stone, had been no sacrifice at all. You cannot lose what you were and be sorry, if what you become is loved.

"Goodbye," he said aloud, and his voice didn't even echo.

He took the lantern and climbed the ninety-two steps and the forty, and did not look back, and found he didn't need to.

* * *

The bakery was dark when he returned, the others gone home with leftovers and promises for the new year — Serah back to Vess and the reformed order, Brannic walking Isolde and Wren home through the snow, Pip vanishing with a wave and, Freya was almost certain, a honey-twist he had not been allotted. Only a lamp still burned in the kitchen, and the smell of tomorrow's starter, and Tomas in his chair by the banked oven with a mug cooling forgotten in his hand, half-asleep the way he always waited up, though he'd deny it.

"You were gone long," Tomas said, without opening his eyes.

"I went to say goodbye."

"To the Gate?"

"To all of it."

Tomas nodded slowly, the way he nodded at dough that had proved properly — acknowledgment without ceremony. Then he hauled himself up, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and took down two mugs instead of one, and poured the spiced cider he kept hidden for occasions he pretended not to mark.

"Midwinter," he said, handing Freya a mug. "Another one."

"Another one," Freya agreed.

They drank standing up in the warm dark kitchen, the two of them, baker and keeper — no, just baker and baker now, and friend, and family of the strangest and best manufacture — while outside the snow came down soft and thick over Mereth, over the free cities, over the whole sleeping world with its walls and its wars and its sealed doors, none of which, tonight, were Freya's to carry.

Tomorrow there would be bread. Tomorrow the bell would ring and Hollow Street would come wanting the heavy loaves, and he and Tomas would give them, and Pip would arrive too early claiming coincidence, and the year would begin the way the good years did: with work, and warmth, and people coming through the door.

Freya finished his cider, banked the oven, and slept without dreaming — or rather, dreaming only of morning.
