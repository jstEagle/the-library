## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Meridian Guild kept its accounts by candlelight, because lamplight was honest and honesty had no place in the trade of impossible things.

Sebastian Reyes had learned this in their first week, when old Master Quillworth—dead now these six years, and still owed a debt of one remembered birthday—had tapped the wax stub on Sebastian's desk and said, "Candles flicker, boy. Flickering makes everything look like it might be worth more than it is. That's the whole business."

They were not a boy. They had corrected him once, gently, and he had looked at them over his spectacles for a long moment and said, "No. I suppose you aren't." And that had been the end of it, which was why Sebastian had loved him, in the quiet way one loves people who ask no questions.

Six years now. Six years of candles and columns and the particular, blessed tedium of other people's miracles.

Sebastian sat alone in the Counting Room, third floor, west wing, with a pot of cold tea going bitter at their elbow and the autumn ledger open before them. Outside, Vellamarch lay under a soft grey drizzle, its ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke into the dark, its harbor bells ringing the half-hour with the sleepy indifference of a city that did not know how lucky it was to be ordinary.

The entry under Sebastian's pen read:

*Received into holding: one (1) bottle, thunder, contained. Seller: the Widow Anselm of Cooper's Row. Terms: she retains visiting rights. Note: the thunder is homesick.*

That last line was not guild standard phrasing. Sebastian had added it themselves, because it was true. The bottle sat three shelves away in the vault below, and twice a week it rattled against its velvet cradle like a dog hearing a familiar footstep. The Widow Anselm came on Thursdays, put her palm flat against the glass, and said, "There, now," until it settled. The Guild charged her nothing for the visits. Sebastian had seen to that, quietly, by entering them as *maintenance costs*. Some debts you carried so others didn't have to.

They dipped the pen. Ink, blot, steady hand. This was the whole art of their life now: small motions, small sums, small hours. A person who wanted to disappear learned that disappearing was not one grand vanishing but ten thousand tiny refusals—don't flinch at thunder, don't look up at lightning, don't ever, ever let your hands itch toward the sky.

Sebastian's hands itched toward the sky most evenings. Tonight they were busy. That helped.

* * *

At half past eleven, the duty bell rang once from the receiving floor, which meant a walk-in client, which meant trouble, because respectable clients made appointments and unrespectable ones rang bells after midnight.

The client turned out to be a young man soaked to the ribs, clutching a hatbox, dripping a trail across the marble that the night porter would grumble about until dawn. He set the hatbox on the receiving counter with the reverence of a man laying down an infant.

"I want to borrow a shadow," he said.

The porter, a vast woman named Brakka with forearms like dock rope, looked at Sebastian. Sebastian was the junior ledger-keeper on nights; this fell to them.

"Yours has run off?" Sebastian asked.

"Worse." The young man's jaw worked. "It's been *lent out already*. To a man in Silverlane. I signed something. I don't remember signing something, but there's my mark on the paper, and now I cast nothing, and my sweetheart says a man with no shadow can't be trusted, and she's right, isn't she? Who'd trust a man with no shadow?"

"Most of our clients," Sebastian said, "but I take your point."

The paperwork took forty minutes, because borrowing back a borrowed shadow required tracing the original loan, and the original loan had been made by a broker named Fenwick who wrote his entries in cipher and, judging by the margins, in brandy. Sebastian untangled it, assessed the transfer fee, noted the interest accruing—one hour of the young man's own memory per fortnight late, standard terms—and watched him leave into the rain casting a shadow slightly too long and slightly too tall, as secondhand shadows always were.

Back upstairs, they entered it all:

*Shadow, one (1), reclaimed from broker Fenwick via subloan. Condition: good, though it flinches at doorways. Client advised: shadows remember where they've been. Do not be alarmed if it points home.*

And beneath that, because the night was long and the tea was cold and no one would ever read the marginalia of a junior keeper:

*Note to self: he wept when it reattached. Noted without judgment. Weeping at the return of one's shadow seems entirely reasonable.*

* * *

The last entry of the night arrived at two in the morning, and it arrived wrong.

It came up by the dumbwaiter from the vault, as sealed deposits did—a leather folio tied with guild cord, tagged for cataloguing—and Sebastian untied it expecting the usual: a deed to a dream, a lease on someone else's luck, a promissory note payable in first kisses or final breaths. The Meridian Guild traded in everything the world priced too strangely for normal markets. Sebastian had catalogued a grandmother's recipe that could make enemies into friends, a river's promise to flood on schedule, a lullaby with a lien against it.

The folio held a single page, old paper, older ink, and Sebastian knew before reading a word of it that it did not belong in the autumn ledger, or any ledger, or any century they understood.

The script was formal, archaic, written by a hand that had never hurried in its life:

*Let it be entered and bound: the covenant stands between the realm of flesh and the realm of stars, renewed each hundredth year upon the Spire of Vows, spoken by a voice that carries the storm. So long as the oath holds, the border holds. So long as the border holds, the night sky keeps its lights and the world keeps its sleep.*

Below the terms, pressed flat into the paper as if stamped there, was a sigil: a slender tower rising into a circle of seven points, like a spire wearing a crown of stars.

Sebastian's hand was on the page. That was the mistake. Cataloguers touched everything; it was habit, it was care, it was how you felt for watermarks and forgery.

The moment their fingertips crossed the sigil, the storm woke.

It happened rarely enough that Sebastian could almost pretend it didn't happen at all—a tightness behind the sternum, a taste like copper rain, the fine hairs of their arms lifting as if the air itself had stood to attention. Once, in their other life, this feeling had meant power beyond reckoning. It had meant clouds gathering at a gesture, thunder answering like a hound called home. Now it meant only danger: a flicker of the old gift, leaking through the seams of a life built to contain it.

Every candle in the Counting Room guttered at once.

Sebastian snatched their hand back. Breathed. Counted—the way they'd taught themselves, all those years ago, in a border town that burned: *one* for the inhale, *two* for the hold, *three* for the letting go. The candles steadied. The pressure behind their ribs subsided to its usual dull ache, the ache that lived in them always, like a splinter of weather.

Outside the window, the drizzle went on falling, innocent and grey. Somewhere across the roofs, a cat knocked over a flowerpot and blamed the wind. The world was fine. The world was always fine, so long as Sebastian Reyes touched nothing, called nothing, was no one.

They looked down at the page again, and the sigil looked back, patient as a hook.

*A voice that carries the storm.* There were perhaps four such voices left in the world, and Sebastian had spent six years ensuring that no record connected any of them to the name in the Meridian Guild's employment rolls. So why was the covenant's founding page—in a hand centuries dead—sitting in the vault of a trades-guild in Vellamarch, tagged for cataloguing like a jar of pickled woe?

And why did the tag bear Master Corvane's personal seal?

Sebastian told themselves the sensible things. Old documents circulated. Collectors died and their holdings scattered. Corvane bought strange paper the way other men bought wine. There was no reason—no reason at all—for the cold spreading through their middle, the animal certainty that the ground beneath their carefully hidden life had just shifted by a finger's width, the way ground shifts before it decides to become a landslide.

They did what they always did with things that frightened them. They filed it.

The covenant page went into the restricted drawer, the one locked with the key only senior keepers were supposed to have, the key Sebastian had copied in their second year because a person who intended to survive needed access to information the way other people needed bread. They wrote the catalogue entry in their neatest, dullest hand:

*Document, one (1), antique. Contents: historical. Sigil: architectural. No valuation pending. Recommend storage, dry, undisturbed.*

*Undisturbed*, they thought. *Yes. Let us both agree to be undisturbed.*

Then they blew out the candles, because the sky beyond the window was going pale at the edges, and dawn meant the day shift, and the day shift meant faces, and faces meant being a person again instead of a ledger. Sebastian gathered their coat, their cold tea, their six years of practiced smallness, and went down the stairs past the vault where a bottled thunder rattled softly in its sleep, homesick for a sky it would never see again.

They did not look at the restricted drawer on the way out.

They thought about it the whole walk home through the waking streets—past the bakers lighting ovens, past the fishwives arguing about eels, past a child stomping puddles with the pure destructive joy of the very young. They thought about it while they barred their narrow door and lay down in their narrow bed in their narrow room above a chandler's shop, where the smell of tallow masked any scent of ozone their skin might give off in the damp.

They thought about it while the rain thickened against the window, harder than the forecast promised, harder than the season allowed, drumming like fingers, like impatience, like something on the far side of the sky tapping to be let in.

And they were still thinking about it—still telling themselves *filed, finished, forgotten*—when every bell in Vellamarch began to ring at once, and the light coming under the door went suddenly, utterly wrong, and Sebastian sat up in the dark knowing, with the certainty of the storm in their bones, that the debt had come due.
