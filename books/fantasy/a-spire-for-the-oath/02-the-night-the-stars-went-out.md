## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with cataloguing miracles, Sebastian had learned, was that they all arrived at once.

The Meridian Guild of Impossible Trades kept its ledgers the way other establishments kept their wine: in cellars, in the dark, and never opened by just anyone. But someone had to catalogue them, and after eleven years of being nobody in particular, Sebastian Reyes had become that someone. The quiet clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a cough that excused them from every social obligation. The ledger-keeper who knew where everything was and pretended to know nothing else.

Tonight's haul sat stacked on their desk like a small, dangerous city. A bottle of thunder, third-hand, still muttering to itself. A shadow on consignment from a woman in the Low Quarter who swore she'd only lend it out until spring. And a promise — an actual promise, folded into a contract no larger than a playing card — bearing interest that compounded nightly and had already grown teeth.

Sebastian dipped the pen. *Item forty-one: one (1) bottled thunderstorm, provenance disputed, contents agitated.*

The thunder bottle muttered louder, as if it objected to "agitated."

"Hush," Sebastian told it. "You're lucky anyone wants you at all."

It was, by any measure, a good night. The guild hall above was emptying, the last brokers drifting home through streets that smelled of roasting chestnuts and river damp. Corvane had gone up to his tower hours ago. The lamps were warm. The work was finite. And for eleven years this had been enough — this small, careful life, built like a wall of dry stones, each one chosen so it wouldn't rattle against another.

Sebastian reached for the next item and stopped.

The ledger page bore a sigil pressed into the margin in old brown wax: a spire, drawn as a single unbroken line that climbed and coiled back into itself, the way smoke does when it changes its mind.

They knew that line. They knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — not by looking, but because it never stops.

The pen trembled. Somewhere beneath Sebastian's skin, under eleven years of practiced dullness, something flickered. The lamp flame leaned toward them. So did the ink in the well, rising half an inch off its surface like a curious animal lifting its head.

*No.* Sebastian set both hands flat on the desk. Breathe. Count the entries. Forty-one, forty-two. You are a clerk. Clerks do not call lightning down on their own furniture.

The ink settled. The flame straightened, embarrassed. Sebastian pressed their thumb over the sigil until the wax's chill faded, then wrote the entry in their flattest hand: *Item forty-three: one (1) seal, archaic, purpose unknown. Recommend storage below.*

Recommend storage below. As if it were a jar of pickled newts.

But their heart was going like a drummer who'd been paid extra, and when they finally blew out the lamp and climbed the stairs to their narrow room, they lay awake listening to the city settle around them, telling themselves the same lie they'd told it for eleven years:

*No one is coming. Nothing is calling. You are safe.*

Outside, the sky was clear. Every star accounted for.

For about an hour.

* * *

Sebastian woke to silence — the wrong kind.

Cities make noise even asleep. Carts, cats, drunks singing to doors that won't open them. This was different: a hush like the whole world holding its breath before a sneeze. Sebastian sat up, and the hair on their arms rose, and the water in the basin began to turn slowly, gently, in circles it had no business circling.

Then the wards went.

There was no sound, exactly. There was a feeling — a vast, distant *twang*, like the largest string in the world being plucked somewhere past the horizon — and every ward-stone in Vantrell rang in answer, a thousand small bells tolling one note too high for ears but not for teeth. Sebastian felt it in their fillings. In their bones. In the place behind their sternum where a storm used to live.

They got to the window in time to see the stars go out.

Not fade. Not cloud over. *Go out*, all of them, within the same hour, in a darkness that rolled across the sky like a shutter being pulled down over the world — north first, then east, then overhead, each star snuffed with a little lurch of finality, as though something very patient were blowing them out one by one and enjoying itself.

The moon lasted longest. It dimmed to a coin, then a ghost of a coin, then nothing at all.

Sebastian stood at the window with their knuckles white on the sill and thought, with terrible clarity: *This is a debt.*

Not a disaster. Disasters were accidents; they scattered, they didn't proceed. This moved in order. This had *sequence*. And Sebastian had spent eleven years among people who traded in obligations, and they knew what a called debt looked like from the outside: the polite notice first, then the visit, then the taking. Something had sent notice to the whole sky.

Below, the city woke screaming.

* * *

By the time Sebastian reached the guild hall, it was pandemonium conducted by lamplight — real lamplight, oil and wick, suddenly worth more than anything in the vaults. Brokers stumbled down stairs half-dressed. Someone's bottled shadow had escaped its jar and was cowering under the great staircase, which told Sebastian everything: even shadows, who lived in the dark professionally, wanted none of this dark.

Corvane stood on the central dais, and Master Aldous Corvane did not look frightened, because Corvane had spent sixty years learning to look like a man reading favorable terms. But his hands were wrong. His hands were doing arithmetic.

"Seal the ledgers," he said. "All of them. Iron boxes, wax, the works. No trade leaves or enters these doors until I say otherwise."

"Master." Pellam, the senior broker, gray-faced. "The wards are down. The border wards. All of them. The Watch is saying—"

"The Watch can say what it likes. Seal. The. Ledgers."

"And the clients?" someone asked. "Half the city will come here tonight thinking we can buy them safety—"

"They'll be right," Corvane said, "and we will charge accordingly once the panic settles. Fear is a seller's market, gentlemen. It always has been."

A few brokers actually nodded. Sebastian watched them do it and felt something cold move down their spine that had nothing to do with the missing stars.

Because here was the thing about Corvane: he'd been master for forty years, through famine and plague and three changes of government, and the guild had never once come close to failing. Never. Other institutions had burned, drowned, been eaten by their own debts. The Meridian endured like bedrock.

*Like bedrock*, Sebastian thought, remembering a sigil pressed in brown wax, *like a foundation stone.*

And then, because the thought arrived fully formed and awful, they looked down.

The guild's foundation stone sat at the base of the great staircase, worn smooth by two centuries of feet, and everyone knew it was plain granite, unmarked, humble. Everyone was wrong. With the wards shattered — with whatever veil had always lain between seeing and knowing torn wide open — Sebastian could see what was cut into it, deep and old and filled with something that glowed like banked fire:

A spire, drawn as a single unbroken line that climbed and coiled back into itself.

The sigil of the Spire of Vows. The sigil from the sealed ledger. Burning now, pulsing now, like a wound that had finally been allowed to bleed.

"Oh," Sebastian said quietly, to no one. "Oh, you *fools*. What did you sell?"

The stone flared. Across the hall, Corvane's eyes found Sebastian's — not the stone, Sebastian noted, *Sebastian*, as if he'd heard the words, or as if he'd known, all along, exactly who would be the one to see it.

His face did something complicated and quick and then became a mask again.

"Reyes," he said pleasantly. "You're needed in the archive. Bring the sealing wax."

The archive. Where the oldest ledgers slept. Sebastian's stomach dropped through eleven floors of careful, hidden living.

"Of course, Master," they said.

* * *

They passed through the anteroom where the guild's enforcers were barring the doors, and beyond the iron-bound oak Sebastian could hear the city: bells, screams, the particular wail of a crowd discovering that prayer has no interest rate and no payout. A woman was shouting that her daughter had been outside when the stars went. A man kept repeating a number — his savings, maybe, or his age, or the number of times he'd begged the sky.

The enforcers' captain caught Sebastian's sleeve as they passed. "Clerk. You seen anything in the books about this? Anything old?"

"I keep the small debts," Sebastian said. "Bottled weather. Borrowed luck. Nothing that could put out the sky."

Which was true, and also a lie, because item forty-three was burning a hole in their memory like a coal sewn into a coat.

In the stairwell down toward the archive, alone at last, Sebastian stopped and let themselves feel it — really feel it — for three full seconds. Dread, yes. But underneath the dread, worse: recognition. That lurching sequence of lights going out. The wards ringing like called notes. A debt being collected in strict order of priority.

Eleven years ago, Sebastian Reyes had fled the capital with their name struck from the rolls and their gift bound in oaths, exiled for storm-calling in a realm that had outlawed it — or so they'd always believed, so the sentence had said. Simple. Legal. Final.

But sentences didn't ring the border wards like a struck bell. Sentences didn't snuff stars in order.

Something had recognized them tonight. Or something had recognized *what they'd been spared from being part of* — and had come to collect the difference.

The lamp in Sebastian's hand shook. Below them, at the bottom of the stairs, the archive door waited, and behind it, in iron boxes and dead languages, lay every secret the guild had ever bought.

Including, Sebastian would have bet the bottled thunder, the one Corvane had sold forty years ago to buy this roof over all their heads.

"You should have stored me below," Sebastian murmured to the absent sigil, and started down.
