## 3. A Visitor from the Warden's Watch

The wards had been Ilse Vane's responsibility for eleven years, and in all that time they had never once required her to climb the Meridian Guild's steps before dawn. The guild kept hours like a cat: technically available, spiritually absent. Its doors opened when they pleased, and its ledger-keepers looked through her as though she were weather.

Today she knocked anyway, because the sky was wrong and someone had to say so out loud.

"Wardens' Watch," she announced to the brass grille. "Regarding the border wards. Which no longer exist."

A slot slid open. A single eye regarded her with the weariness of a man who had been awake all night answering questions he did not enjoy.

"The guild is closed," the eye said.

"The sky is closed," Ilse said. "I'll wait."

She did not have to wait long. Perhaps it was the word *wards* — she had learned over the years that merchants feared many things, but nothing so much as a contract going bad — or perhaps someone inside had finally looked up. The doors opened onto a hall lit by storm-lamps burning low, and the smell of ink and panic. Clerks moved in clusters, whispering over sealed boxes. Nobody met her eyes, which was normal. The speed with which they didn't was new.

"Master Corvane will see you," said a young clerk, and led her through corridors lined with shelves of bottled thunder — she could hear them murmuring to themselves, faint and restless, like the sea kept in jars — to a reading room where two chairs waited beside a table, and a third person stood at the window with their back turned.

Ilse knew that back. She had memorized it once, in a life with better weather.

"Ledger-keeper Reyes," the clerk said, and fled.

Sebastian Reyes turned around slowly, the way people turn when they are hoping the moment will be postponed indefinitely. They looked — older, of course; everyone did; eleven years did not negotiate — but mostly they looked *careful*, which was the Sebastian she remembered. Careful hands, careful face, a careful half-smile assembled from spare parts.

"Warden Vane," they said. "You've come about the wards."

"I've come about the wards."

"Terrible business."

"Sit down, Sebastian."

They sat. That was the first strange thing: eleven years ago, Sebastian Reyes had never once done what they were told on the first request. It had been, she suspected now, a matter of principle, or possibly spite, or possibly love — she had never been able to separate the three.

She set the ward-log on the table between them. It was an old book, its spine cracked, its corners soft with handling. She had pulled it from the archive at first light because something in last night's collapse had bothered her — a pattern, a signature, the way a forged signature bothers a banker who has seen ten thousand honest ones.

"Do you know what this is?"

"It appears to be a log."

"It's the maintenance record for the western border wards. Forty years of entries. Every inspection, every renewal, every repair." She opened it to a ribbon she'd placed there. "And this is the entry from the night the covenant was last renewed. Read the inspector's name."

Sebastian read it. She watched them read it, and watched the color leave their face in the orderly, disciplined way that only people who have practiced losing composure can manage.

"That's my name," they said quietly.

"It is. Dated thirty-one years before you were born."

* * *

The room held its breath. Somewhere down the corridor, a clerk dropped something ceramic and did not swear, which told Ilse everything about the mood of the building.

"There must be a mistake," Sebastian said. "Or a relative. Or—"

"Or," Ilse said, "the name in this log belongs to whoever renewed the covenant forty years ago. A storm-caller. Someone whose gift was written into the ward-stones so deeply that when I inspected those stones last month, your name came off on my glove like fresh ink." She leaned forward. "Names don't do that, Sebastian. Names don't do that unless the person is still *bound*. And last night every ward you're bound to shattered at once, and every star in the sky went out within the hour, and I have spent my whole career being paid to notice when two things happen in the same night."

Sebastian's hands were very still on the table. She remembered those hands doing impossible things — calling lightning out of a clear noon sky once, laughing, showing off — and she made herself stop remembering, because remembering was a luxury with a terrible interest rate.

"You checked the stones yourself," they said. "In the west."

"I inspect them twice a year. It's my post. It has always been my post." She heard the edge come into her own voice and did not sand it down. Somebody had given her that post, eleven years ago, with a smile and a handshake, and she had only begun to wonder, recently, whether the posting had been a kindness or a cage. "You've been bound to my wards since before either of us could walk, and neither of us knew."

"Who else knows?"

"That's the question I came to ask you."

* * *

They looked at each other across the table, and Ilse permitted herself, briefly, the honesty she had been denying herself since she'd seen his name in the log.

She had enforced Sebastian's exile. That was the shape of their history, flat and ugly and true: the order had come down, signed and sealed, and Ilse Vane had carried it out, because she was a warden and wardens carry things out. She had stood at the city gate in the rain and watched them walk out of her life with one bag and no argument, and Sebastian had never once asked her to refuse the order, which was somehow worse than if they had begged.

"I need a storm-caller," she said, when she trusted her voice again. "The wards didn't fail. They were *unmade* — unwoven from the keystone outward, clean as a surgeon's cut. Whatever holds the mortal realm to the star-realm is broken or missing, and the stars went out because the door between us doesn't exist anymore. Things are already coming through the gap. My watchers report movement at the border. Movement that doesn't show up right in lamplight." She tapped the log. "This book says there was once a person who could mend such things. The book also says that person is you, whatever the arithmetic of it means."

"And what would a storm-caller do?" Sebastian asked. "The gift doesn't answer me anymore. It hasn't answered in years. It flickers, sometimes, like a lamp low on oil, and then it goes quiet, and I go back to counting other people's debts." A pause. Their mouth twitched. "Bottled thunder, mostly. We keep a great deal of bottled thunder here. You'd be amazed what people trade when they're desperate."

"I'm not amazed by what people trade," Ilse said. "I'm a warden. Trading is most of my job."

"No," Sebastian said softly, "your job is standing between the trades and the people they eat."

They had always known how to say things like that. It had been infuriating once, in a way she had mistaken for something else.

"The guild's ledgers were ordered sealed at midnight," they went on, more carefully now, testing each word's weight. "By Corvane himself. Every archive, every vault. If you imagine I'm going to walk into the master's private records and start pulling files about a forty-year-old covenant while wearing my own face, I want you to understand that I have spent thirty years learning to be invisible, and I'm rather good at it, and I'd prefer to continue."

"Thirty years," Ilse repeated. "The exile order was eleven years ago."

The pause that followed lasted exactly one heartbeat too long, and Ilse Vane had interrogated smugglers, debt-witches, and one memorable man who claimed to be his own ghost. She knew a slip when she heard one.

"Eleven," Sebastian agreed, too smoothly. "A figure of speech."

"Where were you the other nineteen years?"

"Ledger-keeping."

"Somewhere else."

"Everywhere else."

She let it go. Not because she believed them — she did not, entirely, believe them about anything — but because the sky outside the window was a black lid with no stars under it, and somewhere past the border things that traded in darkness were already moving, and the woman she had chosen to be could wait one day longer to be proven right.

"Here is what happens next," she said, standing, gathering the log. "I report to the Watch that the guild is cooperating fully. You find me whatever this archive is hiding about the covenant and the keystone — quietly, after hours, without your master's knowledge. And when we know what broke the wards, we decide together what kind of fools we're going to be about fixing it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you for falsifying a ward-log, which I'm fairly certain predates your birth, and we let the magistrates sort out the metaphysics." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "You always said I'd catch you eventually."

"I said you'd never catch me," Sebastian said, rising too, "and you said—" They stopped. Whatever they'd been about to quote, they swallowed it whole, and the swallowing cost them visibly.

"I remember what I said," Ilse said quietly.

At the door, she paused with her hand on the frame, because there was one more thing, and professional warden though she was, she had never mastered leaving without it.

"The night you walked out the gate," she said, not turning around, "you didn't fight the order. Everyone fights exile. Murderers fight it. You just — went. I've wondered for eleven years whether you wanted it."

Behind her, silence, and then, in a voice stripped of all its careful assembly:

"Ask Corvane why the order came when it came. Ask him why it came *then*, of all the years he could have chosen. Then ask yourself who benefited from a storm-caller being a hundred miles from the Spire of Vows."

The words landed like cold water. "The Spire—"

"Good afternoon, Warden," Sebastian Reyes said, in the pleasant, hollow tone of a man returning to his ledgers. "Mind the thunder bottles on your way out. Some of them have been feeling temperamental lately. Like the rest of us."

Ilse walked out into the starless morning with the old ward-log under her arm and three new questions where she had brought one, and the largest of them was already taking shape in her mind, patient and terrible:

What, exactly, had Master Corvane been keeping Sebastian Reyes away from — and what had he been keeping it away *for*?
