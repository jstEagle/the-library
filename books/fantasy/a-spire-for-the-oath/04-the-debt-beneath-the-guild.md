## 4. The Debt Beneath the Guild

The sealed archive of the Meridian Guild had, in its long and mercenary history, been described many ways: impregnable, sacrilegious, a vault where honesty went to be appraised. Master Corvane himself called it "the guild's conscience," which told Sebastian everything they needed to know about the man's relationship with his own.

Sebastian had another word for it tonight, standing before its door with a stolen ring of keys and their heart hammering out a rhythm no ledger could balance.

*Solvable.*

The lock was old—older than the famine, older than Corvane's tenure—and it remembered an era when locks were honest about wanting to be opened. Sebastian murmured to it the way they'd once murmured to thunderheads, coaxing rather than commanding. The tumblers turned with a sound like a sigh.

Inside, dust hung so thick it seemed less like neglect than preservation. The guild kept its living ledgers upstairs, all brass fittings and fresh ink, but down here lived the trades nobody wanted to remember making. Shelves sagged under volumes bound in leather gone the color of old bruises. A jar on the corner shelf held something that flickered even now—a promise someone had tried to take back, still arguing with its cork.

Sebastian lifted the lantern and began to search.

They were looking for one sigil: a spire drawn as a single unbroken line, the mark that had flared beneath their fingers three nights ago, the same night every star in the sky had gone out like candles snuffed by an indifferent hand. Three nights, and the city outside had learned new habits—shutters barred at dusk, prayers offered to a sky that answered nothing, markets where certain traders arrived smelling faintly of nothing at all.

*The Spire of Vows.* The words had surfaced from somewhere deep, somewhere they'd buried along with the rest of a life they weren't supposed to remember wanting.

The archive was organized by debt, not date—which was very like the guild, and nearly useless. Sebastian worked through *Debts Outstanding*, *Debts Forgiven (Rare)*, *Debts Repaid in Unusual Currencies*. In that last volume, between a woman who'd settled her account with thirty years of Sundays and a man who'd paid in the color blue, Sebastian found it.

A folio wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax stamped by the spire-sigil.

Their hands were steady. That surprised them. Everything else—their breath, their pulse, the small static charge raising the hairs on their arms—was shaking, but the hands were steady, because some part of them had been opening this folio for forty years without knowing it.

The first page was written in a hand far older than any living member of the guild. The ink had gone brown but the letters held their shape the way oaths do.

*Covenant Between the Realm of Mortals and the Realm of Stars, Renewed Each Century at the Spire.*

Sebastian read the terms twice, then a third time, mouthing them like a novice at prayer.

The stars would keep watch. That was the whole of their obligation, though *watch*, in the old tongue the covenant used, meant something closer to *hold back the dark*. In exchange, mortals would keep faith—would speak the oath aloud each century at the Spire, witnessed by a keystone set into the Spire's heart, which anchored the border wards between what lives in light and what prefers otherwise.

The keystone was not stone at all. It was a promise made solid, and it had been broken—deliberately, carefully, *commercially*—into three fragments.

Sebastian turned the page and found the inventory, added in a different hand. One he recognized.

He'd signed enough of Sebastian's own ledgers to know Corvane's script anywhere: precise, economical, each letter doing exactly the work assigned to it and not one stroke more.

*Fragment the first: sold, Winter of the Hungry Years, to secure grain contracts sufficient to keep this guild solvent through the famine. Buyer: withheld.*

*Fragment the second: sold, Spring following, against debts owed to the memory-brokers of Lowmarket. Buyer: withheld.*

*Fragment the third:* —and here the pen had pressed hard enough to score the page— *sold. Price: this guild's survival, forty more years, and my silence. I record for whoever finds this that I did not do it lightly. I did it because the alternative was watching everyone I am responsible for starve, and I find, having weighed a world against a guild, that I prefer the smaller thing. It is easier to love.*

Below, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*The wards will fail slowly. I will likely be dead before anyone notices. If you are reading this and I am not, know that I would do it again, and that I am sorry, and that neither of these statements is a lie.*

Sebastian stood very still in the dust and the lantern-light and felt the foundation of their careful life shift the way foundations do—not all at once, but with a grinding inevitability, stone finding stone, settling lower.

Forty years. The famine was forty years past. Which meant the night the stars went out had not been a catastrophe arriving unannounced.

It had been an invoice. Long overdue, accruing interest, finally called.

And Corvane had known. Had signed for it. Had filed the proof in a sealed archive and gone back upstairs to his abacus and his tea and his gentle tyrannies over the impossible trades, and let the sky run out the clock.

"You always did read too thoroughly."

Sebastian didn't startle. Some traitor part of them had been listening for the door.

Master Corvane stood in the archive's entrance, lantern in hand, dressed—for once—in plain wool, as if he'd come prepared to be seen clearly. He was not a tall man; he'd never needed to be. He closed the door behind him and shot the bolt, unhurried, a man locking in privacy rather than prey.

"Master Corvane." Sebastian's voice came out level, which felt like the last professional courtesy either of them would extend tonight. "I'm cataloguing."

"At midnight. Through a locked door." He crossed the room and set his lantern beside theirs, so the folio lay in overlapping circles of light. "I wondered when you'd find it. You have the right temperament for it—too honest for the upper floors, too stubborn for exile to take properly." His eyes went to the open covenant. "Though I confess I expected Ilse Vane to reach it first. She's thorougher than you. Don't tell her I said so."

"You knew the wards were failing." Not a question. Sebastian heard their own voice go quiet, the way it did around dangerous things. "You've known for years."

"I've known for forty years, Sebastian. There's a difference, and the difference is most of my hair." Corvane lowered himself onto a crate with the care of a man whose knees had opinions. Up close, in honest light, he looked old in a way he'd managed to hide from the guild floor. "Ask me why. Go on. You've earned the asking."

"Why?" It came out as a crack.

"Because in the Winter of the Hungry Years, I watched the ledger show me exactly how many days of grain remained, and it was fewer days than winter." He said it simply, the way he approved a purchase. "You were a child then. You don't remember the famine. Nobody remembers it anymore, which is the only good news I bought. I sold one fragment, and the brokers came for the second like gulls, and by then I understood the arithmetic: three pieces, one sky, versus four hundred souls under this roof eating bread through a winter that wanted them dead. I chose the souls." He spread his hands, palms up—an accountant's confession. "Every night since, I have checked that sum, and every night it has balanced."

"Then your sums are wrong."

"They're *accurate*. Those aren't the same thing, but accuracy is what I have." Corvane leaned forward, and for a moment something moved behind his face that was not the merchant, not quite. "You think I didn't consider the alternative? Refuse the sale, close the guild, put four hundred people into the street with the famine coming behind them? The dark doesn't pay better than the guild, Sebastian—it pays *differently*. It takes the sky and gives you time. Time is the only currency mortals can mint honestly, and I spent ours well. There are people alive tonight—people trading in that market downstairs, children asleep in the attics—who exist because I sold a piece of rock. You may call that monstrous. I call it bookkeeping."

"It wasn't rock." Sebastian's gift stirred under their skin, static rising off them in waves, the lantern flames leaning toward them like listeners. They reined it in, barely. "It was a promise. The oldest one we had. And you broke it and *filed the receipt*."

"Yes," Corvane said. "That is precisely what I did. And I'd thank you not to moralize at me until you've held a starving city in one hand and a sky in the other and found the sky weighs less." He rose, joints cracking, and looked at the covenant a long moment. "There's a reason I sealed this instead of burning it. Ask that, too."

"Why seal it?"

"Because debts want paying, and hidden debts fester, but *recorded* debts—" he tapped the folio, once, gently, the way he touched anything valuable— "*recorded* debts can be discharged. I couldn't undo the sale. But I could leave the trail. Fragment the third, the one I called 'withheld'—" his eyes met Sebastian's, and there was no triumph in them, only exhaustion worn smooth as river stone— "is not withheld. It's here. Under this guild. Under *us*, if it comes to that. I kept the last piece of the sky in my cellar for forty years like a man keeping poison in the pantry, telling myself I was saving it for the person who could carry it where it needs to go."

The storm under Sebastian's skin went utterly still.

"The person," they repeated slowly.

"A storm-caller in my ledgers, hiding under a false name, filing receipts with hands that could pull lightning out of clear air. You think I hired you for your penmanship?" Corvane almost smiled. Almost. It didn't survive the journey. "I've been waiting forty years for someone the oath might accept. And now the stars are out, the dark is trading in our markets, and Ilse Vane is asking questions I can't afford to answer—so yes, Sebastian. The debt has come due, and I find, at the end of all my sums, that I am the collateral."

Somewhere above them, muffled by floors and stone, a bell began to ring—the guild's alarm, harsh and arrhythmic, nothing like the orderly chimes of closing hour. Then another sound beneath it: voices, many voices, and the particular splintering crash of the front doors giving way.

Corvane's face went grey. "Enforcers," he said. "Or worse. The dark buys enforcers cheaply these days." He snuffed his lantern with two fingers and gripped Sebastian's arm, hard, an old man's strength in it after all. "Take the folio. Take the stairs behind the third shelf. Whatever happens up here—*the fragment is in the cellar under the foundation stone with the sigil burned on it*, and you must get it before they do."

"Corvane—"

"I balanced the books," he said quietly, already turning toward the door, drawing a keyring of his own. "Let them collect."

And the archive door banged open behind him, and the dark came pouring in wearing familiar faces.
