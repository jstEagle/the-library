## 5. What the Dark Trades

Ilse Vane had stood watch over the border wards for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above all others: the wards did not break quietly.

They screamed. They tore. They left scars in the air that ached for weeks afterward, like the memory of a tooth pulled wrong. So when they shattered on the night the stars went out — all of them at once, from the Saltreach to the Thornmarch, a sound like ten thousand bells dropped down a well — she knew it was not an accident. Accidents were local. Accidents apologized.

This had been *collected*.

Three days later, she was still in Vespergate, because Vespergate was where the reports said the trouble was thickest, and because the reports were right, and because she could not stop looking at the sky. It wasn't merely dark up there. Dark would have been bearable. Dark was honest. This was worse: the sky looked *shopped*. Stripped. Like a market stall after closing, every shelf swept clean, and somewhere behind the clouds a merchant counting coin that did not belong to him.

"You're staring again," said Tam, her junior warden, who was twenty-two and had not yet learned that some things you should let your superior do in peace.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring. You've been staring at the same patch of nothing for a quarter hour." He handed her a cup of something hot that claimed to be tea the way a turnip claims to be a swan. "The captain wants us back at the garrison by dusk."

"The captain can want things," Ilse said, and drank the turnip-tea anyway, because three days without sleep made even lies taste like comfort.

The city had not fallen apart. That was the strange part. She'd expected riots, looting, priests screaming on corners about the end of days. There was some of that — there is always some of that; panic is a trade like any other, and Vespergate's merchants of doom were doing brisk business — but mostly the city had done what cities do. It had adapted. It had lit more lamps. It had invented new schedules around its grief.

And then it had started trading.

She saw the first stall on Wrenmarket Row, wedged between a fishmonger and a woman selling charms that were, Ilse noted professionally, complete frauds. The stall had no lantern. It didn't need one. The thing behind the counter was shaped roughly like a tall man, if a man were assembled by someone who had only ever heard men described — joints slightly wrong, smile slightly too wide, skin the color of the space between stars. Where its eyes should have been there was simply depth.

It was doing steady business.

"Memories," Tam whispered. "They're buying memories. That's the third one today. They set up wherever the lamps are thin."

Ilse watched a queue form. Not a long one. A queue of the desperate, which is to say, a short queue that moved slowly, because nobody in it wanted to be at the front.

The first customer was a dockhand with a limp. He spoke too quietly for Ilse to hear, but she saw the transaction: the starless thing reached across the counter and pressed two fingers to his temple, gentle as a mother checking for fever. The man shuddered. Then his face went smooth, unburdened, almost young. He walked away lighter, clutching a small purse that clinked.

"He sold something good," Tam said quietly. "You can tell. Bad memories, they pay less. I asked."

"You *asked*?"

"Undercover work," Tam said, with the pride of a man who had discovered a talent. "I told it I was thinking of selling my memory of my grandmother's funeral. It offered me four coppers and looked insulted. Then I pretended to consider selling my memory of the night my daughter was born, and it went very still, and offered me a house." His voice cracked slightly on the last word. "A *house*, Captain. On Harrow Lane. With a garden."

"What did you say?"

"I said I had to think about it." He stared into his cup. "I'm still thinking about it. That's the problem, isn't it? That's how they get you. They don't take anything. They just make the price visible."

Ilse said nothing, because there was nothing wise to say, and because ahead of them the queue had shortened to a single figure, and the figure was small.

A child. Eight years old, maybe nine, in a coat two winters out of date, standing on her toes to see over the counter. Beside her, a basket of bread — real bread, white flour, the kind that cost more than a laborer's day — and beside the bread, a woman's shawl, folded with terrible care.

"No," Ilse breathed, and started forward, and stopped, because stopping was the job, stopping was always the job, a warden who broke every moment she should stand witness was no warden at all. But her boots argued with her the whole way.

The girl couldn't reach the counter properly, so the starless thing leaned down. It had to fold itself nearly in half to do it, and the folding made a sound like wet rope. Its too-wide smile arranged itself at the girl's eye level.

The girl pointed at the bread. Then at the shawl. Then she touched her own temple, mimicking what she'd seen the dockhand do, and said something Ilse couldn't hear.

*No,* Ilse thought. *Not that. Anything but that.*

But the thing nodded, slow and pleased, and reached out, and the girl closed her eyes.

Ilse watched it happen. Watched the girl's face change — not empty, not blank, the starless things were precise tradesmen, they took exactly what was bought — but rearranged. The particular softness around her eyes, the look children have when their whole face is a portrait of someone they love, simply... smoothed away. Like a page turned in a book. Like a name crossed off a ledger.

The girl opened her eyes, frowned faintly at the shawl as though unsure why she'd brought it, took the bread, and walked away eating it. She passed within arm's reach of Ilse. Her cheeks were full. Her step was light.

She did not know whose face she used to wear.

Ilse stood very still until the girl rounded the corner. Then she walked to the stall, and Tam, wisely, did not try to stop her.

"Trade's closed," the thing said. Its voice was pleasant. That was somehow the worst part. It sounded like a shopkeeper at the end of a good day.

"That child," Ilse said. "Her mother's face. You bought a child's memory of her mother's face for a loaf of bread."

"It was a large loaf." The thing tilted its head, joints creaking. "And the memory was going spare. Children forget anyway. We merely... accelerate the depreciation. All commerce does this, Warden. You simply prefer your thefts slower."

"We don't steal faces."

"No?" The depth where its eyes should be seemed to widen, and Ilse felt, for one vertiginous second, that it was reading her — inventorying her, pricing her, and finding her expensive. "Then what do you call the wardens' oath? *Give us your fear, your doubt, your old griefs, and we will make you instruments.* Every order trades in memory. Ours is simply the honest till."

It began packing up — though pack was generous; the stall simply grew less there, item by item, like a tide going out.

"But you're not here for bread-money," it continued, conversational. "You're here because of what we're buying *in bulk*. The big lots. The old stock." Its smile stretched another impossible degree. "There's a piece of the sky's keystone in this city, Warden. Three pieces, actually, scattered like a broken promise. And we have a buyer. Old client. Very patient. Pays in houses and harvests and mothers' faces, whatever the seller needs most." The stall was nearly gone now, just the voice and the smile hanging in the lamplight-thin air. "You'll want to find them before we do. Though I suspect—" the last of it was already dissolving, voice fraying into the dark like smoke, "—you'll want to find the storm-caller first. Tell her the ledger remembers her."

Then there was only an ordinary gap between a fishmonger and a fraudulent charm-seller, and a small crowd pretending they hadn't watched any of it, and Ilse standing alone with her heart hammering a warden's rhythm: *threat, threat, threat.*

Storm-caller. Sebastian Reyes. Ledger-keeper of the Meridian Guild, if the guild's sealed doors and Corvane's oily letters were to be believed, which Ilse did not. The exile she had been ordered to enforce seven years ago. The person she had loved before the orders, and after the orders, and — if she was honest, and she was trying, lately, to be honest — during the orders, which was the part that shamed her most.

*The ledger remembers her.*

She turned to Tam. "Get me everything the Watch has on the Meridian Guild. Quietly."

"And you, Captain?"

"I'm going to go stare at a different patch of nothing," Ilse said, and went to find a ledger-keeper.

* * *

Sebastian Reyes looked, Ilse thought, like a man who had been arguing with a door and losing.

She found them in the guild's side alley, where the delivery entrance was supposed to be, hammering on wood that refused to open, wrapped in a coat that had once been fine and was now mostly pockets. When they turned, lamplight caught their face, and Ilse saw the last three days written on it: no sleep, no stars, and a specific dread she recognized because she wore it herself.

"You," Sebastian said.

"Me."

"They've locked the archives. Corvane's sealed the ledgers. There are enforcers on the roof who keep *looking at me*, Ilse, and I don't think it's professional interest." They ran a hand through hair that had given up on dignity days ago. "Also the door won't open, and I am technically employed here, which means either I've been dismissed or the building has decided I know too much, and honestly I'm not sure which worries me more."

"Hello to you too," Ilse said. "I watched a child sell her mother's face for bread today."

The argument drained out of Sebastian's shoulders all at once. "Where."

"Wrenmarket. There are stalls — things wearing the shapes of merchants, buying memories. Good ones. Precious ones. They pay in whatever the seller needs." Ilse hesitated, then gave them the rest, watching it land. "And one of them told me to tell *her* — their word, not mine — that the ledger remembers her. And that there are three pieces of something called the sky's keystone in this city, and their buyer is collecting."

Sebastian had gone very still. It was the stillness of someone standing on ice and hearing it speak.

"The Spire sigil," they said finally. "On the foundation stone. In the old ledgers — there's an entry, centuries old, marked with it. I touched it and my storm-gift lit up like a struck bell. I thought it was nothing. I *made* it be nothing." They laughed, a short ugly sound. "Corvane sealed the archives the same hour the stars went out. What is he hiding, Ilse? What did he *do*?"

"That," Ilse said, "is what I was hoping you'd help me find out. Through a door that won't open."

Sebastian looked at the door. Looked at her. And Ilse watched something move behind their eyes — the old recklessness she remembered, the one that had once called lightning down onto a parade ground just to see her laugh, banked for seven years under ledger-ink and caution, stirring like a fire given air.

"There's a window on the archive level," Sebastian said slowly. "It opens onto the cistern shaft. Officially it doesn't exist. Unofficially, I've been using it to avoid Corvane since the famine year." A pause. Their voice dropped. "Ilse. If these things are buying up pieces of the covenant — if something ancient is shopping for the sky itself — then whoever holds those fragments is holding the only currency that matters. And I don't think Corvane sealed those archives to protect the guild."

"No," Ilse agreed. "I think he sealed them to protect himself."

Above them, the starless dark pressed close against the rooftops, patient as a creditor. Somewhere across the city, another stall was opening, another price was being named, another precious thing was changing hands for less than it was worth.

Sebastian exhaled. "Right," they said. "Fetch me at midnight. Wear something you can climb in."

"And if the enforcers catch us?"

"Then we'll sell them the memory of this conversation," Sebastian said, with the ghost of the old grin, "and split the proceeds."

Despite everything — the dead sky, the hollowed child, the seven ruined years between them — Ilse found herself smiling as she walked away into the dark.

*The ledger remembers her.* Well. Let it remember this, too.
