## 6. Three Fragments, One Road

*Sebastian*

The debtors' prison of Hollowmark smelled of wet stone and old arithmetic.

Sebastian had spent eleven years among ledgers, and they knew the smell of compound interest anywhere. It clung to this place like mold—every cell a column, every prisoner a sum carried forward year after year until the numbers grew too heavy to carry at all.

"You're smiling," Ilse said beside them, her warden's coat pulled tight against the damp. "We're breaking into a prison and you're smiling."

"I'm recognizing a colleague's handiwork." Sebastian nodded toward the gatehouse, where the lock was wrapped in three separate wards, each one older than the last. "Whoever keeps these books is terrified of being audited."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." Sebastian pressed their palm against the outermost ward and felt it hum—a familiar frequency, guild-made, guild-kept. Their storm-gift stirred under their skin like a cat waking from a nap, static prickling along their knuckles. They let a thread of it slip out, thin as spider silk, and unpicked the ward's knot the way you'd tease apart a snarl in yarn.

The lock sighed open.

"Eleven years as a ledger-keeper," Ilse murmured, following them into the dark. "And that's what you were doing with your evenings?"

"Mostly I filed things. But a person needs hobbies."

They descended. The prison's keeper found them on the third landing—a gaunt man with ink-stained fingers and the particular pallor of someone who had not seen true daylight since the stars went out. He did not reach for an alarm bell. He looked at Sebastian's face, then at Ilse's warden's badge, and made a calculation Sebastian recognized: which of these two could hurt me worse?

"Wardens don't come down here," he said. "Not even now. Not even with the sky gone wrong."

"The sky gone wrong is why we've come," Ilse said. "There's a piece of the Spire's keystone in your deepest cell. We need it."

The keeper's face did something complicated. Fear, mostly. But underneath it, greed—the oldest ledger-entry of all.

"That piece," he said slowly, "is collateral. Prisoner Marrow owes the house nine hundred crowns. The fragment was appraised at—" he swallowed— "well. It hums. It lights when no lamp should light. It's worth more than every debtor in this building combined."

"And yet you keep it in a cell," Sebastian said, "because you can't sell it. No buyer wants something that *remembers being whole*. Am I close?"

The keeper said nothing, which was answer enough.

Sebastian reached into their coat and produced the one thing a guild-trained ledger-keeper always carried: a contract blank, countersigned by the Meridian Guild's seal, still valid because the guild itself had not yet finished dying. "Here is my offer. The Guild assumes prisoner Marrow's debt in full. Nine hundred crowns, transferred tonight. In exchange, the fragment passes to us, and your books balance."

"That's it? You'd pay nine hundred crowns for—"

"I'd pay anything," Sebastian said quietly, "to put the stars back. So would you, if you thought about it past the next meal."

The keeper stared at the contract a long moment. Then he laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound—and led them down.

Prisoner Marrow turned out to be a woman of perhaps sixty, sitting cross-legged in the dark with the fragment resting on her knees like a sleeping bird. It was the size of a fist, angular, and it glowed faintly with a light that had nothing to do with fire. When Sebastian entered the cell, the fragment brightened.

Marrow looked up. "It's been doing that all day," she said. "Getting louder. Like it knows someone's coming."

"It does." Sebastian knelt and held out their hands. "May I?"

She gave it over without ceremony, but as Sebastian's fingers closed around the stone, she caught their wrist. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"You'll pay for this," she said. Not a threat. A warning. "Everything costs. That's the only law left, with the stars dark. Everything costs, and the price collectors are already walking."

"I know," Sebastian said. "I've read the accounts."

* * *

*Ilse*

The memory-broker kept shop in the Lantern District, in a building that had once been a chapel and now smelled of lavender and cold iron. Ilse stood across the street in the starless dark and hated everything about the assignment.

"We could just take it," she said.

"We could also just die," Sebastian said. "Brokers insure against theft. It's the first thing they buy, before they buy their first memory. There will be wards on that door that would peel the skin off a warden, and I say that with respect for your profession."

"Then we bargain."

"Then we bargain." Sebastian glanced at her, and in the gloom their eyes caught some stray light and held it. "You're good at this part, you know. The standing-in-the-dark-dreading-it part. Very disciplined."

"Don't flatter me while I'm armed."

"I would never. Flattery is a trade good, and you haven't named a price."

Ilse almost smiled. She killed the smile before it could finish, because smiling at Sebastian Reyes was how this whole disaster had started, years ago, back when she'd been young enough to believe that loving a storm-caller and enforcing the law against storm-callers were two jobs a person could hold at once.

They went in together.

The broker was a small, neat woman with silver hair and eyes like polished slate, seated behind a desk covered in little glass vials. Each vial held a curl of pale mist. Memories, bottled and labeled. Ilse had confiscated such things before; she had never gotten used to reading the labels. *First snowfall, 40 years kept. Mother's voice, partial. The day I decided to leave.*

"You want the keystone fragment," the broker said, before either of them spoke. "Of course you do. Everyone wants it. The thing in the market district wanted it too, and it pays better than you ever will."

Ilse went very still. "What thing?"

"A customer." The broker folded her neat hands. "Tall. Wrong. Wore a face like it was borrowed from a church statue. It offered me a vial of my own wedding day—I sold that years ago, mind, so it shouldn't exist—and asked me to name a price for the fragment. I told it the truth. Not for sale. Some inventory you keep because selling it ends the world."

"And it accepted that?" Sebastian asked.

"It accepted it *politely*." For the first time, the broker's composure flickered. "That was worse. It said it would wait. It said waiting was a kind of buying. Then it left, and every clock in the building started running backward, and I have been sitting here ever since deciding whether to sell the fragment to the first honest bidder who walked through my door. Congratulations. You're the first honest bidders."

"What do you want?" Ilse asked.

"For the fragment?" The broker's slate eyes moved to Sebastian, and stayed there. "Not money. Not memories. I want a favor, unwritten and unpriced, owed by a storm-caller to a memory-broker, redeemable once, at a time of my choosing."

"No," Ilse said immediately.

"Yes," Sebastian said at the same moment.

They looked at each other. Something passed between them that had nothing to do with the bargain—an argument conducted entirely in old fluency, in the shorthand of people who had once finished each other's sentences.

"Sebastian," Ilse said low. "Unwritten debts are how Corvane bought this whole mess. You know that. You *found* the covenant. You know what open-ended promises cost."

"I know exactly what they cost," Sebastian said. "I've spent eleven years paying installments." They turned back to the broker. "Agreed. Witnessed by the warden, if she consents to witness."

Ilse's jaw tightened. Every professional instinct said refuse. Every other instinct—the ones she'd buried under a decade of duty and a coat that fit too well—said *say yes, keep them close, keep them alive.* She stepped forward. "Witnessed," she said. "And witnessed means I remember the terms. All of them. Forever."

The broker smiled for the first time, and it transformed her face into something almost kind. "Then we understand each other." She drew a long key from around her neck, unlocked a drawer, and set the second fragment on the desk between them.

It leapt toward the first one in Sebastian's pocket like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

*Sebastian*

They made it four streets before the shadow fell across the road.

It didn't fall so much as *arrive*—a patch of deeper dark where the lamplight simply declined to go. And out of it came the tall shape, wearing its borrowed face: Sebastian's dead mentor, Aldous Wren, rendered in smooth stone-smoothness, smiling with a mouth that moved a half-second behind the smile.

"Two fragments," it said. Its voice was Aldous's voice, and that was the cruelest part. "You work quickly, little storm. He always said you would. Before he sold you."

Sebastian stopped walking. "Sold me?"

"Oh." The thing tilted its head, savoring it. "You don't know. How delicious. Ask the warden. She signed the order."

"Sebastian." Ilse's hand found their arm. "Don't listen to it. It trades in lies—"

"It doesn't lie," Sebastian heard themselves say. "That's the horror of brokers. They never have to lie." They turned to Ilse, and the street felt very narrow suddenly, very cold. "Is it true?"

Ilse's face did something Sebastian had never seen it do in eleven years of careful warden composure: it broke.

"There was a petition," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. To grant you clemency—to let you approach the Spire, prove yourself, be reinstated. Corvane quashed it. He wrote the exile order himself, and he routed it through my office so my signature would be on the enforcement. He told me it was routine. He told me—" her voice caught— "he told me you'd fled before the papers arrived. I believed him. Gods help me, I believed him for twenty years, and then I found your name in the ward-log and understood why it was there. Why it was always going to be there."

The world tilted. Eleven years of hiding, of false names and careful ledgers and a storm-gift kept folded up small enough to pass for nothing—all of it rearranged itself around a single new fact. *Corvane had built the cage. Corvane had needed the cage, because a storm-caller near the Spire might ask questions about keystones.*

"You could have come for me," Sebastian said. It came out smaller than they intended.

"I tried. Twice. Corvane reassigned me both times." Ilse stepped closer, close enough that Sebastian could see her eyes shining in the fragment-light. "And then the stars went out, and I stopped asking permission. That's why I'm here. That's the whole truth of why I'm here."

The entity watched them with Aldous's patient stone face. "There," it said softly. "Truth exchanged freely. Do you see how much better the market runs on honesty?" It began to withdraw into its patch of refusing-light. "Keep your fragments, little storm. Keep the warden too. I have the third piece, and I have all the time that the dark allows. Come to the Spire when you're ready to discuss interest."

And it was gone, and the lamplight remembered how to fall.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Ilse said, roughly, "Say something. Yell at me. I'd deserve it."

"I'm going to yell at Corvane," Sebastian said. "You I'm going to—" They looked down at the two fragments glowing in their hands, humming toward each other like tuning forks finding the same note, and something in their chest hummed in answer. "Ask me again in a hundred miles. Ask me at the top of the Spire."

Ilse huffed—a wet, broken laugh. "Ledger-keeper to the end. Always deferring payment."

"Always structuring the debt," Sebastian corrected. "Come on. Two fragments down. One road left."

Behind them, far off in the direction of the Meridian Guild, a sound rolled across the rooftops—not thunder, though Sebastian's skin prickled with the old familiar static anyway. Something was collapsing. Something was collecting.

And the third fragment, somewhere in the dark, was waiting to be paid for.
