## 7. The Price of the Third Piece

The thing wearing Maren Ostrova's face was waiting for us in the old counting-house on Vellum Street, and it had made tea.

That was the detail that undid me, more than the starless dark pooling behind its eyes or the way its shadow fell in the wrong direction. Maren Ostrova had never once, in thirty years of teaching me the storm-caller's craft, made tea. She considered it beneath a woman who could pull lightning out of a clear sky. "Let the kettle do its own small magic," she used to say. "I'll do mine."

And yet there it was: a chipped brown pot, two cups, steam curling upward in the cold room. The entity had studied her so well it had even gotten her hands right — broad-knuckled, scarred from a lifetime of catching lightning she had no business catching.

"You're not her," I said from the doorway.

"No," it agreed, in Maren's voice, warm as banked coals. "But I bought her memories of you at auction last winter. All of them. I've had a great deal of time to practice." It poured tea into both cups. "Sit down, Sebastian. You look terrible. She would have said that."

Behind me, Ilse's hand found my shoulder. Her grip said *we can still walk away*. Her grip also said *I know you're going to do something foolish*, which after three weeks of hunting fragments together had become her most fluent form of speech.

"We came to bargain," Ilse said.

"Of course you did." The entity gestured to the chairs. "Everyone comes to bargain. That's what this city is now — a market with the lights off. Sit. The tea is real. I paid for it honestly."

Ilse didn't sit. Neither did I, but that was stubbornness rather than sense; my legs had been arguing for sitting since the debtors' prison, where we'd won our second fragment by nearly drowning in a cell full of other people's repossessed memories. My head still rang with someone else's wedding day.

"The fragment," I said. "You have the third piece of the keystone. We want it."

"I do have it." The entity reached into the breast pocket of Maren's coat and set something on the table between the cups: a shard of pale stone no bigger than a knuckle, humming faintly, like a struck bell heard through water. Beside the two fragments in my satchel, it would make three. Three, and then the Spire, and then the oath, if any of us lived long enough to speak it.

It let me look. That was the cruelty of it. It let me look for a long, long moment before it spoke again.

"Here is my price," it said. "Your storm."

* * *

I should explain what the storm-gift is, though I've spent twenty years trying not to think about it, which makes explaining awkward.

It isn't weather. Weather is honest labor; it falls when it falls. The gift is older than that — a covenant-thread in the blood, the ability to call lightning the way you'd call a dog that knows your name. Maren found me at fourteen, standing in a flooded street with my arms raised and every raindrop around me hanging motionless in the air like an audience holding its breath. She took me in. She taught me to hold it, shape it, spend it. And when the Guild of Meridian needed someone to blame for a lightning-strike that killed four people — a strike Corvane himself had purchased, though I didn't learn that until this month — Maren's testimony wasn't enough to save me. Exile did that. Exile, and the erasure of my name from every ledger but one.

The gift got me exiled. The gift kept me alive through exile. I hated it and I fed on it in equal measure, the way you hate and feed on your own heart.

"It isn't mine to give," I said carefully. "It's tied to the old covenant. It predates me."

"Everything predates everyone," the entity said pleasantly. "That has never stopped anyone from selling. I've watched a child sell her mother's face for bread. I watched a warden—" it glanced at Ilse with Maren's eyes, and Ilse went very still, "—nearly sell her grief once, in a bad year. Everyone has a price. Yours is simply larger than most, because you are larger than most. Larger than you've allowed yourself to be, anyway, hiding in your little ledgers."

"Don't," Ilse said quietly.

"I'm only being kind. He'll want to know the terms precisely. Precision matters in trade." The entity folded Maren's hands on the table. "The fragment for the storm-gift, freely given, fully surrendered. Not suppressed — I don't buy damaged goods. Given. And before you ask what happens to it: I drink it. Storms are rare currency in the starless places. There are things out there, past the broken wards, that remember lightning fondly."

"And if we refuse?" Ilse asked.

"If you refuse, I keep the fragment. I keep all the fragments eventually — I am very patient, and the city grows darker by the hour. In nine days the last ward fails entirely, and everything you love becomes inventory." It sipped its tea. "So. Not much of a choice, really. But I find people say yes more readily when they're offered one."

* * *

We stepped outside into the cold to argue, because some arguments deserve fresh air, and because I could not bear to negotiate over Maren's face without a door between us.

"You're not doing it," Ilse said.

"That's funny. It sounded like a question."

"It wasn't." She turned to face me, and the starless dark made her eyes look black, though I knew them to be grey as slate before a storm — a comparison I'd been making since I was nineteen and hopeless about it. "Sebastian. Listen to me. That thing is offering you exactly what you've always wanted. No gift, no exile, no reason for anyone to hunt you. A small quiet life. And you've spent twenty years learning that the small quiet life is a lie you tell yourself while you die slowly in a guild archive."

"That's not fair."

"No. It's true, which is worse." She caught my sleeve. "If you surrender the gift, the Spire may refuse you. Did you think of that? The oath needs a speaker with standing. What standing does a ledger-keeper have?"

"A ledger-keeper who holds the keystone," I said. "Standing enough."

"And if it doesn't? If we climb all that way and the Spire looks at you and finds you hollowed out — Sebastian. *Sparrow.*" The old name landed like a hand on a wound. Only she had ever called me that; only she knew why. "Don't make me climb that Spire carrying a stranger."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Somewhere across the city, a bell rang the hour, though the hour meant nothing anymore; the stars were gone and the bells rang anyway, out of habit, out of defiance, out of whatever it is that keeps people ringing bells in the dark.

"There's something I haven't told you," I said. "About the night they exiled me. Maren came to me before the tribunal. She said — she said the gift was never mine to keep. That it was loaned against a debt, the same covenant-debt, and that someday the collector would come, and I'd have to decide whether I was the kind of person who pays." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I thought she was being poetic. She was being *precise*. She was always precise."

"Sebastian—"

"She sold her testimony to save me and it cost her everything, and I ran, and I hid, and I built a life out of other people's debts because I couldn't face my own." I looked back at the counting-house window, where Maren's borrowed silhouette moved behind frosted glass, pouring tea for no one. "Ilse. Twenty years ago I chose hiding. Every day since, I've chosen it again, smaller and smaller. This is the first chance I've been given to choose anything else. Do you understand? It doesn't feel like sacrifice yet. It feels like coming home by a road I've never walked."

She was crying. She tried not to be, and failed, and wiped her face with the flat angry gesture of a woman arresting her own tears. "You absolute fool," she said. "You impossible, honorable fool."

"You loved me once. You must have known what I was."

"That's exactly what frightens me." She gripped my hand hard enough to hurt. "Then hear my terms. If the Spire refuses you — if that thing cheats us, if you stand hollowed out on some mountain and the sky stays black — I will find a way to make it unmake this. I am a warden. I am very good at making ancient things regret their contracts. Swear you'll fight it if it turns out wrong."

"I swear."

"And—" her voice broke, and she steadied it the way she'd steady a failing ward, with brute force and precision, "—whatever it takes from you, however much goes missing — some part of you will remember that I stayed. Even if the rest forgets. Promise me that part survives."

I kissed her forehead, because I didn't trust myself to kiss anywhere else. "That part," I said, "is load-bearing."

* * *

Inside, the tea had gone cold, and the entity had not touched either cup, because entities do not drink; they only watch.

"Well?" it asked, in Maren's voice, with Maren's patience.

I sat down across from it. Up close, the disguise frayed at the edges — the scars on her hands were drawn slightly too symmetrical, the crow's feet arranged rather than earned. But the eyes behind the starless dark were kind, in the way that mirrors are kind: showing you exactly what stands before them.

"I accept," I said. "Freely given. Fully surrendered. Say the terms back to me, and make them precise. I'm a ledger-keeper. Precision matters in trade."

Something flickered across its borrowed face — surprise, perhaps, or appetite, or the closest thing an ancient hunger has to respect.

"By the old forms, then," it said. "One fragment of the keystone of vows, delivered whole, in exchange for the storm-gift of Sebastian Reyes, given freely, withheld never, reclaimed by none." It slid the shard across the table. "Take up the stone."

I picked it up. It was warm, and it sang against the other two in my satchel, three notes resolving toward a chord.

"Now the payment," the entity said, and it reached across the table and laid Maren's hands over mine, and the world went white.

Not pain. Worse — *departure*. I felt the storm rise in me one last time, all twenty years of held thunder, every raindrop I'd ever frozen midair, the fourteen-year-old with his arms raised and his heart full of sky — and I felt it go, drawn out through my palms like thread from a spindle, singing, singing, and then silent. The silence where it had been was enormous. It was the silence of a room after a clock stops. I hadn't known how loud I'd been my whole life until I wasn't.

I gasped. My hands, on the table, were just hands. Ordinary. Cold. Nothing answered when I reached inward, nothing surged; there was only me, small and mortal and strangely light, like a man setting down a pack he'd forgotten he carried.

"There," the entity said softly, folding the stolen storm away somewhere beyond the walls of the world. "Paid in full. You'll feel strange for a few days. Most sellers do." It stood, gathering Maren's coat around itself, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," it said, not turning around, "she would be proud of you. That memory I kept. I don't part with the good ones."

The door closed behind it, and it was gone, taking the warmth with it.

Ilse helped me up. My legs worked; that was something. Outside, the dark pressed close, but in my fist the three fragments hummed together, complete at last, pulling gently northward — toward the hills, toward the Spire of Vows, toward the last nine days of the world.

"One piece left to pay," Ilse murmured, looking at the fragments, then at me, and I saw that she already understood what the entity's parting words had cost me to hear. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk," I said. And then, because the lightness in my chest was terrifying and wonderful and I had to give it words: "Ilse. I feel like a blank page."

She took my arm. "Then let's go find out what gets written on you."

North, into the starless dark, the keystone singing in my hand like something remembering its name.
