## 8. Corvane's Reckoning

Master Aldous Corvane had survived forty years in the Meridian Guild by observing one iron law: never read your own contracts too closely. The guild's ledgers were written to be consulted, not *understood*. A man who understood every clause he had signed would never sleep again.

He was not sleeping now. He was standing in his office on the guild's third floor, watching the ink move.

It had started an hour after the stars went out — an hour after Sebastian Reyes and that stone-faced warden had broken out of the sealed archive with three fragments of a keystone Corvane had spent four decades pretending did not exist. The ledger open on his desk was the oldest one, the founding book, bound in leather gone soft as glove-skin. Its entries were rearranging themselves. Debts he had recorded decades ago were migrating up the pages like ants fleeing a flooded nest, crowding toward the top, toward the newest line, which read:

*One (1) covenant keystone, sold. Payment deferred. Interest accruing.*

"I know what I paid," Corvane said aloud, to the ledger, to the room, to no one. "I kept the guild alive. Forty thousand souls under this roof ate that winter because of me."

The ink did not care. Ink never cared; that was its great virtue and its terrible honesty. The entry simply sat there, patient as a creditor at a funeral.

Somewhere below, glass broke.

* * *

The enforcers came up the stairs in their grey coats, six of them, led by Hesk — twenty years in Corvane's service, a man whose loyalty Corvane had purchased the way he purchased everything: carefully, in installments, with interest he'd assumed would never come due.

"You've seen it, then," Hesk said. He did not bow. In thirty years, Hesk had never once failed to bow.

"Seen what."

"The doors." Hesk's jaw worked. "Every contract in the vault is glowing. Every seal. They're... reading themselves out loud, Master. The whole ground floor sounds like a counting-house in a fever dream. And the front doors won't open. Not for us. We tried the windows." He paused. "The windows tried back."

Corvane crossed to the office window. Below, Meridian Square was a sea of candlelight and panic, and beyond it the city proper had gone properly dark — not night-dark, but *absence*-dark, the kind of dark that made you understand why the ancients had worshipped stars instead of merely enjoying them. Starless things moved through the streets down there. He could see them if he let himself: shapes like holes cut from the world, drifting through the market stalls where desperate citizens were trading away their mothers' faces, their first kisses, the names of their children, all for bread and candles.

He had done business with darkness before. It had always seemed so reasonable. Darkness never haggled.

"The guild is collecting," Corvane said quietly. "On me."

"Collecting what?"

"Everything." He turned back to the desk. The ledger's pages were turning themselves now, faster, and with each page something left him. It was not painful, exactly. It was more like the moment when you walk into a room and forget why — except the rooms were his life, and they were emptying one by one. He reached for the memory of his mother's kitchen and found wallpaper, a smell of bread, and nothing else. No mother. No kitchen. Just the fact that there had been one, filed away like a receipt.

"Hesk," he said, and heard his own voice go thin. "When you find Reyes — and you will find them, because I am ordering you to, because I have nothing else left to order — tell them..." He stopped. What? *Tell them I'm sorry*? He searched himself for remorse and found the shelf where remorse should have been already cleared, swept clean by some patient clause. "Tell them the third fragment answers to blood. Old blood. Spire blood. That's all they'll need."

Hesk stared at him. "You're giving up."

"I'm being *collected*, Hesk. There's a difference, though I admit it's mostly academic." Corvane managed something that had once been his smile — the one that had closed a hundred impossible trades, the one people said could sell winter to a snowman. It felt strange on his face now, like wearing another man's coat. "Go. Out the archive passage. It still opens for guild blood, and yours counts."

"And yours?"

"My blood opened everything in this building," Corvane said. "That was rather the problem."

* * *

They left him. Of course they did. Loyalty, too, had been a contract, and the guild had foreclosed on all of them at once.

Corvane sat down at his desk, because a man facing ruin ought at least to be comfortable, and watched forty years arrive.

They came politely, in order, oldest first. The famine year, when he had stood in this very room with a broker whose face he suddenly could not recall, and traded the keystone fragment — the heart of the covenant between the mortal realm and the star-realm, the thing that kept the sky stitched to the world — for enough gold to feed the guild through winter. He remembered telling himself it was temporary. He remembered telling himself the stars would forgive. He remembered, most vividly, the exact weight of the fragment in his palm: warm, faintly humming, like a bird deciding whether to trust you.

He had told everyone the guild's survival was the greater good. And here was the joke, the magnificent, damnable joke that the ledger had been waiting forty years to deliver: it hadn't even worked. Oh, the gold had fed them that winter. But the guild ran on impossible trades, and impossible trades ran on the borderlands between realms, and the borderlands ran on the covenant. Every year since, the guild's profits had thinned like broth. Every year, Corvane had signed worse contracts to cover the shortfall — contracts with things that lived in the gaps the weakening wards no longer guarded. He had sold the keystone to save the guild, and the selling had been killing it ever since, slowly, the way a debt kills anything.

He had not saved the guild. He had put it on credit against the sky.

"Oh," Corvane said, to the empty office, to the moving ink, to whatever patient intelligence kept the guild's books. "I'm not the debtor."

The ledger turned its final page.

"*I'm the debt.*"

The name went first. He felt it lift out of him like a splinter drawn clean — *Aldous Corvane, Master of the Meridian Guild* — and he watched it appear on the page in fresh ink, entered neatly, itemized, valued. Then the memories came faster: his first trade, his first betrayal, the apprentice he'd ruined, the apprentice he'd exiled — *Reyes, Sebastian, storm-caller, threat to operations, exile engineered per attached rider* — yes, that one too, that was his, he'd written that boy's ruin himself with a steady hand and called it prudence—

The door burst open.

Sebastian Reyes stood in the doorway with Ilse Vane behind them, both of them streaked with soot and something darker, fragments wrapped and slung across Sebastian's back like a fussy infant. The guild was groaning around them, stones shifting, contracts howling in the vaults below, and these two had climbed straight into the jaws of it — for what, Corvane wondered dimly, for *him*?

No. For the ledger. Sebastian's eyes went past him to the founding book, and Corvane understood, with the last of his clarity, that they had come for the original terms. The oath itself. You couldn't speak a covenant you couldn't read.

"Take it," he said. His voice sounded wrong. Younger. Emptier. "Top shelf of my memory. There's a— there was a—" He frowned. Something about a hiding place. Something about a loose stone. He had known once. He was almost sure he had known once.

Sebastian hesitated in the doorway. Even now — even after everything Corvane had taken from them, the exile, the years, the whole stolen life — there was something in their face that wasn't hatred. Pity, maybe. Pity was worse.

"Master Corvane," Sebastian said. "Come with us. The Spire takes payment, but it also takes witnesses. Maybe there's a version of this where you—"

"Where I what?" Corvane laughed, and the laugh came out hollow, echoing in a chest that was increasingly full of blank pages. "Renegotiate?" He shook his head. "I wrote the guild's rules, child. I know better than anyone what happens when the bill comes due. There's no clause for mercy. I checked. Believe me — I checked for forty years."

The floor tilted. Below them, the great vault doors finally gave way with a sound like a sigh, and the guild's collected debts rose up through the building like floodwater — every broken promise, every rigged trade, every small cruelty filed and forgotten, all of it coming home at last to the man who had signed for it.

Ilse grabbed Sebastian's arm. "We have to go. Now."

Sebastian took the founding ledger from the shelf — the loose stone, of course, he *had* known once — and looked back at him one last time.

"The third fragment," Corvane said, clinging to the one useful thing left in him. "Blood. Spire blood. Tell them I—" But the sentence dissolved before he could finish it, along with the reason he'd wanted to say it, along with the word *sorry*, which left him so smoothly he never even felt it go.

What remained of Aldous Corvane sat in his chair and clutched the desk ledger as the guild came down around him, and in the final moment — the last clear instant before the ink took even the knowing that he had once known things — he understood the oldest rule of the Meridian Guild, the one carved into the foundation stone beneath the sigil of the Spire, the one he had walked over every morning for forty years:

*Every debt has a name.*

His had simply taken longer than most to be called.

* * *

Outside, in the square, the crowd parted around two figures running north — a storm-caller with a dead god's keystone strapped to their back, and a warden with a sword she no longer believed in, moving together through the starless dark toward the one place in the world that was still lit.

Atop the distant hill, impossibly, patiently, the Spire of Vows had begun to glow.
