## 9. The Spire of Vows

The Spire had no door. It had never needed one; the covenant was its own invitation, and for nine hundred years anyone who came to speak the oath had simply been *let in*. Now, at the base of a tower that rose into a sky with nothing left to hold it up, Ilse Vane pressed her palm against cold black stone and felt the wards read her like an unpaid bill.

"It's not opening," Sebastian said behind her.

"I noticed."

"Should I be worried that you said that the way you say 'the prisoner escaped'?"

Ilse turned. In the grey light of a starless dawn—every dawn was grey now, the whole world lit by some dim emergency of the heavens—Sebastian Reyes looked exactly as they had the night she'd found their name in the ward-log: tired, guarded, and pretending very hard to be a ledger-keeper who had wandered catastrophically far from their ledgers. The three keystone fragments hung in a satchel against their chest, and even through the leather she could feel them humming, out of tune with each other and everything else.

"The Spire doesn't recognize us," she said. "Because there's no oath to recognize us *by*. The covenant is dead, Sebastian. We're asking a corpse's house for shelter."

"Then why did it let us climb this far?"

She looked up. The stair wound around the outside of the tower, carved into the stone itself, ascending through dark that thickened as it went. Somewhere above them was the chamber where, every hundred years, a speaker had renewed the oldest contract ever written. Somewhere below them, back in the city, the Meridian Guild was still collapsing inward on itself, its enforcers scattered, its master dead among his ledgers.

"We're not climbing for shelter," Ilse said. "We're climbing to make a delivery."

* * *

They climbed for what felt like a day and might have been an hour. That was the thing about the Spire: time pooled in its corners like rainwater. Ilse counted her breaths instead, the way she'd been trained in the Warden's Watch, and after the ten-thousandth breath she stopped counting because Sebastian started talking, which was worse but preferable.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" they said, slightly winded. "The night I first hid my storm-gift. I was nineteen. A warden patrol came through the market square where I was working, and I shoved all that lightning down inside myself so fast I gave myself a nosebleed. And I remember thinking, *this is temporary. I'll hide until it's safe.*"

"How long ago was that?"

"Fifteen years." A pause. "Give or take the ones Corvane stole from my file."

Ilse said nothing. She had been the one holding the exile order, fifteen years ago. She had delivered it to a young storm-caller with shaking hands and a face she could not let show anything, because wardens were not permitted attachments, and she had already failed that examination thoroughly. Some part of her had assumed, all these years, that Sebastian hated her for it. Another part—the part she kept in a locked drawer—had hoped they understood she'd had no choice, and had spent every year since checking the border reports for their name the way other people checked weather.

"Sebastian," she said.

"Don't." They didn't look back. "If you apologize on this stair, I'll have to forgive you, and then we'll both get sentimental, and I need my strength for whatever's waiting at the top. I'm rationing my emotions. Guild policy."

Despite everything, Ilse laughed. It came out ragged. "You've been spending too much time around ledgers."

"I've been spending fifteen years around ledgers. They're excellent company. They never pretend to feel things they don't." Sebastian's voice dropped. "Unlike some people."

The stair ended.

* * *

The chamber at the top was round and open to the sky, if you could call it a sky. There was only darkness up there now—a vast, textured dark, like velvet stretched over something enormous and breathing. The floor was inscribed with the covenant in letters older than any alphabet Ilse knew, and in the center stood a pedestal of black glass, and beside it, waiting with the patience of a creditor, stood the thing wearing her lover's dead mentor's shape.

It looked like Magister Odile Hark exactly as Sebastian must have remembered her: silver braid, ink-stained fingers, that particular half-smile of a woman who knew where every body in a room was buried. Only her eyes were wrong. They held no reflection at all.

"You brought them," it said to Ilse. "How efficient. I do appreciate a debtor who delivers their own collateral."

"We had a trade," Sebastian said, stepping forward. "The fragment for my gift. That was the bargain."

"That was the *principal*," the entity said gently. "There is also interest."

Ilse's hand went to her blade before her mind caught up. "You said nothing about interest."

"I said nothing about many things. Silence is free. Everything else has a price." The thing that wore Odile Hark's face turned those empty eyes on Sebastian, and its smile widened by a degree that hurt to watch. "You wish to speak the oath. But look at your fragments, storm-caller. Three pieces of the keystone, and they will not join. Why do you suppose that is?"

Sebastian drew them from the satchel: three shards of pale stone, each etched with the Spire's sigil. On the pedestal they lay side by side and refused each other, edges repelling like matched magnets. Ilse had watched Sebastian try to bind them with the last dregs of their storm-gift on the road, and watched the lightning gutter out against them like rain on oiled cloth.

"They need a speaker," Sebastian said.

"They need more than a speaker. They need someone *true*." The entity circled the pedestal slowly, ink-stained fingers trailing the air. "The covenant was never mere words. Every century, the speaker offered something real at the moment of speaking—a truth paid forward, a self made stake. Words without cost are just noise. Your Master Corvane learned that too late, hmm? He bought survival with someone else's collateral and thought the debt would find another ledger." Its smile flickered. "Debts always find their ledger."

"So what does it cost," Sebastian said flatly, "to speak it true?"

And Ilse knew—knew before the answer came, the way wardens know when a trap is sprung, from the sudden hush in the air and the way Sebastian's shoulders set. She grabbed their arm.

"No."

"It hasn't named a price yet," Sebastian said.

"It doesn't have to. I can see it in its *face*—"

"The gift," the entity said, pleasant as a banker. "Was already agreed. And the interest: your exile. All of it. Every year of hiding, every ledger-entry written under a false name, every small careful silence that made Sebastian Reyes the person who stands before me now. You built a life of invisibility, storm-caller. I am offering to buy it entire. Fair market value. You won't miss what you don't remember."

The words dropped into the chamber and rang there. Ilse heard her own breathing, harsh and too fast. Below them the city lay lightless; above them the hollow sky pressed down like a lid.

"There would be nothing left," she said. "Of you."

"There was nothing left anyway," Sebastian said quietly. "That was the problem, Ilse. Don't you see? Fifteen years I've been a ghost keeping books. I hid my gift, I hid my name, I hid—" their eyes found hers, and for once there was no deflection in them at all, no gentle humor standing guard— "I hid the fact that the only person I've ever loved was the one who had to arrest me. If I pay, I lose the memory of being miserable. I lose the ledgers. I lose the fear. What I keep is everyone else's sky."

"You won't keep me," Ilse said. Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and let it crack anyway. "You won't know me. I'll be a stranger at your bedside. Again. Except this time it's forever."

Sebastian smiled—that same crooked, terrible smile she'd fallen for across a ward-warden's desk fifteen years ago. "Then you'll just have to introduce yourself. You're good at paperwork. File a report. Be thorough."

"Sebastian—"

"Ilse Vane." They took her hands. Their fingers were cold; the storm in them was nearly spent, flickering like a candle in a draft. "I spent fifteen years learning that survival isn't the same as living. Let me finally spend it on something."

The entity extended Odile Hark's ink-stained hand toward the pedestal. "Time, children. The dark is patient, but not infinitely so, and my colleagues are already shopping in your city."

* * *

Afterward, Ilse could never have said how long the speaking lasted. Perhaps a minute. Perhaps a year.

Sebastian laid their hands on the three fragments and began the old words—and Ilse realized, with a lurch, that they weren't reading from the inscription. They were speaking from memory. Of course they were. Fifteen years of ledgers; of course they had memorized the covenant the moment they'd found it, in the sealed archive, in another life.

*"I speak for the mortal realm, which borrows its light..."*

The fragments shivered.

*"I speak knowing that all debts are real, and all debts are mine..."*

The fragments slid together—not smoothly, but the way broken bones knit, grating and reluctant—and the sigils on their surfaces began to burn.

*"Let the stars keep faith with us, and we with them, until the last speaker fails or the last debt is paid."*

The entity leaned close, and its voice was almost kind. "Payment, please."

Sebastian closed their eyes.

Ilse watched it happen. Watched the storm leave them first—it rose off their shoulders like steam, all that caged lightning rising in a bright column that the reassembled keystone drank down greedily. Then the memories followed: she saw them go, somehow, flickering behind Sebastian's face like pages turning faster and faster. The market square at nineteen. The exile order in her hands. The Meridian Guild's door, and the first ledger, and the second, and the thousand quiet nights of bottled thunder and borrowed shadows. Fifteen years of a hidden life, paid out like coin from a purse, and Sebastian's expression grew steadily simpler, steadier, younger—

And then it was done. The keystone blazed white on the pedestal, whole at last, and somewhere impossibly far above them the velvet dark tore open, and the stars came home.

They came back all at once, a flood of silver, and the light fell through the open chamber and caught Ilse's face and Sebastian's, and Sebastian blinked up at the sky with the unguarded wonder of someone seeing it for the first time.

"Oh," they said softly. "Oh, that's *lovely*. Has that always been there?"

And Ilse Vane, warden of the Watch, veteran of twenty years of impossible borders, sat down on the floor of the Spire of Vows and wept like the last speaker failing, while beside her a stranger with familiar hands patted her shoulder awkwardly and asked, with genuine concern, whether she needed to sit down, and then, noticing that she already was, whether she perhaps needed some water.

The entity was gone. Paid in full, or satisfied, or simply gone shopping elsewhere. The keystone hummed on the pedestal, warm as a hearthstone.

"Are you all right?" Sebastian asked her, after a while. "Only—you were here for the oath, I think? I remember the words, oddly, but nothing else. It's like a book with the first chapters torn out." They frowned, searching her face with eyes that held no history of her at all. "Do I know you?"

Ilse wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Above them, the stars burned; below them, the city was beginning to light its lamps again, one by one, like a slow heartbeat returning.

"No," she said. "But you're going to. My name is Ilse Vane, and I have a great deal of paperwork for you, and—" she took a breath, and reached for the blank page waiting in front of her— "and I think we should start at the beginning."
