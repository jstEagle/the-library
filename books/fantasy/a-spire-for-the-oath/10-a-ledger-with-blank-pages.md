## 10. A Ledger with Blank Pages

The first thing Sebastian noticed was the light.

It came through a small window in slanted gold bars, and it moved — slowly, patiently, the way light was supposed to move. Sunlight. Which meant there was a sun. Which meant, somewhere beyond that sun, stars had come home.

Sebastian lay still for a long while and let that be enough.

They were in a narrow room they did not recognize: whitewashed walls, a quilt patched in three different fabrics, a chipped blue pitcher on the sill. Their body ached the way a house aches after a storm has passed through it — nothing broken, everything settled differently. And inside their chest, where the storm had lived for as long as they could remember—

Nothing. A quiet like an empty drawer.

They reached for it out of habit, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, and found only smooth silence. No thunder coiled at the base of the spine. No static prickling under the skin when tempers flared. Just Sebastian, ordinary as bread.

They should have wept. They suspected, dimly, that some part of them had already done all its weeping, and had been paid for it in full.

There was a ledger on the chair beside the bed. That, at least, felt familiar — a good solid book, leather-bound, corners worn soft by hands. But when they opened it, every page was blank. Not one entry. Not one impossible trade, not one bottled thunder, not one borrowed shadow accruing interest.

On the first page, in careful, unfamiliar-to-them handwriting, someone had written:

*Property of S. Reyes. Contents to be determined.*

Underneath, smaller:

*No rush.*

* * *

The stranger came in with soup.

She was tall, gray-eyed, wearing a warden's coat with the insignia half-unpicked from the shoulder, as though she'd started removing it and then changed her mind about what to be. Her hair was tied back in a way that suggested efficiency rather than vanity. She carried the bowl with both hands, carefully, the way you carry something that matters more than soup.

When she saw Sebastian awake, she stopped in the doorway. Something crossed her face — grief, relief, and a third thing wearing the coat of the other two — and then it was gone, packed away with a soldier's tidiness.

"You're up," she said.

"I'm up," Sebastian agreed. "Where is this?"

"Harborwatch. A friend's cottage. She owed me a favor." The stranger set the bowl on the nightstand. "You've been asleep four days."

"Four days." Sebastian tried to reach back before the sleep and found fog. Before the fog, more fog, arranged in slightly different patterns. They knew things — they knew what a ledger was, knew the weight of a pen, knew the taste of strong tea and the sound of rain on glass. But the knowing had no walls around it, no rooms, no years. It was knowledge without an address.

"I should warn you," the stranger said, sitting down on the chair with the deliberateness of someone who had rehearsed this, "that the healers say your memory may come back in pieces. Or not at all. What you paid—"

"What did I pay?"

She looked at them for a long moment. Whatever she found in Sebastian's face made her voice gentler.

"The storm-gift," she said. "And the years of your exile. Every one of them. You stood at the top of the Spire and gave them away like a man settling a tab."

"Did I get a receipt?"

Something startled loose in her expression — a laugh, quickly caught and put down, but Sebastian had seen it, and filed it away with the strange possessive satisfaction of a collector.

"You joked about that too," she said quietly. "Right before. You said—" Her jaw worked. "You said you hoped the afterlife kept better books than the Guild."

"Do I know you?"

"No," said Ilse Vane, who had loved them for nineteen years, and whose name Sebastian would not learn until the soup was finished. "Not anymore."

* * *

Recovery, it turned out, was mostly domestic.

Sebastian's mind was a guild archive with the labels torn off. They knew how to read three languages but not which cities they'd learned them in. They could mend a hem, argue a contract, and gut a fish, but when Ilse mentioned *the Meridian Guild*, the word landed in them like a coin dropped down a well — they heard it fall, but never the splash.

Ilse came every day. She claimed, at first, that it was professional duty: wardens monitored covenant-breakers, oath-speakers, persons of interest. Then she admitted the warden's watch had politely retired her, since the wards no longer needed watching and she had developed what her superiors called *an attitude incompatible with institutional trust*. After that she claimed she simply liked the harbor, which was a lie so transparent that Sebastian let her keep it, because a person should be allowed one comfortable lie in a rebuilt life.

She taught Sebastian themselves, piece by piece, with the patience of a woman cataloguing a shipment of very fragile cargo.

"You take your tea bitter," she said. "You always complained about it and never once sweetened it. You hum when you write — badly. You apologize to furniture."

"That last one seems like a character flaw."

"It's a *feature*. You apologized to a door in Vell, once, after kicking it open during a firefight. Apologized mid-firefight. The man shooting at us hesitated just long enough—"

"We were in a *firefight*?"

"Several." Ilse's mouth did something complicated. "You were different, then. Louder. The storm was loud in you." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I think you might like who you are now. I'd like the chance to find out."

Sebastian considered the blank ledger on the shelf, waiting.

"And if I don't remember you?" they asked. "Ever? You'd stay anyway? Watching some stranger fumble with your whole history?"

"I've read every book in my possession twice," Ilse said. "I could stand to have one rewritten."

* * *

They began keeping entries.

Not trades — neither of them was foolish enough to reopen that particular account — but records. Small debts, honestly incurred.

*Day 9: Taught S. the names of the constellations. They were returned; the names were not. S. cried at the Pleiades, and pretended it was the wind. There was no wind.*

*Day 14: S. apologized to a chair. Confirmed: feature, not flaw.*

*Day 21: Walked the seawall. S. asked why I stayed. Told the truth, finally, all of it — the exile I enforced, the orders I hated, the years I spent being angry at them instead of the man who deserved it. S. listened without blinking. Then said: "He sounds terrible. Also, do you want to have dinner?" Nineteen years, and it turns out the hard part was the asking. Note: dinner went well. Note: understatement.*

Sebastian wrote that last entry themselves, over Ilse's objections on grounds of editorial integrity.

Some nights the past surfaced on its own — a dream of ledgers stretching away into darkness, a voice like dry paper saying *every debt comes due*, a flash of green light and someone shouting their name across a collapsing hall. Ilse would find them sitting up in the dark and sit beside them, not touching unless touched, and tell them what the fragments meant until the fragments knitted into something bearable.

"You bought the sky back," she said once, into the quiet. "Whatever else was taken from you, you should know that. On the worst night of my life, you held the whole world together with your bare hands, and then you let go of it, and it *held*."

"Sounds unlike me."

"It's exactly like you," Ilse said. "Both versions of you. That's the part I intend to prove."

* * *

On the ninetieth day, they walked to the headland above Harborwatch, where the grass grew silver in the salt wind, and waited for dark the way you wait for a friend off a late ship.

The sun went down. The east went bruised, then black.

Then, one by one, in no particular hurry — the way old debts are honored when both parties intend to keep meeting — the stars came out.

Sebastian counted them. Not all of them; nobody could. But they counted some, and found the counting itself was enough, an act performed each century at the top of a Spire by whoever loved the world enough to pay for it.

"There you are," Sebastian said softly, to all of it.

Beside them, Ilse slipped something out of her coat. The ledger. Blank pages no longer; ninety days of small entries, in two hands now, because lately Sebastian had been writing in it too.

"I brought you a present," she said.

"You didn't."

"I did. Page one. Read it again."

Sebastian opened the book. Below the old inscription, beneath *no rush*, a second line had appeared in Ilse's square warden's script:

*Amended terms: contents to be written jointly. Duration: indefinite. Interest: compounding daily. Signed—*

The signature space was empty.

Ilse held out the pen.

Sebastian took it, and laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of them, rolling down the headland toward the patient sea — and understood, in a way that needed no memory at all, that somewhere in the hollowed-out years there had been another laugh exactly like it, and that losing it had cost the universe something too, and that tonight it was being restored to circulation.

They signed.

Far overhead, past the wards that no longer needed them, past the Spire standing clean and pale on the horizon like a candle left burning for a traveler expected home, a star that had watched the whole affair flickered — once, twice — in what any honest accountant would have been forced to record, under *miscellaneous income*, as applause.

"Come inside," Ilse said. "It's cold, and I owe you dinner. Day twenty-one. It's in the ledger."

"Then I suppose," Sebastian said, closing the book on its hundred blank pages and its handful of full ones, "we'd better start making good on our debts."

They walked down together, two small figures under an enormous reconciled sky, leaving the rest of the pages for morning.
