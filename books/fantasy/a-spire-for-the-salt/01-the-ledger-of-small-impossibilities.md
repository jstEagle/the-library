## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The trouble with impossible things, Orsen Villalobos had learned by her third year keeping the Saltspire Guild's books, was that they resisted arithmetic.

A jarred thunderclap was not a jar. It did not weigh like a jar, spoil like a jar, or break like a jar—though it could break, and had, once, in the back of a mule cart on the Coast Road, and the mule had never again been persuaded to walk past the warehouse district without trembling. A jarred thunderclap was grief made portable, and grief did not sit neatly in columns.

Orsen dipped her pen and wrote the entry anyway.

*One (1) thunderclap, coastal, late-summer vintage, sold to M—, widow, Harbor End. Price: forty crowns silver and one favor unnamed.*

The favor unnamed was the hard part. Favors accrued interest in ways coin never could. She would have to remember to check the widow's token before quarter-day, to make sure the debt hadn't grown teeth.

Around her, the counting room went about its morning the way a ship goes about its rigging: quiet, constant, everyone knowing their line. Tallow candles guttered in brackets because Corvane Dhal distrusted lamplight—"light you can't carry is light that owns you," he liked to say, which was the kind of proverb that sounded like wisdom until you noticed how much of his philosophy was just thrift wearing a cloak. Below the floor, the tide worked against the pilings of the Sunken House, a slow knuckle-cracking sound that Orsen had long since stopped hearing, except at night, when it was all she heard.

She turned to the next page.

*Three (3) days borrowed youth, traded against fisherman's luck, client: J. Tanner, the *Pale Gull*. Balance outstanding: two days, four months.*

That one still bothered her, and it was three weeks cold. Borrowed youth was guild stock like any other—there were three donors in Saltspire City who sold their surplus springs and summers, and there was always a buyer with a mirror and a grudge against time. But this entry read wrong. The youth had gone out; the luck had come in; the luck had been resold to a merchant captain before the ink dried. And yet the ledger showed an imbalance of two days and change, as if the trade itself had skimmed a little off the top, the way water finds a lower place.

Objects remembered what happened to them. That was Orsen's secret, the one she kept folded under her tongue the way other people kept prayers. The ledger was calfskin and thread and iron-gall ink, and it remembered being written, but it also remembered being *touched*, and beneath the touches it remembered—faintly, the way a wall remembers smoke—the hands that had passed over it during the trade. Two days of youth had gone somewhere. The ledger knew where. It simply couldn't say so in words she could put in a column.

She laid her palm flat on the page. Old habit, disguised as smoothing paper.

The memory came up through the leather like cold through stone: a counting table, candle-smell, and a third hand on the book that did not belong to her or to J. Tanner. Small hand. Ring of some kind, cool metal, worn smooth. A sensation like swallowing a river stone—something swallowed whole and sitting heavy.

Orsen lifted her hand. Wrote, after a moment: *Discrepancy noted. Under inquiry.* Which was guild language for *I am looking into this and I have not yet decided who to tell.*

She was good at that language. She had invented half of it.

* * *

The Saltspire Guild traded in impossible things, and its genius—or Corvane's genius, since the guild had been six smugglers and a leaky boat before him—was that it treated impossibility as inventory. Bottled storms from the squall-farmers off the Teeth. Names that had never been spoken, purchased from their non-owners and used to open houses that had never been built. Years, luck, shadows, sleep, the last breaths of the famous, the first words of the dead. If it could be held, it could be held wrongly, and if it could be held wrongly, it could be sold.

And every sale passed through Orsen's books.

That was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement since she was fourteen years old and Corvane had found her half-starved on the Coast Road with a stolen apprentice's coat and a gift she'd confessed to no one. He had asked her exactly one question: *What can you do?*

*I can tell what things have been near,* she had said, which was a lie by subtraction. What she could do was touch an object and know what it remembered—and objects, unlike people, remembered everything. Every hand, every heat, every fear soaked into wood and metal and paper and stayed. A knife remembered its murders. A wedding ring remembered the argument in the next room. A lock of hair remembered whose neck it had been cut from and whether the cutting was tender.

Corvane had considered this for the length of three heartbeats, then said, "You'll keep my ledgers. Nobody lies to my ledgers."

It had sounded, at fourteen, like rescue. At twenty-six, it sounded mostly the same, which Orsen told herself was the point.

She heard him before she saw him—that was another thing about Corvane Dhal; he moved through the Sunken House like the tide, announced only by the way other sounds rearranged themselves around him. The clerks straightened. The candle flames leaned.

"Villalobos." He set a wrapped bundle on her table without ceremony. He was a broad man going soft at the edges, silver threading his beard, dressed the way he always dressed: well enough to be trusted, poorly enough to be underestimated, which in the free cities was the entire art of power. "New acquisition came in on the night tide. I want it cataloged before anyone else handles it. You know how it is—every hand that touches a thing leaves a print, and prints muddy provenance."

"Everything leaves a print," Orsen said. "That's rather the problem with the world."

"That's rather the profit," Corvane said, and unwrapped the bundle himself, because whatever it was, he didn't trust even his own people to unveil it.

* * *

It was a door.

Not a doorframe, not a door-shaped crate, though it resembled both. A slab, tall as a man and half again as wide, maybe four fingers thick, and pale—a white that wasn't paint or lime but substance, like the color of bone that has never been in the dark. When the wrapping came away, the counting room went quiet in a way Orsen felt in her back teeth. Even the tide below seemed to pause between knocks.

"Salt," Corvane said. "Solid salt, cut as one piece, from somewhere it shouldn't have come. The seller wouldn't say where. The seller took payment in silence, which tells you the quality of what he was selling."

"Where is it now?" Orsen meant the seller. Corvane smiled the way he did when a subordinate asked a question he'd decided not to answer.

"Doesn't matter. What matters is what it is." He ran a thumb along the slab's edge and Orsen watched, with the particular alertness she brought to all of Corvane's gestures, as he flinched—not from sharpness. From something else. "Listen."

"I don't hear anything."

"No," Corvane agreed. "But you will. You hear things the rest of us only feel. Catalog it, Villalobos. Weight, dimensions, composition, condition. And—" he paused at the doorway of the counting room, and for a moment he looked older than his years, and hungrier than his means, "—tell me what it remembers. I know you can. Don't insult us both."

He left. Orsen stood alone with the door that was not a door.

She should write the entry first. Procedure first, always; procedure was the moat around everything she had built here, eleven years of careful, unremarkable, forgettable competence. *Item: door, salt, provenance unknown.* Her pen touched paper.

Then she set the pen down, crossed the room, and laid both palms against the salt.

Cold, first. Cold like deep water, cold like the far side of a wall in winter. And then—

*a country*

—not a picture, not a scene, but the *fact* of a country, arriving in her the way a name arrives when someone says it across a crowded room. Vael. She had never heard of Vael and she knew it instantly, knew it the way you know a word in a language you thought you'd forgotten: a country of terraced shores and white spires, of harbors where the tide came in twice at once, of doors carved from the living salt-cliffs so that leaving a house and entering the sea were the same gesture. A country that had been *unmade*—not destroyed. Unwritten. Struck from maps, from charts, from the memory of treaties, until the world's accountants had balanced it away to nothing.

Except it hadn't balanced. Something had been left over. Something had been left over the way a column refuses to sum, and the leftover was humming under her palms, low and tidal, patient as sediment.

The salt knew it was a door. That was the worst of it. This was not a slab shaped like a door. This was a door doing an extremely patient impression of a slab, waiting, the way the guild's jarred storms waited, for someone to pay the price of opening.

Orsen snatched her hands back. Her heartbeat was wrong—too fast, too loud, loud enough that she checked the doorway to see if anyone had heard it. No one. Good. Concealment was a muscle; you kept it strong by using it.

She returned to her chair. She wrote, in a hand that trembled only slightly and only at the downstrokes:

*Item 44: Door, salt, monolithic. Provenance withheld. Composition: mineral salt, undissolved, age indeterminate. Condition: sound. Notable properties: acoustic anomaly (low-frequency resonance, source unidentified). Recommended storage: dry, secured, restricted access.*

Then, beneath, in the private shorthand only she read:

*It remembers a country that isn't on any map. It wants to be opened. Do not touch it again.*

She looked at the last line a long moment. Then, because she was who she was—an accountant of impossibilities, a woman whose entire survival rested on knowing which truths belonged in which book—she did not strike it.

She blotted it, and closed the ledger, and told herself the small cold feeling under her ribs was only hunger, and went to find something to eat.

* * *

Evening brought the tide and the clients.

The Sunken House did its business after dark, as honest businesses did not. Orsen worked the intake desk twice a week because Corvane said a guild whose clients never saw the ledger-keeper was a guild that would one day find its ledger-keeper had robbed it blind. So she sat behind the grate in the anteroom, lamp trimmed low, and received the free cities' appetite one whisper at a time.

Tonight it was the widow from Harbor End, come back about her thunderclap.

"He won't answer," the widow said. She was perhaps sixty, dressed in mourning that had graduated from fashion into habitat, and she held a squat glass jar wrapped in black cloth as if it were an infant. "I bought it to hear him once more. My husband's voice was—he had a laugh like weather coming in. The broker said a storm remembers, and if I opened the jar on the anniversary, I'd get a piece of that storm, and in the storm I'd hear—"

"Madam," Orsen said gently, "the item functions as described. Jarred thunderclap, coastal vintage. We stand behind our inventory."

"It's not that it doesn't work." The widow's hands tightened on the jar. "It works too well. There's a voice in it. Not his. Someone calling a name, over and over, out on the water. And the name—it's *mine*. My maiden name. No one living knows it. My husband didn't know it."

Orsen kept her face in the neutral configuration she reserved for difficult clients, and behind the neutral configuration her mind did what it always did: reached for the ledger, reached for the entry, reached for the memory the jar would hold. She extended her hand. "May I?"

The widow passed it over. Glass, cold, faintly vibrating, the way a struck bell vibrates long after the ear gives up.

Orsen touched it and the storm rose in her: rain-hammer, green-black sea, and a voice—a man's voice, ragged with salt-spray, shouting a name into wind. Not the widow's name. Older. The name of a woman dead fifty years, shouted by a drowning sailor at a wave that was not listening, and the storm had *kept* it, the way storms keep everything, and the guild's brokers had bottled the storm's anger without ever listening to what the anger was saying.

And underneath that—deeper, the way the salt door had been deeper—

*a country. Vael. Terraces. White spires. Twice-tides.*

Orsen set the jar down very carefully, as if it might notice being noticed.

"The item is genuine," she heard herself say. "The voice is part of the storm's history. I'm authorized to offer a partial refund or an exchange."

The widow took the jar back and held it differently now—less like an infant, more like a letter from someone she'd wronged. "No," she said slowly. "No, I'll keep it. Someone should hear their name said, even—" She stopped. Paid nothing further, owed nothing further, and left, and Orsen watched her go and thought: *that is what we do. We sell people the echoes of things, and we never ask what the echo is still carrying.*

"You're troubled."

Sefa leaned in the anteroom doorway, arms crossed, fire-gifted and young and permanently amused by Orsen's solemnity, holding a candle she never needed. "You've got your audit face on. The one where you've found a discrepancy and you're deciding whether it's a mistake or a crime."

"I always look like this."

"You do," Sefa agreed cheerfully. "That's why it's useful." She pushed off the doorframe. "Boss wants the intake log. Also—" she lowered her voice, glancing at the empty anteroom, "—he's got the new piece moved up to the third landing. Behind a locked door. He's posted Rennick on it. Rennick. Who hates everyone. Whatever's in that salt, Villalobos, he wants it *kept*."

Orsen wrote the intake log. She totaled the evening's accounts. She climbed to her narrow room above the counting floor, lit no lamp—for light you can't carry is light that owns you, and besides, she could read by touch better than most people read by eye—and took out the second ledger.

Every keeper of accounts kept two. The first went to the guild. The second went to herself: dates, entries, discrepancies, the running record of everything she noticed and chose not to say. It was her moat within the moat, proof against the day someone finally asked what Orsen Villalobos actually was. *Runaway apprentice,* she would say, and produce eleven years of dull, meticulous, forgettable diligence as evidence.

Tonight she wrote:

*Thunderclap contains a name and a country. Salt door hums. Both remembering Vael. Item 44 wants opening. C.D. wants it cataloged fast, stored private, guarded hard. He has never guarded anything he didn't intend to sell.*

She stopped. Read that last sentence back. In eleven years she had never once written a suspicion of Corvane Dhal in this book or any other, because suspicion written down becomes evidence, and evidence has a way of finding readers.

Her pen hovered.

Under the entry, in the smallest hand she owned, she added: *Inquiry ongoing.*

Below the window, the tide knocked at the pilings. On the third landing, behind Rennick's sullen vigil, the salt door hummed its patient note—and Orsen lay in the dark and realized, with the clarity she usually only got from touching things, that she had felt that note before. Faintly. For years. In the harbor stones. In old coins. In the calfskin of her own ledgers.

The country that wasn't on any map had been leaving traces in her accounts all along.

She just hadn't known what column to put them in.
