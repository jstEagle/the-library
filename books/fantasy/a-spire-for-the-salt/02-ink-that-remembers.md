## 2. Ink That Remembers

The slab arrived on a Tuesday, in the belly of a grain barge that had no business carrying grain.

Orsen knew this because she kept the business of every barge in the free cities, or close enough to it, and the *Merrow's Tithe* was listed in her ledgers as running salt-pork out of Halloway Inlet. Instead it had come up the Siltcanal at dead low tide, riding high and wrong, and its manifest—written in Corvane Dhal's own cramped hand, which he used for nothing—declared six casks of pickled herring.

Six casks of herring did not require eight stevedores to carry them ashore slowly, sweating, swearing at each other in the low whistles of the dock gangs, taking turns under a single littered frame that had been wrapped in oilcloth and then wrapped again.

Orsen watched from the counting-room window with her pen still wet on the day's entries. Below, the guildyard of the Saltspire looked like any other merchant yard in Brackmaw: crates of contraband lace stamped as wool, a boy scrubbing blood off the cobbles from last night's disagreement over dice, gulls screaming their ownership of everything. The stevedores set the wrapped thing down by the old smokehouse, stood around it a moment as if waiting for it to apologize, and drifted away without being paid.

That was the detail that made her put down her pen. Dock gangs did not work for free. Dock gangs worked for free when Guildmaster Dhal told them what they were carrying, and when knowing was payment enough—or danger enough.

She gave it an hour. She finished the herring entry, invented a plausible weight for six casks of fish that did not exist, and balanced the column so cleanly that any crown auditor who ever read it would believe in that herring harder than he believed in his own mother. Then she took her catalog case and went down into the yard, because if there was a new acquisition, someone would have to catalog it, and that someone was always her.

Corvane was already at the smokehouse, standing over the thing with his sleeves rolled, looking—not at it, she noticed. Around it. The way a man looks at a room where a fight has just happened, checking for witnesses.

"Guildmaster."

"Villalobos." He didn't startle. He never startled; it was one of the things she had catalogued about him over seven years, in a private inventory she kept nowhere but her own skull. "Good. You're quicker than sending for you. Come look at this and tell me what we owe the man who sold it to us."

The oilcloth came away in folds stiff with brine, and underneath was salt.

Not a block of harvested salt, not a market-stall chunk with hay pressed into its faces. A slab the height of a tall man and half again as wide, shaped—deliberately, with a mason's patience and a mason's arrogance—into a door. Frame and lintel and leaf, all one piece, all one substance, white as the inside of an egg and faintly translucent at the edges where the morning light went through it like light through ice. There were no hinges. There was no keyhole. There was a seam where a door opens, cut clean as a wound, and around the whole perimeter ran a border of lettering worn nearly smooth, in a script Orsen had never seen and yet, in the first mad half-second before sense reasserted itself, felt she could almost read.

"It's salt," she said, because someone had to say something stupid so the yard could go on breathing.

"It's salt," Corvane agreed. "Weighs like lead. Came off a wreck forty miles past the Silt Bar, in water no wrecker admits to working. The captain who pulled it up wanted three hundred crowns." His mouth did something at the corner. "I talked him into eighty and a bottle of the good brandy."

"And he threw in the fear," Orsen said. "For free."

Corvane looked at her then, fully, and she saw him decide not to pretend. That was the arrangement between them, the one they had never spoken aloud: he knew there was something in her, she knew he knew, and neither of them named it, because naming things made them real and real things went in ledgers.

"He was pale," Corvane allowed. "His crew wouldn't sleep below decks with it. Said it hummed."

"Salt doesn't hum."

"No," said Corvane. "So touch it and tell me why I shouldn't sell it to the Kellish antiquarians for a fortune and be rid of it."

He said it lightly. He watched her like a man laying bait.

She should have refused. Seven years in the Saltspire Guild had taught her a hundred small refusals: don't handle the bottled storms, don't ask where the borrowed years come from, don't let anyone see you hold an object longer than curiosity requires. Her gift was small—she'd told herself *small* so many times the word had worn smooth as a river stone. Objects spoke to her, that was all. Not voices, exactly. Memories. Press a knife long enough and it remembered cutting; hold a wedding ring and feel, dimly, somebody else's joy going stale. A parlor trick. A bookkeeping talent, really. She read objects the way she read ink, and she had built a life on making sure nobody ever asked what else she might write.

But the door-shaped slab sat in the yard like an unopened letter addressed in a hand she almost knew, and Corvane was watching, and pride is a cheaper sin than most.

She laid her palm against the salt.

It was cold. Not winter-cold. Well-cold, cellar-cold, the cold of deep places that have never once been warm—and it *hummed*, God help her, it hummed, not a sound through the air but a sound through her bones, up her arm and into her chest, a note so low it wasn't a note at all until suddenly it was a chord, and the chord was—

—the sea, but wrong. The sea under a sun too large and too pale, hanging in a sky the color of milk. A harbor city built of white stone, terraces stepping down to the water, every roofline curved like the hull of a ship that had decided to become a house. Gulls, yes, but gulls with dark heads, crying in a register she felt behind her teeth. And people. Thousands of people, moving through streets washed clean twice daily by the tides, wearing robes the color of kelp and rust, and every single one of them, every face the memory poured past her, carried or wore or simply *was* marked with that same worn lettering she'd seen on the frame—

*Vael,* said the salt, in the way objects say things, which is to say all at once and wordlessly and forever. *This is Vael.*

And the second thing it said, arriving a heartbeat behind like thunder after lightning, in a tone she had never felt from any object in her life, urgent and vast and afraid:

*Find me. Find me. We are not gone. Find—*

Orsen snatched her hand back and sat down hard on the cobbles.

The yard swung. Gulls resumed their proper color. Corvane's hand was under her elbow, steadying her, and his face was very close, and his expression was one she had never seen him wear: hunger, held carefully in both hands like a full cup.

"Well," he said softly. "There it is."

"There what is," she managed. Her mouth tasted of brine.

"You tell me. You went somewhere. I watched you go."

Seven years of concealment. Seven years of touching things briefly, casually, of laughing at fortune-tellers and spitting over her shoulder at charms, of being the guild's plain, dull ledger-keeper, invisible as paper. And she had thrown it away in four seconds flat because a rock sang to her.

"I read objects," she said. It came out flat, stripped of choice. "Sometimes. It's nothing. Knives remember cutting. Rings remember weddings. It's—"

"Nothing," Corvane agreed, gently, terribly. "Of course. And what did this nothing remember?"

She weighed it. She was good at weighing; it was her trade. But her hands were shaking, and the taste of brine was in her throat, and underneath everything, louder than caution, the salt's last syllable was still ringing in her bones: *we are not gone.*

"A country," she heard herself say. "A harbor city. White stone. Curved roofs. People with—" she gestured vaguely at her own collarbone, "—marks. Script. Like the border on this thing."

Corvane straightened. Something moved through his face like weather, and when it passed he was the guildmaster again, dry and pleasant and entirely opaque.

"Vael," he said.

The name dropped into the yard like a coin into a well. Every child in the free cities knew the story, or the shape of it: Vael, the ninth free city, the sea-city, which two generations ago—her grandfather's time, maybe further—had simply ceased. Not sacked, not sunk in any storm the ballads recorded. One season it was on every chart; the next season the charts were redrawn, the merchants' treaties voided, the surviving Vaeldish scattered and quiet, and within twenty years you couldn't find Vael on a map if you bribed a cartographer. Countries didn't vanish. Places didn't get *unmade*. And yet.

"They say it drowned," Orsen said. Her voice sounded far away to her.

"They say lots of things." Corvane turned back to the slab and looked at it now directly, head-on, the way he hadn't dared while she watched. "They also say the crown's new king has a hard-on for anything he can't tax. Which makes a door-sized piece of a vanished country either the most dangerous thing in Brackmaw or the most valuable, depending how quietly we move it." He glanced at her sidelong. "I want it catalogued. Full treatment. Provenance, composition, condition, every mark on it copied exact. You're the only one I trust with fine work."

"The stevedores wouldn't touch it."

"The stevedores are superstitious men who think a woman's monthly course can sour milk. You'll do it."

It was not quite an order. With Corvane it never was; orders left marks, and he was a man who kept his hands clean even of fingerprints. He clapped her shoulder once—a rare touch, brief, proprietary—and walked back toward the counting-house, calling for tea as if the morning were ordinary.

Orsen stayed by the smokehouse a long while.

She did not touch the slab again. She wanted to; that frightened her more than the shaking in her hands did. The memory had been vast and cold and beautiful, a whole city pouring through her like water through a net, and beneath the terror of it there had been a feeling she recognized with dread, because she had felt it only once before in her life, at fourteen, the night she ran:

Home. The objects had shown her home before. Never one like this. Never one that called.

She opened her catalog case instead and began, professionally, at the edge. Sketching the border script. Copying the worn letters stroke by stroke onto good paper, her pen moving with a fluency that appalled her—her hand knew these shapes. Not remembered them. *Knew* them, the way her hands knew knots she'd never been taught, the way she'd always known the smell of low tide meant safety, the way certain words in certain dreams came out already pronounced.

Her grandfather had died when she was nine. She remembered his hands, salt-cracked, humming tunelessly while he worked. She had assumed the humming was a habit.

Her pen stopped.

Two generations ago, Vael vanished. Two generations ago, her grandfather had been a young man. She had grown up inland, among people who asked no questions, in a family that answered none. She had run from something at fourteen—she had never let herself finish the thought of what, exactly, in seven years of careful bookkeeping—but she remembered the road, and the men following, and Corvane Dhal appearing out of the rain like a miracle with a ledger of his own, buying her debt off her pursuers with gold and a word in their ears, and asking nothing.

Asking nothing, she thought now, sitting on cold cobbles beside a door cut from a drowned country's stone.

Nobody asks nothing. Nobody buys a stranger girl off the road for free. She had spent seven years grateful instead of curious, because gratitude was warmer and curiosity, in the Saltspire Guild, got people fitted for weighted boots.

The salt hummed its bone-deep chord. Down at the canal, a bell rang the turn of the tide. Somewhere beyond the harbor, beyond the bar, beyond the edge of every map she kept in this office, a country that officially did not exist waited under a milk-white sun, saying *find me* in a voice only she could hear.

Orsen closed her sketchbook, and her hand was steady now, and that was worse.

"Full catalogue," she murmured to the door-shaped slab. "Fine work. Every mark exact."

The salt said nothing. But the hum changed pitch, just slightly, and she chose—with her eyes open, the way you choose to keep walking past a gate at night—not to wonder whether it was answering her, or calling to whatever else in Brackmaw might be listening.

She was halfway across the yard, sketchbook under her arm, when the first crown crier turned the corner by the fish-gates, hammer and nails in hand, and began nailing paper to doors.
