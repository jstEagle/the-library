## 3. A Proclamation Nailed to Every Door

The bells woke Orsen before the criers did.

Not the harbor bells, which rang in patterns she had learned to sleep through—tide-turning, fog-warning, a ship gone aground on the Teeth. These were the Spire bells of Brackmoor, and they rang flat, all struck together, the sound of a city being told to shut up and listen.

She was at her desk in the guildhouse's counting room before dawn, as always, with the ledger of small impossibilities open under her lamp. She had been halfway through reconciling the month's accounts: one jarred thunderclap sold to the widow Anselm for her husband's funeral, paid in grief measured to the ounce; three days of borrowed youth traded to a fisherman named Pell for a season's luck, the interest written in smelt. Ordinary business. The kind of business that kept a city breathing without knowing it breathed.

Then the bells.

She went to the window over the salt-market. The square below was filling—not fast, not panicked, but with the terrible orderly slowness of people who knew that whatever came next was not optional. Criers stood on the fountain steps in crown blue, and their voices carried in the dead air the way only rehearsed voices do.

"By decree of Aldric, First of His Name, rightful King of the Free Cities and All Territories Thereof—" *rightful*, Orsen noted, doing arithmetic even then; you only insisted on *rightful* when everyone knew otherwise— "the Edict of Cleansing is hereby proclaimed."

A woman beside Orsen at the window crossed herself with an old sign, two fingers to the breastbone, a gesture from a faith nobody practiced anymore. Orsen didn't move. She counted instead. It was what she did.

"First: every bearer of the gift, howsoever called or disguised, shall present themselves at any crown registry within thirty days of this proclamation, to be named, numbered, and licensed under the king's peace."

*Second*, said the criers, and Orsen felt the word arrive in her body before her ears finished it.

"Second: those who fail to register shall be accounted enemies of the realm. Their names shall be taken. Their houses searched. Their persons hunted by lawful authority until such time as they are brought to accounting."

Third concerned rewards. Fourth concerned penalties for harboring. Fifth concerned penalties for concealing records pertaining to bearers of the gift—and there, in the fifth clause, spoken in the same flat rehearsed voice as the rest, Orsen Villalobos's safe world tilted on its axis and did not right itself.

*Records.*

Her ledgers. Eleven years of them, bound in calfskin and salt-cured hide, shelved in the counting room behind her chair: every client, every trade, every impossible thing bought and sold in three free cities, entered in her small exact hand. The widow who bought a thunderclap because her husband had died deaf and she wanted him to hear something once. The fisherman who rented youth. The midwife who paid in warm bread for a charm against cold wombs. Every name. Every address. Every description of the gift rendered, because Orsen was thorough, in language precise enough to be assessed, taxed—

*Hunted.*

She had built the perfect instrument. She just hadn't known what it would be used to measure.

Down in the square, a man began to shout something about Aldric's mother and a goat, and the criers' guards took him by both arms, and that was the whole of the morning's rebellion. The crowd dispersed into the streets like water finding drains, and above every door they passed, hammering as they went, crown men nailed up the proclamation in black ink on yellow paper.

Orsen watched them nail one to the guildhouse door itself. Three strikes of the hammer. Then she turned from the window, walked downstairs past the sleeping apprentices, unlocked the counting room, sat down at her desk, and looked at eleven years of evidence.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake, and instead they lay flat on the ledger the way they always did, as if the books were simply waiting for instructions, as if paper were patient, as if paper could wait forever for someone to come and read it.

Paper could. That was the whole problem. Paper outlasted everything except fire, and fire was about to become the most valuable commodity in the free cities.

She heard Corvane Dhal's boots on the stair before she saw him—the uneven left heel, the pause at the top where he always gathered himself, as if climbing stairs were a negotiation he won daily through will alone. He came in carrying two cups of chicory and set one by her elbow without asking, which was his way of saying he'd been awake too.

"You've read it," he said.

"I've read it."

"The fifth clause?"

"Fifth clause." She turned the ledger so he could see it. He didn't look. He looked at her instead, with that gray attention that had made fourteen-year-old Orsen on the Salt Road feel—for the first time in her life—like a thing worth purchasing.

"How many names?" he asked.

"In the active books? Four hundred. Across all years, closer to two thousand. Some are dead. Some are untraceable." She swallowed. "Most are neither."

Corvane nodded slowly, and Orsen watched him think, and for the first time in eleven years she could not tell what he was thinking, and that frightened her more than the yellow papers on the doors.

"Nothing leaves this room," he said finally. "No burning, no moving, no cleverness. Do you understand me? The books stay exactly where they are, looking exactly as they look, until I say otherwise. Panic makes people do stupid things to good paper."

"And when you say otherwise?"

He drank his chicory. "When I say otherwise, Orsen, you'll be the first to know." At the door he paused, half-turned, and gave her the smile he gave clients—the warm one, the one that had closed a thousand bargains. "You've never done anything wrong in your life. Remember that, if anyone asks. You keep accounts. Accounts aren't crimes."

He went down the stairs. Orsen sat in the quiet with four hundred names and a cup going cold, and understood, in the wordless way she understood objects, that something had already gone wrong between them—that the guildmaster had walked into this room holding a decision she couldn't see, and had carried it back out again untouched.

* * *

Across the city, in the barracks behind the Provost's Court, Sergeant Ilsa Merrin read the Edict of Cleansing twice and put it face-down on her knee.

Around her, the squad was doing what squads do: joking too loudly. Voss was showing off a new whistle the criers' office had issued, silver, meant to summon registered hunters to a sighting. Dek was cleaning a weapon that didn't need it. Somebody said the word *pyre* and somebody else laughed, and Ilsa let the laughter wash past her the way she'd let twenty years of it wash past, and thought: *order is mercy.*

She had built her whole life on that sentence. Her father's tavern had burned in the Tithe Riots because there had been no order to speak of, just crowds and fear and a magistrate who fled. What saved cities was law. What saved the weak was procedure. A registry wasn't a pyre. A registry was a list, and a list meant the crown knew who you were and therefore had no reason to be afraid of you, and fear was the thing that actually killed people. She believed this. She had believed it so long it had gone smooth in her mouth like a river stone.

"Reading it again, Sarge?" Voss leaned over. "Looking for the loophole?"

"There are no loopholes," she said. "That's the point of law."

But there was a line. She'd found it on the second reading and couldn't stop finding it now, the way your tongue finds a broken tooth. Clause seven. *Registered bearers may be conscripted into crown service according to need.* Not arrested. Not licensed. Conscripted. And clause nine: *the assessment of need rests with the Office of the Hunt.*

So the registry wasn't a promise of protection. It was a harvest inventory.

"First sweep assignments," Lieutenant Hark called from the doorway, and the room came to its feet. "Merrin's unit takes the Warrens. Registry lists are in your packets. We start with voluntary registrations—hedge-witches, charm-sellers, anyone who filed. We verify, we mark, we move on. Any bearer not on the lists is to be reported, not engaged. Clear?"

Clear, they said. Ilsa opened her packet. Forty-one names in the Warrens alone. Forty-one people who had trusted the crown enough to walk into a registry office and write their gifts down in their own hands, believing—Ilsa could see it now, could see it the way you see a trick only after the card is turned—that writing it down made them safe.

The first address was a crooked house on Tallow Row that smelled of rosemary and old fat. The hedge-witch who answered was seventy if she was a day, hands like tree roots, and she'd laid out tea for three on the table before Ilsa had finished announcing herself, because of course she had. Of course she'd expected courtesy. She had *registered.*

"Sergeant." The old woman squinted at Ilsa's tabs. "You'll want me to demonstrate, I expect. They said verification." She held up her hands, palms out. Between them, obediently, a small flame stood up out of nothing, blue at the heart, no bigger than a moth. She warmed her knuckles over it, the way you'd warm them at any hearth. "Forty years I've lit the birthing-fire on Tallow Row. Every child on this street came home to a hearth I lit. Now it's in a book somewhere."

Ilsa wrote her verification mark. Procedure. She was aware, distantly, of Dek at the window and Voss checking the back room, though the warrant covered only the ground floor and the old woman's eyes followed Voss with something worse than fear—with patience, with the resignation of someone who had decided in advance not to fight whatever came.

"Where do we go after this?" the witch asked, conversationally, while Ilsa blotted the mark. "After registration. Is there a place, or is it just... a smaller and smaller room?" She smiled. She had three teeth. "My sister went to the registries in Carreth, under the old king. In spring they collected them. In summer there was a field outside the walls that smelled for a year."

"That was the old king," Ilsa said. "This is a new edict."

"Yes," the witch agreed pleasantly. "It's a new smell every time, my mother used to say. But it's the same wind."

At the door, Ilsa paused. There was a question of procedure here, a genuine one, and she asked it in the tone she used for procedure: "The registry requires next of kin or household associates. Your listing says none. Is that accurate?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere in the house a kettle was beginning, very softly, to sing.

"I have no family," the witch said. "Never had. No students either. Nobody visits me at all." And her eyes, mild as milk, told Ilsa Merrin with perfect clarity that every word of it was a lie, and that the lie had a name and an address, possibly several, and that the old woman was handing over the space where those names should go empty and trusting a stranger in crown blue to leave the emptiness alone.

Ilsa wrote *none confirmed* in the box.

In the packet, later, folding over the day's reports, she came across the witch's original registration form, filled out in a shaky hand. Under *known associates,* someone had begun to write a name—a first letter, a careful *B*—and then scratched it out and written *none.*

Sergeant Ilsa Merrin, twenty years a believer in lists, sat in the squad room with that form under her thumb and the whole machinery of the Edict humming around her, waiting for her mark.

She detached the corner of the page with the scratched-out letter, folded it small, and put it in her pocket, where no report would ever find it. Then she marked the form *verified, incomplete record, minor discrepancy noted*—a phrase that meant nothing to anyone, that would sit in a file unread, that was in its tiny way the first dishonest thing she had ever written in twenty years of honest paper.

*Order is mercy,* she told herself, walking home through streets papered yellow with proclamations, and for the first time the stone went down rough, catching on something on the way.

In her pocket, folded eight times, a single letter waited to become a person.

* * *

Back in the counting room, long past midnight, Orsen Villalobos dipped her pen.

She had spent the day not writing, which was its own kind of agony, and had spent the evening listening—apprentices dismissed early, Corvane locked in his tower with the guild's advocates, runners coming and going from the other free cities with news: Harlowe's guilds were burning their books outright. Veles was negotiating. Everyone, everywhere, was scrambling, and the Saltspire Guild was sitting on the largest map of gifted souls in existence while its guildmaster smiled at clients and said *wait.*

She could not burn the ledgers. Not yet. If the guild fell, the ledgers were leverage, ransom, insurance—if she burned them and the crown came anyway, there would be nothing left to bargain with, four hundred people stripped naked in a country gone mad. But she could not leave them intact either, because the crown's auditors were coming—everyone said so, auditors first, soldiers second—and her beautiful precise hand would hang them all.

There was a third thing. She had never tried it on anything larger than a coin.

She laid her palm flat on the current ledger and listened the way she listened to objects, letting the paper remember at her: ink drying, lamplight, her own voice dictating entries to herself, the widow Anselm's name, the fisherman Pell's name, four hundred lives pressed flat between covers.

She thought: *this entry was never made.*

The ledger shivered under her hand like a struck bell. Ink moved. The widow Anselm's purchase blurred to gray, then re-formed as a sale of ordinary pickled eels, dated wrong, priced wrong, dull as dishwater. And something went out of Orsen in the same instant—she groped for it and found a hole where a hole shouldn't be. The smell of the harbor on a specific morning, some years ago. Gone. She knew there had been a smell. She could not remember it.

So that was the price. Objects kept their memories, but memory was not free; it flowed like everything else in the world, and she was now upstream of her own life, diverting it page by page into other people's safety.

Four hundred names. She looked at the shelves—all those volumes, all those years—and understood with perfect clarity that she could not save the names and keep herself. It would be one or the other, paid out entry by entry, and she would have to choose, and she was already choosing, she realized; she was already dipping the pen.

She wrote the false entry for the fisherman Pell and lost the tune her mother used to hum while mending nets.

She worked until the candle drowned, and the last thing she erased that night was small and strange: the memory of why she had ever trusted Corvane Dhal. She could still remember *that* she had trusted him. The reason was simply, quietly, gone.

On the shelf, eleven years of ledgers waited in the dark, patient as paper, full of names that were no longer quite true—and somewhere below, nailed to the guildhouse door, King Aldric's proclamation lifted and rustled in the night wind like a living thing, like a hunter sniffing along the trail of ink.
