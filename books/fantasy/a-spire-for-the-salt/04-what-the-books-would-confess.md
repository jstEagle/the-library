## 4. What the Books Would Confess

The Edict had been nailed up for six days when Orsen opened the ledger to the entry for Maren Ostrey and found her own handwriting looking back at her like a stranger's.

Not that it had changed. That was the horror of it. The ink sat where she had left it, in her small precise hand, the letters leaning forward like people in a hurry: *M. Ostrey, fishwife, Quay Street. One luck-charm (river-sourced), paid in salt cod, 4 lbs. Delivered.*

A perfectly ordinary entry. The kind of line that appeared eleven hundred times across the Saltspire Guild's books, so unremarkable that no auditor would ever give it a second glance.

Except that Maren Ostrey had come to the guildhall nine days ago with her luck-charm in her fist and tears in her eyes, and had said, *I don't need a charm anymore, keeper. I need you to forget I was ever here.* And Orsen had taken her cod, written her entry, and watched her go, and only afterward—too late, the way everything important came to her too late—had she understood what Maren had known the moment she walked in.

The ledgers were a map. And the king's men had begun to read maps.

* * *

Orsen worked the night hours in the counting room above the guildhall's salt cellar, because that was when Corvane Dhal did not work, and Corvane Dhal noticed everything. The room was hers in the way that a bird's nest is the bird's: built from her, defended by her, but never owned. Three walls of shelving. A lamp with a whale-oil wick that burned without smoke because she'd paid a charm-smith for it years ago and then, in a fit of caution, written the purchase down as *candles, assorted.* A ledger stand, a stool, a pot of ink gone the brown of old tea.

And the books. Forty-one of them, going back nine years, every transaction the Saltspire Guild had ever made in the free cities of the coast. Bottled storms and borrowed years and names that had never been spoken aloud, all of it rendered down into her tidy columns, because that was the guild's genius and Corvane's first lesson to her: *anything can be smuggled, girl, if you write it small enough.*

She had thought it was a kindness at the time. A way to keep the impossible things honest.

Now she pulled the ninth ledger toward her and read the entry again, and this time she did not look away from what it said.

Because the entry did not say *luck-charm.* It said what she had actually sold Maren Ostrey: a small working, a whisper of the gift, traded at cost because Maren's husband had drowned and her son was sick and the guild's charity had a price like everything else. And the entry said so in words. *Minor gift-working, rendered. Client bears the gift herself—faint, hedge-grade. Paid in kind.*

*Client bears the gift.*

Six words. Under the Edict of Cleansing, six words were a pyre.

Orsen sat with her hands flat on the page for a long time, listening to the guildhall settle around her—the tick of the salt beams, the sea working at the pilings below, somewhere a drunk singing three streets over. Then she picked up her pen, dipped it, and did not write.

She stared at the blank margin instead, and thought about her mother.

Not on purpose. It was simply where her mind went, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. Her mother, who had read what objects remembered the way other women read weather. Her mother, who had taught her the trick of it in a kitchen that smelled of tallow: *hold it, girl, hold it soft, and ask it what it saw.* Her mother, who had been taken on a Tuesday in the autumn Orsen turned eleven, by men with the old king's badge, and never came back, and whose name Orsen had not spoken in fifteen years because a name spoken is a name findable.

She had run from that. All the way to Corvane Dhal and his guild of small impossibilities, where she had built a life out of arithmetic and anonymity, out of being the woman who wrote things down instead of the woman things were written about.

And now her own careful hand was going to hang Maren Ostrey.

* * *

Here is what Orsen Villalobos knew about her gift, which was not much, because she had spent fifteen years not learning more:

Objects remembered. Not everything, and not forever—a knife remembered its maker better than its hundredth buyer; a coin remembered the last hand that gripped it in anger. And she could read that memory, if she held the thing and asked it soft. Her mother's trick. The trick that got her mother burned.

What she had never known—what she learned that night, alone, with Maren Ostrey's entry open under the lamp—was whether the remembering went both ways.

She set down the pen. She pressed her palm flat against the page. The paper was cool, then warm, then it was *speaking*—the way it always spoke, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like iron and rain. The ledger told her what it remembered: lamplight, her own wrist, the scratch of nib on rag-paper, Maren's cod smell lingering in the room, the words going down, down, the words as they were made—

And Orsen, holding it soft, did not ask it what it saw.

She asked it to forget.

*Not the client. Not the sale. Only the six words. Only the truth of her. Make her a fishwife who bought a charm. Make it true.*

The resistance was immediate and strange—not refusal, exactly, but grief. The ledger did not want to lose the shape of what had happened to it. Memory, she understood in that moment, was not ink. Ink was only where memory slept. And you could wake a thing and change what it dreamed, but the dream had to come from somewhere.

It came from her.

She felt it go like a thread pulled out of cloth. Not pain—pain would have been honest, and this was worse, this was *subtraction.* She reached for the memory of Maren's face, the tears, the cod, the door closing behind her, and found the shape of it still there but the color gone, the sound gone, a room she had visited but never lived in. She reached for the taste of the rain that night and found nothing. She reached further, greedy with panic now, wanting to know the cost's edges, and found that the edges were the problem: the ledger had not taken one memory. It had taken the *category.* Maren Ostrey was becoming, in Orsen's head, a name attached to a column of numbers. A line. An entry.

The way Orsen had written her all along.

The ink on the page had changed. She could see it without her gift, ordinary eyes, lamplight: *M. Ostrey, fishwife, Quay Street. One luck-charm (river-sourced), paid in salt cod, 4 lbs. Delivered.*

No six words. No pyre. Just a woman who bought a charm, one of eleven hundred such women, invisible as salt in the sea.

Orsen took her hand off the page and sat shaking in the whale-oil light, doing the arithmetic she was built for, because arithmetic was the only prayer she knew.

*Cost: the memory of one client's face, one night's rain, one small kindness done. Benefit: one woman not burned.*

She had expected the arithmetic to be hard. It was not. That frightened her more than the cost had.

* * *

By the fourth night she had a system.

She worked from the oldest ledger forward, because the crown's auditors would start at the beginning if they started anywhere, and because she wanted to know how the cost changed with distance. It changed like this: the older the entry, the cheaper the edit. A memory from nine years ago was already half-gone; the ledger only needed to take the rest. Recent entries cost full price. And there was a third rate she discovered on the fifth night, the one she never wrote down anywhere, the one she kept only in the body that paid it: some things could not be edited at all, and if you tried, the ledger took something adjacent instead.

She learned that one on Tam Voss.

Tam Voss, cooper, Barrel Lane. Two years ago he'd traded the guild three days of borrowed youth for his daughter's fever to break, and the entry said so plainly: *gift-working, rendered; client hedge-grade, bears the gift; payment: 3 days youth (stored, jar 12).* Orsen held the page and asked it to make Tam a man who'd bought a fever-charm, and the ledger took the edit fine, smooth, cheap—and then it kept pulling, because she had not asked it to stop, because she had not known she needed to, and it went past Tam Voss into her own stores and took the memory of her mother's *voice.*

Not her mother's face. She'd checked that first, that night, cold with dread, and found it still there—tallow-smell, flour on knuckles, the crooked tooth. All of it. But the voice was gone. She sat in the counting room and reached for the sound of her mother saying her name and found a silence shaped exactly like a voice, and she understood that she had paid for Tam Voss's daughter's life with the only recording of it in the world.

She did not cry. Crying was noise, and noise was findable, and she had been unfindable for fifteen years and did not intend to stop.

But she made rules after that. Rules written nowhere, held only in the body that paid:

*One edit per entry. Ask for the minimum. Never hold the page longer than the edit needs. And never, ever reach past the edit to test the edges, because the edges were where she lived.*

* * *

The work went slowly, because the work had to look like nothing.

By day she kept the guild's ordinary business: the storm-jars sold to ship-captains who wanted fair weather held in reserve, the years-of-youth traded by old merchants to young bodies, the names—never spoken, only *held*—that certain clients paid for the way other men paid for absolution. Corvane came up twice in that week, stood in the doorway of the counting room with his grey beard and his eyes like coins, and looked at her shelves, and said, "Ledgers well, Villalobos?"

"Ledgers well, Guildmaster."

"Good girl." And he would go, and she would feel the look he'd given her shelves sitting on her shoulders for an hour after, like a hand.

Because that was the thing she could not say, even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull: Corvane Dhal had saved her. Bought her off the road at fourteen, fed her, taught her letters and figures and the beautiful discipline of writing the impossible small. He had never asked about the trick she could do with objects. He had never needed to. And she had repaid him with nine years of the finest bookkeeping on the coast, and she had told herself that was a fair trade between two people who understood that everything was.

Now she was robbing him. Entry by entry, night by night, she was reaching into his ledgers and taking the truth out of them, and every theft made the books less his and more a lie, and she could not stop, because the alternative was Maren Ostrey in a crown cart, Tam Voss in a pyre, and a line of names behind them as long as her columns.

*Guilt and resolve,* she thought once, absurdly, in her mother's cadence, *are the same muscle pulled in two directions.* She did not know where the thought had come from. Some of her mother was still in her. Some of her mother was in the ledgers, maybe, paid out across forty-one volumes like small change.

* * *

On the ninth night, the auditors came.

Not to the guildhall—nowhere near, not yet. But Sefa brought word, breathless and smelling of woodsmoke, the young fire-gifted runner whose talent Corvane officially did not know about and privately priced into every contract. "Two of them," Sefa said, in the salt cellar, among the storm-jars. "Crown men, grey cloaks, at the Harbormaster's office. They're *buying* shipping records, Orsen. Paying for copies. They said—" and here Sefa's voice dropped, and the jars around them hummed faintly, the way jars did when a storm inside them smelled fear, "—they said the guilds' own books would do half their work for them. That the smugglers write everything down."

After Sefa left, Orsen climbed to the counting room and stood in the doorway and looked at her forty-one volumes, her nine years, her life's work, and saw them for what they had become: not a map of the guild. A map of the hunted. Every hedge-witch, every luck-charmer, every child with a trick of finding lost things, all of it in her tidy hand, all of it legible, all of it *findable*—because she had believed that writing a thing small made it safe, and had never once asked who would hold the book.

She had perhaps a week before the grey cloaks worked their way to the Saltspire. Less, if Corvane decided to cooperate. Corvane, who believed in order the way she believed in ink.

Orsen lit the lamp, sat down, and opened the first ledger to its first page.

Forty-one volumes. Nine years. Hundreds of names, and every edit a piece of herself paid out like small change, and she was already half-spent and the work had barely begun. She thought of her mother's voice, gone. She thought of the taste of rain, gone. She thought of the long ledger of herself, thinning page by page, and made the only entry that mattered, the one she held in her body where no auditor could read it:

*They can have my memories. They cannot have my people.*

She dipped her pen.

She was three pages in when she heard the step on the stair—slow, deliberate, a step she knew—and Corvane Dhal filled the doorway of the counting room, and in his hand, open to a page she had edited two nights ago, was the seventh ledger.

"Villalobos," he said, in the voice he used for pricing things, "why does Maren Ostrey's entry not match her receipt?"
