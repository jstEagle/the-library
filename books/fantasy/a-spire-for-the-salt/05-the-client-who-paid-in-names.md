## 5. The Client Who Paid in Names

The client arrived at the hour the tide forgot.

That was how Orsen had learned to think of it—the slack water between turns, when the harbor went glassy and indifferent and nothing in Saltspire moved unless someone paid it to. The guild's best business was done in that dead hour, when the watchmen dozed and the lamplighters were still drunk from the night before and the sea itself seemed to look away.

Corvane Dhal had taught her that. *Nothing is hidden in darkness, Orsen. Darkness is where everyone looks. Hide things in the boring hours, and they'll stay hidden for a lifetime.*

She thought of his voice now as she descended into the counting room beneath the Saltspire's warehouse, her ledger satchel over her shoulder and a lamp swinging in her hand. She thought of it because she trusted it, and because trusting Corvane Dhal had become, over nine years, less a choice than a habit of the blood.

The client was already there.

He sat at the long trestle table with the stillness of a man who had been told precisely how long to wait and had decided to outlast the instruction. Masked—not the cheap half-masks of carnival season but a full face of lacquered tin, pale as fish belly, with slits for eyes and no mouth at all. It made him look like something dredged up. Like something the tide had returned against its will.

"Guildmaster said midnight," he said. His voice was muffled, flattened by the tin, and Orsen filed away what she could catch of it: educated. Coastal vowels worn smooth by years inland. A man who had worked to change how he spoke, which meant there was a before he didn't want found.

"The guildmaster," Orsen said, setting her lamp on the table, "is not coming. He sent me."

Silence. The eye-slits caught the lamplight and gave nothing back.

"I was told I would deal with Dhal himself."

"You were told wrong, or you're lying about being told. Either way, I'm what you get." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, arranging her satchel on her knees like a shield. "Passage costs what passage costs. If you'd rather deal with the crown instead, their rates are worse."

Another silence, longer. Then the tin face tilted—a small, grudging nod.

"Passage for one. Out of the free cities entirely. Not to Merrow or the Sable Coast—I want past them. Past everything."

*Past everything.* There was only one past-everything anyone whispered about since the Edict, and Orsen felt the salt slab upstairs hum faintly through the floorboards, the way it had begun to do lately, as if it could hear itself being discussed.

"That's a dear crossing," she said. "And paid how? Coin can be traced. We don't take coin for this kind of work anymore."

"No," the client agreed. "I know what you take."

From inside his coat he produced a package wrapped in oilcloth, bound with tarred string, and set it on the table between them without pushing it forward. He left his hand on it a moment longer than he needed to.

"Names," he said. "True names. Written true, spoken true, given under seal by the bearers themselves. You know what those are worth now. You know what they'll be worth when Aldric's auditors finish chewing through Merrow."

Orsen did not touch the package.

Every ledger-keeper learns early that the moment before payment tells you more than the payment does. She watched his hand—gloved, she noticed now, gloved even indoors, even in the dead-hour dark—and watched the small tremor in it that wasn't fear exactly. Fear would have been easier to read.

"Where did you get them?" she asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. Names given freely are one thing. Names taken are another. And names *sold*—" she let the word hang, "—are cursed cargo. Every smuggler knows it. You don't ferry what wants to be found."

The tin mask turned toward her, slow as a mooring buoy coming around on the tide.

"Dhal told me you were careful," the client said. "He told me you were the only one of his people worth the name."

The sentence went into her cold, the way a fishhook goes in—clean entry, no pain yet, all of it waiting.

*His people.* Not *your people*. And Dhal's name dropped casually, familiarly, the way a man drops the name of someone he has dealt with before.

"When," Orsen said, keeping her voice level, "did you speak with the guildmaster?"

"Last month." A pause. "You keep his books. You should know what he sells."

Something in the room changed temperature. Orsen was aware of it distantly, the way she was always aware of objects—the lamp burning its little memory of whale-fat and wick-trimming, the table remembering forty years of elbows and sweat and once, memorably, a knife fight that had ended in an apology. But her own body had gone very still, the stillness of prey that hopes stillness is the same as invisibility.

"Show me the names," she said.

He untied the tarred string. He unwrapped the oilcloth. And inside was a book—small, cheaply bound, the kind sold in any stationer's shop in Merrow for three coppers—and when he slid it across the table toward her, Orsen looked down at the first page and felt the floor of her life tilt.

* * *

She knew the handwriting.

Of course she knew it. She had spent nine years learning it, copying it, balancing accounts against it, deciphering the way Corvane Dhal's pen dug hard at the start of every stroke and trailed off at the end, as if each word began in certainty and finished in doubt. She knew the way he crossed his sevens. She knew the little hook he put on the letter *d*, a habit from some childhood tutor neither of them had ever discussed. She had once told him, laughing over the winter accounts, that his hand was so distinctive he might as well sign his crimes.

*It's why I keep you,* he'd said, and smiled, and she had taken it for a joke.

Now she sat across from a mouthless tin face while her whole world lay open in three-copper binding, and every page was in that hand.

Not the names themselves—those varied, some in different inks, some dictated clearly, the awkward scrawl of people unaccustomed to writing anything down. But the annotations. The valuations. Beside each true name, in Corvane's certain-then-doubtful hand: a price. A date. A buyer's mark she didn't recognize but understood instantly, because it was official, because it was neat, because it was the kind of mark that gets stamped by offices rather than men.

*Hetta Voss, hedge-healer, Threelane Ward — registered under protest — 40 crowns.*

*Marek and family, luck-bearers, the herring fleets — 90 crowns, delivery pending.*

*Sefa Ansel, fire-gifted, delegate —*

Orsen stopped reading.

She looked up. The tin mask regarded her with its lightless slits, and behind it, behind whatever face wore it, she felt—or imagined she felt—patience. The patience of a man watching a debt come due.

"You understand now," the client said quietly.

"What am I looking at?"

"A list of the gifted in the free cities. True names, freely surrendered—which makes them legal instruments, under the Edict. Registration by proxy." He tapped the page with one gloved finger, right beside Sefa Ansel's name, right beside Corvane's valuation. "Your guildmaster has been collecting them for two years. Passage fees. Sanctuary fees. Protection contracts. Everyone who came to the Saltspire frightened, everyone who came to the Saltspire hiding—he took their names as surety. And then he took this book to the crown's envoy and asked what they'd pay."

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight. Outside, somewhere above them, the harbor held its breath between tides.

"Why bring it to us?" Orsen heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away, someone else's voice, a clerk's voice reading figures aloud in a room where nothing was wrong. "If you've already sold it to the crown, why sell it twice?"

"Because the crown pays in charter and protection, and I want passage." The tin face tilted. "Because your guildmaster sold these people and kept the Door for himself. And because—" here the muffled voice dropped lower, almost gentle, "—I'm told you keep the real ledgers. The ones with everything in them. So I imagine you'll want to verify my claims. Check the dates against your own entries. See how neatly his payments align with his disappearances."

*Disappearances.* Plural. Orsen's mind was already moving before she could stop it, pulling threads from nine years of accounts: the widow in Threelane who'd bought a bottled grief and then stopped coming; the luck-fleet brothers who'd paid their sanctuary tithe in autumn and whose stall was shuttered by spring; the quiet exodus of clients who had simply—she'd assumed—moved on, moved coastward, moved anywhere.

She had assumed. That was the poison of it. She had built a career on never assuming, on verifying every entry twice, and for Corvane Dhal alone she had extended credit. Trust, entered in the ledger without collateral, marked *good* in her own hand.

"There's a name missing," she said.

"Is there?"

"Mine." She kept her eyes on the book. "Everyone else who ever signed a sanctuary contract with this guild is in here. I signed one. Nine years ago. My true name is in your book somewhere, or it isn't, and either answer interests me."

For the first time, the client laughed. It was a terrible sound through the tin—hollow, resonant, like wind through a drowned chimney.

"Your name isn't for sale," he said. "I asked. He wouldn't part with it. Do you understand what that means, ledger-keeper? In a book where every soul has a price, you're the only entry marked *retained*."

*Retained.* The word sat in her chest like swallowed seawater.

She thought of the salt slab humming upstairs. She thought of the token at her throat, the old guild coin Corvane had pressed into her hand at fourteen, *keep this always, it means you belong.* She thought of the door-shaped country behind the brine, and of a man who bargained with crowns, and of how a man like that would regard the one thing in his possession that opened doors.

"Passage for one," she said, sliding the book back across the table. "Paid in full. Where do you want to land?"

"Past everything. You said yourself there's only one—"

"I know what I said. Be at the drowned quay in six days. Bring nothing you love." She stood, shouldering the satchel, and paused with her hand on the lamp. "One more question. The buyer's marks in that book. The crown's envoy. Does he have a name?"

"He has a title. Envoy-Captain. And he has a sergeant." A beat. "A woman. Merrin. She does his arithmetic for him."

Orsen carried the lamp up out of the counting room without looking back, and if her hand shook on the ladder, the dark was courteous enough not to record it.

* * *

Her room above the warehouse was narrow as a coffin standing up, which she had always liked—it kept out company. Tonight she barred the door anyway, lit no lamp, and sat on the cot with the client's book copied into her memory, because she had not needed to copy it onto paper. Objects remembered. She had touched the binding, and the binding had told her everything: every hand that had held it, every room it had slept in, every time Corvane Dhal had opened it and added a line in ink the color of dried blood.

Two years. He had been selling his people for two years, and she had balanced his accounts, and found them honest, and gone to sleep content.

She took out the guild token and held it in her palm without reading it. She knew what it would show her if she let it—knew, now, in the way you know a wound before you look. Fourteen years old, mud to the knees, a man kneeling in the road offering bread and belonging, and the price written fine underneath where a child wouldn't think to check.

*Retained.*

Outside, the tide turned at last, and the harbor began to breathe again. Down the corridor, boots crossed the boards toward Corvane's office—unhurried, familiar, the tread of a man who owned every plank they fell upon. They stopped outside her door. One heartbeat. Two.

Then they moved on, and Orsen Villalobos sat alone in the dark, holding a coin that meant she belonged, and began—to her own horror—to plan what she would need to steal from the man who saved her.

The salt slab, three floors above her head, hummed like a struck bell that no one else could hear.
