## 6. Sergeant Merrin's Arithmetic

The hedge-witch's cottage smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and, faintly, of fear.

Sergeant Ilsa Merrin had learned to read that last smell the way other soldiers read weather. It sat in a room like damp in stone. You couldn't see it, but you knew it was there, and you knew what it meant: rain coming, or fire.

"Sit down, Mistress Anwell," she said. "This is a registration review, not an arrest."

The woman sat anyway, because Ilsa had told her to, and because the two soldiers at her door made sitting down seem like the safest arithmetic available. She was perhaps sixty, hands knotted from decades of garden work, and she kept wiping them on her apron as though she could wipe off whatever the crown thought she was.

"You registered in the spring," Ilsa said, opening the folder. Paperwork traveled with her everywhere now; it was the true weight of her rank, heavier than the sword. "Under the Edict of Cleansing, all bearers of the gift must present themselves for annual review. You know this."

"I know it." The old woman's voice was steady. That surprised Ilsa a little. Most of them shook.

"Your declaration states minor gift. Plant-blessing. Encouraging growth in kitchen gardens. Is that accurate?"

"It's accurate." A pause. "It's also nothing. You could give every soldier in your company a trowel and they'd do more good than I ever did with—"

"Mistress Anwell." Ilsa held up one hand, not unkindly. "I don't decide what counts. I record what's declared and pass it up. That's all this is."

That was a lie, and both of them probably knew it was a lie, but it was the kind of lie that let rooms stay calm, so Ilsa told it the way she'd been telling it since spring.

She opened her ledger. The crown had issued them in matching gray covers, one per sergeant, and inside each was a list of names organized by district, by street, by degree of gift. Minor, moderate, significant. The categories had been invented by a clerk in the capital who had never seen a pyre and never would, and Ilsa wrote in them daily with a hand that had stopped feeling like her own sometime around midsummer.

*Anwell, Marta. Minor. Plant-blessing. No dependents. No prior offenses.*

She looked at the entry, then at the woman across the table, and did the thing she always did, the thing that had gotten her through eleven months of this work without putting a rope around her own neck: she performed the arithmetic.

Registration protects. Protection requires accuracy. Accuracy requires reporting. Reporting feeds the registry. The registry feeds—

She stopped the sum there, the way you stop yourself from counting how much money you don't have.

"—your neighbor reported," she said instead, "that you cured a child's fever in the autumn. The Tallow boy. Fever that had run four days."

Marta Anwell's hands went still on her apron.

"That was herbs," she said. "Willow bark and elderflower. Any grandmother in this district could have done it."

"Any grandmother didn't. You did. And the fever broke in an hour, according to the physician who attended afterward, who filed his own report, which is why we're having this conversation." Ilsa turned the page. "Under the Edict, undeclared exercise of gift—even minor gift—is classified as concealment. Concealment carries escalation."

"Escalation." The word came out flat. "You mean my category changes. From *minor* to *moderate*. And then what? What does *moderate* get me, Sergeant? A warmer cell? A shorter rope?"

The younger soldier by the door shifted his weight. Ilsa silenced him with a look.

"I mean," she said carefully, "that the more complete your file, the better protected you are. That is the entire design of the registry. The king's proclamation was explicit. Registration is mercy. The alternative is the hunt."

"The hunt." Marta Anwell laughed, and it was the worst sound Ilsa had heard in weeks, worse than the screaming at Corren's Ford, because it was quiet. "My husband hunted boar, Sergeant. Thirty years, before his lungs went. Do you know what hunting is? Hunting is when the thing being sought does not get a say in whether it's found." She leaned forward. "You keep using words like *mercy* and *protection* like they're doors. But I've watched what walks through them. The Vasse family registered. All six of them, first week, honest as church bells. Where are they now?"

Ilsa knew where the Vasse family was. She had driven the wagon herself, because her lieutenant had a bad knee and someone had to, and because refusing an order in front of three witnesses was a different kind of arithmetic, one that ended with her name on the wrong side of a different list.

"They were relocated," she said.

"To the processing camp at Duncarrow. Yes. I've heard what relocation means." The old woman sat back. Her fear-smell hadn't lessened, but something over it had hardened, like ice forming over water. "Here's what I can't make sense of, Sergeant, and maybe you can help me, since you're the one holding the book. If registration is protection, why does everyone who registers end up gone? And if the gift is so dangerous, why has the most dangerous thing in this village for forty years been the well rope fraying?"

Ilsa looked down at her ledger.

She was good at her job. That was the rot at the center of everything, the thing she admitted to herself only at night, in barracks, staring at the ceiling: she was genuinely, professionally good at it. She found people. She put their names in neat columns. She made the machine run smoothly, and the machine ran on pyres, and somewhere between the second column and the smoke she had stopped being able to call it anything but what it was.

But she wasn't ready to be what Marta Anwell wanted her to be. Not yet. Maybe not ever. So she said, "The fever cure goes in your file as a note, not an escalation. I'll mark it *unverified folk practice*."

Something flickered across the witch's face—not gratitude, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman doing her own arithmetic and arriving, reluctantly, at a number slightly less terrible than expected.

"And next year?" Marta asked. "When some neighbor reports I made their cabbages grow too fast?"

"Next year," Ilsa said, closing the ledger, "there will be a next year. That's more than some people get. Take it."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of pewter. Her corporal, Vey, fell into step beside her, young enough that his conscience still made noise.

"She's harmless," he said. "You know she's harmless. Half these entries are harmless. Why are we even—"

"Because the Edict doesn't have a category for harmless." Ilsa kept walking. "It has *minor*, *moderate*, and *significant*, and the pyres don't distinguish."

Vey was quiet for ten paces. Then: "The Vasses. At Duncarrow. Is it true what they say about the—"

"Don't."

"But Sergeant—"

"*Don't.*" She said it low, and it came out with more edge than she intended, and Vey shut his mouth the way soldiers learn to, quickly and completely, filing the question away in the place where young men store the things their sergeants won't answer. She almost envied him that place. Hers had filled up years ago and started leaking.

They had three more reviews in the district. A cooper whose gift was knowing where things were lost—a useful man, a *minor*, though Ilsa privately wondered how long the crown would consider finding lost children a minor talent. A ferryman who could calm water. A girl of nineteen listed as *significant* whom Ilsa had been dreading for a month, because *significant* meant escort, and escort meant Duncarrow, and Duncarrow meant—

She stopped that sum too. She was getting very practiced at stopping sums.

The ferryman's review took four minutes. The cooper's took six. Neither of them looked at her the way Marta Anwell had, with that terrible quiet laugh, and somehow that was worse. Fear you could work around. Resignation got into your boots and stayed there.

It was full dark when they rode back into the garrison at Millbrook, and the town square was lit, which was wrong, because Millbrook saved its lamp oil like a miser saves coin, and Ilsa reined in and saw the scaffold.

Not a hanging scaffold. A burning platform, iron-banded, with a post in the middle and chains, and a crowd gathered around it in the way crowds gather for executions—too close, and pretending otherwise. A crier stood on the steps of the tithe-house reading from a scroll, and even at this distance Ilsa caught the cadence of crown proclamation, the official rhythm she'd heard a hundred times since spring.

"—by order of His Majesty Aldric, Second of His Name, Protector of the Realm, the following bearer of the gift, having been lawfully registered and thereafter found guilty of concealment and sedition—"

"Sedition," Vey breathed beside her. "Who?"

Ilsa already knew. She'd known since the platform, since the shape of the crowd, since the specific dread that had been walking ahead of her all day like a dog that scents weather.

The name the crier read out was *Anwell, Marta.*

The crowd murmured. Somewhere in it a child asked a question and was shushed. And Ilsa Merrin sat on her horse in the pewter-dark and understood, with the clean horrible clarity of a completed sum, that the review she had conducted that morning—the gentle one, the merciful one, the one where she'd written *unverified folk practice* in a gray ledger to buy an old woman another year—had been the instrument of the woman's death.

Her note had gone up the chain. Her note had been read by someone in the district office who did the arithmetic differently than she did. *Concealment suspected. Escalation warranted.* And the wagon had come while Ilsa was drinking tea with a ferryman, and the review had become a trial had become a sentence had become this: an iron-banded platform and a crowd and a crier, and Marta Anwell being led out in chains, sixty years old, hands knotted from garden work, still wiping them on an apron they'd taken away from her.

She should ride away. There was no regulation requiring her presence. There was, in fact, a strong argument that a reviewing officer's absence was cleaner, deniable, wise.

She didn't ride away.

She stayed, because staying felt like the only honest thing left in her day, and she watched the whole of it. The priest who wouldn't come close. The executioner who was some local man paid in crown silver and hating it, you could see him hating it. And Marta Anwell, strapped to the post, who did not scream and did not pray and did not curse the king, but who turned her head at the last, deliberately, and swept her gaze across the crowd like a woman taking a final inventory of her garden—and for half a heartbeat, across forty feet of torchlight and smoke-smell, that gaze found Ilsa's face.

And stopped there.

There was no accusation in it. That was the unbearable part. Ilsa could have borne accusation; accusation was a debt you could repay or dispute. What was in the old woman's eyes was something closer to *there you are* — recognition, confirmation, the look of someone whose private arithmetic had finally been checked against the official one and found, with sorrow rather than surprise, to be correct.

*I told you,* the eyes said. *You keep using words like mercy. Now you've seen what walks through the door.*

Then the faggots were lit, and Ilsa made herself watch, because looking away felt like the last small theft of a day already built entirely out of thefts, and she had decided—standing in her stirrups with smoke stinging her eyes—that she was done stealing from herself if she was going to go on stealing from everyone else.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around the second bell she gave up pretending, dressed, and took her gray ledger to the barracks table with a candle, meaning to work, meaning to lose herself in the one activity that had never lied to her: neat columns, accurate sums.

The ledger fell open to the district pages on its own. It knew its business better than she knew hers.

She read down the names. *Cooper, Joss. Minor. Ferryman, Aldous. Minor.* And below them, waiting for tomorrow's escort detail, the entry she had been dreading:

*Sefa Ilves. Significant. Fire-gift, uncontrolled. Delegation credential, Saltspire Guild. Escort required.*

A guild delegate. That was new. Most of the registered were farmers and midwives and one memorable man who could tell when milk was about to turn. But the free cities' guilds had begun registering their people in the autumn—batches of names delivered under seal, cooperative, punctual, which had struck Ilsa at the time as either very smart or very strange, and she hadn't had time to wonder which.

*Significant* meant the wagon. *Significant* meant Duncarrow. And Duncarrow, whatever the dispatches called it, was not a place anyone returned from, and Ilsa had helped fill it twice and had stopped sleeping properly after the second time, and here was a girl of nineteen with fire in her blood and a smuggler's guild standing surety for her, scheduled for escort at dawn.

Ilsa sat with the candle and did the arithmetic one more time, all the way through, no stopping at the middle this time.

*Registration protects:* false. *Protection requires accuracy:* false. *Accuracy feeds the registry:* true. *The registry feeds the pyres:* true. *Therefore the work I do well is the work that kills them:* true. *Therefore I am the mechanism:* true.

The candle guttered. Outside, Millbrook still smelled faintly of smoke, and would for days.

Ilsa looked at Sefa Ilves's name for a long time. Then she took out a slip of paper, copied the entry onto it—name, district, credential, escort schedule—in her own precise hand, folded it small, and slid it into the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, where it lay like a coal.

In the ledger itself, she wrote: *Escort deferred pending verification of delegation credentials with Saltspire Guild, Greyharbour.*

It would buy days. Maybe a week. Clerks moved slowly when given paperwork-shaped reasons to, and she had just handed them a beautiful one.

She didn't know yet what she would do with the slip of paper in her pocket. Warn the guild? Burn it? She only knew that for the first time since spring, the sum had come out on the side of the living, and that she had committed, in ink, to standing between a girl and the crown's wagon—and that the moment the district office noticed the deferral, someone would start asking questions, and those questions would have her name at the bottom of them.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark with a stolen name against her ribs, listening to Millbrook breathe, and far off toward the coast she thought she heard—though it may have been only the tide—something like a door groaning open.
