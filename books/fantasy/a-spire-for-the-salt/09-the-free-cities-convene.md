## 9. The Free Cities Convene

Sefa Ashqar had set fire to three things in her life on purpose and one by accident, and she had expected the smuggler's parliament to feel like the accident—chaotic, hot, nobody sure whose fault anything was. Instead it felt like a funeral where the corpse refused to die quietly.

The Salt Cellar had been a salt warehouse once, back before the free cities decided that "free" meant nobody's law but their own. Now it was a vault of a room below the Tidehouse district, ringed with old brine-stained pillars, its ceiling hung with the rusted hooks that had once carried quarter-tons of white gold. The delegates sat in a circle because smugglers distrusted any arrangement with a head to it. Forty-one chairs. Forty-one guilds, coteries, brotherhoods, sisterhoods, and one woman from the Lamp-Keepers' Union who claimed to represent an entire district and whom nobody believed.

Sefa was twenty-three and her chair had been borrowed from a delegate who'd died of a bad bottle two days after King Aldric's Edict went up nailed to every door in Brackwater. Nobody had contested her seat. That told her something about how they saw her—young, expendable, a seat filled the way you fill a hole in a wall—and she spent the first hour of the parliament deciding not to be angry about it. Anger was fuel. You didn't pour all your fuel on the floor just because people looked at you like kindling.

Corvane Dhal spoke last of the first three, because he always arranged to speak last of the first three, and Sefa watched the room lean toward him the way water leans toward a drain.

"You've heard the argument for running," Corvane said. He didn't stand. He never stood; standing was for men who needed height to be believed. "Maren's made it well. Run west, run to the grass kingdoms, scatter like seed." He nodded across the circle at a lean woman with river-grey eyes who took the acknowledgment without blinking. "You've heard the argument for fighting. Young Ashqar's made it with more heat than I expected and less patience than I hoped."

A ripple of laughter. Sefa kept her hands flat on the table and let it pass. *Heat*, she thought. They'd learn what her heat did when it was given somewhere to go.

"But I'd ask you to consider," Corvane went on, "what the crown actually wants. Not us. Aldric doesn't care whether we live or die—he cares whether anyone in his kingdom can do what we can do without his permission. That's the whole of his fear. A king who can't control the gift is a king on borrowed time, and he knows it. So he offers charters. Licenses. A legal trade, sanctioned, taxed, protected." He spread his hands, palms up, the gesture of a man showing he carried nothing. "The Saltspire Guild has survived four kings by selling what kings wanted and staying out of the way of what they feared. This king wants legitimacy. I say we sell it to him."

"Legitimacy," Maren said flatly. "You mean we register."

"I mean we negotiate terms. There's a difference, and the difference is everyone in this room stays alive to argue about it."

"The difference," Sefa said, before she could decide not to, "is paper."

The circle turned toward her. She hadn't been called on. She spoke anyway, because her grandmother had burned in a registered quarter thirty years ago—the registration lists had told the soldiers exactly which doors to knock on—and because if she waited until she was calm she would never speak at all.

"Aldric isn't offering licenses. He's offering a census. Every name written down is a name that can be found later. Registration isn't protection, Master Dhal. It's inventory."

"Spoken like someone who's never negotiated with a crown," said Halveth of the Lantern Guild, a fat man whose gift, as far as anyone knew, was making lamps burn slightly longer than they should.

"Spoken," Sefa said, "like someone who's read one."

She pulled the copy of the Edict from her coat and unfolded it on the table. She'd read it eleven times. It had a rhythm to it, the way dangerous documents always did—*for the ordering of the realm, for the safety of the multitude, for the prevention of abuses unknowable to honest subjects*—and buried in the fourth clause, in language so dry you could strike a match off it, was the phrase *and such registers shall be transmitted quarterly to the Office of Cleansing for audit.*

"Quarterly," she repeated. "To the Office of Cleansing. Does anyone here believe an office with that name audits anything except pyres?"

Silence. Good silence, though. The kind where people were thinking instead of laughing.

Corvane let it sit a moment, then said, gently, terribly gently: "And your alternative, Ashqar? I hear you have one. There's talk of a door."

There it was. Sefa felt her pulse jump and hated that it jumped. She had told exactly three people about the rumor—a slab of salt in the Saltspire vaults, humming like a struck bell, older than every map in the free cities. One of those three people was sitting twelve chairs away with his hands folded and his face full of reasonable doubt.

"I'm told the Saltspire Guild recently acquired a curiosity," she said. "Door-shaped. Made of salt. I'm told it hums."

"And I'm told," Corvane said, "that stories grow in the telling. A slab of salt. What would you have us do, child—carry it out to sea and knock?"

The word *child* landed the way it was meant to. Half the circle smiled. Sefa counted them, the smilers, and filed their faces away.

"What I'd have us do," she said, "is ask why the Saltspire Guildmaster bought a door-shaped piece of a country that officially never existed, and why he hasn't mentioned it to this body until I forced it into the light."

For the first time that evening, something moved behind Corvane Dhal's eyes that wasn't patience. It was gone in a blink, sanded smooth, but Sefa had spent her life around fires and she knew what a flare looked like before it caught.

"I acquired salvage," he said, "as my guild acquires salvage, in the ordinary course of trade. If it proves to be more than salvage, this body will be the second to know."

"Second," Sefa said. "After your buyers."

"After my guild," Corvane said, and his voice cooled by a degree that the whole room felt. "Which is the only loyalty I've ever claimed. I'd advise each of you to make the same calculation. Loyalty to your own tables. Everything else—" he gestured at the edict lying on the table, at the whole burning world outside the cellar walls— "everything else is negotiable, or it kills you."

He sat. He'd won the hour; she could see it in the way shoulders settled, in the way Halveth began whispering to his neighbor about terms and charters as though bargaining were already decided. Factions hardening into futures, right there along the circle: the runners clustering near Maren, the bargainers orbiting Corvane, and scattered between them a handful of faces turned toward Sefa like moths checking whether she was a flame worth the cost.

Forty-one chairs. She counted nineteen who wouldn't follow her anywhere, eleven who'd already chosen flight, nine who'd already sold themselves in their heads to Aldric's charter. That left two unaccounted for. She found them at the far edge: a lamp-gifted boy barely older than herself, and a silent woman with dockworker's hands who represented no guild anyone could name and who had spoken exactly zero words all night.

Two. It wasn't nothing. Fires started smaller than that.

* * *

The parliament broke at midnight without a vote, which was how parliaments of thieves always broke—with everything deferred, everyone armed with reasons, no one committed. Sefa stood on the wharf outside the Tidehouse afterward and watched the fog come in off the shallows in long grey ropes, and made herself breathe the cold until the fire under her sternum settled from a roar to a banked glow.

"He'll bargain," said a voice behind her. The dockworker woman. Up close she was fifty-something, salt-and-pepper hair cut short, forearms scarred in the pattern of old rope burns. "Dhal. He's already bargaining. Men like him start negotiating the moment the threat appears. The meeting tonight was theater so the rest of you would feel consulted."

"Do you know him?"

"Everyone knows him." The woman spat tidewards. "I know his ledgers better than most. My name's Kess. I keep accounts for people who don't want accounts kept."

Sefa studied her. "Then you know about the door."

"I know about the ledger that mentions it." Kess's eyes were the flat grey of the fog. "Entry forty-four, last spring. *Acquired, provenance withheld: one door, salt, sealed. Buyer's instruction—no catalog entry, no word to the parliament.*" She paused. "Funny thing about that instruction. The Saltspire keeps a cataloger. Careful girl. Reads deep. If Dhal wanted that thing hidden, why hand it to the one person in his guild who can hear what it remembers?"

The question went through Sefa like a splinter working toward the heart. "Unless he needed something read that only she can read."

"Or unless," Kess said, "he's keeping it quiet because a door out of Aldric's kingdom is worth more sold than shared. Charters are cheap, girl. Kings give those away. A country that vanished off the maps two generations ago—" she shook her head slowly. "That's a treasure. And Corvane Dhal has never in his life given a treasure away when there was a buyer standing in front of him."

The fog thickened. Somewhere out past the breakwater, a bell buoy clanged twice and went quiet, as if it had said too much.

"If it's real," Sefa said, "it changes everything. No registers. No pyres. No bargaining. We don't need to win—we just need to leave."

"And if it's real," Kess said, "then Aldric needs it destroyed worse than he needs any list of names. Because a door that opens onto a country his own grandfather erased—that door makes him a liar in front of the whole world." She turned to go, then stopped. "Watch Dhal's clerk. The quiet ones see everything, and Dhal trusts her with papers he'd burn before showing his own captains. Whatever he's truly doing, it's written down somewhere near her."

"She's not gifted," Sefa said. "Everyone says."

Kess smiled for the first time, and it wasn't a warm smile. "Everyone says a lot of things. So were you, three months ago."

* * *

Sefa didn't go home. Home was a rented room above a chandler's shop with one exit and a landlady who asked questions, and after tonight she trusted neither. Instead she walked the long way through the Warrens, the undercity where the free cities kept everyone who'd ever fallen off a ledger—lamp-gifted boys running light-fairs in drowned cisterns, hedge-healers trading poultices for bread, children who warmed their own hands in winter and tried not to let anyone see them do it.

This was what she fought for. Not guilds, not chairs, not the dry arithmetic of charters. This: a girl of six in a doorway, cupping a flame the size of a thumbnail, hiding it the instant footsteps echoed. Sefa stopped, crouched, and showed the child her own palm with a rose of fire blooming in it, wide as a tulip, harmless as lamplight.

The girl's eyes went huge. Then—worst thing in the world—she looked over her shoulder to check who else had seen.

"That's right," Sefa said softly, closing her hand. "You hide it. For now. Things are going to change, little spark. Either we find a door out of here, or we build a fire big enough they stop hunting us. One of those. I promise you one of those."

She walked on, and the walk did what walks did: sorted her anger into plans. Tomorrow she would find the Saltspire clerk. Tonight—

Tonight the bells started.

Not the harbor bells. Those rang in patterns; these rang ragged, panicked, all of them at once, and beneath them came the sound Sefa had learned to know in another city in another life: boots. Many boots, iron-shod, coming down into the Warrens from every stair at once.

Torches bloomed along the upper galleries. She heard the crash of a cistern-market scattering, a scream cut short, and then a voice amplified by some sound-carrying trick of the crown's artificers, rolling down through the undercity like a landslide:

*"By order of Sergeant Merrin, Crown Constabulary: this quarter is closed for inspection. All persons will remain in place. All persons bearing the gift will present themselves for registration. Resistance is treason. Treason is fire."*

Sefa stood in the middle of the stair, fire rising up her arms before she knew she'd called it, and made herself think, made herself *not* burn, made herself count exits the way she'd counted chairs.

Nineteen against. Eleven gone. Nine sold. Two maybe. And now, out of the dark above, the whole machinery of the crown pouring down into the one place where her people actually lived.

So much for parliament, she thought. Time to find out what the ledgers know.

She pulled her coat tight around her banked fire and ran toward the Saltspire quarter, taking the routes only the hunted knew, while somewhere behind her a locked gate slammed and a sergeant's voice asked someone, very quietly, to open it.
