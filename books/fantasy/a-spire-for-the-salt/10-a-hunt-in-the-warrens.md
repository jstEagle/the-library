## 10. A Hunt in the Warrens

The undercity of Brackmoor smelled of tallow and drowned things.

Sergeant Ilsa Merrin stood at the top of the Warden's Stair with her lantern shuttered to a coin of light and listened to the dark below. Sixteen steps down, the Warrens began—old salt-cellars, smuggler runs, cisterns abandoned when the river changed its mind two hundred years ago. The maps called it a district. The people who lived there called it nothing at all, because most of them were not on any list Ilsa had ever been given to read.

Tonight she had a new list. It was short. Four names. Three addresses. One question mark.

"Form up," she said quietly, and her six soldiers came down the stair behind her like water finding a drain.

She had done this arithmetic before, on the march from Harrowgate: a registered hedge-witch, an old man who could make bread rise twice, a child who spoke to gulls. Registration feeds pyres, not protection. The thought arrived now uninvited, wearing Corporal Vess's face from three nights ago, when he'd asked her, cheerfully, how many of the registered they'd actually *protected* so far.

"None," she'd said, and he'd stopped smiling, and neither of them had written it down.

"Left fork," said Danner, the tracker, at the first junction. "Fresh lamp oil. Someone's moved through recently."

"Someone moves through here every hour," Ilsa said. "That's the point of a warren."

But she took the left fork anyway, because instinct was the only instrument she trusted more than a map, and tonight both of them pointed down.

* * *

Orsen Villalobos knew the left fork was wrong.

She pressed herself into the alcove behind the rain barrel and counted heartbeats until the lantern-glow at the junction swung away. Boots. At least seven sets. The crown's sweep teams worked in sevens—one officer, one tracker, four line-soldiers, one torch-bearer whose only job was to make sure nothing flammable survived being looked at.

Seven. And somewhere ahead of her, the Ferrow family, moving too slowly.

She'd found them at dusk in the Tallowmarket, huddled by the fish-stalls where the smell covered fear. Maren Ferrow, a net-mender whose knots untied themselves when she was frightened—which she was now, permanently. Her husband, big and slow and gentle, who could quiet any animal by looking at it. Two children. The girl, Pim, eight years old, who made small things float when she laughed, which meant she hadn't been allowed to laugh in eleven days.

They had come to Orsen because of a rumor that ledger-keepers knew ways out. The rumor was true. What the rumor didn't say was what each way out cost, or that Orsen had spent the last week rewriting her own books so thoroughly that half her memory of her mother's voice was gone, traded away entry by entry like coins from a purse that never refilled.

She didn't have time for grief. She had a route.

Not the left fork. The left fork was the obvious route, the one on the old smuggler charts, the one the crown's trackers would know because the crown bought its charts from men like Corvane Dhal. The real route went right, through the collapsed cistern, along the ledge above the black water where the footing was honest but narrow, then up through the bone-dry well shaft into the chandlery on Candle Street. From there, the harbor. From there—

From there was tomorrow's problem.

She waited for the lantern-light to fade, then slipped right, moving fast, whispering the route under her breath like a prayer she'd invented herself: *cistern, ledge, well shaft, chandlery.* Objects remembered routes if you told them firmly enough. She'd once taught a door hinge to remember being oiled, and it had stayed silent for a year.

"Cistern," she breathed at the mouth of it, and felt the old stone settle, satisfied, as though it had been waiting all this time to be useful again.

* * *

"The cistern's collapsed," Danner said, holding his lantern high. Rubble choked the far end, a spill of stone like a broken jaw.

Isla studied it. "Collapsed recently?"

Danner touched the rubble edges. Fresh dust. No moss regrown over the fracture lines. "Could be. Could be knocked down on purpose."

"To block us?"

"To slow anyone following someone." He looked back at her. "There's a ledge above the waterline, Sergeant. If you knew it was there."

"If you knew it was there," Ilsa said, "you weren't reading crown charts."

"No, Sergeant."

No. You were reading guild charts. She kept that thought behind her teeth. Every hunter in the free cities had learned the same lesson in the last month: the Saltspire Guild's ledgers were the truest map of the gifted that existed, and the crown's auditors had been crawling through seized copies for weeks. But ledgers recorded sales, not smugglers' roads. Somewhere between the paper and the streets there was a gap, and people were living in that gap, and tonight Ilsa was being paid to close it.

"Rope," she said. "We go over the rubble. Quietly."

Vess grinned at her in the lantern light—the grin of a man who still believed they were doing something clean. She envied him. She was beginning to hate how much she envied him.

* * *

The ledge was exactly as narrow as Orsen had promised herself it would be.

Behind her, Maren Ferrow moved with her hands flat against the wet stone, breathing in the careful rhythm Orsen had drilled into her. Ahead, the husband—Tam, she kept reminding herself, his name is Tam—carried the sleeping boy on his shoulders, one hand steadying the child, the other trailing along the rock. Little Pim walked between them, solemn as a funeral, because Orsen had knelt down at the cistern mouth and said, very seriously, "Pim. Tonight, no laughing. Can you do that? Tonight you're the bravest one, and brave ones hold their laughter like a held breath."

Pim had nodded with the terrible gravity of the obedient, and had not laughed since, even when Tam slipped to the knee in the dark and swore in a whisper.

Orsen counted them past each marker. Cistern. Ledge. The place where the wall bulged like a swallowed fist—she put her palm on it as she passed, and the stone gave her back a flicker of itself: masons, generations ago, singing while they worked, and a woman's voice laughing at their song, and she wrenched her hand away because she could not afford to stand in a dead woman's joy right now.

Her gift was a door that opened both ways, and lately everything on the other side wanted to pull her through.

"Well shaft next," she whispered. "Then daylight. Then the chandlery, and a man named Osk who owes me for a jarred thunderclap and will hate me forever for calling it in."

Maren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You sold a thunderclap?"

"To a widow whose husband died without saying goodbye. It was the last thing he never got to say." Orsen kept her eyes on the ledge. "The guild sells impossible things. Sometimes they're the right impossible things."

"And sometimes?"

Ahead of them, faintly, a lantern bloomed in the dark behind.

"Sometimes," Orsen said, "run."

* * *

They heard the family before they saw them—a scuff, a child's shoe on loose shale—and then Danner's light caught the tail end of movement at the top of the well shaft: a big man hauling himself up, a rope of some kind, a shape disappearing into darkness overhead.

"Contact!" Vess shouted, and the Warrens ate the word and gave it back tenfold, echoes tumbling away in every direction, and Ilsa thought, uselessly, *now every soul down here knows we're coming.*

"Hold!" she snapped. "Hold positions!"

Because the arithmetic had changed. A family running was a family cornered, and cornered people with the gift did unpredictable things, and unpredictable things in a confined space killed soldiers. She had seen a registered cooper fold a squad of four inside his own barrels like a god folding paper. She did not want to see what a frightened father could do.

"Danner. Up the shaft. Slowly. Lantern high, weapon low."

"Sergeant—"

"That's an order. If there are children up there, we do this my way or we do it badly."

Danner went up the shaft like smoke, and Ilsa stood at the bottom with her hand on her sword she hoped not to draw, and listened to the undercity breathe around her, and thought about the hedge-witch's kitchen, and the tea she'd drunk there, and the name she'd pocketed instead of reporting, folded into her cuff like contraband.

*How many have we protected?*

None. Not one. The list grew; the pyres grew; the arithmetic refused to balance no matter how she carried the numbers.

Above her, faintly, a hatch banged shut.

* * *

Osk the chandler had a bolt-bar across his back door and a face like a closed ledger.

"You said you'd never call it in," he said.

"I said you owed me," Orsen said, shouldering past him into the tallow-smelling warmth. "This is me collecting. Family of five. They need the cellar until the tide turns, then the fish-cart route to the harbor stairs. You know it. You've used it."

"I've *never*—"

"Osk." She turned and let him see her face, all of it, the exhaustion and the fear and underneath both the thing that had made Corvane Dhal buy a starving apprentice off the road nine years ago: she did not blink first. "There are seven crown soldiers in the Warrens below your floor. In about ninety seconds one of them is going to open that trapdoor you're standing next to. Would you rather argue with me, or explain to them why your cellar smells like frightened children?"

Osk looked at the trapdoor. Looked at her. Swore with the fluency of a man who'd spent thirty years around sailors, threw the bar, and started moving barrels off the cellar hatch with the speed of genuine repentance.

Tam Ferrow came up the ladder last, the boy still asleep on his back, and set him down among the candle-frames like laying an offering on an altar. Pim peered around her mother's skirts at the walls of dipped candles, hundreds of them, hundreds of small patient flames waiting to exist.

"They're pretty," Pim said.

"Not yet," Osk said gruffly. "Nothing down here gets to be pretty till morning."

Orsen was already at the front window, easing the shutter to a finger's width, watching Candle Street run empty and silvered under the moon. Behind her she heard Maren murmuring to Tam, heard the boy stir and whimper and go quiet again, heard Osk barricading the cellar entrance behind a wall of tallow crates.

Ninety seconds, she'd promised. She'd made it up. But the habit of promises was load-bearing, and she leaned on it now, counting.

At sixty, boots on cobblestones. At eighty, the knock—not a knock, a blow, a fist like a ram, and a voice pitched to carry:

"Open in the name of King Aldric! This house is entered under the Edict of Cleansing!"

* * *

Ilsa hit the chandlery's back door second, after Vess's boot, and came through into candlelight and tallow reek with her sword drawn and her heart doing something complicated she refused to name.

The room was empty.

Five people. She'd seen five people—counted them, a tracker's reflex, big man, small woman, two children, and a fifth figure at the top of the shaft, quick and dark, gone before the light reached her face. Five people did not evaporate.

"Search it," she said. "Cellar. Loft. Everywhere."

Vess found the trapdoor behind the tallow crates in under a minute, because Vess was good, and beneath it a cellar full of candles and nothing else. Danner came down from the loft shaking his head. The front room held a counter, a strongbox, a sleeping cat who regarded the invasion with magnificent contempt.

"Back door?" Ilsa asked.

"Bolted from inside," Vess said. "Still bolted."

Five people in a sealed box. Ilsa stood in the middle of the warm room and let her eyes move the way Danner had taught her years ago: don't look for people, look for what's *wrong*. The candles were racked in perfect rows. The counter was dusty except for a swept arc where someone had recently leaned. The cat—

The cat was looking at the cellar stairs.

Not at the trapdoor. At the *stairs*, at the space beside the third step, where the shadows lay a little differently than shadows should.

Ilsa had grown up in a fishing town where the old women still muttered the pre-Edict blessings, where a neighbor had once calmed a runaway horse by looking at it, and everyone had agreed, comfortably, that this was just a way with animals. She knew what the gift looked like when it was small. She knew what it looked like when it was hiding.

"Everyone out," she said. "Front room. Now."

"Sergeant?" Vess, wounded.

"Now, Corporal."

When they'd gone she stood alone at the head of the cellar stairs, sword lowered, and said to the shadows beside the third step, in a voice she kept level with effort:

"I count five. Big man, small woman, two children. And whoever showed the others the way up the shaft, because that route isn't on any chart I've ever seen, and I've seen most of them."

Silence. The kind of silence that has a heartbeat in it.

"I'm going to tell you what happens next," Ilsa said. "My orders are to take you to the registrars. You know what the registrars are. I'm going to walk back up those stairs, and I'm going to search this building for one hour, thoroughly, out loud, so you can hear everywhere I am. Then I'm going to report the house cleared. Whether that's true is going to depend entirely on whether you're still beside the third step when I come back down."

She turned. Took one step. Stopped.

"The child floated a toy boat in the harbor at Lowmark two days ago," she said quietly. "A fisherman reported it. That report is why we're here. Whoever you are—you got them this far. Get them the rest of the way."

And she climbed the stairs, and began searching aloud, loudly, badly, starting—as any thorough soldier would—with the loft, which was the farthest room from the cellar.

* * *

Beside the third step, in a fold of shadow that Pim's small gift pulled around them like a blanket, Orsen Villalobos held her breath and memorized the sergeant's voice.

She couldn't see her. That was the worst of it—that the woman hunting them existed for Orsen only as boots on boards above, as a voice pitched low and level, as the deliberate badness of a search that started in the farthest room. Tam's huge hand rested on her shoulder, trembling. Maren had both children gathered in against her chest. Nobody moved. Nobody breathed.

*I count five,* the voice had said. *Big man, small woman, two children. And whoever showed the others the way.*

She knows, Orsen thought. She knows exactly, and she's choosing.

Orsen had spent nine years keeping accounts. She knew a falsified entry when she heard one. She knew the sound of a number carried wrong on purpose, a column balanced by a hand that wanted it balanced. The sergeant upstairs was falsifying her own report, entry by shouted entry, and Orsen sat in the dark and understood that she was listening to a woman committing treason one room at a time.

Why?

The question lodged in her like a splinter. Corvane had taught her that everyone had a price; the guild ran on prices; her whole life was a ledger of what people cost. But this woman wasn't buying anything. She was spending—career, safety, probably her neck—for strangers behind a staircase, and asking nothing.

Unless she was asking something. Unless this was the long game, the soft voice before the harder one, the registrar's kindness that fed pyres—

*Get them the rest of the way,* the voice had said.

Above, the searching moved to the front room. Drawers. Shutters. The strongbox lid, slammed theatrically.

Orsen made the decision the way she made all her decisions now: quickly, and knowing she'd pay for it later in something she couldn't afford to lose.

She eased a hand from the shadow-fold, and laid two fingers on the cold stone of the third step, and pushed a memory into it—the sergeant's voice, the whole speech, word for word—so that the step would remember it, and she could come back someday, when there was time, and read it again, and understand.

Stone kept what it was given. Stone forgot nothing.

People were supposed to be different. Lately Orsen wasn't sure.

* * *

An hour, as promised. Ilsa searched the chandlery the way a woman searches who wants her searching witnessed: thoroughly enough for the record, carelessly enough for mercy. Then she assembled her squad in the street under the moon.

"House cleared," she said. "Log it. We move to the Tannery Row address."

Vess looked at her a moment too long. Danner looked at her a moment longer than that. Trackers noticed things; it was the whole trade. But Danner had also been at Harrowgate, and at the pyre outside Fenwick, and whatever arithmetic he was carrying, he added tonight's numbers to it in silence and said only, "Yes, Sergeant."

They marched. The boots faded up Candle Street, six sets and one, growing small, growing gone.

In the cellar, Orsen counted to three hundred before she let the shadow-fold down.

Then there was no time for philosophy. There was Osk grumbling and guiding, the fish-cart at the harbor stairs with its driver paid triple and sworn to silence, the tide turning, the gray water opening toward the estuary and the sea roads and—somewhere past all of it, humming in her blood like a struck bell—the Door of Brine, and the shore under a sun that wasn't theirs.

Maren Ferrow gripped Orsen's hands at the gangplank hard enough to hurt. "Come with us."

"I can't. There are others." There were always others. That was the whole disease of her, the whole purpose. "The cart driver knows the crossing. Trust him further than you trust me."

"Why would we trust you at all?" Tam asked—not unkindly, honestly, a man who quieted animals wanting to know what she was.

Orsen looked back once, up the harbor hill, toward Candle Street, toward a locked gate and a woman who had lied beautifully for strangers and marched away into the service of a king who burned people.

"Because I keep accounts," Orsen said. "And tonight, so does she."

The cart rolled. The water took them.

Orsen stood on the empty stairs until the fog closed, and then she turned and walked back into the city, toward the Tidehouse, because Sefa had sent word at dusk—a single line, in a hand shaking with fury:

*They found my name on a list. Come to the Tidehouse. Bring everything you know about who wrote it.*

Orsen already knew who wrote it. She had known since the masked client, since the handwriting like a blade drawn slow.

What she didn't know—what she walked into the dark to learn—was whether she was ready to say it out loud.
