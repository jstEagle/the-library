## 11. The Price of One Edit Too Many

The master ledger weighed nine pounds and remembered eleven years, and Orsen Villalobos had spent the whole of a windless afternoon deciding to destroy herself with it.

She sat in the counting room with the door barred and the lamp turned low, because what she was about to do felt like a crime even though it was the opposite of one. Around her the guild's papers slept in their pigeonholes—bills of lading, tide-charts, the small honest lies of a smuggling house. And beneath them, under a loose floorboard she had pried up herself three years ago, the true ledger: the one with names.

Not the clients' names. She had already handled those, entry by entry, over six nights of careful, sickening work. A jarred thunderclap sold to Widow Anselm, who only wanted to hear her dead husband's voice one more time—*Anselm* was out of the book now, and with it the memory of the widow's kitchen, the smell of her bread, the way her hands shook when she'd counted out coins she couldn't afford. Orsen had paid for that erasure without knowing the price in advance. That was the cruelty of the gift: you learned the exchange rate only after the transaction cleared.

Three days of borrowed youth traded to a fisherman for his luck—*gone*. The fisherman's face went with it, and the particular gray of the harbor on the morning he'd come in. She had checked, afterward, standing at the counting-room window: the harbor was gray, everyone said so, but she had to take their word for it. Somewhere behind her eyes was a hole shaped like a color.

Six names. Six small amputations. She had told herself the ledger was clean, that the crown's auditors would find nothing but salt and smuggled silk.

Then the wagons came.

She'd watched them from the upstairs window at dusk—crown wagons, canvas-shrouded, escorted by riders in the king's gray. They hadn't stopped at the guildhall. They'd stopped at the Tidehouse, where Corvane had gone the evening before without telling anyone where, and they had stayed two hours, and when they rolled away Corvane had stood in the doorway a long moment, looking at nothing, before he came back inside and smiled at her and asked how the cataloguing went.

She had said *fine*. She had smiled back. She had gone upstairs and been sick into the basin.

Because she understood, lying awake in the dark, what she should have understood sooner: the client list was one betrayal. The master ledger was the whole map. Every name she'd cut from the entry pages still lived in the master's spine—the running total, the cross-references, the little marginal codes she herself had invented to track which clients were gifted and which merely desperate. Her own system. Her own cleverness. Eleven years of meticulous bookkeeping, and every page of it was a confession written in her hand, addressed to whoever knew how to read it.

The crown's auditors knew how to read it. She had trained two of them, once, before the Edict, when the guild had sold the crown a shipment of bottled weather and Corvane had wanted the transaction to look legitimate. They were competent. They were thorough. One of them, Vess, had a gift for patterns the way she had a gift for objects—no magic to it, just a mind that found the shape hiding inside numbers.

Vess would find the shape. Vess would find *her*.

So: the master ledger. All of it. Not six names—eleven years.

She took out the loose floorboard and lifted the book into her lap, and it was heavier than nine pounds. Books always were.

* * *

There was a way to do this and a way not to do it.

The wrong way was entry by entry, the way she'd done the clients. Touch a page, read what it remembered, rewrite the memory. Slow, and each small edit took its small tithe—a face, a color, the taste of a particular meal—and eleven years of entries would strip her down to something with her name and none of her life.

The right way—the way that frightened her so badly she'd needed all afternoon to stop shaking—was to do it whole. Not page by page. Book by book. To take the entire memory of the ledger at once, the way you'd take a country at once instead of besieging it town by town, and rewrite it in a single act: *this book has never recorded a gifted client. This book is the account of an honest smuggling house. This book remembers only salt, silk, and small crimes.*

One edit. One price.

She didn't know what the price would be. That was the thing she couldn't get around. The gift had a logic she'd never fully mapped—she could rewrite what an object remembered, but the memory had to go somewhere, and the somewhere was her. Small edits took small things. A whole book, taken whole, would take—

She wouldn't think it. She thought it anyway.

She thought about her mother.

It was strange, the things you inventory when you're pricing yourself. She sat with the ledger in her lap and went through her own memory like a merchant assessing stock. Her mother's hands—she had those. Flour on them, a scar at the base of the thumb where a knife had slipped. Her mother's voice, low and amused, saying *count it twice, Orsen, once for the merchant and once for yourself.* Her mother's name: Maren. Her mother's death, when Orsen was nine, the fever, the narrow bed, the neighbor woman who'd taken her in and sold her, eventually, to the road.

And her mother's face.

She reached for it and found it—she was almost sure she found it. Brown eyes. A face that was tired and kind and sharp around the mouth from laughing at things she shouldn't. But when she tried to hold it, to look at it the way you look at a person across a table, it moved. The eyes drifted toward gray. The mouth was her own mouth, or the neighbor woman's, or nobody's. She could describe the face perfectly. She could not see it.

Maybe that was normal. Nine years old was a long time ago. Everyone said memories faded. Everyone said—

Everyone said, and Orsen had learned not to trust everyone, and least of all herself. She had made six edits in six nights. She had no way of knowing what she'd already paid. That was the trap inside the gift: the ledger of her own losses was kept by the same hand that had burned the receipts.

*Count it twice,* her mother's voice said. *Once for the merchant and once for yourself.*

"Once for the merchant," Orsen said aloud, to the empty room, and her voice cracked on it, and she pressed her palms flat against the ledger's cover and began.

* * *

The book remembered everything at once, and it was like drowning in paper.

She had braced for the flood of entries—the thunderclap, the borrowed years, the names, the codes, the tide-charts of human traffic that she herself had drawn. What she hadn't braced for was the book remembering *her*. Eleven years of her own handwriting. The first entry she'd ever made, age fourteen, three days after Corvane bought her off the road: *1 bolt salt-gray silk, delivered, paid.* Her hand had shaken. She could feel it shaking in the memory of the ink. The lamp in the counting room that first night. Corvane's shadow in the doorway, and his voice: *Neat figures, little keeper. The guild loves neat figures.* The way she had glowed. The way she had spent eleven years glowing at the memory of being called *little keeper*, and had never once asked herself why a man who bought a child would want her figures neat.

The book remembered the client who paid in names. It remembered the masked figure in the back room, the purse of paper slips, each one a true name written in a hand she now knew too well. It remembered her own hand copying those names into the master's margins before she understood what she was copying. Her system. Her codes. Her map of the hunted, drawn in her own neat figures, and sold—no. Given. Handed to Corvane like a child handing a parent a drawing.

The book remembered everything, and she took all of it, and she said the words.

*You have never recorded a gifted client. You are the ledger of an honest house. You remember salt, and silk, and small crimes, and nothing else. Nothing else. Nothing—*

The book let go.

* * *

She came back to herself on the floor. She didn't remember falling. The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil. Her hands were still flat on the ledger's cover, and the ledger was—different. Lighter. She lifted it and it weighed what a book should weigh, nine pounds of paper and binding and nothing else, the way a knife weighs nothing after it's gone in.

She opened it with fingers that didn't feel like hers.

The pages were clean. Not blank—clean. Entries in her handwriting, eleven years of them, all salt and silk and small crimes. *1 bolt salt-gray silk, delivered, paid.* No codes. No margins. No names that mattered. A perfect, boring, honest book, and any auditor in the king's gray could read it front to back and find nothing but a smuggler's taxes.

It had worked.

She started to laugh, and the laugh came out wrong, and halfway through it she stopped, because she was reaching for something and the something wasn't there.

She inventoried. It was what you did. Hands: present. The counting room: present. The harbor, gray—still gray, still secondhand. Her mother's voice: *count it twice.* Present. Her mother's name: Maren. Present. Her mother's—

She reached for the face and found the shape of a hole. Not a faded memory. Not the soft blur of nine years old. A hole, with edges, the way a missing tooth has edges your tongue keeps finding. She knew there had been a face there. She knew its description the way you know a description in a book: brown eyes, tired, kind, sharp around the mouth. She could recite it. She could not see it, and worse, she could not *miss* it properly, because missing requires remembering, and the remembering was what she'd paid.

She had paid her mother's face for a book full of salt and silk.

Orsen sat on the floor of the counting room with the honest ledger in her lap and did not cry, because she suspected—correctly, though she couldn't have said how she knew—that the crying would cost something too, now. That she had spent down to some last line of credit. That the woman sitting on the floor was the remainder after the transaction cleared, and the remainder had to be careful.

*Count it twice,* said the voice, and it was warm, and it was low, and it was amused, and she would never again be able to check it against a face.

"Once for the merchant," she whispered. "Once for myself." And then, because the arithmetic had to come out somewhere: "I don't remember what the second count was for."

She got up. She put the ledger back under the floorboard—no. She stopped. She put it on the desk instead, in plain sight, square to the desk's edge, the way an honest bookkeeper leaves an honest book. Let Vess find it. Let Vess read every page and find the shape of nothing.

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, because there was one entry left to make, and this one she would keep in the only ledger the crown couldn't audit.

*What I have paid,* she wrote, in her neat figures: *my mother's face, and whatever else I will discover, day by day, is gone. What I have bought: every name in this house, hidden. Worth it. Worth it. Worth it.*

Three times. Once for the merchant, once for herself, and once—she pressed the pen down until the ink bled—for the face she could no longer see, so that somewhere, in some record, it would be written down that the face had existed, and had been loved, and had been spent.

She was blotting the page when she heard the front door of the guildhall open below—unbarred, unannounced, at an hour when no honest client came—and she heard footsteps on the stairs that she knew, that she had catalogued among her most reliable memories, and she heard Corvane Dhal's voice say, pleasantly, from the doorway behind her:

"Burning the midnight oil, little keeper? I do hope the books are ready. The king's men arrive at dawn."

* * *

She turned, and her face was the face of a bookkeeper who had been working late, because that was the only face she had left that was worth anything.

"The books are ready," she said.

Corvane crossed the room and looked down at the master ledger, square on the desk, and Orsen watched him read her the way she read objects—with his hands, almost, hovering over the cover, and she realized with a cold, clarifying jolt that she had never once wondered whether the guildmaster had a gift of his own, or whether the man who bought a child for her neat figures could simply *tell* when a book had been touched by someone like her.

He smiled. It reached his eyes. It had always reached his eyes; that was the terrible thing about him.

"Clean," he said. "You've outdone yourself." And then, lightly, the way a man sets down a cup: "There's a client coming through the Tidehouse tomorrow. Someone important. I'll want you there, little keeper. There are names to be counted."

She held his smile with her own, and behind it she was counting—once for the merchant, once for herself—and finding, for the first time in eleven years, that she no longer knew which count was which.
