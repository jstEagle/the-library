## 12. Bargain at the Tidehouse

The note came folded inside a delivery of lamp-oil, which was Corvane's way of saying *don't be seen reading this.* I found it because I find everything—because for eleven years I have been the woman who knows where every coin, every secret, and every sin in the Saltspire Guild is filed. The paper was good stock, the hand was not Corvane's, and the message was four words long:

*Tidehouse. Midnight. Come alone.*

It wasn't addressed to me.

I stood in the oil-stink of the storeroom with that little square of cream paper and felt my heart do something it hadn't done since I was fourteen years old on the Salt Road with a dead man's token in my fist. It went quiet. Completely quiet. The way a house goes quiet when you realize someone else is in it.

Because I knew whose appointment this was. I keep the appointment ledger. There was nothing in it for tonight—Corvane had struck the entry himself, in his own hand, which he never does, because Corvane Dhal believes with a feverish, religious intensity that a ledger must never lie even when its keeper does. For him to remove an entry, the entry itself had become the danger.

And Corvane had left the oil invoice on top of the note. Corvane, who has not misplaced a document in thirty years. Corvane, who taught me that carelessness is a language and every slip is a sentence.

He wanted me to find it.

Or he wanted someone to find it, and I was simply the fastest reader in the building.

I told myself both of those things, and then I told myself a third thing, which was the true one, and which I had been not-saying to myself for three weeks since the masked client paid in names: *you already know what he is. You have known since the handwriting. You are only going to watch for the pleasure of being wrong.*

I am very good at arithmetic. It is the first skill a ledger-keeper learns and the last one she loses. And the arithmetic said: masked client, list of true names, payment in *passage*—passage to where, Orsen, passage to where, when the only door out of a country that officially does not exist is a slab of salt that hums under your fingers like a struck bell—and a guildmaster who meets the crown's envoy at midnight and strikes the appointment from his own book.

I put the note back under the invoice. I went upstairs. I ate supper with Corvane in the guildhall's long room, at the long table, under the map of the free cities that he had drawn himself, and I passed him the bread, and he asked after my cough—I had invented a cough two days ago so that I would have a reason to be awake at odd hours—and he said, "You work too hard, little ledger. The books will keep."

"The books always keep," I said.

He smiled at me. He has a good smile. That is the worst thing about him, or one of the worst, and I have a list.

At half past eleven he took his gray cloak and his walking stick with the silver eel's-head grip, and he told the night porter he was going to pray at the Drowned Chapel, and I watched from the gallery as the door closed behind him, and I gave him four hundred heartbeats, and then I followed.

* * *

The Tidehouse stands on the last quay of Gullwater, the one the sea took back forty years ago and never fully returned. At high tide it is an island. At low tide it is a ruin with a causeway, and at midnight in the dark of the moon it is the most private room in the free cities, because no honest person goes there and no dishonest one can be followed across the exposed flats without leaving a print in the wet sand.

So I did not cross the causeway.

I am not Sefa, to walk through fire and out the other side. I am not Isla Merrin, to kick a door in. I am Orsen Villalobos, and my gift is smaller and meaner and older than either of theirs: I read what objects remember. And the Tidehouse, thank every drowned god, is very, very old, and it has been *remembering* for a long time.

There is a mooring ring on the seaward side, iron, green with salt-rot, set into the rock below the ruined boathouse. I have touched it before, on an audit two years ago, when Corvane wanted to know who had been using the old quay and I put my palm flat against the ring and got back: rope, rope, rope, a woman's laugh, rope, a crate that screamed softly to itself for three days, rope. Objects remember what touches them. And the boathouse above the mooring ring has a hole in its roof where the gulls got in, and under the hole is a beam, and the beam has been leaned on by smugglers waiting out weather for longer than the free cities have had names.

I climbed down to the ring at half past midnight, in the black before the tide turned, and I pressed my bare hand against the iron, and I opened myself the way you open a door you are afraid is already open.

The beam gave me voices. Not words, exactly—objects don't keep words, they keep *impressions*, the shape of sound the way a footprint keeps the shape of a foot—but the beam had been leaned on tonight, within the hour, by two bodies, and the iron below it had felt their weight through the floor, and between the two of them I could reconstruct the conversation the way you reconstruct a fish from bones. Mostly. Enough.

Two men in the Tidehouse. One heavy, one slight. The heavy one had leaned on the beam for a long time, in the posture of a man listening. The slight one had paced. The iron remembered the slight one's boots: quick, quick, pause. Quick, quick, pause. A man talking himself into something, or out of it.

And the iron remembered a third thing, which I did not expect, which I have not stopped feeling since, the way you don't stop feeling a splinter: it remembered *fear*. The beam held the heavy man's weight and the beam held his fear, and fear has a taste even to iron, and the taste was cold and official, like a seal pressed into wax.

The heavy man was afraid of the slight one.

The heavy man was Corvane Dhal. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Thirty years of leaning on things in this city. The beam knew him the way the guildhall knows him.

Corvane Dhal, who once held a knife in his teeth to swim a strait with me on his back when I was fifteen and feverish. Corvane Dhal, who was afraid.

I stayed crouched at the mooring ring with my hand on the iron and the tide whispering back in across the flats, and I listened to a dead beam tell me a living secret, and I waited for them to come out.

* * *

They came out an hour before dawn, when the sky was the gray of old dishwater. I had moved by then—up the rock, into the boathouse, flat against the wall where the beam's own memory would hide me, because there is a kind of camouflage in being inside the thing you are reading. The slight man went first, down the causeway, quick quick pause, quick quick pause, and even in the dark I knew the walk, because I have audited the crown's requisitions and the envoy to the free cities signs his name with that same self-satisfied little hitch: Master Envoy Callow Vess, a man whose boots have never once been wet by accident.

Corvane came out last. He stood in the broken doorway of the Tidehouse for a long moment, and the lightening sky put him in silhouette, and he looked—there is no kind way to say this—he looked *old*. He looked like a man standing in the doorway of a house he had just sold out from under the people sleeping in it.

Then he did something I will carry to whatever grave is coming for me. He reached into his cloak and took out a small object, and he held it up to the gray light, and I knew its shape before I knew what it was, because I had catalogued it, because I catalogue everything.

The guild token. The one from the Salt Road. The one he bought me with.

He turned it over twice, slowly, like a man checking a coin for clipping. Then he put it away, and he came down the causeway toward me, and I did the only thing I could do, which was to stop existing. I made myself into ledger-keeper's nothing, the gray absence I had spent eleven years perfecting, and he passed within six feet of me on the causeway and did not turn his head, because Corvane Dhal does not see what the books do not tell him to see.

That used to be my safety. That night it felt like the punchline of a joke only I would ever hear.

* * *

The beam had given me bones. I needed the fish. So the next day I went where I should have gone the moment I read the note: to the objects.

The Tidehouse's great room had been swept, but sweeping is a lie told with a broom. In the corner behind the broken dais I found a candle stub, burned down to a thumbnail, and I have read enough candle stubs to know they are the gossips of the object world—wax remembers heat, and heat remembers voices better than iron does, because a candle is *present* for everything said over it. This one had burned for perhaps two hours. It had been lit at midnight and pinched out at the end.

I sat down on the cold flagstone in the ruined Tidehouse with a candle stub in my cupped hands, and I read it, and this is what the night said:

Two voices. The slight one—Vess—did most of the talking, and his talking had the texture of a contract being read aloud. The heavy one—Corvane—spoke less, and when he spoke, the candle remembered the words as *shapes*, and I am going to set down the shapes as honestly as I can, because I promised myself in the ashes of my mother's face that I would never again flinch from what a reading gives me.

Vess's voice, in the candle's memory: *the Edict is not a request, Dhal. His Majesty has auditors in every free city by spring. When they come, they will not find a guild. They will find a conspiracy with excellent bookkeeping.* A pause. Quick, quick, pause. *You can be a conspiracy. Or you can be an instrument. The charter survives either way. Only the name changes.*

Corvane's voice, and here the candle's memory went strange and hot, the way wax does when a hand trembles near the flame: *You are asking me to sell the Door.*

*The crown does not know what it is,* Vess said. *That is the beauty of it. A door of salt on a drowned quay. We will take it, weigh it, and grind it into the sea walls of the capital, and your impossible country will be a rumor with no address, and the gift will have nowhere to run to, and the Edict will close like a hand. All you must do is tell us where it stands and stand aside when we come for it.*

*And the people on your list.*

*Are already sold,* said Callow Vess, with the mildness of a man discussing freight. *You sold them yourself, guildmaster. We are merely discussing the delivery.*

And Corvane Dhal—the man who pulled me out of the mud of the Salt Road, who taught me my letters and my ledgers, who never once in eleven years raised his voice to me—Corvane was silent for the length of ten heartbeats, and then he said, in a voice the candle kept as something small and scraped hollow:

*The charter. In the guild's name. Not mine. And the children's names come off the list.*

*Two names,* said Vess. *You may keep two.*

And that was the bargain. That is the bargain, sitting right now on the table between the Saltspire Guild and the crown of King Aldric like a plate of food no one wants to eat first: the Door of Brine—the last way out, the way I opened a crack onto a shore under a foreign sun, the way out for every name in my ledgers, for Sefa with her banked fire, for the family in the warrens whose route I still know by heart—in exchange for a charter, a legal fiction, a piece of vellum that says the Saltspire Guild may go on smuggling *permitted* impossibilities with the crown's blessing.

He is trading a country for a license.

He is trading every soul I have spent three weeks bleeding my own memory to hide, and he has asked for two of them back, and I do not know yet which two, and I am afraid—I am so afraid—that I am one of them.

* * *

I did not go back to the guildhall. I walked the low-tide flats until the water came in behind me, and then I walked the high road, and then I walked the warrens, and by the time the sun was up I had talked myself through every door out of the truth and found them all locked.

He bought me off the road at fourteen. I have known that since the token. He bought me, and he asked in return for my gift, quietly, forever—the token remembers the shape of that bargain too, the *find the gifted, keep the lists, be my eyes and never my voice*. I told myself for years that the asking was the price of the saving, that all shelter has a rent. Fine. All shelter has a rent.

But there is a difference between a rent and a sale. And there is a difference between a man who shelters the hunted and a man who *farms* them.

Because here is the arithmetic I could not make balance, and I am very good at arithmetic: if Corvane has been selling names to the crown since before the Edict—since the masked client, since the handwriting I recognized, since longer, since maybe always—then every entry in my ledgers, every careful coded line I kept for him, every *one jarred thunderclap, one widow's grief, paid in full*, was not bookkeeping.

It was inventory.

He did not save a runaway apprentice on the Salt Road. He acquired one. A gifted child who could read what objects remember, who could find the hidden and catalogue the impossible, who would grow up loyal because loyalty was the leash. And now the leash has pulled taut, because the crown has offered him a charter, and a charter is just a leash with the crown's seal on the other end, and Corvane Dhal has spent his whole life holding one end of a leash and has never once been on the wrong end of it and does not intend to start.

I stood on the high road above Gullwater with the sun coming up over a country that officially does not exist, and I made myself say it in words, inside my own skull, where no object could ever read it and no man could ever sell it:

He is going to give them the Door. He is going to give them Vael. He is going to grind the last way out of the world into the king's sea walls, and he will do it with my ledgers in one hand and my leash in the other, and he will call it mercy, and he will even believe it is mercy, because Corvane Dhal has never in his life done a cruel thing that he could not first do a kind thing to.

I loved him. I want that set down somewhere no one will audit. Not the way the ballads mean it—there was never time for that kind, and he was old, and I was mud and salt and secrets—but the way you love a wall that holds up the roof. The way you love the thing that stands between you and the weather. And now I have seen the wall from the other side, and the other side is a door, and the door is open, and the weather is coming through.

* * *

I went back at noon. I walked into the guildhall with my ledger-keeper's face on and my ledger-keeper's nothing around me like a cloak, and Corvane was in the counting room with the morning's correspondence, and he looked up, and he smiled his good smile, and he said, "You look tired, little ledger. Bad night?"

"Bad tide," I said. "The flats were up past the mooring ring. I couldn't get a reading."

Something crossed his face. It was there and gone, quick as a fish under a boat—something that was almost relief and almost disappointment and was, I think now, the look of a man who has just checked a lock and found it holding. "Tides lie," he said, and went back to his letters.

And that was when I knew I could not do this alone, and I could not do it slowly, and I could not do it with the careful patience of a ledger-keeper, because the crown's wagons would come for the Door within the month and Corvane's pen was already moving toward the signature.

There was exactly one person in the free cities who wanted the Door as badly as I did and had the temper to do something about it, and she was currently under a formal vow of silence in the guild's east wing for setting fire to a delegate's chair at the parliament, and her name was on Corvane's list—I had seen it, in his hand, in the masked client's payment: *Sefa of the Ash Quay, fire-gifted, unregistered.*

I found her in the east wing, sitting in the dark with her hands open on her knees, and when I lit the lamp she looked up at me with eyes like banked coals, and I said the only true thing I had left:

"Sefa. I need to show you your name in his handwriting."

She stood up. The lamp flame leaned toward her like it was listening.

And behind us, down in the counting room, I heard Corvane Dhal begin to dictate a letter to the capital, in a warm and reasonable voice, about *certain arrangements to be concluded at the Tidehouse, at the turn of the spring tide.*
