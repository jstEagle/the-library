## 13. Sefa's Fire

Sefa found the list by accident, which was the only way anyone ever found anything in Corvane Dhal's guildhall. He kept his truths like a miser kept coins—buried, weighted, and counted nightly.

She hadn't come for truths. She'd come for lamp oil.

The parliament had broken up two nights past with nothing settled and everyone angry, and she'd spent those two nights not sleeping, turning over Corvane's smooth voice in her head like a stone with something wrong underneath it. *Bargain,* he'd said. *We bargain, we survive. The crown wants order, not ash.* And half the delegates had nodded along, because Corvane Dhal could make surrender sound like strategy.

Sefa didn't nod. Sefa burned things when they needed burning, and her whole body had been telling her since the Tidehouse vote that something in that building needed it.

So: lamp oil. The guildhall storerooms on the third floor were always unlocked after midnight—Corvane's arrogance, or his carelessness, or both—and she'd slipped in through the coal chute with a hood over her fire-gift and a lie ready on her tongue if anyone asked. *I'm fetching ink for the delegate's office. I'm checking the stores. I'm nobody.*

Nobody. That was what the free cities had made her, one registration notice at a time.

The storeroom she wanted was locked.

That alone should have sent her back down the chute. Locked doors in the Saltspire Guild meant either treasure or trouble, usually both, and Sefa had survived nineteen years in the warrens by never opening doors that someone else had decided to close. But the lock was old brass, green with salt rot, and the door itself was newer than its frame—a replacement, recent, hasty—and there was a smell coming through the gap at the threshold. Paper. Old paper, and under it, faint as a dying candle, tallow and quill-ink.

Ledgers. Someone was keeping ledgers in a locked room above the lamp oil.

Her gift woke in her palms the way it always did when she was frightened or furious, which for the last two years had been most of the time. Heat crawled up her forearms. She pressed her hands flat against the wood until the lock's brass went soft as butter and gave.

Inside: shelves. Rows of them, floor to ceiling, and every shelf stacked with books. Not the guild's public accounts—she knew those, fat and dull and full of fish-tariff figures. These were different. Smaller. Bound in leather gone soft with handling, their spines cracked from being opened too often, too urgently. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

She pulled one down at random and opened it by feel, because her fingertips had started to smoke and she didn't trust herself to hold anything gently.

A name. A street in Saltrow. A description of a woman who could make milk keep for a month in high summer. A price paid. A date.

She pulled another. A boy in the Tidewalk district who could find lost things. Another. A man whose shadow moved independently. Another, and another, and another, each entry written in the same careful clerk's hand, each one a life reduced to a line item, each one—

She stopped.

Halfway through the fourth book, in an entry dated three years past, she found herself.

*Delegate Sefa Anharra. Fire-gifted, uncontrolled under stress. Purchased protection following the Lantern Street incident. Payment rendered: ongoing political usefulness. Status: asset, active.*

*Asset.*

She read it again. Her own name, in that tidy hand, filed between a luck-bringer and a storm-jar seller like a bolt of cloth or a cask of brandy. *Asset, active.* As if she were a ship. As if she were cargo.

The heat came off her in waves then, real heat, and the shelf in front of her began to brown at the edges. She didn't stop it. She couldn't remember, afterward, whether she'd even tried.

Because she understood now—not slowly, not with dawning horror, but all at once, the way a fire understands dry timber—what this room was. Not a refuge. A catalogue. Every name the guild had ever sheltered, every debt of protection called in, every favor converted into leverage, all of it written down in a clerk's beautiful hand so that it could be *used*. Sold to the crown, maybe. Traded. Bargained away in whatever arrangement Corvane Dhal was building behind the parliament's closed doors.

*Bargain,* he'd said.

This was the bargain. This room. These names.

The oil was still in the chute below where she'd cached it. She fetched it without deciding to. She remembered, later, walking down the stairs with the first book held against her chest like a wounded bird, and then she remembered standing in the middle of the main hall with five jars of lamp oil and no memory of having carried them up.

She poured the first jar out in a long arc across the floor.

"You know," said a voice behind her, "I audited that floorboard last spring."

Sefa spun, flame already climbing her wrists.

Orsen Villalobos stood in the doorway of the hall, a lantern in one hand and a ledger—the same kind, the same binding—as the ones upstairs clutched in the other. She looked tired the way stone looks tired: permanently, structurally, as though exhaustion had been built into her at the quarry.

"The grain runs east-west," Orsen continued, as though Sefa weren't glowing like a forge. "Oil follows the grain. You'll take out the hall, the stairs, and the records annex before you take out the locked room. Fire climbs. The room you want is above you."

"I don't want the room." Sefa's voice came out low and rough. "I want it *gone*."

"I know."

"He wrote me down." The words tore out of her. "Three years I've sat in his parliament. Three years I've argued for these people, bled for them, and he wrote me down like—" She couldn't finish. The flames on her wrists turned white.

"Yes," Orsen said. "He did."

"And you *knew*." It wasn't a question. Sefa could see it in the woman's face, in the way she didn't flinch from the light. "You're the clerk. That's your handwriting. That's *your* hand, Villalobos. Every name in that room went through you."

Orsen set the lantern down. Slowly. Deliberately, the way a person sets down a weapon they've decided not to use.

"Every name," she agreed. "Including mine."

The white flame flickered. Sefa stared. "What?"

"Page forty-one, second book from the window. Runaway apprentice, object-reader, purchased off the road at fourteen." Orsen's voice stayed flat, but Sefa saw her hands. Saw the fine tremor in them. "He bought me the same way he bought you. We are all entries in that room, Sefa. I've known longer than you have. That's the only difference between us right now, and it's the difference between us because I've been too much of a coward to burn it myself."

"Then move." Sefa raised her hands. "I'm doing it tonight. Me, not you. You've had your chance—"

"*Wait.*"

The word cracked across the hall like a whip, and something in it—not authority, something rawer—made Sefa pause with fire pooling in her cupped hands like water.

"There are four hundred and twelve names in that room," Orsen said. "Four hundred and twelve people who are alive right now because the guild's protection held. If you burn that room tonight, you don't burn Corvane. He sleeps in the Tidehouse wing; he won't even smell the smoke. You burn four hundred and twelve people's cover. You hand King Aldric a bonfire of evidence and a list of the dead who died running. Is that what you want? Ash and a body count?"

"I want him to *pay*—"

"So do I." Orsen took one step forward, into the ring of Sefa's light, close enough that the heat must have been scorching her face. She didn't blink. "But payment isn't fire. Payment is taking everything he built and making it useless. Payment is the truth, Sefa, and I am so tired of carrying it alone."

For a long moment the only sound was the oil ticking as it soaked into the floorboards, and Sefa's own blood in her ears.

Then she closed her fists, and the fire went out.

"Talk fast," she said. "And start with why a clerk keeps a lantern lit in a hall she's about to let burn."

* * *

They sat on the steps of the dais where Corvane held court, because there was nowhere else, and Orsen talked.

She told her about the ledgers—all of them, the public ones and the true ones, the architecture of a smuggling empire built on paper. She told her about the gift: reading what objects remember. "A coin remembers whose pocket it warmed. A knife remembers what it cut. Paper remembers everything." She told her about the night, weeks ago, when she'd realized what her ledgers were worth to Aldric's auditors—a complete map of every gifted soul in the free cities, indexed and cross-referenced by the one person the crown would never suspect.

She told her about the edits.

"What edits?" Sefa asked.

Orsen was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flatter, careful, the way people speak about wounds they've stopped looking at.

"When I rewrite what paper remembers, the memory has to go somewhere. It comes out of me instead. I altered an entry once—a widow in Saltrow who paid us in bottled storms. Made the guild forget her gift. Cost me the smell of my mother's kitchen." She turned the ledger over in her lap. "I've done it forty-one times since. Small erasures. A friend's laugh. The color of a door I grew up behind. Last week I rewrote the master ledger wholesale to blind the auditors." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "I don't remember my mother's face anymore. I traded it for four hundred strangers."

Sefa felt her rage shift shape—still hot, but aimed differently now. Not at the clerk. At the arithmetic. At a world where women like this one had to pay for other people's lives in pieces of themselves while men like Corvane slept sound in the Tidehouse wing.

"And the Door?" she said quietly. "Parliament heard rumors. The salt slab. Tell me that's real, because if it isn't, I'm going back upstairs for the oil."

"It's real." And here, for the first time, Orsen smiled—a small, fierce thing, like a match struck in a cellar. "It opens onto Vael. The country that never existed. I've stood on its shore. There's sun there that isn't ours, Sefa. Grass that grows in salt. Whatever guards it, whatever the crossing costs—we can get them out. All of them. The four hundred and twelve. Maybe more."

"But Corvane—"

"Corvane offered the Door to the crown." Orsen said it simply, and it landed like a hammer. "Two nights ago. I stood behind a screen in the Tidehouse and listened to him trade the last way out of this country for a charter with his signature on it. He's selling the Door, the names, and us. All of it. Everything except himself."

The silence stretched. Somewhere overhead, wind worried at the shutters.

Sefa looked down at her hands. Hands that had burned a tithe-house at fifteen. Hands that had been learning, painfully, slowly, to build warmth instead of destruction—to heat bathwater for sick children in the warrens, to keep a greenhouse alive through frost. Hands that wanted, more than anything in the world, to make one particular man into smoke.

"We steal it," she said. "Before his wagons come. Before the envoy returns. We take the Door out from under him."

"*We*?" Orsen raised an eyebrow.

"Don't flatter yourself. I need a clerk. Somebody has to know which locks remember being picked." But Sefa heard her own voice soften, and hated it a little. "There's a third, if you can stomach it. The sergeant. Merrin. She pocketed a name at the Ashford registrations instead of reporting it—I watched her do it. She's cracking, Villalobos. Cracking the useful way."

"A hunter."

"A mirror." Sefa stood, brushing oil from her skirts. "You said payment is truth. Fine. Here's mine: I nearly torched four hundred people tonight because I couldn't stand being an entry in someone else's book. So the truth is—I need this to be more than arson. I need it to be a rescue. Help me make it one."

Orsen rose too, and for a moment the two of them stood in the great hall of the man who owned them both, in the smell of lamp oil that would need mopping up before dawn, and something passed between them that neither would have called friendship yet, but that both would later recognize as the beginning of it.

"One condition," Orsen said. "Nobody burns anything else without telling me first. I've lost enough memories to last a lifetime. I'd like to keep the next few."

Sefa laughed—a short, surprised bark, the first in days. "No promises. But I'll warn you."

At the door, Orsen paused. "Sefa. The list you found. Did you look at all of it?"

"Most of it. Why?"

"Because the client who paid in names—the masked buyer, three weeks ago—that list came from Corvane too. If your name was in the storeroom..." Orsen's face had gone pale in the lantern light. "Check the sale copies. Third shelf, black binding. Check who else he sold."

Sefa climbed back up the stairs with her heart hammering, and pulled down the black-bound book, and opened it by the light of her own cupped, trembling flame.

Her name was there. In the sold column. Alongside sixty others—and near the top, dated just yesterday, a name that made the fire in her hands gutter and flare:

*Sergeant Ilsa Merrin, Crown Hunter, 4th Regiment. Registered sympathizer. Status: flagged for arrest.*

"She's already marked," Sefa whispered, to the empty room, to the woman waiting below. "Villalobos—they've already marked the sergeant."
