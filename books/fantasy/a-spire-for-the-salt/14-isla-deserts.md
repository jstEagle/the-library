## 14. Isla Deserts

The order came at dusk, written in the king's own hand, and it was only three lines long.

*The village of Marrow's Rest has failed to surrender its registered bearers. Burn the registers. Burn the houses. Burn the bearers. Set an example.*

Isla Merrin read it twice by lantern light in the command tent, while around her the vanguard's officers ate a supper of salt pork and boiled barley and talked about horses. Captain Veyne, who had signed for the order, was telling a story about a race he'd lost as a boy. He told it well. He had a good laugh, open-faced, the kind of man you'd trust with your purse.

She looked at his laughing mouth and thought: *he'll do it without hating anyone.* That was the worst part. Not cruelty. Arithmetic. Marrow's Rest was forty households, eleven of them registered bearers, one day's ride from the coast road where Aldric's supply trains would pass within the month. Eleven lives against the security of a road. Any quartermaster could make that sum come out right.

"Something wrong, Sergeant?" Veyne asked, catching her look.

"No, sir."

"You've been to Marrow's Rest before."

"Registered them myself. Last spring." She kept her voice level. "Quiet village. They gave up their names without a fight."

"And now they won't give up their neighbors." He shrugged, spreading butter on bread. "That's what happens when you're kind, Merrin. They mistake it for weakness." He said it without malice too, which somehow made it worse. "We ride at first light. I want ash on the wind by noon."

Isla stood in the tent flap long after the officers turned to their wine, watching the dark come down over the camp, and she did her own arithmetic, the one she'd been doing since the hedge-witch in the undercity, since the name she'd pocketed instead of reporting, since the night she'd lain awake cataloguing every pyre she'd lit with paperwork instead of fire.

Registration feeds protection. That had been the promise. Register, and be counted, and be safe.

She had watched the lists move from her office to the provosts' office to the pyre-crews' office, and at no point along that chain had anyone done anything except exactly what they were ordered to do, and the result was smoke. Order was mercy. She had believed it the way you believe a floor will hold you. And now the floor was tilting, and everything she'd stacked her life on—duty, procedure, the clean weight of the king's seal—was sliding toward the same fire she'd been sent to light.

She went back to her tent and took out the list.

It was the master register extract for the coastal districts, the one she'd carried through three campaigns, the one that made her useful. Forty-one names in her current sector. She'd read them so many times she knew them like a psalm: *Aldous Wren, cooper. The sisters Fen, weavers. Maren Cole, midwife.* People. Just people, with trades attached, the way you'd list livestock.

Except now there was a forty-second name, added in her own hand, hidden between two entries: the hedge-witch from the warrens, the one whose eyes had begged her not to write anything down even as Isla wrote it down. Except Isla hadn't reported that one. It lived on the page like a splinter.

She sat with the list until the camp went quiet, and then she did the thing she could not un-do.

She folded it into her coat, against her heart, and she blew out her lamp.

* * *

Marrow's Rest smelled of woodsmoke already when they rode in—bread ovens, morning fires, the ordinary smell of a place where people expected to keep living—and Isla hated how much it hurt, that ordinary smell.

Forty households. She remembered them from last spring: the miller with flour in his beard who'd joked about the crown finally taking an interest; the old woman, Sera Tull, who'd registered her gift of finding lost things and then immediately used it to find Isla's dropped glove, pressing it back into her hand with a conspirator's wink. *There, Sergeant. First freebie.*

They did not resist with weapons. That was the terrible thing. When the troop came up the cart-road with torches unlit but visible, the village simply... closed. Doors shut. Shutters barred. And from inside the houses, from behind the windows, came singing.

Not a battle hymn. A work song, the kind harvesters sing, low and steady, dozens of voices braided together, and threaded through it—faint, unmistakable—was the hum Isla had learned to dread in registered bearers: the sound of small gifts being used all at once. She couldn't see what they were doing. But the horses began to shy. The torches guttered though there was no wind. And the well water, when a trooper drew some for the horses, came up tasting of salt—the sea, impossibly, eight days inland.

"They're resisting," Lieutenant Hask said, dismounting, sounding almost pleased to have it confirmed. "That's the order confirmed, then. Captain?"

Veyne looked at the closed doors, at the shuttered windows, at the thin ribbon of song coming through it all, and Isla watched him arrive at the arithmetic without flinching. "Form the line," he said. "Sergeant Merrin. You know these people. Go to the door of the headman's house and offer terms one final time. Names surrendered by noon, or we proceed."

"Yes, sir."

She walked up the cart-road alone. The singing didn't stop, but it changed—she could hear it change, the way a room goes quiet around an argument. At the headman's door she knocked, and after a long moment it opened on a chain, and Sera Tull's grandson—a boy of maybe twelve, wide-eyed—stared up at her.

"I need to speak with your grandmother."

"She said you'd come." The boy's voice shook. "She said you'd come with fire."

"Not yet."

"That's what the other sergeant said. In Corvane's city. Before." He shut the door.

Isla stood on the step with the sun climbing and the song going on behind the walls, and she understood, suddenly and completely, that there were no terms left to offer. Terms required two parties who believed the same thing about tomorrow. These people had already done their arithmetic. Eleven names, forty households, one road—they had decided that the sum was wrong everywhere, always, no matter who did the counting.

She walked back down the cart-road. The singing followed her.

"Well?" Veyne asked.

"They refuse."

He nodded, unsurprised, and turned to his line. "Torches."

And Isla Merrin, seventeen years in the king's service, who had registered the gifted because registration meant protection, who had believed order was mercy the way other people believed in gods—stood in a village street with a torch being pressed into her hand, and heard herself say:

"No."

The word came out small. Veyne turned.

"What did you say?"

"I said no." Louder now, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "Captain, these are registered bearers. They complied with the Edict. Their names are on crown lists that I personally compiled. If registration delivers them to a pyre, then the registration was never mercy—it was a census for the slaughter. I won't be part of it."

The line went very quiet. Somewhere a horse shifted its weight.

"Merrin." Veyne came close, and his voice was gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet. "You're tired. You've been in the field a long time. Give me the torch and go sit by the wagons, and we'll speak of this later—"

"I'm giving you my answer, sir. Not my torch."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him re-calculate—not her loyalty, that was already ash, but her usefulness, her rank, the cost of making an example of a decorated sergeant versus the cost of letting defiance stand. She saw him decide that the example mattered more.

"Sergeant Isla Merrin," he said, formally, loudly, for the benefit of the line, "you are relieved of command. You are under arrest for refusal of lawful orders and suspected sedition. Disarm her."

Two troopers moved toward her, apologetic men, careful hands, and Isla let them take her sword, because fighting them would only get someone hurt and change nothing. But when the second one reached for her coat, she turned, fast, and put her shoulder into him, and ran—not for the trees, but straight up the cart-road, into the village, hammering on the headman's door.

"Open! Please—I have thirty seconds—"

The chain rattled. The door swung. She fell through it into a kitchen that smelled of bread, and the boy bolted it behind her, and through the window she saw the torches finally catch, saw the first house begin to burn, saw Veyne's face turn toward the door she'd vanished through with an expression she'd remember the rest of her life: not rage. Recalculation.

"They'll breach in minutes," the boy said. "Grandmother says the cellar. There's a way out through the—"

"I know about the cellar." She didn't, actually. But Sera Tull was already there, lifting the flagstones, ancient and calm, winking at her again—that same conspirator's wink, aimed now at a different crime. "First freebie," the old woman said.

* * *

The tunnel came out in a bramble-choked ravine half a mile east, and eleven people climbed out of the earth into the grey light of a burning village's smoke-shadow, and Isla counted them automatically, out of habit, the way she'd counted them on the register.

Aldous Wren the cooper. The Fen sisters. Maren Cole the midwife. Sera Tull and her grandson. Seven more. All eleven names from her sector's list, all here, all alive, looking at her with expressions she couldn't read.

"You're the one who wrote us down," Maren Cole said. Not accusing. Just establishing the record.

"Yes."

"And now?"

"Now I'm the one they're hunting." Isla pulled the folded list from her coat—her own copy, the master extract, forty-one names plus her splinter of a forty-second—and held it out. "These are all of you. Every registered bearer in the coastal district. What the crown knows, this paper knows. I should have burned it months ago. Instead I'm giving it to you, because I can't be trusted with it anymore, and neither can any office."

Wren took it carefully, like it might bite. "What are you saying, Sergeant?"

"I'm saying the law has finished with me, and I find I don't mourn it." Her voice was steady but her hands weren't, and she let them shake where everyone could see. "I spent seventeen years believing that if we just wrote everything down correctly, the writing would protect people. It doesn't. Writing is just a cage with good penmanship. So." She looked at each of them in turn. "I have no warrant, no rank, no legal existence as of this morning. What I have is fifteen years of knowing every patrol route, safehouse, and blind spot in this district, and a very good memory for which magistrates can still be shamed and which ones can't. If you want it, it's yours."

Silence, except the far-off crackle of Marrow's Rest becoming an example.

It was Sera Tull who broke it, of course. "You found your way here," she said. "Lost things. That's my gift, finding lost things, and I'm telling you—" she tapped Isla once on the breastbone, right over the folded nothing where the list used to be, "—you're the lost thing today, girl. Everything else is details."

The Fen sisters laughed, wet-eyed. Maren Cole put her hand briefly on Isla's arm. And Aldous Wren folded the list into his coat and said, "Then we'd best move, because by noon every road out of this district will have Veyne's riders on it, and by evening our names will be worth gold in every garrison from here to the Spire."

"Where do we go?" the boy asked.

Isla opened her mouth to say the safehouse she trusted least, the border route she'd mapped and never used—and stopped. Because there was another answer, wasn't there? A rumor moving through the undercities faster than any crier: a door. Salt. Brine. A country off the maps. She'd dismissed it as smugglers' talk, the kind of tale that thins out the desperate and fills the mass graves.

But she'd seen a salt slab humming in a guild warehouse once, on an audit, and she'd thought nothing of it, and she thought of nothing harder now than she'd ever thought of anything.

"There's a rumor," she said slowly. "About a Door. Smugglers run it—or something does. The Saltspire Guild." She watched the name land on them; even here, even now, everyone knew the guild that traded in impossible things. "If it's real, it's real for everyone. If it's a trap, I'd rather walk into it carrying these people than spend the rest of my life running from a fire I lit."

"The Tidehouse," Maren Cole said unexpectedly. "My cousin's daughter married into the harbor folk. If anyone knows how to find the guild's people, it's the tide-readers."

"Then that's our road." Isla looked back once, at the smoke standing over Marrow's Rest like a black tree, at the mounted figures moving through the ruins, at the country that had been hers for seventeen years and was no longer. Somewhere in that smoke was her sword, her commission, her name in the army rolls—all the paper versions of herself, burning on schedule.

Let them burn. She was done being a list.

"Keep off the ridgelines," she said, and turned east, and the eleven of them moved out through the brambles toward the coast, toward the tide, toward whatever waited at the end of a rumor—

—and behind them, faint on the wind, she could already hear the horns: the hunt called, the dogs loosed, her description passing from mouth to mouth. *One deserter, armed with nothing, traveling with eleven known bearers.*

Eleven names in her keeping. And somewhere ahead, in a city of ledgers and salt, a woman who kept books for smugglers—who might, if the rumors were true, be the only person in the free cities who knew exactly what paper could and couldn't be trusted—

Isla quickened her pace.
