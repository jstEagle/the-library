## 15. Three Thieves of a Country

**Orsen**

The Tidehouse stood on stilts over the drowned quay, its legs furred with barnacle, its windows long gone to salt-haze. Once it had been a counting-house for the pearl divers. Now it was where Corvane kept the thing he had decided to sell: a door-shaped slab of white salt, humming in the dark like a struck tuning fork.

Orsen crouched in the fog at the edge of the quay and listened to the water slap the pilings, and tried to remember what her mother's face looked like.

Nothing came. There was a shape where the memory should be—a warmth, a direction, like a room she'd once stood in. But the face itself had been spent, spent along with a hundred other pieces of her, traded away entry by entry to blind the crown's auditors, to hide the gifted, to keep the ledger honest when the ledger was the only honest thing left in the free cities.

She touched the empty place the way you touch a missing tooth. Then she stopped touching it, because Sefa was hissing at her from the shadow of the first piling.

"You're doing the thing again," Sefa whispered. "The thing where you stand in the rain and have a crisis."

"It's not raining. It's fog."

"It's emotional fog, then. Move."

They crossed the quay in the gray hour before dawn, when the tide was lowest and the crown's sentries were coldest and stupidest. Orsen had walked this route in her head forty times. Ledger-keeper's habit: know the numbers, know the exits, know exactly how much everything weighs. The salt door weighed, according to Corvane's own acquisition ledger, four hundred and eleven pounds. Which was why they'd brought Isla.

Isla Merrin was waiting at the Tidehouse's rear stair, out of uniform, in a fishwife's oilskin, with a handcart and the expression of a woman who had recently committed treason and found it agreed with her. Two weeks since she'd walked out of the burning village at Saltmere with its people scattered behind her like dropped coins. Two weeks since her own name had gone onto the very list she'd once enforced.

"You're late," Isla said.

"You're early," Orsen said.

"I've been early my whole life. It's what made me a good soldier." Isla looked at Sefa, and there was something careful in it—two weeks of carefulness, of learning to stand next to fire without flinching. "You're sure about the powder?"

"Very sure," Sefa said, patting the satchel at her hip. "The wagons come up the coast road at first light. The escort stops at the tollhouse for the changing of the watch. That's four minutes where nobody official is looking at the quay. We take the door, we take it down the old pearl ramp, we put it on a barge, and by the time anyone asks questions the fog has eaten everything."

"And if the fog doesn't cooperate?"

Sefa smiled, and the smile had a little light in it that wasn't entirely a figure of speech. "Then I make my own weather."

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa had never thought of herself as a thief. A delegate, once. A voice in the smugglers' parliament, once. A girl whose name was on a list written by a man she'd toasted wine with, currently.

But a thief, no. Thieves took things that belonged to someone. The salt door didn't belong to Corvane Dhal. It belonged to a country that had been erased from every map, to every soul who'd ever been told their gift was a debt to be managed. Corvane was just the man currently standing on it.

She went up the rear stair first because fire goes first, that was the whole doctrine of her life now. The Tidehouse interior was one long room, rafters dripping, and there it stood at the center of the floor on a pallet of oak: the door. White salt, man-high, door-shaped, with a lintel and a sill and a keyhole of dark crystal where a lock should be. Even in the fog-dim it seemed to make its own weather, a faint coolness in the air, a hum she felt in her back teeth.

"Gods," Isla said behind her, quietly. "It's real. I half thought you were all mad."

"It's real," Orsen said. "And it's awake. Don't touch the keyhole. It reads you."

"What does it read?"

Orsen was quiet a moment. Sefa watched her put her hand near—not on—the dark crystal, and watched the crystal brighten like an eye opening.

"Names," Orsen said. "It wants a true name, freely given. Corvane told me that once, when he thought I wasn't listening. He's been trying to get one out of me for six years." She pulled her hand back. "We're not giving it anything tonight. We're just stealing it. It can be offended later."

Sefa knelt at the satchel and started laying the powder charges along the seaward wall. Not to blow the Tidehouse—just enough to drop the old pearl ramp's counterweights, the ones the divers used to haul their catch up from the deep beds. Physics was just fire that had learned patience. Her grandmother had taught her that, before the crown taught her grandmother other things.

Outside, far up the coast road, a lantern swung. Then another. Then the deep, patient groan of wagon wheels.

"Early," Isla breathed. "They're early. That's my trick."

* * *

**Isla**

Isla Merrin had spent eleven years learning how the crown moved: its schedules, its habits, its arithmetic. First light, changing of the watch, four unsupervised minutes at the tollhouse. She had written half those schedules herself. So when the wagons came up the coast road a full hour before first light, with lanterns burning and an escort of twelve, she understood two things at once.

The first was that someone had told them.

The second was that the someone was a man who kept ledgers.

"They know," she said. "Corvane sold us the schedule and then sold them the correction. We're the diversion. We were always the diversion—he wants us caught here, red-handed, so his delivery looks like a recovery."

Orsen's face did something terrible and small. Not surprise. Confirmation. "The acquisition ledger," she said. "He logged the transfer for tomorrow. I read it. I thought— I thought we had until tomorrow."

"You thought a man who sells children off the road wouldn't sell you," Isla said, and hated herself for the cruelty of it, and said it anyway, because kindness was a luxury for after.

Down on the quay, lanterns were spreading. Boots on wet stone, a dozen men fanning out in the practiced geometry of a sweep. Isla had run that geometry a hundred times. She knew where its seams were.

"Ramp's the only way down to the barge," she said. "They'll cover it in ninety seconds. Sefa—can you make fog?"

"I can make *fire*."

"Fire makes fog. Smoke. Enough smoke and their lanterns become a liability—they'll douse them or blind themselves. Either way, we get a count of ten." Isla pulled the fishwife's oilskin tighter and felt, absurdly, like a soldier again. Not the crown's soldier. Some other kind, one with no manual yet. "Orsen. The door. Can you move it?"

"It weighs four hundred and eleven pounds."

"Then we don't move it. We sink it." She pointed at the black water below the Tidehouse, where the old pearl beds went down thirty fathoms into cold and dark. "Salt doesn't rot. Salt doesn't drown. The crown can't carry off what it can't find, and nothing finds anything down there. We drop it off the ramp, mark the spot, come back with divers when the hunt's moved on."

Sefa was already grinning. Orsen looked at the door—the humming, patient, door-shaped door—and Isla saw her put her hand flat against the white salt, and saw the salt brighten under her palm like something recognizing her.

"It remembers being a doorway," Orsen said softly. "It doesn't like being cargo." She looked up. "But it'll hold its breath. Doors are good at that."

* * *

**Orsen**

The first charge went off with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Sefa's smoke rolled across the quay in a fat gray tide, and the lanterns below became smudges, and the shouts became confused, and Isla was moving—gods, the woman could move—hauling the handcart to the ramp head while Orsen and Sefa put their shoulders to the salt door.

It was heavier than four hundred and eleven pounds. It was heavier than it had any right to be, and Orsen understood, with her palm against it, that the weight was a *choice*. The door was asking. *Who moves me, and why?*

"For them," Orsen said through her teeth, not sure if she was talking to the door or to the other two. "For everyone on Corvane's list. For the ones already burned. Freely. We're doing this freely."

The door's hum changed pitch. It did not get lighter. But it got *willing*, which was different, and the three of them walked it down the ramp one groaning step at a time while the smoke boiled and somewhere below a sergeant's voice—she knew that voice, she'd hidden a family from that voice, and the voice had let her, and she'd never said thank you—bellowed for lanterns to the seaward stair.

Then the smoke thinned too fast, torn by a wind off the water that Sefa hadn't made, and the fog ripped open like a curtain, and there was Corvane Dhal at the foot of the ramp.

Not running. Not shouting. Standing with his hands folded behind his back, in his good coat, in the middle of a crown operation, entirely at his ease, as though the quay were his counting floor and the soldiers his clerks. The escort's captain stood at his shoulder, and did not arrest him, and Orsen understood that too, all at once, the way she understood ledgers: Corvane hadn't sold them the schedule. He'd sold the crown *her*. The Door's keeper, delivered. The name freely given—except nothing about this was free.

"Orsen," Corvane said, in the voice he'd used when he bought her off the road at fourteen, warm as a hearth and priced accordingly. "Put it down, child. You'll hurt yourself."

Sefa's hand came up with fire already crawling along her knuckles. Isla stepped in front of them both, oilskin billowing, a fishwife with a soldier's stance.

And Orsen, with her hands on a door that remembered being a doorway, felt the dark crystal keyhole brighten against her wrist, felt it listening, felt it *waiting*—

—and heard herself say, before she could stop it, before she could weigh it, the first two words of a name she had never told anyone alive.
