## 16. Corvane Closes His Hand

The fog came off the water in layers, and we moved through it the way we'd learned to move through everything—Sefa first with her small flame cupped like a stolen egg, Isla behind her with a soldier's stride she couldn't quite disguise, and me last, with the salt door's cradle-straps cutting into my shoulders and my mother's face gone from my memory like a coin dropped down a well.

We had the Tidehouse to ourselves for exactly as long as it took to believe we did.

The Tidehouse was Corvane's name for it, though it had been a customs shed before the free cities were free, and a customs shed would be what the crown's clerks saw when they came for it in the morning. Wagons had been promised. I had read the requisition myself, weeks ago, before I began unmaking it: *one (1) artifact, salt composition, door-form, weight approx. nine hundredweight, to be conveyed to the capital under seal.* The crown wanted the Door of Brine the way a miser wants a key—not to use it, but to make sure no one else could.

So we would steal it first. Nine hundredweight of salt, three thieves, one foggy night, and a tide that would turn before dawn.

"Left side's rotten," Isla said, testing the quay boards. "Keep to the wall."

"You've looted buildings before," Sefa murmured.

"I've searched them. Same skill. Worse manners." Isla had been a sergeant eleven days ago. Now she walked with her sword wrapped in sacking and her warrant burned, and every time a gull cried out over the water she flinched like a woman hearing her own name called at her own trial. She hadn't told us what the village looked like when it burned. She hadn't needed to. It was written in the way she checked each shadow twice.

I set the straps down at the waterline, where the Door had lain half-buried in silt since the guild moved it out of the vault. It hummed. It always hummed now, low and constant, like a kettle two rooms away, like a throat about to speak. I had touched it enough in the past weeks to know its memory by heart: a shore under a sun that wasn't ours, white stone, green water, a country that maps had agreed to forget and that had quietly declined to stop existing.

"Talk to me," I said, because the Door answered better when I narrated. Sefa had laughed at me the first time. She didn't laugh anymore. "We're taking you out of here. There are people coming. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, and the crown is coming behind them with fire, and you're the only way across. I need you wide open tonight. I need you generous."

The hum deepened. Frost feathered up the salt in fern-patterns, and through my palms I felt it thinking—that vast, patient, tidal attention, older than the guild, older than the free cities, older maybe than the idea of doors.

"It likes you," Sefa said. She'd crouched at my shoulder, her flame shuttered now, her face all shadows and cheekbone. "It's been humming at you since the vault. It doesn't hum at me."

"It hums at everyone. It's a door. Humming is most of what it does."

"It hums *differently* at you." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't match her voice. Sefa had found her own name on Corvane's list, in Corvane's hand, priced and sold, and she had nearly burned the guildhall down around that knowledge. What was left of her rage lived in her now like a banked coal—useful, and always one breath from the open air. "Orsen. If this goes wrong tonight—"

"It won't."

"If it does. If he's waiting. I want you to know I don't regret the alliance. Whatever happens."

"That's a strange thing to say before a theft."

"It's a strange life." She stood, and her flame came back out, small and steady, throwing our shadows long across the rotten boards. "Isla. How long?"

Isla had the tide-table open against her knee, and beside it, folded, the list—the crown's list, the one she'd carried as a hunter and now carried as prey. She read it the way she read everything: like arithmetic.

"Low water at the fourth bell. Crown outriders at the Shallows by the sixth, if the roads hold. If we're not moving this thing by the fifth bell, we're not moving it at all." She looked up. "Which means we have one bell to lift nine hundredweight of salt onto a barge that isn't here yet."

"Rell's coming," I said. "Rell's always late, but he comes."

"Rell," Isla said, "is a smuggler."

"We're all smugglers tonight."

"No," Isla said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat honesty of a woman who had stopped lying to herself and found she couldn't stop doing it to others. "Tonight I'm a deserter, she's an arsonist, and you're a bookkeeper who's forgotten her own mother's face to keep strangers off a list. Rell is a smuggler. He'll come when it profits him, and he'll leave when it doesn't. Plan for the gap."

I wanted to argue. I had been arguing with Isla's arithmetic for two weeks and losing, because her arithmetic was always right. That was the worst thing about her. That was, I suspected, the thing that had made her a good hunter and was making her a better fugitive: she counted everything, including herself.

So we counted. The cradle-straps, the rollers we'd stolen from the fish-market, the length of greased plank that would take the Door from quay to barge. Sefa warmed the rollers so the salt wouldn't crack against the cold. Isla watched the water. I put my hands on the Door and told it, again, in the low voice I used for ledgers and for frightened people, that it was time.

We had it half-lifted—Sefa and me under the near edge, Isla taking the far side with a strength that surprised her as much as us—when the lanterns came on.

All of them. Every lantern along the quay, and the ones in the Tidehouse windows, and the two hung from the crane-arm overhead, so that the fog turned the color of old honey and the three of us stood lit up like figures on a stage.

"Oh," Sefa said softly. Her flame went from the size of an egg to the size of a fist. "Oh, he's *good*."

"No," said a voice from the Tidehouse door. "I'm thorough. It looks the same from underneath."

Corvane Dhal came down the quay steps the way he did everything: unhurried, well-dressed, with the air of a man arriving slightly early to an appointment the rest of us hadn't known we'd made. He wore his gray traveling coat and his guildmaster's chain, and behind him, spaced along the quay rail like chess pieces, stood eight of his people. Not soldiers. Guild. Faces I knew. Maren, who kept the wine-cellar and the wine-ledgers. The Tetch twins, who could hold a whisper in a bottle for a year. Osk, who owed me for three edits I'd made to keep his sister off a list.

"Osk," I said. "Don't."

Osk looked at the ground. Maren looked at me, and her face was sorry, and her feet didn't move.

"Put it down," Corvane said. "Gently. It's worth more than all of you."

We didn't put it down. That was the thing nobody had planned for, I think—not even him. We stood there, three thieves under nine hundredweight of humming salt, and held on.

"Orsen." He said my name the way he'd said it the first day, at fourteen, on the road, when he'd crouched down to my level and offered me his hand and a place by his fire. I had read that memory out of an old guild token a month ago, and it had broken something in me that I was still learning to walk around. *What did he ask in return? Her gift, quietly, forever.* "You're hurting yourself. The straps are wrong for that weight. Put it down and we'll discuss this like the family we are."

"We're not a family," Sefa said. "Families don't sell each other."

"Some families," Corvane said, "are the only reason their children survive long enough to be angry about it." He came closer, boots quiet on the wet boards, and stopped just outside the reach of Sefa's flame, which had grown again without her seeming to notice. "Sefa. Your name was on a list because your name needed to be on a list. There is a difference between writing a thing down and meaning it, and a guildmaster's whole art is knowing the difference. I never sold you. I bought you time. Ask Orsen—she's the one who reads the books. Ask her what the margins say."

"The margins say," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "that you met the crown's envoy at the Tidehouse on the night of the dark moon. That you offered him the Door in exchange for the charter. I heard you, Corvane. You said, *give me paper and I'll give you the way out of the country.*"

Something moved behind his face. Not guilt. Recalculation.

"You were in the loft," he said.

"I was in the loft."

"And you've been in my ledgers for eleven years." He sighed, and for one unguarded moment he looked tired—old, even, though he couldn't have been fifty—tired the way a man looks when the performance runs long and the audience won't leave. "Then you know I keep my accounts balanced. Every debt paid. Every favor priced. You've read my hand for a decade, girl. Have you ever once found me cruel?"

"I found you *exact*," I said. "It's worse."

Behind him, Isla shifted her grip on the Door, and I felt it through the salt—a tremor, a deepening of the hum, like a sleeper turning toward a voice. And Corvane heard it too. I watched him hear it. His head tilted, very slightly, the way a man tilts his head at a sound only he recognizes, and I understood with a lurch that he had heard the Door before. That he knew its voice. That whatever he'd learned at the Tidehouse from the crown's scholars and his own long, patient reading of my notes, he had learned it partly by watching me touch this thing and writing down what happened.

"You've been keeping notes on me," I said.

"You keep notes on everything," he said. "It's what I admire about you." He spread his hands. "So let's do accounts. Openly, for once, since you've decided we're enemies. The crown comes in two days. Aldric's vanguard is at the Shallows; I have the requisitions, the same as you. The free cities will burn their own harbors and it will slow him by a week, and then he'll be here, and there will be a pyre in every square for everyone whose name is written anywhere. Including, Orsen—*especially*—yours. You've been editing my books for a month. Don't look surprised; I audit too. Every edit you make, something goes out of you. Your mother's face. The taste of plums. The name of the street you grew up on. You've been paying for strangers with yourself, and I am the only man in the free cities who has ever thought that was a bad bargain."

"Then what," Sefa said, "is your bargain?"

"Mine," Corvane said, "is the only one on the table."

He walked to the Door. He put his hand on it—the first time I had ever seen him touch it—and the hum changed. Not louder. *Closer.* Like a dog hearing its name in another room.

"I bought this from a Vaelish wreck-diver nineteen years ago," he said. "Do you know what he told me, before he took his coin and drank himself dead in Gulltown? He told me the door doesn't open for strength. It doesn't open for blood. It doesn't open for crowns or charters or the cleverest smuggler in the free cities." He looked at me over the salt, and his eyes in the lantern-light were the same eyes that had crouched down to a fourteen-year-old on the road and offered her a fire. "It opens for a keeper. One who gives it a true name, freely given. Freely, Orsen. That's the whole mechanism. That's the entire lock. A name, given away on purpose, to a door, forever."

The quay was very quiet. The fog moved. Somewhere out on the black water, I thought I heard oars—Rell, late as ever, or the crown, early as death.

"And you want me to give it," I said.

"I want you to give it *to me*," Corvane said. "That's the part you haven't read yet. The name has to be given to a keeper—a person, a steward, someone the Door trusts to hold the threshold. And then the keeper opens it for whoever they choose. The wreck-diver gave his to a woman in Vael. She's dust now, and the door's been orphaned ever since—half-open, humming, homesick." He smiled, and it was the smile from the road, from the fire, from every kindness he had ever priced into my life. "You were always going to be my keeper, Orsen. I've been raising you for it since you were fourteen. Give the Door your name, and give the keeping to me, and I will hold it open until the last soul in the free cities is through. Every gifted child on your edited lists. Every family in the warrens. Everyone. You'll have your escape for all of them, and I'll have my guild, and the crown can have ash. Nobody has to burn. Isn't that what you've been bleeding yourself for?"

And the trap closed, and it was beautiful, the way his traps were always beautiful, because every part of it was true. The Door did want a name. I could feel it wanting, the way you feel a held breath. He *would* hold it open—he was exact, he kept his accounts, he had never once been cruel. He would hold it open for everyone I'd ever tried to save, and all it would cost was everything I was, poured through his hands forever, my name his, my gift his, my threshold his, the one door out of a dying country held shut or open at the pleasure of the man who had bought me off the road and called it rescue.

"That's not freedom," Isla said quietly. "That's a leash with a country on the end of it."

"It's an *arrangement*," Corvane said. "The world runs on arrangements."

"The world," said Sefa, and her flame stood up off her palm, tall and steady and suddenly much less careful, "runs on fire and tide, and both of those are free."

"Put it out, girl."

"Make me."

And that was when I felt the Door *choose*.

It came through my palms like cold water rising. All these weeks I had talked to it, narrated to it, begged it open a crack at low tide, and I had assumed I was the one asking. But the hum had never been a kettle. It had been a question, asked over and over into the fog: *who? who? who?*—and Corvane had heard it and built a trap out of the answer, and he had been wrong about the mechanism in one detail, one small, fatal, Corvane-exact detail.

The name had to be freely given. But the Door did the choosing of who it would take.

And it had been listening to me for a month.

"Orsen," Corvane said, and for the first time in eleven years I heard something in his voice that wasn't certainty. "What's it doing?"

The frost was climbing. Not fern-patterns now but letters, or the ancestors of letters, white script blooming up the salt in lines that hurt to look at, and the hum had become almost words, and out on the black water the oars had stopped, and every lantern on the quay guttered at once as if something enormous had drawn breath.

"Orsen," Corvane said again. "Whatever it's offering you—whatever it's *showing* you—the price is your name. Your whole name. You give that, you never get it back. Do you understand me? There's no editing that ledger. There's no—"

The first horn sounded from the Shallows.

It came thin and long across the water, and then a second horn answered it, and then a third, and Isla said, in the flat voice of a woman whose arithmetic has finally come due: "That's not Rell. That's cavalry. The vanguard's a day early."

Corvane's hand was still on the Door. Mine was too. Between our hands, the salt blazed white, and the Door of Brine began, very softly, to open.

* * *

The crack of it let out air that smelled of green water and white stone and a sun that wasn't ours, and every person on that quay—guild and deserter and arsonist and thief—turned toward it the way flowers turn, without deciding to.

"No," Corvane said, and grabbed my wrist. "Orsen. *Listen to me.* If you give it your name now, in front of all of us, it binds to *you*, and everything I've built—"

"Is ash," I said. "You said it yourself. The crown can have ash."

The second horn sounded closer. Down the Shallows road, torchlight was beginning to move like a lit fuse, and somewhere behind us in the fog Rell's barge was either arriving or it wasn't, and Sefa's flame had grown tall enough to throw light on the water, and Isla had drawn her wrapped sword one-handed without letting go of the Door.

Corvane's grip tightened on my wrist. His eyes were wet. That was the worst of it. That was the thing I would carry across whatever threshold waited: he was not lying, and he was not pretending, and he loved me in the only currency he had ever been able to count.

"Give it to me," he whispered. "Let me keep it. Let me be the one who stays."

And the Door, patient as tide, waited to hear what name I would say.
