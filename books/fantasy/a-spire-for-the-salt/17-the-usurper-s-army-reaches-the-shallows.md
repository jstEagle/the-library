## 17. The Usurper's Army Reaches the Shallows

Isla Merrin had spent eleven years learning to count people. It was the first thing they taught you in the crown's service, before the warrants and the pyres and the paperwork of mercy: *count them, Sergeant, because a number is the beginning of order.* She had counted registered households in Ashgrave. She had counted the names on lists that fed the fires. She had counted, once, with her hand steady and her stomach nowhere at all, the forty-one souls who came out of a burning village because she had unlocked the doors.

Now she stood on the seawall above the Freeharbor road and counted an army.

She gave up at four thousand and started counting sails instead, out on the grey water where the usurper's fleet came on like a slow landslide. Sails she could count. Sails were honest. The army was not — it kept unfolding as it reached the shallows, companies spilling from transports into the surf, banners of King Aldric's triple crown hanging wet and heavy in the windless morning. The sound of them carried across the water: not drums yet, just ten thousand men breathing, wading, arriving, like the sea had decided to grow an enemy overnight.

"You should not be here," said Dagny Hale beside her. The old ferryman's hands did not leave the wheel of his cart. "A deserter on a seawall with the king's own fleet in her eyes. That's a short story with a rope ending."

"They'd have to catch me first." It was meant to be light. It came out flat, because nothing in Isla was light this morning. "How many can you carry?"

"On the *Kindly Tide*? Sixty, packed like herring. Eighty if we don't mind sinking." He squinted at the road behind them, where the refugee column already stretched back past the salt-works, past the tanneries, past the horizon's curve of dust and fear. "That's not eighty people, Sergeant."

"Isla."

"That's not eighty people, *Isla*."

She knew. She had been doing the arithmetic since dawn, and it was the same arithmetic that had cracked her open in a hedge-witch's kitchen two months ago, except now the numbers had grown teeth. Four thousand coming ashore by nightfall. Twenty thousand more behind them. And here, on the wrong side of the water, every gifted soul in the free cities who had heard the rumor — the rumor that would not die no matter how carefully no one confirmed it — that there was a door.

"There's a story," Dagny said, quieter. "Going down the column. That if you reach the drowned quay at low tide, the sea opens."

"Do you believe it?"

"I believe," he said, "that twenty thousand people believing it is the only thing keeping them walking instead of kneeling in the road to wait for the triple crown." He flicked the reins. "Get in or get off my cart, Sergeant. I'm making for the quay either way."

Isla got in. She had stopped being a sergeant; she had not yet learned what she was instead. But she could count, and she could organize, and if the world was going to end like a tide coming in — inevitable, patient, indifferent — then someone ought to be standing at the waterline telling people which way to run.

* *

The free cities burned their harbors at noon.

It was not panic. That was what unsettled Isla most, watching from the heights above Saltreach's docks as the guild delegates — she recognized the fire-gifted girl, Sefa, small and bright as a struck match at the center of it — walked calmly along the wharves with torches kindled in their bare hands. The cities had voted. Smuggler's parliament, the story went, all the guildmasters in one room, and for once in their venal lives they had chosen drowning over selling.

The warehouses went first, tar-black smoke rolling fat and sweet across the water. Then the cranes, folding into the flames like courtiers bowing. Then the piers themselves, hissing where the tide licked at the burning edges, and the whole harbor of Saltreach became a wall of orange between the city and the sea.

"No port, no landing," said a voice at Isla's shoulder. A boy, fourteen maybe, with river-grey eyes that didn't quite track together. Gifted — she could feel it now, the way you felt weather changing, since she'd started paying attention to what she'd spent years refusing to notice. "That's the thinking. Make him anchor in open water. Buy three days, maybe four."

"Three days," Isla said. "And then?"

"And then he lands anyway, on whatever beach is flattest, and comes for us." The boy shrugged, terribly casual, the shrug of someone who had done his grieving early. "But there's a door. You've heard. Everyone's heard."

"Where did the rumor start?"

"Don't know. Some say a ledger-keeper. Some say the salt itself started humming and told the fishwives." He grinned, quick and crooked. "Doesn't matter, does it? A true door and a false rumor do the same work right now. They make people move."

They made people move. All afternoon the roads of the free cities flowed toward the drowned quay — carters abandoning carts too broken to roll further, grandmothers carrying grandchildren who carried, in turn, jars and birds and bundles of letters, a one-eyed priest of some drowned god singing hymns that made the horses shy. And threaded through them, everywhere, the ones the edict had hunted for a year: a woman whose shadow moved a half-second out of step with her body; twins who finished each other's sentences before either spoke; a man leading a goat that, when Isla looked at it twice, was not entirely a goat.

A year ago she would have written their descriptions in her book. Her hand actually ached for the book, phantom-weight at her hip where the ledger had hung through eleven years of service, and she understood with something like nausea that the ache was habit, nothing more. Habit and arithmetic. *Count them.* Well. She was still counting. She had simply changed what the numbers were for.

"Name?" she asked each family at the crossroads below the heights, where she'd set herself up with Dagny's cousin and two boys with strong backs, sorting the flood into columns. "Not your name. Your skill. What breaks when you're frightened, and who does it hurt?"

It was the only registration she would ever conduct again, and she conducted it ruthlessly: fire-gifted to the rear with the other fire-gifted, because frightened fire in a crowd was a massacre waiting for its excuse. Healers forward, where the trampling happened. The ones whose gifts she couldn't classify — the goat-man, the shadow-woman, a girl who wept pearls until she dehydrated — she sent to the middle, with instructions to hold hands and be patient, which was advice for surviving anything.

By dusk the columns stretched six miles back toward the burning harbors, and the smoke had turned the sunset the color of an old wound, and somewhere out on the darkening water Aldric's vanguard was making camp in the shallows, their cook-fires reflected on the waves like a second, colder burning.

Three days. Maybe four.

Time ran out like a tide. You never saw it turn; you only saw, all at once, that it had.

* *

On the second night the wind changed, and brought them the smell of the pyres.

Not Saltreach — the pyres there were ships' timber and clean tar. This was older, meatier, the smell Isla knew from Ashgrave and the villages whose names she had carried like stones for a year. Aldric did not wait to conquer before he punished. He burned the coast road behind him, village by village, so the survivors would run ahead of his army and spread the terror faster than his cavalry could ride it.

They began arriving at midnight. Not walking — running, staggering, crawling, families fused together by fear into single many-legged creatures, and behind each group the sky glowed a little redder, the way it always did, the way it always would, the arithmetic of mercy failing in every line and column of the world.

"We can't outrun them," said the pearl-girl's mother, hands slicked with tears that caught the firelight like coins. She said it to Isla, because Isla had a clipboard, and clipboards meant authority, and authority meant someone would fix it. "You have to understand. We can't *outrun* them."

"You don't need to outrun them," Isla said. "You need to arrive."

"Arrive where?"

"The quay. Tomorrow. Low tide's at midmorning." She did not say *the door will be open*, because she did not know it, because no one knew it, because the whole plan of twenty thousand lives rested on a slab of salt and a rumor and a ledger-keeper whom Isla had met exactly once, across a locked gate in the warrens, in a breathless moment that felt now like a letter from a former life. "If the rumor's false," she added, because lying to the terrified was a cruelty dressed as kindness, "you'll want to be at the quay anyway. Boats are kinder than beaches, if it comes to boats."

The woman looked at her for a long moment. Firelight. Pearls. Behind her, the glow of everything she had ever owned becoming smoke.

"You're one of them," the woman said. "Hunters."

"I was."

"What are you now?"

Isla looked down the road, at the columns flowing in the dark like something alive, at the fires eating the coastline mile by mile, at the whole country — the impossible, officially-nonexistent escape route of a smugglers' guild — gathering itself around one word: *door.*

"Early," she said. "I'm early. Get your daughter some water. We march at first light."

* *

The third day, the sea fought for the usurper.

No one had thought about the tide except the fishermen, and the fishermen tried to warn the parliament and the parliament had already burned itself down to its last arguments. But the drowned quay sat low, and the spring tide of that week was a famously vicious one, and by noon the escape route — the rumor, the hope, the whole geography of salvation — stood ankle-deep in cold grey water, and the refugees crowded onto the heights above it and looked down at their deliverance underwater.

"It'll go out," Dagny said. He'd grounded the *Kindly Tide* on the mud and was repitching her seams while sixty people watched him with the eyes of the already-drowned. "Tide always goes out. Question's whether it goes out before the cavalry comes round the headland."

"When does it come?" Isla asked.

He looked at the sun, then at the water, then at her, and did arithmetic of his own, the ferryman's version: loads and crossings and minutes. "Too close," he said. "Whatever happens on that mudflat tomorrow, it'll happen fast."

So they spent the third day building order out of desperation, and this, Isla found, was the thing she had been training for her entire life without knowing it. Not hunting. This. She drew lines in the mud of the cliff-top with a burnt stick and assigned the whole ragged nation of the desperate their places: families with children nearest the descent path, the strong flanking the weak, fire-gifted stationed at the four corners with orders she made Sefa herself repeat back — *not unless I give the word, not unless it's the last word there is.* The boy with the river-grey eyes turned out to be able to whisper across distances, and she posted him on the highest rock as her herald, her voice multiplied, her orders flying down the miles of column faster than any rider.

Twenty thousand people, learning to be ready. They sang, that last evening, all of them at once and none of them together — different gods, different cities, different tongues — and the sound went up off the cliffs and out over the water toward the usurper's campfires, and Isla chose to believe Aldric's soldiers heard it and were afraid.

She wrote no names. She kept no list. But she counted them, one last time, all twenty thousand and more, and she counted them the only way that had ever been true: *each one. Each one. Each one.*

* *

The fourth morning came up blood-colored, and the tide went out.

It went out slowly, the way it always did, baring the drowned quay stone by stone — old pilings, a customs house roofed in barnacles, and there, at the quay's landward end, unmistakable even at a distance, a shape that made the whole cliff-top gasp at once. A door. Standing alone, upright in the mud, tall as two men and white as bone, salt-scoured and humming, or perhaps that was the crowd, perhaps that was twenty thousand hearts finding one rhythm at last.

Isla stood at the head of the descent path with her stick in her hand like a marshal's baton, and looked out at the door, and looked back at the columns, and looked east, where the headland ran down to the sea.

The headland was filling with horsemen.

They came over the ridge in column, pennons snapping, maybe six hundred of them, the vanguard's best, sent ahead to harvest the rumor before it could ripen. Behind them, far off across the flats, the main host was already moving, a brown tide-line of infantry, hours behind but coming, always coming, the way a tide comes.

"Now," Isla said, and the whisper-boy took it up, and the word rolled down six miles of cliff in under a minute: *now, now, now.*

The columns broke into motion — orderly, she would think later, miraculously orderly, twenty thousand people walking downhill toward a door in a mudflat while cavalry wheeled onto the flats behind it. Dagny launched the *Kindly Tide* on the first of the flood. Somewhere near the front of everything, small figures were running ahead of the crowd toward the door: a fire-bright girl, a grey-eyed boy, and — yes — the ledger-keeper, Orsen Villalobos, arms raised, reaching the salt slab first, laying her hands against it as the first riders came down onto the hard sand at a canter.

The door did not open.

Orsen pressed, and shouted something the wind ate, and pressed again, and the door stayed shut, white and silent, and the cavalry lowered their lances, and behind Isla the whole human tide of the free cities slowed, and stalled, and began — horribly, inevitably — to compress against itself, twenty thousand people learning in one long terrible moment that rumors can drown you as surely as armies.

Then Orsen Villalobos turned to face the crowd, lifted her voice against the wind and the hooves, and began, clearly, deliberately, to speak her own name.
