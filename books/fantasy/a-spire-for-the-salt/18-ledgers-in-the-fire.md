## 18. Ledgers in the Fire

The ledgers weighed more than a body.

Orsen had carried them her whole life, it seemed—up stairs, down stairs, across courtyards in the rain, into vaults and out of them, always careful, always precise, the way you carry something that could kill you if you dropped it wrong. Forty-one years of the Saltspire Guild's memory, bound in calfskin and salt-stained board. Every impossible thing the guild had ever moved. Every name.

Tonight she carried them down to the Fishmarket Square, and she did not carry them carefully.

The fog had come in thick off the harbor, the kind of fog the free cities wore like a cloak in the last days before Aldric's vanguard arrived, and under the fog the square was full. Not the usual market crowd—there was no market anymore, the fish long since fled the burning harbors—but the desperate, the displaced, the ones who had heard the rumor of a door and come to stand near it even if they couldn't touch it. They pressed against the barriers the crown's auditors had thrown up around the Tidehouse, and they watched Orsen Villalobos walk into the square with a handcart stacked with books.

Sefa walked on her left. Isla Merrin on her right, in a stolen sergeant's coat two sizes too big, her hand nowhere near the sword at her hip because a sword was the last thing this needed to be.

"You understand what you're about to do," Sefa said. Not a question. Sefa didn't ask questions she already knew the answers to; it was a habit of fire-gifted people, Orsen had decided, to treat the world as something already decided.

"I'm going to commit treason," Orsen said. "Again. It's becoming a habit."

"You're going to burn the only map to every gifted soul in the free cities." Sefa's jaw was set, but her eyes kept flicking to the crowd, to the barriers, to the dark windows of the Tidehouse where the crown's auditors had been working for six days with their little knives and their little stamps, cataloguing the Saltspire Guild the way you catalogue a dead whale. "And every name you ever hid in there. Every edit you made. Everything you spent yourself to bury."

"I know what's in them."

"Your mother's face is in them."

Orsen stopped the handcart.

She had not told Sefa that. She had not told anyone that. But Sefa had watched her come back from the master ledger two nights ago, had watched her stand in the doorway of the counting room with her hands empty and her eyes full of a hole, and Sefa was fire, and fire understood what it consumed.

"My mother's face is in them," Orsen agreed. "And so is a widow in Gulltown who bought a jarred thunderclap to bury with her drowned son. And so is a fisherman who traded three days of his youth for a season of luck. And so are four hundred people whose names exist nowhere else on this earth, because I put them there, and because I kept them there, and because for eleven years I told myself that keeping a name safe in a ledger was the same as keeping it safe."

She started walking again. The wheels of the handcart squeaked. Somewhere in the fog a bell was ringing—not an alarm, not yet, just the harbor bell tolling the hour, and it sounded like a countdown.

"And now?" Sefa asked.

"Now I've learned the difference."

* * *

The pyre was already built. That had been Isla's contribution, and Isla had built it the way she did everything: precisely, legally, and in plain sight. A customs platform at the edge of the square, stacked with driftwood and broken crates from the burned harbors, dry as a sermon. The crown's auditors had assumed it was for their own records—copies, duplicates, the paperwork of conquest—and had signed off on its construction with the cheerful efficiency of men who believed that paper, properly stamped, was a kind of god.

Isla had signed the requisition herself. Sergeant Merrin, retired as of three days ago, hunted as of three days ago, still carried a stamp that opened doors.

"Platform's soaked at the base," she murmured as they reached it. "I made sure. It'll catch slow. Slow is what we want—time for the crowd to see, time for the auditors to come out and be seen failing to stop it." She looked at Orsen, and in the fog her face was the face Orsen had first seen across a locked gate in the warrens, and it was not the face of an enemy anymore, and it was not quite the face of a friend, and Orsen thought that was probably the truest kind of face there was. "Once it starts, there's no edit. No rewriting. I need you to understand that, ledger-keeper. This is the one thing in your life you can't take back."

Orsen looked at the books.

Forty-one volumes. She knew them the way you know your own hands. She knew the weight of each one, the smell, the way the third volume's spine cracked at the Gulltown entries. She knew where every hidden name lay—under freight codes, under weather notations, under the little marginal marks that meant nothing to an auditor and everything to a keeper. She had spent eleven years building a language of concealment so perfect that even she sometimes forgot she was the only one alive who could read it.

That was the trap. That had always been the trap. Corvane had understood it before she did: a secret kept by one person isn't a secret, it's a leash. And the crown had understood it too, which was why their auditors were in the Tidehouse right now with her ledgers' sisters, working through the guild's public records, cross-referencing, building the list that would feed the pyres in the capital. Her ledgers were the map. Her edits were the corrections to the map. And she was the only key.

Burn the key, and the map became noise. Burn the map, and every hidden name became free—not safe, nothing was safe anymore, but free. Unfindable by paper. Unownable by ink.

"Orsen." Sefa's voice, low. "The auditors. East side. They've seen us."

Of course they had. Six auditors in crown grey, pushing through the crowd with the offended urgency of bureaucrats confronting an unlicensed emotion. Behind them, at the Tidehouse doors, a taller figure had emerged into the fog, and Orsen's heart did the thing it always did, the stupid loyal thing, before her mind caught up and reminded it.

Corvane Dhal did not run. Corvane never ran. He walked through the crowd the way water finds its level, and the crowd parted for him because eleven years of habit dies hard, and he stopped at the base of the platform and looked up at Orsen with the ledger-cart between them like a border.

"Orsen." His voice carried the way it always carried, warm and low, a voice built for counting rooms and confidences. "Whatever you think this accomplishes—"

"I know what it accomplishes." She lifted the first volume. Volume one. The year she'd arrived, fourteen years old, sold off the road to a man with kind eyes and a long arithmetic. The year of the jarred thunderclap. "It accomplishes an ending."

"The guild kept those people alive—"

"The guild sold them." Her voice cracked out over the square, louder than she'd meant, and the crowd went still, hundreds of faces turning up toward her. She hadn't planned to say it here. But here it was, and here she was, and there was a strange freedom in the fact that she could no longer remember her mother's face and therefore had nothing left to lose that she could name. "I've read the ledgers. All of them. I wrote half of them. And I'm telling you what's in them: when the Edict came, Corvane Dhal didn't hide the gifted. He invoiced them. Names, locations, gifts, prices—sold to the crown's envoy at the Tidehouse, in this square, while he smiled at us across the counting table. I heard him do it."

The fog seemed to lean in. Somewhere in the crowd, a woman made a sound like something breaking.

Corvane's face did not change. That was the terrible thing about him; she'd loved that about him once, the stillness, the certainty, the way the world broke against him like water against a spire. Now she watched that stillness work on the crowd and understood it had always been a weapon.

"Orsen," he said gently. "You're tired. You've spent so much of yourself in those books. Come down, and we'll—"

"Volume one," Orsen said, and struck the customs flint against the platform's iron rail.

* * *

The first book caught slow, exactly as Isla had promised. The calfskin cover blackened, curled, and then the pages inside went up with a soft rushing sound, like a tide coming in, and Orsen held it up so the crowd could see—so they could see the names going, the freight codes and the weather notations and the little marginal marks, four hundred years of the Saltspire Guild's careful, criminal memory dissolving into orange light.

She threw it onto the pyre and took up volume two.

The auditors reached the platform. Isla met them—not with her sword, with her stamp, holding it up like a relic, barking customs law at them in a voice that had commanded sweeps through the warrens, and the auditors hesitated, because bureaucracy is a faith and Isla still wore its vestments, and in that hesitation Orsen threw volume three, and volume four.

The crowd had begun to move. Not away—toward. She could feel it, the shift in the square's weight, people pressing forward not to stop the burning but to witness it, and among them faces she knew from the ledgers, faces she had hidden in margins and under weather notations, and they were watching their own names go up in smoke with expressions she would carry for the rest of her half-erased life. Grief. Terror. And underneath both, dawning, incredulous: relief.

The widow from Gulltown was there. Orsen didn't know how she knew—the woman's face was ordinary, forgettable, the kind of face Orsen's gift would have read in a heartbeat once, the kind of face she could no longer read at all—but she knew, the way you know rain coming. The woman had bought a jarred thunderclap to bury with her drowned son. She was standing at the front of the crowd with her hands open at her sides, and she was smiling, and Orsen thought: that's what it looks like. That's what it looks like when a name stops being evidence and becomes a person again.

Volume nine. Volume twelve. The spine of the third cracked at the Gulltown entries one last time, and then it didn't crack at all.

"Orsen." Corvane was still at the base of the platform. He hadn't tried to climb it. He hadn't called the guards—there were no guards, the vanguard hadn't come, and Orsen understood suddenly that he had come alone, that he had watched her build this night the way she had once watched him build ledgers, and he had chosen to witness rather than prevent, and she didn't know what that meant yet. "You're burning yourself. You know that. Everything you spent—your mother's face, your gift's memory, all those pieces of yourself—you're burning them too. They were in those books. You were in those books."

"I know." She held up volume twenty-three, the one with Sefa's name in it, bought and paid for by a masked client whose handwriting she had once known better than her own. "That's the point, Corvane. That's the whole point. A person shouldn't be findable in a book. Not by a king. Not by a guildmaster." She looked down at him, and the firelight moved across his face, and for one moment—one terrible, human moment—she saw not the man who had sold his people but the man who had bought a half-starved girl off the road and taught her that numbers could be a kind of shelter. "Not even by someone who loved her."

"Did I?" he asked. Quietly. Just for her, under the roar of the pyre. "Love you?"

"You did what you called love," Orsen said. "I'm burning the difference."

She threw volume twenty-three, and it turned end over end through the smoke, and landed open, and the flames took Sefa's name first.

* * *

By the time the last volume went in—the master ledger, the great black book, the one whose rewriting had cost her her mother's face—the sun was coming up grey behind the fog, and the square was full of ash falling like the world's gentlest snow.

The auditors had withdrawn to the Tidehouse. Isla stood at the platform's edge with her stamp finally, visibly dead in her hand, and she dropped it into the coals herself, and Orsen watched a woman spend her whole former life in one small gesture. Sefa had her arm through the Gulltown widow's arm, and was saying something to her that made the woman laugh through her tears, and the sound of it went through the square like a spark through dry grass—laughter, in a city under edict, in a country already half-conquered.

Corvane was gone.

Orsen found his tracks in the ash later, leading away from the platform toward the harbor, and she stood at the edge of the burned square with her hands black to the wrist and her pockets full of nothing, and she let herself feel it: not triumph. Not yet. Something smaller and stranger, like a door opening a crack onto a shore under a sun that isn't yours.

Four hundred names, free. Herself among them—her name had been in the master ledger, in Corvane's own hand, *Orsen Villalobos, keeper, gift of reading, price paid*, and she had watched it burn with the rest, and the ash of it was on her skin now, and she was no one's entry anymore.

The harbor bell began to ring again. Not the hour this time. The alarm.

Down at the drowned quay, through the thinning fog, she could see them coming—riders, many riders, the vanguard's outriders in the king's grey and white, moving fast along the coast road toward the rumor of a door. And behind her, at the Tidehouse, the crown's auditors were spilling out into the square with the news of the burning, and the two tides of Aldric's machine were converging on the one place in the free cities where four hundred freed souls had left to run.

The Door of Brine.

Orsen turned from the ash, and started toward the water, and the crowd—her crowd, the crowd of the unfindable—turned with her.
