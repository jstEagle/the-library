## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Vellum Guild kept its ledgers the way other institutions kept prisoners: under lock, under guard, and under no illusions about escape.

Isandro Galloway had counted the vault's holdings eleven times tonight, which was ten more times than rigor required and exactly as many as her nerves demanded. The audit room sat beneath the guildhall like a stone heart, lit by three lamps whose oil smelled faintly of rosemary—a precaution against paper moths, or against thinking too clearly, she'd never learned which. Shelves climbed the walls in ranks. Each ledger wore a spine-label in Corvane's immaculate hand: *Storms, Bottled. Years, Borrowed. Names, Unearned. Debts, Inherited. Forgiveness, Discounted.*

She ran her thumb down the margin of the storm-ledger and felt the hum.

That was the thing people never believed until they held one of these books. Impossible things did not sit quietly on a page. A bottled storm, catalogued properly—*Lot 447, acquired off the Teeth, vessel: blue glass, stopper: wax sealed thrice, provenance sworn by two witnesses and a drowned man*—still rolled faintly under the vellum, like weather pacing a cage. When she leaned close she could smell ozone. Somewhere behind that neat column of figures, a thunderhead the size of a cathedral pressed its shoulder against blue glass and waited for someone stupid enough to pull the stopper.

"Fourth audit," said Corvane from the doorway. "You've done it four times."

"I'm verifying consistency."

"You're hiding." He came in carrying two cups and the particular walk he used when he'd already decided what she was hiding. Corvane had been appraising impossible things for forty years, and it had given him a face like a folded map—every line deliberate, every crease earned. He set a cup at her elbow. Tea, not wine; he knew her habits better than her own handwriting. "The counts match. They matched the third time. They will match the fifth."

"The fifth will be for pleasure."

"Mm." He lowered himself into the opposite chair with the sound old chairs and older men make in agreement. For a while they worked in the lamplight together, pens scratching, the storm humming its low note, and Isandro let herself pretend this was all the world was: ink, order, a mentor who insulted her tea-drinking, and shelves of wonders that stayed where they were put.

It lasted four entries.

The next lot was years. Borrowed ones. She turned the page and there they were in their gray columns—*six months, drawn against an unnamed future; two years, collateral: a first kiss; nine days, lender unknown, interest accruing in dreams*—and the familiar ache opened under her ribs the way a door opens onto a cold room.

Five years ago tonight, her god had last spoken to her.

Not in words, exactly. Her oath-mark sat at the base of her throat, a small sigil burned into the skin the day she swore herself to the Quiet Hand—the god of scribes, of promises kept, of debts honestly tallied. Mercenaries swore to plenty of louder gods: war gods, coin gods, the god of dying well. Isandro had chosen the one that stood in the back of the temple and checked the arithmetic. When you took a contract in the Quiet Hand's name, your word held. That was the whole theology, and it had suited her down to the bone.

And the god had talked back, in its way. A warmth in the mark when a promise was honored. A pressure, a disquiet, when she was about to sign something crooked. Once, memorably, an actual sentence, arriving whole in her skull like a letter slid under a door: *Read the second line again.* The second line had contained a clause that would have made her an accessory to a massacre. She'd walked away from that contract and slept for eleven hours.

Then five years ago tonight, mid-prayer, mid-breath, the warmth stopped. No explanation. No fading. Just silence, clean-edged, as if a page had been turned somewhere and the next page was blank.

She had prayed every night since. She kept it up the way she kept the ledgers—out of discipline, out of love, out of the stubborn belief that a thing done well mattered even when no one was counting. But she'd stopped telling anyone, including herself, that she expected an answer.

"You're doing the throat thing," Corvane said, not looking up.

"I don't have a throat thing."

"You touch your mark when you're thinking about the Hand. You've been touching it all evening." He set down his pen with the care of a man setting down something he might later regret picking up. "Five years tonight, isn't it."

The lamps ticked. The storm hummed.

"I don't keep track," Isandro said, which was a lie so flat even the ledger would have refused to record it.

"No. Of course not." Corvane drank his tea. His eyes, above the rim, were doing something complicated. "When I was your age I lost a god too. Not the Hand—mine was the Drowned Lady, before her temple sold the waterfront and moved inland to sell something else. It's a peculiar grief. No body to bury. No one sends flowers. The other priests tell you the silence is itself a teaching, and you want to strike them, because you know—if it's a teaching, then the teacher is still there, choosing not to speak, which is worse."

"How did you get over it?"

"I didn't. I got a desk." He smiled, thin and real. "Grief becomes inventory eventually, girl. You shelve it. You audit it once a year, on the anniversary, whether you mean to or not. And then one night you realize you've spent an entire quiet evening counting storms with someone whose company you'd choose over speech from heaven anyway."

Isandro looked at him across the lamplight and felt the ache rearrange itself into something bearable. "That's the worst comfort I've ever been given."

"It's the only kind I stock." He tapped the open year-ledger. "Walk me through the borrowings. There's an entry bothering me."

Grateful for the work, she bent over the column. "Three anomalies. First—" she turned pages "—a withdrawal of one year, dated six nights ago, borrower unnamed, purpose unwritten. We don't allow unnamed borrowers. Second, the collateral listed is 'a kingdom's patience,' which is not a currency we accept, and yet the entry cleared. Third, the appraiser's seal on the page isn't ours."

Corvane went very still. Stillness, in Corvane, was loud.

"Show me the seal."

She rotated the ledger toward him. The stamp in the corner was a circle enclosing a design she couldn't quite fix in her eye—a sigil that seemed to rearrange itself slightly each time she blinked, the way a word stops looking like a word if you stare too long. Her oath-mark prickled, cold, for half a heartbeat, and she frowned and pressed her thumb to it without thinking.

"That's not a guild mark," she said.

"No," Corvane agreed quietly. "It isn't."

"Whose is it?"

"I don't know." He drew the ledger toward him and studied the anomalous entry the way he studied everything—like a man appraising an object for sale, asking not *what is this* but *what will this cost*. "An entry that clears without our sanction means either someone forged our authority, or something outranks it. Both possibilities annoy me. The second annoys me more, because I can name everyone with authority over this guild, and none of them are gods."

"You think a god borrowed a year?"

"I think," Corvane said, "that lately the ledgers have been humming more than usual. Storms restless. Years restless. Even the unearned names have been murmuring in their drawer—you've heard them, don't pretend you haven't, the whole third floor hears them after dark. Things in storage know things, Isandro. They always have. It's why we catalogue them instead of trusting them." He closed the ledger with both hands, gently, as one closes a door on a sickroom. "We'll flag the entry. Quietly. You'll take it to the conclave Thursday."

"Thursday's three days off."

"And it will still be strange on Thursday. Strange keeps." He rose, joints announcing themselves, and gathered his cups—and paused, halfway to the door, in that way he had of delivering his real remark disguised as an afterthought. "One more thing. The star-charts came back from the observatory today. The astronomer wants a word with whoever handles our celestial futures. Apparently several of the fixed stars have stopped appearing in the projections."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Corvane said, "that either the sky has developed an accounting error, or we have. And I have personally audited our books for forty years." At the door he stopped again, and this time he didn't disguise anything. "Go home, Isandro. Sleep. Say your prayers to whatever's listening or isn't. The ledgers will still be here tomorrow, and so will I."

* * *

She did not go straight home, because home was a room above a chandler's shop with one window and too much quiet, and quiet was the one commodity the Guild dealt in that she could never afford.

Instead she took the long way, up through the guildhall's upper floors, past the reading rooms where apprentices dozed over contract law, out onto the loggia that looked east over Cael Saren's lower city. Night lay warm on the rooftops. Lamps strung along the harbor made a spilled necklace of gold. Beyond the breakwater, the border wards marked the edge of things—not visible, exactly, but present, the way a fence is present to a dog: a low steady hum at the bottom of hearing, the sound of the world agreeing to hold its shape.

Isandro leaned on the balustrade and spoke to her god, silently, the way she had every night for five years.

*Audit report,* she thought, because she had long ago given up on eloquence and settled for bookkeeping. *Day one thousand eight hundred twenty-six of your silence. Holdings: unchanged. Debts: paid. Faith:* —and here, as always, she paused over the column—*faith: carried forward.*

She waited the customary span. Nothing answered, and nothing didn't-answer in any new way, and she exhaled the disappointment out with the sea air, trained by now to let it go like breath.

Below her the city went about its business. A drunk sang three streets over, badly. A watchman's lantern swung along the quay. In the guildhall behind her, Lot 447 hummed inside its ledger, and the unearned names murmured in their drawer, and somewhere in the stacks a borrowed year ticked closer to its due date, and none of it—none of it—gave her any warning at all.

Later, she would replay this hour obsessively, hunting for the omen she must have missed. There wasn't one. That was the part that frightened her most, afterward: that the world could sit so calmly in its chair, lamp lit, ledger open, right up to the moment the rules broke.

She was turning to go when the hair on her arms lifted.

Not wind. Not cold. Something else—a pressure change, a wrongness in her inner ear, as if the whole sky had leaned in close to listen. The harbor lights guttered, all of them, in the same instant, hundreds of flames bending toward the water though there was no wind. The drunk three streets over stopped singing mid-word.

And her oath-mark went cold. Not prickling, not aching—*cold*, a ring of winter at the base of her throat, the Quiet Hand's oldest signal, the one that meant: *stop. Do not sign. Do not swear. Do not promise anything.*

Five years of silence, and the first word the god sent her was a warning with no content at all.

Isandro stood very still on the loggia with her hand pressed to her throat, watching the harbor, watching the wards' invisible fence-line shimmer faintly into visibility like heat-haze in reverse—and above the breakwater, above the whole eastern curve of the world, she saw the first star go out.

No flicker. No dimming. It simply ceased, the way a candle ceases when snuffed between wet fingers, and the darkness where it had been seemed deeper than ordinary darkness, as if the sky were being subtracted rather than shadowed.

The second star went. Then a constellation, whole. Then the band of the River of Night began to gutter out star by star, fast now, a tide of black rolling westward across the heavens toward the city, toward the guildhall, toward her—

Isandro Galloway, keeper of ledgers full of impossible things, auditor of storms and years, stood on the loggia and did the only thing her whole life had trained her to do.

She reached for pen and paper.

Her hand shook so badly the first stroke tore the page, but she wrote it anyway, fast, in the shorthand of a woman who knows that testimony matters: *Eleventh hour. Stars failing east to west. Wards audible. Mark cold. God speaking—or something wearing His voice—*

The last star died.

The dark that came down over Cael Saren was total, and it was listening.
