## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with impossible things, Isandro had learned in her nine years at the Vellum Guild, was that they were almost never impossible when you actually held them in your hands. They were just heavy, or damp, or faintly humming with an intent you didn't care to name.

The bottled storm on the counter before her was all three.

She turned it slowly under the lamplight, watching the cloud inside churn against the glass like a wasp in a jar. The seal of wax had been stamped twice—once by the storm-catchers off the Gray Reach, once by the Guild's own assayers—and the second stamp was smudged, which meant someone had been careless, and careless people handling weather was how entire harbors ended up somewhere else.

"Entry forty-one hundred and six," she said, without looking up. "One bottled tempest, provenance Gray Reach, condition—" she tilted it again, listening to the low grind of wind against glass, "—agitated."

Corvane didn't glance over from his own desk across the audit room. He never did until he was ready, a habit he'd cultivated the way other men cultivated gardens. "Agitated is not a word for ledgers."

"It's the honest one."

"The ledger doesn't want honesty. It wants *condition: stable*, and your initials beneath it, and then we can both go home." He dipped his pen. He was a thin man gone thinner with age, all elbows and ink-stains, with a voice like paper being folded. "You've been staring at that bottle for a quarter hour. Either appraise it or adopt it."

Isandro set it down. "It's worth eleven marks if it's calm and a funeral if it's not. I'm deciding which column to be brave in."

"*Stable*," Corvane said, "with a marginal note. That's what marginal notes are *for*. So that when the harbor master comes screaming, we can point at the note and say, we knew, we told you, we are blameless as snow." A dry pause. "Snow that charges commission."

She smiled despite herself. This was the shape of their nights: the two of them alone in the audit room above the counting floor, the lamps turned low, the great ledgers open like sleeping animals. Below them the Guild hall had gone quiet, the last clients gone home with their purchases—a vial of borrowed years, a deed to a name not yet earned, someone's grandmother's luck decanted into a thimble. The Vellum Guild traded in what couldn't be traded, kept accounts of what shouldn't exist, and charged fairly for both. It was, Isandro had always thought, the most honest dishonest institution in Cael Saren.

And above it all, invisible, worn into the skin of every guildsworn member like a brand no one could see: the oath-mark. Hers sat below her left collarbone, a small cold weight shaped like an open book. It hadn't spoken in five years.

That was the thing she carried under the wit and the neat handwriting, the hollow she'd learned to walk around the way you learn to avoid a loose stair. Every guild oath was sworn to the Ledger-God—the Quiet Accountant, the folk called it; Brannoc's old order had called it something longer and sadder—and every sworn mark answered its god. You felt it when you broke faith. You felt it, faintly, warm as a hand on the back of your neck, when you kept it. Five years ago, on a Tuesday of no particular importance, the warmth had stopped.

No decree. No sign. Just silence, like writing a letter every day to someone who had died and nobody telling you.

Corvane said her mark still glowed when he looked at it sidelong with whatever old craft he pretended not to have. She'd stopped asking him about it. Asking made the silence worse somehow, made it into a thing with edges.

"Forty-one oh-six," she said at last. "Condition stable. Marginal note: keep away from harbors, children, and optimists." She signed. The pen scratched. Outside the tall windows, the sky over the city of Vel Sarran was clear and crowded with stars, the autumn constellations wheeling exactly where they were supposed to be, indifferent and punctual.

She remembered, later, that she had looked at them. Remembered thinking: *still there. Good.*

Then the wards went.

* * *

There was no sound, at first. There was light—or rather there was the memory of light, a vast pale lattice that hung over the borders of the kingdom the way frost hangs on a window, visible only to those who dealt in such things. Every ward-mason, every hedge-priest, every guildsman who handled warded goods felt it the moment it happened, the way a man feels a tooth crack: a wrongness arriving all at once, complete, undeniable.

Isandro felt the ledger in front of her go strange.

Not the words—they stayed inked and proper—but the *weight* of them, the sense she'd always had, unspoken even to herself, that entries in a guild book were held in place by something older than law. Promises stuck to pages because promises stuck to everything. And just for a heartbeat, everything unstuck. The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The dust in the air stopped falling. Her own breath hung in her chest like an item awaiting appraisal.

Then the world snapped back, and every star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. Gone—one breath they were there, a thousand small fires, and the next breath the sky was a smooth black lid pressed down over the world, so absolute that the darkness seemed to have texture, to press gently against the glass like deep water.

Isandro was on her feet without deciding to stand. Her chair rocked behind her. Across the room Corvane had risen too, and his face in the lamplight was the color of the paper he worked with.

"That's not cloud cover," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Clouds have edges."

"No," Corvane said. "They do."

Below them, the counting floor erupted.

They took the stairs together, and Isandro would carry the sound of that descent with her for the rest of her life: the roar of a building full of traders realizing at the same instant that the rules had changed. Clients who had been lodging overnight in the client wing came stumbling out in nightclothes clutching their purchases. A storm-broker stood in the middle of the hall screaming at the ceiling skylight as though the sky owed him an explanation. Someone had knocked over the shelf of borrowed years, and little glass phials rolled everywhere, each one glowing faintly with the time inside it, and no one stopped to pick them up because everyone was looking up through the skylight at nothing.

"Everyone stay calm!" The hall steward, a broad woman named Dessa whose voice could usually part crowds like a prow parts water, was failing utterly. "It's weather! It's—it's an eclipse!"

"Eclipses end," someone shouted back, and the crowd made a sound Isandro had never heard from a trading floor before—not anger, not greed, but fear, raw and animal, the sound of money discovering it might not matter.

Her mark chose that moment to wake up.

Five years of silence, and now—cold. Cold like a coin left in winter stone, spreading out from beneath her collarbone and down her arm until her fingers ached with it. She gasped and caught the edge of a desk. Around the hall she saw others flinch too, guildsworn hands going to chests and throats and wrists, wherever their own marks lay. Old Master Ellery dropped his lantern. Somewhere a woman began to recite the First Entry—the opening line of the Ledger Canon, *"All debts are witnessed, none are forgotten"*—over and over in a cracked whisper, like a prayer, which was exactly what it was.

"It's never done that," Isandro said. She wasn't sure to whom. Corvane had reached her side, and his hand closed on her shoulder, and his grip was shaking.

"I know," he said.

"Five years of nothing, and now it—"

"I know." His eyes weren't on her. They were on the skylight, on the black, and his face had gone past fear into something she had no name for, some expression she'd only ever seen on clients who came to sell things that should not be sold. "Isandro. Listen to me. Go back upstairs. Check the night's entries."

"Are you mad? Look at the sky—"

"*Check the entries.*" And there was something under the command, something urgent and almost gentle, and later—much later—she would understand it had been pity. But that night she only frowned, and obeyed, because he was Corvane, because he had taught her the trade, because following instructions was easier than standing in a crowd of screaming strangers under a sky with no stars in it.

* * *

The audit room looked the same as she'd left it, which was somehow the worst thing about it. Lamps low. Ledgers open. The bottled storm sitting on the counter where she'd abandoned it, its inner cloud now pressed flat against the glass on every side, straining outward toward the window and the starless dark beyond, as if it wanted to go home.

She sat down. She pulled the night's ledger toward her and began, mechanically, to check.

Entries forty-one hundred through forty-one oh-eight: fine. Proper. Her own handwriting, her own initials. Forty-one-oh-nine, a transfer of safekeeping for a reliquary key, fine. Forty-one ten—

Forty-one eleven was not hers.

The page itself was right. Same vellum, same ruling, same faint smell of the sizing the Guild used. But the hand was not hers, and it was not Corvane's, and it was not any of the nine recognized ledger-hands of the house. It was elegant and slightly archaic, letters formed with a formality centuries out of fashion, and it described—in the flat, precise language of inventory—an event that had happened four minutes ago:

*Night entry, unnumbered. On this date, at the hour of the turning tide: the border wards of Cael Saren fail, all of them, at once, cause unknown. The stars are withdrawn from the sky, all of them, at once, cause known to this office. The guild oath-marks register the change. One (1) ledger page appears in the audit room of the Vel Sarran house, written before the events it records. Appraisal pending.*

Isandro read it three times. Her hands were very steady, which she noted distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

*Written before the events it records.*

She touched the ink. It was dry. Of course it was dry. It had been dry for—how long? Since when? Since the moment the wards fell? Before?

*Cause known to this office.*

"What office?" she said aloud, and her voice sounded small and stupid in the empty room, and the black sky outside the window pressed its face against the glass and gave no answer.

She heard footsteps on the stair, slow and deliberate, and Corvane came in carrying two cups of tea as though it were any ordinary audit night, as though the world had not visibly broken, and Isandro watched him and understood, in the way you understand a thing all at once and spend the rest of your life catching up to the understanding, that he was not surprised.

He set one cup by her hand. He glanced at the open ledger. He did not look startled.

"Who wrote this?" she asked.

"Drink your tea," Corvane said.

"Corvane. Who wrote this?"

He lowered himself into his chair across from her, and for a long moment the only sound was the storm in its bottle grinding softly against the glass, wanting out, wanting up, wanting a sky that had been taken away. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost its dryness entirely. What remained underneath was old, and tired, and afraid.

"There is a page in every house ledger," he said, "in every guild archive going back four hundred years, written in that same hand. Most clerks work thirty years and never see one. You've seen yours on your first bad night, which tells me either the gods love you or—" he stopped himself, and picked up his tea, and did not drink it.

"Or what?"

"Or they're keeping an account of you specifically." He looked at her then, really looked, and his eyes went briefly—to her collarbone, to the place where her mark burned cold beneath the cloth—and away again, quick as a man touching a hot pan. "Pack a bag, Isandro. Tonight. Small things, valuable things. Don't tell anyone where you're going."

"Why?"

"Because at dawn," Corvane said quietly, "a summons will arrive from the capital. From the palace. They'll ask for the Guild's best appraiser to come look at the crown jewels." He rose, gathered his coat, moved toward the stairs with the stiff dignity of a man walking to his own execution and hoping no one would notice. At the door he paused, his back to her.

"How do you know that?" Isandro asked.

"Because," he said, "I wrote it down. Four days ago. In my own hand, in my own book, before I knew why." And he descended the stairs without looking back, leaving her alone in the lamplight with the impossible page, the cold brand under her collarbone, and the bottled storm straining against its glass toward a sky with nothing left in it.

Outside the window, far to the east, past the dead wards and the blind fields, the capital of Cael Saren lay under the same lidless dark—and something in it, Isandro was suddenly certain, had already begun to write her name.
