## 3. A King Wearing Something Else

The summons arrived the way all the Guild's summons arrived: too politely.

It came by courier at dawn, a folded sheet of vellum sealed in green wax, and the wax itself was the first wrong thing. Green wax meant Guild business. Green wax with the conclave's triple-knot pressed into it meant the Guild's *business*, capital-lettered, the kind that got people appraised rather than paid. Isandro turned it over twice before she broke the seal, standing in the doorway of her narrow room above the counting-house, the morning fog still dripping off the eaves.

*By order of the Ruling Conclave, Isandro Galloway, Appraiser Second Rank, is commanded to present herself at the seat of the Guild in Cael Saren, there to render professional judgment upon certain items of the royal treasury, at the request of the Crown. Travel expenses authorized. Duration: no more than nine days.*

*No more than nine days.* As though the conclave feared she might like the capital. As though they'd already decided how long she was worth.

She read it three times. The words didn't change, which was, she had learned over five years of a silent god, the only reliable property of words.

"Royal treasury," she said aloud, to the fog, to nobody. "They want the crown jewels appraised."

Which was nonsense. The crown jewels of Cael Saren had been appraised down to the last garnet a century ago and re-verified every reign since; the Guild kept the inventory in a fireproof vault three floors beneath the counting-house, and Isandro herself had audited it twice. You did not summon an Appraiser Second Rank across four hundred miles of bad road to count pearls that had been counted. You summoned her because you wanted her eyes on something else and needed a respectable reason to put her in the room.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited, cold as the oath-mark under her sleeve — because you wanted her in the capital when whatever had eaten the stars finished eating something bigger.

She thought about refusing. She drafted the refusal in her head on the walk down to the counting-house, three versions, each more courteous and more final than the last. But the summons said *commanded*, and the Guild's commands had teeth, and besides — Corvane was already waiting in the audit room when she came down, standing at the long table with his hands folded behind his back like a man at a funeral trying to remember what the deceased had looked like alive.

"You got yours too," she said.

"I got mine a day early." Corvane was seventy if he was a day, a dry stick of a man with ink-stained knuckles and the particular patience of someone who had spent forty years watching other people make expensive mistakes. He had taught her the ledgers. He had taught her the *other* ledgers, the ones that recorded things like the going rate for a borrowed year or the shelf-life of a bottled storm. He had never once lied to her, which in the Vellum Guild was a rarer virtue than honesty. "I'm to accompany you. Appraiser First Rank, senior witness. Standard for items of national significance."

"Which items are nationally significant, exactly?"

"The summons doesn't say." He said it too smoothly. Isandro had spent years learning to hear the seams in Corvane's voice, the places where the words had been fitted together in advance. There was a seam right there. "The Crown's request came through channels. The channels didn't elaborate."

"What channels?"

"The kind you don't ask about." He gathered his traveling case, the old leather one with the brass corners, and did not meet her eyes while he did it. "Pack for cold, Isandro. The capital's fog comes off the sea, and it gets into everything. Bones. Ledgers."

"Corvane."

"Hm?"

"Nine days ago the stars went out. Every one of them, in one breath, and the border wards came down the same night, and the Guild's response is to send two appraisers to count jewelry." She let that sit on the table between them like a coin of dubious weight. "What does the conclave know that I don't?"

For a moment — she would swear to this later, under oath if it had still counted for anything — Corvane's hands went still on the case. Not the stillness of calm. The stillness of a man holding a door shut.

"The conclave knows what it knows," he said, "and it will tell you what it tells you, and if you're wise you'll appraise what you're asked to appraise and write down what you're asked to write down." He lifted the case. His eyes came up at last, and whatever was in them was not in the voice at all. "And Isandro. If you see something in that palace that frightens you — don't touch it twice."

* * *

The road to Cael Saren took six days, and the world got worse the whole way there.

Isandro had traveled the king's road before, five years back, on her last Guild errand of any distance. She remembered it as a green corridor: hedgerows, shrine-posts every mile with their little brass bells, way-stations where the wardens checked your papers and the ward-lanterns burned blue and steady. The blue lanterns were the oldest magic in the kingdom, older than the crown, older than the Guild — each one anchored to the border wards, each one a small promise that the world's edges were holding.

Now the lanterns burned white. All of them, the whole road, a string of blind little moons, and the wardens had stopped checking papers because there was nothing left to check them *against*. At the third way-station Isandro watched a warden hold a traveler's charter up to a white lantern, and the lantern did not glow the green of a valid document or the red of a forgery. It did nothing. The warden shrugged and waved the man through, and the man's face, as he passed, was the face of someone who had just learned his name was optional.

"The wards didn't break," Brannoc would have said, if Brannoc had been there. Brannoc wasn't there yet; Brannoc was a chapter she hadn't met. But she had already begun rehearsing the theology of it, the way she rehearsed everything, because rehearsing was how she kept the silence in her chest from getting loud. *The wards didn't break. Something ate the promise they were made of.*

On the fifth night, at a way-station inn with a white lantern burning over the door, Corvane sat across from her over a supper of gray stew and finally, briefly, took the lid off.

"The conclave received a communication from the palace," he said, very quietly, in the pauses of the inn's bad fiddle music. "Not a request. A communication. The king's steward wrote that His Majesty wished the treasury appraised *in person*, by Guild hands, before the solstice. The conclave asked why. The steward's reply was one sentence." Corvane turned his spoon over in the stew without eating. "The reply was: *The king wants to know what his things are worth now.*"

"Now," Isandro repeated.

"Now. After." He looked up. "The conclave debated for two days whether to answer at all. Then they debated for one more day about who to send. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Two days of argument, from a conclave that once decided a tariff dispute in an afternoon. They're frightened, Isandro. The Guild is frightened. And when the Guild is frightened, it sends its best eyes and hopes the eyes come back."

"And if they don't come back?"

"Then the ledger records a loss." He said it in the flat cadence of doctrine, and then, quieter, in his own voice: "I'd rather it didn't."

She wanted to ask him what he'd seen in the conclave's faces. She wanted to ask why a summons for a jewel appraisal had been sealed with the triple-knot, which the Guild used for executions and erasures. She asked neither, because the fiddle struck up something faster, and because there is a kind of question you save until you're standing in the room where the answer lives.

* * *

*The ink is wrong today. I don't know how else to write it. I have ground the same soot-and-gum mixture for eleven years and today it comes out of the horn gray, and when I write the king's style — Aldric, by the grace of the deep springs, keeper of the wards — the gray dries darker than the page, as if the words are sinking somewhere. His Majesty has not slept in nine days. I know because I have not slept in nine days, because he has taken to dictating at all hours, and what he dictates is — I am a scribe of the fourth chair, I have copied treaties and death warrants and I am not easily moved — what he dictates is lists. Inventories. Every promise the crown has ever made, in order, going back four hundred years. He remembers all of them. He remembers them better than the archive does. Yesterday he recited a water-rights accord from the reign of Maravel the Second, verbatim, including a marginal note in Maravel's own hand that exists in no copy I have ever seen. When I asked how he knew it, he smiled at me. I am writing this at my own desk, in the dark, with the door barred, and I am going to write the only true thing I have written in nine days: that was not the king's smile. The king smiles with one side of his mouth, the side with the scar. This smile used both sides, evenly, like a door opening. And behind it, in his eyes, something was reading me the way I read a page.*

*— from the private notebook of Tam, scribe of the fourth chair, Cael Saren*

* * *

The palace of Cael Saren stood on its bluff above the gray sea like a held breath, and Isandro felt the wrongness of it before she passed the outer gate.

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it. White stone, deep eaves, the famous spire with its crown of weathervanes all pointing — she stopped on the causeway and looked — all pointing *inward*. Every vane on the spire, every one, aimed at the center of the building, as if the wind itself had been asked to pay attention.

"Appraiser Galloway." The steward who met them was a narrow man named Vess with the gray, overworked face of someone who had been polite for too many consecutive days. "His Majesty will receive you tomorrow, after you've completed the inventory. He's very eager for your findings." Something in the way he said *eager* made Isandro think of a man describing a dog that had started watching him eat.

The treasury work took the whole of the next day and was, on its surface, exactly what the summons promised: garnets, pearls, the great chain of office, eleven swords of state, a salt-cellar carved like a swan. Isandro appraised them with the cold fluency of her rank, and Corvane recorded, and the numbers were all correct and all beside the point. Twice she caught the palace servants watching her from doorways. Twice she caught the white lanterns — the palace had its own, dozens of them — flickering when she walked past, a stutter like a held note breaking.

It was near dusk, the light going long and copper through the high windows, when Vess returned and said the words she had been waiting six days to dread.

"His Majesty will see the senior appraiser now. Alone, if you please. He wishes to show you one item not on the inventory."

Corvane's hand found her elbow, brief and hard. *Don't touch it twice.* Then he let her go, because doctrine said the Crown's request was the Crown's request, and because — she saw it in his face as the doors closed behind her — he had known this was coming, and had argued about it for two days, and had lost.

The king's receiving room was round, and quiet, and wrong.

Not grand — that was the first shock. She had expected the weight of Cael Saren, the old gold, the banners of four hundred years. Instead the room was nearly bare. A table. Two chairs. A single window looking west at the darkening sea. And on the table, on a square of plain cloth, sat the royal sigil: the great seal-medallion of the kingdom, older than the crown that copied it, a disc of dark metal worked with the figure of a spire inside a circle of script.

King Aldric stood at the window with his back to her, and for one merciful moment he was only a man: broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, the scar on the left side of his mouth, wearing a plain wool tunic like a farmer asked to play a king in a pageant. Isandro had seen his likeness on coin and charter her whole life. She knew the set of those shoulders.

"Appraiser Galloway." He did not turn. His voice was the king's voice, tired and level. "They told me the Vellum Guild sends its best eyes. I want you to look at something with them."

"The sigil, Your Majesty?"

"It stopped being mine nine days ago." Now he turned, and Isandro's professional calm, the armor she wore into every negotiation, developed its first crack. Because his eyes were *tired*, genuinely tired, a man's tired eyes — and underneath the tiredness, far down, like a lamp at the bottom of a well, something else was looking out. Something that was not tired at all. Something that had all the patience of deep water. "Or perhaps I stopped being its. Appraise it. That's what you do, isn't it? You tell people what things are worth. Tell me what I'm worth."

"Your Majesty—"

"Touch it." He said it gently. That was the horror of it, later, when she had time to take the horror apart and examine its workings: he said it *gently*, with the king's own kindness, like a physician asking her to cough. "You'll want to. Your guild trains you to touch. Go on."

Every doctrine she had said *don't*. Every instinct, honed on bottled storms and borrowed years, said that you did not put your skin on an artifact of unknown provenance in a room with one door. And underneath all of it, cold and bright as the oath-mark under her sleeve, her god's silence said nothing at all, the way it had said nothing for five years, and she was so tired of the nothing.

She touched the sigil.

The metal was warm. That was the second crack in the armor — it should have been cool, everything in that sea-facing room was cool, but the sigil was warm as a sleeping animal, and the moment her fingers closed on its rim the warmth *closed back*.

She saw the king's memories.

Not all of them. A coat, hung on a hook, and she was seeing it the way you see a coat in a dark closet — by the shape it made of the absence inside it. A boy on a seawall counting gulls. A young man kneeling in a temple, swearing his coronation oath with tears on his face because his father had just died and he had never wanted any of it. A famine year — she felt its hunger in her own belly, felt the queues, the gray bread, the letters from the border provinces that said *the wells are failing, the wards are thinning, help us* — and a night in this same round room, this same table, when a desperate king had asked the old power under the mountain what it would take. And the answer, patient as sediment: *everything, freely given.* And the king — she felt his hands shake, she felt him do it *anyway*, because four hundred years of kingship is just the promise that you will pay for your people with yourself — the king had said yes.

That was the coat. Those were the memories, folded and hanging, worn by something that had never been a boy on a seawall, that had never wept at a coronation, that had learned hunger from the inside of a famine and found it *instructive*.

And the thing turned its attention on her.

It was not a face. It was older than faces. It was the pressure of a thumb on wet clay, deciding what shape the clay would be. It looked at her the way she had watched Tam describe being read — like a page — and she felt, with a clarity that would live in her bones for the rest of her life, that it was *considering her handwriting*. What she was written in. What she might be rewritten as.

Her oath-mark went cold. Not burning — cold, the way the stars had gone out, cold as a snuffed candle, and in the five-year silence of her god she heard — or felt, or was simply, suddenly, *sure* — one thing: that the thing in the sigil knew about the silence, and was pleased by it, and had perhaps arranged it the way a farmer arranges a fallow field.

She took her hand off the sigil.

Her knees nearly went. The room came back — the copper light, the sea, the two chairs, the king standing three feet away with both sides of his mouth curved in an even, door-shaped smile.

"Well?" said the thing wearing Aldric, in the king's tired voice. "What am I worth?"

And beneath it — she would spend chapters and blood and a broken oath proving it to herself, but she heard it *now*, one grain of it, a man's voice through glass, through well-water, through the folded coat of his own stolen memories — beneath it, faint as a lullaby through a wall, the king himself:

*— run —*

No. Not that word. Not yet. That word would come later, at a black-market shrine, from a different silence. What she heard now was smaller and worse, a single name, half-formed, the way a drowning man says a name:

*— Sefeya —*

Then it was gone, smoothed over, and the smile was only a smile, and the king said pleasantly, "You look pale, Appraiser. The sea fog does that. Vess will see you fed."

* * *

She did not remember walking back down the corridor. She remembered Corvane catching her arm at the turn of the stair, and she remembered that her hands were shaking so badly she could not have held a pen, and she remembered what she said to him, because she said it twice, because once was not enough to make it real:

"It's not the king. Corvane. The king is still in there. He's a *prisoner* in there, and the conclave knows, don't they, that's why the triple-knot, that's why nine days—"

"Isandro."

"—and the sigil *knew me*, it looked at me like a ledger, like an *entry*—"

"Isandro." His grip tightened. He glanced down the corridor, at the white lanterns, at the servants, at the walls of a palace that had begun, she now understood, to listen. When he spoke again his voice was the flattest she had ever heard it, doctrine-flat, conclave-flat, and that flatness frightened her more than the thing in the sigil had. "Tomorrow at first light we ride for the counting-house. You will write your report. You will write that the jewels are genuine and the sigil is sound. You will write *nothing else*."

"Corvane, I touched it, I *saw*—"

"I know what you saw." And there — for half a breath, in the copper light of a corridor in a palace where every weathervane pointed inward — the old man's face broke, just slightly, like ice over deep water. "Which is why, Isandro, before you write anything at all — you need to know what the Guild does to loose ends."

Behind them, far down the corridor, a white lantern flickered.

And in the dark of her room that night, unable to sleep, Isandro took out her traveling ledger and turned to a fresh page — and stopped.

The page was not fresh. At the top, in handwriting that was almost hers, in ink that was almost her ink, someone had already written tomorrow's date.

Below the date, a single line:

*The appraiser has seen the coat. Adjust the inventory.*
