## 4. What the Guild Does to Loose Ends

Corvane had kept the same office for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never once been summoned to the Conclave Chamber. Summoned was the wrong word, he corrected himself, climbing the spiral stair with his bad knee complaining at every turn. *Invited.* The Vellum Guild did not summon; it extended courtesies. It wrote polite letters on paper worth more than a farmhand's year of wages. It asked rather than ordered, and then it made certain the asking left you no room to refuse.

The letter had come an hour after dawn, delivered by a courier whose face Corvane did not recognize and whose boots were wet though it hadn't rained since the night the stars went out. That detail had stayed with him through breakfast, through the careful arrangement of his desk, through the long walk across the guildhall's marble floors where junior clerks bowed to him and he found himself wondering which of them would be assigned to inventory his effects.

The Conclave Chamber occupied the top floor of the guildhall, a room most members knew only by rumor. Corvane had appraised its contents once, years ago—a routine valuation of the tapestries—and even then he had noticed that the room held nothing personal. No portraits. No mementos. Just nine chairs arranged in a crescent behind a table of black glass, and above them, where any other institution would hang its founder's likeness or its patron's sigil, a blank stretch of wall that had been polished so many times it shone like still water.

He understood now why they kept it blank. You did not put a face on the thing you served. You gave it a name instead, and names, as every member of the Guild learned in their first year, could be owned.

"Master Corvane." The speaker sat at the center of the crescent: First Appraiser Heshel, a woman so old her skin had gone the color of vellum itself, thin and translucent and faintly luminous. Her voice was dry as ledger-paper. "Thank you for coming promptly."

"You flatter me, First Appraiser. I came as quickly as my knee allowed."

No one smiled. Nine faces regarded him from behind the black glass table, and Corvane counted them out of habit—Heshel; the twins Ondrel and Ondra, who appraised living memory; Master Quillon of the Sealed Vaults; and five others whose specialties he knew but whose names he had never bothered to learn, because in thirty-one years they had never mattered to him. The Guild's hierarchy was a machine for making people irrelevant to one another. He had always thought it efficient. Standing here, he thought it might be something else entirely.

"You are wondering why you've been invited," Heshel said.

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"Your apprentice has been to the capital."

It was not a question, so Corvane did not answer it. Isandro had returned from Cael Saren four days ago, pale and silent, carrying a report sealed in wax she had refused to let him read. She had said only: *The crown jewels are not what the manifest says they are. And the king—* and then she had stopped, and looked at him with an expression he had never seen on her before, and asked him what the Guild's doctrine said about a client who was not a client at all.

He had told her to write it up properly and let the archivists sort it. He had told her that because it was the correct answer, the doctrinally sound answer, the answer that had kept him alive and comfortable for three decades. And because he had told her that, she had gone to the archives herself, alone, at night, and pulled records she should not have touched, and now he was standing in a room he had spent his career avoiding while nine people who had never mattered decided what to do about the one person who did.

"Master Corvane," said Ondrel—or possibly Ondra; the twins were indistinguishable unless one knew their hands—"your apprentice accessed the Sigil Archive three nights past. Section Nine. Restricted holdings."

"She has clearance."

"Section Nine requires conclave-level clearance," said the other twin.

"Then there has been an error in our filing system," Corvane said, "which I will of course investigate personally—"

"There was no error." Heshel's voice cut clean through his. "She picked the lock. A skill, we note, that appears nowhere in her training record. One wonders where she learned it."

"One wonders," Corvane agreed pleasantly, "why a guild that trades in impossible things keeps locked doors at all. Surely anything truly valuable should be impossible to want."

The silence that followed had texture. Corvane had spent his life reading silences—the pause before a client named their true price, the held breath between an offer and an acceptance—and this one read like a blade being drawn slowly from oiled leather.

"You are fond of her," Heshel said. Not a question either.

"I am fond of good work. She does good work."

"We have read her report on the capital appraisal." Heshel drew a folder from beneath the black glass and laid it flat, but did not open it. "Do you know what she saw?"

"I assume she saw whatever was there to be seen."

"What was there," said Master Quillon, speaking for the first time, a soft man with soft hands and eyes like coins left too long in water, "was the Sigil. Wearing the king. She touched the royal seal and the Sigil touched her back. Do you understand what that means, Master Corvane? It means the oldest power in the world has taken notice of a journeywoman appraiser. It means she is now either an asset or a liability, and the Guild has a very old procedure for determining which."

"And what procedure is that?"

"The same procedure we use for everything." Quillon smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "We weigh it."

Corvane looked around the crescent of faces and felt, for the first time in thirty-one years, the full weight of what the Vellum Guild actually was. He had always known the trade was strange—bottled storms, borrowed years, names not yet earned—but he had told himself, as everyone told themselves, that the strangeness was commerce, that somewhere above the oddities was a plain market with plain rules. Now, looking at the blank wall above the nine chairs, he understood that there was no market. There was only appetite, and bookkeeping.

"How long," he asked quietly, "has the Guild served it?"

Heshel's translucent eyelids lowered and rose. "Served is an inelegant word. We maintain accounts. The Sigil maintains... liquidity. Every oath sworn in this kingdom passes through ledgers we keep. Every promise is collateral. This is not servitude, Master Corvane. It is *infrastructure*."

"And when the infrastructure develops opinions? When it eats a king and starts rewriting contracts unilaterally?"

"Then we adapt," Heshel said, "as we have always adapted. The stars going dark was not a catastrophe, Master Corvane. It was a *renegotiation*. The wards fell because their terms expired. New terms are being drafted. Our role—as it has ever been—is to ensure the transition proceeds without disorder." She folded her hands on the black glass. "Which brings us to your apprentice. She knows things that cannot yet be public knowledge. She has felt the Sigil's attention, and attention, once given, can be traced. If she speaks of what she saw—if she runs, if she writes letters, if she simply *tells the wrong person at the wrong tavern*—the renegotiation becomes complicated. Complications are expensive."

"You're asking me to authorize her silencing."

"We are not asking you anything," said Ondrel-or-Ondra. "We are informing you of a decision already weighed. Your invitation tonight is a courtesy. The Guild extends them to mentors. It is considered proper."

*Proper.* The word landed in Corvane's chest like a coin dropped down a well—he heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom.

"What does silencing entail?" he asked. His voice sounded very far away to himself. "Precisely."

Quillon consulted a card on the table before him, as though the answer required reference. "Memory excision, standard depth. Followed by reassignment to a provincial branch under a new name. She will live out her days appraising silverware in some coastal town, contented, harmless, unaware she ever saw a king wear something else." He glanced up. "It is, genuinely, a mercy. The alternative procedure is reserved for those who resist."

"And if she resists?"

"Then the alternative procedure," Quillon said, "and your file joins hers in the furnace. Loyalty is weighed too, Master Corvane. Weigh yours carefully."

* * *

He walked back down the spiral stair with his knee screaming and barely noticed. The guildhall was quiet at this hour, the night clerks bent over their desks in pools of lamplight, and Corvane moved among them like a ghost taking inventory of everything he was about to lose.

His office. Thirty-one years of it. The brass scales his own mentor had left him. The drawer where he kept Isandro's first appraisal—a chipped teacup she had valued at three coppers and then, in a footnote, at *one memory of the woman who owned it*, because even at nineteen she had understood that value lived in what things meant and not what they cost. He had laughed until his ribs hurt. He had thought: *this one will outlast me.*

He sat at his desk and opened the lower drawer, the one with the false back, and took out her file.

It was thinner than he expected. Files always were; they reduced a life to entries. *Isandro Galloway. Indentured at eleven, orphaned, oath-marked at thirteen by the god Vessia-of-the-Open-Hand, patron deity of unkept promises kept anyway.* He remembered the day she'd come to him with the mark fresh on her wrist, furious and frightened, asking him what a person owed to a god who wouldn't say what it wanted. He had told her the truth as the Guild taught it: that all vows were currency, that hers would appreciate. He had not told her the truth he'd learned tonight—that the currency went somewhere, that somewhere was hungry, that her god's silence these five years might not be absence at all but *collection*.

He turned the pages. Training record. Commendations. The incident with the smuggler in Vell's Crossing that she'd resolved by appraising the man's guilt and selling it back to him at a price he could pay. Page after page of a person rendered into entries, and at the bottom of the last page, in Quillon's neat hand, the disposition order:

*Subject to be silenced. Standard depth. Scheduled: third bell tomorrow.*

Third bell tomorrow. Twelve hours. Twelve hours and then Isandro Galloway would wake up in a coastal town loving silverware and remembering nothing, and the world would go on being eaten, tidily, with excellent bookkeeping.

Corvane sat for a long time with the file in his hands. He thought about doctrine. Doctrine said the individual was an entry and entries existed to balance. Doctrine had fed him, housed him, made him someone whose opinion carried weight in rooms he'd never enter. Doctrine was the only thing he had ever loved longer than the Guild itself.

And then he thought about a nineteen-year-old girl laughing over a teacup, and about the expression on her face when she'd come back from the capital, and about how she had asked him *what do we do when the client is the monster*, believing—actually believing—that he would know. That he would tell her the truth. That the Guild was a thing that could be trusted with the question.

He had failed her once by giving the safe answer. He would not fail her twice.

There was no way to warn her directly; the conclave would watch his correspondence, and a warning sent was a confession written. But Isandro had been his student for fourteen years, and Corvane had taught her the first lesson of appraisal himself: *never look at what a thing is; look at what it costs the person holding it.* She would understand a message built that way. She was clever enough, and frightened enough, and he had made her suspicious enough.

So he did the small things. He sent a clerk to her lodgings with a routine notice—an audit of her travel expenses, due within the week, with a line item flagged for review: *miscellaneous disbursements, capital, unverifiable.* Bureaucratic nonsense. But the word *unverifiable* was one they had used between themselves for years, a private joke about clients whose stories didn't hold: *that account is unverifiable, best not to stand near it when it's called in.* She would read it and hear him saying *they know, they're coming, run.*

And then he took her file to the brazier.

The paper caught reluctantly, the way old vellum does, curling and browning at the edges before the flame took hold. Corvane watched Isandro Galloway's life curl into ash—her indenture, her commendations, her oath-mark, the disposition order with Quillon's tidy signature—and he understood that he was not destroying a record. He was destroying the *entry*. As long as the file existed, she was a line in the Guild's books, a debt to be collected, a loose end to be trimmed. Burned, she became something the ledgers could not price: a person unaccounted for. An entry the Guild would have to write by hand, slowly, hunting her through a country gone dark, while the ash of her old life drifted up the flue and out into a starless sky.

When it was done he sat back in his chair, knees aching, hands smelling of smoke, and permitted himself exactly one minute of grief—for the office, for the doctrine, for the comfortable man he had been at dinner yesterday, who had believed the Guild was merely strange rather than complicit.

Then he opened his desk, took out a fresh sheet of the Guild's finest paper, and began to write his resignation. It would be refused, of course. Resignations from the inner circle were not accepted; they were *processed*. By morning he would be a liability too, and by tomorrow night perhaps a second file would sit in a drawer with his name on it, waiting for a third bell.

Let them write it. He had thirty-one years of experience hiding things in ledgers, and he intended to spend every last trick of the craft buying his student time—false entries, misfiled warrants, a forged transfer order sending the enforcers to the wrong city gate on the wrong day. He was an old man with a bad knee and no gift for running. But he had spent his whole life learning what things cost, and he finally had something worth the price.

Somewhere across the dark city, Isandro Galloway was reading a bureaucratic notice and beginning, he hoped, to be afraid in the right direction.

Run, girl, he thought, feeding his own file's first page to the brazier. Unverifiable. Best not to be standing anywhere at all when they call it in.
