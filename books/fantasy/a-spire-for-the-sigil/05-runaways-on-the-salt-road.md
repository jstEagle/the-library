## 5. Runaways on the Salt Road

The capital's gates closed behind me at the hour when honest people were asleep and the dishonest were at their best work.

I had left the Vellum Guild house by the coal chute, because the front door had been watched since dusk by two men wearing the gray sashes of Guild wardens who did not know my face but knew my description. I had left with nothing but my ledger satchel, a knife Corvane had given me eleven years ago on the day I swore my oath, and a coin purse heavy enough to buy passage out of Cael Saren if I could find anyone fool enough to take it.

The city at night was a body pretending to be calm. Lamplighters worked their rounds with hands that shook. In the temple district, every bell tower stood dark; the bell-ringers had stopped ringing when the stars went out, as though sound itself might draw attention from whatever was looking down—or not looking down—at us now. Above the rooftops there was only sky like a lid of wet slate, featureless from horizon to horizon, so complete in its emptiness that my eyes kept trying to build stars into it, little ghosts and afterimages, and failing.

My oath-mark ached under my collar. Not burning now—cold, a slow cold like a stone held too long against the skin. Five years of silence from my god, and in one night He had gone from silent to *absent*, the way a room goes absent when someone you love walks out of it for good.

*Keep moving,* I told myself. Grief was a luxury. The wardens would check the coal chute by dawn.

The Salt Road began at the River Gate, where the customs officers had all gone home to pray to gods who were, presumably, also not answering. Beyond the gate the road ran white and pale under the starless sky—a crushed-shell causeway raised above the marshland, old as the kingdom, built by hands no ledger recorded. It was the one road out of the capital that moved goods without moving paper, which meant it was the one road smugglers loved.

I had audited enough contraband entries to know its rhythms. What I hadn't known, until tonight, was what traveled it at the hour before dawn.

I heard them before I saw them: wheels, but muffled—wool wrapped around iron rims—and voices kept low, and then, absurdly, a snatch of singing, a woman's voice carrying a dockside tune about a fisherman's wife and a very forgiving sea. They came around the bend of the causeway in a wagon painted the flat brown of nothing worth stealing, drawn by two mules, and standing up on the driver's bench was a broad woman with a red kerchief tied over her curls and a smile that suggested she found the end of the world moderately entertaining.

She saw me before I decided whether to step aside or hide. She reined in.

"Well," she said. "Either the stars going out has made people bold, or you're the worst bandit I've ever seen. You're standing right in the open. With a *satchel*."

"I'm not a bandit."

"Pity. Bandits tip better." She looked me over with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a horse—not unkindly, just thorough. "Guild boots," she said. "Vellum Guild, if I'm any judge, and I am. Running away from them or running an errand for them?"

"Running from them."

"And the truth comes out." She grinned wider. "Running *from* is my favorite kind of passenger. They pay double and ask fewer questions. Climb up."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You're standing on a smugglers' road at three in the morning with nowhere else to be, arguing terms with the person offering you a ride. That's agreement with extra steps." She kicked the brake loose. "Ovesa Vane. Captain, owner, and sole moral authority of this operation. Get on or get walked on."

I got on. It says something about how far I'd fallen that this felt like the most sensible decision I'd made in days.

There was a third figure on the wagon bed amid the crates—bales of wool, supposedly, though the bales clinked when the wheels jolted. He sat with his hood back, a big man gone soft around the middle, with a tonsure grown halfway out and a wooden symbol at his throat that had been deliberately defaced, the sigil of his order scratched down to bare grain. His eyes were kind and tired.

"Brannoc," he said, by way of everything.

"Isandro."

"A guildswoman and a godless priest on a stolen wagon under a starless sky." Brannoc smiled faintly. "The world ends and we all find better company. Hold on—the causeway gets lively past the third mile."

He was not wrong. The Salt Road, I discovered, was not entirely loyal to geography. Twice in the next hour the marsh shifted around us—I felt it rather than saw it, a wrongness like a word mispronounced—and each time Ovesa simply turned the mules left instead of right, murmuring to herself, consulting a folded scrap of paper that glowed faintly at the edges. The second time, the road we'd been traveling simply wasn't there anymore behind us, drowned in reeds, and I decided I had drunk less than I'd thought or considerably more than I remembered.

"That's Wren's doing," Ovesa said, catching my look. "Our cartographer. She draws the roads that move. Best in the business, mostly because she's the only one in the business."

"There are maps for this?"

"There's exactly one map for this." Ovesa's grin softened into something almost proud. "And she doesn't sell it. Don't take it personal—she doesn't let anybody see much of it. You'll meet her at the camp."

We came off the causeway as the sky went from slate to a bruised, starless gray. Dawn happened without ceremony; the light simply seeped up out of the ground, since nothing was willing to come down from the sky. The camp was in a hollow of dunes behind a derelict tollhouse, half-hidden under nets and driftwood: two wagons, a cookfire, a line of laundry, a lean-to stacked with cargo whose labels had all been carefully scraped off. A dozen people moved through the camp doing morning things—mending, cooking, arguing about a card game—with the practiced ease of folk who had made a life inside the cracks of another life.

And apart from all of it, at a folding table near the water, sat a woman with ink-stained fingers and a look of ferocious concentration, drawing.

Ovesa led me straight to her. "Wren! Found us a stray. Says she's running from the Guild."

The cartographer looked up, and I forgot, briefly, what I was going to say.

It wasn't beauty, exactly—or it was, but the kind that sneaks up on you sideways. Sharp-boned, sun-browned, with a scar through one eyebrow and eyes the gray-green of deep water, and an expression that assessed me the way Ovesa had but went deeper, past the boots and the satchel, to somewhere underneath. Her gaze snagged, just for a heartbeat, on my collar. On the place beneath it.

"Guild," she said. Her voice was low and even. "You're a long way from your ledgers."

"They're a long way from wanting me back."

Something in her face eased by a degree. "Then sit down before you fall down. You look like a page that's been written on too many times."

I sat. She pushed a tin cup of something hot and terrible across the table at me—chicory, salt, and optimism—and went back to her work, and I watched her draw despite myself.

What she drew should not have existed. Roads, yes—but they moved under her pen, or seemed to; lines that had curved eastward a moment ago bent westward as she worked, correcting themselves, and small annotations crawled along the margins in her cramped hand: *unstable since the wards fell. Third crossing gone. Do not use the ferry at Mireholt.* And threaded through the roads, in washes of pale color, were other marks—stains, smudges of gray and violet that spread outward from certain points like ink dropped in water, and every stain, every single one, was oriented toward the capital. Toward the palace hill. As if the whole country were a wound, and the city its festering heart.

"The stars," I said quietly. "You've mapped it already. Whatever happened last night."

Wren's pen stopped.

"It didn't happen last night," she said, without looking up. "It's been happening for years. Slowly. Last night it just—" She hesitated, choosing the word with care, the way she chose her lines. "Finished. The stains all reached the same depth at once. Like water finding its level." Now she looked at me, and there was something in her face beyond caution—something that might have been fear, offered to a stranger because keeping it alone had become too heavy. "You felt it too. Don't ask how I know. People who run from the Vellum Guild don't feel things the way other people feel them."

Under my collar, the cold ache of the oath-mark pulsed once, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

"I need to go north," I said. "Toward the coast. There's someone I have to reach, and answers I have to dig up, and neither of them will wait."

"North is where we're headed," Ovesa said, appearing with a plate of fried something. "Coincidence. We like coincidences. They're free."

"What's the price?"

"For you?" Ovesa chewed, considering. "Hands. You're guild-trained, which means you can read, write, cipher, spot a forgery at thirty paces, and probably talk your way past a customs man. My crew can do maybe two of those things on a good day. You work passage. Fair warning—" she pointed her fork at me— "we lost our bookkeeper to a marriage in Harrowmere, and I've been keeping accounts in my head, and my head is a liar."

Brannoc snorted into his cup. "Her head is a catastrophe. Last month she wrote down that we spent forty crowns on 'miscellaneous virtue.'"

"It was a donation to the shrine at Saltmouth."

"It was a bribe to the harbormaster, and you know it, and the ledger knows it, and the ledger is ashamed."

Despite everything—the burned bridges behind me, the cold mark at my throat, the empty sky pressing down like a held breath—I laughed. It came out rusty, startled out of me, and three heads turned toward it as if laughter were rarer than stars these days. Maybe it was.

"All right," I said. "I'll keep your books. But I do it properly. Double entry, dated entries, and no 'miscellaneous virtue.' If I'm running from the finest record-keepers in the world, I'm not going to embarrass the craft."

"Wren?" Ovesa said. "She rides with your wagon. Any objection?"

Wren had gone back to her map, but her pen had stilled again, and she was looking at me with that deep-water gaze, weighing something I couldn't see. "No objection," she said at last. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "Anyone the stains point at is welcome to stand next to me. Misery loves accurate company."

I didn't ask what she meant. I would learn, later, and wish I'd asked sooner.

* * *

We traveled north for six days, and the world taught me new ways to be wrong about it.

I learned that the Salt Road crew—Ovesa called them the Tideborn, though they were neither born of tide nor especially poetic—moved cargo that ranged from the merely illegal (salted eels, untaxed brandy, prayer-candles blessed by defrocked priests, which was to say Brannoc) to the genuinely impossible. One crate held a storm in a bottle, sloshing faintly when the wagon jolted, and I caught myself auditing it by reflex: *one bottled gale, provenance uncertain, declared value insultingly low.*

I learned that Brannoc had been a priest of the Quiet Hand, an order devoted to my own silent god, and that he had left the cloth not in despair but in disgust—his superiors had declared the silence doctrine, turned absence into theology, preached that the god's quiet was itself a message. "They built a church out of a locked door," he told me one evening, watching the fire. "I wanted to know who had the key. Turns out nobody does. Or nobody admits to it." He said it lightly. He did not look at my collar when he said it, which was its own kind of courtesy.

I learned that Wren slept badly, sat facing exits, and talked in her maps the way other people talked in their sleep. And I learned, on the fourth night, why she never let anyone hold them.

We had made camp in a fold of hills where the starless dark came down close, and I woke past midnight to find her table lamp still burning and her hunched over the master chart, adding layer after layer to it. I meant to walk past. I didn't.

Up close, the map was worse than stains. Under the colored washes, in fine gray linework, she had drawn the land itself changing—villages shrinking, roads forgetting their destinations, whole districts rendered in a tentative, dissolving line, as if the country were being sketched by an artist losing confidence. And everywhere, converging on the capital like veins toward a heart, the stains. Except—

Except for one place where the pattern broke. A single point in the northern hill country where the stains swerved, parted, flowed around a small blank circle the way a stream flows around a stone.

The circle sat exactly where we had camped tonight.

"That's you," Wren said quietly. She hadn't looked up, but she'd known I was there; I was beginning to suspect she always knew. "Or it's where you sleep. I started noticing on the second night. Wherever you are, the pattern bends." She finally raised her eyes to mine, and there was no accusation in them, only a tired, careful wonder. "I've mapped this rot for nine years, guildswoman. Nine years of watching it eat names and roads and promises. You're the first thing it's ever gone *around*."

"My oath-mark," I heard myself say. "It's been cold since the night the stars went out. Before that it was silent five years. I assumed my god had—" I stopped. I had never said the next part aloud. "Abandoned me."

"Or," Wren said softly, "something between you is eating the messages. And whatever it is, it doesn't like the taste of you." She rolled the chart closed with gentle hands, the way one closes a coffin or a letter. "Get some sleep, Isandro. Tomorrow we cross the Graywash, and I need you sharp, because the Graywash is where my map stops being sure of itself."

* * *

The Graywash earned its name. It was a river valley where fog lived year-round, thick as wool and twice as concealing, and the smugglers' route through it depended entirely on Wren's charts—on landmarks that were not always where they should be, on a bridge that appeared on Tuesdays and some Thursdays, on cairns that Wren herself had built and maintained like a woman tending graves.

We crossed at midmorning, wagons in single file, Ovesa walking ahead with the chart open on her forearm like a battle commander reading terrain. The fog swallowed sound and direction alike. Twice we stopped dead while Wren stood motionless in the white, head tilted, listening to something none of us could hear, before saying "left here" in a voice that permitted no debate.

It was on the far side, where the ground climbed out of the fog into weak gray daylight, that Brannoc, riding rear guard, gave a short sharp whistle.

Riders behind us. Three of them, coming up out of the mist at a canter, and even at that distance I knew the cut of the coats—dark wool, high collars, and at the shoulder a badge I knew better than my own handwriting.

Not Guild wardens. Guild *collectors*. Wardens watched; collectors collected. Debts, dues, loose ends. Their leader wore his hair cropped for a helm and carried a document case chained to his wrist, and I knew with a certainty that settled in my stomach like swallowed ice exactly whose name was written in that case.

Mine.

"Off the road," Ovesa snapped, and the camp that had seemed casual for six days became a machine—cargo covered, weapons out of nowhere, mules turned calmly off the track as though this were routine, because for them it was. Wren pulled me behind the lead wagon with a grip hard enough to bruise. Through the gap between crates I watched the riders rein in twenty paces off.

The collector unfolded a paper. Even at distance I could hear the voice, dry and official as a court summons.

"By authority of the Vellum Guild conclave: Isandro Galloway, appraiser second class, is named in default of covenant. Surrender of person and effects is requested. Resistance will be noted in her file."

"Noted in her file," Ovesa muttered beside me. "These people threaten you with *paperwork*."

"It works," I whispered. "That's the horror of it. It works."

Brannoc stepped forward into the open, hands visible, defaced symbol swinging at his throat. "Brothers," he called, using the old address of the orders, warm as a hearth. "The lady owes you nothing. Whatever's in that file, it was written by frightened men. Take the road home. Nothing here needs collecting."

For a moment—one long, strange moment—the lead collector hesitated. I saw his eyes flick to Brannoc's broken symbol, to the fog at his back, to the wagon train of unremarkable brown. I saw him weigh, visibly, the cost of a fight against the shame of returning empty-handed to men who kept files on everyone, including him.

Then the fog behind his shoulder moved wrong. It gathered, coiled, and shaped itself—and out of it walked a fourth figure no rider had announced, tall and gray-robed, moving with the unhurried certainty of something that had never needed a horse.

The collector's face went white. He knew it. Whoever sent it, it wasn't the conclave's usual tool.

The figure raised its head, and though the fog hid everything above its chin, I felt its attention find me through the wagon wall, through the canvas and the oak, as precisely as a finger laid on my skin. Beneath my collar, the oath-mark went from cold to *arctic*.

"*Appraiser Galloway*," it said, and the voice was the sound a ledger makes when it closes. "Your account is overdue."

Beside me, Wren's hand found mine in the dark under the wagon. Her palm was ink-stained and steady, and she did not let go.

"Right," said Ovesa cheerfully, drawing a pistol that looked far too large to be legal anywhere. "New plan."
