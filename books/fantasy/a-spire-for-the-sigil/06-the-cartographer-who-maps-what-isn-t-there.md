## 6. The Cartographer Who Maps What Isn't There

Wren had three rules for the maps that kept her alive, and she broke all of them the night she showed Isandro Galloway the truth.

The first rule was: never let anyone watch you draw. The second was: never let anyone touch the finished work. The third—the one she'd written on the inside of her own wrist in ink that didn't wash out—was *the maps are not for showing, they are for surviving.*

She told herself, later, that it was the road that made her do it. The Salt Road between Hallowmere and the river crossing had a habit of relocating in the dark, sliding a half-mile east or west like a snake shedding a bad decision, and if you didn't know where it was going to be by morning, you woke up in a barley field with no road at all and a compass that pointed, sullenly, at your own chest. Ovesa knew the road the way a drunk knows the walk home—instinct, luck, and a lot of swearing. Brannoc knew it the way a man knows a former lover: with resentment and a detailed memory of old wounds. But Wren knew it because she drew it, every night, and every morning it was where her pen said it would be.

That was the secret. That was the thing that had gotten her defrocked, exiled, and very nearly drowned in the harbor at Coriel's Landing: her maps didn't record the world. They *preceded* it.

She sat in the wagon's back with the canvas flap tied open, the last grey light of a starless dusk pooling around her knees, and she drew the Salt Road's tomorrow. Around her, the crew did the small work of surviving: Ovesa arguing with the mule, which was winning; Brannoc boiling something that smelled like regret and onions; Isandro sitting on the tailgate with her ledgers open, doing whatever it was guild clerks did to pass the time on the run from their own murder.

Wren watched her over the top of the map. She'd been watching her for six days, which was four days longer than Wren watched anyone, and she still couldn't get the arithmetic right. Isandro Galloway was supposed to be cargo—rescued from a Guild checkpoint, owed passage, owed nothing else. But cargo didn't sit up at night memorizing the route. Cargo didn't ask questions like *why does the compass point at you* with the flat, patient voice of a woman reading a ledger line that didn't add up. Cargo didn't have an oath-mark on her wrist that made Wren's ink crawl in its pot.

The ink was crawling now.

Wren looked down. In the little horn cup beside her knee, the black liquid had begun to move—not swirling, not bubbling, but *leaning*, the whole surface tilting toward Isandro like iron filings finding their north. It had been doing it since the capital. She'd told herself it was the wagon's motion. The wagon was currently stopped.

*Third rule,* she reminded herself. *The maps are not for showing.*

But the stars were gone. Every one of them, gone in a single breath six nights ago, and Wren had spent that breath on her knees in a ditch outside Coriel's Landing because her maps had shown her it was coming—shown her three weeks before, a slow black bloom spreading across the sky-page where the constellations used to be, and she'd told no one, because who tells a stranger *the sky is going to be erased, I have it here in graphite*? She had watched the darkness come on paper first. She was the only person alive who could say that, and she was so tired of being the only person alive who could say anything.

"Your ink's trying to escape," Isandro said, without looking up from her ledger.

Wren's pen stopped. "It does that."

"Mm." Isandro turned a page. The sound of it was very loud in the dusk. "It's been doing it since the capital. It leans at me when I sit downwind. It leaned at me this morning when I was washing my face, and Brannoc thought I'd cursed the water." She looked up then, and her eyes in the grey light were the color of the sky where stars used to be. "I keep ledgers, Wren. I know what an unexplained entry looks like. Yours has been sitting in my book for six days, accruing interest."

Wren set down her pen. Picked it up. Set it down again. "You want the truth."

"I want the truth," Isandro agreed, "or I want a lie good enough to survive the river crossing. Your choice. I'll know which one it is either way."

* * *

Here is what Wren should have said: *It's a trick of the light. It's an old habit of the ink, it does that in cold weather. Here, look, nothing, nothing, nothing.*

Here is what she said instead, because the starless dark was pressing on the canvas like a thumb on a bruise, and because Isandro Galloway had a voice like a closed door that had decided, just this once, to stay open:

"Show me your wrist."

Isandro didn't flinch. Wren gave her credit for that; most people flinched when a stranger asked for their wrist, and guild clerks flinched hardest, trained as they were to know exactly what a mark on the skin could bind them to. But Isandro set down her ledger, pushed back the sleeve of her left arm, and held out the oath-mark to the dying light.

It was a fine thing, even now. Wren had seen a hundred oath-marks in her cartography years—the Guild branded its own, and half the monasteries inked theirs, and once, in a market in Vash, she'd seen a slave's collar-scar that had grown into something with the same geometry, which was a thought she kept in a locked drawer and never opened. Isandro's was a circle of script so small it looked like a stain from a distance, a ring of god-language wound twice around the forearm, written by no human hand. It had gone cold and white at the edges since the night the stars went out. Wren knew that too, because she'd been watching.

"May I?" Wren said.

"You may."

She touched it with two fingers, the way she'd touch a page she was afraid of tearing, and the ink in the horn cup *sang*—a thin, high note, below hearing, felt in the teeth. Isandro's breath caught. Wren snatched her hand back.

"Sorry. Sorry. I should have warned you."

"Warned me about what?" Isandro's voice was steady, but her pulse was jumping under the skin Wren had just touched, and Wren could feel it still, a ghost-tremor in her own fingertips. "What did you just do?"

"I listened." Wren pulled the horn cup into her lap and held it up so Isandro could see the ink leaning, straining, all its weight thrown toward the oath-mark like a dog at the end of a chain. "That's what I do. That's what my maps are. Everyone thinks a cartographer draws what's there—roads, rivers, mountains, the boring bones of the world. I did too, once. I was very good at it. Too good. I started noticing things that shouldn't have been on the maps at all."

She reached into the map-case at her hip—the oilcloth roll she slept with, ate with, would pull from a burning building before she pulled a child—and unrolled it across the space between them, weighting the corners with the ink cup, a spool of thread, Isandro's own ledger, which she took without asking, which Isandro allowed without comment, which was its own small miracle.

The map was of the western provinces. It was beautiful; Wren permitted herself that vanity even in the middle of confession. The coastlines were fine as hair, the mountain hatching was so precise it looked engraved. But threaded through the beauty, in ink so black it seemed to sit *above* the paper rather than on it, were stains.

Not blots. Not spills. They had shape. They had *growth rings*, like the cross-section of a tree that had been poisoned in its sapling years. One sat over the ruins of the old temple at Marrow Fen. One had swallowed the whole of the Greymarch. One, small and fresh and terrible, sat over the village of Dunlow, where Wren had drawn a tiny annotation in her cramped hand: *names gone, ask no one, they cannot tell you.*

And all of them—every stain, every ring, every spreading darkness on the page—were connected by lines so faint you had to tilt the map to catch the light on them. And all the lines ran to the same place.

The capital. Cael Saren. Where the lines converged they didn't tangle; they *braided*, tightening into a single cord as black and fine as a hair, and that cord ran off the edge of the map.

"Where does it go?" Isandro asked quietly.

"Off the page." Wren turned the map over. On the reverse, drawn in the same black ink, was a human forearm. Life-sized, or near it. And around the wrist, in a ring of script too small to read, wound twice—there was a stain.

Isandro went very still. Stillness, Wren was learning, was what Isandro did instead of screaming. She'd seen it at the checkpoint, when the Guild enforcers had called her name and she'd gone quiet as a closed book. She was doing it now.

"That's my arm," Isandro said.

"That's your arm."

"You drew my arm on the back of a map of the western provinces."

"I drew it three weeks ago," Wren said. "Before I met you. Before the checkpoint. Before the stars went out." She made herself keep talking, because if she stopped she would never start again, and because the truth was a door that only opened outward. "My maps come *first*, Isandro. I don't draw what is. I draw what's coming. I have done since I was nineteen years old, and the church called it heresy and the Guild called it fraud and both of them were wrong, it's neither, it's just—" She gestured helplessly at the ink, still leaning, still singing its note below hearing. "It's the world telling me where it's going, and me writing it down fast enough to matter."

* * *

The mule had won its argument with Ovesa, and the wagon creaked into motion, and the grey dusk thickened toward a dark that no longer had any business being called night, because night had stars and this had nothing. Brannoc lit the lanterns without being asked, and their light made a small warm room of the wagon's back, and inside that room Wren laid out the rest of it, because Isandro had said *all of it or nothing* and Wren found, to her own horror, that she wanted to give her all of it.

"The stains aren't random," she said. "I spent four years thinking they were. Plagues, curses, bad aquifers, the usual explanations you tell yourself so you can sleep. But they have a pattern. They grow along the lines. The lines all run to the capital, and they're getting *faster*—Dunlow went blank in a single night, do you understand? A whole village of names, erased between one sunset and the next. And the night the stars went out, every line on every map I own jumped at once. Like a pulse. Like something swallowed."

Isandro was looking at the map of her own arm. "The Sigil," she said.

"You know that word."

"I've touched it." Isandro said it the way people report a wound. "In the capital. I was sent to appraise the crown jewels and I touched the royal sigil and there is something *inside* the king, Wren. Something old. It was wearing his memories like a coat." She looked up. "I came to you because your compass pointed at you and your ink leans at me and because in six days you're the only person I've met who isn't either trying to kill me or lying to me about the sky. So tell me the rest. Why does the thing inside the king care about my arm?"

Wren took a breath. This was the part she'd never said aloud to anyone, the part that lived below even the heresy, and saying it felt like pulling a splinter that had grown roots.

"Because you're not on the map as a witness," she said. "I need you to understand—I didn't choose this. I don't choose any of it. I wake up and my hands draw what they draw. Three weeks ago my hands drew your arm, and the stain on it, and the lines running to it from the capital, and the lines running *from* it—" She turned the map. Below the inked forearm, where a cartographer would put the title, she had written a single word in the god-script that no one had taught her: the script that grew on oath-marks and old temple stones and, lately, on the margins of every map she made.

Isandro read it. Wren watched her read it. Watched the color leave her face in the lantern light, watched her lips move around the translation the way a person moves their lips around a name they haven't spoken in five years of grief.

"*Key,*" Isandro said.

"The lines don't pass through you," Wren said. "They *end* at you. Or—" she turned the map again, the inked arm, the ring of script, the stain that sat over it like a shadow over a lamp, "—they end at the mark. Whatever the Sigil is, whatever it's doing to the king and the sky and the names of villages, it's all running through that oath on your wrist. You're not in the story, Isandro. You're in the *mechanism*."

The wagon jolted over a stone. Neither of them moved to steady the maps.

"Five years," Isandro said at last, and her voice had gone somewhere Wren hadn't heard it go before—down past the dry wit, past the ledger-clerk's calm, into a cellar where a woman had been keeping a grief the size of a god. "Five years of silence. I write to my god every night. Letters. Hundreds of them. I keep them in a box because I can't bear to burn them and I can't bear to send them nowhere. And you're telling me the silence has a *shape*. That it's not absence. That it's—" her hand closed over the oath-mark, over the ring of cold white script, "—that it's *plumbing*."

"I'm telling you what the maps say," Wren said softly. "I've never once known them to lie. I've known them to be cruel. Those are different things."

Isandro laughed, or something adjacent to it, and dragged a hand down her face. "A key. To what?"

"I don't know." Wren gathered the map, rolled it, and then—breaking her second rule, breaking it so thoroughly she might as well burn the rule and salt the ground—pressed the roll into Isandro's hands. "But I know where the lines *come* from. Under the braided cord, under the capital, there's an older set of lines. Fainter. They don't run to the Sigil. They run *away* from it, west, toward the coast, toward water. Toward something the Sigil is sitting on top of. Or holding down."

Isandro looked at the map roll in her hands. Then at Wren. And Wren, who had spent eleven years being the strange one, the exiled one, the woman whose ink crawled and whose compass pointed at her own chest, watched this stranger—a guild clerk, a heretic's clerk, a woman with a silent god and a price on her head—look at her the way no one had ever looked at her: not with fear, not with hunger, not with the careful patience you give a madwoman. With *recognition*. With the expression of someone finding a page of their own ledger in a stranger's book.

"Tomorrow," Isandro said, "you're going to draw me the road to the coast."

"Tomorrow," Wren agreed, "I'm going to draw you the road to the coast."

"And tonight—" Isandro hesitated, and for the first time in six days she looked, briefly, uncertain. "Tonight, could you—draw something small? Something harmless. I want to see it happen. I want to know it's real, because if it's real, then the arm is real, and if the arm is real—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'd rather know tonight than at the river."

Wren took out a fresh sheet. Thought. And drew, in three quick lines, the dawn: the road bending east around the barley field, the sun coming up red over the hills, and on the road, a rider. Small, distant, unremarkable—a figure on a lathered horse, coming hard from the capital road, with the Guild's grey cloak whipping behind.

She signed and dated it, as she always did. Then she looked at the date, and her stomach dropped, because the date was wrong. The date was *yesterday's*.

The ink was still wet when they heard the hoofbeats.

Isandro was on her feet before the sound resolved, ledger forgotten, oath-mark bared to the lantern light like a drawn knife. "Wren," she said, in the voice of a woman reading an entry she never wrote, "how fast can you draw us somewhere *else*?"

Wren was already unrolling a blank sheet.
