## 7. Oaths Are Currency

Brannoc had buried three gods in his life, and he had learned the same lesson each time: the digging was never the hard part. The hard part was the moment you set down the shovel and realized you were the one who had to keep talking to the empty hole.

He stood in the back room of the Salt Road's worst-kept secret—a shrine that smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, hidden behind a fishmonger's cellar three streets from the river. The old gods' niches had been converted to storage. Jars of pickled eel where the Mother of Tides once kept her offerings. A crate of smuggled lace where the Lantern-Brother's candle had burned for six hundred years. Only one niche still held anything holy: a cracked bowl of river water that Brannoc himself had filled, because someone had to, and because he had not yet learned how to stop.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money," Ovesa said, shouldering past him with a crate of contraband prayer-flags. "It doesn't. Nothing in this room owes anybody anything. That's the whole point of the place."

"That," Brannoc said, "is exactly the problem."

He heard them come down the stairs before he saw them—the particular uneven tread of Wren guiding Isandro, who still moved like a woman whose bones had been rearranged by bad news. Five days out of the capital, and the mercenary still flinched at lamplight. Brannoc understood. He had flinched at lamplight for two years after the Synod stripped his orders from him. Some fires follow you.

"Sit," he said, gesturing to the upturned crates arranged in a rough circle. "All of you. What I have to say, you all need to hear, and I'd rather say it once."

Ovesa dropped her crate with a thump that rattled the eel jars. "Is this a sermon? Because I told you, Brannoc, I'm a paying customer for miracles but not for sermons."

"It's an accounting lesson."

That got their attention. It always did, in this world. Even smugglers respected a ledger.

* * *

"I was a priest of the Quiet Hand for nineteen years," Brannoc began, when they had settled. "Before the Synod defrocked me, before I started moving cargo for Ovesa, I kept a shrine much like this one. Smaller. Poorer. And I will tell you the thing they taught me on my first day of novitiate, the thing every priest in every order of the old faith learns before they learn to tie their own sashes."

He held up one hand, fingers spread.

"Oaths are currency."

Wren, curled on her crate with her ink-stained fingers laced over one knee, tilted her head. "Meaning what, exactly? That's a metaphor, surely."

"No," Isandro said quietly. "It isn't."

Brannoc looked at her. She sat very straight, the way soldiers and accountants sit when they already suspect the answer and are only waiting for the arithmetic to confirm it. Her right hand—the one with the oath-mark, the coiled sigil of her guild-god burned into the skin between thumb and wrist—lay open in her lap, and he noticed she was not looking at it. She was very deliberately not looking at it.

"You know," he said. "Tell them what you know."

"A vow is a binding," Isandro said. Her voice had the flat cadence of a woman reading from a ledger, which Brannoc recognized as armor. "When two parties swear, something is created. A debt, a promise, a thread. The old faith held that these threads are real. That they have weight. That the god—the Quiet One, the Listener, whatever name you prefer—holds them in trust. Every contract sworn in good faith, every wedding vow, every soldier's oath, every prayer that begins *I promise*. They're all entries in the same book."

"And who keeps the book?" Wren asked, though her voice had gone careful.

"The god," Brannoc said. "That's the whole of the divine office, girl. Not thunderbolts, not miracles. Custody. The Listener holds the world's promises the way a bank holds gold—because a promise only has value if someone guarantees it will be honored. That's what prayer is, at the bottom of it. A deposit. You swear something to the god, and the god holds it, and the holding is what makes it real."

He let the silence sit for a moment. Tallow smoke curled toward the ceiling.

"Now," he said, "consider the Sigil."

Ovesa grimaced. "I'd rather not. It gives me a headache."

"The Sigil is not a god. It's a *creditor*." Brannoc leaned forward. "It eats oaths. Devours them. Not metaphorically—when it takes a vow, the vow is gone. The debt is transferred. And here is the part that took me nineteen years of priesthood and one very bad night to understand: when the Sigil consumes a sworn promise, it doesn't just take the promise. It takes the *prayers attached to it*. Because a prayer is just an oath with the terms left blank. *I promise to be faithful. I promise to remember. I promise to come home.* Every one of those is a thread running to the Listener's hand. And the Sigil has been cutting threads."

Isandro had gone very still.

"Five years ago," Brannoc said gently, "the prayers stopped being answered. Not because the god abandoned the world. Because the god's hands were full. Because something was swallowing everything it was given. The silence isn't absence, Isandro. It's a mouth."

* * *

Wren spoke first, and Brannoc was coming to expect that—she was the one who couldn't abide a silence with a shape in it.

"That's not possible," she said, but her voice wobbled, and her hand had drifted to the satchel of maps at her hip. "The volume alone. Every vow in the world—there are millions sworn every day. Weddings, contracts, oaths of office. No power could consume that."

"It doesn't have to consume them all at once," Brannoc said. "It's patient. It's been patient for a long time. Think about what a vow is worth, Wren. Think about what you could *build* with that much credit. Every promise in a kingdom is a foundation stone. Swallow enough of them and you don't just have power—you have *substance*. You can wear a country like a coat."

"Like a crown," Isandro said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife.

"You've seen it," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question. "In the capital. You touched something you shouldn't have been able to touch."

"I appraised the crown jewels," Isandro said. Her flat ledger-voice had developed a crack down the middle. "The royal sigil. I put my hand on it because that's the job, and for about three seconds I was somewhere else. Someone else's memories. A man planting an orchard. A girl learning to whistle. And underneath it, something *reading* those memories. Not cruelly. The way a tailor reads a body. Taking measurements." She looked down at last at her oath-mark. "It's wearing the king."

"And the Guild sent you to do it," Brannoc said. "The Vellum Guild, keepers of impossible things, sent their best auditor to put her hand on the Sigil's front door. Ask yourself why."

"I've been asking myself nothing else for five days."

"Then let me save you the trouble." Brannoc rose and crossed to the one holy niche, the cracked bowl of river water. "The old orders didn't all die of neglect. Some of them were bought. Some of them were eaten so slowly they mistook digestion for promotion. I don't know what the Vellum Guild is to the Sigil—tool, servant, or next meal—but I know this: the Sigil does not leave loose threads. And you, Isandro Galloway, are the loosest thread in Cael Saren."

Ovesa had stopped pretending to sort her crate. "You're saying the Guild will come for her."

"I'm saying the Guild may already have come for her, and she got out by the width of a night." He turned back to the circle. "But that's tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem is this."

He knelt by the bowl.

"This water is dedicated to the Listener. Old rite, old words, no power in it that I know of—the god's been silent five years, same as for everyone. But a shrine is a shrine. A dedicated vessel is a dedicated vessel. And if what I believe is true—if the god isn't gone but *blocked*—then there may be a hairline crack in the wall. A place where something might get through."

"What are you proposing?" Isandro asked.

"I'm proposing you try to pray."

The word hung there. Brannoc watched her face do several things in quick succession—disbelief, anger, and beneath both, a rawness so naked it hurt to look at. He had seen that face before. In the mirror, two years ago, the night he'd stopped being a priest and started being cargo.

"I've tried," she said. "For five years I've tried. Every night. You don't think I—"

"You've prayed the way you write entries in a ledger," Brannoc said. "Carefully. Formally. *O Listener, hear your servant.* Structured prayer. Sworn prayer. Prayer with terms and conditions." He held up a hand before she could object. "I'm not mocking you. That's how we were all taught. But if the Sigil is eating structured vows—if it's swallowing everything with a contract's shape—then every formal prayer you've sent in five years may have been intercepted. You haven't been talking to a silent god, Isandro. You've been writing letters to a house with a thief in it."

The color left her face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"So what do I do?" she asked. "Pray *badly*?"

"Pray *freely*." Brannoc gestured at the bowl. "No form. No request. No vow. Just—talk. The way you'd talk to someone you trust and don't need anything from. If there's a crack in the wall, it won't be in the formal channels. It'll be in the mess. The unstructured. The thing the Sigil can't digest because it isn't currency at all."

* * *

They gave her the room, more or less. Ovesa took the opportunity to argue with the fishmonger upstairs about the quality of his eel, loudly, which Brannoc suspected was deliberate—a wall of noise to cover whatever happened next. Wren stayed. She sat on her crate in the corner with her maps forgotten in her lap, and she watched Isandro the way she probably watched a road that had started to move: with total attention and no intention of intervening.

Brannoc knelt across the bowl from Isandro, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

"Tell me about her," he said. "The god. Before the silence."

Isandro's jaw worked. "Why does that matter?"

"Because you can't talk freely to a stranger, and right now that's all the Listener is to you. Five years of silence turns even a god into a stranger. So remind yourself who you're talking to."

A long pause. The tallow flame guttered.

"She found me when I was fourteen," Isandro said at last, and her voice had lost the ledger-flatness entirely. It was younger. "I was—nobody. A debt-collector's daughter in a river town, good with numbers, good with a knife, good at nothing that mattered. The other orders wanted warriors and saints. The Quiet One wanted a bookkeeper. She came to me in a dream and showed me a ledger with every promise anyone had ever made to me in it, and she said, *someone has to keep the accounts honest.*" A rough almost-laugh. "She recruited me with accounting. I've never told anyone that."

"Sounds like her," Brannoc said. "The Listener always hired the ones who counted things. Because counting is a kind of caring. You only count what you don't want to lose."

Isandro's breath caught. She looked at the bowl of water, at the small cracked thing that had once been a holy vessel and was now just a bowl in a cellar that smelled of eel.

"She'd say something like that," Isandro whispered. "She'd say it exactly like that, and then she'd make me write it down."

"Then write it down now," Brannoc said. "Not in a ledger. Just—say it. To her. Whatever it is. No terms. No oath. No *I promise*. Just the truth, freely given, with nothing owed on either side."

Isandro closed her eyes.

Brannoc watched her struggle with it—watched the trained forms rise in her like scar tissue, the formal invocations, the careful guild-cadence, and watched her push them down one by one. Her lips moved. At first he caught only fragments.

"*I kept the accounts,*" she murmured. "All five years. I know you couldn't—I know something's wrong, I felt it, I felt it the night the stars went out, the mark went *cold*, and I thought—I thought you'd died, I thought I'd failed some test, I thought—"

Her voice broke. She steadied it.

"I'm not asking for anything. I need you to hear that. I'm not asking. I just—wanted you to know I didn't stop. Whatever took your voice, whatever's between us now—I didn't stop counting. I never stopped."

The water in the bowl shivered.

Brannoc's heart slammed against his ribs. He had performed ten thousand rites in nineteen years and the water had never once moved—not for him, not for any of it, not since the silence fell. The tallow flame bent sideways though there was no draft. The cellar's damp cold deepened, sharpened, became something with *attention* in it.

"Isandro," he breathed. "Isandro, don't stop—"

"I'm not—" Her eyes were still closed, her face wet. "I'm not asking for anything. I just wanted—"

The water stilled.

And Isandro Galloway went rigid, every muscle locking at once, her head tipping back like a puppet whose string had been plucked. Brannoc lunged forward but Wren was faster—she was already off her crate, one hand hovering near Isandro's shoulder without touching, her face white as her paper.

The oath-mark on Isandro's hand had changed. The coiled sigil that had been cold and dead for five years was now traced in lines of faint silver light, and the light was moving, flowing, like something written by an invisible hand at terrible speed.

Her lips parted.

One word came out. Not in her voice. In a voice like the space between two thoughts—vast, and quiet, and so tired it made Brannoc's eyes burn to hear it.

"*Run.*"

Then it was gone. The light guttered out. The water was just water. Isandro slumped forward and Wren caught her, both arms around her, lowering her gently to the cellar floor as Brannoc grabbed the bowl to keep it from overturning.

For a moment nobody spoke. Upstairs, Ovesa was still loudly disputing eel.

"Did you hear it?" Wren whispered. "Brannoc. Did you hear what she—"

"I heard."

"Was that—was that *her*? The god?"

Brannoc knelt beside Isandro's slack face. She was breathing. Her eyes moved beneath her lids, fast, dreaming or traveling. The oath-mark on her hand was dark again—but the skin around it, he noticed, had gone pale as paper, as if something had been drawn out of her along with the word.

"That was a god with one hand free," he said quietly. "Reaching through a crack in the wall. Spending everything it could scrape together to send a single word." He looked up at Wren, and found that his own hands were shaking. "Do you understand what that cost? Do you understand what it means, that the first thing it said—after five years, after everything, with one word to spend—was *run*?"

Wren's arms tightened around Isandro. "It means the wall is coming down."

"It means the wall was never a wall. It's a mouth." Brannoc rose, and the old priesthood rose with him, nineteen years of buried doctrine standing up straight in his spine for the first time since the Synod took his sash. "And it means the Sigil knows she reached through. The moment that word crossed, something on the other side felt it. The Sigil felt its meal move."

He was already moving toward the stairs, toward Ovesa, toward the loud argument that had been covering them, when Isandro's hand shot out and caught his wrist.

Her eyes were open. She was sitting up, Wren still holding her, and her face was not the face of a woman who had fainted. It was the face of a woman who had been given orders.

"It's not what you think," she said. Her voice was hoarse, scraped. "The word. Everyone's going to think it means run *from* the Sigil. Hide. Get off the road."

"Isn't it?" Brannoc asked.

"No." She pushed herself to her feet, unsteady, and looked at the bowl of water with an expression Brannoc had last seen on the face of a young woman in a dream, being handed a ledger and told to keep the accounts honest. "The Listener doesn't tell me to hide. She's never once told me to hide. Five years of silence, and the first word she spends everything to say—" Isandro's jaw set, and grief and resolve braided together in her face like two strands of one rope. "It's not *run away*. It's *run toward*. There's a direction. There's always a direction. She's telling me where to go."

"Where?" Wren asked.

Isandro looked at Brannoc. "You said the old orders kept shrines. Real ones, before they were bought. Where's the oldest one you know? The one place the old faith started—the first altar, the first dedicated ground. Where did the Listener first put her hand on this world?"

Brannoc felt the answer rise in him like cold water, and with it, unbidden, the memory of the last sermon he ever preached, the one that got him defrocked—the one where he'd stood in front of his congregation and said *the silence is not absence, something is eating our prayers, and we must find out what.*

"Drowned Vesper," he said. "The temple in the fens. The first shrine. The place where, if the old stories are true, the Listener first listened." He met her eyes. "It's eight days' hard travel through country the Guild watches. And if the Sigil felt you reach through tonight, then every road between here and there is about to get very, very interested in us."

"Then we'd better not take the roads," Isandro said.

From the top of the stairs, Ovesa's voice came down, all the bluster gone out of it: "Somebody want to tell me why the fishmonger's eel jars are all rattling?"
