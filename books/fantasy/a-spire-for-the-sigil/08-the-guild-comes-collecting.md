## 8. The Guild Comes Collecting

The river at Marrow Ford ran the color of tarnished silver, and Isandro had learned in five days of travel that Wren's maps did not so much describe it as negotiate with it.

"Crossing's good until moonrise," Wren said, crouched at the bank with her charcoal stub moving across paper that seemed to know what she wanted before she did. "After that the ford shifts east. The river's been walking for three hundred years and it doesn't like being told where to stand."

"Neither do I," said Ovesa, who was already elbow-deep in the wagon's undercarriage, greasing an axle with tallow she'd sworn was candle fat and refused to discuss further. "Get the mules harnessed. Brannoc, stop praying at the water and help me."

"I'm not praying." Brannoc straightened up from where he'd been laying flat stones into the mud, his defrocked hands careful even at this smallest act of devotion. "I'm apologizing."

"To the river?"

"To whoever's listening. Habit." He smiled, but it didn't reach far. Since the shrine, since the word *run* had come through Isandro's broken connection like a coin dropped down a well, he had taken to small rituals of contrition, as if he could apologize the god back into speaking.

Isandro checked the wagon's cargo one last time by feel: salted pork, smuggled ink, a crate of bottled weather Ovesa had acquired from sources she described only as *creative*. And beneath the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, her own ledgers—the pages that proved the Vellum Guild had been feeding oaths to something wearing a king. Five days on the Salt Road and the weight of them hadn't lightened. Paper was supposed to be lighter than guilt. Nobody had told her guild paper.

Doss came back from the treeline with his bow unstrung and slung casual across his back, the way he carried everything, as if the world were a joke he'd heard first. He was nineteen, all elbows and freckles, the youngest of Ovesa's crew, and he had appointed himself Isandro's shadow within an hour of meeting her because, he said, anyone hunted by their own family must be worth knowing.

"No fires on the far bank?" he asked. "It's going to be cold."

"One fire, low, behind the rocks," Ovesa said. "You're not a beacon, you're a traveler."

"I'm a very warm beacon."

"Doss."

"Warm. Traveler. Got it."

Isandro watched him jog back to help harness the mules and felt something in her chest she hadn't felt since Corvane had burned her file instead of her: the dangerous, irrational warmth of belonging to people who were not contractually obligated to keep her. She knew what it cost. She was beginning to suspect she'd pay it anyway.

They crossed at dusk.

The ford was knee-deep and lied about it—one step the water took her to mid-thigh, cold enough to make her gasp—and the mules complained in the language of mules, which is complaint without end or object. Wren went first, staff probing, reading the riverbed like text. Brannoc hauled the cart. Ovesa swore with the fluency of a woman raised in three ports. Doss splashed everyone.

And Isandro, halfway across, felt the oath-mark on her wrist go cold.

Not burning now. Cold. Like a coin left overnight in snow, like a door opening onto winter. She stopped in the current, water shoving at her legs, and looked downstream at the dark.

"What is it?" Wren was beside her instantly, one hand at her elbow.

"The mark." Isandro turned her wrist. Even in the failing light, the sigil-shaped scar of her oath stood out pale against her skin—not red, not hot, but white with cold, rimed faintly, impossibly, with frost. "Something's coming. Something that carries my guild's seal."

Ovesa had gone still in the shallows. Thirty years of smuggling lived in the way she tilted her head, listening past the river's voice. Then Isandro saw it too—or heard it: the particular quality of silence downstream, the absence of night birds, the way the reeds had stopped their talking.

"Lanterns off," Ovesa breathed. "Off, off—all of you. Get to the far bank and get into the trees. Now. Quietly."

They moved. It was the longest hundred paces of Isandro's life, hauling the cart through the shallows while every nerve screamed at her to run, run, run. They got the mules up the bank, got the wagon into the alder scrub, got themselves into the black lattice of trunks—and then the lanterns came around the bend downstream.

Six of them. No—eight. Carried low, spaced like soldiers, each flame hooded green. Guild work-lights. Isandro knew them the way you know your own handwriting. Behind them, wading the ford without hurry, came figures in gray traveling cloaks, faces masked with vellum—actual vellum, stretched and painted blank, featureless as unwritten pages.

Enforcers. Not the conclave's clerks, not auditors. Collectors.

"Oh, hells," Ovesa whispered. "That's a lot of parchment faces."

"They're here for me." Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "If I give myself up—"

"If you finish that sentence I'm throwing you in the river." Ovesa's whisper had edges. "Look at them. That's not an arrest party. Arrest parties bring rope and paperwork. That—" she nodded at the long, narrow cases two of the enforcers carried on their backs, the shape of them unmistakable to anyone who'd grown up around weapons, "—is a collection notice."

Wren's hand found Isandro's in the dark. Cold fingers. Steady, though. "How many?"

"Eight visible. There'll be a reserve." Isandro closed her eyes and let herself think in ledger-lines, because panic was just arithmetic done badly. "Collectors work in quires. Eight sheets to the quire, three quires to the gathering. If they're doctrinally correct there are twenty-four, and the rest are holding the road north and south."

"And if they're not doctrinally correct?"

"Then they're eight, and we're lucky, and I've never once been lucky."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh or a prayer. "Twenty-four against six."

"Five," said Ovesa. "I don't count myself against my own crew." She was already moving, low along the ground, and they followed because following Ovesa was simply what bodies did near her, the way water followed slope. "Doss, trees, high, your bow. You're not fighting—you're eyes. Call positions like market prices, loud when you need us to move, quiet otherwise. Brannoc, Wren—wagon, under the false bottom, stay flat no matter what you hear. If it goes wrong, you drive east and you don't stop for anything with a face."

"And you?" Isandro asked.

Ovesa grinned, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing in the dark. "I'm going to do some accounting."

* * *

What Ovesa did was this: she walked down to the ford, alone, carrying a lantern and a bottle of plum brandy, and hailed the enforcers like a merchant hailing customers.

"Evening, gentlefolk! River crossing's treacherous after dark—careful on the middle stones, she'll take a leg if she takes a mind." Her voice carried clear across the water, warm and stupid and harmless. "Any of you fine folk drink? I've got a bottle that would peel paint but tastes like heaven's own orchard going down."

The lead enforcer stopped in the middle of the ford. The blank vellum mask caught the lantern light and gave nothing back. When it spoke, the voice came out flat and doubled, as if two throats shared one mouth.

"Cargo manifest. Names. Purpose of passage."

"Well, that's a shame about the brandy." Ovesa set the lantern on a rock, uncorked the bottle, took a swig that would have felled a lesser woman. "Manifest's in the wagon. Names are mine to keep. Purpose is profit, same as yours, I expect."

"We serve the Ledger Unwritten."

Isandro's blood went cold again. She knew that phrase. Every guildschild knew it as doctrine's deepest shelf—the founding mystery, the thing apprentices were told they'd understand when they were ready. *The Ledger Unwritten.* She'd assumed it meant the god. The silent god, keeping accounts no mortal could read.

Standing in the dark watching blank-faced collectors wade toward her friends, she understood it differently. A ledger unwritten was a ledger waiting to be written. A page with room on it. An appetite.

The lead enforcer took another step. Then another. And its head turned—not toward Ovesa, not toward the wagon, but toward the treeline. Toward Isandro.

"It knows the mark," Isandro breathed. "Corvane burned my file but the mark itself is a record. It's written into me. They can read me from across a river."

"Then stop standing where they can read you," Ovesa called back cheerfully—and threw the bottle.

It arced over the lead enforcer's head and shattered on the stones behind the second rank, and the brandy, which Ovesa had absolutely laced with something from her coat pocket, went up in a sheet of blue-white flame that turned the ford into a line of burning water. Mule-tallow and saltpeter and gods-knew-what; Ovesa's improvisations were the reason half the harbormasters on the coast had gray hair.

"NOW!" she roared, in a voice entirely unlike a merchant's.

The night came apart.

Doss's arrows came out of the trees like punctuation—two enforcers down before they'd finished turning, shafts through the vellum masks, through whatever was beneath. The others moved fast, faster than men should, spreading into a crescent with the mechanical precision of a sentence closing around a name. Two broke for the wagon. Brannoc rose out of the brush to meet them with a quarterstaff and ten years of frustrated liturgy in his arms, and the sound of wood on vellum-masked bone was like a drum in church.

Isandro ran the other way—toward the flank, toward the two enforcers circling wide to cut the road east—and as she ran she did the thing she'd spent her whole life doing, the thing that had made the Guild value her before it wanted her dead.

She opened a ledger.

Not a book. Herself. Every collector carried a warrant-sheet somewhere on their body—a strip of vellum inscribed with the authority of the conclave, the legal engine that made their violence lawful. Without the warrant, they were just masked strangers with swords, and more importantly, their orders were void, and enforcers bound by writ were bound *literally*, in the old way, the way Isandro's oath was bound. Break the writ, break the will.

She hit the first one from the side, both of them sprawling in the mud, and her fingers found the case at its spine even as its elbow found her ribs. Pain, white and clarifying. The case split. The warrant spilled out—a ribbon of script glowing faintly, hungry, *live*—and she did the only thing you could do to a living document you couldn't fight.

She amended it.

Her knife traced one clean stroke through the word *collect*, and in the space above it, bleeding, shaking, she wrote in the old shorthand every guild archivist knew: *returned. account settled.* The writ flickered like a snuffed lamp. Beneath her, the enforcer stopped moving—not dead, just empty, its purpose cancelled, its vellum mask sagging over a face that was, horribly, ordinary. A clerk's face. Someone's son.

The second enforcer's blade took her across the shoulder before she could rise.

She rolled, tasted mud and copper, heard Wren screaming her name from the wagon—and then Doss was there. Out of the trees, out of position, out of every order Ovesa had given, because Isandro was down and Doss had never in his life stayed where he was put when someone he loved was bleeding.

His arrows took the second enforcer twice. But there were more coming out of the dark—there *had* been twenty-four, Isandro had been right, she was always right about numbers and it never once saved anyone—and three of them converged on the boy with the bow like ink closing over a word.

He killed one. He wounded another. He was laughing—he was actually laughing, some wild young battle-joy or terror wearing laughter's clothes—and he turned to check on Isandro, to grin at her, to say something she would spend the rest of her life trying to remember.

The third enforcer's sword went through him below the ribs and came out his back.

* * *

Afterward, Isandro could never sequence it properly. Memory gave it to her in shards, the way glass gives itself up.

Ovesa arriving like weather, like a landslide given opinions, her hooked blade taking the third enforcer's arm and then everything else. Wren dragging Isandro upright by the collar, shouting words Isandro understood later to be *stay down, stay down, you're hit*. Brannoc standing over the wagon with his staff broken in half and both halves still swinging. Fire on the water. Green lanterns gutted. Vellum masks floating in the shallows like shed skins, and beneath them, everywhere beneath them, just—people. Tired people. Bound people.

And Doss in the mud at the edge of the trees, with Isandro's coat folded under his head because she'd gotten there first and it was the only thing she had to give him.

"There were twenty-four," he said. His voice was small and amazed. "You said twenty-four. You were right."

"You weren't supposed to count," Isandro said. Pressure on her shoulder, someone's hands, Wren's hands, sewing or burning or both. "You were supposed to be eyes."

"Yeah." He tried to smile. Blood at the corner of his mouth. "Bad trade, though, innit. Eyes for—" He coughed. Looked up at the starless sky, which pressed down close and black as a lid. "Can't see any stars anyway. What's the point of eyes."

"Don't," Ovesa said. She was kneeling on his other side, holding his hand, and her voice was steady in the way of things about to break. "Don't you dare do the dying-wit routine, you horrible boy, you're nineteen, you're—"

"Captain." His grip tightened on hers. "The brandy was terrible. I never told you. Worst thing I ever drank. I drank it every time." A breath that rattled. "Every time you let me."

Ovesa made a sound Isandro had never heard her make and hoped never to catalog again.

"I'll log it," Doss whispered. His eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere kinder. "In the ledger. One bottle. Terrible. Worth it." And then he wasn't counting anymore.

* * *

They buried him at dawn on the far bank, because the river had taken enough. Brannoc spoke the rites from memory—half-defrocked, half-invented, wholly sincere—and Ovesa drove the marker-stake herself: a length of wagon axle, because Doss had always said he'd grow into the family business eventually.

No one slept. Wren sat with Isandro while Brannoc stitched the shoulder wound proper, and neither of them spoke, because there was a kind of silence that was also a conversation, and they had begun, without deciding to, to share it.

Near noon, Isandro took out her ledgers.

Her hands shook. She turned to the last clean page, and above the entry she wrote the date, and beneath the date she wrote, in her precise, merciless, beautiful archival hand:

*Doss of the crew Ovesa. Age nineteen. Carried eyes for us and gave more than eyes. Debt incurred: one bottle of plum brandy, terrible, drunk gladly. Debt owed: everything. Status of account: paid in full by the deceased. Entered by Isandro Galloway, who will carry the balance.*

She showed it to Ovesa. The captain read it twice, jaw working, then pressed her thumb below the ink hard enough to bruise the page.

"That's how it's done, then," Ovesa said roughly. "That's how you keep them."

"That's how I keep him," Isandro said.

They broke camp an hour later, quieter, leaner, the wagon one soul lighter and all of them heavier for it. As they took the eastern road, Wren fell into step beside Isandro and, without looking at her, slid something across: a scrap of her map-paper, freshly drawn.

On it was the road ahead—and beyond the road, far to the south, a stain in the ink shaped like a drowned bell, with a single line of Wren's cramped annotation beneath:

*Where the god went silent. Three weeks' travel. The stains are thickening—we're running out of map before we run out of world.*

Isandro looked from the paper to the starless sky, then to the four people walking beside her who had become, somewhere between a river crossing and a grave, the only ledger she cared to balance.

"Three weeks," she said, and shouldered her pack. "Then we go find out who's been listening."
