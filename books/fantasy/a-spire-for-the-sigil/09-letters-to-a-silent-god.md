## 9. Letters to a Silent God

They made camp in the hollow of an old road, where the hills folded together like hands cupped around a coal. Ovesa called it a pocket—the cartographer's word, borrowed and bent—and it was true that the world seemed to have forgotten this place. The Salt Road ran somewhere above them, but down here there was only dry grass gone silver with frost, a ring of stones Brannoc had laid out with a defrocked priest's fussy precision, and Wren's small fire burning low and orange, throwing shadows up the walls of earth.

Isandro sat with her back against her saddle and the ledger across her knees, and for a long time she did not open it.

The others were settling into their night-shapes: Ovesa arguing cheerfully with the river about tomorrow's crossing, as if the water might negotiate; Brannoc mending a strap with thread he blessed under his breath out of habit, though his god was three years behind him; Tam hunched over a scrap of parchment, still writing letters to a court that would have him flayed if they arrived. And Wren—Wren lay on her stomach at the fire's edge, chin on her fists, unrolling one of her impossible maps by firelight, tracing lines that Isandro now knew were not roads but wounds.

Five days since the river crossing. Five days since Pell had died holding the line with a smuggler's cutlass and a guildsman's arithmetic, buying them thirty seconds that had cost everything. The crew did not talk about it. They simply moved differently now, the way water moves differently after it has been through a gorge. Ovesa laughed just as loud, but checked on each of them more often. Brannoc said the burial rites without stumbling once. And Wren had started sitting closer to Isandro at the fires—not touching, never quite touching, but near enough that Isandro could feel the warmth of her like a second, smaller hearth.

Isandro opened the ledger to the back pages.

The official entries ended where the ambush began. After that came only blank leaves, and then—the reason she had carried this book like a wound for five years—a sheaf of loose papers, folded and refolded so many times their creases had gone soft as cloth. Letters. Forty-one of them. She knew the number because she counted them the way other people count scars.

She had written the first one the night after the silence began. Her god—the Quiet Ledger, some called it; hers had never needed a name, only attention—had stopped answering on the eve of the harvest festival, mid-prayer, mid-sentence, the way a conversation dies when someone walks out of a room. She had prayed again. And again. And on the third night, furious and hollowed out, she had done the only thing her training left her: she had picked up a pen.

*You went silent,* she had written. *I am noting it in the record.*

That was all the first letter was. Two lines. But the letters had kept coming, because grief is a ledger too—it wants entries—and for five years she had written to a god that did not answer, folding each letter away unsent, an account receivable from a debtor who had vanished.

She hadn't read them aloud to anyone in her life. She wasn't sure she was going to now. Her thumb moved along their edges, feeling the paper whisper.

"Those aren't ledger entries," Wren said quietly, without looking up from her map.

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation. Wren had a way of seeing things sideways—noticing what a thing *was* by noticing what it wasn't. Isandro had watched her do it with roads, with rivers, with people.

"No," Isandro said.

"Ledger entries don't get folded that many times." Wren rolled the map closed with deliberate care, as if putting something away. "Things you mean to keep flat don't get soft."

Across the fire, Brannoc looked up from his mending. He didn't say anything either, but he set down the strap and the needle, which from Brannoc was the same as clearing a room. Even Ovesa's argument with the river trailed off. Tam's pen stopped scratching.

The fire popped. Isandro found, to her mild surprise, that she wanted to read them. That underneath five years of discipline and dry wit there was a simpler thing: she was tired of carrying them folded.

"The god I'm sworn to," she said, "went quiet five years ago. Not dead—I'd have felt it die, the way you feel a tooth come loose. Just... gone. Mid-sentence. I've written to it every season since." She lifted the sheaf. "These are the letters."

"You wrote forty letters to a god that never answered?" Ovesa said, and then winced, because even Ovesa could hear how that sounded, coming five days after they'd buried Pell.

"Forty-one," Isandro corrected. "I count everything. It's the oath."

"And you're going to read them," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question either. He said it the way he said blessings—gently, giving her permission she already owned. "Out loud. To us."

"To whoever's listening," Isandro said. "Which, historically, is no one."

* * *

She started with the first one because it was shortest, and because starting anywhere else would have been cowardice dressed as craft.

"*You went silent. I am noting it in the record.*"

She paused. The firelight moved on Wren's face, on the fine bones of it, the ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted. A cartographer's hands. A mapmaker who drew what wasn't there.

"I was very proud of that one," Isandro said. "Two lines. Very controlled. As if the whole thing were a filing error and someone upstairs would eventually fix it."

"What happened next?" Sefeya asked softly.

Sefeya. The princess had been with them nine days now, having walked out of a palace that was editing her own memories, carrying nothing but a lullaby and a stubbornness that Isandro privately suspected could crack stone. She sat close to Brannoc, wrapped in one of Ovesa's spare coats, listening the way children listen—with their whole bodies.

Isando unfolded the second letter. "*Third night of silence,*" she read. "*I have tried every rite I know. The candle takes the flame but nothing answers. Either you have died, or you have left, or you were never there and I have spent eleven years talking to my own reflection. I would like to know which. I am owed that much.*"

She heard herself, the young woman in those words, and winced. "*I am owed that much.* Even angry, I was still keeping accounts."

"That's not a sin," Brannoc said. "That's a prayer wearing armor."

"The next ones are worse. They get longer. You can watch me come apart in real time, season by season." She unfolded the seventh letter. This one was from winter, two years in. "*It snowed today. First real snow since you went quiet. I stood in the guild yard and let it land on my face and I thought: this is the kind of thing you used to make me notice. You taught me that attention is a form of love. Now the snow falls and nobody is attending to it but me, and I don't know if that makes me faithful or alone.*"

Her voice caught slightly on the last line. Not much. Enough.

Wren had gone very still.

Isandro read on. The tenth letter, written the night her mentor Corvane first suggested, gently, that maybe her god had been a metaphor all along. The fourteenth, written in a fever, half delirium, in which she accused her god of being a coward and then, in the postscript, took it back. The nineteenth, which consisted entirely of a list of small beautiful things she had seen that day—a child juggling stolen apples, frost making lace of a spiderweb, a drunk man weeping with joy outside a bakery—as though she could bait the silence back with evidence of wonder.

"*Come look,*" that one ended. "*There's still so much to see.*"

By now the crew had drawn in around the fire without anyone deciding to. Ovesa sat cross-legged with her arms wrapped around her knees, uncharacteristically silent. Tam had abandoned his letter to the court entirely. Even the river seemed quieter.

"The twenty-sixth is short," Isandro said. Her throat was tight. She read it anyway. "*I dreamed you answered. In the dream you didn't say anything—you never say anything—but I woke up certain, absolutely certain, that you had been listening the whole time. It lasted until noon. Then I remembered that certainty is just hope that's learned to lie.*"

Silence held the camp like a held breath.

Then Wren said, "Read the last one."

"Wren—"

"Read the last one. Please."

Isandro looked at her. Wren was sitting up now, hugging her knees, firelight catching the sharp line of her jaw, and there was something in her face that Isandro had learned to read the way Wren read terrain: a steepness, a warning of depth. Whatever she was asking for, it cost her.

Isandro unfolded the forty-first letter. The most recent. Written six nights ago, in the aftermath of blood and loss, by firelight not unlike this one.

"*I don't know if you're gone or listening or eating prayers in the dark somewhere,*" she read. "*But tonight a man named Pell died so that strangers could live, and I held his hand while it happened, and I want you to know that whatever you are, wherever you are—that was holy. Not the dying. The choosing. If you're listening: that's your answer about whether anything matters. We matter it into being, every day, without your help.*"

She stopped. The paper trembled slightly in her fingers.

"*And there is a woman,*" she read, and here her voice did fail her, briefly, and she steadied it the way she'd steady a shaking hand before signing something binding, "*there is a woman who draws maps of things that shouldn't exist, and when she looks at me I feel attended to. Not watched. Attended. Like the snow, like the frost-lace, like everything you taught me to notice. Maybe that's you. Maybe that's her. Maybe I finally understand that it doesn't have to be either.*"

She folded the letter along its soft old creases.

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, on the real road, wind moved through grass.

"That one I haven't sent either," Isandro said quietly. "Obviously."

* * *

For a long moment no one spoke. Then Ovesa cleared her throat loudly, announced that someone had to check the pickets and that the pickets were definitely not going to check themselves, and dragged a grumbling Brannoc off into the dark with her, with the unsubtle grace of a woman who could navigate any river but preferred navigating other people toward each other. Tam followed soon after, murmuring something about ink that needed stirring.

Which left the two of them and the fire.

Wren unrolled a fresh sheet of paper. She didn't look at Isandro while she did it. She pinned the corners with stones from the ring, smoothed it once, twice, and then sat looking at the blank surface the way other people look at deep water.

"My parents were mapmakers for the Crown," she said.

Isandro said nothing. She had learned that with Wren, silence was a door held open.

"They mapped what was ordered. Coastlines, borders, tax districts. My mother used to say a map is a promise the land makes to be found." Wren's finger traced the blank page without touching it. "When I was seventeen I started drawing other things. Places I'd never been. Roads that appeared overnight. Stains, I call them now—I didn't have the word then. I just knew the land hurt in certain places, and my hand knew where, and I couldn't stop drawing it."

"The Sigil," Isandro said.

"I didn't know its name. I just knew that when I showed my mother the maps, she burned them. When I showed the Guild of Cartographers, they burned *me*—professionally speaking. Struck my name. Exiled me for falsification." Wren smiled, and it was a terrible, brittle smile. "Falsification. I was drawing the truth ahead of schedule."

She pulled a folded paper from inside her coat—soft-creased, worn at the edges, folded as many times as Isandro's letters—and set it on the blanket between them.

"I wrote one letter too," Wren said. "Never sent it. Read it or don't."

Isandro took it. Unfolded it. The handwriting was precise and getting less so as it went, the script of someone losing a argument with themselves line by line.

"*To the Guild of Cartographers, and to my mother, who will read this or won't: You struck my name from the rolls, which is fitting, since names are what I draw. Here is what you should have asked me instead of what I saw: ask me why the roads move. Ask me why the stains grow. Ask me why every map I draw, no matter where I begin it, ends with a mark I cannot explain over a city whose name I keep forgetting. I was exiled for seeing clearly. Fine. I will see clearly alone. But someday the land will make good on what my maps promise, and you will wish you had asked the girl with the strange maps where the hurting places were, while she still loved you enough to answer.*"

"It's unsigned," Isandro said.

"I couldn't sign it. They'd taken the name anyway." Wren looked at the fire. "That's what exile is. Not leaving. Being erased in advance."

Isandro understood, then, with a clarity that felt almost physical, why Wren had sat so close to the fire all these nights. Why she mapped what wasn't there. Why she had asked to hear the last letter. Two women, carrying unsent words to people who had stopped—or perhaps never started—listening.

She reached out and touched the letter, lightly, where a signature should have been.

"You sign it now," Isandro said. "Not for them. For us. Everything in this camp gets entered properly. That's the rule."

Wren looked at her hand on the paper, then up at her face. Something moved through her expression—fear and relief braided so tightly they were indistinguishable.

"Wren," she said, and wrote it, small and clear, in the space she'd left. Then, after a moment, beneath it: "*who sees.*"

"There," Isandro said. Her voice was rougher than she intended. "Entered into the record."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the stars—or the place where stars should be—pressed down through the gap in the hills, Wren spoke again from the dark beside her.

"The stain over the capital," she said. "On my newest map. It's changed shape since we left the river."

Isandro turned her head. Wren was lying on her side, facing her, eyes open.

"Changed how?"

"It's not spreading anymore." Wren's voice was very quiet. "It's *converging*. All the little stains across the country—they're pulling inward, like water finding a drain. And there's a new mark, Isandro. Small. Bright. Right where—" She hesitated, and Isandro watched her decide to say it. "Right where your oath-mark sits. On every copy of every map I've drawn since the river. Even the ones I drew before."

The cold that moved through Isandro then had nothing to do with the frost.

"Before?"

"Before," Wren said. "Maps I drew when you were still a name in a guild ledger I'd never heard of." She reached out, and this time she did touch—fingers finding Isandro's wrist, turning it gently, finding the oath-mark beneath the sleeve: the old sigil of her vow, cold as riverbed stone. "Whatever you are, Isandro Galloway, you were always going to end up here. And I think—" her voice caught, steadied, "—I think the thing in the capital knows it too. Because tonight, for the first time, my maps drew something new at the center of the convergence."

"What?"

"A door," Wren said. "Opening."

Above them, through the cup of the hills, the starless sky seemed to lean closer, listening.
