## 10. The City That Forgot Its Name

Ovesa Kell had run the Salt Road for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things worth learning: never trust a cargo you can't smell, never trust a passenger who won't eat, and never trust a city that's too quiet on market day.

Vessamar was all three.

She saw it from the deck of the *Kindly Thief* while they were still an hour out, the city rising from the river delta like a jaw of yellow stone. Vessamar was supposed to be loud. It sat at the confluence of two trade rivers and a caravan road, and in Ovesa's experience it had always roared—fishmongers and spice-brokers and rope-sellers screaming each other hoarse from dawn to dark, gulls stealing bread off the scales, harbor pilots brawling over berths like it was a blood sport. She'd once watched a man sell the same barrel of pickled eels four times in a single afternoon through sheer volume of voice.

Today the harbor was silent. Not empty—silent. Boats moored in their slips. Cranes stood loaded with cargo nobody was moving. And the people, when she could make them out, walked the docks the way mourners walk a cemetery aisle: heads down, hands folded, mouths shut.

"Captain," Brannoc said beside her. The ex-priest had gone gray around the mouth, which for Brannoc meant his god-sense was itching. "You feel that?"

"I feel a harbor full of merchants not selling anything," Ovesa said. "Which is either a miracle or a plague."

"It's neither." He rubbed his arms like he was cold. "It's quiet. Inside-quiet. Like a room where somebody just stopped talking."

Wren came up from below decks with her maps rolled under one arm, hair escaping its braid in pale wisps. She didn't say anything at first. She just stood at the rail and looked at the city, and then she unrolled the newest chart across a crate and weighted its corners with belaying pins.

The stain had grown.

Ovesa had seen Wren's strange cartography often enough now to stop flinching at it—the way she inked not roads but pressures, not coastlines but concentrations, a geography of something that shouldn't have had a geography at all. The capital was a black bloom at the center of every sheet, spreading outward like ink dropped in milk. But Vessamar, which three weeks ago had been only a smudge, a faint gray shadow at the edge of the paper, was now a second bloom. Nearly as dark.

"That fast?" Ovesa asked.

"That fast." Wren traced the edge of the stain with one fingertip, not quite touching the ink. "Three weeks ago it was a bruise. Now look—" Her finger moved to the river channels feeding into the delta. "It's following the water. Or the trade. Everything that flows through here is carrying it downstream."

Isandro leaned against the mainmast, arms crossed, ledger tucked under her elbow like she slept with it, which as far as Ovesa could tell she actually did. "Or everything that passes through is carrying it," the mercenary said. "Ships. Caravans. Contracts. If the Sigil eats oaths, then a trade city is a banquet hall. Every bill of lading is a promise. Every debt is dinner."

Ovesa looked at her crew—at Brannoc with his cold-sense itching, at Wren with her haunted charts, at Isandro with her burned oath-mark hidden under her sleeve and her dead god riding silent on her shoulder—and felt the particular weight settle onto her chest that she'd learned to recognize as *this is going to cost me*.

"Well," she said, clapping her hands once, because a captain's job was to make terrible ideas sound like plans. "Good news: nobody in that harbor knows my face, my ship, or my crimes. Bad news: I owe a man in Vessamar forty crowns, which means I'm walking into a city full of creditors wearing a debt like a bell around my neck. So we go in quiet, we sell the silk fast, and we find out what's eating this place before it eats us. Questions?"

Nobody had questions. They never did. That was the trouble with hiring people smarter than you—they already knew the answers were bad.

* * *

They came ashore at the fish-quay, where the silence got teeth.

Ovesa had expected quiet. She hadn't expected *wrong*. The fish were there, silver heaps of them on the slabs, fresh-caught and gleaming—but no one was haggling. A woman stood behind her stall staring at her own hands. A boy sat on an upturned crate turning a coin over and over, and when Ovesa passed close enough she heard him whispering to himself, over and over, "I had a name. I had a name. I had a name."

She stopped. Old instinct told her to keep walking; newer instinct, the one that had grown since the stars went out, told her to ask.

"Hey. Kid." She crouched to his level, keeping her voice easy, dockside-casual. "What's your name?"

He looked up at her with eyes too old for his face. "I don't remember," he said. Not confused. Not frightened. Just stating a fact, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "I know I had one. My mother called me it every morning to get me up. I can almost hear her saying it. But when I reach for it, it's—" He made a gesture, fingers closing on smoke. "There's a hole shaped like me."

Ovesa stood up slowly. Down the quay, she caught Isandro watching her, and the mercenary's face had gone the color of old tallow.

They found more of it the deeper they went into the city. A chandler who introduced himself as "the chandler"—no name offered, and when Ovesa pressed, he blinked at her like she'd asked him to recite the color of Tuesday. A tavern whose signboard hung blank above the door, paint peeling away from bare wood as if the letters had simply been *unwritten*. Inside, the tavernkeeper poured ale without being asked and couldn't tell Ovesa how long he'd kept the tap, or what the tavern had been called before, or whether he'd ever had a wife, though a woman's portrait hung behind the bar and he looked at it the way you look at weather in another country.

"Documents too," Tam said.

The court scribe had joined them at Harrow Ford, and Ovesa still wasn't entirely sure how she felt about having a defector from the palace in her crew—a thin, precise man who apologized to furniture when he bumped it. But he was useful, and he was terrified in an educated way, which was better than being terrified in a stupid way. He'd spent the walk from the quay collecting scraps: a shipping manifest from a warehouse step, a tax notice nailed to a post, a child's copybook abandoned in a gutter.

He laid them out on the tavern table. Ovesa leaned over them and felt her stomach drop through the floorboards.

Blank. All of them. Not redacted, not damaged—blank. The tax notice still bore its official seal, its wax stamp, its border flourishes, but where the writing should have been there was nothing, just clean parchment, as if the words had been swallowed whole and left no bones. The copybook was worst. Rows of practice letters marched down the first pages, a child's careful hand learning its alphabet—and then, mid-page, the letters thinned, wavered, and stopped, and the rest of the book was empty paper.

"The names go first," Tam said quietly. "Then the records. Then—" He turned the copybook toward them, to the last page with writing on it. The final legible line was a practice sentence every schoolchild in Cael Saren learned: *My name is ——— and this is my hand.*

The dash where the child's name should have been was blank. But the sentence continued after it. *And this is my hand.* The hand remained. Only the name was gone.

"They're being eaten selectively," Isandro murmured. She was turning the tax notice over, holding it near the lamp, and Ovesa saw her lips move as she read the blankness the way you'd read a scar. "Not erased. Consumed. There's a difference. Erasure leaves damage. This leaves—" She hesitated.

"Appetite," Brannoc said.

Everyone looked at him. The ex-priest sat with his big scarred hands flat on the table, and for once he didn't look like a man missing his god. He looked like a man who understood the thing that had taken everyone else's.

"At the shrine at Marrow's Crossing," he said, "we learned that oaths are the oldest currency. Older than coin. When you swear something, you make a little knot in the world, and the world remembers it. The Sigil eats knots." He tapped the blank page. "A name is an oath your parents swore on your behalf. A record is an oath the city swore about itself. This thing isn't vandalizing Vessamar. It's *collecting*."

"And the people?" Ovesa asked. "The kid on the quay? The tavernkeep?"

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. "Still owed," he said finally. "That's what frightens me. The Sigil takes the promise and leaves the debtor standing there, feeling the shape of what's missing. It doesn't want them dead. Dead men can't miss anything."

Ovesa looked around the tavern. At the other tables, the evening crowd sat drinking in silence—not sullen, not hostile, just *hushed*, like the whole room was holding its breath between heartbeats. A dice game was underway in the corner, and nobody was shouting. Dice games without shouting. She'd sailed past the edge of the map, apparently.

"Right," she said, and heard her own voice come out harder than usual, which was her way of keeping it from coming out scared. "New plan. We sell the silk tomorrow at dawn, we take payment in goods, not script—I'm not letting some blank-eyed factor hand me a contract that'll be empty paper by noon—and we're gone by the next tide. We don't sleep here twice."

"You think it spreads that fast?" Wren asked.

"I think," Ovesa said, "that I've smuggled contraband through nine kingdoms, and I've never once seen a city where the *silence* was the cargo. Whatever's doing this, I'm not giving it a second night to learn my ship's name."

She regretted the words before they finished leaving her mouth. Across the table, Isandro's eyes flicked up and met hers, and neither of them said what they were both thinking: that the *Kindly Thief* was registered in three harbors, insured under two false companies, and bound by a dozen contracts of affiliation and debt—and that if names were food, then Ovesa Kell had just set out a feast and invited the thing to dinner.

* * *

They took rooms above the tavern because the alternative was sleeping on the ship in full view of a harbor that had stopped pretending to be a harbor, and Ovesa had learned long ago that visible fear drew sharks faster than blood did. She paid the tavernkeeper in coin. He counted it twice, carefully, like a man handling a language he used to speak.

She woke in the black hour before dawn to the sound of Wren crying.

Not loudly. Wren didn't do anything loudly. It was the small, strangled sound of someone trying very hard to be silent and losing, and it came through the wall from the next room, and Ovesa lay in the dark listening to it and feeling like a thief, which was ironic, because she was one by trade and it had never bothered her before.

She got up. Knocked softly. Wren's voice said "come in" in a wrecked whisper.

The cartographer sat on the floor amid her maps, lamp turned low, and she wasn't even trying to hide her face. Tears cut clean lines through the road-dust on her cheeks. In her lap lay a leather folio, worn soft at the corners, stuffed with papers—and Ovesa knew that folio. Everyone in the crew knew it. Wren guarded it like a relic and had never once let anyone see inside.

Now it was open, and Ovesa wished to every god living and silent that she hadn't knocked.

Letters. Dozens of them, in a fine, cramped, educated hand—the handwriting of someone trained for better things than smuggling. Letters addressed to a dozen different names in a dozen different cities. *Mother. Father. Master Ellery at the Cartographers' Hall. Dearest Sef.* And every single one of them was blank.

"Wren," Ovesa said carefully.

"They were real yesterday." Wren's voice came out flat and strange, the voice of someone reporting from very far away. "I checked them at Harrow Ford. I check them every few days, I always—I wrote them years ago, when I ran. I never sent them because sending them would've led the Hall straight to me, but I kept them because they were proof. Proof I had a mother. Proof I had a teacher who—" Her breath hitched. "I checked them at Harrow Ford, Captain. The ink was right there. And tonight I open the folio and it's *paper*. It's just paper. It reached into my bag. Into my *past*."

Ovesa crouched down across from her. Outside the window, Vessamar lay gray and breathless under a starless sky, and somewhere out in that silence the Sigil—or its appetite, or its shadow, whatever you called the thing when you didn't have the nerve to call it a god—was digesting a city one name at a time.

"Listen to me," Ovesa said. "I'm not good at this part. Brannoc's the one with the comforting voice. Isandro's the one with the tragic backstory and the meaningful pauses. Me, I'm good at running cargoes and lying to customs men. But here's what I know." She took one of the blank letters gently out of Wren's hands and held it up. "This is paper. You're not. Whatever it ate, it ate the *record*, not the woman. Your mother taught you to draw coastlines. Your teacher gave you those steady hands. That's not written anywhere. That's *you*. It can't eat you unless you let it do the paperwork."

Wren laughed—a wet, broken, genuine laugh—and scrubbed her face with her sleeve. "That was terrible, Captain."

"Aye. Told you. Comforting's not my cargo." She squeezed the cartographer's shoulder and stood. "But here's the part that's not terrible. Tomorrow we're gone from this city by noon, and then we're sailing straight at the thing that's doing this, because I've decided I'm done running ahead of it. I spent nineteen years staying ahead of every creditor, every guild, every king's man. And this—" she gestured at the blank letters, at the window, at the whole starless, suffocating night— "this is the first debt collector I've ever met that I can't outrun. So we stop running. We find out where it sleeps, and we burn its ledger."

From the doorway behind her, quietly, Isandro said: "Its ledger is the Archive of Vows. Under the capital."

Ovesa turned. The mercenary stood wrapped in her cloak, oath-mark arm hugged to her chest, and her eyes had the fixed shine of a woman who had done math all night and hated the total.

"You knew that already," Ovesa said. "How long?"

"Since Marrow's Crossing. Since Brannoc explained what the Sigil eats, I've suspected. Tonight confirmed it." Isandro stepped into the room and looked down at Wren's blank letters, and something moved behind her face that Ovesa had learned to read as grief being converted, alchemically, into purpose. "Every oath ever sworn in Cael Saren is stored beneath the throne. Every name, every contract, every vow—including mine, including yours, Captain, including the registration of your ship. If we want to take back what it's eaten—if we want to give these people back their names—we don't fight it in the streets. We break into the Archive."

Silence, except the river breathing against the pilings outside.

Ovesa looked at the blank letter in her hand. At the child on the quay saying *I had a name* like a prayer to a god who wasn't listening. At her crew—her ridiculous, damaged, precious crew—gathered in a lamplit room in a dying city, all of them looking at her, waiting, because gods help them, they'd decided she was the one who made the decisions.

She thought about the forty crowns she owed a man in Vessamar, and realized with a strange, floating calm that she might never pay them, and that for the first time in her life she didn't care.

"Right," Ovesa Kell said. "Somebody wake Brannoc. We sail with the morning tide—and we're going to rob the most dangerous vault in the world."

Outside the window, far off across the silent rooftops, a bell began to toll—and then stopped mid-stroke, as if it had forgotten why.
