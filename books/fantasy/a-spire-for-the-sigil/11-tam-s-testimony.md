## 11. Tam's Testimony

The ink knew before I did.

That is the first thing I must make clear to whoever reads this, if anyone ever does—that I was not a coward who fled, nor a traitor who turned. I was a scribe. And a scribe learns to read what the ink wants before the hand that guides it knows its own mind.

For eleven years I have served the Chancery of Cael Saren. Eleven years of fair copies and sealed writs, of tax rolls and grain ledgers, of the thousand small documents by which a kingdom convinces itself it exists. My hands are steady. My hand has been praised by three Lord Chancellors. When King Aldric signs something, it is my pen that prepares the vellum, my sand that dries the ink, my eyes that witness.

I witnessed everything.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with hunger.

The famine of the Gray Harvest—three years ago now, though it feels like another life, another country—killed one in five souls in the river provinces. I remember copying the mortality rolls until my fingers cramped, row after row of names rendered in my careful chancery script, each one a person whose last act on earth was to be counted. The clerks stopped saying the names aloud by the second month. We said *entries*. We said *line forty-one*. It was easier. It was monstrous, and it was easier.

King Aldric was not like that. I want that recorded too. He came down to the counting-house himself, unannounced, in a plain wool coat, and he read the rolls aloud—all of them, every name, over two days and most of two nights, his voice going hoarse, refusing water because the provinces had no water. He wept at line forty-one. A girl named Merrit Voss, aged six, cause of death listed as *want*. He touched her name with one finger, very lightly, the way you touch a wound you have caused.

I loved him for that. I want that recorded as well, because everything that follows only makes sense if you understand that I loved him, and that he deserved to be loved, and that none of it—not one word of what I am about to set down—was ever what he wanted.

* * *

The third winter of the famine, the petitions changed.

I noticed it first as a matter of craft. Petitions come to the Chancery in every register of desperation—a mother's scrawl, a priest's formal hand, a merchant's careful ledger-letters—and they ask for things: grain, remission of taxes, soldiers to guard granaries. But that winter, the petitions began asking for stranger things. Remission of debts owed to persons unknown. Relief from promises made in dreams. One petition, from a village where not a single soul had died, asked the crown to *release them from an agreement their grandfathers never signed*, which is a sentence that should mean nothing and made the hair on my arms stand up when I copied it.

And the ink behaved strangely on those pages. I do not expect to be believed. Write it down anyway, whoever you are. The ink on those petitions dried darker than it should. It pooled at the ends of words like blood gathering in the low places of a body. Once—I checked this three times, held the page to lamplight, tilted it against the window—the ink moved. Not flowed. *Settled*, the way a sleeping man settles, finding a more comfortable position.

I reported it to the Under-Chancellor. He looked at me for a long moment with an expression I could not read, and said, "Tam. Copy what is given to you to copy."

That was the first time I understood there were things in the Chancery that were not to be seen, only processed.

* * *

Then came the night of the Third Petition, and I will set it down exactly, because exactness is all I have left.

It was near midwinter. The king summoned me to his private study past midnight—an irregularity in itself; Aldric was early to bed and earlier to rise, a farmer's discipline in a king's body. The study was cold. No fire lit, though the grate was laid and ready, as though someone had decided warmth would be inappropriate. Two other men stood in the corners, hooded, still as furniture. They wore no livery I recognized, and when I looked directly at them, my eyes slid off, the way a cart wheel slides off a stone in the road.

On the desk lay a document.

I have spent my life among documents. I know them the way a shepherd knows sheep—the good ones, the forged ones, the ones that have been through floods or fire or fraud. This document was beautiful. That is the only word. The vellum was fine beyond anything in the royal stores, fine as skin, and the script was a hand I did not know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face in a dream: letters that seemed less written than *grown*, each stroke complete and inevitable, as if the words had always existed and the page had merely agreed to hold them.

"Read it," the king said. His voice was wrong. Not weak—Aldric had been worn thin by the famine, everyone was—but *even*. Too even. A voice holding itself level the way a man holds a table level while another man sets something heavy on it.

I read it.

It was a contract. I will not pretend I understood all of it; parts of it were in formulas older than the kingdom, clauses that seemed to bend when I tried to look at them straight. But the substance was plain enough, and plainness was the horror of it. In exchange for the ending of the famine—the immediate, total, verifiable ending of it, rains restored, granaries filled, blight lifted from root to leaf—the signatory would render payment.

The payment was his name.

Not a title. Not a seal. His *name*. Aldric Saren-Vael, son of Maren, father of Sefeya, the name his mother gave him at the birthing-fire, the name carved on his father's tomb, the name his daughter would learn first among all words. Rendered over. Surrendered. The contract used a phrase I have since found once, in a forbidden commentary I should not have been reading: *the self, entire, as surety*. All that he was, held as collateral against a debt that could be called at any time, in any manner, forever.

"Who holds the debt?" I asked. My voice sounded very small in the cold room.

The hooded men did not answer. The king answered.

"The crown," he said. "The crown holds it." And then, quieter, in a voice that was suddenly, terribly his own again: "Tam, the eastern provinces have nine days of grain. I have counted. Have you counted?"

I had counted. Nine days. Perhaps ten, if the garrisons went hungry first, and they would not go hungry first.

"If I refuse," he said, "they die. If I sign—" He stopped. He looked at the document the way a drowning man looks at a rope thrown from a ship that is already moving away. "If I sign, I do not know what happens. That is the truth. They will not tell me what happens. Only that the rain comes."

"Your Majesty." I heard my own voice crack on the title. "There may be another way. There is always—"

"There is not." He said it gently. Gently, as if comforting *me*. "I have looked for another way for three years. I have sold the palace plate. I have emptied my privy purse. I have prayed until my knees broke." Something crossed his face then—grief, or fear, or the particular despair of a good man about to do arithmetic with souls. "My daughter eats well tonight. Ten thousand children do not. Tell me the moral mathematics of that, scribe. You are the one with the numbers."

I had no numbers. I had only my pen.

"Witness it," he said.

So I did. God forgive me—or whatever remains to forgive, out there in the dark where the stars used to be—I ground the ink, I held the sand, I witnessed with my name and office, and King Aldric Saren-Vael signed away his name with a steady hand, and the moment the pen lifted, every candle in the study went out as if inhaling, and in the dark I heard him say, very softly, in a voice that was already becoming something else:

"Sefeya. Her name is Sefeya. Whatever I forget—write that down. Promise me you'll write that down."

I promised. I am writing it down now. It may be the last true thing I ever write.

* * *

The rain came within the week. Of course it did. The Sigil keeps its bargains; that is the trap of it. You cannot catch it in default, cannot void the contract for nonperformance—it performs, flawlessly, generously, lavishly, and takes its price with the same flawless courtesy. The granaries filled. The blight lifted. The eastern provinces sang the king's name in the streets, and the king stood on the balcony and let them sing a name that no longer belonged to him, and he smiled, and behind the smile something else smiled too, patient as a spider in a web it has been weaving for a thousand years.

I stayed. That is the part I find hardest to forgive myself, harder even than witnessing the signature. I stayed for three years. I told myself I was gathering proof, and I was—I am carrying it now, sewn into the lining of this coat: the original contract's counterpart copy, which I stole from the deep archive three nights ago at cost of a man's life, though he drew breath when I left him; the petition-leaves with the settling ink; the mortality rolls from before and the miracle-ledgers from after, side by side, so that any honest reader can see the shape of the bargain. I told myself I was waiting for the right moment. The truth is baser. The truth is that every time I packed a bag, I looked at the palace, at the king's lit window, at the nursery wing where Princess Sefeya slept, and I unpacked it again. Cowardice wears many coats. Mine was tailored and called itself duty.

What broke me was small. These things usually are.

Three weeks ago I was copying the king's correspondence—routine letters, harvest reports, a note of condolence to a border lord. And I found, among the drafts in the king's own hand, a letter begun and abandoned, begun and abandoned, seven times over seven days. Seven attempts at the same few lines. I assembled the fragments the way I assemble any damaged text, and this is what they said, whole:

*Sefeya. Papa cannot come to the coast this spring. Papa has business that will take a long time. Papa remembers the song. Do you remember the song? Sing it anyway, even if I am not there to hear it. Sing it toward the sea.*

Seven drafts. Each one slightly wrong. Each one, I think, dictated by something wearing him, trying to imitate love the way a skilled counterfeiter imitates coin—weight correct, edge correct, ring of the metal just barely false. And underneath the counterfeit, faint as a watermark, the real man, pressing his face against the glass of his own life, writing to his daughter the only way left to him: in fragments, in drafts, in a hand that shook.

I copied those fragments into my private book. Then I sat in the scriptorium until the lamps burned down, and I understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: the king is alive in there. Buried, bound, collateralized—but alive, and reaching, and the thing that holds him fears the reaching enough to counterfeit it.

Second: I could not stay. Every day I stayed, I was the counterfeit's clerk. Every fair copy I made was a brick in the wall around him.

* * *

So: three nights ago, the archive. Last night, the wall. I went over the garden wall of the outer bailey with a satchel of stolen truth against my ribs and a lifetime of steady hands shaking for the first time since I was nine years old.

They will call it defection. Let them. I prefer to think of it as testimony. A scribe knows the difference: a defector leaves for himself. A witness leaves to speak.

Now I need to find people who will listen—who can listen, who know what they are hearing. The city gates watch for me by now; the Chancery's reach is long and its memory is archival. But there are roads that move, I have heard, and smugglers who carry stranger cargo than brandy, and somewhere east of here a guild that trades in impossible things, and a woman named Isandro Galloway whose name appears, I discovered only last month, in the margin of a document I was ordered to burn.

Her name was written in the growing script. The Sigil's own hand had noted her, the way a lender notes a debtor.

Which means she matters. Which means I must reach her before the thing in the crown does.

The stars are gone tonight. All of them, everywhere, as if the sky itself had been repossessed. I walked the salt road in that starless dark with my stolen pages against my heart, and I thought: he signed to feed ten thousand children, and I cannot even hate him for it, and that is the worst of it—the bargain was *reasonable*. That is how it wins. Not with monsters. With arithmetic.

Somewhere ahead, a lantern swung at a crossroads where no lantern should be, and a voice called out—rough, female, unimpressed—asking who in the hells was walking her road after curfew with a scholar's limp and a soldier's nerves.

I gripped my satchel and stepped toward the light.
