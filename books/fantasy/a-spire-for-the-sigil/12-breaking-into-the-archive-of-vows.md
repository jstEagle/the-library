## 12. Breaking Into the Archive of Vows

The capital of Cael Saren had always smelled of ink and candle tallow. Now it smelled of nothing at all.

Isandro noticed it first at the gate, where the customs officer asked for her papers and she watched his lips move and understood that his words were arriving a half-beat late, like a letter delivered after its own news had gone stale. The city was still functioning. Markets opened. Bells rang the hour. But everything ran with the loose, drifting quality of a machine whose operator had stepped away, and Isandro knew—with the cold certainty of someone who kept ledgers for a living—that an operator *had* stepped away. Or something had stepped in.

"You're staring," Wren murmured beside her, hood up, satchel of maps pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "People stare in cities all the time."

"People stare at fruit stalls," Isandro said. "I'm staring at a city that's forgotten how to want anything."

They passed beneath the aqueduct arch into the Lamplighter's Quarter, where Ovesa had arranged—through three smugglers' contacts and one bribe paid in bottled fog, an old guild stock Isandro still carried from her audit days—a room above a dye-house that smelled of mordant and secrets. Brannoc was already there when they arrived, sitting very still on the edge of the cot, hands flat on his knees, wearing the expression of a man who had spent the afternoon listening to something terrible.

"Well?" Ovesa demanded. She'd come in through the window because doors were for people with legitimate business, and she was eating someone's dried plums without apology. "You've had your face like a wet funeral since noon. Say it."

"The Archive is awake," Brannoc said.

The room went quiet enough that Isandro could hear the dye-house vats churning below, slow and patient as digestion.

"Define awake," she said.

"I mean it's *listening*." Brannoc looked up at her, and she saw that his hands, flat on his knees, were trembling very slightly—the hands of a man who had once been a priest and had never entirely stopped hearing things. "I went to the shrine district like we planned. Sat in the ruin of Saint Vessa's. And I could feel it, Isandro. Under the whole city. Every oath anyone ever swore down there—it's not sleeping. It's not even storing anymore. It's being *read*. All of them, all at once, like a man running his finger down a ledger line by line by line." He swallowed. "It's looking for something."

"Or someone," Isandro said quietly.

Wren had gone pale in the way she did—not dramatically, just the blood draining out of her freckles until she looked drawn in charcoal. She unrolled her map across the dye-stained table without being asked, and there it was: the capital rendered in her impossible cartography, the streets drawn true but overlaid with the stain-marks only she could chart, the Sigil's influence spreading through the city like ink through wet paper. At the center, beneath the palace hill, the stain was no longer a stain. It was a wound. A black circle so dense it had begun to bleed outward in fine dark threads, and every thread terminated—if you followed them far enough, across provinces, across seas—at small marks scattered through the world like punctuation.

One of those marks sat over Isandro's own wrist. Wren had drawn it there herself, weeks ago, her pen hesitating before it touched the paper.

"It knows I'm here," Isandro said.

"It knows *something's* here," Wren corrected. "There's a difference, and right now that difference is the width of a knife blade, and we are walking it tonight regardless." She looked up, and under all the fear in her face there was that stubborn bedrock Isandro had come to rely on more than she'd ever admit aloud. "The contract's down there. The original. If Aldric ever consents to be unbound, it has to happen against the actual document—we've been through this. There's no other copy. Tam was clear."

Tam. Somewhere in the palace above them, presumably, a court scribe was committing treason in careful increments, and Isandro found herself thinking about him with something close to affection. Men who defected with paperwork instead of daggers were her favorite kind of people.

"Then let's talk about the door," Isandro said, and pulled her guild credentials from her coat. "Because I'm going to walk through the front one."

* * *

The Archive of Vows had been built, according to the histories Isandro had audited twice during her apprenticeship, by a king who wanted to die honestly. Which king depended on which historian you paid. What all the accounts agreed on was the architecture: a vault cut into the limestone beneath the palace hill, so deep that the stair descending to it numbered nine hundred steps, each step carved with the name of an oathkeeper who had died in service. You literally walked down over the dead to reach the living record.

What the histories did *not* mention—and what Isandro learned only when Corvane, in one of their last conversations before everything broke, had drunk too much plum wine and told her—was the mechanism.

"Every oath has weight," Corvane had said, staring into his cup. "You know that. You're bound yourself; you've felt it. Now imagine a building designed to hold all of them. Every promise ever sworn in Cael Saren, written onto vellum cured in oath-tallow, sealed with the swearer's own intention. The documents aren't records, girl. They're the oaths themselves. Burn a contract down there and the promise dies in the world. That's why they built it so deep. That's why they built it so well." He'd laughed then, ugly and short. "And that's why the Guild keeps its best appraisers on friendly terms with the under-wardens. Everything down there eventually needs authenticating."

So it was that at the eleventh bell, Isandro Galloway stood at the Archive's surface entrance wearing her formal guild robes—which she had kept, folded, through two hundred miles of flight, because she had known, with the same ledger-keeper's instinct that had kept her alive this long, that she would need them—and presented her credentials to a warden whose eyes had gone the particular flat gray of a man being read rather than reading.

"Appraiser Galloway," she said. "Vellum Guild, third chair. The conclave requires verification of the high contracts. Sealed writ." She handed over the document Tam had forged, which bore a conclave seal so good that Isandro herself had examined it for ten minutes trying to find the flaw and failed.

The warden held the seal up to his lamp. For a long moment, nothing in his face moved at all, and Isandro felt the hair rise along her arms, because behind those flat gray eyes something vast and patient was deciding whether she was worth the trouble of noticing.

Then the warden blinked, and he was merely a tired bureaucrat again. "Third chair," he said. "Haven't seen a third chair down here in years. Mind the ninth landing. The steps have been... settling."

Behind her, in the shadows of the colonnade, she heard Ovesa exhale through her teeth.

* * *

The plan, such as Ovesa had built it, was elegant in the way most of her plans were elegant: briefly, violently, and only from a distance.

Ovesa and Brannoc took the maintenance shafts—the old air-channels cut for the oath-tallow vats, which vented heat from the deep levels and which Ovesa had learned about from a smuggler who had once moved contraband prayer-scrolls through them. Their job was fire. Not to burn anything—gods, never that—but to be ready to burn a specific thing if Isandro and Wren found it and needed a distraction catastrophic enough to cover an escape. Wren came down the front stairs with Isandro, carrying her maps rolled in an appraisal case, playing the part of a junior authenticator with a bad cough and worse posture. It was, everyone agreed afterward, typecasting.

The stair swallowed them.

Nine hundred steps. Isandro counted them the way she counted everything, because counting was how she kept the world from dissolving. The walls sweated limestone water. The carved names underfoot grew older with every descent—modern names first, crisp-cut, and then letters softening into medieval roundness, and then names in scripts so old that the language itself had died, worn nearly smooth by centuries of feet. By the four-hundredth step, Isandro realized she was walking on promises made by people whose gods predated her own, and that some of those oaths were still, somehow, holding. She could feel it through her boots. A faint hum. Like standing on a bridge over deep water.

"Tell me something true," Wren said suddenly, somewhere behind her. Her voice echoed strangely, flattened by stone. "Anything. I need a voice that isn't mine in my head right now."

Isandro considered the dark ahead of her. "When I was nineteen," she said, "I audited a shipment of borrowed years. Three decades of them, decanted into glass, sold by a dying woman to a young couple who wanted time together. My job was confirming the vintage." She took another step. "I held one year up to the lamp. Just one. It looked like light through honey, and inside it I swear I could see shapes—ordinary days, meals, arguments, someone laughing. And I thought: this is what a year actually is. Not a number. *This.* And I signed the authentication, and the couple got their year, and I have thought about that glass every day since, because—" she stopped. Started again. "Because it's the closest thing to holy work I've ever done, and my god was already silent by then, and I didn't tell anyone that it felt like an answer anyway."

The echo died away. Then Wren's hand found hers in the dark—briefly, firmly, saying everything neither of them had words for yet—and let go.

"Four hundred sixty-one," Wren said. "Keep counting, ledger-keeper."

* * *

The bottom of the stair opened into a hall that stole the breath clean out of Isandro's chest.

She had prepared herself for shelves. Rows, stacks, the dusty grandeur of any great library. What she had not prepared for was the fact that the Archive was *alive*.

The contracts hung in the air.

Not all of them—the oldest, the deepest strata—but tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands, suspended in the vast cathedral-dark of the central vault in slow orbit around a core of light that was not light, exactly. Each document turned gently on its own axis, trailing faint threads of gold and shadow that connected it to others: marriage contracts braided together in shining cables; trade agreements clustered in glittering shoals; a single oath of vengeance hanging apart from everything else, wrapped in its own red gloom, still burning after two hundred years. The sound of the place was worst of all. A murmur. Millions of voices whispering simultaneously, not words but the *texture* of words, the feeling of people everywhere in history meaning what they said with their whole chests.

"Oh," Wren breathed. Her hand found Isandro's sleeve and gripped it hard. "Oh, it's beautiful. Why is it beautiful? It shouldn't be beautiful, it should be *horrible*, and it's both, I can't—" 

"I know," Isandro said. Her voice came out rough. Because it was beautiful, and that was the trap of it. This was what human faithfulness looked like when you gathered all of it in one place and let it breathe. And coiled at the center of it, drinking it, was the Sigil.

She could see it now that she knew to look. The core-light wasn't a core at all. It was a mouth. The golden threads flowed inward, all of them, an ocean of promises draining slowly toward a point of perfect darkness, and the darkness was patient, and the darkness was *reading*.

"Don't look at it directly," Isandro murmured. "Brannoc said—"

"I remember. Feel it looking back." Wren tore her gaze away with visible effort. "The contract. Where?"

Isandro had spent three days with Tam's stolen floor-plans memorizing the answer. "Royal contracts don't circulate. They're sequestered. There's a annex off the east gallery—the Crown Vault—sealed with the king's own intention, which means—" she grimaced, "—which now means it's sealed with *its* intention. But seals answer to precedence. An older, stronger claim can open them. Guild law, chapter one: the oldest oath outranks the newest."

"And your oath is five years silent, so it's old, but is it *older than the crown*?" Wren asked.

"No idea," Isandro said. "But I know someone whose oath predates the dynasty." She touched the scarred skin over her wrist, where her god's mark lay cold and unreadable. "*You* do. If you'll lend it to me."

The mark did not answer. It never answered. But as she walked toward the east gallery, Isandro felt—for the first time in five years—something that was not silence. A pressure. An attention. Like a door held almost open.

* * *

The Crown Vault was a black iron door in a wall of black iron doors, and every other door in the gallery was merely locked, while this one was *watched*. Isandro felt its attention the moment they rounded the corner—an ancient, procedural awareness, older than the thing currently wearing the king, because it had been set by a man who wanted to die honestly, and honest intentions apparently outlasted everything.

She pressed her palm flat against the iron and spoke the only invocation her order had ever required. Not a spell. A statement of account.

"I am Isandro Galloway, bound by oath to the Listener, who heard every vow before this vault was dug. I claim precedence of witness. Open for audit."

Nothing happened for the space of ten heartbeats. Fifteen. Wren's grip on her satchel strap whitened. Somewhere far above, faint as memory, the coronation bells began to rehearse—

The door sighed open like a man surrendering.

Inside, on a plinth of black basalt, under glass older than the glassblower's guild, lay a single sheet of vellum no larger than a dinner menu. It should not have been enough. It was the size of a market receipt, and it held a kingdom's throat.

Isandro lifted the glass with hands that shook only slightly, and read.

The contract was written in Aldric's own hand—she recognized the script from Tam's descriptions, the strong downstrokes gone hurried near the end. It was short. That was the horror of it. Famine rules had been invoked, the old emergency clause, the one that let a sovereign stake anything to save his people. And at the bottom, where the terms ended, the signature was not a signature. It was his full name, written out entire—Aldric Saren Valemont, every syllable, given freely—and below it, in ink that was somehow darker than the rest, a second party had countersigned with a single symbol: the Sigil itself, drawn in one continuous line that hurt to follow with the eye.

"There," Wren whispered. "That's it. That's the original." Her fingers hovered over the page without touching. "If we can get him here—get him to put his hand on this and *choose*—"

The lights in the main vault changed.

It happened all at once, the way the stars had gone out: every golden thread in the great dark hall reversed its flow. The slow inward drift became a rush. The murmur rose to a roar, millions of voices crying out at once, and Isandro knew with absolute certainty that whatever was at the center had finished reading—and had found the page it was looking for.

The red-gloated oath of vengeance, alone in its corner, flared like a struck match.

No—not that one. Beside it. Another document, one Isandro had walked past without a glance, was rising now out of the general drift, turning toward her, and she saw the guild wax upon it, her own name in the header, her own file—

Her file. Her *burned* file. The one Corvane had fed to the flames rather than to the conclave's enforcers. Except fire, Isandro realized, ice flooding down her spine, had never been the point. In the Archive, nothing burned. Things were only ever *filed elsewhere*.

The document drifted toward the Crown Vault door, unhurried, patient, and on it she could see the conclave's judgment written in fresh ink: *APPRAISER GALLOWAY — TO BE COLLECTED.*

Behind her, from the direction of the maintenance vents, came the unmistakable sound of Ovesa swearing with tremendous creativity, followed by Brannoc shouting, *"They're coming down the stair—nine hundred of them—"*
