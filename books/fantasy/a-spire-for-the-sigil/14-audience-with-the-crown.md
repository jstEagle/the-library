## 14. Audience with the Crown

The petition cost me forty-one guild marks, a forged letter of introduction from a jewel-house in Vell Maris that no longer existed, and the last of my patience.

"Forty-one," Wren had said when I told her, in the rented room above the dyer's shop where we'd spent three days memorizing the palace's service corridors off Tam's stolen schedules. "Forty-one marks to stand in a room with the thing that's eating the world."

"To stand in a room with a man," I said. "That's the whole wager."

She hadn't liked it. She'd liked it even less when I explained that she couldn't come—that a cartographer with ink-stained fingers and a face the harbor watch had started sketching onto notices would undo everything before I got past the second gate. She'd argued, and Brannoc had argued on her side, and Ovesa had argued for a third option involving fire that I declined to entertain, and in the end it was Sefeya who settled it, the way children sometimes do without meaning to.

She'd looked up at me with her father's gray eyes and said, "If you see him, tell him the lullaby still works. Tell him I checked."

So now I stood in the antechamber of the Hall of Audiences in Cael Saren, dressed in the dove-gray of a junior appraiser, my guild seal heavy in my breast pocket, and I repeated Sefeya's words to myself like a rosary. *The lullaby still works. I checked.*

* * *

They made me wait two hours, which I understood was policy and also theater. Kings let petitioners marinate; it softened them, made them grateful for the crumb of attention when it finally came. The antechamber was beautiful in the way things are beautiful when money is trying very hard to compensate for something—gilded ribs overhead, a floor of pale marble quarried from mountains that no longer existed because the Sigil's expansion had eaten the passes, tapestries depicting the royal line back nine generations. I counted the faces out of boredom and then, out of habit, out of the ledger-keeper's sickness that lives in my hands, I began appraising them.

That was when I noticed.

The ninth-generation king, Aldric the Founder's grandfather, had been painted with a signet ring on his right hand. So had the eighth. And the seventh. Every monarch down the line wore the same ring—the royal sigil, the coiled mark of house Saren—and every portrait after the fourth generation back had been *touched*. Not restored. Touched. The paint around each ring sat a half-shade too fresh, the lines too clean, as if some careful hand had come through the centuries and repainted one small detail over and over, keeping it vivid while everything else faded.

Keeping the door oiled.

I was still staring at the fourth tapestry when the chamberlain's clerk appeared and said my name—or rather, said the name I'd written on the petition, *Mistress Ilva Corren, appraiser, Vell Maris consortium*—and led me through doors tall enough to admit a siege engine.

* * *

The throne room of Cael Saren had been built to make a human being feel like an insect, and it succeeded. Vaulted ceiling lost in shadow. Columns like the legs of drowned giants. At the far end, on a dais of black stone, the throne itself: a chair of ironwood and silver that had probably been comfortable once, before ceremony got its teeth into it.

King Aldric sat in it the way a coat sits on a hook.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had. He was not slumped. He was not vacant-eyed or slack-jawed; the stories smugglers told each other over cheap wine had him drooling and chained, and the stories were wrong. He sat upright. His hands rested on the arms of the throne with correct, deliberate grace. His face was composed, mildly attentive, wearing the expression of a man listening to a petition he has heard four hundred times.

But nothing behind it moved. That was the horror of it—not absence but *stillness*, the stillness of deep water, of a held breath, of a house where every clock has stopped at the same hour. He was a man-shaped room, and something lived in the room, and the something was looking at me through the windows of his eyes.

Guards flanked the dais. Courtiers lined the walls. A scribe—I recognized the build, the ink-stained cuffs, even at this distance: Tam, playing his role with his head bowed—waited at the recording desk to take down whatever nonsense I'd come to say.

"Mistress Corren." The voice came from the throne, and it was Aldric's voice, warm and measured, the voice of a man who had once talked a famine city into hope. It used his throat beautifully. That was somehow worse than a monster's rasp would have been. "You bring a request regarding the crown regalia."

"Your Majesty." I bowed to the correct depth for a guild journeyman—low enough to show respect, high enough to keep my eyes up, because I needed to see his face. "The Vell Maris consortium has been engaged to assess certain items of the royal treasury against outstanding debts of the estate of Lord Halven, deceased. Several pieces bear hallmarks consistent with items listed in the crown inventory of your grandfather's reign. I am authorized to request examination."

A lie braided out of true threads, which is the only kind of lie worth telling. There *had* been a Lord Halven. There *were* disputed pieces. The paperwork, which Wren had forged with a cartographer's fanatical precision, would survive any audit for exactly as long as I needed it to.

"You may approach," said the king.

I crossed the marble. My boots sounded very loud. Somewhere in the walls, faint as a pulse under bandages, I felt the wards humming—the same hum I'd felt the night the stars went out, the night my oath-mark burned cold, except here it wasn't background. Here it was *concentrated*, thick as tallow, and the closer I came to the throne the more I understood that the entire palace was built around the throne the way a wound builds around a splinter.

Ten feet away, the guards stopped me. Protocol. I knelt on the marked line and waited.

And the king leaned forward.

It was a small motion. A courteous one—a monarch inclining toward a petitioner so the poor creature needn't shout. But I saw the effort of it, saw something in the mechanics of the movement grind against something else, like a man pushing a door open against a strong wind, and for a moment—one moment—his eyes were not still water. They were *afraid*.

Then the composure slid back down like a visor, and he gestured, and the guards brought forward the tray.

* * *

They gave me gloves and tweezers and a jeweler's loupe, and for the better part of an hour I did genuine work, because the best cover is competence. I turned rings and brooches and a coronet of white gold under the light, called out hallmarks and assay dates, and Tam's pen scratched them into the record, and the court watched me with the bored hostility of people watching a tradesman touch their treasures.

The sigil-ring came last.

It sat on velvet at the center of the tray, and it was wrong in a way I could not immediately name. Gold, obviously old, worked in the shape of a coiled serpent devouring its own tail—but the coil was not closed. There was a gap in it, a deliberate incompleteness, and the gap was not empty. When I looked at it directly, my eye kept sliding off, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. The wards in the walls hummed louder. My oath-mark, scarred white and silent for five years, lay cold against my forearm beneath my sleeve, colder than it had ever been, cold as a coin left in snow.

"An unusual piece," I said, to fill the silence, to give myself permission to keep looking.

"It is the oldest thing we own," the king said. "Older than the crown. Older than the name." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Would you say it is valuable, Mistress Corren?"

Trap or test, I couldn't tell, and it didn't matter, because the truth was the safest answer. "No, Your Majesty. Its value cannot be assessed. Some things aren't currency. They're *collateral*."

The stillness in the room changed quality. The courtiers didn't move, but the air did—the wards' hum dropped half a tone, like a great animal shifting its weight. On the dais, the thing wearing Aldric studied me with fresh attention, and I felt its gaze the way you feel weather change: pressure behind the eyes, the taste of copper, the certainty of being *read*.

"You are an interesting appraiser," it said.

"I'm a thorough one, Your Majesty."

"Thoroughness is a form of hunger." The king smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, practiced and kind, deployed by something that had learned it from the outside, the way a crow learns a whistle. "Everything hungers. Even gods. Especially—" and here the voice paused, savored, "*especially* the ones who stop answering."

My blood went to ice water. It knew. Or it suspected. Or it simply enjoyed fishing, the way such things enjoy fishing, casting lines into every visitor to see what bit. I kept my hands steady on the tray. I kept my breathing at the pace of a tired professional. Inside, I recited ledger entries to myself—bottled storm, lot 114, provenance verified; borrowed year, lot 7, interest accrued—to give the hungry thing in the room nothing but arithmetic to chew.

"I've seen what happens to those," I said carefully. "In my trade. Things that stop answering usually haven't gone anywhere. They're just full."

Silence. Long enough that I heard Tam's pen stop scratching.

Then the king laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled, and for one vertiginous instant it was *his* laugh, the laugh of a man, and the man was looking out through his own eyes at me with an expression of naked, desperate recognition, like a drowning sailor sighting a lantern—

"—full," he echoed, and his hand, the right hand, twitched toward the empty gap in the serpent's coil, and his lips moved around words that weren't coming, that were being pressed back down, and I watched him fight for it, watched the muscles of his throat strain against something invisible, watched the guards shift and the courtiers frown and the wards shriek up a tone into hearing range—

"*Sefeya.*"

It came out as a whisper. Barely breath. Two syllables, cracked down the middle, forced through glass.

The name hit the room and the room *recoiled*. The wards screamed. Every candle guttered sideways though there was no draft. For a quarter of a heartbeat the king's face was a father's face—wet-eyed, wrecked, begging—and then the Sigil slammed down over him like a portcullis and the composure returned, seamless, and the throne room was bright and orderly and cold again, and everyone went on pretending nothing had happened because everyone in that room had learned what happened to people who didn't pretend.

"What was that?" the chamberlain demanded, rounding on me. "What did you say to His Majesty?"

"Nothing, my lord. I spoke of collateral."

"He is tired," the king said, mild as milk. "The audience is concluded. Mistress Corren—you will receive word regarding the estate matter." And beneath the words, beneath the voice, so low I might have imagined it, in the cadence of a man praying rather than a king dismissing: *again. come again. please.*

* * *

They escorted me out through the service corridors, past the kitchens, past the wardens' post where a bored sergeant checked my seal against a list, and I walked with the tray-less calm of a woman whose errand had concluded, and inside I was screaming.

Three seconds. Maybe less. Three seconds of a father surfacing through thirty feet of black water to gasp his daughter's name before the weight of the thing above him pressed him back under.

He'd heard her. Somehow, impossibly, across all that distance and all that dark, he had heard a child singing a lullaby in a smuggler's safehouse, and he had fought his way up toward the sound hard enough to crack the surface for three seconds. Which meant the hold was not perfect. Which meant the consent they needed—the willing consent, the impossible clause buried in the contract—was not sealed behind an unbreakable wall. It was sealed behind a door that a lullaby could rattle.

Tam caught me at the last gate, ostensibly to return my examination gloves. His fingers shook as he folded them into my hands, and tucked inside the linen was a scrap of paper with four words in a court scribe's neat hand:

*Solstice. Choir-line. West stair.*

I closed my fist around it and walked out into the gray afternoon, past the gates, past the ward-stones humming their fat satisfied hum, down streets where people hurried home beneath a starless sky they had already begun to treat as normal, and I did not stop walking until I reached the dyer's shop and the stairs and the room where they were waiting.

Wren saw my face first. She stood up so fast her chair went over.

"You found him," she said. "The man. You found him."

"For three seconds." My voice came out scraped. I sat down heavily on the edge of the bed, and Sefeya climbed up beside me without asking, the way children do, and put her small hand on my arm right over the oath-mark. "Three seconds. And he spent them on you."

"On—"

"He said your name, Sefeya. He fought the thing that owns him, in front of its own altar, in front of a hundred people, and the only thing he could get out was your name."

The girl went very still. Then she did something I wasn't prepared for: she nodded, once, businesslike, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like a soldier clearing rain. "Then he can hear us. If he can hear, he can answer. Brannoc said the contract needs him to *say yes*." She looked around the room, at each of us in turn, a nine-year-old general reviewing troops. "So we make him loud enough to say it."

Ovesa laughed—a short, sharp bark with no humor in it, only admiration. "Out of the mouths of babes. Right. Loud enough to say yes." She turned to the table, to Wren's maps with their spreading stains all converging on the palace, and her finger came down on the capital like a knife point. "Solstice is eleven days out. They mean to bind every oath in the country in one ceremony, and we just learned the linchpin can hear a lullaby through the walls." Her grin was the grin she wore going into storms. "Somebody find me a choir."

Brannoc was quiet through all of it, and when I glanced at him I saw why: he was looking at the scrap of paper I'd passed to Wren, at Tam's four words, and his lips were moving silently over them. *West stair.* He looked up at me, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he hated.

"The west stair goes down," he said. "Under the palace. Under the Archive." He swallowed. "Isandro—the choir-line isn't decoration. Whatever they're singing on the solstice, it runs through the Archive of Vows. Through the contract itself. Tam isn't just giving us a way in."

He set the paper down on the map, dead center, on the black stain that pooled beneath the throne.

"He's giving us the throat."
