## 15. The Princess Who Remembers

Sefeya had counted the stars once. She remembered that much.

She had been six, maybe seven, sitting on her father's shoulders in the observatory garden while he pointed up and named them in the old tongue — *Vessa, the Lantern; Corrin's Eye; the Three Sisters who quarreled across the sky* — and she had counted until she lost her place at forty-one and laughed so hard she nearly fell off his back, and he had caught her by the ankles without even looking, because fathers always know where their daughters are falling.

That was the memory she kept coming back to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Not because it was precious — though it was — but because it was wrong. It was wrong in a way no one else could see.

The palace said there had never been an observatory garden. The maps showed a granary on that ground, old as the walls themselves. The servants, when she asked, smiled with faces like closed doors and told her the princess must be tired, the princess should rest, the princess was dreaming again. Her governess had stopped correcting her weeks ago and started simply watching her instead, with a small furrow between the brows, like a scribe checking a sum that wouldn't come out right.

And the sky — the sky had no stars at all now, just a smooth black lid pressed down over the world, and everyone acted as though that were normal, as though the night had always been a ceiling instead of a window.

But Sefeya remembered counting to forty-one.

She sat cross-legged on her bed with the blankets pulled around her like a tent and did what she always did when the palace got too quiet: she took out the little book.

It wasn't a real book. It was a scrap of vellum folded four times, small enough to hide in the seam of her sleeve, and on it, in her careful, crooked hand, she had written down every memory the palace insisted she didn't have. The observatory garden. The smell of her father's cloak — cedar and ink and horse. The way he sang when he thought she was asleep. The name of her nurse before Nurse Maren, which had been Ilsette, except there was no record of any Ilsette anywhere, and Maren had been "with the family for years," years that didn't add up against anything Sefeya could remember.

The book had eleven entries. She read them every night like prayers, and every morning she checked them against the world, and every morning the world had shifted a little more underneath her, like sand under a tide.

Last week, entry four had been true and then it hadn't. Entry four said: *Father took me to the harbor and bought me candied ginger from a woman with a red scarf.* She had gone looking for the woman with the red scarf — not to prove anything, just because she wanted candied ginger — and the harbor stalls were all run by men, and none of them sold ginger, and when she described the scarf the harbor master looked at her with such gentle, terrible pity that she ran all the way back to the palace and cried into her pillow where no one could hear.

That was how it worked, she was learning. It didn't take things away all at once. It let you doubt yourself first. It made you do its work for it.

*You are dreaming,* the palace murmured, in its warm rooms and its smiling servants and its endless, seamless records. *You are a tired child. Sleep.*

She pressed her thumb hard against her wrist until it hurt, and she whispered the entries aloud, one through eleven, and she did not sleep.

* * *

The lullaby was the only thing the book couldn't hold.

She'd tried writing it down once — the words of it — and the words came out strange on the page, flat and foreign, like a letter written by someone pretending to be her father. Because it wasn't really about the words. It was about the sound of his voice doing something it never did in daylight: going soft, and low, and a little bit unsure, the way people sing when they're singing to someone they love more than they're afraid of being heard.

*Down where the river forgets its name,*
*the lantern sleeps but burns the same.*

He sang it with his hand heavy and warm on her hair, and his heart beating slow above her ear, and sometimes his voice caught on the second line — she could still hear exactly where it caught, a tiny hitch, like a step on a stair you've climbed ten thousand times — and she used to think that meant he was sad, and she used to think that was strange, because kings weren't supposed to be sad, and then she grew older and understood that the catching was the whole song. That was the part that was real.

*And if the dark comes drinking deep,*
*the lantern keeps what we will keep.*

She hummed it now, alone in her room, quiet enough that the listening walls couldn't carry it. That was another thing she'd learned: the palace listened. Not the servants — the palace itself. Doors opened too smoothly. Echoes came back a half-beat late. Once she had sung the lullaby at full voice by the window, missing her father so badly it felt like drowning, and within the hour three separate people had come to ask if she was well, and the next day her music tutor introduced a new lesson on "proper court songs," and the lullaby had begun to feel less like hers and more like contraband.

So she kept it small. Kept it inside. A coal cupped in two hands, carried through wind.

Because here was the thing Sefeya knew, the thing she would have told anyone who asked, if anyone had ever asked: the lullaby was proof. Not of the garden or the harbor or Ilsette — those could be argued away, those could be dreams. But the hitch in his voice, the catch on the second line — that was not a thing a child invents. That was a thing a child receives. Someone had sung it to her. Someone with a heartbeat she could still count in her memory, steady as a clock, and that someone had been her father, and her father had been *sad*, and sad kings who sing lullabies to their daughters are not the same man as the one who now stood at ceremonies with his face like a coin — bright on both sides, blank on both sides — and never, ever sang.

Whatever wore him had missed one thing. One small, soft, useless thing. A song for putting children to sleep.

Useless things, Sefeya was beginning to understand, were the hardest kind to edit.

* * *

They came for her on a Tuesday, which she noted with some satisfaction, because Tuesdays were the days the kitchens made honey-bread, and if the world was going to end it might as well smell good.

Not soldiers. Soldiers would have been simpler. These were three people in traveling clothes who arrived through the servant stairs with the ease of water finding cracks: a broad woman with a captain's swagger and a knife she kept touching like a worry-stone; a big grey-bearded man whose eyes had the particular gentleness of someone who had been broken carefully and put back with his own hands; and a young woman with ink-stained fingers and a leather tube slung across her back, who looked at Sefeya the way nobody had looked at her in months — like she was a person, and a person worth looking at.

"Your Highness," the captain said, and sketched a bow that was mostly mockery and entirely sincere underneath. "Ovesa Marrow, late of no fleet in particular. This is Brannoc, formerly holy, currently useful, and Wren, who draws better than she talks."

"I talk fine," said Wren.

"You draw better than anyone talks." Ovesa crouched to Sefeya's level, which surprised her; adults almost never did that unless they wanted something small. "We're here because your father is in trouble. Real trouble. The kind that doesn't get fixed from inside this house, because the house itself is part of it."

Sefeya looked at the door. Looked back. "If I scream, you have maybe forty seconds."

"Forty seconds," Ovesa said approvingly. "See, I like her already. But you're not going to scream. You know why?"

"Because you'll kill me?"

"Gods, no. Because you've been waiting for us." The captain's grin faded into something steadier. "Don't tell me you haven't. A girl who hides notes in her sleeve seam — yes, we know, your friend Tam has a big mouth and a bigger conscience — a girl like that has been waiting a long time for somebody to walk through the wrong door and say the true thing out loud."

Sefeya's heart was going very fast. She made herself stand very still, the way you do when a wild animal has finally come near enough to touch. "What true thing?"

"That the king isn't the king anymore," Brannoc said quietly. "That something old is wearing him. And that there may be a way to get him back."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, faintly, the smell of honey-bread.

"And you need me," Sefeya said slowly, working it through, because she was her father's daughter and she had learned arithmetic from ledgers, "for something only I can do. Something the thing wearing him doesn't know about."

Wren stepped forward then, and unslung the tube, and drew out a map — but not a map of roads or rivers. It was a map of stains, grey blooms spreading across the country like mold through bread, all of them reaching toward the capital, toward the palace, toward a single mark drawn at the center of the throne room. And beside the map, held out in her ink-stained hand, a page of notes covered in a cramped, urgent script.

"We think," Wren said, "that there are things it can't change. Things it can't digest. We think memories given freely — love with nothing owed for it — sit outside its reach. And we think—"

"The lullaby," Sefeya said.

They blinked at her.

"He sings it when he thinks I'm asleep." Her voice cracked, and she let it; she was done hiding cracks, cracks were all she had left. "There's a place where his voice catches. On the second line. Whatever is wearing my father can copy his face and his seal and his signature and his stupid ceremonial voice, but it can't copy the place where his voice catches, because that isn't a thing he does. It's a thing he *is*. And I'm the only one left alive who knows where the catch goes."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Brannoc, the former holy man, knelt down — all the way down, knees on stone, which was either madness or reverence and Sefeya suspected it was both — and said, "Then you're not cargo, Highness. You're the key."

"I know," Sefeya said. "I've known I was the key since the harbor woman disappeared. What I want to know is whether any of you can fight."

Ovesa laughed, sharp and delighted. "Oh, we're going to get along famously."

* * *

They left through the wine cellar, because of course they did; Sefeya had read enough smuggler stories to know that everything important happened in wine cellars. Tam met them at the last turning with a forged travel writ and eyes red-rimmed from crying or not sleeping, probably both, and he pressed a bundle into her hands — her own hidden book, retrieved from her room, plus food, plus a small knife with a plain wooden handle.

"For cutting rope," Tam said. "Only rope. Promise me."

"Only rope," Sefeya promised, and meant it, mostly.

The tunnel smelled of damp stone and old barrels, and it was dark, and it was narrow, and it was the freest Sefeya had felt in a year. She walked between Brannoc's solid warmth and Wren's quick breathing, and behind them the palace settled back into its seamless silence, and ahead of them the dark went on and on, and she found herself humming — barely, barely — the first line of the lullaby.

*Down where the river forgets its name.*

Brannoc's hand came to rest, light as a bird, on top of her head. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Some songs you carry for someone, and some songs someone carries for you, and both kinds keep the lantern lit.

At the tunnel's mouth, night air hit her face — real night air, cold and honest — and Sefeya stopped and looked up, out of habit, at the place where stars should have been.

Empty. Smooth. A lid.

"One year ago," she said, to all of them, to no one, "I counted forty-one stars from my father's shoulders. I'm going to count them again. All of them. With him standing there. That's not hope, that's a plan."

Ovesa snorted. Wren smiled in the dark. And far off across the fields, toward the drowned places at the edge of the map where the stains ran deepest, something answered — a single bell tolling under the earth, once, like a lantern remembering how to burn.

Brannoc went very still.

"That," he said softly, "was not supposed to be possible."

Sefeya tightened her grip on the little book in her sleeve, twelve entries now — she'd added the twelfth on the stairs, in the dark, in her crooked hand: *I am leaving, and I am not dreaming* — and followed the others down the hill, toward the bell, toward the water, toward whatever came next.
