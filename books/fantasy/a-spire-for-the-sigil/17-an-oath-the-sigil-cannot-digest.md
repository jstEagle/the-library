## 17. An Oath the Sigil Cannot Digest

The drowned temple did not let us leave easily. Its threshold clung to our boots like river mud, like a hand not yet finished holding. Even now, a full day's climb above the sunken valley, I could still feel the water's cold in my bones—not the honest cold of rain or winter, but the held-breath cold of a place where something enormous was waiting without moving.

We made camp in the ribs of an old watchtower on the ridge, its stones furred with moss, its broken parapet framing the starless sky like a black bowl turned upside down over the world. The lid, Wren had called it once. She was right. Each night it seemed a finger-width lower.

Brannoc built the fire small. He always did now, as if brightness were a currency we could no longer afford to spend.

"Say it plainly," he said, settling across from me. "You've been composing something since the temple. You get that line between your brows, the one you make at ledgers when the numbers won't confess."

I fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch. "An oath the Sigil cannot digest," I said. "That's what the inscription said. That's what the god needs—someone to offer a vow that won't be swallowed. And I've been turning it over every way I know how, and I keep arriving at the same ugly conclusion."

"Which is?"

"Every oath I know how to swear is poisoned." I spread my hands, the ledger-keeper's hands, ink-stained even after days in the wild. "Think about it. A vow of protection—the Sigil eats that, because protection is a debt, and debts are its bread. An oath of vengeance? Feasts for a century. An oath sworn for reward, sworn out of fear, sworn to bind someone else—all of it, all of it is currency, and the Sigil is a mint. It doesn't just eat promises. It *is* the economy of them."

Brannoc nodded slowly. The firelight moved in the hollows of his face. He had been a priest long enough ago that his faith had callused over rather than healed, but he still had the habit of listening as if the silence itself might answer through him.

"The old texts said it plainer," he murmured. "*A gift given wholly returns to the giver.* I thought it was about alms."

"It's about everything." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it did when Corvane used to make me read contract terms aloud to clients who didn't want to hear them. "To free the god—to give the Sigil something it cannot swallow—we have to swear an oath with no condition attached. No reward asked. No escape clause. Nothing owed back. Not even survival." I looked up at him. "Do you understand what that means? It means walking into the throne room carrying something the most hungry thing in the world genuinely does not want. It has to be worthless to it. Which means it has to be priceless to us."

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. Somewhere below us, wind combed through the drowned valley and made the flooded temple's bell toll once—a sound like a struck tooth.

"There are few such vows left in the world," he said finally. "I swore one once, when I took orders. Or I thought I did. Looking back—" His mouth twisted. "Looking back, I wanted the certainty more than the service. That's why the Sigil's hunger found so much of me to chew on. That's why it hurts, what we saw down there."

In the temple's last dry chamber, before the water, we had seen it: the god, or the shape where a god should be, folded inside the Sigil's amber grasp like a moth kept by a collector. Not dead. Swallowed whole, held as collateral against the world's promises. Brannoc had put his hand to the stone and wept without sound, and none of us had pretended not to notice, because pretending was past us now.

"And you?" Brannoc asked. "What do you carry that the Sigil wouldn't want?"

I almost answered honestly. My mouth was already shaping it—*nothing, I have nothing left that isn't owed*—when Wren came up from the lower landing with her maps rolled under her arm and frost in her dark hair, and the sentence died somewhere behind my teeth.

Brannoc saw. He was an infuriatingly perceptive man. He said nothing, only rose, pressed my shoulder once, and went to spell Ovesa on watch, leaving the fire and the night and Wren to arrange themselves around me.

* *

Wren spread her charts on the flat capstone beside the fire, weighting the corners with stones, and I came to kneel across from her because not coming would have required an explanation, and explanations were the one thing I had run out of.

The stain-map had grown worse. Her cartography was unlike anyone else's—roads drawn in moving ink, coastlines annotated with tides that hadn't happened yet—but the stains were the part I'd learned to dread: washes of grey-violet spreading outward from the capital like mold through bread. Tonight there were new ones. They crept along the trade roads. They pooled in the city that had forgotten its name. And they converged, all of them, every branching vein of influence, toward two points marked in her precise, unhesitating hand.

The capital.

And the back of my left hand.

"You've stopped marking mine," I said. "The stain on the oath-mark. Three nights ago it stopped changing."

"I didn't stop." Wren didn't look up from her brush. "It stopped changing. There's a difference." She dipped the brush, hesitated, set it down. "Stains grow when the Sigil is pulling. Yours has been pulled as far as it pulls. Now it's just—" she searched for the word, tapping the chart twice— "*anchored.* Like the map decided where you belong and is waiting for you to agree."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a cartographer, not a comfort." But she smiled when she said it, small and sideways, and the firelight caught the scar through her eyebrow, the one she'd never explained and I'd never asked about, because some of what we didn't say to each other had become its own kind of language.

We worked in the easy quiet of people who have shared too many watches to need words. She inked; I audited. That was the joke between us—I had taken to auditing her maps the way I audited ledger entries, checking her distances against Brannoc's paces, her landmarks against Tam's documents, finding errors that were never errors but always thanked her for anyway. It gave my hands something to do that wasn't shaking.

The solstice was nine days off. We all knew it; Ovesa had scratched a tally onto the watchtower wall, and each morning we woke to one fewer mark. Nine days until the coronation-rite, when the Sigil intended to bind every oath in Cael Saren at once—every marriage vow, every soldier's enlistment, every guild contract, every whispered promise between lovers—swallowed in a single gulp beneath a sky already pressing down like a closing hand. After that, unbinding Aldric would be like unringing a bell the size of the world.

Nine days. And the only weapon we had was a vow the Sigil couldn't digest, which meant a vow with nothing in it for the swearer, which meant—

"Wren," I said.

"Mm."

"What did exile cost you?"

Her brush stopped. Of everyone aboard, only she had never told me the story. Ovesa's crimes were legend and Brannoc's fall was scripture, but Wren's past was a blank space on her own map, carefully left uninked, and I had honored that the way I honored any closed ledger.

She set the brush down with great care, as if it might break, or as if she might.

"You first," she said. "What did your god's silence cost you?"

"That's not fair. You know that one."

"I want to hear it anyway. You've never said it straight out. You circle it like a client circling a price." She folded her hands. "Five years of silence. Tell me what that was."

I looked at the fire instead of her, because some things could be said to flames that couldn't be said to faces.

"When I took my oath, I felt it," I said. "Not words. Not a face. Just—attention. Like being held in someone's gaze so steady you could build a life on it. Every day for years, that attention was there. I wasn't anybody important. I was a clerk with a sword and a bad temper. But I was *regarded*, and the regarding never wavered." My throat tightened. I kept going, ledger-flat. "Then one morning it wasn't. Five years ago. And the worst part wasn't the loss. The worst part was the doubt. Because if the regard could simply *stop*, then maybe it had never been real. Maybe I'd been praying into a well I dug myself. Maybe every moment of grace I remembered was just—" I gestured helplessly— "a merchant's optimism. Faith as inventory I couldn't move."

"And now?"

"Now I know it was listening." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Now I know it heard every word of every letter I wrote and never answered because it *couldn't*—because it was being held hostage in amber by a thing that eats promises—and I don't know if that's better or worse. It's better. It's infinitely worse. It means the silence was never abandonment. It means I spent five years grieving a living thing."

Wren reached across the chart. She didn't take my hand—that would have smudged the ink, and she knew I'd mind—but she laid her fingers lightly over my wrist, over the pulse, and left them there, and we watched the fire eat its small meal of twigs while the starless sky leaned close to listen.

"My family were map-binders in Vessik," she said at last, quietly. "You know what a map-binder does? Takes surveyors' notes and makes them true. Draws the line, and the road becomes the line. It's holy work, if anything is. It's also power, and my family sold theirs to whoever paid." Her thumb moved once against my wrist. "When I was nineteen I found out whose money bound our maps. Guild contracts. Roads drawn to funnel armies, borders drawn to start wars, a famine corridor drawn into existence because someone wanted grain prices to move. My own hand was going to inherit the pen that drew those lines. So I burned the family archive and left the same night. They struck my name from the binder rolls. In Vessik, that's not disowning. It's erasure. Officially, I was never born."

"That's why you draw roads that move," I said. "So no one can use them."

"So no one can use them," she agreed. "Exile cost me my name, my craft's legitimacy, and everyone I'd ever known. And I'd do it again tomorrow, before breakfast, without slowing down." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were fierce and tired and utterly certain. "Some things you burn your whole life down for, Isandro Galloway, and the burning doesn't hurt nearly as much as the not-doing would have."

The fire popped. High above, in the black bowl of the sky, nothing moved.

I sat very still, because something had just happened in my chest that I could only describe as a lock recognizing its key, and I was afraid that if I breathed wrong, I would say it. All of it. Right there, with her fingers on my pulse, where she would feel the truth of it whether I spoke or not.

Because I understood. Sitting on a cold capstone with ink drying on her chart and her name struck from every roll that mattered, I finally understood the inscription in the drowned temple.

*A gift given wholly returns to the giver.*

Every oath I had ever considered offering was a transaction. Protect the king, and my guild-sisters live. Break the contract, and the world keeps its shape. Save the god, and maybe—maybe—it would speak to me again, fill the five-year hole, regard me once more. Every version was barter dressed in vestments. The Sigil would smell the price on any of them and swallow it whole.

But this. This thing that had been growing between Wren and me through shared watches and unsaid things and fingers resting on pulses—this had no terms. I wanted nothing back. I had never wanted anything back. I loved her the way the temple inscription meant, the way Brannoc's old texts meant: wholly, without condition, without even the secret hope of reciprocation. If she never knew, it would lose nothing. If she knew and turned away, it would lose nothing. It was not a purchase. It was not a debt. It was simply true, the way a coastline is true whether or not anyone surveys it.

An oath the Sigil could not digest. Not because it was strong, but because it was free. Because hunger has no grip on a gift.

* *

They found me at dawn on the parapet, doing sums that weren't sums.

Ovesa arrived first, stamping frost off her boots, pressing a tin cup of terrible tea into my hands. "You look like a woman who's solved an equation and hates the answer," she said, which was Ovesa's way of asking.

"The answer's expensive," I said.

"They're always expensive." She leaned on the parapet beside me, squinting east where the sun insisted on rising anyway, into a sky that gave it no stars to displace. "Question is whether you can afford *not* to pay. Nine days, Galloway. Whatever you're carrying, decide fast, because I sail this crew toward the capital in eight, and I'd rather we all knew what we're dying about." She said it lightly, the way she said everything lethal, and clattered back down the stairs calling for breakfast as if the fate of nations were a scheduling matter.

Tam came next, pale and precise, with the defected court papers spread in the wind like a gambler showing a losing hand. "The rite requires the sovereign's consent renewed at the moment of crowning," he recited. "Aldric must *choose*, again, freely—or at least appear to. Sefeya's lullaby may crack him open long enough. But the Archive contract will resist dissolution unless—" he caught my expression and stopped. "Unless what?"

"Unless it's offered something it can't hold," I said. "Don't ask me yet. Please."

He studied me with those careful scribe's eyes, the eyes of a man who had watched a good king sign away his soul to feed a starving country and had learned exactly how much mercy complicated things can hold. Then he bowed, slightly, and gathered his papers, and did not ask.

Brannoc came last, as the sun cleared the ridge. He stood with me a while without speaking, watching the light crawl gold across the drowned valley below, where a god waited in amber and a temple held its breath.

"You've decided," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"May I know what?"

"No."

He nodded slowly, and there was grief in it, and respect, and something that might have been envy. "That's how you know it's real," he said. "The old books say a gift spoken of too early becomes a bargain. Keep it in your chest, Isandro. Let it stay worthless to the world right up until the moment it costs you everything." He touched his brow in the old blessing—the one his order used for the dying and the betrothed, which had always been the same gesture—and went down to breakfast.

I stayed on the parapet.

Below me, my strange family argued cheerfully about route options and ration mathematics. Wren's voice carried up through the stairwell, sharp with mock exasperation at something Ovesa had done to one of her charts, and I closed my eyes and memorized the sound of it, the way a cartographer memorizes a coastline she may never see again.

Eight days. One throne room. One freely-given vow with no conditions, no escape clause, nothing asked in return—and only one person at that fire who carried such a thing inside her, whole and unspent.

I had written letters to a silent god for five years. Tonight, I would write one more, and this one I would not send.

*Dearest Wren,* I composed silently, watching the sun climb into the empty sky. *By the time you read this, I will have lied to you beautifully, and I am sorry. Know that nothing I do in the throne room is a sacrifice. It is a payment I owe no one, for a love I was never billed for, given to a universe that cannot digest it. That's not bravery. That's arithmetic. Some numbers only balance when you give everything away.*

Down the stairwell, her voice rose again, laughing now.

I opened my eyes, and began, quietly, to plan the lie.
