## 19. Unbinding

The throne room of Cael Saren had been built to make a person feel small. Its ceiling rose in vaulted arcs of white stone ribbed with silver, and the pillars marched down either side like columns of frozen soldiers, and the floor was a single slab of pale marble polished so finely that it held your reflection like still water. Every inch of it had been designed by architects who understood that power is mostly theater.

The Sigil had redecorated.

The silver ribs of the ceiling were black now, veined through like rot spreading through fruit flesh. The pillars wore the darkness too, climbing from their bases in slow tendrils that pulsed faintly when you looked away and were still when you looked back. And the throne itself—once gilded oak carved with the faces of every monarch since the founding—was sheathed in something that was not metal and not wood but a smooth, seamless dark, as if the old crown's seat had been swallowed whole and this was what remained on the outside.

Isandro stood at the far end of the hall with Wren's hand pressed hard against her own, and she thought: *it looks like a ledger.* The whole room. Black ink bleeding across clean pages. She had spent eleven years reading accounts of impossible things, and now she was standing inside one.

"You're shaking," Wren murmured.

"I'm cold." It wasn't entirely a lie. The oath-mark on her left forearm had been burning cold for three days—not the ice-pain of the night the stars died, but something steadier, a low hum like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Her god was close. Whatever was left of him. Swallowed, Brannoc had said, held inside the thing wearing the king as collateral, the way a creditor holds a debtor's deed.

Around them, the sabotage was already in motion. Through the tall windows Isandro could see Ovesa's crew moving along the rooflines of the outer ward, cutting the choir-lines one by one—the silver threads strung between the spires that would carry the nation's oaths into the Sigil's mouth at sundown. Somewhere below, Tam was walking into the archive offices with forged credentials and a face like a man who belonged there, because after twenty years of court service he finally did. Brannoc was at the gates, doing whatever defrocked priests do when they need a door to forget how to be locked.

And Sefeya—

Sefeya was walking down the center aisle alone.

She was nine years old, and she wore a plain gray traveling dress because her father's tailor had not survived the rewriting any better than her memories had, and her small fists were clenched at her sides the way they had been clenched since the drowned temple, since Isandro had told her what they needed from her and asked—begged, actually, in words she'd never said aloud to anyone—for forgiveness in advance.

"The guards won't stop me," Sefeya had said on the road, with the terrible practicality of children who have already thought everything through. "The Sigil doesn't guard against me. I'm part of its collection."

Now the doors at the far end of the hall stood open, and no one stopped her. Two figures flanked the throne where guards should have been—shapes in royal livery that moved wrong, joints bending in sequences bodies weren't built for—and they turned their heads to watch the princess pass, and let her.

"They know," Isandro breathed. "They're letting her through."

"Of course they are." Wren's voice was steady, which meant she was terrified; Isandro had learned to read her maps the same way. "It doesn't matter what she does unless someone unbinds him. They think we've come to fail at the hard part."

"Then let's disappoint them."

They walked. The marble gave back their reflections, and Isandro noticed, with a lurch, that hers was wrong—the oath-mark on her reflected arm was bright, luminous, a line of gold-white light crawling up from wrist to elbow, while her real arm showed only the familiar scar-dark brand. As if the reflection knew something she didn't yet.

Ahead, Sefeya reached the foot of the dais and stopped. And up close, Isandro could see the king.

Aldric sat in his swallowed throne like a man made of wax left near a fire. His body was right—a big man gone gaunt, hands knotted in his lap, a king's posture worn down to its skeleton—but his eyes were the problem. They were open. They had been open, Tam testified, for three years. And behind them was nothing that blinked, nothing that tired, a patient black attention looking out through the shape of a man the way you look out through a window on a rainy day.

The Sigil spoke without moving his mouth. The voice came from everywhere—from the pillars, from the floor, from the space behind Isandro's teeth.

*THE LEDGER-KEEPER. YOU BROUGHT THE CHILD BACK TO ME.*

"She came home," Isandro said. Her voice sounded thin in all that dark. "Kings' daughters do."

*I KEPT HER NAME SAFE WHEN SHE WAS BORN. THE FIRST PROMISE I EVER MADE IN THIS HOUSE. SHE WILL BE SO MUCH EASIER TO HOLD NOW THAT SHE IS SMALL ENOUGH TO CARRY.*

Sefeya didn't flinch. Isandro watched her plant her feet, watched her take the breath that Brannoc had taught her, deep and slow, the breath before prayer. Then the princess opened her mouth and sang.

---

It was a lullaby. That was all. Four lines, an old tune in an old mode, the kind of song that has no author because it has always existed, passed mouth to mouth down generations of tired parents. *Sleep, my heart, the day is done, / the river carries off the sun, / and what was sworn will keep till morrow— / sleep, my heart, and borrow sorrow...*

Her voice was small and slightly flat and absolutely fearless.

The effect was immediate and obscene. The black veins in the pillars *shrieked*—there was no other word for it, a soundless shriek felt in the bones rather than heard, the way a building screams in wind. The shapes in livery staggered. And King Aldric—

King Aldric blinked.

It was such a small thing. Isandro had seen men die with more drama. But those dead black eyes closed and opened, and when they opened again they were wet, and brown, and *his*, and for the first time in three years a man looked out of the window instead of the weather.

"Sefeya." His voice was gravel and rust. He tried to rise and couldn't; the throne held him the way a debt holds a man. "Little star. You shouldn't—you have to go, you can't be here, it uses—" His gaze found Isandro over his daughter's head, and something in his ruined face sharpened. "You. The guild woman. The appraiser."

"Isandro Galloway."

"Galloway." A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could bleed. "I signed your guild's charter. Did you know? Twenty-two years ago. I read every clause." His eyes went distant, then focused again with visible effort, a swimmer surfacing. "Listen to me. It will offer you anything. It offered me a kingdom and it paid—that's the horror of it, it *pays*, every bargain keeps its letter—it just never tells you what the coin is made of until the weighing."

"Aldric." Isandro climbed the dais steps. Behind her, Wren had moved to Sefeya and put both hands on the girl's shoulders, anchoring them both. "We found the contract. In the Archive. We know how it ends."

"There is no end." His voice cracked. "I looked. For three years I looked, in the small hours when it sleeps in my marrow—I am not permitted to want, do you understand? Wanting is consent, and consent feeds it—"

"Wanting isn't enough," Isandro said. "That's exactly why it ends. The contract requires the sworn party's willing consent to dissolve. Not desperation. Not exhaustion. Willingness. Freely given." She held his gaze. "You have to choose this, Your Majesty. Nobody can choose it for you. Believe me, several people here would very much like to."

Something moved behind the king's eyes—hope, or fear, or the place where those two things become indistinguishable. And then his face went slack, and the black came flooding up from underneath like ink through water, and the Sigil looked out at Isandro through her king's face and smiled with muscles it was only borrowing.

*A FREELY GIVEN VOW,* it said. *YES. WE HAVE READ YOUR LEDGERS TOO, GALLOWAY. WE KNOW WHAT YOU INTEND. AND WE ARE NOT AFRAID OF IT.*

"Why not?" Isandro kept her voice level. Her heart was slamming so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

*BECAUSE NO ONE HAS EVER DONE IT. IN ALL THE LONG ACCOUNTING OF THIS WORLD, EVERY OATH EVER SWORN WAS BOUGHT WITH SOMETHING. SAFETY. LOVE. SALVATION. AN AFTERLIFE. EVEN YOUR GOD'S COVENANTS—ESPECIALLY YOUR GOD'S—WERE PURCHASES. I EAT THEM ALL. THERE IS NOTHING ON ANY MENU THAT I CANNOT DIGEST.*

"That's true," Isandro said.

The Sigil waited. It was patient beyond the comprehension of anything born; it could wait out mountains.

"So here's mine anyway," Isandro Galloway said, and took a breath, and broke her own heart wide open on the steps of a stolen throne.

---

Later—much later, in the long white aftermath—Wren would ask her how she'd known what to say, and Isandro would tell her the truth: that she hadn't. That she had stood on the dais with the Sigil smiling through the king's borrowed face and realized that she had rehearsed nothing, prepared nothing, drafted nothing, and that this was precisely the point. You cannot write a freely given vow in advance. The moment you draft it, it becomes a transaction—a performance, a bid for outcome. It has to be spoken raw, unwritten, straight from the account with no entry made first.

So she didn't plan it. She just opened her mouth.

"I don't want anything from this," she said. "Not the world saved. Not the stars back. Not even you"—and here she looked at Aldric, at the man drowning behind his own eyes—"going free, though God knows I want that, want it like air. Hear me, thing in the crown. This is not a trade. There is no price. There is no consideration. There is no *because*. I am going to say a vow that gives itself away completely, and keeps nothing back, and expects nothing, and if you can digest that—if you can find the hook in it, the payment, the hidden ledger-line—then eat it and be fed."

*AND WHAT VOW IS THAT?* Something in the vast patience had shifted. Not fear. Curiosity. Hunger leaning forward.

Isandro turned. Found Wren's eyes across the width of the dais. Wren, who mapped roads that moved because she had been exiled from every road that stayed. Wren, who had shown Isandro the stains on her charts converging on her own arm and hadn't once asked what it would cost her to stand next to it. Wren, whose hand was on a child's shoulder, holding them both steady, the way she had been holding Isandro steady since a river crossing that cost them Renna and sealed them into something none of them had ever named out loud.

They had never named it. Isandro understood, in the last clear moment before she spoke, that naming it now—as reason, as cause, as currency—would ruin it. Love named as consideration becomes a purchase. So she said it without saying it. She aimed the vow at Wren and let the meaning land where it landed.

"I give myself," Isandro said, "to the keeping of what cannot be owned."

The words came out of her like blood from a wound—warm, unstoppable, hers.

"Not to a god. Not to a crown. Not to the Guild or the road or the ledger or the sky. To the keeping of it. The storms in their bottles. The years in their jars. The names not yet earned and the names being stolen tonight. The silent places and the singing ones. The king behind his eyes and the child who sang him back and the cartographer who never once asked me to be less than I am." Her voice did not shake. She would remember that later with astonishment. "I swear to hold what cannot be held, to guard what pays no toll, to keep faith with the ungovernable—and I ask for nothing. No protection. No reward. No heaven, no harvest, no answer, ever. Take it or leave it. It is not for sale because it is not for anything. It is only given."

Silence.

The Sigil reached for the vow the way it had reached for ten thousand years of oaths—she felt it happen, felt the great blind appetite close around her words like a jaw—and then, for the first time in the entire history of promises, it found nothing to bite.

There was no hook. There was no price. There was no *because*.

An oath of pure gift has no handle. You cannot swallow what refuses to be food.

The Sigil screamed.

---

This time the scream was audible. It tore out of the throne room, out of the palace, out of the ground under the capital, a sound like every contract in the world burning at once, and perhaps that is exactly what it was. The black veins in the pillars burst apart into ash that fell upward. The seamless dark of the throne split along seams that had been invisible for three years, and through the cracks Isandro saw gold—old, honest, gilded oak, the faces of dead kings staring out in relief—and beneath the gold, paper. Pages. Thousands upon thousands of pages packed into the throne's hollow heart like a wasps' nest, every oath ever sworn in Cael Saren, and they were catching fire.

The contract binding Aldric burned first. Isandro saw it go—a ribbon of flame racing up through the throne's core—and she saw the moment it reached the signature, because the whole room *exhaled*, a pressure dropping like the air before rain breaks.

King Aldric came back into his body the way a drowning man comes up through water.

He made a sound—half sob, half laugh—and his hands came free of the armrests, and he slid off the dais steps on his knees to reach his daughter, and Sefeya crashed into him, and the two of them held each other in the middle of the falling ash and the rising light, and neither of them said a word because there were no words, there were only four lines of an old lullaby that Sefeya was crying and laughing and singing into her father's shoulder while he shook.

And then the god came home.

It did not arrive. It *returned*—flooded back into the world through every crack the Sigil had ever left, through every broken ward and blank page and erased name, and Isandro felt it pass through her like a tide through an open door. The oath-mark on her arm blazed white-hot and then, for the first time in five years, warm. Warm the way a hearth is warm. Warm the way a hand on your shoulder is warm.

She heard it. Not words. Something older than words—a presence settling into the world's bones the way a house settles at night, creaking, sighing, *inhabited again*. And threaded through that vast quiet return, addressed to her and her alone, one impression clear as a struck bell:

*Well kept. Well given. Now rest.*

The Sigil unraveled starving. That was the last of it, and Isandro watched every second, because someone should witness it and she was, after all, a keeper of accounts. With the contract burnt and the oaths released and nothing left in its belly, the thing that had eaten prayers and worn crowns began to consume itself—its borrowed body sloughing off the throne in sheets of grey, its voice thinning from thunder to whisper to the sound of a ledger closing, and the last thing it said, in a voice so small it barely carried across the marble, was almost pitiable:

*But everything was owed to me. Everything was owed.*

"No," Isandro said softly, to the empty air where it had been. "That was the lie. That was always the lie."

The last of the dark lifted off the ceiling like smoke finding a chimney. Through the shattered windows, over the rooftops of the capital, the sky—

The sky was changing. The lid was coming off. Far above the city, where there had been nothing for a year but a flat starless pressing black, a single point of light pricked through, then another, then a scatter of them, tentative as the first notes of a musician tuning.

Isandro sat down on the dais steps beside a weeping king and his daughter, and Wren came and sat down next to her, close, their shoulders touching, saying nothing, needing to say nothing, and together they watched the stars come back.

One by one.

By the hundreds.

By the thousands, until the whole broken sky was stitched with light, and somewhere in the courtyard below Brannoc was shouting himself hoarse, and Ovesa's whoop echoed off the spires, and Tam walked slowly out onto the palace steps with his scribe's case hanging forgotten from one hand, looking up like a man seeing color for the first time.

Isandro's arm ached. She looked down. Where the oath-mark had burned dark for five years and cold for three days, her skin was marked now with a thin line of scar-white—clean, bright, finished, like a ledger line ruled and closed.

Except it wasn't closed. Because as she watched, the white scar shimmered, and new script surfaced along it, fine and faint and unmistakably alive, and Isandro read the first fresh entry her god had ever written on her skin, and laughed until she cried.

It said:

*What will you do now that nothing is owed?*

* * *

She was still holding Wren's hand when dawn came.
