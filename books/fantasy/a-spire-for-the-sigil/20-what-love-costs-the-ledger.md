## 20. What Love Costs the Ledger

The throne room still smelled of burned paper.

That was the thing Wren would remember, later, when she tried to explain it to anyone who hadn't been there: not the light, though the light had been blinding enough to leave ghosts behind her eyes; not the sound of the contract tearing, which had been less a sound than an absence, a silence so total it felt like the world holding its breath. What she remembered was the smell. Like every promise ever written in that room had caught fire at once, and the smoke smelled the way she imagined forgiveness would smell, if forgiveness had a smell at all.

Isandro lay on the steps below the empty throne, and Wren could not make her legs carry her fast enough.

She went down them half-falling, catching herself on the banister, on the wall, on nothing. Ovesa got there first — of course Ovesa did, Ovesa moved through chaos the way water moved through cracks — but it was Wren's knees that hit the marble beside her, Wren's hands that hovered over Isandro's face without quite daring to touch it, because Isandro looked *elsewhere*, somewhere far past the ceiling, past the starless sky, past anything Wren knew how to map.

"Hey," Wren said. "Hey. Ledger-keeper. Come back."

Isandro's eyes moved. Found her. Focused with visible effort, the way a person surfaces from deep water.

"Wren." Her voice came out scraped raw, a whisper dragged over gravel. "Did we—"

"You did." Brannoc's voice, from somewhere above and behind; Wren couldn't look away from Isandro's face to see where he stood. "By every rite I know and a few I'd sworn off, you did."

"The king," Isandro said, and tried to sit up.

Wren pressed her back down with one hand flat against her sternum, feeling the too-fast flutter of her heart beneath. "Aldric is Aldric. Sefeya's with him. He cried. You missed it because you were busy being unconscious and dramatic."

Something flickered across Isandro's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of the dry wit that had carried them across a country gone dark, worn down to almost nothing by whatever it cost to speak an oath the Sigil couldn't swallow.

"It worked," Isandro said, as if confirming it for herself.

"It worked," Wren said.

And then Isandro's eyes rolled back, and she went limp as a dropped rope, and Wren stopped being able to form words at all.

* * *

They carried her out of the throne room because no one would hear of leaving her in it. Tam argued for the royal physician — "She saved the king's life, the palace owes her its entire medical staff" — but Brannoc shook his head slowly, in the way he did when doctrine and instinct pulled opposite directions.

"She didn't faint," he said. "She was *spent*. There's a difference, and the difference matters. Whatever answered her vow is still... near. I've felt this quiet before. In the temple. Before the drowning." His hands, scarred from five years of defrocked priest's labor, flexed at his sides. "We keep her somewhere small. Somewhere human. And someone stays with her."

"I'll stay," Wren said, before she knew she'd said it, before the thought had finished forming.

Ovesa looked at her sideways, and something passed between them — not teasing, for once, which from Ovesa was its own kind of benediction. "Course you will," she said. "Map-girl knows where things are. Even things that aren't there yet."

* * *

They gave her a room in the palace's old guest wing, a chamber that had clearly not been used since some forgotten reign: dust sheets over furniture shaped like sleeping animals, a window narrow as an arrow-slit, a bed with a mattress stuffed, judging by the smell, with lavender that had given up any pretense of freshness decades ago. It was perfect. It was small and human and it smelled like time passing, and Wren spread her maps across the floor anyway because her hands needed work or they'd start shaking and not stop.

Isandro lay in the bed and did not wake.

Her breathing was slow and even, deeper than sleep. Her skin had a strange luminosity to it, faint as moonlight on paper. And on the inside of her left forearm — Wren had seen the oath-mark a hundred times by now, the dark sigil-line that had burned cold the night the stars went out, that had ached through every mile of the Salt Road — the mark had changed. It was white now. Scar-white, smooth as old bone, standing out against her skin like a road drawn on a map with no destination marked.

Wren sat beside the bed with a lamp turned low and watched her breathe, and mapped the rise and fall of her chest without meaning to, because mapping was what Wren did when the world frightened her, and the world had never frightened her more than right now, when the danger was over.

Hours passed. Somewhere beyond the arrow-slit window, the sky stayed black and empty. But it was a different blackness now, Wren thought. Before, the dark had been a lid pressing down. Now it felt more like a held breath. Like something waiting to exhale.

Near midnight — or what Wren guessed was midnight, with no stars to reckon by — Isandro stirred.

Not waking. Her lips moved, shaping words too soft to catch. Her fingers twitched against the blanket. And the white mark on her arm began, very faintly, to glow — not burning, not cold, just luminous, like a page held up to candlelight from behind.

Wren leaned close.

"—and what then?" Isandro whispered, to no one in the room. "What do you want me to—"

Silence. But it was a listening silence. Wren had spent her life reading landscapes, reading the way roads bent around hills that weren't there anymore, and she knew listening when she heard it, even wordless. Something vast and patient was paying attention to this room.

"What will you do," breathed the voice — except it wasn't a voice, it came from everywhere and nowhere, from Isandro's own mouth and from the space between heartbeats, "now that nothing is owed?"

Wren went very still.

The question hung in the air like incense smoke. It was not addressed to her. She was certain of that, and equally certain she was not meant to be hearing it, and utterly unable to leave.

"Nothing is owed," Isandro repeated, in her sleep, in her trance, wherever she was. Her brow furrowed — the same furrow Wren had watched a hundred times over ledgers, over maps, over Wren's own confession by the fire. The furrow of a woman confronting an entry that refused to balance. "I don't understand. Everything is owed. Everything has always been—"

"*No,*" said the not-voice, gentle as falling snow. "*That was the Sigil's arithmetic. Mine is different. Ask her.*"

And the glow faded, and Isandro sighed — a long, long exhale, five years of held breath finally released — and slept true sleep at last, dreamless and deep.

Ask her.

Wren sat back slowly. Her hands had started shaking after all.

* * *

Dawn, if such a thing existed anymore, brought gray light through the arrow-slit and the smell of bread from somewhere below, because even apocalypses paused for breakfast, and the palace kitchens apparently ran on a schedule older than kings.

Isandro woke like a swimmer coming up through fathoms — slow, blinking, disoriented — and the first thing she did was look at her arm. The second thing she did was look at Wren.

"You stayed."

"Someone had to guard you from the dust sheets. They were closing in."

Isandro laughed — a wrecked, rusty sound, half cough — and winced, and lay back. "How long?"

"All night. You talked, a little. To..." Wren hesitated. "To your god, I think. It asked you a question."

He watched Wren's face carefully, and Wren watched hers, and neither of them looked away. Outside the arrow-slit, the sky remained dark, but Wren would have sworn it felt thinner somehow. Closer to breaking open.

"What question?" Isandro asked quietly.

Wren took a breath. "It asked what you'll do now that nothing is owed. And then—" Her voice caught, just slightly. "Then it told you to ask me."

Isandro stared at her. And Wren watched understanding move through her like dawn moving across a landscape — slowly, irrevocably, lighting things that had been dark for a very long time.

"Oh," Isandro said softly.

"Yeah," Wren said. "Oh."

They sat in the gray light and the distant smell of bread, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because some entries, Wren thought, take longer than others to write. Some balances take a lifetime.

But Isandro reached out across the blanket, and found Wren's hand, and laced their fingers together with the careful deliberation of a woman writing something down she intended to keep.

"No conditions," Isandro murmured. "No reward. Just—this. If you want it."

Wren squeezed her hand, and outside the window, faint as a rumor, the first star of the evening blinked into existence above the horizon.

"It's morning," Wren said, smiling through tears. "But yes. I want it."

And far above them both, patient as ever, another star waited its turn to come home.
