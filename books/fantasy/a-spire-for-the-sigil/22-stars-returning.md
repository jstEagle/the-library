## 22. Stars, Returning

The sea off the Corrow Shoals had a way of holding light after sundown, as if the water were reluctant to let the day go. Isandro Galloway stood at the *Kindly Omen*'s stern rail with a ledger open against her forearm, not reading it, and watched the east darken by degrees.

One year. She had counted it the way she counted everything now—in entries rather than days. Three hundred and sixty-five lines in the new book, most of them small: a coat mended for a stranger in Pell's Harbor, two barrels of honest nails sold at an honest price, a name returned to a woman who had forgotten it in a city that had forgotten everything else. Small entries. The kind the old Vellum Guild would have laughed at. The kind that weighed nothing at all, which was, she had learned, another word for priceless.

"You're doing the squinting thing," Wren said from beside her, materializing the way she always did—quietly, at the edge of vision, as though she'd been mapped there all along. "The ledger-squint. Like the numbers owe you an apology."

"They usually do." Isandro closed the book. "This one doesn't. I just like holding it. Old habit."

Wren leaned her forearms on the rail. Her cartographer's hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle—some things never changed, thank every listening god—and there was a new map rolled under one arm, because there was always a new map. "The sky's clear tonight," she said, and her voice did something careful around the words, the way everyone's did now when they spoke of the sky.

It had been clear for months. Clear wasn't the same as full.

Isandro looked up. The darkness overhead was deep and clean, the first true dark the world had known since the night the stars went out—and out of that darkness, here and there, points of light were returning. Not all at once. Never all at once. They came back the way spring came back to the high passes: one stubborn witness at a time, each one arriving alone and unannounced, as if none of them wished to admit the others had also been away.

Sailors on other ships called them *the Returned*. Superstition said each star came back carrying something it had seen while it was gone. Isandro put no faith in that. But she had watched enough of them reappear over this past year to know that no two of them arrived looking quite the same.

"There," Wren said softly, lifting her chin toward the southeast. A new point had kindled above the horizon, faint as breath on glass. It held. It brightened. Somewhere below decks, someone whooped—the *Omen*'s crew kept a running tally chalked inside the galley door, forty-one stars this month, and Ovesa had been known to pay a copper to whoever spotted one first.

"That's the seventh tonight," Isandro said. "Ovesa's going to be insufferable."

"Ovesa is always insufferable. It's load-bearing."

Isandro laughed, and felt the laugh settle somewhere in her chest where, two years ago, there had only been the cold burn of an oath-mark and the terrible arithmetic of duty. Her wrist was bare now beneath its wrapped cord. Where the mark had seared itself white the night the Sigil unraveled, the skin was smooth and pale as vellum—blank, spent, paid in full. Some nights she still caught herself reaching to touch it, the way an amputee reaches for what's gone, and found nothing there but her own pulse.

Her god said nothing. Her god had said nothing for six years, if she was honest, and said nothing still.

But silence, she had learned, came in more than one language.

* * *

Dinner was served on deck because Sefeya refused to eat below when there was sky to watch, and nobody aboard had the heart—or frankly the tactical positioning—to refuse the princess anything. She was eleven now, tall for her age, with her father's dark eyes and her own ferocious opinions about proper lullaby technique. She sat cross-legged on a coil of rope with Tam doting nervously at her elbow, correcting the princess's table manners and being cheerfully ignored.

"—and Papa says that when the stars are all back, he'll hold a real festival, not a court one, a *people* one, with dancing in the streets and no speeches—" Sefeya paused, skewering Tam with a look. "Tam, tell them about the speeches."

"There will be speeches," Tam admitted, with the resigned air of a man who would be writing them. "There are always speeches. But I have drafted them short. Two minutes. Perhaps three."

"Two," Sefeya said, in a tone that made it law.

Brannoc rumbled a laugh from his place at the head of the crate he used as a chair. The ex-priest had gone grey at the temples this year and broader through the shoulders, as though grief, having nowhere else to go in him, had been rendered into stone. He raised his cup. "To the king's two-minute speeches. May every miracle be equally brief."

"To miracles," Ovesa said, appearing over the rail-side with a bottle in one hand and the particular grin that meant she'd already had some of the bottle. "Brief or otherwise. And to my ship, which has hauled smuggled gods'-truths and honest cargo both, and never once asked me which was which."

"*Your* ship," Brannoc muttered. "The ship I won in a card game."

"The ship you lost to me in a card game, yes. History is written by the victors, priest." Ovesa dropped down beside him and poured. "Also written by whoever holds the deed."

Isandro watched them bicker and thought, not for the first time, that the old ledgers had taught her to value the wrong things entirely. The Guild had recorded worth in scarcity—one bottled storm, one borrowed year, one name not yet earned, each precious because few. But sitting here on a salt-scoured deck with these five improbable people, passing bread that was slightly stale and wine that was frankly terrible, she understood that abundance had its own ledger. That some wealth compounded only by being spent.

Sefeya caught her watching and grinned. "Isandro. Tell the star story."

"I've told it four times."

"Tell it *properly*. Tam tells it wrong. He puts himself in it too much."

"I was present!" Tam protested. "I defected! It was very brave!"

"It was," Isandro agreed, and Tam sat up, mollified. She turned to Sefeya. "All right. Once upon a time, a king signed his name away to save his people from starving—"

"And it was a bad bargain," Sefeya recited, clearly having memorized this part, "but it wasn't a *wicked* one, that's the important bit—"

"—that's the important bit. And a thing older than kings wore him like a coat and started eating every promise in the world, starting with the smallest ones, because small promises go down easy. And a guild clerk who knew too much ran away with a smuggler, a defrocked priest, and a cartographer who drew maps of things that hadn't happened yet—"

"Things that weren't supposed to exist yet," Wren corrected mildly, from her place at Isandro's shoulder.

"—and a scribe who was braver than his handwriting suggested. And they broke into an archive, and stole a contract, and sang a lullaby at the worst possible moment—"

"In tune," Sefeya said firmly. "That's why it worked."

"In tune," Isandro conceded gravely. "And a vow was spoken that couldn't be swallowed, because it asked for nothing back. And the thing that ate oaths starved, because it had finally been offered a meal it couldn't digest. And the world's promises came flooding home like water finding its old channels." She paused. "And the stars began to come back. One at a time. Because even stars, it turns out, prefer to arrive on their own schedule."

Sefeya nodded, satisfied, and returned to her bread. Around the circle, nobody spoke for a moment. They rarely did, after the story. It was their liturgy now, Isandro realized—not the blood-written oaths of the old Guild, not the iron contracts of the Archive, but this: a story told aloud, freely, to a child, changing a little with every telling, asking nothing of anyone who heard it.

Brannoc cleared his throat. His eyes were suspiciously bright. "I'm going to check the rigging," he announced, and went to check rigging that did not need checking, in the dark, badly.

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted below or into their watches, Isandro climbed to the bow. This was her hour—the middle watch, when the sea went glassy and the ship's wake was the loudest thing in the world. She brought the ledger, because she always brought the ledger, and sat with her back against the forestay.

She didn't expect company. But footsteps came soft along the deck, and Wren folded herself down beside her with the ease of long practice, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"She's asleep," Wren reported. "Finally. She made me promise the stars would wait for her if any new ones came before dawn."

"Will they?"

"They're stars. They have terrible manners." Wren tilted her head back. Above them, the sky had filled further while they ate—Isandro could see constellations half-assembled, familiar shapes returning joint by joint, like friends walking up a road out of fog. "Forty-four now. The tally-keepers in the galley are going to need a bigger door."

Isandro opened the ledger on her knees but didn't write. "Do you ever think about how it ended?" she asked quietly. "What we did? What it cost?"

"Every day."

"Every day," Isandro echoed. She thought of the friend who had died at the river crossing, whose name she had entered in the new book's first page in letters she'd pressed so hard they'd torn the paper. She thought of Corvane, who burned her file instead of her and then begged her, in the dark beneath the capital, to stop—and who now ran half the reformed Guild with tired eyes and clean hands, trying to make amends one impossible transaction at a time. She thought of Aldric, restored and diminished, a good man learning to live inside the memory of what his desperation had cost, signing reparations with a hand that shook.

"I keep waiting for it to feel finished," she said. "It doesn't. It feels *ongoing*. Like an account that never closes."

"That's because it doesn't close." Wren said it simply, the way she read her maps—stating what was there. "That's the whole lesson, isn't it? Your old Guild thought promises were debts. Closed figures. You pay, it ends, the ledger balances and you walk away clean." She reached over and tapped the open book on Isandro's knee. "But your new book records gifts. Gifts don't balance. They just keep moving."

Isandro looked down at the page. Tonight's entry was already written; she'd made it at dusk. *Taught Sefeya to mend a net. She was worse than expected. Laughed anyway.* Beneath it, blank space, waiting.

Above the bow, low over the water where the horizon was beginning to remember dawn, something happened.

At first Isandro took it for a wave-flash, phosphorescence breaking along the shoals. Then it came again—a slow pulse of light, silver-white, rising from the sea itself and climbing, and she was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, Wren beside her, both of them gripping the rail.

The light rose and gathered and did not scatter. It hung off the port bow, perhaps a hundred yards out, shaped like nothing and like everything—a column, a veil, a held breath—and the sea around it went mirror-still, reflecting not the sky that was but the sky that would be, every star in place, every constellation whole, a heaven completed hanging upside down on the water.

And in the silence of her own chest, in the blank white scar where an oath had lived and died and freed her, Isandro felt something that was not a voice and not a command and not a debt.

It felt like attention. Like being listened to. Like a presence that had eaten every prayer thrown at it for six years and found them all wanting, and now, offered nothing—no plea, no bargain, no frightened demand—simply sat with her in the dark, the way you sit with someone you chose.

*Well,* the silence said, in the language of silences. *Here we are.*

"Here we are," Isandro whispered aloud, and Wren's hand found hers on the rail, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing owed, and nothing needed, and the light on the water pulsed once—gently, almost amused—and sank back into the deep like a sleeper turning over.

The stars overhead went on arriving. One, then another, then a third, unhurried, each on its own schedule, each coming home.

In the galley below, the tally would grow by three by morning. Ovesa would owe coppers. Sefeya would demand the story again, properly, with the correct number of stars in the ending. Tam would begin drafting a two-minute speech and finish at nine pages. Brannoc would pretend he hadn't cried. And Isandro Galloway, former keeper of impossible things, would open her book and record the night exactly as it was:

*Three more came home. Nobody promised them anything. They came anyway.*

She smiled, and closed the ledger, and stood with her family under the mending sky until the sun came up.
