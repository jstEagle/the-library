## 1. The Weight of Empty Ledgers

The snow came down the way it always did on the Kestrel side of Thornfell: sideways, with intent, like it had a grudge against anything warm-blooded that dared to cross its passes. Cyrus Voss had been walking in it since before dawn, and by now the cold had stopped being an enemy and become something closer to a companion—unfriendly, familiar, and utterly indifferent to whether they lived or died.

They had learned to think of the mountain the same way.

The pass was called the Widow's Stitch, a narrow seam between two shoulders of black stone where the wind funneled through and screamed like something being skinned alive. Cyrus knew every handhold, every shelf of ice thick enough to bear weight, every crevice where a body could wait out a patrol. Twenty years of smugglers' knowledge, packed into one skull, carried by one pair of boots. That was the whole of the Voss guild now. One ledger, crumbling at the spine, with a single name still written in it in ink gone brown as old blood.

Cyrus Voss. Last entry. Final page.

Their grandmother would have wept to see it. Or laughed. Elira Voss had been hard to read even when she'd been alive, and she'd been dead for eleven years now, and the guild had been dying long before that, and Cyrus had stopped mourning things a long time ago because mourning was heavy and they already carried enough weight over these passes to break a mule's back.

Tonight's burden was salt. Forty pounds of Greyhollow white salt sewn into oilcloth and lashed to their shoulders, bound for the shrine village of Emberhold, where the priestesses paid in dragon-scale charms that fetched three times their weight in silver down in the lowland markets. It was small work. Humiliating work, the elders would have said, back when there were elders. But the elders were dead or scattered or worse, and small work was what kept a name fed, and a name was the only inheritance Cyrus had ever been given that couldn't be stolen.

Well. That and the passes themselves. The mountain remembered the Voss family, if no one else did. There were doors in Thornfell that opened to Voss blood and nothing else, though Cyrus hadn't needed one in years, and tried not to think about why the stone still knew them.

*Keep moving*, they told themselves, for the hundredth time since dawn. *Cold is honest. Cold only wants you dead. Everything else wants more than that.*

The wind shifted, and for a moment it dropped—the strange, sudden hush that came to high places when the weather changed its mind—and in that hush Cyrus heard something that did not belong on the Widow's Stitch.

Breathing. Ragged, wet, wrong.

They went still, one boot half-lifted, and let their eyes do the work. The light was failing, that blue-grey hour when the snow lost all depth and shadow, but twenty years of reading mountain dark doesn't leave a person. Fifty paces ahead, tucked into the lee of a split boulder—a place Cyrus had sheltered in a dozen times themselves—there was a shape that wasn't stone.

A person. Small. Curled on their side like something trying to become smaller than itself.

Cyrus's first instinct was the old instinct, the guild instinct, the one that had kept the last Voss alive this long: *walk around*. Not your problem. Not your cargo. Dead men on the passes were common as cairns, and the ones who weren't dead yet were usually bait, and either way they weighed forty pounds of trouble you hadn't budgeted for.

But then the shape moved, and one hand lifted—not reaching, just lifting, the way a drowning man lifts a hand—and Cyrus heard the sound the person made trying to speak, and it was the sound of someone who had been calling for help until their voice broke and then kept calling anyway.

"Damn it," Cyrus said, to the wind, to the mountain, to themselves. "Damn it."

They shrugged the salt off their shoulders and crossed the fifty paces at a run.

* * *

Up close, the courier was younger than Cyrus had expected—younger than Cyrus, certainly, and Cyrus was barely past twenty-five. A girl, maybe nineteen, dressed in the grey-and-iron livery of the Lowcourt post, which meant she was official, which meant she was trouble, because official couriers didn't cross the Widow's Stitch in winter unless they were carrying something worth killing for, and the blood soaking black into the snow beneath her confirmed that somebody had agreed.

She'd been cut. Twice, maybe three times—one long slash across her thigh that had bled through the bandage she'd tried to tie, and something lower, in her belly, that she was keeping her mittens pressed against like she could hold herself together through sheer stubbornness. Her lips were blue. Frost had gotten into her eyebrows and made them white, so her face looked oddly unfinished, like a sketch someone had abandoned.

"Don't," she rasped, when Cyrus knelt. "Don't waste... warmth on me. Listen."

"I can get you down to Emberhold. Shrine village, half a night—"

"Emberhold's burned." She laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up red that steamed in the cold air. "Everything below Kestrel's burning. That's why I came up. Nobody watches the top of the world." Her eyes found Cyrus's face and fixed there with terrible focus, the focus of someone rationing what was left of them. "You're a smuggler. Good. Good. Smugglers know roads that don't exist."

"I know the roads that pay," Cyrus said. "Who cut you?"

"Riders." Something moved behind her eyes at the word—fear, but older than fear, the kind that gets passed down like an heirloom. "Bone masks. You've heard the stories. Everyone's heard the stories, and everyone decides they're stories, right up until—" She stopped. Saved breath. "Listen to me. My name doesn't matter. My cargo does."

"You're not carrying anything. I checked your pack—"

"It's not in the pack." Her hand crawled across the snow, leaving a smeared red line, and found the front of Cyrus's coat, and gripped it with fingers that had no strength left and somehow wouldn't let go. "Here. Under my heart. Where it stays warm."

Cyrus hesitated. Then they pulled open the courier's coat, and there, against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth scorched brown at one corner as if something had very nearly eaten it, was a scroll case of black lacquer, banded in silver, stamped with a seal Cyrus didn't recognize and felt, absurdly, that they should.

"Take it," the girl whispered. "South. Not to the Lowcourt—they sold the road already, I watched them sell it. Take it to..." Her voice failed. She swallowed, tried again. "There's a fire under the mountain. An old fire. The oldest. It's going out, and when it goes out—" Her breath hitched. "When it goes out, everything underneath comes up. Do you understand? The map shows the way down. The map shows the way *down*, and it has to go down before the way closes, and you have to—"

"Slow down. Who are you carrying this for?"

"The mountain," she said, and smiled, and it was the worst thing Cyrus had seen in years, because it wasn't a mad smile. It was relieved. "I was born in Emberhold. The priestesses chose me when I was six. They said I'd carry the last message of the old fire." Her grip tightened. "And here you are. Someone came. Someone actually came."

"I'm not anyone. I'm a salt-runner."

"You're a Voss." Cyrus went rigid. The girl's frost-white brows drew together in something like apology. "The map told me. It... it knows names. It's been whispering yours since I took the case. *Voss. Voss.* Like it was checking." She pushed the lacquered case into Cyrus's hands. Her skin was grey now. "It's drawn by one of yours. I saw the signature when it glowed. Elira."

The name went through Cyrus like a blade finding a gap in armor. Their grandmother's name, spoken by a stranger bleeding out on the coldest pass in Thornfell, attached to a thing that whispered.

"I don't take cargo I don't understand," Cyrus said, and hated how unsteady their voice came out. "That's rule one. Rule one has kept me alive."

"Rule one dies tonight, then." The girl sagged back against the stone. The pressure on her belly had slackened; the snow beneath her had stopped steaming, which meant the blood was cooling, which meant there wasn't much left to cool. "Please. I walked four days with this inside my coat. Four days telling myself someone would come. Don't make it have been for nothing." Her eyes were losing their focus, drifting toward the sky, then dragging back with visible effort. "The riders will come for it. They'll come for whoever holds it. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. That's the whole message, really. *Sorry, and hurry.*"

The wind screamed down the Stitch again, and Cyrus made the mistake of looking away from her face for a single second—just to shield the lacquered case from the blowing snow—and when they looked back, she had turned her head toward the peak of Thornfell, invisible above the storm, and her expression had gone soft and listening, like a child hearing a mother call from another room.

"There," she breathed. "Do you hear it?"

"I hear wind."

"No." Her lips moved around the words like they were too heavy to lift. "Under the wind. The fire. It's so *quiet* now. When I was little it used to hum through the floor of the shrine. The whole mountain hummed. Now it's—" Her hand found Cyrus's wrist and gripped, once, hard, with the last of whatever she was. "Hurry. Please. Before the humming stops."

And then she let go—not just of Cyrus's wrist, but of everything, all at once, the way the dying do, like setting down a load too heavy for any living shoulder. The storm filled the space where her breathing had been.

Cyrus knelt in the snow beside a stranger's body for a long time. Long enough for frost to begin writing its white script along their own collar. They closed the girl's eyes, because someone should, and arranged her hands over the wound in her belly, because someone should, and said the few words the guild used to say over its dead—words Cyrus hadn't spoken aloud in eleven years, and which came out rough and rusted:

"Road's ended. Walk the next one easy."

Then they stood, and took up the lacquered case, and felt it—there was no other word for it—*warm* against their chest, warmer than any lacquer had a right to be, beating faintly like something sleeping.

*Not your cargo,* said the old voice, the survival voice. *Bury her, take your salt, deliver to the shrine—if the shrine stands—forget the whispers, forget the name, forget the fire.*

But the case was warm, and somewhere beneath the wind the mountain had gone quiet in a way mountains shouldn't, and a girl who had walked four days to die had pressed her trust into Cyrus's hands with the last strength in her body, and the last Voss had learned long ago that some weights you don't choose.

Some weights are handed to you by the dying, and after that, putting them down isn't possible. Only carrying them.

Cyrus lashed the salt back onto their shoulders—it seemed obscene to abandon it now, the last ordinary thing in the world—and turned south into the storm.

That was when they saw the lights.

Three of them, high on the eastern ridge, where no road ran and nothing living climbed: pale, swaying points of light like lanterns, moving with the smooth, tireless gait of things that did not need to rest. And even at that distance, even through the screaming wind, Cyrus heard—or imagined they heard, and would spend years deciding which—a sound like bone clicking against bone, keeping time.

Coming down. Coming this way. Coming, without hurry, without mercy, exactly the way hunters come when they already know where the quarry stands.

Cyrus ran.
