## 2. A Stranger's Last Breath

The snow came down the way it always did on the Kestrel side of Thornfell: sideways, mean, and with an opinion about you.

Cyrus Voss pulled the oilskin tighter around the delivery case strapped beneath their coat and kept walking. One foot, then the other. That was the whole of the craft, some days—not the daring cuts through customs lines or the clever false bottoms in wine casks that the old songs liked to sing about, but this: putting one boot in front of the other until the pass was behind you and the coin was in your hand and nobody had died. The guild had a saying for it, back when there was a guild to say things. *The mountain doesn't care if you're brave. It cares if you're stubborn.*

The ledger in Cyrus's memory held that saying in their grandmother's hand, though they'd never met Elira Voss. She'd been twenty years dead by the time Cyrus could read, and the guild—what remained of it—had been dead with her, more or less. What remained was a name on a crumbling page, a network of safehouses that had gone cold one by one like hearths in an abandoned house, and Cyrus. Last heir of nothing much. Carrier of parcels no honest courier would touch.

The delivery today was simple. A sealed case of something heavy and faintly warm, bound for a shrine-keeper at the Greyhollow fork who asked no questions and paid in silver that didn't ask any either. Three days' walk. Two done.

Then the wind shifted, and brought them a smell that didn't belong.

Blood has a particular voice in cold air. It cuts through pine and stone-smell and the iron tang of coming storm, and it speaks a language every smuggler learns before they learn to read. Cyrus stopped mid-stride, boots half-sunk in the drift, and turned their face into the wind like a hound.

There. Off the trail, maybe forty paces down the slope where the scree gave way to a shelf of black rock, a shape lay half-buried in white. A sledge, overturned. And beside it—

"Ah," Cyrus said softly, to no one, because there was never anyone. "No."

Every sensible instinct said walk on. That was the first law of the passes: you don't go toward trouble, you go around it, and you're grateful for the miles. Trouble on the Kestrel Shelf meant riders, or worse, and a lone carrier with contraband against their ribs had no business playing shepherd to anyone's misfortune.

Cyrus went toward it anyway. They'd have liked to pretend, later, that they didn't know why. But they knew. Twenty years of being the last name on a dead ledger taught you exactly what it was to lie bleeding somewhere with no one obliged to come.

* * *

The courier was young. That was the first thing Cyrus registered, kneeling in the snow and brushing the drift away from the still shape—young, younger than Cyrus, with frost in his lashes and a hole in his side that had soaked his grey riding coat black. His eyes were open. They tracked to Cyrus's face with a slowness that meant he was spending what he had left very carefully.

"You're not them," he rasped. His voice was wrecked, scraped raw by cold and screaming, though the pass had been silent as a tomb when Cyrus walked it.

"No," Cyrus agreed. "I'm not them." They were already working at the coat, already pressing their palms flat over the wound out of a habit older than sense. Blood welled between their fingers, shockingly warm. "Who did this?"

The courier's mouth moved. A laugh, maybe, or a cough; with him it was hard to tell the difference. "Bone faces. Came off the ice. Out of nowhere, like—they don't leave tracks, do you know that? No tracks." His hand crawled across the snow, found Cyrus's wrist, gripped it with a strength that surprised them both. "Listen. Listen to me. There isn't time for you to be kind."

"There's always time to be kind," Cyrus said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it. "Let me get you under the shelf, out of the wind. There's a fire-kit in my pack—"

"Stop." The word cracked like a whip, and then he spent a long moment just breathing, wet and labored, while Cyrus knelt in the drift feeling the warmth drain out of him through their fingers. "The case. My case. Under me. Take it."

"I'm not taking your cargo, friend. I'm taking *you*—"

"*Take it.*" And then, softer, with something in his face that broke Cyrus's argument in half: "Please. I carried it three hundred miles. Don't let it be for nothing."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. Snow gathered on their shoulders and in their hair and they didn't move to brush it off. Then they reached beneath the dying man's body, grimacing at the intimacy of it, and worked free the satchel strapped there. It was heavier than it looked. Leather scorched brown-black across one flank, as if it had been held too near a forge—or pulled out of a fire. Something inside shifted with a dry whisper, like paper, like many papers.

"What is it?" Cyrus asked.

"The road," the courier whispered. His eyes had gone strange now, fixed on something past Cyrus's shoulder, up the slope, into the falling white. "The only road. You have to understand—the fire under the mountain is going out. Do you hear me? The fire under the mountain is *going out*, and when it does—" He shuddered. "When it does, everything underneath comes up. Everything. The wards are woven from its burning. Every ward. Every seal. Every door we nailed shut a thousand years ago."

"You're feverish," Cyrus said gently. "You've lost blood. Let me—"

"They'll come for it." His grip tightened on Cyrus's wrist, hard enough to bruise. "They can't take it back. Promise me. Swear it on whatever you love. They cannot have it back."

And here was the thing Cyrus would turn over a thousand times in the years after, lying awake in strange beds: they almost refused. Almost told him the truth, which was that Cyrus Voss carried goods, not causes; that oaths were cargo too heavy for a one-person guild; that the mountain's old fires and old doors were stories for shrine-keepers and children, and Cyrus dealt in the present tense only. The words were right there, lined up and ready.

But the courier's eyes were dark and clear and terribly sane, and he was dying in the snow because he hadn't let go of whatever was in the satchel, and Cyrus Voss had spent twenty years being the last person who'd bother.

"I swear it," Cyrus heard themselves say. "They won't have it back."

Something in the man's face unclenched. He exhaled—a long, rattling sound, like wind leaving a house—and his hand slipped from Cyrus's wrist into the snow.

"For my mother," he murmured. "Tell her I made the crossing. Tell her—" His gaze drifted upward again, and this time it stayed there, fixed on the grey ceiling of the world, and whatever he saw in it, he didn't come back to report.

The wind filled the silence behind him. It always did. That was the thing about these passes; grief had nowhere to echo, so the mountain simply swallowed it, the way it swallowed everything—tracks, voices, names.

Cyrus knelt there longer than was wise. Long enough for the blood in the snow to crust pink at the edges. Long enough for frost to begin its patient work on the young man's open eyes, and Cyrus couldn't bear that, so they closed them with two fingers and murmured the only passing-words the Voss family had ever kept: *"Road's ended, traveler. Walk the one after."*

Grandmother's words, according to Maren. Cyrus had never thought much about them before. They thought about them now, kneeling in the drift with a stranger's warmth still on their hands and a stranger's burden against their ribs, and they felt—for the first time in years—the full strange weight of carrying a dead woman's sentences around inside them like coins in a pocket.

Then the light changed.

* * *

It wasn't much. A thickening in the snowfall upslope, a darkness moving against the white the way smoke moves against flame. Most people wouldn't have marked it. But Cyrus had spent their whole life reading mountains, and mountains spoke first through light.

They went flat against the rock without deciding to, heart slamming, and watched the ridge above the trail.

Three shapes crested it. Horses—or things shaped like horses, too tall in the leg, moving with a smoothness that made Cyrus's stomach drop, because living animals picked their way over scree and these things flowed across it like water finding a level. Their riders sat straight and still. And their faces—

Masks. White as fresh bone, featureless but for narrow eye-slits, and even at this distance, even through the churn of snow, Cyrus felt those slits like points pressed to their own skin. Bone-masked riders. Nursemaid tales. *Be good or the hollow men ride you down and carry you under the hill.* Every child in the shadow of Thornfell grew up on them, and every adult laughed at them over ale, and here they were, real as weather, sweeping the slope below the ridge in a slow, methodical arc.

Looking for something. Looking for someone.

For the courier, Cyrus realized. For the case. For whoever had taken the case.

For Cyrus.

Their mouth went dry as ash. The satchel seemed suddenly to weigh as much as a corpse. *Leave it,* whispered the sensible voice, the survivor's voice, the voice that had kept one last Voss alive through twenty lean years. *Drop it in a drift and walk away. You swore an oath to a dead man, and dead men collect no debts.*

But the courier's eyes had been so clear. *Don't let it be for nothing.*

The lead rider's masked face turned. Slowly. Toward the shelf of rock. Toward the disturbed snow. Toward the small dark shape of a body that should have had a living body beside it, and didn't anymore.

Cyrus moved.

Not along the trail—that was death, open ground and a downhill race against things that didn't tire. Instead they scrambled sideways, low and fast, to where the shelf's lip broke apart into a jumble of fallen slabs, and found what they'd hoped against hope was there: a seam. A crack in the mountain's skirt, barely wider than a shoulders' breadth, breathing out air that smelled of deep stone and old iron.

A smuggler's chute. Grandmother's generation had threaded these slopes with them—escape-ways and drop-shafts, hidden roads for hidden goods. Most had collapsed. This one hadn't, quite.

Cyrus jammed the scorched satchel inside their coat, took one last look at the young man sleeping under the snow—*tell her I made the crossing*—and poured themselves into the dark.

The mountain closed over them like water.

* * *

Falling down a smuggler's chute is not like falling anywhere else. It is controlled, mostly, if you know the trick of it—elbows and knees kissing the walls, weight rolled onto the outer edge of each turn, letting the stone decide most of it and correcting only when the stone gets greedy. Cyrus had learned it at fourteen, in a chute behind Maren's inn, with Aunt Maren shouting technique up the shaft and laughing at every curse that floated back down. They weren't laughing now. The chute went down farther than any they'd known, and darker, and twice it narrowed so cruelly that the satchel caught against the stone and Cyrus hung there in absolute blackness, wedged, listening to their own heartbeat and imagining—vividly, helplessly—bone-white masks tilting over the lip of the shaft far above.

Neither time did masks appear. Neither time did anything follow them down. Whatever the riders were, they did not seem built for crawling into the earth's seams, and that was a small mercy Cyrus clung to like a rope.

At last the shaft spat them out.

They landed badly, in a heap, in a space that rang hollow around them—a waystation, they realized slowly, picking themselves up by feel. An old one. The Voss kind: a chamber cut into the mountain's root, with a cold hearth, a rack gone to rust, and a cache-niche behind a loose stone that Cyrus found by touch alone, because their hands remembered what their mind had filed away. Inside: a stub of tallow candle, a striker, and a clay jar of hardtack that might have been older than Cyrus. Guild stores. Ghost provisions, left by people twenty years dead for travelers who never came.

Cyrus lit the candle. Its little flame stood up trembling, and by its light they saw the room properly—saw the faded chalk marks on the wall, the tally-signs and route-glyphs of a guild that had once owned these mountains the way rivers owned valleys. Someone had drawn a spire on the far wall, long ago, and struck a line through it.

Cyrus sat down against the cold stone with the candle between their feet, and shook. It took a while. They let it. There was no one to see, and shame was a luxury for people with company.

When the shaking finally ebbed, they unslung the satchel and set it in their lap.

Up close, the scorching looked stranger—not random burn, but a pattern, radiating outward from the clasp as if the leather itself had flinched from something inside. The buckle was stamped with a sigil Cyrus didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye shut by a scar. The whispering weight within shifted when they tilted it. Papers. Many papers, folded together, and something else beneath them with a heat of its own—faint, banked, like a coal wrapped in ash.

*The fire under the mountain is going out.*

"Fever-talk," Cyrus said aloud, and the empty room ate the words. Except. Except that the shrine-keeper at Greyhollow had paid double last month for a crate of candles, muttering that the old fires ran short and the winter wards cost more to feed every year. Except that the passes had been colder this season than any Cyrus could remember, cold enough to crack the wine in Maren's cellar, and Maren had said—laughing, but not entirely—*the mountain's getting stingy with its warmth, chick. Old age.*

Except that bone-masked riders had crossed a hundred miles of open country to run down one courier, and riders did not do that for candle crates.

Cyrus worked the clasp open.

Inside lay a map. That was the only word for it, though it was like calling a cathedral a building. Folded vellum, soft as cloth with handling, its edges charred to lace—and drawn across it in ink that seemed, in the candlelight, almost wet: the spine of Thornfell, rendered in lines finer than any cartographer's, and threaded through the mountain's body like veins through a hand, roads. Roads where no roads were. Roads going *down*. Passages bored through the living rock, spiraling toward a mark at the mountain's heart that the mapmaker had rendered not as a symbol but as a small, furious bloom of red-gold pigment that still seemed to pulse, faintly, like something breathing.

And in the corner, in a cramped, confident hand, a title:

*The Ember Road — E.V.*

Cyrus stared at those letters for a long time.

E.V. Elira Voss. The grandmother who was a story, a signature on a ledger, a set of sayings and a grave Cyrus had never visited. The woman who had supposedly drawn the guild's oldest charts, all lost in the fire, all gone, all *gone*—and here was one, scorched but alive, humming with a heat that had nothing to do with any candle, charting roads into the mountain's secret heart in her own unmistakable hand. Cyrus knew the hand. They'd grown up with it on the flyleaves of Maren's books, on the recipe cards, on the single surviving page of the guild ledger that Maren kept wrapped in oilcloth like a relic.

*Elira Voss drew this,* Cyrus thought, and the thought went through them like cold water through cloth. *Elira Voss drew the road under the mountain, and a boy bled out in the snow to bring it to me, and things wearing bone came over the ridge to take it back.*

The candle guttered. In its flicker, for half a heartbeat, Cyrus could have sworn the red bloom at the map's center pulsed—once, slow, like a sleeping heart.

Above, muffled through fathoms of stone, came a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Hooves, or the memory of hooves, moving along the rim of the world.

Cyrus snuffed the candle, folded the map against their heart, and sat in the dark with their knees drawn up, listening to the mountain breathe around them.

Tomorrow they would go to Maren. Tomorrow they would demand answers with this vellum spread on her kitchen table and watch her face while she lied or didn't. Tomorrow they would try to give this burden to someone older and wiser, someone whose name still meant something to somebody.

Tonight, in the black root of the mountain, the last of the Voss hugged a dead stranger's parcel to their chest and admitted the truth they'd been outrunning since the snow changed color:

Nobody else was coming. Whoever this belonged to now—it was theirs.

The hooves faded. The dark stayed. And somewhere far above, under all that snow, a young man's mother waited for news of a crossing her son had made.
