## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

The safehouse was an old Voss cache carved into the shoulder of Wolfjaw Tor, one of the dozen Cyrus still knew the knock-signals for, though they had stopped believing anyone else would ever answer them.

It answered anyway—or something did. The door recognized the rhythm of their knuckles on the cold stone: three quick, two slow, a pause like a held breath, then one more, soft, almost apologetic. The counterweight groaned somewhere inside the rock, and a slab of granite swung inward on greased iron pins that had waited twenty years for use and had not rusted once, because Voss money bought oil from dwarven wells and Voss hands applied it even when there was no one left to protect.

Cyrus stood in the doorway a long moment before entering, snow gathering on their shoulders, the courier's blood stiffening in the creases of their knuckles. The mountain breathed out at them—a smell of tallow gone waxy with age, of cedar lining, of dust that had been undisturbed so long it had become a kind of silence made solid.

"Hello," Cyrus said to the dark, because it seemed rude not to, and because saying it aloud made the place real in a way that thinking it could not. "I'm the last one. Sorry about the mess."

The mess was themselves: soaked to the knee, half-frozen, carrying a dead man's map and a dead man's last words rattling around inside their skull like a coin in a tin cup. *The fire under the mountain.* Four words. The kind of thing a dying smuggler says when he wants you to carry more than cargo.

They got the door shut behind them and lit the lamp that hung by habit of the hand beside the entrance—still full, still good, the wick trimmed by whoever had maintained this place through all the years the guild supposedly didn't exist. That was a thought Cyrus set down carefully and did not pick up again. There would be time for wondering who had kept the caches oiled. First there was firelight, and dry wool from the stores, and the small brutal mercy of warming their fingers until the ache became a sting and the sting became nothing.

The cache was deeper than most: two chambers, the outer for goods, the inner for people. Someone had slept here recently enough that the bedroll bore the impression of a body. Someone had eaten here—the remains of hardtack in a tin, a spoon laid neat as an offering. Cyrus stood over those small evidences and felt the hair rise along their arms. Not fear exactly. Something older and stranger. The sensation of walking into a room where a conversation has just ended, where the air still holds its shape.

*You're being haunted by housekeeping,* they told themselves, and unpacked the map.

They had wrapped it in the courier's cloak, because it seemed wrong to let it touch bare snow, and because the scorched vellum had felt warm against their chest the whole way down from the pass, warm in a way that leather shouldn't be, warm like a bird held too tight. Now, by lamplight, they unrolled it across the cedar table and weighted the corners with candlesticks and a tin of boot-blacking and sat looking at it while the fire in the little stove ticked and settled.

At first glance it was only what it appeared: a chart of the Thornfell country, drawn with the economy of someone who had walked every line of it. The passes were right. The false trails were right—the little dotted paths that led nowhere, the decoy camps marked with the old guild sign, a thorn crossed by a key. Whoever drew it had known the trade the way a shepherd knows his hills: not as lines on paper but as weather, as danger, as the smell of a slope after rain.

Then Cyrus looked closer at the ink, and the warmth under their sternum flared like a swallowed coal.

The roads into the mountain should not have existed.

Every child raised in the shadow of Thornfell learned the catechism early, usually from a grandmother or a frightened aunt: the mountain is sealed, the mountain is sleeping, the mountain is watched. The Undercourt beneath it was a story told to keep children away from cave mouths, and like most stories meant to frighten children, it worked precisely because no adult would quite deny it. The courts above—the Winter Court at Greyhollow, the river lords, the crown's magistrates—had fought their border skirmishes and signed their treaties, but none of them claimed any road into Thornfell itself. The mountain's gates were stone. Its passages were legend. Its heart belonged to the dragon, and the dragon's fire belonged to the wards, and the wards belonged to nobody living.

And yet here, in fine brown-black lines that seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it, was a road that began at a goat-track above the Kestrel Shelf and went *in*. It threaded past a marker labeled in a tiny, precise hand—*the weeping gate; do not sing near it*—and continued down, and down, the cartographer's confidence never faltering, through junctions marked with symbols Cyrus did not know and warnings written in margins so narrow the letters leaned sideways like people crowding a doorway. Here: *the halls shift at moonset; sleep before you walk.* Here: *trade nothing. answer nothing. if a door offers you rest, it is lying.* Here, in ink so faded it was nearly breath: *I am sorry.*

Cyrus read that last line four times.

The stove popped. Outside, wind worried the tor's stony scalp, and somewhere below in the valley a wolf said something brief and bitter to another wolf. Cyrus sat very still, the way they'd learned to sit in hedgerows and customs sheds and the backs of wagons while soldiers counted barrels two feet away—stillness as discipline, stillness as prayer—and tried to make the handwriting resolve into anything other than what it was.

It was Elira Voss's hand.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. *It was hers*, the whole architecture of it, the tall narrow capitals with their slight forward lean, as if every word she wrote were leaning into the wind of her own hurry. Cyrus knew that hand the way they knew their own heartbeat, which is to say intimately and mostly without gratitude. Their grandmother had died when they were nine, but she had left behind eleven years of letters, ledger entries, labels on jam jars, notes tucked into the pockets of coats passed down twice before they fit. She had taught Cyrus their letters by writing smuggling routes in flour on the table and letting the child trace them with one finger. *This is the way to Marrow Ford. This is the way home.*

Cyrus had burned most of those letters in the first raw year after the guild fell—not from grief but from fury, the particular fury of the young who inherit debts they never agreed to. They regretted it now with a completeness that felt like losing the person a second time.

But the hand. The hand remained. And it was on this map, in passages no living soul claimed to know, describing doors that offered rest and lied about it, apologizing in a margin for sins Cyrus could not yet name.

"You," Cyrus said quietly to the map, "are going to be trouble."

The vellum did not disagree. If anything—if such a thing were possible—it seemed to settle under their gaze the way a cat settles when finally acknowledged, the faintest tension going out of its surface, the scorched edge near the courier's blood cooling by a degree Cyrus could feel through their fingertips and could not prove.

They did what Vosses did with trouble. They catalogued it.

The outer roads were conventional, masterful, unremarkable except for their accuracy. It was the inner network that made the skin crawl: a spider's web of ways beneath the massif, converging—Cyrus traced it with one finger, not touching the ink, close but never touching—converging on a single point deep under the peak. There the cartographer had drawn a symbol like a flame inside a circle, and around it a ring of smaller marks that might have been script or might have been teeth. No label. Every other feature on the map, however terrible, however impossible, bore some instruction, some warning, some wry aside in that leaning hand. The center of the web alone was silent.

*The fire under the mountain,* the courier had said. Blood on his teeth, snow melting red around him, his hand crushing Cyrus's wrist with the last strength of a man spending himself entirely in one motion. *She'll want it back. Tell her—I kept my word.*

*Her.* He hadn't said Maren. Maren was an aunt by marriage and rumor, not blood, and the courier had been dying, and dying men are careless with pronouns. But Cyrus had carried the word down the mountain in the same pocket as the map, and now both came out together, and lay on the table between the candlesticks, and refused to be separated.

Elira Voss had been dead twenty years. But the map was warm, and the ink was fresh in places, and the apology in the margin looked to Cyrus's trained eye—trained on seals, on forged customs stamps, on the subtle dating of documents—like it had been written within the decade.

Maren it was, then. Maren, who ran the inn at Coldharrow crossroads three days' south, who had taken Cyrus in after the fall with a hardness that was almost kindness, who had never once asked what Cyrus carried or where, because asking questions was how Vosses died and Maren Voss—Maren by marriage, Maren the widow, Maren who had married into the family and stayed after everyone else fled or perished—knew better than most how the family ended.

Three days south. Through snow. With bone-masked riders somewhere behind them on the high passes, riders whose masks Cyrus had seen only for a heartbeat before dropping down the smuggler's chute with the map crushed against their chest—but a heartbeat had been enough. White bone, carved smiling. Eyes that were not eyes but hollows with a light in them like the far end of a tunnel.

They banked the stove, ate hardtack that tasted of the years it had waited, and spread their bedroll in the inner chamber. Sleep came the way it always did for smugglers: suddenly, completely, like falling off a ledge into black water.

* * *

The dream was of flour on a tabletop.

Big capable hands drawing a road in white powder, and a child's finger following, and a voice like smoke and honey: *This is the way under, little spark. Learn it well, because someday the mountain will need to know your name.* Laughter then, the child laughing because grandmothers say strange things and children laugh to fill the gaps in understanding. The flour-road curved. It went down and down, past the weeping gate, past the lying doors, toward a center where something burned.

Cyrus woke with their hand outflung, palm flat against the floor of the inner chamber, fingers splayed the way you splay them to stop a fall. The lamp in the outer room had guttered low. And—

They heard it before they understood it: a sound like a page turning. Soft. Patient. From the outer chamber, where the map lay weighted under candlesticks on the cedar table.

Cyrus rose without lighting anything. Twenty years of moving quiet in bad places lived in their knees and ankles, and they crossed the threshold into the outer dark with breath held shallow, one hand finding the long knife they slept with the way other people slept with prayers.

Moonlight came through the ventilation slit, thin and blue, and lay across the table in a bright bar. The map was open on it. Cyrus had rolled it closed before sleeping—they were certain, certain as they were of their own knocking-signal, certain as they were of the taste of hardtack. The map was open. And in the blue light, the fine brown-black ink of the inner roads was moving.

Not flowing. Not crawling, nothing so vulgar. It was *settling*, the way water settles, the way a sleeper turns toward warmth: lines easing a fraction, a junction refining itself, a warning in the margin sliding a quarter-inch nearer the road it described—as though the map were a living thing adjusting its blankets. And along every hidden passage, faint as heat-shimmer, the roads glowed. Not all of them. Only those nearest the point where the goat-track above Kestrel Shelf descended—the route Cyrus had come by. The routes radiating outward from *them*.

The warmth in their chest, the one they'd been pretending since the pass was only memory and fright, pulsed once, in time with the glow.

Cyrus's hand was still on the knife. Slowly, feeling foolish and ancient and very young all at once, they reached out and touched the corner of the vellum.

The settling stopped. The glow dimmed to embers. The map became, in every observable particular, a piece of scorched parchment on a table in a cold room, and the silence came back so thoroughly that Cyrus's own pulse sounded loud in it.

They stood there until the shivering started, and then they laughed—one short, ugly bark of a sound, swallowed at once by the stone.

"All right," they whispered to their dead grandmother's handwriting, to the mountain overhead with its sleeping fires, to the courier whose blood was still under their fingernails no matter how they scrubbed. "All right. I hear you. I don't believe you. But I hear you."

They rolled the map closed, tied it with cord, and bound the roll against their chest where the warmth could do whatever it was doing without supervision. Then they sat by the rekindled stove until dawn came gray through the vent slit, drinking tea made from pine needles and watching the door, and making lists the way their grandmother had taught them: things known, things suspected, things feared. The first list was short. The third was getting longer by the hour.

Things known: the map was Elira's. The roads were real or the ink thought it was. Bone riders wanted it badly enough to kill a courier in open snow.

Things suspected: the courier had meant Maren. The caches had been maintained. Somebody had survived the fall of the guild and chosen silence over flight, and had kept oiled hinges and trimmed wicks for twenty years like a vigil.

Things feared: that the map was warm because it knew them. That the roads glowed toward them the way flowers turn toward light. That somewhere in the margins of their grandmother's careful hand, in the spaces between warnings about lying doors and singing stones, was the explanation of what the Voss family had sold, and to whom, and why every road on that map led down.

When the light was strong enough to travel by, Cyrus packed the cache to rights, left coin in the tin where the hardtack had been—an old courtesy, payment for use, the guild's one unbending law even now that the guild was one person and a ghost—and knocked the closing signal on the doorframe: two slow, three quick, and the final knock soft as forgiveness.

Three days south to Coldharrow. Three days of open snow, easy sightlines, and the memory of white bone smiles.

They stepped out into morning. The wind had turned; it came up from the south now, off Coldharrow and the low country, and it carried a smell that did not belong to winter at all. Smoke. Woodsmoke, and under it something sharper, chemical, wrong—the smell of a burn pile, of a torch applied to timber on purpose.

Cyrus stood on the tor's shoulder with the wind pulling at their coat and felt the map warm against their sternum, patient as a debt.

"Maren," they said aloud, to no one, to the smoke, and started south at a pace that was not yet running but remembered how.
