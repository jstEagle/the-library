## 4. The Guild That Isn't

The road to Maren's inn was a road Cyrus had walked a hundred times and hated a hundred times more than any other.

It wound up out of the Greyhollow lowlands through country that had once been Voss country — before the fall, before the fire, before the name became something people said in the lowered voice reserved for the drowned and the disgraced. Now it was just poor land: terraces gone to bracken, shepherd huts with their roofs caved in like cupped hands that had given up holding anything, dry-stone walls running over the hills in long gray stitches that no one had tended in twenty years. Twenty years exactly. Cyrus had done the arithmetic on that more than once, walking this road, and had never liked where the sum came out.

Snow clung in the hollows. The morning was the color of pewter, and the wind came down off Thornfell's shoulders smelling of iron and old ice, the way it always did when the mountain's mood turned. Somewhere above the cloud line, the peak sat under its perpetual haze of warmth — travelers swore you could see the dragon's glow on clear nights, a red seam along the summit like a banked hearth seen through a keyhole. Cyrus didn't look up at it. Looking up felt too much like asking questions.

They walked with the courier's satchel against their spine, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in a spare shirt, wrapped a third time in denial. They had not opened it since the safehouse. They had not needed to. It had been heavy enough just to carry.

*The fire under the mountain,* the dying man had said. *Tell them it's still owed.*

Cyrus hadn't told anyone anything. Telling meant naming what they carried, and naming things made them real, and Cyrus Voss had survived twenty years by keeping everything — debts, feelings, family history — folded small and sewn into a pocket no one else knew existed.

But there was one person alive who would know what that handwriting was.

* * *

Maren's inn stood where the pass road split toward the Kestrel Shelf, a squat stone building with a slate roof gone green at the edges and a signboard that read THE LAST REST in paint so faded it might as well have said nothing at all. Smoke rose from the chimney, thin and steady. A mule dozed in the yard, hip-shot against the fence post. Everything about the place announced itself as ordinary with the desperate emphasis of something that had practiced being ordinary for two decades.

Cyrus knew better. They had grown up in the cellar of that ordinariness.

The kitchen door opened onto warmth and the smell of onions and bay leaf. Maren Voss — Maren Halloran now, though she'd stopped correcting people years ago because the correction itself drew attention — stood at the great hearth with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her forearms scalded pink from a lifetime of lifting pots she never let anyone else touch. She was a broad woman built like the doorframe of a keep, gray shot through her black hair like frost working into stone, and she did not turn around when the door opened.

"Back door," she said, without looking. "I've told you. Front doors are for customers."

"Hello to you too, Aunt."

That got her around. Her face did the thing it always did when she saw Cyrus — a quick, unguarded softening, there and gone like a fish rising, smoothed over immediately by the practical frown she wore like armor. She wiped her hands on her apron twice, which meant she'd been nervous before Cyrus even arrived.

"You look half-froze."

"I'm fine."

"You're never fine. Sit." She pointed at the bench by the fire with a wooden spoon as if it were a scepter. "There's stew. Don't tell me what's in the saddlebag, I don't want to know, and if it's spirits we'll drink one bottle and you'll carry the rest out before dark."

"It's not spirits."

"Good." She ladled stew without further ceremony, set the bowl down hard enough that broth jumped the rim, and only then looked at Cyrus properly — the long look, the one that went from boots to hairline and took inventory like a customs officer. Whatever she found there made her frown deepen. "You've been in the high passes."

"Routine run."

"In winter? Alone?" She sat down across from them, which she almost never did; Maren ate standing, worked standing, slept sitting up in the chair by the hearth like a dog that couldn't quite trust the house. "Cyrus. Look at me. What happened up there?"

And there it was — the whole problem with Maren, laid out in one question. She saw too much. She always had. When Cyrus was nine and came home with a split lip and a lie ready, Maren had taken one look and said, *Who hit you, and how much are they owed*, and had gone out that night and settled it herself in ways Cyrus wasn't shown until years later. She was not a woman you lied to successfully. She was a woman you lied to briefly.

"A man died in the snow," Cyrus said. Because there was no point building anything smaller on top of it; Maren would dig through to the foundation anyway. "Courier. Bone riders ran him down on the Shelf. He gave me something before he went."

The spoon in Maren's hand stopped moving.

She didn't drop it. She set it down — very carefully, very quietly, the way you put down something fragile when your hands aren't steady enough to trust — and Cyrus watched the color leave her face the way water leaves a lock when the sluice opens. Not all at once. In stages. First the warmth went out of her cheeks, then the color, then something older and deeper underneath both.

"What kind of something," she said.

"You know what kind."

"Cyrus—"

"It's a map, Aunt." Cyrus kept their voice level. It cost effort. "A burned map. And it's written partly in Gran's hand."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the kettle to go cold on its hook. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. The mule in the yard stamped once and thought better of it.

Maren stood. Walked to the window. Stood there with her back to Cyrus, arms folded, looking at nothing — or at something twenty years away, which amounted to the same thing. When she finally spoke, her voice had the flatness of a road after a rockfall: passable, but only just.

"The Voss guild died twenty years ago," she said. "Burned out. Every ledger seized, every route closed, every name scattered or buried. There is no guild. There is an inn, a mule, and a niece who should know better than to bring ghost-paper into my kitchen."

"That's what everyone says. Dead twenty years. You all say it like it's liturgy." Cyrus pushed the stew away untouched. "Dead guilds don't get mourned in secret. Dead guilds don't leave widows who flinch at the word *map*. Something killed it, Aunt, and whatever it was, it isn't finished, because last night bone riders chased a man to death trying to take this thing back. So either you tell me what happened, or I walk out that door and go find out the stupid way, alone, and you can spend the rest of your life standing at this window wondering which snowdrift I'm under."

Maren's shoulders moved. It might have been a breath. It might have been the beginning of a laugh that died somewhere in the climb.

"You sound like her," she said quietly. "Your gran. Same way of cornering a person. Same bloody patience about it."

"Maren."

"Don't 'Maren' me." She turned then, and her eyes were wet and furious in equal measure, and she jabbed a finger at Cyrus like she could pin them to the bench. "Do you know what I gave up to make sure you'd never have to ask these questions? I changed my name. I buried my ledgers. I learned to cook — *cook*, Cyrus, I spent ten years learning to make bread that would bore a magistrate to tears, because boring is safe, because invisible is alive. Your grandmother sold everything we were so that you could grow up dull. And you come through my door smelling of high snow with a dead man's parcel on your back, asking me to dig up the grave we paid for in blood."

"Then tell me what it cost," Cyrus said. "Because someone's collecting on it, and I'd rather hear it from family."

Something in that landed. Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every one of her fifty-odd years and several she hadn't reached yet, and when she crossed the kitchen it was slowly, like a woman wading upstream, and she held out her hand.

"Show me."

* * *

Cyrus unwrapped it at the scrubbed table, three layers deep, and the Ember Map came out into the lamplight like a wound being uncovered.

Up close, in good light, it was worse and stranger than it had been in the safehouse. The vellum was scarred with old burns — not random ones; the scorch marks followed the ink, as if the roads themselves had once caught fire and been put out badly. And threaded through the cartographer's careful script, unmistakable to anyone who had ever received a birthday letter, ran Elira Voss's handwriting: slanted, impatient, gorgeous. The same hand that had labeled Cyrus's childhood drawings of dragons *ASPIRATIONAL*. The same hand that had signed the last letter anyone in the family ever got from her, before she went up the mountain one autumn and did not come back down.

Maren looked at it for perhaps four heartbeats.

Then she crossed the kitchen in three strides, swept the lamp off the table, and thrust it into the hearth.

"No—" Cyrus was up, grabbing her wrist, and the two of them froze in a tableau — Maren straining toward the fire, Cyrus hauling her back, the lamp swinging wild shadows across the walls.

"You don't understand what that is," Maren hissed. Her grip, even caught off balance, was iron. "Twenty years, Cyrus. Twenty years I have waited for that paper to come back through my door, and I prayed every winter it never would. Burn it. Burn it now, burn it in the big fire, and salt the ash, and we never speak of—"

"You *knew*." It came out of Cyrus lower than they intended, rough as gravel. "You knew it existed. You knew she made it. You told me she died owing nothing — you told me the guild fell apart over a customs dispute, you told me—" Their voice cracked and they hated it, hated the nine-year-old in it. "What did she sell, Maren?"

Maren went still.

Not calm-still. The stillness of a woman hearing, after two decades, the exact knock she'd been dreading. Her eyes went to the map, to the hearth, to the shuttered window, to the map again — doing sums Cyrus couldn't see, and arriving at an answer she clearly despised.

"Not here," she whispered. "God help us, not with the shutters open and the kettle warm. Downstairs. And if I tell you, you swear to me — swear on her name, on whatever of her you actually loved — that you'll listen all the way through before you decide I'm a coward."

"I already think you're a coward."

"Good. Then my honesty will be a pleasant surprise." She pulled the trapdoor ring in the corner of the pantry floor, and cold air breathed up out of the dark, smelling of earth and old apples and something faintly mineral, like the inside of the mountain itself. "Bring the map. If it's going to get us killed either way, it can at least be useful first."

* * *

The cellar was older than the inn. Everyone in the valley said so — that the Halloran inn's foundations predated the village, predated the road, maybe predated the crown, and that some of the stones in the deepest wall were dressed in a style no mason living could match. Cyrus had played down here as a child among the barrels and preserves, and had always vaguely wondered why one wall was bricked up where a doorway clearly used to be, and why Maren never let lamps burn near it.

Now Maren lit a single candle, set it on a barrel head, and stood facing that bricked-up doorway with her arms wrapped around herself.

"Your grandmother didn't just draw roads," she said. "She was the guild's roadfinder. Best there ever was. She could feel a pass before it opened and smell a tollgate three valleys off. The elders used to joke she had mountain blood." A pause. "They weren't joking. They just didn't know it."

"Maren—"

"When the guild got desperate — the year the crown tariffs broke us, the year we lost the eastern routes — the elders sent word down. Into the mountain. Under Thornfell." Her voice had gone thin and careful, like someone crossing ice. "There's a court down there, Cyrus. Old. Hungry. They trade in things that shouldn't be tradable, and they'd been sealed behind wards for longer than anyone remembers, and those wards are fed by the dragon's fire at the summit. The elders asked them for a road. A road no soldier could find, no taxman could follow. And the court agreed. For a price."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"They wanted a soul," Maren said. "A Voss soul. Freely given, freely signed. And Elira — Elira, who could have refused, who the elders had no right to ask — Elira looked at the guild books, looked at the forty families on the payroll, and signed herself away. Her name into their ledger. Her soul held in trust below, due and payable when the contract matured." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Cyrus had ever heard her make. "The road came. The guild survived. The pardons came through within the season — every elder's name cleared, every debt forgiven. Funny how easily the crown forgave us, once certain parties below had spoken to certain parties above. We bought our lives with her, Cyrus. All of us. The guild, the elders, the pardon — everything we were for twenty years was bought with your grandmother's soul."

Cyrus heard their own breathing. It sounded very loud in the stone room.

"And then?"

"And then the elders panicked." Maren's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Once the road was open, they realized what they'd done — not the sin of it, mind, they never lost sleep over that — the *risk* of it. Because a contract signed in blood doesn't die with the signer. It runs in the line. And if the court ever came to collect early — if the dragon's fire ever failed, if the wards thinned — the debt would fall on Elira's heirs. On the next Voss. On *you*."

"So they killed the guild."

"They erased it. Scattered the families, burned the true ledgers, spread the story of the customs dispute. Changed names. Buried routes. Made the Voss name mean *dead* so thoroughly that no one below would bother looking for heirs." Her jaw tightened. "I kept the inn because someone had to keep watch over the last of the family. That's the truth of it. That's the whole ugly truth, and now you have it, and now you understand why that paper has to burn."

Cyrus looked down at the map in their hands.

At the burned roads. At the patient, slanted handwriting. At the small annotation in the border margin they hadn't been able to read by safehouse lamplight and could suddenly, horribly, read now, by candlelight, in a cellar under a bricked-up door: *For whoever comes after. I'm sorry it has to be you.*

"She knew," Cyrus said. "She knew they'd try to erase us. She hid the map so the road would survive even if the name didn't."

"Yes."

"And the courier. The bone riders. The fire going out under the mountain." Cyrus lifted their eyes to their aunt's face. "The contract's maturing, isn't it. The dragon's dying. They've come to collect the next signature."

Maren didn't answer. She didn't have to. Her silence was answer enough, and it was worse than any confession, because in it Cyrus could hear twenty years of vigilance collapsing all at once — the inn, the mule, the boring bread, the changed name, all of it a dam built against exactly this night, and the night had come anyway.

"Burn it," Maren said again, gently now, pleading. "Please. Let the road die with the debt. Let them have no heir to find. Disappear, tonight, and I'll—"

Above them, faint but unmistakable through the floorboards, the yard gate creaked.

Neither of them moved. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

Hooves. More than one set. Slow, unhurried, deliberate — the pace of creatures that had never once needed to hurry, and the sound of them on frozen mud made a wrong note that Cyrus felt in their teeth, a rhythm just slightly off from anything living.

Maren blew out the candle.

"Cellar tunnel," she breathed, pressing the map hard against Cyrus's chest with one hand while the other found the loose brick behind the apple barrels. "Behind the wall. It comes up in the gully past the sheepfold. Run, and don't stop until—"

The front door of the inn, upstairs, opened with a sound like a sigh.

Someone knocked politely, twice, on a door they had already opened.

"Cyrus," Maren whispered, and shoved them into the dark.
