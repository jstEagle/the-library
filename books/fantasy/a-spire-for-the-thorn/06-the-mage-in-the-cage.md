## 6. The Mage in the Cage

Greyhollow smelled of wet stone and old fish, the way all market towns did when the mountain's shadow swallowed them by mid-afternoon. Cyrus pulled the hood lower and moved with the current of bodies flowing between the stalls, one more tired traveler among hundreds. Three days since Maren's inn had burned. Three days of sleeping in culverts and eating hard bread bought with coins counted twice. The map lay against their chest beneath two layers of wool, warm from their own body heat, which was either comfort or curse—Cyrus hadn't decided yet.

The market sprawled through a natural gorge where the Greywater fell in tiers, its terraces carved into the cliff face like the ribs of some enormous drowned beast. Lamplighters were already working their way down the switchbacks, and the smell of frying onions and tarred rope mingled into something almost like home. Almost. Home had burned to ash on Maren's doorstep.

Cyrus bought nothing. Watched everything.

It was the cages that stopped them.

They stood at the market's lowest tier, near the water, where the fog came off the falls thick enough to drink. Six of them, iron-barred, wheeled like carts, each bearing the seal of the Undercourt's bounty office—a circle bisected by a descending line, stamped in wax so black it looked like a hole punched in the world. Four held men who stared out with the flat resignation of those already sold. One was empty, its door hanging open as if whatever it had contained had been taken early.

The sixth held a woman.

She sat cross-legged in the center of her cage as though she'd chosen the spot, hands resting on her knees, eyes closed. Her hair was cropped short and uneven—prisoner's shears—and her wrists bore the telltale silver rings that suppressed a mage's craft. But what caught Cyrus's attention, what made their breath go shallow behind the fishmonger's stall, were the marks on her skin.

They started at her throat and spiraled outward across her collarbones, down her arms—fine lines of ink that moved. Not tattooed. *Written.* Cyrus could see them even at this distance, even in the failing light: characters crawling over her like ants made of midnight, rearranging themselves in slow, patient patterns. The same hand. The same impossible script.

The same handwriting as the map folded against Cyrus's heart.

"You're staring," said the fishmonger, not unkindly, as he wrapped a mackerel in newsprint for another customer. "Don't. She's marked for the deep delivery. Court mage gone wrong, they say. Threw in with something under the hill." He spat over his shoulder, superstitious. "They'll take her down at moonrise when the tithe-wagon comes."

"Down where?" Cyrus asked, though they knew. Gods help them, they knew exactly where.

The fishmonger shrugged. "Wherever such as her belong. Not our business. Nothing's our business in Greyhollow—that's how we keep our roofs."

Cyrus moved on. Kept moving. Told themselves every step away from the cages was a step toward sense: find a room, find the north road, be gone before the tithe-wagon came. The map against their chest pulsed faintly, or imagined itself pulsing, and Cyrus thought of Maren's face in the firelight, pale as whey: *Burn it, child. Whatever it costs you, burn it.*

Instead they found themselves standing at the edge of the lamplight, watching the cage, watching the woman breathe, watching the ink crawl its patient circuits over her skin like a message trying very hard to be read.

The moon would rise within the hour. The wagon would come. And Cyrus Voss, last heir of a dead guild, who had sworn on Maren's scorched doorstep never to be responsible for anyone ever again, heard their own voice say, quietly, to no one:

"Well. Damn it all anyway."

* * *

Wren had counted four hundred and eleven heartbeats since the last time someone had looked at her with anything other than fear or appetite.

She kept count because counting was a discipline, and discipline was all the craft they'd left her. The silver rings on her wrists drank her magic the way sand drank rain—not entirely, never entirely, but enough. Enough that she couldn't burn the lock. Enough that she couldn't whisper the bars soft as willow. Enough that all she could do was sit in this cage by the roaring falls and practice the oldest discipline of all, the one they taught first at the Court Above and last forgot:

*Endure.*

The ink helped. Her own work, inked into her skin over seven years, sigils of small mercies: one to dull pain, one to slow hunger, one to hold her mind still when the rings tried to make it fog. They crawled their rounds across her collarbones like faithful hounds. She fed them a thread of will each hour, thin as spider silk, and they asked no more than that. Good servants. Better company than she'd had in months.

She'd known, of course. From the moment the bounty officers dragged her through this market in chains, she'd felt it—the wrongness under the town, the pull of the deep ways beneath the gorge. Greyhollow sat atop an old artery of the Undercourt; she could feel the mountain's pulse through the floor of her cage, slow and vast and *hungry*. Moonrise would bring the tithe-wagon, and the wagon went down, and nothing that went down came back up speaking its own name.

Unless.

Her eyes had been closed, but her ears were open, and ears were a mage's second-best instrument. For the past quarter hour, someone had been circling her cage. Careful footsteps. A body that knew how to move unseen—weight rolling heel to toe, pauses timed to the crowd's noise. A smuggler's walk, if Wren had ever heard one, and she had, in another life, in another court, reviewing the testimony of captured guild-runners whose sentences she'd been asked to sharpen into legal instruments of the crown.

Then the footsteps stopped, and a voice said, quietly, to no one: "Well. Damn it all anyway."

Wren opened her eyes.

The stranger stood at the edge of the lamplight—young, hooded, sharp-faced, with the kind of stillness that came from years of being hunted rather than hunting. Their gaze met hers through the bars, and Wren saw the exact moment recognition struck them: not of her face—they'd never met—but of the marks on her skin.

Their hand went, involuntarily, to their own chest.

*There,* Wren thought, and let a slow breath move through her, careful not to show it. *You carry it. Whatever it is, you carry it against your heart.*

She spoke without moving her lips much—a habit learned in cells. "You have questions," she said. "I can see them from here. Ask the one that matters."

The hooded figure glanced around—the market emptying now, lamplighters gone, only the falls' roar and a pair of bounty officers dicing by a brazier thirty feet off. When the stranger spoke again, their voice was low and roughened, genderless by design. "You know these marks."

"I wrote them." Wren unfolded herself and rose, and the silver rings sang their thin note of suppression as she moved, but she was used to singing along with it. "Seven years of work. Every one of them earned." She tilted her head. "And I know that look. You've seen my handwriting somewhere it shouldn't be. Somewhere that frightened you."

The stranger's jaw tightened. "If I say yes?"

"Then we have perhaps half an hour before the tithe-wagon arrives, and you can decide whether that fright is worth doing something reckless." Wren let her gaze drop pointedly to the lock. "Or whether you'll sleep tonight knowing you watched them take me down the hill, and spent the rest of your life wondering what I'd have told you."

"That's manipulation," the stranger said.

"That's arithmetic," Wren said. "Manipulation is prettier."

For three full breaths the stranger didn't move. Then they swore again, softly, with feeling—the oath of someone raised around dockworkers and thieves, fluent in profanity as a mother tongue—and stepped into the lamplight.

* * *

Cyrus worked the lock picks with hands that refused to shake, mostly through stubbornness. Behind them, the woman—*Wren*, she'd given as her name, offered like a coin she suspected might be counterfeit—kept up a low murmur of commentary.

"Third pin. Feel for the give."

"I know how to pick a lock."

"Mm. Then you know the tumbler's been salted. Bounty offices do that—crude warding in the mechanism. Touch it wrong and it shrieks loud enough to wake the gorge." A pause. "Your third pin is currently touching it wrong."

Cyrus eased the pin back, sweating. "How can you possibly know that?"

"Because I'm looking at it." And there was something in her voice then—not arrogance, but a strange, weary certainty—as though the warding were written somewhere only she could read. "Silver sings to me even through these rings. Everything warded does. It's a small talent. Useless at court, invaluable in a cage." Her mouth curved, dry. "Which the court presumably never considered."

The lock gave with a click soft as a snapped thread. Cyrus exhaled and straightened, flexing cramped fingers. "Right. Can you run?"

"I can walk quickly with dignity."

"Wonderful. We're going to die."

"Not yet." Wren stepped out of the cage, and for a moment the moving ink on her skin seemed to brighten, drinking the lamplight, and Cyrus felt the map against their chest answer—a warmth, a tug, like a compass needle finding north. Wren's eyes widened fractionally. She'd felt it too. "Interesting," she breathed. "Very interesting. Later. Run now, wonder later."

They ran.

Not far—the brazier-dicing officers looked up at the sound of footfalls, and one shouted, and then the night filled with whistles, the ugly cascading whistles of a market town raising its watch. Cyrus took Wren's wrist and hauled her left, away from the main stairs, toward a memory: the fish-cleaning gutters, the drainage chutes, the smuggler's geography every Voss child learned with their letters. Down through the reek of offal, over a wall slick with fish scale, into a maintenance tunnel where the Greywater thundered close enough to soak them both to the bone.

Wren laughed once—a startled, involuntary sound, half terror and half delight—as Cyrus shouldered open a hatch that hadn't been opened in years. "You *know* this place."

"Grew up in places like it." Cyrus wedged the hatch shut behind them and dropped the bar. Above, muffled by stone and water, the whistles continued, furious and aimless. "Every market town on the mountain has a second skeleton. Smugglers built half of Greyhollow's cellars. My family—" They stopped. Swallowed the word *family* like a stone.

"Your family," Wren prompted, when the silence had gone on too long.

"The dead ones," Cyrus said, "built the rest."

They stood in the dark of the tunnel, dripping, breathing hard, the roar of the falls filling the world. Then Wren reached out and touched Cyrus's chest—lightly, a question asked with fingertips—and Cyrus, exhausted and grieving and past caring, nodded.

The map came out into the thin light of Cyrus's shuttered lantern, and Wren went still.

Not the stillness of shock. The stillness of a scholar meeting a text she'd only read about in forbidden chapters—hunger and horror holding each other perfectly balanced. Her fingers hovered above the parchment without touching, tracing the air above roads that shifted as Cyrus watched, faint gold threads crawling like the ink on her own skin.

"Living ink," she whispered. "Bound-script. I've seen fragments. Pages in the court archives that were locked behind three wards and a law." Her eyes lifted to Cyrus's face. "This is a whole chart. Do you understand what this is? This doesn't just *show* the Undercourt's roads—it *negotiates* with them. It redraws as the halls shift. It's alive."

"It's my grandmother's," Cyrus said. "Elira Voss. Her handwriting. I'd know it anywhere—I learned my letters copying her ledger-books." Their voice came out flatter than they intended. "And the Undercourt wants it badly enough to burn my aunt's inn and hunt me across three passes. So either you tell me what it is, mage, or you tell me why I shouldn't leave you in this tunnel."

Wren didn't flinch. She looked at the map a long moment, and then at Cyrus, and something in her expression shifted—the wary arithmetic giving way, briefly, to something rawer.

"Because," she said quietly, "the court marked me for the Undercourt because I spent seven years studying soul-contracts. Because I asked one question too many about what happens to the names the deep court collects. Because I tried to unmake one of those contracts once, and it cost—" She stopped. Started again. "Because I am, as far as I know, the only person alive who can read living bound-script without it reading me back. And because you just broke a bounty-office lock for a stranger in a cage, which means either you're the bravest fool on this mountain or the most desperate, and I suspect you're both."

The falls roared. The ink on her skin crawled its slow circuits. On the map, a road that hadn't existed a moment before faded softly in and out of being, like a fish surfacing in dark water.

"The map glowed," Cyrus said finally. "When I touched it. Blood, the first time—an accident, a paper cut. The roads lit up like embers. Since then it... responds. Sometimes I think it's waiting for something."

Wren closed her eyes briefly, as if bracing. "Show me your hand."

Cyrus extended it. Wren turned it over, studied the palm, and her breath caught—just barely, just enough for Cyrus to hear it over the water.

"What?"

"There's ink on you," she said. "Faint. Along the life-line. It wasn't there when you cut your palm, I'd wager—it's come since." She looked up, and her eyes in the lantern light were steady and grim. "The map isn't just alive, Cyrus Voss. It's *choosing*. Living bound-script binds itself to bloodlines. Your grandmother drew this. Your blood opens it. And the Undercourt has been hunting you like a debt-collector hunts a debtor's heir."

The name hung between them—*Voss*—spoken aloud for the first time since the inn burned. Cyrus realized they'd given it away without noticing, somewhere between the lock and the running, and found they didn't regret it. That was its own kind of frightening.

"So what do I do?" they asked. "Burn it? Maren wanted me to burn it."

"And did you?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Cyrus looked down at the map—at Elira's handwriting, looping and sure, drawing roads no honest cartographer would dare. At the border of the parchment, where the faintest script curled, dense and tiny, like a letter waiting to be read. They thought of Maren's face going pale. Of the riders on the ridge. Of a dying courier's blood soaking into snow.

"Because it's the only thing left that my grandmother chose," they said. "Everything else was taken. The guild, the name, the—everything. This, she *made*. I want to know why."

Wren studied them for a long moment. Then she began, methodically, working at the silver rings on her wrists.

"What are you doing?"

"These suppress my craft. They also suppress the sigils I've inked into my skin—which means I've been running on fumes since the arrest." The first ring clicked open. She hissed softly, as if a wound were reopening. "If we're going to travel together, you should know what I actually am, rather than what the cage implied. And I need to know what I'm traveling with. So." The second ring clicked. She flexed her fingers, wincing, and the ink-marks on her arms brightened visibly, like banked coals stirred. "Ask me anything. One question. Make it count."

Cyrus considered. The obvious question was *what is the Undercourt*, or *what does the map truly do*, or *can you protect us*. Instead they heard themselves ask:

"Why were you smiling? In the cage. When you thought no one was watching. You were smiling."

Wren blinked. For the first time since the fish-market, she looked genuinely off-balance.

"I was practicing," she said at last. "A discipline. You smile on purpose so the fear can't arrange your face for you." She paused, and the ghost of that same smile crossed her mouth again—realer this time, rueful. "Though I'll admit, some of it was because I'd decided that if the tithe-wagon came, I'd go down the hill having never begged. And then you appeared, swearing at the dark, and picked my lock badly, and I thought: *there's a person who breaks rules for strangers and hates themselves for it.* Those are rare. Those are worth following off a cliff."

"Off a cliff?"

"The map leads under the mountain, Cyrus Voss. Wherever your grandmother's roads go, we follow them eventually. That's cliffs." She rolled her shoulders, testing her returned craft, and the lantern flame leaned toward her like a curious animal. "So. Are we partners, or are we two fugitives who happened to flee in the same direction? Decide quickly. The watch will widen their search, and I know a way out of Greyhollow that doesn't use gates—but only if you trust me enough to follow."

Outside, distantly, a whistle sounded—closer than before. The hunt, tightening its noose.

Cyrus looked at the mage with the crawling ink and the practiced smile, at the map warm against their chest, at the dark tunnel that smelled of fish and freedom. Every sensible instinct said *alone, stay alone, alone is safe.* Every sensible instinct had been wrong since the courier bled out in the snow.

"Together," they said. "But I count the supplies."

"Naturally." Wren was already moving up the tunnel, lantern high, and the ink on her skin gleamed like a constellation learning to fly. "First rule of partnerships: the paranoid one manages the money."

"How many rules are there?"

"We'll invent them as we go. Second rule: never stop moving while the whistles are still singing."

And they ran again—together this time—up through the bones of Greyhollow, while behind them the tithe-wagon arrived at an empty cage, and somewhere beneath the mountain, something ancient turned a page and began, patiently, to write a new name.

* * *

The ink moved first.

Wren felt it before she understood it—a prickle along her forearms, her sigils rising like hackles on a dog. She slowed at the mouth of the drainage culvert where it opened onto the terraced slopes above Greyhollow, one hand out to stop Cyrus, and watched the marks on her own skin bend, all of them together, toward the northwest. Toward Thornfell's massif, black against the stars.

"They're pointing," Cyrus said beside her. Not a question. They'd seen it too—the way the fine lines of her bound-script had become compass needles.

"My sigils are made of the same school of craft as your map. Living ink answers living ink." Wren flexed her fingers and the marks slowly settled, though they kept twitching northwest like sleeping dogs dreaming of the hunt. "Something on that side of the valley is calling to everything of its kind. Either the map's maker left more work in the world—" she glanced sidelong at Cyrus, "—or something under the mountain has noticed what we're carrying and is hungry."

Below them, Greyhollow's lights were waking in angry clusters, the watch moving door to door. Somewhere down in the fish-market, a bounty officer was screaming at an empty cage.

"The Kestrel Shelf road," Cyrus murmured. "It runs northwest. High, exposed, but the riders won't expect anyone to take it this time of year."

"You know it?"

"I know every road on this mountain that respectable people avoid." Cyrus pulled the hood up, and for a moment the lantern light caught their face—young and old at once, worn hollow by three days of flight, yet lit through with something that had not been there in the fish-market. Purpose, maybe. Or the beginning of it. "Can you climb?"

"I was a court mage, not a court ornament. Yes, I can climb."

"Then stay close, don't sing, and if anything with a bone mask crosses our path—" Cyrus paused, and Wren heard the grief underneath the practical words, old and fresh at once, "—you run. Don't argue, don't cast, don't be clever. Run. I've seen what they leave behind."

Wren looked at this stranger—this desperate, brave, self-flagellating fool who had picked her lock badly and meant every word—and did something she had not done in seven years of enduring.

She trusted them.

"Lead on, Voss," she said.

They went up into the dark together, two small figures on the shoulder of a great sleeping mountain, and far below, in the deep places where the falls could not reach, something turned another page.
