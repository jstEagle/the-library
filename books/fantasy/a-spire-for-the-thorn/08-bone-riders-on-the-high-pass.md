## 8. Bone Riders on the High Pass

The Kestrel Shelf had earned its name honestly, Wren said, because only kestrels and fools crossed it willingly.

Cyrus Voss counted themselves among the second category as the wind came off the eastern face of Thornfell like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath. It found every gap in their coat, every seam at the wrists, every place the cold could slip in and set up housekeeping. Three days out of Greyhollow, two days since the ink on the Ember Map had glowed beneath their blood, and already Cyrus had begun to dream of low places—cellars, culverts, the warm dark under Maren's inn before it burned.

High places offered no such comfort.

The Shelf itself was a ledge cut into the mountain's shoulder by some ancient argument between stone and weather, wide enough for two walkers abreast if neither of them breathed deeply, running along the cliff face for half a mile before it narrowed to a spine of rock barely wider than a coffin laid lengthwise. Below them, the drop went down and down into cloud, so far that the eye gave up measuring and simply reported *void*. Above, the cliff rose sheer and grey, streaked with old ice that caught the thin morning light and threw it back the color of old knives.

"Talk to me," Wren said behind them. Her voice was steady, but Cyrus had learned in three days of travel that steadiness was something Wren built, brick by brick, over whatever was actually underneath. "I don't like how quiet you get."

"I'm counting," Cyrus said.

"Counting what?"

"Steps between here and the bridge. Keeps my mind off the drop."

"And how many is that?"

"Lost count when I looked down."

Wren made a sound that was almost a laugh, almost a prayer. The wind took most of it.

The Ember Map rode against Cyrus's chest, wrapped in oilcloth, tied tight beneath their coat where it lay warm as a sleeping animal. They had stopped consulting it openly after the third day, because Wren had noticed the roads inside it shifting when they looked away—passages rearranging themselves like sleepers turning over—and because Cyrus did not like the way their own reflection looked when the map's glow lit their face from below. Their grandmother's handwriting crawled along its borders still, patient as frost: *the Kestrel crossing holds, though the ropes will not.*

*Though the ropes will not.* Elira Voss had been dead twenty years, and she was still warning them about bridges.

Cyrus heard the riders before they saw them.

It was not hoofbeats—the bone riders did not ride anything so honest as horses—but a sound underneath hearing, a dry clicking that lived in the teeth rather than the ear, like knuckles cracking in another room, like the sound the Undercourt's ledger might make if it turned its own pages. Cyrus's hand found Wren's arm without deciding to. Wren had gone rigid already. She had heard it too.

"Don't run," Wren whispered. "They're drawn to running. Like hounds to a hare's heartbeat."

"Then what do we do?"

"We walk. Quickly. Calmly. Toward the bridge." A pause. "And we hope the ropes have more spine than the map promised."

Cyrus looked back once, because looking back is the oldest human failing and they had never once managed to cure themselves of it.

Far behind them, back along the Shelf where it widened toward the stair they'd climbed at dawn, the light changed. It was subtle at first—a shadow where no shadow should be, given that the sun stood high and pale above the eastern crags—and then the shadow resolved into shapes. Four of them. Tall, wrong-jointed, moving with a smoothness that made Cyrus's stomach try to climb its own ladder. They wore masks of yellowed bone fitted close to their skulls, carved into faces that were almost human, almost gentle, the kind of smiling faces painted on funeral urns. Their cloaks moved as though underwater. Where their feet touched stone, the frost did not melt but spread.

"They've been following us since Greyhollow," Wren breathed. "Since the market. Maybe since the cage."

"You said they'd lost us in the quarries."

"I said I hoped."

The wind screamed up the face of the mountain, and beneath it Cyrus heard it again: *click-click-click*, patient as a clock. One of the riders tilted its head, birdlike, and though the mask had no eyes—only shallow carved hollows—Cyrus felt its attention land on them like a physical weight, cold through the coat, cold through the skin, settling somewhere behind the sternum where the map lay burning.

They walked.

The Shelf narrowed. The world became stone underfoot, void below, cliff above, and the wind pushing at them sideways like a hand trying to shove them off the edge of everything. Cyrus kept their eyes on their boots, on the next hold, on the worn depressions where centuries of smugglers' feet had polished the stone. Their grandmother had walked this Shelf. That thought arrived unbidden and stayed like a splinter. Elira Voss had walked this exact ledge with this exact map against her ribs, and somewhere ahead of her the same rotten bridge had waited, and behind her perhaps these same riders, patient as winter. The road didn't just remember her. It was remembering Cyrus now, step for step, and Cyrus couldn't decide whether that was comfort or horror.

"There." Wren pointed past them.

The bridge hung across a gulf where the Shelf broke apart entirely—a gap in the mountain's ribcage forty feet wide, maybe fifty, spanned by ropes gone grey as grave-hair and planks that had been planks once and were now mostly suggestion. The far side jutted out of the mist, a knob of rock crowned with a leaning cairn-stone. Beyond it the Shelf resumed toward the high pass and the shrine Wren kept talking about, the one where watchers had once tracked the dragon-fire on the peak.

The ropes swayed. Not with the wind—with nothing. They swayed the way a hanged man sways long after the wind has died, and Cyrus hated the map, hated Elira's calm handwriting, hated being the last Voss standing on a ledge with death walking up from behind.

"It'll hold," Wren said.

"The map says otherwise."

"The map says the ropes won't hold. It doesn't say the bridge won't." She was already untying the coil of smuggler's line from her hip—the good cord, waxed hemp, worth more per foot than the rope itself. "We go one at a time. Weight apart. And we tie off." She glanced back along the Shelf, where the riders had grown from shadows into certainty, close enough now that the click-click-click needed no imagination. "Quickly, Cyrus."

Cyrus anchored the line around a spur of rock that seemed trustworthy, which on the Kestrel Shelf meant it hadn't moved recently. Wren went first—she argued for it in three words, *"you carry the map"*—and stepped onto the first plank with a grace that surprised them. She was lighter than she looked, or braver, or both. Plank, plank, gap, plank. Halfway across, one board cracked like a shot and dropped into the void, and Cyrus's heart dropped after it and somehow climbed back up. Wren froze, arms out, breathing.

"Keep going," Cyrus called, and was startled by how steady their own voice came out. "Don't look down. Look at me."

Wren looked at them. Something passed across her face—not fear anymore, something warmer and more dangerous—and she kept going, hand over hand on the grey rope, until her boots hit solid stone on the far side and Cyrus let out a breath they'd been holding since Greyhollow.

Then it was their turn.

Cyrus swung the map-bundle higher on their back, gripped the rope, and stepped out over nothing.

The first few boards held. The wind did not—it came in gusts now, deliberate, as though something on the Shelf were exhaling toward them, and each gust leaned on Cyrus like a debt collector. Halfway across, the rope under their right hand gave a sound they would hear for the rest of their life: a soft, fibrous sigh, like an old woman settling into a chair she intended never to rise from again. Strands parted. Not all of them. Enough.

Behind them, on the near edge of the chasm, the first rider arrived.

Up close it was worse. The bone mask was not a carving; it was a face, grown not made, and beneath the hem of its cloak there were no feet, only a slow unraveling of darkness that touched the stone and taught the stone to frost. It did not hurry onto the bridge. It simply began to walk, and the planks did not creak beneath it—they *whitened*, aging decades with each step, rotting forward to meet its feet like grass bending to hail.

"Go!" Wren shouted from the far side. "Cyrus, *go!*"

Cyrus went. Three boards, four, five, the damaged rope groaning overhead, fibers singing. Six. The rider was a quarter of the way across and the planks ahead of it were dying in sequence, brown to grey to powder, and Cyrus understood with terrible clarity that the bridge would fail behind them and possibly beneath them, that the mathematics of this crossing were simple and merciless, and that the only variable left was speed.

Seven boards. Eight. The far anchor-post rose out of the mist.

Then the rope failed.

Not the one they gripped—the other, the balance-line, parting with a crack that sounded like the mountain clearing its throat. The whole bridge lurched sideways, swinging out over the void like a pendulum, and Cyrus's boots skated off a plank that chose that instant to crumble, and suddenly they were hanging—one hand on a rope that was shedding strands like hair, the other clutching the anchor-line Wren had rigged, the map a crushing weight between shoulder blades and the entire grey world swinging beneath them.

Below: cloud. Nothing else. The mind insisted there must be a bottom and offered nothing but white.

"Cyrus!" Wren was flat on her stomach at the far edge, reaching down, her fingers maybe a foot above theirs. "Give me your hand!"

The rider was ten boards back and closing, unhurried, killing the bridge plank by plank, and the remaining rope was fraying above Cyrus's grip with each swing. Cyrus could feel the arithmetic in their shoulders: the weight of their body plus the map plus the waxed line, versus fibers that had been dying since before either of them was born.

"Wren—" Their voice came out smaller than they wanted.

"Reach, damn you."

Cyrus reached. Missed by inches. Swung back, reached again, and this time Wren's fingers closed around their wrist—not gently, nothing gentle in it, a grip like a shackle—and for one suspended moment they hung there, connected, over the mouth of the world, while the last rope sang its dying note above them.

The rider was four boards away.

Three.

And here is the thing Cyrus Voss would think about afterward, in warmer places, beside fires, beside Wren: in that moment they did not think of the map. They did not think of the ledger whispering their name, or the guild, or the debt written in their grandmother's hand, or any of the vast machinery of obligation that had dragged them onto this Shelf. There was no room for duty. There was only Wren's grip on their wrist, her face white and furious and alive above them, and a thought that arrived whole and undeniable and terrifying in its simplicity:

*Not her. Whatever it costs, not her.*

The rope let go.

Cyrus fell—and pulled. Pulled *up*, using the last swing of the broken bridge like a smuggler uses a counterweight, the trick their grandmother's ghost had apparently left coiled in their muscles: give the fall direction, make the drop do your work. The anchor-line snapped taut, their boots found the cliff-face and kicked off it, and momentum carried them up in a great arc over the gap while the ruined bridge dropped away beneath them, planks spinning down into the cloud like cards thrown from a window.

Wren hauled. Cyrus's hands found rock. Together, swearing, gasping, they dragged each other onto the far side and collapsed against the cairn-stone as the bridge's last remnants vanished into the mist below.

Silence. Wind. The click-click-click, distant now.

Across the gap, the lead rider stood at the broken edge, head tilted, considering. It raised one long hand—not in threat, Cyrus realized, but in something worse. In *acknowledgment*. Then it turned, and the four of them poured back along the Shelf like spilled ink running uphill, unhurried, patient, certain.

They knew another road. They always knew another road.

* * *

They did not stop moving until the Shelf climbed away from the chasm and folded into a hollow beneath an overhang of blue ice, a smuggler's cache marked by three stones stacked elbow-high, exactly as the map had shown. Only there did Cyrus allow their knees to quit.

Their hands would not stop shaking. They watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though the tremors belonged to someone else—someone sensible who understood what had nearly happened, unlike Cyrus, whose body was only now catching up to the fact of the rope.

Wren crouched in front of them. "Let me see your wrist."

"It's fine."

"It's bleeding. Let me see it."

Her grip was careful where it had been desperate. She unwound a strip of clean linen from her kit, wet it from the waterskin, and worked it around the raw ring the rope had burned into Cyrus's skin. Her hands, Cyrus noticed, were shaking too. She pretended they weren't. Cyrus pretended not to notice. Between them they maintained the fiction that either of them was composed.

"That pull," Wren said quietly, tying off the bandage. "At the end. The counterweight. That wasn't luck."

"Smuggler's trick. My grandmother used it on the Tarrel viaduct, according to—" They stopped. Swallowed. "According to stories."

"You saved my life on that bridge. Before the rope broke. When you told me to look at you." Wren's voice had gone rough. "You didn't think about it. I saw your face. You didn't think."

Cyrus looked at the bandage, at her fingers resting light on their pulse, at the small fire she was already coaxing alive in the lee of the stones despite the wind's opinion. "There wasn't time to think."

"No," Wren agreed softly. "That's what frightens me. What's left when there's no time to think." She fed a stick to the flame. "You reached for me like I was the thing you'd grab in a burning house."

"You were standing in the burning house."

"Cyrus."

"Wren." They held her gaze, and found, to their surprise, that they weren't afraid of what was in it. "Whatever else happens—whatever the map wants, whatever the Undercourt wants, whatever name they take off me in the end—I'm not leaving you on a bridge. Write that down somewhere. You're the mage; you probably know a spell for it."

Something broke open in Wren's face then, brief as lightning, quickly covered—but covered too late, and both of them knew it. She looked away first, busying herself with the fire, and Cyrus let her, because some mercies are quieter than words.

* * *

The inventory, taken an hour later, was grim.

The bridge had kept their lives and little else. The supply roll—food for six days, the tent, the spare line, half the lamp-oil—had been lashed to the rope-bridge rail while they crossed, standard smuggler's practice, weight off the back on bad crossings. It had gone into the cloud with the planks. What remained was the map, Wren's satchel, a knife apiece, the waterskin, a heel of bread, and a strip of dried mutton that Cyrus divided into portions with the solemnity of a priest dividing relics.

"Three days of food," Wren said. "Four days to the shrine on the high pass, if the weather holds, and the weather has never once held in my life."

"Two days if we push."

"If we push, we arrive too tired to think, and thinking is the only weapon we have that the riders can't rot."

Cyrus chewed their portion slowly, watching the light on the peak. From here, through a notch in the crags, Thornfell's summit was visible for the first time since Greyhollow—and Cyrus frowned at it, the frown deepening by degrees.

The mountain had a crown of snow. Every child in the passes grew up knowing it, drawing it, swearing by it: the white cap that never melted, warmed from within by the old fire, the dragon's breath keeping the stone itself alive. Travelers navigated by it. Songs were written about it.

The crown was dimmer than Cyrus remembered. Not gone—nothing so dramatic—but dulled, the way coals dull when the bellows stop, the way a lamp looks through fogged glass. And around the summit, where the evening should have gilded the snow gold, there was a faint grey shimmer instead, like heat-haze in reverse. Cold-haze. The air above the peak seemed to *drink* the light.

"Wren," Cyrus said.

She followed their gaze. Heavens, but Cyrus watched the color leave her face in real time, draining down through her jaw like water through sand.

"That's not weather," she whispered.

"No."

"The wards are fed by the fire. If the fire dims enough to show at this distance—" She stood abruptly, pacing the small hollow, hands moving as if she could shape an answer out of the air. "Villages sit on those wards, Cyrus. Whole valleys. The border-towns, the free-paths, everything the courts haven't bothered to garrison because the mountain does the work for free. If the fire fails, it isn't just a dragon dying on a peak. It's every seal under Thornfell opening at once."

Cyrus looked down at their bandaged wrist, then at the oilcloth bundle they'd already begun unwrapping, because there was nowhere left to hide from it. The Ember Map lay open on the stone between them, its living ink stirring like something waking, and there—high on the mountain's flank, along the route they'd take tomorrow—was the mark of the shrine, drawn in Elira Voss's small, precise hand. Beside it, almost too fine to read, a single line of script:

*From here you will see whether her bargain was enough.*

"Whose bargain?" Wren asked.

Cyrus traced the words with a fingertip, careful, the way one touches a sleeping animal. The ink curled faintly toward the touch, recognizing them, and the cold that moved through Cyrus then had nothing to do with the wind.

"Ours," they said.
