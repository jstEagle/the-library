## 9. The Dragon's Guttering Light

Wren had not prayed in eleven years, and the shrine on the Kestrel Shelf made her remember why.

It was a small thing, as shrines went—a hollow scooped into the mountainside, its walls furred with frost-lichen that glowed faintly blue when the wind moved through it. Someone had once tended this place. Someone had set candles in niches worn smooth by centuries of thumbs, had hung copper bells from the ceiling on threads gone green with age, had carved a single word above the entrance in a script so old even the court archives only half-remembered it: *remember*. But the candles were long burned to stubs, the bells silent, and snow had drifted across the threshold like a held breath.

She stood at the shrine's mouth while Cyrus saw to their dwindling supplies inside, and she looked north, and she felt the cold come down off Thornfell's peak like something with intent.

The mountain had always burned. That was the first truth every child in the nine valleys learned before they learned their letters. Thornfell smoked gently at its crown the way an old man breathes in sleep—steady, warm, alive. The dragon was older than the courts, older than the ledger, older perhaps than the stone itself, and its fire did not merely sit in the caldera like a lamp in a window. It *worked*. Wren had studied the theory at the Spire Academy until the theory was beaten out of her: heartflame fed the wards, and the wards were not walls but a great slow turning of the world's own heat, a wheel of warmth spun so long that everything within its reach had forgotten what stillness was. The villages farmed in soil that should have been too high and thin. The passes stayed open in winter. The rivers ran unfrozen past towns whose names ended in *-melt* and *-warm*.

All of that was a debt. And debts, Wren knew better than anyone, came due.

"Cyrus," she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened by the wind. "Come here. You need to see this."

Cyrus emerged with the map case slung across their chest, cheeks raw from the climb, and followed her gaze up. For a long moment neither of them spoke.

Thornfell's peak was dimming.

Not dark—not yet—but the plume that should have risen gold-lit against the morning clouds was pale, gray-edged, thin as breath on glass. Even at this distance, even through the shimmer of cold air, Wren could see the difference, because she had grown up in sight of the mountain the way other children grew up in sight of the sea. The peak had been a constant. A fact of the sky. And now it flickered.

"It's never done that," Cyrus said quietly. "Has it?"

"No." Wren's breath fogged and tore away. "It hasn't."

She watched Cyrus's face do the thing it did when fear arrived and was immediately put to work—the jaw setting, the eyes narrowing, grief converted to forward motion. She was beginning to know that face. She resented how quickly she was beginning to know it.

"The wards," Cyrus said. "That's what you told me. The fire feeds them."

"And the fire is failing." Wren turned back toward the shrine, because looking at the peak any longer felt like watching someone bleed. "Come inside. There's something I need to show you, and I'd rather show it to you sitting down."

* * *

Inside, the shrine was one round room, its floor scattered with old offerings gone to dust—dried flowers, a child's wooden bird, coins from three defunct mints. Cyrus built a small fire near the entrance where the smoke could find the draft, and Wren knelt by the map and unrolled it across the flat top of the offering stone.

The Ember Map breathed under her hands. She would never get used to that—the way the ink moved when it thought no one was looking, roads sliding a finger's width east or west, a pass opening where a ridge had been. Living cartography, drawn in Elira Voss's hand, keyed to blood Wren still didn't fully understand. She had spent two days now teaching herself its grammar, and it had taught her three things in return: that it responded to Cyrus, that it lied beautifully to everyone else, and that it was afraid.

Ink, she had thought at first. Ink cannot be afraid.

But look at it now. The deep ways beneath Thornfell—the passages that led down, down into the dark the surface courts preferred to pretend was a story—were drawn in a different pigment altogether. Not black. Not red. A color between, a dried-blood brown that seemed to drink the light of their small fire. And those lines were moving faster tonight. Fretting. The roads beneath the mountain were rearranging themselves the way a sleeper shifts when a nightmare takes hold.

"Show me again," Cyrus said, crouching beside her. "What am I looking at?"

"Two things." Wren touched the peak, where the cartographer's ink had rendered the caldera as a coiled knot of gold. Except the gold was fading. As she watched, a thread of it went out entirely, and the surrounding lines dimmed like a lamp running low on oil. "This is Ashvane. The last dragon. Its heartflame is the engine of every ward in the nine valleys. When I was at the Academy, we were taught the wards were self-sustaining. Eternal. It was a comfortable lie." Her finger traced outward, following the web of fine silver lines that spread from the caldera across the whole map—over the passes, the valleys, the free-towns, all the way to the borders of the map itself. "Every one of these is a ward-line. Every village, every road you've ever kept safe, sits inside this net. And the net is strung from the dragon's fire."

"And if the fire goes out—"

"The net falls." She said it plainly because there was no kind way to say it. "Not all at once. In rings. The outermost wards will fail first—the border stones, the river-wards, the charms that keep the deep roots of the mountain quiet. Then the nearer ones. And underneath all of it—" she tapped the fretting brown roads, "—is the Undercourt. Sealed, starved, and patient for longer than your family has kept ledgers. The wards don't imprison it, exactly. They *starve* it. Keep the hunger pressed down where it can't reach anything warm. Take the pressure off, and it doesn't have to break out. It just rises."

Cyrus was silent for a moment. In the firelight their face was all planes and shadow, and Wren caught herself cataloguing it again—that habit she couldn't seem to break, learning someone the way she'd once learned spells, line by line, against the day she might need to protect them.

"My grandmother drew this map," Cyrus said at last. "Twenty years ago. She marked every ward-line. She must have known what the debt was doing to the fire."

"Or she knew what the court below intended to do about it." Wren sat back on her heels. "There's more. Look at the border of the map. Bottom edge, left corner. Have you looked closely at the marginalia?"

"I looked. Old notes. Supply lists, mostly. Distances."

"Some of them." Wren eased the edge of the parchment into the firelight and angled it so the light raked low across the paper. Raking light was the trick—she'd discovered it by accident the second night, when a candle guttered—and in that slanting glow, writing appeared in the margins that wasn't writing at all in ordinary light. Fine, cramped script, pressed so hard the stylus had nearly cut the vellum. "Elira wrote in two hands. The confident hand for the roads. This one—" the hidden hand, the frightened one, "—for everything she didn't want found."

Cyrus leaned close. Their shoulder brushed hers; neither of them moved away. Together they read:

*The fire dims faster than they promised me it would. They said my soul would buy the guild twenty years. They did not say the twenty years was all the fire had left. What else did they not say? What else is owed?*

Below it, in the same crushed script:

*I have begun marking the ward-lines so that whoever comes after me can see the shape of the cage. If you are reading this, the cage is open or opening. Do not go down for me. Go up. Save the fire first. A soul can wait in the dark. A valley cannot.*

Wren heard Cyrus's breath change. She didn't touch them. Some griefs you had to let stand alone or they collapsed on you both.

"She knew," Cyrus said finally. The words came out scraped. "She knew the whole time. She sold herself and they *lied to her*—"

"They always lie." Wren heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't try to soften it. Eleven years since the Academy had cast her out, and the anger still lived in her like a splinter worked too deep to draw. "That's the first lesson of contracts, Cyrus. The Undercourt's, the crown's, the Academy's—it makes no difference. The party holding the pen holds the truth as well. Your grandmother signed away her soul believing she was buying time. Perhaps she was. But the ones who brokered it—" she thought of the elders, the pardons Odo had spoken of, the machinery of respectable men dividing up a woman's life, "—they counted the years differently than she did."

Outside, the wind changed. Wren felt it before she heard it—a pressure shift, a wrongness in the way the cold lay against the shrine's mouth. She looked up sharply.

"What?" Cyrus said.

"Listen."

At first there was nothing. Then, faint beneath the wind: bells. The copper bells hanging from the shrine's ceiling, dead for decades, stirring without any draft reaching them. One gave a single cracked note, low and mournful, and went still.

Wren stood slowly. "Old wards," she said. "Shrine-wards. They used to ring when the mountain's balance shifted. Pilgrims called it the dragon turning over in its sleep." Another bell shivered, soundless, straining against its green thread. "They're not asleep-turning now. Something is pulling at the wheel."

* * *

They climbed above the shrine as the afternoon failed, up a spine of bare rock to a shelf where the whole southern face of the world opened beneath them, and that was where Wren understood the true size of what was coming.

The valleys lay below like a map made real—nine of them, stitched together by the grey threads of roads, each one dotted with the small warm lights of hearths beginning to be lit against the evening. Smoke rose straight from a hundred chimneys in the windless cold. Somewhere down there were mill-towns and shepherd-hamlets, children being called in to supper, old men arguing over cards in taverns lit by ward-lamps that had burned unchanged for four hundred years.

And the cold was walking toward them.

She saw it first as a discoloration on the high slopes—a band of white frost creeping down Thornfell's flank where no frost had any business being, moving with a slow, deliberate smoothness that no weather had ever managed. Where it passed, the dark green of pine forest paled and stiffened. A stream that threaded silver down one gully simply stopped glittering, its water locked mid-laugh.

Then the lights began to go out.

Not all at once. One by one, in the nearest valley, the ward-lamps along the river road guttered—amber, then brown, then nothing. A farmhouse's windows went dark though she could see the chimney smoke still rising; the household wards had died and taken the hearth-charms with them, leaving the family to huddle around raw flame. Down in the market town—Lowmere, she thought the name was, famous for its winter apples—the great ward-stone in the square, which glowed soft as a moonstone every night of every year, pulsed once, twice, like a heart missing beats, and dimmed to a dull ember-red.

"They're feeling it," Wren whispered. "Gods. They're already feeling it."

Cyrus stood beside her, very still. "How long?"

"That depends on how much fire is left." Wren did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the shrine, and hated every number. "The wards fail in rings, from the outside in. The border stones are already dark—I'd wager the frontier villages woke this morning to frozen wells. Lowmere's ring is third from the center. Given what we saw today..." She exhaled. "Weeks. Perhaps less. The last ring—the one around the caldera itself—will hold longest, because it's closest to the source. But when it fails, the Undercourt doesn't rise. It *arrives*. Everything it has spent centuries pressing against the seal comes up at once, hungry, and the first things it meets are the nine valleys full of people who have done nothing to it except live warmly above its head."

"And the courts?" Cyrus's voice had gone flat and hard. "The surface courts. The ones with armies. What are they doing?"

"Watching. Arguing. Counting what the crisis might be worth to them." Wren thought of the chancellery, the immaculate robes, the way her mentor's trial had been conducted as a formality with the verdict written beforehand. "They'll mobilize eventually. Not to save the valleys. To claim whatever rises. Power abhors a vacuum, and the Undercourt's fall is going to leave the largest one in living memory." She shook her head. "No one is coming to save these people, Cyrus. No army, no court, no miracle of statecraft. Which means the only variable left in the entire equation is us, and a dying dragon, and whatever your grandmother hid inside that map."

The first stars were out now, hard and sharp in the freezing air. Below, the dark patch of dead lamps in Lowmere had spread. Somewhere a dog was barking—long, panicked, unanswered. The frost-band crept another arm's length down the mountainside.

Cyrus turned to her. In the starlight their eyes were very bright, and Wren recognized the expression on their face because she had seen it in mirrors across eleven bad years: the look of someone who has just stopped being able to walk away.

"The vault," Cyrus said. "Her lantern. My grandmother's soul. You said the map shows the deep ways—all the way down."

"It does. Through the Hollow Fair, past the herald's table, into the vault of names. If the roads haven't shifted beyond reading by the time we get there." Wren hesitated, and then said the rest, because Cyrus deserved the whole weight of it, unsoftened. "But understand what you'd be carrying, and why. The heartflame isn't a treasure, it's a *piece of the dragon*—torn out and hoarded, probably as collateral for Elira's contract. It can't just be picked up. It needs a vessel, a bearer willing to carry burning, and the court below will know the moment it moves. We would be stealing the keystone out of their vault while their house is already shaking. Every rider, every mask, every starving thing down there will come for us."

"Good," Cyrus said.

Wren blinked. "*Good?*"

"Because right now they're hunting us anyway, and I'm tired of being the prey." Cyrus looked back at the valleys, at the spreading dark, and Wren watched the last of the smuggler's caution burn away like fog in sun. "My grandmother bought twenty years with her soul and got lies for it. The elders sold her contract for pardons. The courts above will sell the valleys for territory. Everyone in this story keeps signing other people's lives away to save their own skin." They turned back to her, and their voice dropped, roughened. "I'm done being part of that economy. So yes—we go down. We take back her soul, we take back the fire, and we put both back where they belong. And if the ledger wants a name for it—" the ghost of the old Voss crooked smile, gone as fast as it came, "—it knows where to find me."

It was madness. It was suicide with extra steps. Wren had watched a better mage than she would ever be die attempting a fraction of what Cyrus was proposing, and every scarred instinct she owned was screaming at her to argue, to plan, to find the flaw and press on it until the whole reckless scheme collapsed.

Instead she heard herself say, "Then we do it properly. No more wandering. From here we move with purpose—down through the Kestrel Shelf, along the old pilgrim road to the Mouth. And Cyrus—" she caught their sleeve, made them look at her, "—if we're doing this, you learn everything I know about soul-contracts. Every law, every loophole, every technicality the ghost-market runs on. Because the ledger is a machine, and machines can be broken, but only by someone who understands exactly which gear to snap. Promise me you'll learn it. All of it. However ugly."

"I promise."

Below them, in the deepest dark of the nearest valley, the last ward-lamp of Lowmere's outer ring went out, and the town's remaining lights huddled closer together like sheep scenting wolves. The frost reached the first pine trees and the trees went white and silent, and somewhere beneath all that stone, in halls of bone and memory, something ancient lifted its head and began, at last, to hope.

Wren took one long look at the guttering peak, committing it to memory—the exact shade of its failing gold, so that whatever they became on the road ahead, she would remember what they were fighting for.

"Come on," she said. "We've got a dragon to wake."

They turned from the shelf and started down into the dark, two small figures on an enormous mountain, carrying a map that remembered, a magic that was burning itself thin, and between them—unspoken, unpriced, the one thing in the nine valleys that no ledger had ever managed to hold.

Behind them, the copper bells of the shrine rang once more, all together, clear as a warning carried up from the deep places of the world.

Then the wind took the sound, and the night closed over the passes like water.
