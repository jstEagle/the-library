## 11. The Court Above

Wren felt the summons before she saw it.

It came as a prickle along the inside of her wrist, where the court's sigil had been branded when she took her oaths at fifteen—the mark they had not bothered to burn away when they cast her out. A scar, they'd said, was reminder enough. Now the scar was waking, crawling with heat like an insect trapped beneath the skin, and she knew what it meant: the Circle of Ashes had need of her.

She stopped on the quarry road so suddenly that Cyrus walked three paces past before noticing.

"What is it?" Cyrus's hand went to the knife at their hip out of habit. Three days since Greyhollow, and they still moved like a hunted thing—shoulders drawn, eyes always sweeping the ridgelines. "Riders?"

"Worse." Wren pushed back her sleeve. In the grey morning light, the old brand had flushed from white to a sullen rose-gold, the interlocking rings of the royal college glowing faintly, like coals remembered rather than burning. "They've called me home."

Cyrus stared at it. "I thought you said they wanted nothing to do with you."

"They wanted nothing to do with me *disgraced*." Wren pulled the sleeve down, though the heat stayed. "Disgrace is a door that swings both ways, Voss. When the court needs something badly enough, they'll open any of them."

"The map."

"The map." She could feel it through the oilcloth where it rode against Cyrus's chest—a presence she was learning to read the way she'd once read ley-lines, its living ink humming faintly against her own senses. "They've felt the wards thinning same as everyone. And they know, somehow, that there's a road into the mountain. They don't want to save the dragon, Cyrus. They want to *use* the door before it closes."

The wind came down off Thornfell carrying the smell of snow and something else beneath it—sulfur, faint and wrong, like a fire dying in another room. Wren had been smelling it for days now. She wondered if Cyrus noticed, or if smugglers simply filed such things away with all their other quiet knowledge.

"So we avoid the roads," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question; it was arithmetic. "Cut south through the high fells, cross the Stonebridge toll at night—"

"No." The word came out flatter than she intended. She made herself look at them. "You don't understand what a summons *is*, because you were never inside. It isn't a letter. It isn't men riding out to find you. It's a—" she searched for the words, gesturing at the air as if she could shape it there, "—a net laid across the world. Every waystation, every toll-house, every village priest with a scrying bowl. My name goes into the net, and the net tightens until I'm in it. There is no avoiding the roads, Cyrus. The roads will come to us."

* * *

They came at dusk, two days later, in the town of Marrowgate-in-the-Weald, where the fells finally gave way to the first true valley and the inns had names instead of numbers.

Wren knew within moments of walking through the gate. The town was too clean. Soldiers in royal livery—deep green, silver stag—at the customs house, at the well, at the shrine where travelers traditionally left offerings. Not hunting, exactly. *Waiting.* Positioned the way a falconer positions jesses, with patient hands and no urgency at all, because the bird has nowhere else to be.

"We can still turn around," Cyrus murmured. They stood in the shadow of a wool-merchant's overhanging eaves, and Wren watched them count soldiers with a professional's eye—four at the gate, two at the shrine, one more lounging by the stable row who was not lounging at all. "There's a drover's track north of here. We lose nothing but a night's sleep."

"It's already done," Wren said quietly. Across the square, by the customs house, a soldier had unrolled something—a chart, a scrying-cloth, ink-dark even at this distance—and was feeding it drops from a small vial. She felt the answering tug in her wrist like a hook settling into flesh. "Feel that? That's my name in their mouths. Another hour and every soldier in this town will know my face the way they know their own mothers'. We're not slipping a net, Voss. We're already in it. All we get to choose is which mesh we break."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. Then: "Which mesh, then?"

Wren looked at the map of the town in her head—the one she'd memorized twenty years ago as an apprentice, when her whole duty had been knowing the land better than the land knew itself. Marrowgate sat on the lip of the old quarries. Flooded quarries, drowned when the crown abandoned them a century back, connected by cuttings and adits and half-drowned galleries that no honest map recorded, because no honest map cared about the wounds men leave in mountains.

She almost laughed. Almost.

"There's a saying at the college," she said. "*The court above maps the surface of things.* It was an insult, mostly. Come on."

* * *

The quarries took them at moonrise.

The water was black glass under a thin skin of ice at the edges, and it smelled of stone-sap and old rain, cold enough that breath hung about them in scarves. Wren went first, along the quarry rim where the cutting-sheds had rotted into skeletons, following a memory of a memory: an apprenticeship field-exercise, a master who'd said *know the wounds of the earth, for the court never will.*

"Left here," she whispered, at a collapsed gantry. "There's an adit behind the fall. It runs dry for half a mile, then floods to the waist."

"And you know this how?"

"I failed my second-year field examination very badly."

Cyrus made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Behind them, above, a horn sounded—one long note, then two short. The net, tightening. Wren did not look back. Looking back was how you fell into flooded quarries.

The adit was exactly as she remembered: a black mouth breathing cold, the timber lintel soft with rot. Inside, the darkness was total, and total dark was the college's business too—they trained mages to carry light in their palms like a coin to be spent carefully. Wren cupped her hands and kindled a mote of foxfire, pale green, no brighter than a dying star.

It showed her walls slick with weepstone, a passage barely shoulder-wide, and Cyrus's face floating beside her, sharp-boned and watchful, eyes catching her little light and holding it.

"You're smiling," Cyrus said.

"I'm remembering why I left." She started forward, water sounds rising ahead of them like applause. "They teach you the land is a text to be read and corrected. Never that it might have its own handwriting."

* * *

The water came to their waists, then their ribs, then—briefly, in one drowned gallery where the ceiling pressed close and the foxfire lit the underside of the surface like the sky of a drowned country—to their chins. Wren kept one hand on the guide-rope some forgotten quarryman had strung, rust flaking off against her palm, and thought, absurdly, of her mentor Master Illaine, who had taught her that magic was a ledger too: everything borrowed, everything repaid.

Illaine would have understood the flooded quarries. Illaine would have walked in laughing.

Illaine was eight years dead, and Wren had helped kill her.

"Stop," Cyrus said suddenly.

Wren froze, water lapping at her collarbones. Ahead, the gallery bent, and around the bend—light. Not hers. Lantern-light, hard and orange and organized, the light of men who had entered a tunnel in formation.

Voices carried strange over water. She caught fragments: *"—the adit at the far cutting—" "—her mark's warm, she's close—" "—the Voss girl too, mind, the warrant says alive—"*

"They followed the mark," Wren breathed. The brand at her wrist pulsed, and she understood with sick clarity: it had never just been waking her. It had been *reporting*. A beacon wearing her oath like a lamp-room wears a wick. "Gods. Twenty years cast out and I'm still theirs. Still *instrumented*."

"Cyrus." She said their name and it came out steady, which surprised her. "I can dampen it. Pull the heat out of the brand for an hour, maybe two. But I'll need to stop moving, and it will cost me—you'll have to get us both to the far adit."

"How much will it cost you?"

"That's not your—"

"*How much?*" The word cracked off wet stone. In the foxfire's last threads, Cyrus's face was very close, very pale, eyes blown wide and furious and afraid—not for themselves. Wren had spent twenty years cataloguing the ways people looked at her: as a tool, as a disgrace, as a warning. No one had ever looked at her like the fear was for her.

"A fever," she admitted. "A bad one. A day in bed, if we find a bed."

"Then we find a bed." Cyrus took her hand in the dark, water streaming between their fingers, and pressed the rope into it. "Dampen it. I'll pull. You've carried me since Greyhollow. Let me carry something for once."

So Wren closed her eyes in the drowning dark of the crown's oldest wound and reached into her own wrist and *snuffed* the brand like pinching a wick, and felt the cold pour in where the summons had lived, and heard Cyrus move ahead of her through black water with a smuggler's certainty, towing her by the hand, murmuring the way one murmurs to a frightened animal—or a friend: *left here, mind the winch-chain, nearly there, nearly there, Wren, nearly there.*

* * *

They surfaced into a cutting open to the stars, both of them shaking, and Wren's teeth began chattering before she got two full breaths into her body. The dampening worked faster than she'd promised. By the time Cyrus hauled her up the cutting's rubble slope to the ruins of a winding-house, she was shivering so hard her vision stuttered, and the fever was already lighting coals behind her eyes.

"Bed," Cyrus announced to the ruined building, as if it were contractually obligated to produce one.

What it produced instead was an old drying-kiln, its iron door hanging open, straw and sacking still heaped inside from some contractor's abandoned job. Cyrus built a fire small and smokeless the way only a lifelong criminal could, fed Wren every strip of dried meat they carried, and sat with their back against the kiln's warm iron while she burned.

The fever dreams were of the college. Of course they were.

She dreamed of the Hall of Registers, where she'd sworn her oaths at fifteen, kneeling on stone worn smooth by a thousand knees before her. She dreamed of the Chancellor's voice, mild and terrible: *A mage serves the crown, and the crown serves the realm, and thus the mage serves everything—which is to say, the mage belongs to no one and nothing, least of all herself.* She had believed it. That was the cruelty. At fifteen she had believed it the way you believe in sunrise.

And she dreamed—fever-honest, fever-cruel—of Illaine. Of the night in the restricted archive when they had found the contract together, an Undercourt soul-debt signed by a court official two hundred years dead, still *live*, still accruing interest in human souls paid quietly into the deep every generation. Of Illaine saying, *we can unmake this, Wren. Contracts are texts, and texts can be edited.* Of the ritual going wrong—not because the theory was wrong, but because the court arrived, and someone had reported them, and the counter-binding had needed two mages and given its whole weight to the senior.

Of Illaine's last look. Not angry. *Relieved*, almost, that it was Wren walking out with the knowledge and not dying in it.

*You see now,* the dream-Illaine said, standing in the drying-kiln's doorway with starlight through her and firelight through her both at once. *Above or below, it's all one ledger. The court above wants the map for the same reason the court below wants your blood, child: to keep writing on other people.*

When Wren woke, grey dawn stood in the kiln doorway, and Cyrus was asleep sitting up, chin on chest, one hand still loosely closed around Wren's cold fingers as if letting go had been the harder discipline than staying awake. Someone had draped both their cloaks over her.

She lay still and watched them breathe and let herself feel, fully, for the first time in twenty years, the shape of the thing she'd lost at fifteen: not the college, not the power. This. Being carried. Being worth carrying.

*Foolish*, said the Chancellor's voice in her memory. *Sentiment is a leak in the vessel.*

*Then let me leak*, she answered the dead man. *Everything I sealed up for you rotted anyway.*

Cyrus stirred. Their eyes opened, found her immediately, checked her—forehead, color, focus—with a brisk tenderness that pretended to be practicality.

"Fever's down," they said. "You slept like the dead. I didn't—I checked you were breathing. Several times."

"I know." She sat up, joints protesting, and the movement brought the whole previous day back with it: the lantern-light around the bend, the warrant read aloud over black water. *The Voss girl too. Alive.* She watched Cyrus arrive at the same memory, watched their jaw set.

"They said 'the Voss girl,'" Cyrus said slowly. "Not 'the courier's killer' or 'map-thief.' They knew what to call me before anyone above ground knew I existed. Which means the court knows about the Voss debt. Knows about the Undercourt's claim." They looked at her, and Wren saw the cold arithmetic finish itself behind their eyes. "They don't want the map to seal the mountain, do they. They want it because the mountain is opening either way, and whoever holds the road holds the negotiation."

"Yes."

"And they sent a *warrant* for us. Politely. Alive."

"The court doesn't break things it might someday use." Wren flexed her wrist. The brand lay dormant, white as old wax, but she could feel it the way you feel a healed break before weather. It would wake again. Next time she'd know to expect it—but expectation wasn't escape. Sooner or later, the net would draw a mesh she couldn't slip, a tunnel that flooded to the ceiling, a squad positioned at the only exit. "We have perhaps two days before the dampening fails and they can find me again. Two days of grace, and then we're running blind against an enemy that owns every road, every record, every name in every registry from here to the sea."

Cyrus was silent for a long moment. Then they reached into their shirt and drew out the Ember Map, unwrapped the oilcloth, and spread it across their knees in the kiln's last warmth.

The living ink had shifted again. Roads Wren had never seen threaded the parchment's middle country, fine as veins in a leaf—and one of them, new since the quarries, ran from a point marked with a tiny rendered tower (a customs house? a college waystation?) south and west, toward the free-towns beyond the crown's easy reach, where the map's geography grew strange and salt-rimmed.

"There," Cyrus said, touching the road's western end, where the ink gathered thick around a name written in an alphabet that hurt to read directly. "Whatever's written there, the map thinks it matters. And it's pointing us out from under the court's thumb while it does." They looked up, and for the first time since the courier died in the snow, Wren saw something in Cyrus's face that wasn't grief or fear or duty. It was almost hunger. "Free-towns take no warrants. Saltcinder least of all—we run cargo there when nowhere else will have us. If we can reach it before the brand wakes..."

"Three days' hard travel," Wren said. "Through country the crown watches lightly because it's worthless."

"Country nobody wants," Cyrus agreed, a ghost of the smuggler's grin surfacing, sharp and bright in the ruin of their exhaustion. "The only kind we've ever been safe in."

Wren looked down at the map, at her own reflection faint and broken in its living sheen, and thought of Illaine, and of ledgers, and of the fact that the road west had appeared only after she'd doused her own beacon in the dark for a stranger's sake.

Perhaps the mountain had its own handwriting after all.

"West, then," she said, and began rolling up her sleeves to check the wardwork on her wrists—both of them now, the brand and the scars alike, everything the courts above had ever written on her body. "But we travel by night and sleep in daylight, and the first thing you're teaching me is how a smuggler crosses open ground."

Outside the drying-kiln, far to the east, a horn sounded—two long notes this time, and beneath them, so low it might have been imagination, a vibration in the ground like a vast slow breath drawn beneath Thornfell.

The mountain, exhaling. Or inhaling.

By the time they crossed the ridge at dusk, the peak behind them had dimmed by another shade, and the villages in the valleys below were lighting their fires early.
