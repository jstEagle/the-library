## 12. Salt and Cinder

Saltcinder announced itself the way a wound announces infection: by smell.

Cyrus caught it from the ridge road, two hours out—a brine-and-smoke reek that clung to everything the town touched, soaked into the timber of its crooked houses and the hair of everyone who lived there. Below, the free-town sprawled along a gray crescent of coast like something spilled and never cleaned up. Drying racks stood in rows on the shingle, hung with kelp that steamed in the cold sun. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys, none of it straight; the wind off the water saw to that. Gulls screamed over the fish-market with the entitlement of landlords.

"Charming," Wren said behind them. Her voice was rough. Three days in the flooded quarries had left her hoarse, and she'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around dawn.

"It's honest," Cyrus said. "That's more than you can say for most places."

They came down through the terraced gardens where nothing grew but samphire and stubbornness, past a shrine to some drowned god whose stone face wore a beard of salt, and onto the causeway that crossed the tidal flats. The flats stank at low tide—mud and rot and old fish—but they were also why Saltcinder stayed free. Any army stupid enough to cross them would arrive knee-deep, exhausted, and smelling worse than the town did. The crown's writ ended where the mud began. So did the Undercourt's, if it had ever reached this far north at all.

Cyrus felt the map against their chest before they felt anything else—the faint warmth of it, like a hand pressed flat beneath their coat. Since Greyhollow it had been doing that, warming when Wren came near, warming when danger did. It was learning them. Or remembering them. They hadn't decided which possibility they liked less.

* * *

The Salt Market ran the length of the harbor road under a roof of patched sails, and it sold everything the sea gave grudgingly and the mountains gave secretly. Cyrus moved through it the way their grandmother must have moved through it forty years ago—reading exits, counting guards, pricing every face. Old habits. The only inheritance they'd wanted.

They bought what they needed in pieces, splitting the work so no single stallholder could say later what the pair of strangers had carried away. Rope from one chandler, pitch and lamp oil from another. Dried cod and hard bread and a wheel of cheese so salty it made Wren's eyes water. A whetstone. Two heavy cloaks oiled against spray, because the passes ahead climbed back into snow. Cyrus paid in mixed coin, southern mint and northern, the way Voss couriers always had, and kept their hood up and their voice flat.

It was Wren who noticed the man.

She didn't say anything at first. She just drifted closer to Cyrus at the rope-seller's stall and murmured, "Third rack of drying nets, by the tar barrels. He's been facing us since the fish-market."

Cyrus looked without looking. The man was fifty-odd, built like a barrel someone had tried to stand upright, with a face weathered down to knots and a missing left ear. He wore a fisherman's oilskins, but he stood like a man who'd spent his life below decks or underground, weight low, hands loose. And across his knuckles, half-hidden by tar stains and age, was a tattooed mark Cyrus knew better than their own heartbeat.

A key crossed by a thorn. The Voss guild-sign.

The world went narrow and loud. Cyrus's hand found the knife at their belt without any decision being made about it.

"Easy," Wren breathed. "He hasn't called anyone. If he wanted us taken, we'd already be."

"He's Voss," Cyrus said, barely moving their lips. "Or he was."

"Then maybe he's the answer you came here hoping not to find."

Cyrus hated her a little for saying it out loud. They finished paying the chandler, took the coil of rope, and walked—not toward the man, not away from him, just walking, letting him make the choice. Behind them, boots crunched on shell-dust. He fell in alongside as casually as an old friend, which was somehow worse.

"Not here," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Tar-house on Anchor Lane. Blue door. When the bell rings for the evening tide." And he peeled away toward the nets again, leaving Cyrus holding a rope they no longer remembered buying.

Wren watched him go. "You know him?"

"No." It was true and it wasn't. "I know the mark on his hand. That's Odo Brack. Or it was—he was my grandmother's second. He vanished the year the guild died. Maren told me he drowned."

"And now he sells fish."

"Now he waits for bells," Cyrus said. "Which means he wants something. Or owes something. With Voss men it was always one or the other."

* * *

They spent the waiting hours badly. Cyrus counted their remaining coin three times and got three different answers, because their hands wouldn't stay steady. Wren sat in the loft room of the lodging house with the Ember Map open on her knees, though neither of them needed to look at it anymore; the roads were printed on the inside of Cyrus's eyelids now, glowing faintly whenever they closed their eyes. She traced the border lettering with one fingertip, not touching the ink itself. She'd learned that much caution in the quarries, after the ink had crawled an inch toward Cyrus's sleeping hand before she snatched the map away.

"It's quiet tonight," she said, which was her way of saying *I'm watching it anyway.*

Outside, the tide-bell rang—three strokes, deep and bronze, rolling up from the harbor mouth. Saltcinder's whole rhythm hung on that bell. Nets in, lamps lit, doors barred. And somewhere on Anchor Lane, a blue door opening for two people it had been waiting for.

The tar-house was a long low room that smelled exactly as advertised, dark as a pocket and twice as warm. A dozen fishermen drank in silence at scattered tables, the kind of silence men buy deliberately at the end of a day. The blue door let them into a back passage, then a cellar, then a second cellar that wasn't on any plan of the building, and Odo Brack was sitting there behind a table with a lamp turned low and two cups poured.

He looked older than his tattoo. Everyone did. But his eyes were the same eyes Cyrus had seen staring out of guild portraits in Maren's back hall—sharp, patient, and deeply tired.

"Cyrus Voss," he said.

"That name's dead," Cyrus said. "You'd know. You helped bury it."

Something moved behind his face—not quite flinching. Acknowledging the hit, and letting it land. "Sit down, child. Both of you. I've been twenty years getting ready to say what I'm going to say, and I'd rather say it sitting."

Wren took the stool first, positioning herself where she could see both the door and Cyrus. Cyrus remained standing a moment longer, cataloguing the cellar: one exit behind Odo, one ladder up, a crawl-space under the stairs that might take a person of determination. Then they sat.

"You were Elira's second," Cyrus said. "You signed the guild rolls. You taught the chute-runs through Kestrel Shelf. And then the guild burned its ledgers, the elders scattered, and Odo Brack drowned in a lake that doesn't exist. I grew up on those stories."

"The stories were the pardon," Odo said. "Every one of us got a story. Mine was drowning. Maren's was grief. The elders' was—" He stopped. Drank. His hand wasn't steady either, and that frightened Cyrus more than anything else in the room. "Their stories were lands and titles, and they're all dead now anyway, so it hardly matters what they bought."

"The map," Cyrus said. "Tell me about the map."

Odo set down his cup. For a long moment he studied Cyrus's face as if comparing it to another face worn underneath, and whatever he found there seemed to settle him.

"You know your grandmother drew it."

"I know her handwriting is on it. I know the ink moves. I know it opens doors that drink blood." Cyrus leaned forward. "What I don't know is why a smuggler's chart leads into the Undercourt. Smugglers go around things, Master Brack. Not down."

"Elira didn't draw it to get in." Odo's voice dropped, and the lamplight made his shadow huge on the stone wall behind him. "She drew it to get *out*. There's a difference, and the whole world's suffering for forgetting it."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above, the tide-bell's last echo had died, and even the fishermen's murmur seemed to have drained away through the floor.

"She went down in the winter of the famine year," Odo said. "You'll have heard the clean version—that she bargained with the mountain spirits for rain. What she bargained with was the court beneath Thornfell. The wards were failing even then; the dragon was already sick, though only the mages knew. The passes were closing. No trade, no tolls, no guild. The Voss ledgers were bleeding out, and the crown was circling like a gull over a gutted fish, waiting to take what was left. So she went down with two of the elders and she made a contract. Her soul, held in their vault, against the guild's survival. Against safe roads. Against twenty years of prosperity that let every one of us eat."

Cyrus heard their own pulse in their ears. "And the map?"

"She came back changed. Quiet. She started drawing at night—charts no courier could use, roads that shifted, ink that wouldn't dry. We thought the bargain had cracked her. It wasn't until near the end that I understood. She wasn't mapping the Undercourt because she loved it. She was mapping it because she meant to go back. To unmake the contract. She hid the true road inside the false ones, and she bound the map to Voss blood so no one else could ever use it against her." His jaw worked. "Then the elders found out."

The word *elders* came out of him like a tooth coming free.

"They were dying, by then. Old men, old women, and the prosperity was ending—the dragon sicker, the wards thinner, the debt coming due the way such debts do. The crown offered pardons. Full amnesty, lands, pensions—for the guild's dissolution and the surrender of all its obligations." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "All its obligations. Do you understand? Every contract the Voss guild held was an asset. Including hers. Including *her*. They sold Elira's contract to the crown's negotiators—who passed it straight down to the Undercourt's agents—to sweeten the pardon. Sold the woman who'd bought their survival, back to the thing she'd sold herself to, and walked away rich."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

Cyrus became aware, distantly, that they were standing. They had no memory of rising. Their hands were braced on the table and the wood creaked under them, and Odo hadn't moved, hadn't reached for any weapon, just sat there taking the full weight of what he'd said like a man standing in the surf on purpose.

"They killed her?" Cyrus heard themselves ask. The voice didn't sound like theirs. It sounded like the ledger's whisper, the one that had been calling their name in dreams.

"No. Worse. They let the contract collect her. The court came up the old road on the night the guild dissolved—came for what it was owed—and the elders opened the gate for it. She fought. Of course she fought; she'd spent a year carving escape routes into parchment. But she was sixty years old and alone, and the people she'd bled for were holding the door open." Odo's eyes were wet and furious. "I was on the wall that night. My job was to keep watch and raise no alarm. I raised no alarm. That's my confession, Cyrus Voss, and you can spend it however you like."

For a moment the cellar tilted. Cyrus saw it all at once—the inn burning, Maren's white face, the riders with their bone masks, the map warm as a living thing against their chest—and understood that every mile of the hunt, every death, every whisper in the dark, was interest accruing on a payment their family had made twenty years ago with money that was never theirs.

"They didn't just betray her," Cyrus said. "They betrayed the *terms*. The contract was hers to keep or break. Selling it—"

"—voided nothing, because the court doesn't recognize sales between thieves," Odo finished. "But it broke the last protection she had. And it left the debt anchored to the bloodline instead of the woman. That's why the riders hunt you, child. That's why the ledger whispers your name. As far as the Undercourt is concerned, the Voss account is still open. Still accruing. And the collateral—" he nodded upward, toward the mountain invisible beyond the town's smoke, "—is the fire that keeps ten thousand people alive."

Wren spoke for the first time, quietly. "The elders' pardons. Are they written contracts too?"

Odo looked at her with something like respect. "Sealed, signed, witnessed. Crown wax and all. Why?"

"Because contracts bind names," she said, "and names can be struck."

Cyrus turned to look at her. She met their eyes, and in the lamplight he saw it there—not a plan yet, but the seed of one, planted in the same soil where his rage was hardening into something with edges. Something usable.

Odo pushed back his chair and went to a loose stone in the wall. From the cavity he drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it, and laid its contents on the table between the cups: a ring of keys gone green with disuse, a folded paper sealed with crown wax, and a small iron token stamped with a key crossed by a thorn.

"My pardon," he said. "Never spent. I couldn't make myself spend it. Twenty years selling herring and hating myself, keeping this, waiting for—" he gestured at Cyrus, at the whole improbable shape of them, "—waiting for the ledger to send someone. Take it. All of it. The seal will open crown archives if your mage needs it. The key still fits the old tollhouse under Kestrel Shelf, if you live that long. And the token—" his voice caught, just barely, "—the token says any Voss man living owes you shelter, passage, and silence. There are four of us left that I know of. Make it five who didn't run."

Cyrus looked at the little iron thorn-key in the lamplight. Their grandmother's sign. Their grandmother's sin, and her courage, and her hidden roads—all of it piled on this table between two cups of tar-house wine.

They took the token. Closed their fist around it until the edges bit.

"When the bell rings tomorrow," Cyrus said, "we're gone, north, before the tide turns. If anyone asks—"

"No one will ask," Odo said. "This town has been asking nothing for twenty years. It's very good at it."

They climbed the ladder into the ordinary dark of the ordinary night, and Saltcinder smelled only of salt and smoke and fish, and the stars over the water were indifferent and bright. At the lodging-house door Wren stopped, and did not touch them, and said, "You're not going to sleep tonight."

"No."

"I could make you something. Valerian. Poppy."

"If I sleep, it whispers." Cyrus opened their hand. The token had printed its shape into their palm, red and exact. "Let it see I'm awake."

* * *

They left at gray dawn, ahead of the bell, ahead of the tide, packs heavier and lighter at once. The causeway was empty, the flats steaming, the gulls already screaming their endless litigation overhead. Halfway across, where the mud-flats widened to a mile of shining gray on either side, Cyrus stopped.

Not because of anything. Because of everything. Because the rage had finally cooled into a shape they recognized—one they'd seen in their grandmother's handwriting, in every careful line of a map drawn by a woman planning her return. Not fury. Direction.

"We're not running anymore," they said.

Wren, a step ahead, turned. Wind pulled at her hood. "No," she agreed. "We're hunting. There's a difference, and I'm given to understand the whole world suffers when people forget it."

Cyrus almost smiled. Then the map warmed against their chest—sharply, suddenly, the way it warmed for danger—and far behind them, back along the causeway, at the edge of hearing, the tide-bell began to ring.

Three strokes. Then a fourth.

There was no fourth stroke. There had never been a fourth stroke.

Somewhere in Saltcinder, behind them, a door was opening that shouldn't be, and the morning light on the water had gone the color of old bone.
