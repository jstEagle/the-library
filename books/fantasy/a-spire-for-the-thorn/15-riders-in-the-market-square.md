## 15. Riders in the Market Square

Saltcinder smelled of brine and burnt sugar in the early morning, and Cyrus had almost—almost—forgotten what it felt like to be hunted.

They had been in the free-town four days. Four days of sleeping in a real bed at the Drowned Lantern, of haggling over rope and salt-pork with coins Odo pressed into their hands as if apology could be minted. Four days of Wren laughing at something a fishwife said, the sound catching Cyrus off guard every time, like a bell rung in an empty room. Four days of pretending the map folded against their ribs was only parchment, that the ink did not stir when they dreamed.

Odo had taken them to the morning market himself, threading the stalls with the ease of a man who had traded in every port from here to the Kestrels. He bought dried figs because Wren liked them. He argued the price of lamp oil down to nothing through sheer force of personality. He walked with a limp he never explained and a knife he never stopped touching, and when Cyrus asked him, once, why he'd left the guild, he had only shrugged and said, "The guild left me first, lad. I just made it official."

Now the market square was screaming.

It began as a silence—a wrongness, the way gulls go quiet before a squall. Then the fishwife's voice rose and broke, and Cyrus turned from the oil-seller's stall and saw them come out of the fog bank off the harbor: three riders on horses that were not entirely horses, moving with the smooth wrong gait of things that had learned to imitate walking. Their masks were bone, yellow-white, featureless but for eye-holes that drank the morning light. Where their hooves struck the cobbles, frost bloomed and spread like spilled milk.

"Down," Odo said. He didn't shout it. He said it the way he'd said everything since Greyhollow—quiet, certain, final—and his hand closed on Cyrus's shoulder and put them behind a stack of lobster pots as if Cyrus weighed nothing at all.

The first scream came from somewhere near the well. Then many screams, overlapping, a fabric tearing down its seams.

"They can't smell the map," Odo murmured. His breath smoked in air that had gone suddenly, impossibly cold. "Only blood. Only Voss blood. So you'll do exactly as I say, and you won't argue, because I've been arguing with fools your whole life and I don't have time for one more."

"Odo—"

"The deep ways." He nodded toward the far corner of the square, where a customs house squatted against the sea wall, its lower courses black with old weed. Beneath it, if he remembered the stories right—and Odo always remembered the stories right—there was a door into the salt galleries, the drowned tunnels the smugglers had used before the guild ever had a name. "There's a hatch under the customs stairs. Iron, painted green, rusted shut. Get through it, follow the water sound, and don't stop for anything that calls you by name."

Wren's hand found Cyrus's in the shadow of the pots. Her fingers were cold. Her eyes were already doing the arithmetic she did so well—the riders, the exits, the crowd packed between them like kindling—and Cyrus watched her arrive at the same answer he did.

"He means to stay," she said quietly.

"I know what he means."

* * *

The bone riders did not gallop. That was the horror of it. They came on at a walk, unhurried as tax collectors, and the crowd broke around them like water around stones, and wherever the frost touched a stall it blackened. One rider turned its mask toward the fishwives huddled by the scales and paused—paused, considering, hungry—and Cyrus understood with a lurch that these were not hunters only. These were foragers. The Undercourt was starving, and the market square was laid out before it like a table.

"No," Cyrus breathed, and started to rise, and Odo's grip closed hard enough to bruise.

"You can't save them by dying here." His voice was gravel-soft. "You save them by getting that map so far under the mountain those things drown chasing it. That's the whole job, lad. It was always the whole job."

A rider's head turned. Slowly. Toward the lobster pots. Toward them.

It had seen something—or sensed something—the flicker of Voss blood quickening in fear. The blank bone face tilted, and beneath the mask came a sound like a page turning in an empty room.

"Now," Odo said, and shoved them both out into the open.

They ran. Cyrus would remember the run in fragments forever: Wren's braid coming loose; a child's abandoned shoe upright and untouched in the middle of all that panic; the frost spreading along a wall in patterns that looked, horribly, like handwriting. Behind them Odo's voice rose over the square—not shouting, still not shouting—calling out in the old trade-tongue of the guild, the cant-talk of passes and tolls, and the effect on the nearest rider was immediate and terrible. It stopped dead. All three stopped, masks swiveling toward him, as if an old debt had spoken its name.

Because it had. Because Odo had been guild once. Because there were thirty years of small signed bargains in his blood, and the riders could taste them.

"The stairs," Cyrus gasped. The customs house loomed. The green hatch sat in its shadow, rust weeping down the stone like old tears.

Wren reached it first, dropped to her knees, and spat a word that made the iron groan. The rust sloughed away in flakes; the hinges shrieked; cold air breathed up from below, smelling of salt and deep stone and something older, mineral, like the inside of the mountain's own lungs.

"In," she said. "Cyrus. *In.*"

Cyrus looked back.

Odo stood in the center of the square with his knife drawn and his lame leg planted wide, an old man in a patched coat facing three riders out of a child's nightmare, and he was smiling. Not bravely. Not sadly. With the plain contentment of a man who has finally found work worth doing.

"Fifty years I ran from that ledger," he called across the square, to Cyrus, past the riders, as if they were not there at all. "Your grandmother gave me my first route when I was fourteen and wet behind the ears. She'd have hated this. She'd have done it anyway." He raised the knife in something between salute and blessing. "Tell the map I said hello."

The first rider moved.

Cyrus did not see the killing. They saw the beginning of it—the rider closing the distance at its terrible unhurried walk, Odo stepping *toward* it, knife low, weight forward, a guildsman's stance older than any of them living—and then Wren's hands were on their collar and Cyrus was falling into the dark of the hatch, and the last thing they heard from the world above was not a scream at all.

It was laughter. Odo's laughter, rough and real, ringing off the sea wall even as the cold came down like a slammed door.

Then Wren wrenched the hatch shut behind them, and the bolt fell, and there was only darkness and the sound of water somewhere ahead, and the small, wrecked noise Cyrus did not realize they were making until Wren's arms came around them in the black.

* * *

The salt galleries ran under the town like veins under skin.

Wren conjured light—her magic came harder now, Cyrus had noticed; each working cost her something visible, a grayness at the lips, a tremor in the fingers—and the flame that bloomed above her palm was thin and blue and threw their shadows long down a tunnel ribbed with salt crystal. The walls glittered. Everything glittered, terribly beautiful, like the inside of a geode, like the court they were walking toward.

They followed the water sound. That was the rule Odo had given and it was the only rule they had. The trickle grew to a stream, the stream to a channel running along a carved gutter that no one had maintained in a hundred years, and still the road held true, sloping gently inland, gently down.

Cyrus walked. That was all they could manage. Put one foot, then the other, and let the grief trail behind them like the sound of the water, always present, never quite catchable.

"He knew," Cyrus said finally. The words came out cracked, unused. "In the Lantern, two nights ago. He told me the riders would come eventually. He said—" Their throat closed. They forced it open. "He said he'd been saving up a debt of his own to spend. I thought he was joking. He was always joking."

"Wren said nothing. She walked close, close enough that their shoulders brushed the salt walls, and when she finally spoke her voice was very gentle.

"People who carry guilt a long time start looking for a place to set it down. He chose his place. That's not your doing, Cyrus."

"It's my name that killed him."

"It's the name that's kept you alive," she said. "That's the whole cruelty of it. Every gift your grandmother left you is double-edged, and you keep getting cut on edges you didn't forge." She lifted her burning hand higher; blue light washed the tunnel, and in it her face looked carved, exhausted, fierce. "So we make it worth something. That's the only memorial anyone gets down here. Worth."

Cyrus laughed—an ugly sound, half sob—and scrubbed a sleeve across their eyes. "Worth," they echoed. And then, quieter, the truth rising like the water beside them: "I'm done running, Wren. Do you hear me? Greyhollow was running. The quarries were running. Saltcinder was running with our eyes shut, sitting in one town four days while the ink crawled on the map and I pretended I didn't feel it pulling. Odo died buying us a head start and I am *done.* Thornfell. The gate. Whatever's inside. We go straight there, we don't detour, we don't hide, we don't wait for the next alley to need holding."

Wren studied them for a long moment. The blue flame shivered between them.

"And after?" she asked softly. "When we're inside and the ledger knows your name? What then?"

"Then I'll deal with that too." Cyrus touched the map through their coat, through the linen, feeling the faint warmth of it, the faint pulse. "My grandmother sold herself to buy a future she thought the family deserved. The elders spent her like coin. Odo spent himself the same way, today, for us. Everyone in this story keeps paying for the Voss name." They began walking again, faster now, down and down toward the mountain's dark root. "It's time somebody ended the account."

Behind and above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and salt, Saltcinder's bells began to ring—slowly at first, then all together, the town mourning its dead and warning its living. Somewhere in that count, Cyrus knew, a name would be read out that they would carry for the rest of their life, however long the ledger allowed it to be theirs.

The tunnel bent. Ahead, where no light should have reached, something glowed—a faint gold seam in the rock, warm as ember, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart.

Wren saw it too. Her breath caught.

"Cyrus," she whispered. "The map isn't waiting for us anymore. It's *coming to meet us.*"

The gold light brightened, and from somewhere deep beneath their feet—far below the galleries, below the drowned roads, deeper than any map had ever dared draw—came a sound like a vast page turning.
