## 16. The Mouth of Thornfell

They smelled the mountain before they saw it.

Three days out of the deep ways, and the air changed—sulfur threading through the pine-bitter cold like a whisper through a locked door. Wren noticed it first. She stopped on the trail with her head tilted, listening to something Cyrus couldn't hear, and said, "It's breathing."

"Mountains don't breathe."

"This one does." She turned to face the wall of grey stone rising ahead of them, higher than any peak they'd crossed, its summit lost in a permanent collar of cloud. "Thornfell's been sealed for two hundred years, but the wards are woven from fire, and fire needs air. Every breath the mountain takes comes from somewhere." Her eyes found Cyrus's, and in them was a fear she didn't bother hiding anymore; they were past that now. "And every breath goes back to somewhere too."

Cyrus pulled their coat tighter and said nothing, because what was there to say? The map had led them here without hesitation for six days of walking—through flooded quarries where royal soldiers' torches had swept the water above their heads, through Saltcinder's burning alleys with Odo's blood still ghosting under their fingernails—and now it lay flat in their hands, quivering like a living thing, all its inked roads converging on a single point at the mountain's root: a black mark shaped like a mouth.

*The gate opens only to Voss blood,* Elira's hidden letter had said. *I made sure of it. Let my heirs walk in when no one else can.*

Cyrus had spent twenty years learning that inheritance was just debt with better manners. Now they walked toward it anyway, because Odo had died buying them this road, because Maren's inn was ash, because a courier had bled out in the snow with a stranger's name on his lips, and because somewhere beneath this stone a dragon's fire was guttering toward darkness, taking ten thousand lives down with it.

The trail ended at noon.

Not gradually—the way trails usually did, thinning into deer paths, then game traces, then suggestion—but abruptly, as though someone had cut it off with a blade. Ahead of them the forest simply stopped, trees leaning away from a sheer cliff face as if afraid to touch it. And set into that cliff, half-swallowed by centuries of lichen and frost-weep, stood a door.

It wasn't a door the way doors were supposed to be. It was a seam in the mountain itself, thirty feet high, its edges carved with spirals so worn they might have been water damage or might have been writing. No handle. No hinges. No keyhole. Just the seam, and the silence pressing against it from both sides.

Wren approached it slowly, one hand raised, palm out, the way she approached anything that might be warded. Cyrus watched frost form on her fingertips—not from cold, she'd told them once, but from magic pulling heat out of the world to feed itself—and watched the frost crawl toward the stone and stop dead at an invisible boundary, as though hitting glass.

"Warded," she confirmed quietly. "Old. Older than the court's working. This isn't a lock someone picked or a seal someone broke." She lowered her hand. "This is a lock that was never meant to open for anyone."

"It opens for Voss blood," Cyrus said. "That's what the letter says."

"Your grandmother wrote that letter two hundred years ago."

"Sixty," Cyrus corrected. "She died before I was born, but Maren knew her. Sixty years is still a long time for a promise carved in stone." They looked up at the seam. "But the map hasn't lied to us yet."

"The map redraws itself every time the Undercourt moves," Wren said. "That's not the same as honest."

"No," Cyrus agreed. "But it's close enough."

They made camp within sight of the gate—Cyrus refused to turn their back on it, some instinct older than reason insisting it needed watching—and ate hardtack and dried plums in silence while the light failed. The mountain breathed around them, long slow inhalations they could feel in the ground, in their teeth. Once, near midnight, Cyrus woke to find Wren sitting up, staring at the seam, which had begun to glow.

Faintly. Barely. A thread of ember-orange tracing the spiral carvings like veins filling with blood, pulsing in time with the mountain's breath—or perhaps with something else entirely, something deeper, something counting down.

"Wren."

"I see it."

"What is it doing?"

She didn't answer immediately. When she did, her voice had gone very careful, the way it did when she was choosing words the way a surgeon chose blades. "It knows we're here."

*

Morning came grey and windless, the kind of morning where sound carried strangely and everything felt provisional, unfinished, a world holding its breath. Cyrus broke camp without speaking, packed the last of their supplies, and walked to the gate with the Ember Map held open before them like a shield or a confession.

Up close, the carvings resolved into language. Not any script Wren recognized—she traced the spirals with her eyes and muttered about pre-court glyphwork, about tongues that predated the surface kingdoms entirely—but Cyrus recognized something else in them. A rhythm. A repetition. The same phrase, over and over, winding around the doorframe like a rope coiled around a wrist:

*Voss. Voss. Voss. Voss.*

"They carved our name into a mountain," Cyrus said softly.

"Or the mountain grew around your name." Wren stood beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Names have weight, Cyrus. You know that better than anyone—you've been carrying yours like a sack of stones since the day you were born. Imagine what happens when a name gets carved into living rock and left there for sixty years to sink in."

Cyrus drew the knife.

It was a smuggler's knife, plain and well-used, the edge honed on a hundred different whetstones across a hundred different passes. It had opened crates and cut ropes and, twice, opened human flesh in defense of Cyrus's life. It had never been used for ceremony. But ceremony, Cyrus had learned, was just violence wearing its good clothes, and this—this was going to be violence either way.

"If this doesn't work," Cyrus said, "we're standing in front of a locked door on a hunted road with no supplies and no way back that doesn't run through Saltcinder's bones."

"If this works," Wren countered, "we're walking into a mountain full of ghosts and bone-masked riders and a court that eats souls, following a map drawn by your dead grandmother, betting everything on a technicality you learned three days ago."

"So we're agreed it's terrible."

"Completely terrible." She took their free hand—the left, leaving the knife-hand unbound—and squeezed it once, hard, the way she did instead of saying things her pride wouldn't let her say. Then she let go, stepped back, and became the mage again: arms folded, watchful, ready to burn the world down around them if it came to that. "Do it."

Cyrus pressed the knife's edge to the meat of their right palm, just below the thumb, and hesitated—one heartbeat, two—with the strange clarity that sometimes came at the exact moment before pain. They thought of Maren's face in the firelight, saying *burn it*. They thought of Odo turning to face the riders alone. They thought of a woman they'd never met, sixty years dead, whose handwriting felt more familiar than their own.

Then they cut.

The pain was clean, bright, almost a relief after days of dull dread. Blood welled up dark and immediate, and Cyrus slapped their palm against the stone before they could think better of it, smearing red across the carved spirals, across the repeated name, across sixty years of waiting stone.

For a moment, nothing.

Then the mountain drank.

There was no other word for it. The blood vanished into the rock like rain into parched earth, and the stone *shuddered*, a groan rolling up through the cliff face and into the forest behind them, startling birds from a hundred trees. The spiral glyphs flared ember-orange, brighter than the night-glow, bright enough to throw shadows, and the seam began to move.

Not swinging. Not sliding. *Unclenching.* The mountain's mouth opening tooth by grinding tooth, stone dragging against stone with a sound like the world's largest millstone, dust sifting down in golden curtains through the new light spilling from within. And what light! Not sunlight—something warmer, redder, older, the color of embers banked for the night, the color of the forge before dawn, the color of—

"Fire," Wren breathed beside them. "Deep fire. That's the dragon's light. Cyrus, that's *Ashvane's* light, still burning, still—" Her voice cracked. "We're not too late."

"Not yet," Cyrus said, and stepped forward into the mountain's open mouth.

*

Inside was a road.

A single passage, wide enough for four abreast, its walls smooth as river stone and veined with the same ember-light that had lit the gate. Warm air flowed outward past them, smelling of sulfur and hot metal and, faintly, inexplicably, of baking bread—a smell so domestic, so utterly wrong for this place, that Cyrus laughed aloud, a short startled bark that echoed down the passage and came back changed, multiplied, as though the mountain were repeating it thoughtfully to itself.

"Don't laugh in there," Wren said sharply. "Sound carries differently underground. Things learn your voice faster when you give it to them freely."

Cyrus sobered. "What kind of things?"

"The kind that live in a court that trades memories for warmth." She stepped through the threshold beside them, and Cyrus watched her shudder as the gate's boundary-crossing washed over her—some pressure, some weight, like wading through deep water. "Everything below has an appetite, Cyrus. Remember that. Everything below is hungry, and most of it is polite about it."

Behind them, the gate ground shut.

The sound was final in a way few sounds ever managed to be—not loud, exactly, but absolute, a period at the end of a sentence, a door closing on a life. Cyrus turned in time to see the last thread of daylight narrow, flicker, and vanish, and felt something twist in their chest that they refused to name as grief. There would be time for grief later, if there was a later. Right now there was only the road, and the ember-light, and Wren's hand finding theirs in the sudden dimness.

They stood there a moment, hand in hand at the mouth of the deep ways, two people with no guild and no home and no names worth carving in stone anymore, about to bet the last thing they had on a dead woman's cleverness and a living woman's stubbornness.

"For the record," Cyrus said quietly, "if we die in here, I want it noted that I saved your life first. Kestrel Shelf. Rope bridge. You're welcome."

"For the record," Wren said, and there was laughter in her voice, fragile but real, the kind of laughter people made on purpose when the alternative was screaming, "you also dragged me out of a cage in Greyhollow, which makes us even, and you stole my rations for a week afterward, which makes you a thief."

"A smuggler. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Not anymore." Cyrus squeezed her hand. "Ready?"

"No," Wren said honestly. "But I've stopped pretending that matters."

Together, hand in hand, they turned and walked into the breathing dark, and the mountain closed its teeth behind them like a secret being kept.

The ember-light pulsed once, twice, and then began to lead them downward, along roads that remembered a woman named Elira, toward a court that had been waiting sixty years for a Voss to come home.

And far above, muffled by miles of stone, the last dragon's fire guttered, and guttered, and held.
