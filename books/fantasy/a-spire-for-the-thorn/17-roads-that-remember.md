## 17. Roads That Remember

The gate closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Cyrus stood in the dark beyond it, palm still stinging where the stone had drunk their blood, and listened to the mountain breathe.

That was the only word for it. The passage ahead swelled and narrowed in slow rhythm—not wind, not water, but something muscular and vast, the corridors themselves expanding like the ribs of a sleeping animal. Dust stirred against Cyrus's cheek on the inhale. On the exhale, warmth rolled past them from somewhere deep below, carrying a smell of hot iron and old honey and, beneath it all, something faintly sweet that made the back of their teeth ache.

"It knows we're here," Wren said softly. Her mage-light hovered between them, a bead of pale gold that threw their shadows long down the throat of the passage. "Or rather—it remembers you're here."

"Remembers?"

"The walls aren't stone the way we think of stone." She pressed her hand flat against the nearest surface. It gave, slightly, like skin over muscle. "This is memory given weight. Thornfell's deep ways were never carved by picks, Cyrus. They were *grown*, by whatever bargains the old families struck—and the Voss bargain runs deepest of all. These halls are shaped out of what your grandmother knew."

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "Then it's going to be full of things I don't want to know."

Wren looked at them—really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether to say the kind thing or the true one. She'd been choosing true more often, lately. Cyrus was beginning to suspect it was her way of being kind.

"Probably," she said. "Come on."

* * *

The first fragment came an hour in, though time down there moved strangely, stretching like taffy between the mountain's breaths.

They rounded a bend where the corridor split three ways, and the left-hand wall lit up.

Not mage-light—something warmer, amber as lamplight through whiskey, and moving. Figures swam up inside the stone the way fish rise under ice. A young woman with a lantern, dark hair braided tight for running, ducking beneath a beam that no longer existed. She pressed herself into an alcove, killed her light, and waited while boots passed overhead—boots that echoed from nowhere, belonging to no one, a sound the mountain had kept the way a shell keeps the sea.

Cyrus stopped walking so fast they nearly stumbled.

"That's—" Their voice cracked. They tried again. "That's my grandmother."

"You're certain?"

"I've seen her face every day of my life." Maren kept a portrait above the inn's hearth, Elira Voss at forty, sharp-eyed and unsmiling, a woman who looked like she was listening to a room rather than to anyone in it. The girl in the wall was twenty years younger than the portrait and wore the same expression exactly. "She used to run this route. Before I was born. Before any of it went wrong."

The fragment played itself out with dreamlike patience. Young Elira counted her breaths in the alcove—Cyrus found themselves counting along, absurdly, heart climbing with hers—then slipped out, crossed the passage, and pried loose a stone that wasn't a stone. Behind it: a niche, and in the niche a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She took it. She left a copper coin in its place, tails-up, some smuggler's courtesy to whoever came next. Then the amber light dimmed, and the figures sank back into the rock like sediment settling, and the wall was only a wall again, breathing slowly in the dark.

"There," Wren said quietly, pointing.

Where the vision had been, the corridor now continued—straight, clean, wide enough for two. Where before there had been three branches, two of them choked with rubble older than the guild itself.

"The road opened because she remembered it," Cyrus said. Understanding arrived cold and enormous, settling over their shoulders like a wet cloak. "It's not showing me the way. It's asking her. And she's—she's answering."

"She's answering *you*." Wren took their hand. Her fingers were cold; mage-work always left her chilled, like she'd been standing in a river. "You're Voss blood at a Voss door. The mountain can't tell the difference between you yet. Maybe it never will."

They walked on, down the road a dead woman had opened for them.

* * *

The fragments came faster after that, as if the first one had taught the mountain they were willing to watch.

A stretch of corridor where the air smelled suddenly of pipe smoke and wet wool, and the walls filled with a tavern scene rendered in amber and shadow: Elira at a table with three men, laughing at something, her laugh silent but unmistakably hers—the way her whole face committed to it, eyes vanishing, shoulders shaking. One of the men slid a folded paper across the table. Her laugh died mid-motion. Cyrus watched her read it twice, fold it away, and finish her drink without another smile. The other two men kept talking. She was already somewhere else entirely, behind her own eyes, doing sums they couldn't see.

"I know that look," Cyrus murmured. "I make that look."

"I know," Wren said. "I've seen it. Usually right before you do something reckless."

The wall faded. The road bent left, following her memory of leaving.

Farther on, a chamber widened into a rough dome, and the ceiling bloomed with light: a night sky, stars in their proper winter constellations, and beneath them a memory of Elira lying on her back on some hillside Cyrus didn't recognize, teaching a small child to find the Kestrel. The child's voice came thin and strange through the stone, high and piping, and Cyrus's whole body went rigid before they understood—

"That's my mother." The words fell out of them like stones down a well. "She can't have been more than six."

He'd never thought of Maren as small. Maren was granite, Maren was the inn's foundation stones, Maren was arms like dock rope and a voice that could call across a valley. But the child in the ceiling was tiny, mitten-handed, pointing wrong at the sky, and Elira laughed her whole-face laugh and guided the small finger north until it found the right star.

"Maren told me our grandmother died before she was born," Cyrus said. Their voice sounded far away to their own ears. "She told me that for thirty years. And here she is, learning constellations from her."

"People lie about the dead when the truth is dangerous." Wren's light drifted upward, illuminating the stars from below until they seemed to burn. "Your family has been lying since before you could walk. It doesn't mean they didn't love each other. Sometimes it means exactly that."

The ceiling dimmed. The child and the woman dissolved into the stone. And the road ahead opened again—wider now, sloping down, the breath of the mountain growing deeper and slower, as if they were descending toward its heart.

* * *

They camped in a side pocket that Elira's memory obligingly revealed—a smuggler's cache, dry-floored, with niches for candles whose stubs had crumbled to wax ghosts decades ago. Wren set wards in a ring around their bedrolls, thin silver threads of working that hummed almost below hearing. Cyrus built a fire from the last of their wood and tried not to look at the walls.

The walls looked back anyway.

Sleep took them in pieces, and in the gaps between pieces, the mountain spoke.

Not in words. In scenes. Lying half-dreaming, Cyrus watched the amber light move behind their eyelids: Elira crossing a frozen river at dawn with contraband strapped to her back, testing the ice with each step like a prayer. Elira arguing with a man whose face was blurred, always blurred, some trick of the stone or some mercy of it—arguing with her hands as much as her voice, furious, grief-stricken, while behind her a door of black glass stood open onto darkness. Elira kneeling. Elira signing something. Elira walking out of the black door alone, and the door closing, and her standing in the empty hall with her hands pressed flat against her thighs like a woman holding herself together with pressure alone.

Cyrus woke with tears freezing on their lashes and didn't remember starting to cry.

"Cyrus." Wren was already awake, sitting up, watching them with an expression caught between alarm and helplessness. "You were saying her name. Over and over. *Elira, don't, Elira, don't.*"

"It showed me the night she sold herself." Cyrus sat up. The fire had burned to coals; their shadows leaned close around it. "Or the shape of it. The faces won't come clear. Just her. Just how she looked after." They scrubbed a hand down their face. "Twenty years I thought the Voss luck ran out because the world turned hard. It didn't turn hard. We *spent* it. She spent us, buying us time, and everyone who came after just—lived in the house her soul paid for and called it inheritance."

Wren moved around the coals and sat beside them, shoulder to shoulder, the way she did when words weren't the point. For a while neither of them spoke. The mountain breathed. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of living stone, wind moved over Thornfell's shoulder.

"When I was cast out," Wren said at last, "my mentor told me something I hated. She said guilt is just love with nowhere to go." Her hand found Cyrus's in the dark. "You can't carry her sin for her. But you can carry her the way these walls do. Not the sale. The rest of it. The river crossings. The coins left tails-up. Teaching a child the stars."

Cyrus turned their hand over and laced their fingers through Wren's. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. You were too busy being dramatic to notice."

Despite everything—despite the bone riders and the burning inn and Odo's blood on Saltcinder cobbles—Cyrus laughed. It came out ragged, but it came. Above them, faintly, the mountain's breath hitched, almost like laughter too.

* * *

Morning—or what passed for morning, the body's stubborn insistence on hours—found them moving again, and the mountain had changed its mood.

The passages grew grander as they descended. Ceilings arched high enough to swallow Wren's light whole. Veins of mineral glittered in the walls like frost on a window, and the amber memories came thicker now, overlapping, sometimes two or three playing at once in different stretches of stone, so that walking the corridor felt like walking through a house where every window framed a different hour of one woman's life.

Elira haggling with a ferryman. Elira binding a wound on a stranger's arm with the brisk tenderness of someone who'd done it a hundred times. Elira standing at the mouth of a cave with snow in her hair, looking back at the world above with an expression Cyrus recognized down to the bone, because they'd worn it every day since the courier died in the snow: the look of someone measuring exactly how much trouble they were in and deciding, against all evidence, to walk into it anyway.

"She was good," Cyrus said quietly. They hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Everyone talks about her like she was hard. Cold. The great Elira Voss, all ledger and iron. But she wasn't. Look at her. She *cared*. About all of it. Every job, every person."

"That's what made the bargain possible." Wren's voice was gentle and terrible. "The Undercourt doesn't buy from people who don't love anything. It waits for the ones who love too much to let something break."

The road forked. Without discussion, without even slowing, both of them took the branch where the amber light burned brightest—as if Elira's memory were a lamp left in a window, and they were simply coming home.

And then the corridor opened into a hall that stopped Cyrus's feet as surely as a wall.

It was a map.

Not drawn—*built*. The floor spread out before them in polished black stone, and veins of gold ran through it in branching lines, rivers and roads and passes, and Cyrus knew them, knew every one, because they had spent three weeks studying their grandmother's Ember Map by candlelight in safehouses and way-stations. This was the same chart, rendered vast, the deep ways of Thornfell laid out like a city plan. And at its center, where the map on vellum showed a blank space marked only with a small inked thorn, the golden lines converged on a raised dais shaped like a coiled serpent—or a spire—or a question.

Above the dais, suspended in nothing, hung a single point of red light. It pulsed. Slowly. Like a heartbeat. Like a guttering lamp.

"Ashvane," Wren whispered. Her wards flickered up along her arms unbidden, silver bristling. "That's the dragon's fire. We're seeing it. Through the mountain's own memory." She turned to Cyrus, and in the red pulse her face looked carved, ancient, afraid. "Cyrus, it's smaller than it should be. I've read the old surveys—the heartflame should blaze like a second sun down here. That's an ember. That's a candle in a draft."

The red light pulsed again, weaker. Cyrus felt the mountain feel it—a shudder running through the floor, through the walls, through the slow tidal breath of the passages, a flinch of pain too vast to be anything but honest.

"How long?" Cyrus asked.

"Days. Maybe less." Wren was already calculating, lips moving, hands sketching signs in the air. "If the wards fail while we're still inside—"

"Then we don't let them fail while we're still inside." Cyrus stepped onto the golden roads, onto their grandmother's knowledge made solid, and looked down the longest line of all—the one that ran straight as a thrown spear toward the pulsing red heart of the mountain. "Show me her fastest road," they said aloud, to the walls, to the woman in them. "You got everything else through. Get us through too."

For a long moment, nothing. The mountain breathed. The ember pulsed.

Then, all along the great black floor, the golden lines began to move—flowing, rearranging, converging—until a single road blazed brighter than all the rest, running down and down into the dark, toward the heartbeat of the dying fire.

And beside it, rising out of the stone in letters of amber light, in a hand Cyrus had known since childhood from the flyleaves of Maren's books, three words:

*Hurry, little thief.*
