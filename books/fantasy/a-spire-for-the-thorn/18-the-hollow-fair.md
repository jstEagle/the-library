## 18. The Hollow Fair

The road changed its mind about being a road.

Wren felt it before she saw it—the stone under her boots softening in its rhythm, the way a heartbeat changes when someone is about to speak. She had spent six years studying the Undercourt's magic from behind barred windows and worse reputations, and she knew the signs of a threshold the way sailors know weather: a taste like struck flint on the tongue, a warmth rising from below that had no business existing this deep, and a sound—distant, patient, enormous—that might have been music if music had ever learned to grieve.

"Cyrus." She caught their sleeve. "Stop."

Cyrus stopped. They had learned to do that when Wren used that particular tone, the one she'd once used on novices who were about to touch things they shouldn't. Ahead of them, the passage widened without warning, walls falling away like hands opening, and the dark ahead was not empty.

It was lit.

Not with firelight, exactly. With something older and hungrier than fire—a glow the color of candleflame seen through amber, or through skin. It rose from a vast hollow where the mountain had been hollowed and hollowed again, tier upon tier descending into warmth and murk, and it was full of people.

Or what had been people.

"The border market," Wren breathed. "The Hollow Fair."

"I've heard smugglers talk about it." Cyrus's voice was flat, which meant they were frightened; Wren had learned their tells by now, the way fear flattened them into something economical and hard. "Half of them were drunk when they talked about it. Half the stories contradict the other half."

"Most of the stories are wrong because most of the storytellers came back." Wren adjusted the strap of her satchel, feeling for the chalk stubs and salt phials she'd carried since Greyhollow. "The ones who didn't come back don't tell stories."

Below, the fair moved. Figures drifted between stalls built of rib-bone and woven shadow, of things Wren recognized as court-craft and things she didn't recognize at all. And everywhere, threading between the stalls like current between reeds, were the ghosts.

They were not the wispy, sorrowful things of chapel tales. These ghosts had weight—or wanted it. Some wore coats stiff with frost. Some huddled around braziers that burned nothing visible, holding their hands out to warmth they could only borrow. One sat at the mouth of an alley made of stacked crates, turning a copper coin over and over in fingers that could not feel it, watching it catch the amber light with the devotion of the starving watching bread.

"They trade," Cyrus said quietly. "Memories for warmth."

"You can see that?"

"I can see that they're cold." Cyrus's jaw tightened. "Everyone down here is cold."

They descended together. There was no other way forward—the map's living ink had crawled itself into a single bright thread pointing down—and besides, Wren thought grimly, there were worse things than walking openly into a market. There was sneaking into one and being caught at it.

* * *

The Fair smelled of snowmelt and roasting chestnuts and something beneath both, a smell like the inside of a locked room, like air kept too long. Wren catalogued it automatically, the old discipline asserting itself even now, even here: *sensory anchors, ground yourself, name what you perceive so it cannot unname you.* That last part was not standard academy training. That last part was her own invention, purchased with scars.

The vendors turned to look at them as they passed. Not all of them were dead.

A woman selling candied fruit from a lacquered cart had a pulse Wren could see fluttering at her throat—living, then, but living *here*, which raised questions Wren did not want answered. A man weighing out pinches of grey powder from brass scales had eyes like coin slots. And everywhere the ghosts, pressing close to any source of heat, drifting after Cyrus and Wren themselves because two warm-blooded bodies walking through the Fair were worth more than any brazier.

One touched Wren's shoulder. She felt it as a pressure of cold, precise as a fingertip, though nothing solid met her coat.

"Please," the ghost said. It had been a young man once; he still had the haircut of a soldier, cropped short against a helmet long since rotted elsewhere. "Just a little. I have a good one. My mother's kitchen. Bread on the sill. I'd only sell the smell of it, not the memory itself—I'm not a cheat, I'd never sell the whole—"

Wren stopped. Against every rule she had ever written for herself, she stopped.

"I don't carry warmth to give," she said.

"You do." His face—grey, translucent at the edges, but a face, with longing in it like a wound—tilted toward hers. "You're burning. Both of you. Living people burn so wastefully. You spend your warmth on breathing. On *walking*. You could spare a coal's worth and never miss it, and I would give you—" He fumbled at his breast, producing nothing, holding up nothing, offering everything. "I have my mother's kitchen. I have my wedding day. I have the last thing my brother said to me. Name your price."

"Wren." Cyrus's hand found her arm. Their grip was careful. "We can't."

"I know we can't."

"He's starving."

"I know he's starving." Wren looked at the ghost, and hated every institution that had ever taught her the correct response to suffering was procedure. "I'm sorry. We have nothing to trade that wouldn't cost us what we came to keep."

The ghost's shoulders bowed. But he nodded—slow, courteous, terrible in his courtesy. "You're going down, aren't you. To the Court proper." When neither of them answered, he smiled, and the smile was worse than weeping. "Then find Sable-Handed Ilse. Find her before anyone else finds you. Tell her Tam sent you, and that I remember our bargain fondly, and she'll hear you out. She remembers bargains. It's all she does now."

He drifted back toward his brazier that burned nothing, and folded himself around its absence of heat like a man folding around a wound.

* * *

Sable-Handed Ilse kept a stall near the Fair's heart, and Wren knew it was hers before anyone told her, because it was the only stall in the whole amber-lit hollow that was tidy.

Every other vendor's goods sprawled—jars of murmuring memories, bolts of secondhand warmth, trays of small bright moments pinned like moths. Ilse's stall was a single counter of black wood, swept clean, bearing three items arranged in a row: a bell with no clapper, a key with no teeth, and a ledger bound in something Wren refused, on principle, to identify.

Ilse herself was tall and narrow as a closed door. Her right hand was indeed black—not gloved, not stained, but black the way deep water is black, absorbing the amber light rather than reflecting it. Her left hand was ordinary, chapped, human. She was alive. Wren was almost certain of it. Almost certain was a dangerous amount of certain, down here.

She did not wait for them to speak.

"Tam sent you." Her voice was dry paper. "He always sends me the ones with kind faces. Thinks kindness will move me. Kindness moves nothing in this market except prices." Her coin-slot gaze went from Wren to Cyrus and back. "You're the mage who unmade herself. And you're the Voss."

Wren felt Cyrus go still beside her, the stillness of a drawn blade deciding whether to be sheathed.

"We haven't told anyone our names down here," Cyrus said carefully.

"You didn't need to. The mountain told the Fair. The Fair tells everyone; that's what markets are *for*." Ilse tapped the black counter with one ordinary fingernail. "Sit. Stand. Whatever you do, listen. You're going to the Court, and you're going to try to take something out that doesn't belong to either of you, and you are going to fail unless you understand what you're walking among. I sell understanding. Today I'll give it free, because Tam asked, and because—" the ghost of some expression crossed her living half of a face— "because I liked your grandmother, Voss. She overpaid for a cup of soup once and never let me forget it."

Cyrus's breath caught audibly.

"She came through here?" Wren asked.

"Twice. Once going down, once coming up. Coming up, she looked like a woman who had signed something and regretted the shape of her own signature." Ilse's black hand rested on the ledger. "Which brings us to the lesson. Free of charge, so pay attention, because free things in this market are the most expensive things on offer."

She opened the ledger. Wren leaned forward despite herself—and saw that the pages were blank. Not empty. *Blank*, in the way that a scream leaves silence blank afterward.

"The Court keeps accounts," Ilse said. "That is the first thing surface folk never understand and the last thing they learn. Your courts above have laws, and laws bend, and clever people bend them. The Undercourt has *accounts*, and accounts do not bend. Every bargain struck under Thornfell is entered. Every soul sold is a line item. Every debt accrues interest measured in years of warmth." Her finger traced a blank page. "And every account has a name attached. That is the whole secret of the place, mage. Not power. Not hunger, though it starves, yes, it starves, it has starved for centuries. *Names.*"

"Why names?" Cyrus asked. Their voice was rough.

"Because a name is how the Court holds what it owns. Sell your soul and the contract needs a signature. Owe a debt and the collector needs to know whom to collect from. The ledger takes names the way a hook takes fish—not cruelly. Efficiently." Ilse looked up. "And here is the part you must carve into your bones before you go another step downward. The ledger takes names, and a nameless one cannot leave."

Silence pooled between them. Somewhere across the Fair, a ghost laughed at some joke thirty years dead, and the sound was the loneliest thing Wren had ever heard.

"What does that mean, exactly?" she said. "Nameless."

"It means what it says. The exits of the Undercourt are doors with locks, and the locks are shaped like names. To pass outward, the door asks you who you are. Answer truly, answer with the name the ledger knows, and you pass. Arrive at the door and have no name to give—" Ilse spread her ordinary hand, an oddly gentle gesture— "and the door has no reason to open. You become a resident. Permanently. There are ghosts in this Fair who are not ghosts, mage. There are people who walked in with names and traded those names away piece by piece—one memory here, one promise there, a bargain too clever by half—and discovered at the end that they had sold themselves the lock off their own door." Her black hand turned palm-up. "I nearly did, once. Hence the hand. Hence the ledger business. I keep accounts now myself. Small ones. Honest ones. It's the only armor this market respects."

Cyrus was very quiet. Wren watched them—the set of their shoulders, the way their thumb worried the strap of the satchel where the Ember Map rode against their ribs—and saw the thought forming behind their eyes, the way ideas formed in Cyrus: slowly, stubbornly, like ice building across a pond, until it would bear weight.

*They're thinking about names,* Wren realized. *Their name. What it's worth. What it costs.*

She said nothing. Not yet. But she marked it, and she was afraid.

* * *

They bought nothing at Ilse's stall, but they stayed an hour anyway, because Ilse talked the way dying men talk—compulsively, precisely, hoarding nothing—and what she gave them was worth more than goods.

She drew the layout of the Fair in spilled sugar on her counter: the tiers of stalls, the alleys where the Court's bone riders patrolled (slower here, she warned, more courteous here, because even riders must observe market law), the road down to the Court proper and the checkpoints along it. She told them which vendors were alive and which were something else wearing aliveness like a borrowed coat. She told them the herald's name—not his true name, no one had that, but the name he wore, the name he offered others, *Vessin*, and Wren filed it away with a scholar's reflex: *a false name given freely is often a leash offered politely.*

"And the vault," Cyrus said finally. "Where they keep the souls."

Ilse's sugar-drawn streets lay scattered under her palm before Cyrus finished asking.

"No," she said. "Ask me anything about the market. Ask me nothing about the vault. If I draw you a map to the vault, the ledger enters it, and then I owe the Court a favor, and the Court's favors are how people like me end up with hands like this." She lifted the black hand slightly, letting the amber light drown in it. "You'll find the vault yourselves or you won't. You have blood that opens stones, Voss. Use it sparingly. Blood that opens stones also opens *accounts*."

They rose to leave. As they did, Ilse spoke once more, quieter, and Wren understood this was the true payment Tam had purchased with his kindness and his mother's kitchen and his brother's last words.

"One more thing. For the mage." Her coin-slot eyes fixed on Wren. "You came down here planning to burn yourself as fuel. Don't insult me; I've watched a hundred mages walk this market and they all walk the same, measuring everything against what it will cost them personally, pricing their own bodies like goods. The Court will let you. The Court *prefers* it. A mage who spends herself is a mage who never asks the better question."

"Which is?"

"What the system cannot survive losing." Ilse began sweeping her sugar streets back into a jar, unhurried. "Contracts need signatures. Accounts need names. The whole edifice runs on the assumption that everyone who enters it wants to leave it carrying themselves intact. Consider, before you reach the Court, whether intactness is really the prize you think it is—and whose rules taught you to want it." She glanced up, and for one moment the market-hardened dryness cracked, and underneath was just a woman, tired, alive, one hand ruined by an old mercy. "Tam thinks you're kind. I think you're dangerous. Try to be both. It confuses them."

* * *

They walked the Fair's edge as the amber light dimmed—though whether it dimmed toward evening or merely exhaled, Wren couldn't say. Down here the hours were negotiable.

Cyrus hadn't spoken in a long time. Wren counted it: four hundred paces of silence, past a stall selling bottled laughter gone flat, past a ghost choir rehearsing a hymn none of them fully remembered, their harmonies fraying at the edges like cloth worn thin. Finally Cyrus stopped at a railing of black iron overlooking the Fair's lowest tier, where the road to the Court descended into a gate of ribbed stone, and leaned on the rail, and looked down at all that borrowed warmth.

"My grandmother came through here," Cyrus said. "Twice. She overpaid for soup."

"Yes."

"I keep trying to picture it." Their voice was low. "Elira Voss, standing in this exact spot. Did she lean on a rail? Did she look down at all this and think, *I can beat this*?" A short, bitter breath. "Because that's what I'm thinking. And I know—*know*—that's what she thought. And look what it bought her. A lantern in a vault. Twenty years of a guild built on her sale. Me."

Wren came to stand beside them. Below, the ghosts moved through their market, buying warmth they could not keep, selling memories they could not bear, and every transaction was a small tragedy conducted with perfect courtesy.

"Ilse said something," Cyrus continued. "About names. About the doors asking who you are." They turned, and in the amber light their eyes were the color of banked coals. "What if the answer wasn't a name at all? What if you walked up to the door and it asked *who are you*, and the true answer was—" they gestured, at the Fair, at the mountain, at Wren— "*this*. Here. Now. With you. Not Voss. Not heir. Not a line in anyone's book."

"Cyrus." Wren kept her voice level with effort. "Do you hear what you're describing?"

"A way out that isn't a lie."

"A way out that's a grave. Ilse told us plainly—nameless ones don't leave. You start pulling threads from your name down *here*, in front of the ledger's own market, and—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, softer. "Whatever you're building in your head, build it slowly. Build it where the walls don't have ears. Because I have watched one person I loved feed themselves to a contract already, and I am telling you now, in front of every ghost in this pit: not you. Whatever the plan is, whatever the clever technicality is, not *you*."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind their expression—surprise, maybe, or the strange new tenderness that had been growing between them in the deep places, tenderness like green shoots through rubble. Then they smiled, small and crooked and entirely too calm.

"Noted," they said.

It was the tone of someone agreeing to a discussion, not a conclusion. Wren knew the difference. She had negotiated with courts.

Before she could press, the Fair changed.

It happened all at once, the way weather changes on a mountain. The amber light guttered. Across every tier, the ghosts went still—not settling still, but *startled* still, heads lifting like deer scenting smoke. The braziers burning nothing burned it brighter. And from the ribbed gate at the bottom of the hollow, from the direction of the Court proper, came a sound Wren had heard described in banned texts and never hoped to hear firsthand: the tolling of a bell with no clapper.

Like the one on Ilse's counter. Like all of them, everywhere, ringing at once.

The Fair's crowd began to move—all of it, all at once, flowing away from the lower gate like water finding its level. And through the parted market, ascending the great stair from the depths, came figures in bone masks, walking not with the patrol's courtesy but with *purpose*, and at their center, robed in a darkness that drank the amber light the way Ilse's hand drank it, a slender figure Wren recognized from a thousand forbidden illustrations.

The herald. Vessin. Come up from the Court.

And he was not searching the Fair. He was not scanning the crowd. He was climbing directly, unhurriedly, toward the railing where two living trespassers stood exposed in the thinning light—with the smile of a host who has known exactly where his guests were since the moment they arrived.

"Wren," Cyrus said quietly. "Time to not be here."

But the crowd had closed behind them like water, and the stair was the only way, and the herald was already smiling up at them through the amber dark.

"*There* you are," the bone mask seemed to say, though no words crossed the distance yet. "The Court has been waiting so patiently."

The bell tolled again, and every ghost in the Hollow Fair turned its hungry face upward—to the two warm, living, named things standing at the rail, worth more than every brazier in the market combined.
