## 19. Masks at the Table

The invitation came the way frost comes—silently, everywhere at once, and without any possibility of refusal.

Cyrus woke in the guest-chamber they had not chosen, in a bed they did not remember lying down upon, beneath a ceiling of black stone veined with slow-moving silver. The Hollow Fair's ghost-merchant had warned them away from the palace road. They had taken it anyway, because the map said to, because the roads here obeyed nothing but memory and appetite, and now the mountain's deep heart had simply *decided* to host them.

On the pillow beside Cyrus's head lay a card of grey vellum. The writing was not ink. It was a groove pressed into the paper itself, as if the words had been carved from the inside out:

*The Court of Beneath requests the pleasure of the last Voss at table. Attire will be provided. Hunger will be patient.*

*Hunger will be* — Cyrus read it three times before the shape of the sentence settled into their stomach like a swallowed stone.

The door opened on its own. Beyond it stood a servant—or something shaped like one—wearing a livery of woven shadow and a face like smooth river clay, featureless except where suggestion made features: a dip for eyes, a soft ridge for a mouth. In its hands it carried folded garments, dark silk shot through with threads that glimmered faintly ember-orange when they moved.

"The herald bids you dress," it said. Its voice was the sound of wind through a keyhole. "And the other one also."

"The other one," Cyrus repeated. "You mean Wren."

The servant tilted its head, considering the word as if tasting an unfamiliar spice. "The mage. Yes."

* * *

Wren had been awake for hours.

She had felt the summons coming since midnight—a pressure behind her sternum, the old familiar tug of court magic reaching for her, though this court's pull felt different from the surface crown's. The royal summonses of her former life had been chains dressed in velvet: formal language, gilded seals, the implicit threat of ruin for noncompliance. This was simpler. This was hunger. It didn't ask. It opened its mouth, and you found yourself already walking into it.

She sat at the chamber's narrow window, watching the Undercourt breathe.

That was the only word for it. The city below her window—if city was the right name for a sprawl of spires and hanging gardens grown entirely of pale root and phosphorescent fungus—expanded and contracted in a slow rhythm, streets shifting a hand's width each cycle, bridges lengthening, towers leaning closer together like conspirators. She had spent three years of her exile studying soul-contracts, and she knew what fed architecture like this. Not stone. Not magic, exactly.

*Memory.* The whole court was built out of eaten things—the memories of the dead, worn as mortar; the warmth of names, burned as fuel.

And it was thinning. She could see it now that she looked properly. The glow in the fungal gardens stuttered. The rhythm of the breathing city had a hitch in it, a catch like a singer running out of breath. Whatever the dragon's fire had once poured down through Thornfell's roots, the flow had slowed to a trickle, and everything down here was quietly, patiently starving.

A knock she hadn't heard approaching. The clay-faced servant stood in her doorway with the same folded silks.

"I don't dress for my enemies," Wren said.

"You misunderstand," the servant replied, mild as milk. "You are not dressing for the enemy. You are dressing for dinner. It is the enemy who dresses for you." It set the clothes down and withdrew, and Wren stared after it for a long moment, feeling the truth of the thing land somewhere uncomfortable.

She dressed. Of course she dressed. But she hid three sigils of unbinding in the seams first, stitched them herself with thread pulled from her own hem, and told no one—not even the room, not even the listening dark—that she'd done it.

* * *

The banquet hall defied geometry the way dreams do, accepting the impossibility without apology.

They walked to it along a corridor that lengthened obligingly underfoot, Cyrus and Wren side by side, both clad in the provided silks—hers charcoal, his ember-dark, the colors of coals at two stages of dying. Servants lined the way, clay-faced and silent, holding candles whose flames burned downward, dripping cold light onto the floor.

"They're watching us," Cyrus murmured.

"Everything here is watching us," Wren murmured back. "Walk like we expected this."

The hall itself was long and low, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, lit by a thousand candles arranged in patterns that hurt to look at directly—spirals within spirals, arrangements that suggested letters in no alphabet Wren knew, or perhaps in every alphabet at once. The table ran the length of the room, black wood polished to mirror-depth, set for three.

Three places. Two guests.

The third chair faced them from the table's head, and in it sat the herald.

It wore, as its riders wore, a mask of bone—but finer, older, carved with lines so delicate they might have been script or might have been veins. Its body was robed in layered greys, and where hands emerged from the sleeves they were long-fingered, elegant, and moved with a care that suggested great age or great fragility or both. When it rose to greet them, it bowed—actually bowed, low, the mask dipping toward the floor.

"Cyrus Voss," it said. The voice was beautiful, and that was somehow worse than ugliness would have been—warm, resonant, the voice of a beloved teacher or a parent reading aloud. "And the mage Wren of the Ninth Circle, expelled, disgraced, and nonetheless here, which I find more interesting than anything else about tonight." It gestured to the seats. "Sit. Eat, or don't. The food is real, which is more than can be said for most hospitality in either court."

Cyrus sat. Wren hesitated one heartbeat longer—long enough to mark the position of every exit, all four of them, including two that hadn't existed when they entered—and then sat as well.

The courses began arriving. And this was the strangest thing yet, stranger than the masks, stranger than the breathing city: the food was *good*. Roasted tubers glazed with something honeyed and smoky. Dark bread still warm. Fruit that tasted of summer orchards neither of them had ever visited—someone else's remembered summers, Cyrus realized, served up sweet on borrowed plates.

"Your court is starving," Wren said, setting down her fork. No preamble. No courtesy. She'd never been good at the games the surface court played over dinner, and she saw no reason to learn them for this one.

The herald's mask turned toward her. When it spoke, there was no offense in its tone—only a kind of tired honesty that caught Cyrus off guard.

"Yes," it said. "We are."

* * *

Cyrus had prepared for many versions of this evening. Threats. Riddles. Temptations offered in silk-wrapped voices. They had rehearsed defiance in front of the guest-room mirror until their jaw ached.

They had not prepared for the herald to sound *tired*.

"Starving how?" Cyrus asked, and hated themselves a little for asking, and asked anyway.

The herald folded its long hands on the table. "How does anything starve? Slowly. Then quickly." It gestured upward with one finger, toward a ceiling none of them could see, beyond which lay miles of stone and then, far above, the caldera where a dying fire guttered. "Your grandmother knew us better than anyone on the surface ever has. Elira Voss came down these roads forty years ago with a guild dying above her—pardon revoked, hunters at her heels, children in her cellars. And she found us also dying. The dragon's fire was strong then; it is strong still, in its fashion. But the wards it feeds do not merely keep us *in*, child. They kept us *fed*."

"Fire doesn't feed anyone," Wren said flatly.

"Soul-fire does." The mask tilted, and candlelight slid across its bone surface like water over a skull. "The flame at Thornfell's heart is not mere heat. It is the oldest living contract in the world—the dragon's own life, freely given, burning as a promise that the deep places stay deep and the shallow places stay warm. We drank the overflow. A little. A trickle, sanctioned by the first bargain, paid in kind—we kept the roads, we honored the dead, we gave passage to those the surface discarded. There were centuries of balance." The hands tightened, just slightly. "Then the fire began to fail. Not our doing. Not yours. Age, perhaps. Or grief—dragons die of grief, did you know? Ashvane has been alone a very long time."

Cyrus went very still. "Ashvane."

"The last dragon. You know the name?" Something in the mask's posture shifted—an attentiveness, a lean forward, subtle as a predator adjusting its weight. "Of course you do. Your grandmother's map ends at the caldera. She always intended—" The herald stopped itself. Touched two fingers to the bone cheek in a gesture that might have been amusement or might have been pain. "Forgive me. An old creature rambling. Let me speak plainly, since your mage prefers plain speech, and plain speech is a luxury I have not enjoyed in a century."

It rose. The candles dimmed—not extinguished, but lowered, as if the room itself were leaning in to listen.

"We are starving, and when we finish starving, we will not die politely. A hungry court becomes a desperate court. Desperate courts breach walls. Our riders will stop honoring the old limits. The surface will burn, and then the surface courts will come down here with armies and fire and no distinction between the guilty and the starving, and everything beneath the mountain will end screaming—including, I should mention, several thousand souls currently held in trust. Souls that could otherwise go free." The mask turned to Cyrus. "Unless the contract renews."

"And renewal requires," Cyrus said slowly, each word laid down like a card they already suspected was marked, "Voss blood."

"Renewal requires a Voss signature," the herald corrected gently. "Blood is traditional. Ink suffices. What matters is consent—freely given, as your grandmother's was given, as the dragon's was given. That is the law older than both our courts: nothing taken lasts; nothing given runs out." It resumed its seat, and the candles brightened again, and the servants brought some final course Cyrus never afterward remembered eating. "One signature. One name in the ledger beside your grandmother's. And the trickle becomes a river again—for a generation, for two. The villages keep their wards. The riders keep their borders. Everyone keeps everything."

"And what do we lose?"

The herald spread its hands, an oddly human gesture from so inhuman a frame. "Nothing you would notice. A little warmth. A little memory, perhaps, over the years. Your grandmother lived a full life above before her debt came due below—you've seen her lantern, haven't you?" At Cyrus's flinch, the mask inclined, almost apologetic. "Yes. You've seen it. Guttering, but not gone. Contracts are not cruelty, Cyrus Voss. They are *deferment*. She bought her guild forty years with forty years of her self, and the guild lived, and you are sitting here because of it. I do not ask you to thank her. I ask you to continue her arithmetic."

Silence stretched. Somewhere far off in the breathing halls, a door closed with a sound like a sigh.

* * *

Wren watched Cyrus's face across the black table, and what she saw frightened her more than the mask had.

Not temptation—she'd have known how to fight temptation, had fought it before, in another life, over another contract, and lost someone to it. No, what frightened her was that Cyrus wasn't tempted. Cyrus was *calculating*. She could see the smuggler's mind working behind their eyes, weighing the offer, testing its joints for weakness, hunting the flaw the way they'd hunt a guard rotation on a walled manor.

Because the offer was good. That was the trap of it. Wren had sat across from royal ministers spinning lies wrapped in ermine, and none of them had ever made an offer this clean, because none of them had ever meant it. The herald meant every word. That was what made the Undercourt more dangerous than any throne above: it was honest. Starvation had burned away everything except need, and need did not lie.

"You're telling us the truth," she said aloud, and the herald's mask turned to her. "Why? Truth is expensive. You must know we'll try to twist it."

"Truth is cheap when you are dying," the herald said. "Lies are for creatures with time." It leaned forward, and the candle flames all bent toward it at once, a small tide of light. "But since you ask, mage—I will tell you one truth more, gratis. You carry sigils of unbinding in your seams. Left cuff, right hip, collar. Good craftsmanship. Your mentor taught you the left-cuff placement; he favored it himself." Wren's blood went cold. The herald's voice stayed gentle as banked embers. "Keep them. I do not say this as mockery. If tonight ends badly for you—and it may, not by my choice but by the momentum of courts—it will matter that you came armed. I respect it. I respected your mentor too. He also dined here. He also listened. He also thought he'd found the flaw."

"What happened to him?" Wren asked. Her voice came out steady, which cost her more than crying would have.

"He found it," the herald said softly. "That was the tragedy. The flaws are real. The contracts *can* be broken. But breaking is not the same as surviving, and the ledger—" it touched the bone mask, briefly, almost tenderly, "—the ledger remembers everyone who tries. Ask him yourself someday, if you find his lantern. He is not where you think he is."

Wren's hands had gone white-knuckled in her lap. She made herself unclench them, finger by finger, and reached under the table until her knuckles brushed Cyrus's—just contact, just anchor, nothing either of them would acknowledge in front of the enemy.

Cyrus pressed back.

* * *

Later—much later, though time moved strangely down here and Cyrus couldn't have said whether the meal had lasted an hour or a night—they were escorted back through the breathing corridors, alone together for the first time since the table.

The guest-chambers had merged while they dined. The two rooms had become one room, without consultation, without apology, containing one window, one hearth of cold blue flame, and one bed large enough for two people to lie on it staring at the ceiling without touching, which is exactly what they did for a long time.

"It was true," Cyrus said finally. "All of it. I kept waiting for the lie and it never came."

"That's the worst part." Wren's voice was hoarse. "My mentor used to say the deepest pits aren't dug with deceit. They're dug with true things, arranged in order, so that every step down feels like standing up." She turned her head on the pillow. In the blue light her face was pale and sharp and more afraid than she would ever admit while vertical. "Cyrus. The arithmetic is real. One signature saves thousands of lives above and frees souls below. If we walk away, people freeze when the wards fall. Real people. Maren's innkeepers' neighbors. Children."

"I know."

"If we sign, we feed a machine that ate your grandmother and my mentor and a dragon's generosity and half the memory of everyone who ever wandered down here thinking they'd found a shortcut."

"I know."

"So what are you calculating over there? Because I can hear it. You've been calculating since the soup."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, it was barely above a whisper, as if the walls—which breathed, which remembered, which belonged to the court—might be listening.

"The herald said renewal requires a Voss *signature*," they said. "Freely given. Consent. Those were its exact words, and it was proud of them—it thinks its own law protects it. Every contract down here rests on one rule: *nothing taken lasts; nothing given runs out*." They turned their head, and their eyes in the cold firelight held something Wren had never seen in them before—not fear, not resolve, but the particular glitter of a smuggler who has spotted the unlocked window in a fortress everyone swore was sealed. "It asked for my name, Wren. It assumes the name is mine to give. It assumes the ledger owns what the ledger records."

"And?"

"And tomorrow I want to see the vault," Cyrus said. "I want to see her lantern. I want to stand in front of that ledger and read every entry in the Voss line, top to bottom, before I put anything anywhere near it." A pause. The blue flame flickered. "Grandmother left roads inside that map that only traitors knew. Maybe she left something else too. Maybe the flaw isn't in the contract at all. Maybe it's in the *name*."

Wren stared at them, understanding arriving slowly and then all at once, like cold water rising. "Cyrus. Whatever you're thinking—"

"Sleep," Cyrus said, and reached over, and took her hand in the dark, deliberately this time, in full view of whatever listening walls cared to watch. "Tomorrow we bargain."

Far below them, deeper than the vault, deeper than the dragon's stolen heartflame, something vast turned a page.
