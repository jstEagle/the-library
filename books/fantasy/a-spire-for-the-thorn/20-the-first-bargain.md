## 20. The First Bargain

Wren had counted the doors.

It was a habit from her court days, when every corridor in the Spire of Glass was measured in wards rather than paces, and she had learned to read a building the way other people read faces. So as they walked the deep ways beneath Thornfell—down halls of black stone veined with slow silver light, past arches that opened onto nothing and doorways that opened onto everything—she counted, and the count kept failing. Forty-one doors since the last turning. Then forty-one again, but different: taller, older, their lintels carved with names in a script that writhed when looked at directly. The Undercourt did not build. It *accumulated*, the way a wound accumulates scar tissue, layer over layer over layer, each age of its hunger pressed flat into the walls.

Cyrus walked ahead of her, one hand trailing along the stone, and Wren hated how natural it looked on them. Hated how the mountain seemed to lean toward them like an old dog hearing a familiar step. Elira's blood in these halls, Elira's debts in these walls, and Cyrus carrying all of it without complaint, without even asking what it was costing them.

That was the thing about Cyrus Voss that Wren had learned to fear: they never asked what anything cost until the bill came due. They paid in silence and called it duty.

"You're doing the thing," Cyrus said, without turning around. "The counting thing."

"I'm not doing any thing."

"Your lips move. When you're counting." They glanced back, and the silver light caught the sharp planes of their face—the cheekbone Maren had once broken in a smuggling dispute, the scar through the brow from a Kestrel Shelf winter. "You've done it since Greyhollow. It means you don't trust where we are."

"It means I know where we are," Wren said. "Which is worse."

Ahead, the corridor opened into a gallery so vast the light failed before its far wall. Lanterns hung in the dark above—not fire, not quite, but pale moths of flame that drifted on no wind, each one burning with a small, stubborn brightness. Wren's skin prickled. She knew what burned in lanterns down here. She'd seen the shelves in the herald's antechamber, seen the way each little light pulsed with something too rhythmic to be mere fire. Heartbeats. Names. Souls in jars, sold or stolen or bargained away by people who had needed something badly enough.

Cyrus stopped at the gallery's edge. Below them, sunk into the floor like a coin pressed into wax, stood a door.

Not tall. Not grand. A plain smuggler's door, iron-banded, with a lock shaped like a closed hand—the kind of lock that had been old when Elira Voss was young, the kind you found on cache-houses in the passes. But the bands were wrong. They were made of ledger-lines, fine as hair, ink-black and moving, crawling slowly across the iron like veins under skin. And above the hand-lock, in letters of the same living ink, hung a single word:

*PRICE.*

"Oh," Cyrus said softly.

"Don't," Wren said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You breathed in a particular way. I've been traveling with you long enough to know your breathing." She caught their arm. The muscle under her fingers was taut, already decided. "Whatever's behind that door—and we both know it's her—you don't pay whatever this asks. We find another road. There's always another road. You taught me that. Smugglers' first rule, you said, back in Saltcinder, when you were stealing my boots—"

"I was *borrowing* your boots."

"—you said there is always another road. So say it back to me now."

Cyrus looked at the door for a long moment. The lantern-flames overhead drifted closer, drawn by something—or listening. When they spoke, their voice had gone quiet in the specific way that meant they'd already gone somewhere Wren couldn't follow.

"The map shows three roads to the vault. Two are sealed. The third goes through the Hollow Fair, and we both saw what the fair does to people who walk through twice." Their jaw tightened. "Elira's lantern was guttering, Wren. You saw it. Whatever time she has left, it's short, and the dragon's dying makes it shorter. Every hour we spend finding a cleverer way is an hour off her light."

"And so you'll just open your hands to this place and let it take whatever it wants."

"No," Cyrus said. "I'll give it exactly what I choose. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and if you can't see it, then—" They stopped. Exhaled. "I'm sorry. I'm not angry at you. I'm angry at the arithmetic."

Wren released their arm. Her hands wanted to shake and she refused to let them; twenty years of court discipline lived in her spine for exactly such moments. "Then let me do it," she said. "A memory of me. My mentor's face, my years in the Spire—they're worth more than anything I have left. Let me—"

"No."

"*Cyrus.*"

"They don't want my memories, Wren." Cyrus turned fully to face her then, and up close she could see the exhaustion banked in them like coals, the nights of ledger-whispers and ink-crawling dreams worn into the hollows under their eyes. "They want Voss things. Voss blood bought this door, Voss blood sealed it, and Voss blood will open it—but the toll has to hurt, or it isn't a toll. A memory of mine. Someone I love." Their voice frayed on the last word and they pulled it straight again. "It's the only coin they'll take."

The lanterns had gathered above them now, dozens of them, hanging in a slow revolving cloud like moths around a lamp that wasn't there. Listening. Wren felt watched in a way that went deeper than eyes.

"And which memory," she said carefully, "were you planning to feed them?"

"That's not your decision."

"Neither is dying of self-sacrifice by inches, and yet here you are, planning it like an itinerary."

Something almost like a smile crossed Cyrus's face and died. "Maren," they said.

The name dropped between them like a stone into a well, and Wren heard the splash of it go all the way down.

"No."

"She's the deepest root I have. She raised me after—" Cyrus's breath caught and steadied. "After everyone else. Every lesson, every winter, every time I thought the Voss name meant something, it was her voice in it. If I give the court a stranger, they'll spit it back and take interest besides. It has to be real. It has to *cost*. So it's Maren."

"Maren might still be alive." Wren stepped closer, lowered her voice though the whole mountain could likely hear her heart. "We saw the inn burn, yes. We saw riders. We did not see a body. You told me yourself—Voss people don't die easy, they die *inconveniently*. If you give away every memory of her—if you carve her out of yourself at the roots—then even if she's alive, even if she's waiting at the end of all this with her arms open, you'll look at her and see a stranger. Is that the rescue? Is that what she gets? To survive the Undercourt only to be erased by the person she bled for?"

Cyrus flinched. Actually flinched, shoulders drawing in, and for a moment Wren saw the child underneath the smuggler, the one who'd been handed a burning guild and a dead family and told to carry both.

"If it comes to that," Cyrus said quietly, "she'll understand. She'd trade me the same way. She'd call it arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." Wren laughed, and it came out cracked. "You keep using that word like it absolves you. Arithmetic is how the elders sold your grandmother. Arithmetic is how the surface crown decides which villages to warn. Everyone who's ever fed someone to a fire called it arithmetic first."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. None of this is fair, and I am so tired of fair." She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, against the ache there. "Do you know why I was cast out of the court, Cyrus? Not the official reason. The true one. I tried to unmake a soul-contract, and the contract took Aldous instead—my mentor, the man who taught me everything, whose last words were that I should have let him go. And I have spent eleven years learning the shape of that mistake so I would never make it again. Never trade a person I love for a principle. Never decide alone what someone else is worth." Her voice broke and she hated it and kept going. "And here you stand, about to do exactly that, to *yourself*, in front of me, and ask me to call it courage."

The lanterns had gone very still. Even the ink-veins in the door seemed to pause, quivering, attentive. Somewhere far above—or below—a bell tolled once, a sound with no echo, swallowed whole by the stone.

Cyrus reached out and took her hand. Their palm was cold; the mountain had been drinking their warmth for days. "I'm not asking you to call it anything," they said. "I'm telling you because you deserve to know before I do it, not after. That's the only apology I have. I know it's not enough."

"It's not."

"I know."

"You could turn back."

"I can't."

"You *could*. There's a road out of here. Odo's maps, the deep ways, the—"

"Cyrus," Wren said, and then couldn't finish, because saying *don't* felt like trying to hold back a river with her hands, and Cyrus was already turning toward the door.

They drew the Ember Map from their coat. In the dark of the gallery it glowed faintly, roads shifting beneath its vellum skin like fish under ice, and Wren watched her own careful wardwork from weeks ago still holding at the edges—her magic knitted into their grandmother's ink, two hands across time holding one map together. Cyrus pressed the map flat against the iron door.

The hand-shaped lock opened.

Finger by finger, joint by joint, with a sound like old wood and older grief, until the door swung inward on darkness that was somehow warmer than the hall—darkness with a pulse in it, a slow tidal breathing, the vault beyond exhaling the scent of hot copper and snowmelt and, impossibly, woodsmoke. Hearth-smoke. The smell of Maren's inn on a winter morning.

Wren's stomach dropped. "*No.* It's showing you—it's baiting the hook with—"

"I know what it's doing," Cyrus whispered. But they didn't step back. Of course they didn't. "It's showing me what it takes. That's honest, at least. More honest than the surface court ever was."

The darkness spoke. Not in words, not at first—in sensation, a pressure against the mind like a thumb testing ripe fruit, weighing, choosing. Wren threw up a ward on instinct, silver threads snapping taut between her fingers, and the pressure slid around them without effort, polite as rain around a stone. Not hostile. Patient. The patience of something that had waited twenty years for a Voss to stand at this door and knew, with terrible certainty, that one eventually would.

*Cyrus.* Their voice came out steady. Too steady. "I offer a memory freely given. Maren Voss. All of her. The price you set—I'll meet it."

*Don't,* Wren started to say, and the word died in her throat, because the darkness had already begun to pour itself forward, and she understood with cold clarity that the bargain was not being struck now. It had been struck the moment Cyrus breathed the name aloud. This door ran on Voss consent, and consent had been given, and the only thing left to negotiate was the depth of the cut.

The smoke-scent thickened. For one horrible instant Wren saw it take shape in the doorway: a kitchen, a kettle singing, a broad woman with flour on her forearms laughing at something off-scene—Maren, alive, rendered in perfect detail, warm as bread, *real*—and Cyrus made a sound Wren had never heard from them, small and undone, and reached toward it—

—and the vision folded itself neatly, lovingly, into Cyrus's open hand, and went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

Cyrus swayed. Wren caught them around the ribs as their knees buckled, lowering them to the stone floor of the threshold, and for three heartbeats their eyes were open and empty as winter windows—looking at her, through her, past her, at nothing at all.

Then they blinked. Focused. Frowned at her, politely, the way one frowns at a stranger standing too close.

"Wren," they said slowly. Testing the name. "Wren. Yes. You're—the mage. From the market. From the cage." A pause, delicate and dreadful. "I remember the cage. Why do I remember the cage and not—" Their hand rose to their temple, pressing. "There's a hole. There's a Maren-shaped hole and I can feel the edges of it and I don't—"

They stopped. Looked at their own hand, the one that had held the vision, as if it belonged to someone else.

"What did I give?" they asked. Very quietly. "What did I just give?"

And Wren, kneeling on the cold stone with her arms still around the person she loved, who had traded away the woman who raised them to buy passage to a soul already half-spent, felt the truth arrive like ice water: that fury and heartbreak and terror had all arrived at once, and none of them would help, and she loved them anyway, and she said:

"You gave her away. And she may still be alive. And when we find her, you won't know her face."

Cyrus stared at her. Behind them, unhurried, untriumphant, the door ground open another inch—admitting them to the vault, to the shelves, to the guttering lantern with her grandmother's name—and somewhere in the dark below, faint as a memory of warmth, something that might have been Elira Voss sighed, and waited, and hoped.

Wren helped Cyrus to their feet.

They walked through the door together, and the lock closed behind them like a fist.
