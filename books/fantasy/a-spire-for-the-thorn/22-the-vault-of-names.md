## 22. The Vault of Names

The door did not look like a door until Wren touched it.

It had been a wall of the deep ways for the better part of a day—black stone veined with something that might have been fossilized lightning or might have been vein-work, pulsing faintly in time with a heartbeat that was not theirs. Cyrus had walked beside it with their palm trailing the rock the way their grandmother's map demanded, and when the map's ink finally stopped crawling, pooling instead into a single burning sigil shaped like a keyhole, the stone sighed.

That was the only word for it. A sigh, long and tired, as if the wall had been holding its breath since before Cyrus was born.

"Stand back," Wren said, though her voice caught on the second word. She had been thin since the geode cavern—not in body, but in something beneath it, the way a candle is thin after burning through the small hours. Her magic still clung to her like smoke that hadn't decided to leave. "Contracts guard this place. If they don't know your name, they can't argue with you."

"And if they do?"

"Then we find out how much of you they want."

Cyrus pressed their bleeding palm—still raw from the gate, from the memory-price, from every threshold since Saltcinder—to the sigil, and said nothing at all. That was the trick of it now. They were learning to say nothing. The ledger whispered *Voss* in their sleep; the roads remembered Elira; but here, in this last black corridor, silence was armor.

The wall drank the blood. The sigil flared white, then gold, then a color Cyrus had no name for—a color like the moment before grief becomes anger—and ground apart along seams no chisel had ever cut.

Cold air rolled out. Not dead cold, not tomb cold. Cold the way a winter river is cold: alive, moving, carrying things on its current. Sounds came with it. Faint, far away, layered under the stone's silence like voices heard through ice.

Lanterns.

* * *

The vault was not a room. It was a harvest.

Shelves rose into darkness on both sides of an aisle long enough to swallow a village street, and every shelf was crowded with lanterns—thousands of them, tens of thousands, receding into a black so complete that the far end might have been another world. Each lantern held a flame, and each flame burned a different color, and each color moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, the way a face moves when it speaks, the way hands move when they plead.

Cyrus stopped walking because walking required a body, and their body had forgotten what it was for.

"They're souls," Wren said softly. She had gone very pale, which on her brown skin looked like frost creeping across dark water. "Not pieces of them. Not echoes. The whole of them, folded down small enough to fit behind glass." Her hand rose toward the nearest shelf and hovered there without touching. "This one—" The lantern before her finger burned a deep sea-green, and inside it something turned over, slow and dreaming. "This was someone who loved the coast."

"You can't know that."

"The fire remembers what the person loved. It's the last thing to go." Wren's voice frayed at the edges. "I told you I tried to unmake a contract once. My mentor had one of these. His name was Aldous Vey, and his flame was the color of a library in autumn, and I thought—I *thought*—if I could just pull hard enough at the knot of the binding, I could unwind it and give him back." She swallowed. "The knot pulled back. It took him apart instead. I watched the light go out of the glass like a snuffed candle, and the court wrote it down as my failure, not theirs."

Cyrus reached out—not for the lanterns but for her, finding her wrist in the dark, feeling the tremor there that she would never have admitted to. "Then we don't pull knots," they said. "We read the fine print. That's all a Voss has ever been good for."

Something almost like a laugh escaped her. It died quickly, though, because the aisle was listening. The flames nearest them had begun to lean—all of them, hundreds of small fires tilting toward the two intruders like wheat bending toward sun, like faces turning toward a sound.

"They can see us," Cyrus whispered.

"They can *hope* at us," Wren corrected, and the correction was worse. "Keep walking. We're looking for a Voss."

* * *

They found her by the sound.

Not by the map—the map had gone strange and quiet in Cyrus's satchel ever since the gate, its ink lying flat and dormant as though it had delivered them where it wanted and could afford to rest now—but by a sound like wind through a cracked window, thin and rhythmic and wrong.

Elira Voss's lantern sat on a shelf of its own, set apart from the crowded ranks, on a plinth of black glass near the vault's far heart. Beside it, caged in a lattice of the same glass, burned something that made the soul-lanterns look like matches.

The heartflame.

It did not behave like fire. It behaved like a living thing asleep—coiling and uncoiling, vast even in captivity, its light shifting through colors the way a storm shifts across a valley. Where Elira's lantern guttered, small and unsteady, the heartflame roared silently against its cage, and Cyrus understood with a lurch that the court had not merely stolen it. They had penned it beside a soul the way farmers pen a bull beside a starving horse, letting its heat bleed through the bars, keeping one alive off the other's ruin.

Keeping the dragon's own fire warm while the dragon above froze.

"Oh," Wren breathed. "Oh, that's cruel. That's beautiful and cruel."

"That's a bargain," Cyrus said. Their voice came out flat and old, older than they were. "Everything the Undercourt touches turns into a bargain. Look at her."

They knelt before the shelf. Up close, Elira's lantern was plain—dented tin, the kind sold in any market in Greyhollow, scratched with initials some child had once carved and someone had never bothered to sand away. *E.V.* Inside, the flame was the gray-gold of banked embers, and it moved in a pattern too regular to be random: a slow lean left, a slow lean right, like a woman pacing a small room. Like a woman waiting.

"Grandmother," Cyrus said.

The word felt absurd in the vastness, swallowed whole by ten thousand burning souls. But the flame stopped its pacing.

It turned toward them. There was no other way to describe it—the ember-light gathered itself toward the side of the glass facing Cyrus, pressing close, the way a cat presses to a door it knows will open eventually.

"She can't hear me," Cyrus said, and hated how young their voice sounded. "Can she? Wren, tell me she can't hear me, because if she's been in here twenty years hearing this place—"

"She can hear you." Wren knelt beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Souls aren't deaf, Cyrus. They're the opposite of deaf. They're all ear and no mouth. That's the torture of these things—you feel everything, everyone, every prayer spoken over your glass, and you can't answer." Her jaw tightened. "Aldous heard me for three years before I found him. He heard everything I said to his empty chair."

Cyrus looked at the little gray-gold fire pressing itself against the glass, and twenty years rearranged themselves in their chest. Every coin their grandmother had supposedly squandered. Every debt the elders named her for. Every story Maren had cut short with a shake of her head and a change of subject. She had been here. All that time, she had been *here*, pacing her small glass room, listening to a family that believed she'd sold herself out of greed.

She had sold herself out of love. They had known that since chapter and verse of this whole wretched road. But knowing it here, in front of the proof, in front of the *pacing*—

"I'm getting you out," Cyrus said. Their voice steadied as they spoke, which surprised them. "I don't care what it costs. I've already paid memories. I'll pay more. I'll pay whatever's left after."

The flame flared—a brief, bright pulse, gray-gold going white at the edges—and then it did something no lantern in the aisle had done.

It dimmed deliberately. Pulled inward. Shrank from them.

"No," Wren said quietly.

"What? What does that mean?"

"It means she understands you perfectly," Wren said, "and she's telling you no." The mage's hand closed over Cyrus's wrist—not restraining, holding, the way you hold someone at the edge of a drop. "Look around you, Cyrus. Count the shelves. This isn't a vault for one soul. It's a granary. And the court is starving. When the dragon's fire fails completely—and it's failing, we saw it dimming from the shrine—they won't ration anymore. They'll open every lantern in this room and drink."

"Then we break them all."

"With what?" Wren's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "My magic is nearly spent. Your blood opens doors, it doesn't shatter bindings—the gate drank it, it didn't crack. And even if we had the power, even if we could shatter ten thousand contracts at once, the backlash would—" She stopped. Started again. "Aldous. One knot, one small soul-contract, and it took him apart when I pulled wrong. These are elder bindings. Generations deep. You don't untie those with fury."

Cyrus stood. Their legs felt like borrowed things, but they stood, and they turned to face the endless shelves, the ten thousand small fires leaning toward them with ten thousand kinds of hope, and the arithmetic of the thing settled onto their shoulders like snow.

One lantern. One grandmother. Two hands.

And beside her, behind its cage of black glass, the heartflame coiled and uncoiled, vast and patient and dying by inches as it fed a court that would never thank it.

"We carry her out," Cyrus said at last. "And we carry the flame. That's the mission. That was always the mission. Everything else—" They gestured at the impossible shelves, and their voice cracked despite them. "Everything else is a war for people with armies."

"Cyrus."

"Don't." They held up a hand. "Don't do the gentle voice. Don't tell me we can't. I didn't come this far to stand in front of her and explain why I'm leaving her in a jar."

But Wren wasn't doing the gentle voice. She was staring past Cyrus, at the plinth, at the space between Elira's lantern and the heartflame's cage—and her face had gone through pale into something beyond it, something Cyrus had only seen once before, on the Kestrel Shelf, in the half-second before the rope bridge gave.

"Cyrus. Look at the glass."

They looked.

Between the two captives—between the guttering gray-gold soul and the roaring imprisoned fire—strands had begun to form. Fine as spider silk, faint as breath on cold air, threads of light running from the heartflame's cage to Elira's lantern. Feeding her, the way the court fed. Except these threads weren't coming from the court.

They were coming from *her*. From the dregs of Elira Voss's soul, willingly given, thread by thread by thread, for twenty years.

"She's been feeding it back," Wren whispered. "Whatever she could spare. She's been giving her own fire to the dragon's fire, trying to keep it alive from inside the cage." Her voice broke on the last word. "She knew. All this time, she knew what the debt was for. She's been paying interest."

Cyrus reached for the lantern, and their fingers had barely brushed the tin when the vault changed.

It was not a sound. It was an absence of sound—the sudden, total hush of ten thousand flames leaning *away*, recoiling from something at the aisle's mouth, the way animals go silent before a predator enters the field. The cold air stopped moving. Even the heartflame drew itself tight against its cage, coiled small and watchful.

Footsteps. Unhurried. Dry as pages turning.

Wren's hand found Cyrus's in the dark and gripped hard, and neither of them pretended, this time, that the trembling was only one of theirs.

From the black throat of the aisle, where ten thousand soul-fires bent away like grass before a scythe, a figure emerged—and in the light of Elira's guttering lantern, Cyrus saw that it wore a bone mask polished smooth as riverstone, and it carried, cradled in arms that were far too long, a book.

The book was open. The book was whispering.

And the whisper, soft and intimate as a lover's mouth against the ear, spoke a name Cyrus had sworn never to answer:

*"Voss."*
