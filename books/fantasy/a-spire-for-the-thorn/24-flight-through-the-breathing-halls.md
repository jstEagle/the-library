## 24. Flight Through the Breathing Halls

The vault's door came down behind them like a guillotine of stone, and the mountain began to scream.

Wren felt it first in her teeth—a high, grinding note that lived in the walls themselves, as if Thornfell had woken from a long sleep and found thieves in its chest. The corridor ahead of them, which had been a smooth throat of black glass a heartbeat ago, shuddered and began to fold. Not crumble. *Fold*, the way a hand closes, the way a lung contracts, the passages remembering that they were never roads at all but organs, and organs could clench.

"Run," Cyrus said, and there was no argument to be had.

They ran.

Wren ran badly. She knew it even as her boots struck the glassy floor—her magic was still knotted around the wards she'd torn open at the vault door, threads of it trailing back through the dark like silk caught on thorns. Every stride pulled those threads tighter. Every stride cost her something she would not be able to name until later, when later came.

Behind them, the ledger hall lit up.

She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people whose names were still their own. But she heard it—the sound of ten thousand soul-lanterns flaring at once, a sound like a choir inhaling, and beneath it the dry whisper of pages turning, turning, turning. The ledger had noticed the theft. The ledger was telling its masters.

"They're coming," Cyrus said. Not a question. Cyrus Voss had spent three weeks learning the acoustics of being hunted, and Wren had learned to trust that ear more than her own eyes.

"How many?"

"All of them."

*All of them.* The bone riders had been bad enough on the surface, where they were bound to roads and ridgelines. Down here they were something else entirely—here they were the court's own blood, quick and wrong, moving through the breathing halls the way fish move through water. Wren had seen one up close in the Hollow Fair, had watched it tilt its mask toward a ghost-child begging for warmth, and she still dreamed about the patience in that gesture.

The corridor bent left, then right, then left again, contracting around them. The walls sweated. That was new—or perhaps not new, perhaps only now visible: beads of moisture crawling down the black glass, each one catching the light of Wren's ward-spell and holding it like a tiny captive star.

"Cyrus." Her voice came out thinner than she wanted. "The map. Which way?"

Cyrus had the Ember Map out and running, the living ink boiling across the vellum as the halls shifted under them. For one terrible moment the parchment showed nothing at all—just blankness, the map as blind as they were—and then Elira's handwriting surfaced like a drowned woman rising, and a road bloomed across it in letters of ember-red.

*"Left at the weeping wall. Trust the old blood."*

"Left," Cyrus said.

They went left.

* * *

The weeping wall was exactly what it sounded like: a curtain of moisture falling without source across an archway of fused bone. Wren ducked through it and the water hit her shoulders cold as grave-dirt, and for half a second she was somewhere else—Greyhollow market, rain on the cage bars, Cyrus's face appearing between them with a crowbar and no good reason to help her—

"Wren!" Cyrus's hand closed on her wrist, hauling her out of the memory before it finished forming. The halls did that. She'd warned Cyrus about it days ago: *the walls replay what they've swallowed.* But knowing didn't blunt them. The Undercourt's corridors were paved with everything the deep had ever taken, and it liked to show you yours.

Behind them, hoofbeats. No—that wasn't right either. Bone riders didn't ride, not here. It was the sound of joints, many joints, unfolding and refolding, a sound like a forest of knuckles cracking in unison, and it was getting louder with a speed that made her stomach drop.

"The wards," Cyrus said, skidding around a bend. Ahead the passage split into three throats, two of them already narrowing, glass teeth sliding shut. "You're still holding the vault wards open, aren't you? I can see it. You're gray, Wren. You look like candle smoke."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying beautifully, which is worse."

She almost laughed. That was the cruelty of Cyrus Voss—they could make you want to laugh while the world folded shut around you, and you'd do it, you'd laugh, because some traitor part of you had decided weeks ago that this person's voice was the sound safety made.

"I can't let them close," she said instead. "If I release the wards, the ledger seals itself, and everything we took—Elira's lantern, the heartflame—it all goes back to them. Everything we've burned to get here goes back to them." She flexed her fingers. They were shaking. When had they started shaking? "So I hold them. That's the arithmetic."

"That's not arithmetic, that's suicide with extra steps."

"Cyrus—"

"No." Cyrus stopped dead in the narrowing passage, turned, and grabbed her by both shoulders, and the heartflame in its iron lantern swung between them, throwing light like a small furious sun against the walls. Up close, Wren could see what the running had hidden: soot in the creases of Cyrus's eyes, a cut along the jaw still bleeding freely, and underneath all of it a ferocity that had nothing to do with anger. "Listen to me. I have buried everyone. My grandmother by contract, my guild by betrayal, Odo by alleyway. I am not burying you in a hallway because you think holding a door open is your job."

"It *is* my job. It's the only thing I'm still good for." The words came out before she could stop them, raw and true, and she hated how true. "I was cast out, Cyrus. My own college put me in a cage and sold me south. The one thing I know how to do is stand between people and things that eat them, and I am *standing*, so let me—"

"No."

It was said quietly. That was the worst of it. Cyrus didn't shout. Cyrus took the lantern off their belt, pressed it into her hands—warm, impossibly warm, the stolen heartflame pulsing against her palms like a second pulse—and said, quietly, in the voice of someone who had run out of everything except certainty:

"You hold the wards. Fine. But you hold them walking. And if you start burning yourself thin to buy us seconds, I will put you over my shoulder and carry you, and I will let the wards close, and I will find another way or die trying—but I will not spend you. Do you understand me? I don't spend people. That's what the elders did to my grandmother. That's what the crown would do to both of us. We are not them."

The passage groaned. Glass teeth ground closer. Somewhere behind, the joint-sound of the riders filled the dark like a tide coming in.

And Wren Halloran—disgraced, caged, burned-out, twenty years past the last time anyone had refused to spend her—found that she could not speak at all.

So she nodded. And they ran.

* * *

The halls fought them the whole way.

Not passively—actively, intelligently, the way a body fights a fever. Corridors tilted underfoot until running became climbing. Stairways unspooled into flat stone mid-stride. Once, a whole gallery of the breathing halls exhaled, a wind hot and carrion-sweet, and Wren felt it pluck at her ward-threads like fingers testing knots.

She held. Gods, she held. She could feel every thread now, could feel where they anchored—in the vault door, in the ledger's binding, in the deep law that said *a debt once witnessed cannot be unwitnessed*. Holding them was like carrying water in her cupped hands at a dead sprint. Every jolt spilled something. She kept pouring herself back in.

The cost announced itself in small indignities first. Colors going slightly wrong—the heartflame's gold shading toward white, Cyrus's dark hair shading toward absence. Sounds arriving late. Then the larger ones: her knees buckling on a stair that hadn't existed a moment before, Cyrus catching her elbow without breaking stride, saying nothing, saying everything by saying nothing.

"Don't," she managed.

"I'm not saying anything."

"You're saying it very loudly."

A sound escaped Cyrus then that was half laugh, half sob, and they plunged on together through the folding dark.

The Ember Map led them true. Wren marveled at it even now, even with her vision graying at the edges—marveled at Elira Voss, dead fifty years, still smuggling her grandchildren home through the body of the thing that owned her. *Trust the old blood,* the ink had written. The old blood was everywhere down here. The walls themselves remembered Elira: twice they passed alcoves where the glass replayed her, a young woman with Cyrus's stubborn jaw picking locks, bribing ghosts, laughing at things that should not be laughed at. Once the walls showed her pregnant, hand spread over her belly, standing before a sealed door and saying words Wren couldn't hear. Cyrus looked away from that one. Wren reached back without looking and found Cyrus's hand, and they ran like that, linked, through the memory-haunted dark.

Then the riders caught them.

* * *

It happened at a junction where six corridors met in a vaulted chamber like the inside of a bell. The joint-sound swelled from three directions at once, and Wren understood, with the cold clarity of exhaustion, that the halls had herded them. Every turn since the weeping wall had been offered, not chosen. The mountain had been funneling them here the way a throat funnels a swallow.

They came out of the dark on all sides. Not horses—never horses, down here—but shapes that moved like horses had once moved, assembled from pale segments and wrapped in masks of yellowed bone, each mask different, each mask smiling. A dozen of them. More. Their eyeless faces turned toward the lantern in Wren's hands, and the hunger coming off them was almost gentle, the way winter is gentle, the way starvation is patient.

"Cyrus." Wren's voice was very calm. Calm was what happened when there was no room left for anything else. "The far arch. The one with the red seam. Can you make it?"

"With you?"

"With you carrying me, if it comes to that."

"It won't." Cyrus set her down against the chamber wall—gently, the way you set down something breakable, which under the circumstances she chose to find insulting rather than accurate—and turned to face the riders with the empty iron lantern cage in one hand and a smuggler's hooked knife in the other. "I need thirty seconds. My grandmother taught me a trick for locked doors and worse things. Give me thirty seconds and don't argue."

"What trick—"

But Cyrus was already moving, low and fast, knife flashing, and Wren had no breath left to waste on questions. She raised her hands and poured herself into the wards.

Holding the vault wards open meant holding a wound open. She could feel the ledger straining against her grip, feel the court's will pressing through the tear like floodwater through a failing dike. So she did the thing her mentor Sable had died teaching her not to do: she stopped rationing. She opened every gate inside herself at once and fed the wards everything, her reserves, her discipline, the careful architecture of restraint she'd built over twenty years—all of it, out, now, because Cyrus needed thirty seconds and she had exactly enough self left to count them.

The magic went through her like fire through a paper house.

She saw the fight in flashes, afterward, the way you see lightning: Cyrus rolling under a rider's scything limb and hooking its knee-joint apart; Cyrus slamming the empty lantern cage down over a bone mask and shouting words in the smugglers' cant that made the thing recoil as if scalded; Cyrus bleeding from a dozen shallow cuts and grinning, actually grinning, because this was what Voss blood was *for*, this was the inheritance no ledger could strike out. And always, always, Cyrus glancing back at her—one heartbeat in three—to check that she was still upright.

She was upright. Barely. The wards screamed through her and she screamed silently back, and somewhere in the white noise of it she felt a thread begin to fray—not a ward-thread, one of hers, something deep and structural, something that would not grow back—

Twenty seconds.

Cyrus broke the last rider's mask with the heel of the lantern cage and ran for her, scooped her up—actually *scooped* her, one arm under her knees, the heartflame clutched between them, blazing—and bolted for the red-seamed arch.

"You said—" Wren got out, "you wouldn't—"

"I said I wouldn't *spend* you. Carrying isn't spending. Hold on."

The arch swallowed them. Behind them the bell-chamber filled with the sound of masks reassembling, patient as winter, and ahead—

Ahead the corridor ended.

Not narrowed. Not folded. *Ended*, in a wall of seamless black glass, blank as the day the mountain was born, and the Ember Map in Cyrus's belt showed nothing at all, and the joint-sound was rising again behind them, and Wren felt the last of her strength gutter like a lamp out of oil.

"We're walled in," Cyrus said. Flat. Breathing hard. "We're walled in and I'm out of tricks."

Wren, half-conscious, burning thin as thread, looked at the blank wall and thought—with the strange lucidity that comes at the very bottom of oneself—*the walls remember her. The walls are made of her.*

"Cyrus," she whispered. "Talk to the map. Talk to *her*. Smugglers hide roads inside roads. If anyone left a way out of a sealed room—"

Cyrus stared at her. Then at the wall. Then drew the Ember Map, and pressed it flat against the black glass, and said, in a voice gone quiet and fierce:

"Grandmother. We could use a traitor's road right about now."

The map's blank vellum shivered.

And in the dark of the glass, faint as a spark under ash, a line of ember-red began to draw itself.
