## 26. The Road Only Traitors Know

The road Elira had hidden in the map was not drawn so much as buried. Cyrus felt it rather than saw it: a hairline seam of ink beneath the painted halls of the Undercourt, like a scar under skin, visible only when they tilted the parchment toward the light of Wren's cupped flame.

"She hid it inside the border," Cyrus said, tracing the line with a fingertip that trembled more than they wanted to admit. The lantern that held their grandmother's soul hung at their hip, its guttering glow warm as a held breath. "Inside her own signature. She signed herself into the edge of the map and made the edge into a door."

"Traitors' roads always run along the margins," Wren said quietly. "That's where no one looks. That's where she lived, isn't it? In the margins of her own guild."

Cyrus didn't answer. The walls of the passage behind them still shuddered with distant pursuit—the deep ways groaning as the Undercourt's hunters cast about for their scent—and there was no time for grief, though grief pressed at the back of their throat like cold smoke. They had carried their grandmother's lantern out of the vault with the dragon's stolen heartflame cradled above it in a cage of Wren's woven light, and now every step was a negotiation between hope and the certainty that hope was exactly what the herald wanted them to carry.

The seam-road opened ahead of them as they walked. It did not look like the other passages of Thornfell's deeps, which breathed and shifted like the insides of some vast animal. This one was old in a different way—old the way smuggler's chutes were old, cut quick and dirty by hands that needed to move fast and leave nothing behind. Rough stone. Chiseled footholds worn smooth by boots long dead. And everywhere, scratched into the walls at intervals, a small mark: a thorn wound through a circle.

"The Voss sign," Cyrus said, stopping at the third one. Their voice came out strange. "She marked her own secret road with the guild sigil."

"So her children could find it," Wren said. "So you could find it."

"My father never spoke of any road like this. He said the mountain had two faces—the passes we ran and the courts below—and that no Voss ever went down unless they were dead or desperate."

"He lied," Wren said, not unkindly. "Or he didn't know. Families are just stories people agree to tell each other. Some stories get edited."

The road climbed. It climbed for hours, switchbacking through stone that grew warmer underfoot, then warmer still, until Cyrus could feel it through the soles of their boots—a deep, pulsing heat, like standing on the chest of something enormous and slow-breathing. Ashvane. The dying dragon whose fire kept the mountain's wards intact, whose stolen heartflame now swung in its cage of light against Cyrus's chest with every step, heavy as a sin.

The heat should have comforted them. Instead it felt like walking into the open palm of something that might close.

They stopped once to rest, in a widening of the tunnel where an ancient fire-scar blackened the walls. Wren built no fire; there was no need. Cyrus ate half a strip of dried elk and couldn't manage the other half. The lantern at their belt flickered, and for a moment—a trick of the eye, surely, exhaustion bending the light—the flame inside it leaned toward them, the way a cat leans toward a familiar hand.

"I'm taking you home," Cyrus whispered to it. "I'm taking all of you home."

The flame steadied. Or didn't. They got up before they could find out, because sitting still meant feeling everything, and feeling everything would put them on the ground and keep them there.

* * *

Wren counted their remaining magic the way a miser counts coins, and the total frightened her.

She walked behind Cyrus—always behind, these last days; her wards were strongest thrown backward over their shared trail—and she tallied what was left of herself. Three strong workings. A handful of small ones. After that, she would be drawing on marrow, on the bright fluid at the base of her spine, on the parts of a mage that did not grow back. Her mentor Sera had called this accounting "keeping the candle," and she had taught Wren to do it every night before sleep, and she had died at nearly empty, burned to a wick trying to unmake a soul-contract not so different from the ones stitched through Cyrus's family like dark thread.

*I will not do what she did,* Wren told herself, and knew it might be a lie. Love was already rearranging her arithmetic. When Cyrus stumbled on the slick stone, Wren's hand was out before thought, and the small warding shimmer that steadied them both cost her something she felt in her teeth.

"You're spending too much," Cyrus said. They had noticed. Of course they had noticed; smugglers noticed everything, especially what people tried to hide. "Covering our trail. I can see the seams of it glowing."

"It's cheap work. Trail-warding is candlework."

"Cyrus." They almost smiled at hearing their own name used as admonishment—she'd been doing it for days now, and it never stopped catching somewhere under their ribs. "Don't lie to a liar."

Wren let the ward behind them gutter out. The darkness that rushed in felt like exposure, like walking naked through a hall of mirrors, but she kept her count honest after that, and hated how quickly the honesty emptied her.

They talked instead, to fill the climbing dark. It was the only currency they had left that wasn't bleeding.

"Tell me about the first thing you stole," Wren said.

Cyrus huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. "First thing I remember stealing. Not the first thing I took—that would be bread, probably, everyone starts with bread. But the first real theft. I was nine. My father sent me up the Kestrel trail with a delivery I wasn't supposed to look at, which of course meant I looked. It was a wedding ring. A woman's ring, silver, with a chip of moonstone. I held it up to the sun on the high shelf and it threw blue light on the snow, just this little coin of blue, and I thought—I thought, *this is what I'll be. Someone who carries light where it needs to go.*" They were quiet for three steps. "I delivered it. Didn't even skim the fee. My father was furious when he found out I'd looked. Said looking was how smugglers died."

"And you've been looking ever since."

"Somebody has to. Everyone else in my family got very good at not seeing things." Cyrus's hand brushed the wall, where the thorn-and-circle mark appeared again, patient as gravestones. "She saw things, though. Grandmother Elira. You can feel it in this road. Every choice of angle, every foothold—it's someone thinking three moves ahead and caring whether the person behind her lives."

"Yes," Wren said softly. "You can feel it."

Because that was the truth she hadn't said aloud yet, the truth sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal: the road reminded her of Cyrus. Cut fast, marked carefully, made by someone who expected to be followed by someone they loved. She had watched Cyrus move since the market in Greyhollow, since the rope bridge, since the geode cavern where they had finally stopped running long enough to kiss her with shaking hands, and everything Cyrus built had that same double nature—escape route and invitation, all at once. *Follow me. Be safe. Come on.*

Ahead, the air changed. Wren smelled it first—sulfur and clean cold, wind moving where no wind should be, and beneath it something green and living, pine sap and snowmelt. Surface smells. Mountain-top smells.

"There," Cyrus breathed.

The road ended at a wall of stone that wasn't a wall. Up close, Wren could see the seam—a crack wide enough to pour light through, widening as they approached, as if the mountain remembered whose blood had carved it. Beyond it: sky. Actual sky, bruised purple with pre-dawn, and the silhouette of the caldera rim against the first pale stars, close enough to touch.

So close. The heartflame pulsed in its cage, faster, like a heart recognizing home.

"We made it," Cyrus whispered. It wasn't triumph in their voice. It was disbelief, the particular disbelief of people who had been hunted too long to trust an open door.

Wren should have said something. Later, she would spend years wishing she had said something. But she was keeping her candle, counting her coins, and so she only reached out and took Cyrus's hand, and together they stepped through the crack in the world, onto the shoulder of Thornfell, under the open and indifferent stars.

Behind them, the seam sealed itself with a sound like a struck bell.

And then, from the darkness at their backs, came laughter.

* * *

Cyrus knew the sound before they turned around. You didn't forget a voice that had dined with you politely while explaining the terms of your damnation.

"No," they said. Just that. A single flat syllable aimed at the universe.

The herald stood at the caldera rim where the road met the mountain's crown, bone mask white in the starlight, robes stirring in the wind that poured up from the dragon's failing warmth below. He was alone. That was somehow worse. Behind him, where Elira's secret road had surfaced, the stone had gone glassy and dead, fused shut, a black scar on the mountainside still smoking faintly at the edges.

"A beautiful road," the herald said, and his voice through the mask had the texture of dry leaves settling. "We wondered for a century why the mountain would not let us map that region. Now we know. She walled off her own treachery and left it as an inheritance." He inclined his head, courteous as a knife going in. "Your grandmother was remarkable, Cyrus Voss. We enjoyed her company, in the early centuries. Before the arrangement curdled."

"You followed us." Cyrus's hand found the lantern at their hip. Their other hand found the short knife they'd carried since Saltcinder, since Odo died in an alley buying them eleven seconds. "Through the breathing halls. Through the fair. How?"

"We did not follow." The herald spread his hands, and the gesture was almost gentle. "We *read*. The ledger knows where its debts walk. You have carried your grandmother's contract up this road like a torch, child. Did you imagine we would need to chase the flame? We merely walked to where it was going."

Wren moved then—not toward the herald but between him and Cyrus, and her remaining magic rose around her in a thin bright lattice, and Cyrus heard in her voice the exact moment the arithmetic failed: "Stay behind me."

"Wren." The herald's masked face tilted. "The disgraced daughter of the Amber Hall. We know your name too, though yours is written in different books. Expulsion. Heresy. The unmaking of contracts, that old forbidden art." He clicked his tongue against the bone. "You burn so brightly and hold so little fuel. It is the tragedy of your kind."

"The exit," Cyrus said to Wren, low and fast, eyes never leaving the herald. "He sealed the road. Is there another way down? The caldera—"

"The caldera is where he wants us." Wren's lattice hummed. "But it's also where Ashvane is. And the flame—" The heartflame surged in its cage, pulling toward the rim like a dog scenting home. "It doesn't matter what he wants anymore. If we can reach the dragon—"

"Then finish your errand," the herald said, and stepped aside.

It was the worst thing he could have done. He swept one robed arm toward the caldera path—an invitation, broad and gracious—and the emptiness of his confidence filled the whole night. Below them, visible now past the rim, Thornfell's crater glowed a sullen, drowning red, dimmer than any living fire had a right to be, and in that light Cyrus could see the shape coiled at the bottom: vast, cracked, still. Ashvane, the last dragon, lying in her own fading warmth like an ember in ash.

"Go," the herald said. "Return the heartflame. Wake her if you can. We want her awake, Cyrus Voss. We have wanted nothing else for a hundred years." The mask caught the dying red light and seemed to smile. "A dying dragon seals our court in poverty. A waking one, bound to a starving Undercourt by the contract you carry—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The trap closed in Cyrus's mind with an almost audible snap: not the road, not the seal, not the riders who even now would be finding other ways up the mountain's flanks. The trap was the errand itself. The rescue they had bled and traded memories for. Every step toward saving Ashvane was a step toward delivering the court its salvation wrapped in Voss blood.

"You're lying," Cyrus said. "You have to be. The wards fail without her fire. Your court starves—your own herald said so at your table."

"Our court starves," the herald agreed pleasantly. "Which is precisely why we no longer require the wards to hold."

Wind came off the caldera, hot and sulfur-sweet and thin, and beneath it—so faint Cyrus might have dreamed it—came a sound they had heard once in dreams and once in the whisper of crawling ink: a great ledger's pages turning, turning, turning, somewhere far below the stone, patient as winter.

Wren's hand found theirs. Her fingers were cold. Her lattice was flickering. And when she spoke, her voice was steady in the way of things that had passed beyond fear into decision.

"Then we change the terms," she said. "Again."

Above them, against the grey beginning of dawn, Thornfell's peak went suddenly, utterly dark—and every ward-bound lamp in every village on the mountain's slopes went dark with it, all at once, like held breath.

Below, in the crater, Ashvane stirred, and the ground shivered, and the herald raised one hand as if conducting a symphony only he could hear.

"Come," he said. "The dragon is waking either way. Let us see what she wakes *to*."
