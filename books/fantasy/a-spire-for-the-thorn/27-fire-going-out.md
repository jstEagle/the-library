## 27. Fire Going Out

The Smuggler's Road spat them out into daylight that felt wrong.

Cyrus had known every kind of light the mountain offered—the amber slant of dawn through the passes, the green-bellied glow of the deep ways, the cold silver the moon poured over Thornfell's shoulders in winter. This was none of those. This was a thin, exhausted brightness, like a lamp running low on oil, and it came not from the sun but from the ground beneath their feet.

The caldera rim stretched before them, a broken crown of black glass and slagged stone, and below it, in the great bowl where fire had lived for a thousand years, something vast was breathing badly.

"Oh," Wren said softly, and that was all. *Oh.* As if someone had shown her the end of a story she'd spent her whole life studying.

Cyrus climbed the last ridge with Elira's lantern cradled against their chest—her soul-light guttering gently inside the stolen heartflame's iron housing, the two fires pressed together like strangers sharing warmth on a long road—and looked down at the last dragon in the world.

Ashvane lay coiled in the caldera's throat like a river that had forgotten how to move. Cyrus had seen her once before, from a distance, years ago: a shape on the peak at dusk, wings half-spread to catch the wind, scales catching the last sun so that the whole mountain seemed crowned in copper. The villagers called it a blessing when she showed herself. The smugglers called it a warning to keep their cargoes honest.

There was nothing crowned about her now.

Her scales had gone the color of old ash, grey shot through with hairline cracks, and through each crack glowed a dull ember-red that pulsed out of rhythm with her breathing—too fast, then too slow, then not at all for three terrible seconds before dragging itself back. Her wings lay folded wrong, one joint bent beneath her at an angle that made Cyrus's own shoulders ache in sympathy. Frost rimmed her nostrils. *Frost*, on a dragon, at the mouth of a volcano.

And the wards—Cyrus felt their absence more than they saw it. All their life, Thornfell had had a particular hum to it, a pressure behind the ears like the moment before a storm, which every child of the passes learned to stop noticing. It was gone. The mountain stood open like a house with its doors torn off, and the cold coming up from below was not weather-cold. It was the cold of a room where something hungry has been waiting a very long time for the lock to fail.

"Ashvane," Wren called down, and her voice cracked on the scale of what she was doing—shouting courtesy across a mile of burning stone at a creature older than the courts, older than the ledger, maybe older than names themselves. "Ashvane, we bring the heartflame."

The great head lifted.

It took a long time. Muscles shifted under ash-grey hide like tectonic plates deciding whether to quarrel, and the neck rose by degrees, joint by grinding joint, until those eyes found them—eyes like banked furnaces, gold gone mostly dark, with only a coin-sized ember still alive at the center of each.

"*Small things,*" Ashvane said. Her voice was not a roar. It was worse than a roar: a whisper the size of an avalanche, so low Cyrus felt it in their teeth, in the hollows of their bones, in the scar on their palm that the mountain's gate had drunk from. "*I remember small things. Crawling over my mountain with your little fires and your little bargains.*"

"We're not—" Cyrus started.

"*All of you bargain.*" The head swung toward the lantern at Cyrus's chest, and one vast nostril flared, drawing in their scent. The ancient eyes narrowed—or tried to; the lids moved like rusted gates. "*You smell of Voss. You smell of ink. You smell of* below." A pause, and the whisper dropped even further, until it was almost tender, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet. "*You smell of the ledger. What have you let it write on you, small thing?*"

Cyrus looked down at their hands. The cut from the gate had scabbed over and split again on the climb, and the line of it sat across their palm like a sentence half-finished.

"My grandmother sold herself to save her family," they heard themself say. "I've come to un-sell her. And to give this back." They lifted the lantern. Inside, the heartflame turned restlessly, that stolen knot of dragonfire they'd pried from the vault beside Elira's guttering soul—it pressed against the glass like a moth against a window, and every time it flared, Ashvane's whole body shuddered with something that might have been hunger or grief or both.

"*Then come down,*" the dragon said. "*Before the last of me goes out. Before the ones beneath remember how to climb.*"

* * *

The descent into the caldera took an hour that felt like a season. There was no path; there was only slag and scree and ribbons of heat rising from vents that should have been roaring and were only sighing. Twice Cyrus's footing gave way and Wren caught them—her grip stronger than her slight frame suggested, her face drawn and grey from holding the ledger's wards open during the flight through the breathing halls. She hadn't slept. Neither of them had. The magic she'd burned was still bleeding out of her in small ways: her fingertips left faint afterimages when she gestured, and when she spoke, her breath fogged with tiny motes of light, as if her words were coming apart at the edges.

"You shouldn't have done what you did back there," Cyrus said, near the bottom. "Holding the wards. You're still—"

"Still leaking? Yes." Wren gave a thin smile. "I know what I spent. I'd spend it again. Argue with me when we're not standing in a volcano."

"That's exactly when I want to argue with you."

"Then live long enough to be tedious about it." But her hand found theirs and held on, and neither of them let go until the ground leveled and there was nowhere left to walk but toward the dying fire at the center of the world.

Up close, Ashvane was not majestic and she was not monstrous. She was *old*. She was old the way a beloved chair is old, worn smooth and broken-in and sagging in the places where a body had rested ten thousand times. Her breath came in shallow pulls, and where it escaped between her teeth it was cool—not hot, *cool*—and smelled of snow and struck flint. Lichen grew in the seams of her scales. Things had made homes in her. The world had begun taking her back before she'd even finished leaving it.

She watched them approach with that coin-sized ember in each eye, and when Cyrus stopped an arm's length from her muzzle—close enough to feel the faint warmth of her, close enough that the heartflame in the lantern strained toward her like a dog toward home—she laughed. It was a terrible sound, a landslide wrapped in sorrow.

"*They sent a child,*" she said. "*The courts, the guilds, the ledger-lords below—a hundred years of scheming, and it comes down to a child with their grandmother's handwriting on a scrap of skin.*"

"I'm not a child."

"*You are to me. Everything is. That is the curse of being last.*" The ember-eyes closed briefly. "*Last dragon. Last Voss. We are a matched set, you and I, small thing: the final entries in books no one should have been writing.*"

Cyrus knelt and set the lantern on the glassy ground between them. "Take it back."

"*No.*"

The word landed like a dropped anvil. Wren made a small sound of disbelief. Cyrus just stared.

"It's yours," they said. "It was stolen from you—we got it out of the Undercourt's vault, it cost—" They thought of the memory of Maren, gone soft-edged now, a photograph left in the rain. "It cost enough. Take it."

"*I cannot.*" Ashvane's head lowered until her muzzle nearly touched the lantern, and the heartflame inside beat against the glass in slow, desperate pulses. "*Fire does not return to a hearth that is already cold. That is not stubbornness, child, it is nature. My flame went out of me a long time ago—what burns in me now is only the memory of burning, and memories cannot hold a gift this size. If you open that lantern and pour it into me as I am, it will pass through me like water through a burned-out log, and it will scatter, and then it will be lost forever, and the mountain will go dark, and everything under it will come up.*" The whisper thinned almost to nothing. "*Unless.*"

"Unless what?"

The dragon's eye fixed on them. And Cyrus understood, before she said it, because some part of them had understood since the gate drank their blood—that the mountain remembered Vosses, that roads followed their grandmother's ghosts, that every bargain in their family's history had ended with somebody paying in the only currency that couldn't be counterfeited.

"*Two ways,*" Ashvane said. "*One: you end me. Quickly, cleanly, before the wards collapse on their own. A dead dragon's bones hold ward-shape for a generation—maybe two. Long enough for the surface courts to build something cruder. Walls. Armies. Graves.*" A pause. "*Two: you rekindle me. Pour the heartflame through a living vessel instead of a dead one. Give the fire somewhere warm to land.*"

"How?" Wren asked, though her voice said she already knew. "Through what vessel?"

"*A name,*" said Ashvane. "*A soul. Something bound and burning and freely given. The same currency the court below has always traded in. Your grandmother bought a guild with hers, little Voss. You could buy a mountain with yours.*"

The caldera was silent except for the sigh of the dead vents and the small, steady ticking of the lantern glass cooling.

Cyrus looked at the heartflame. They thought of the ledger hall, the quill in their hand, the moment they'd driven it through the Voss entry and felt every contract in their family's history snap like harp strings. They thought of Elira's voice rising out of the lantern—*the Smuggler's Road, mine alone, take it, take it, I hid it for someone brave enough to look*—and how freedom had sounded in her, real freedom, after a century of servitude. They thought of Maren shoving them into the cellar tunnel while the inn burned, of Odo holding the alley, of Wren saying *I'd spend it again.*

"No," Cyrus said.

"*You have not heard the terms—*"

"I don't need the terms. Everyone always wants to sell me the same thing with different wrapping." They stood, and their legs shook, and they made their voice flat and hard the way Grandmother's letters had taught them to read contracts: *watch what is not written, little bird; the trap is always in the margins.* "My grandmother signed because they told her it was the only way. The elders sold her because they told themselves it was the only way. Every Voss for a hundred years has paid the price that was put in front of them and called it duty. I'm done paying prices other people set."

"*Then the mountain falls,*" Ashvane said, and there was no anger in it, only a vast and ancient tiredness. "*Three days. Perhaps four. The wards are thread and I am the last knot. When I go out, the spire rises, and the hungry ones climb, and the villages in the valleys—you have seen them, small thing, you have drunk their ale and stolen their horses—*"

"I've seen them." Cyrus crouched again, level with the great eye, close enough to see their own reflection warped in the dark gold of it. "So here's what I've got instead of my soul. I've got a map that redraws itself. I've got blood the mountain recognizes. I've got a grandmother who smuggled anything past anyone, including, apparently, death. And I've got a mage who unmade one soul-contract already and only died a little doing it." Behind them, Wren made a noise that was half protest, half laugh. "You say fire won't go back into a cold hearth. Fine. Then we don't put it back into you the way it was taken out. We find another road. There's always another road. That's the first thing my family ever taught me."

"*There is no other road into a dying dragon.*"

"There's never been a Voss trying before."

The ember in Ashvane's eye flickered. It might have been amusement. It might have been the wind.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the dragon's flank, because there was nowhere else and because, absurdly, she was still the warmest thing on the mountain. Wren built a fire from scrub-pine and the pages of a court primer she'd carried for luck and fed to the flames with visible satisfaction. Above them the stars came out hard and clear through air that should have shimmered with heat and didn't.

Cyrus spread the Ember Map on the stone between them. It had faded almost entirely since they'd left the deep ways—the roads drinking themselves back into blankness, their purpose shifting, hunting for new shape—but around the margins, faint as breath on glass, Elira's handwriting still looped and curled. Cyrus traced it with a fingertip.

"She knew," they said quietly. "In her letter. 'When the fire dims, the debt comes due'—she wrote that a hundred years ago. She knew the dragon would fail someday, and she knew her contract was tied to it. She wasn't just buying the guild time. She was leaving instructions."

"For whom?" Wren asked.

"For whoever came after her angry enough to look." Cyrus's finger stopped on a symbol near the map's western edge—one they'd taken for a compass rose, weeks ago, in another life. It wasn't. It was a knot, a looping bind-mark, the same sigil that had sealed the mountain's gate, the same mark branded into every Voss ledger page. "Every secret in our family is a smuggling route. Every curse is cargo. She hid the answer the way she hid everything—in plain sight, marked as something boring."

Wren leaned over the vellum, and her light-flecked breath stirred its surface. "That mark means *bond*. Binding. In court cant it's the sign for a contract sealed in flesh." She looked up, and in the firelight her exhaustion was luminous, her eyes too bright, and Cyrus loved her so fiercely it felt like another wound. "You're not thinking of binding yourself to her. To the dragon. As a vessel."

"I'm thinking there's a difference between giving your name away and lending it."

"Cyrus—"

"Everyone below trades in souls because a soul is permanent collateral. Nobody lends. Nobody writes terms. Because nobody who deals in souls ever expected to meet someone rude enough to ask for a receipt." They tapped the bind-mark. "But that's what this says, if I'm reading her right. Not *sale*. *Bond*. A bond can be structured. A bond can expire. A bond—" their voice caught, and they let it, "—a bond can be shared. Fire won't stay in a cold hearth. So we don't make a hearth. We make a bridge. Between her flame and whatever's left of mine, with Wren's craft holding the shape, and the mountain's own memory—its *roads*, its *recognition*—doing the work the ledger used to do. A contract with no court attached. The first free contract in the history of either side of this mountain."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere far below them—farther than geology, deeper than sense—something vast turned over in its sleep and did not quite wake.

Wren sat back slowly. "It's mad," she said. "Soul-work without a court to anchor it killed Aldous. It nearly killed me." Then, softer, with the ghost of her old crooked smile: "He didn't have a map drawn by a woman who cheated the Undercourt. And he didn't have you." She reached across the fire and took Cyrus's hand, the scarred one, and pressed her thumb gently along the half-healed line. "We do it together or not at all. Swear it."

"Together," Cyrus said. "Not at all otherwise."

They sat like that as the fire burned down, shoulder to shoulder against the dragon's impossible flank, and above them Thornfell's peak stood black against the stars where the aurora of its wardlight should have danced. Below, Ashvane's breathing slowed toward morning, each pull longer than the last.

Cyrus drifted at the edge of sleep, and it was there—at the threshold, in the ungoverned country between waking and dreaming—that the whisper found them again. Not the ledger's voice this time. Something larger, colder, patient as pressure. The sound of a spire pushing upward through living rock, inch by inch, tasting the thinning wards the way a tongue tastes a crack in a tooth.

*Three days*, it seemed to say. *Perhaps fewer. The children of ash grow bold. The table is being set.*

Cyrus opened their eyes. On the Ember Map, in the blank space where the Undercourt's halls had once been drawn, fresh ink was seeping up through the vellum—new roads, climbing, branching, reaching for the surface. And at their highest point, right at the edge of the caldera's rim, the lines were forming a shape Cyrus recognized from nightmares and heralds' masks alike:

a door, opening.

"Wren," they whispered. "Wake up. It's not waiting three days. It's coming *now*."
