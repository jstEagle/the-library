## 30. What a Name Is Worth

*Cyrus*

The herald's candle burned down to a stub while Cyrus smiled and nodded and let the terms settle over them like snow.

Renew the contract. Sign as a Voss. Take up the pardon, the guild, the weight of two hundred years of bargains struck in the dark beneath the world—and the Undercourt would withdraw, the wards would hold, the villages on the mountain's skirts would never feel the cold that was already creeping into their wells. It was a generous offer. It was, Cyrus thought, watching the herald's bone mask catch the candlelight, the same offer their grandmother had been made, dressed in newer silk.

They walked out of the spire's antechamber under flag of parley, past the starving courtiers whose beauty was a kind of hunger, past the riders with their masks of yellowed bone, and down the long throat of the risen tower into the ordinary night of the caldera rim, where the wind smelled of ash and snow and, faintly, impossibly, of dragon.

Wren was waiting where they'd left her, wrapped in both their cloaks, her hands cupped around a fire that gave no heat because she hadn't the strength to give it any. She looked up, and whatever was in Cyrus's face made her stand too fast, swaying.

"You signed." Her voice cracked on the second word. "Cyrus, tell me you didn't—"

"I signed."

The sound she made was not a word. It was the noise a person makes when the ground opens under them, and Cyrus crossed the distance between them in three strides and caught her hands, and said, quietly, in the space between the wind's breaths:

"And then I drove the quill through the Voss line. In front of the herald. In front of the ledger's own wardens. And I invoked the ghost-market law."

Wren stared at them. The fire between their hands guttered, steadied, guttered again. Above them Ashvane's caldera glowed a dull, failing orange, like a hearth in a house where everyone had died.

"You what."

"Article the ninth. The law of the Hollow Fair. The one your merchant ghost recited while selling a dead man's childhood for a cup of warmth." Cyrus's breath fogged and tore away. "A name freely given can be freely sold. A name freely struck—by its own hand, before witnesses, with the ink still binding—voids every contract written against it. Every debt. Every bargain. Every pardon." They heard their own voice go strange. "Including the one that built the guild."

For a long moment Wren didn't move. Then she sat down. Not gracefully—she simply folded, the way a marionette folds when the hand lets go, and Cyrus went down with her, into the snow, into the wind's keening, and held on.

"It won't work," she said into their shoulder. "It can't. The ledger's been keeping names since before the mountain had a name. You think it honors a law it lets ghosts trade by? Cyrus, that law exists so the court can *collect*. It's a hook dressed as a courtesy—"

"It's a law," Cyrus said. "And the court is starving, and starving things keep their own rules, because rules are all they have left." They pulled back far enough to see her face, lit orange and shadow. "You told me once that contracts bind names, not blood. That's why they need a Voss to sign. Not Voss blood. Not Voss heirs. The *name*, written in the ledger's own hand, bound in its own ink. So if the name goes—"

"Everything bound to it goes with it." Wren's eyes had gone wide and dark and terrible. "Elira's sale. The elders' bargains. Odo's pardon—gods, the *pardon*, Cyrus, the thing that let the guild exist at all, the thing they sold your grandmother to buy—"

"Unravels." The word came out of Cyrus like a splinter drawn from flesh. "All of it. Every Voss pact, from the first to the last. And Elira's lantern with it. Her soul walks free, Wren. Free of the court, free of the debt, free of us."

"And you." Wren's hands came up and closed on their collar, and her voice shook with something that was fury wearing grief's face, or the other way around. "You'll be no one. Not stateless—*nameless*. The ledger won't have you. The surface court's rolls won't have you. You won't be able to inherit, to own, to swear an oath, to marry, to be *buried* under any name but the wind's. A nameless one cannot leave the Undercourt—that's what the merchant said. Do you understand what you're planning? If this goes wrong you won't die. You'll just *stay*. Forever. A debt with a heartbeat."

"Yes," Cyrus said.

"That's all? *Yes?*"

"What would you have me say?" They laughed, and it came out ragged. "That I've thought it through? I've thought it through every way there is, Wren, since the candle burned down, since before the candle, since the night I found a courier dying in the snow with my grandmother's handwriting in his fist. I've tried to find another road. There isn't one. The herald offered me the same bargain she took, and if I sign it clean, in fifty years some child of mine—or some child the court *makes* mine—will be standing on this same rim, being offered it again. That's what a name is worth to them. An endless chain of hands to hold the debt." They cupped her face. Her skin was cold as the snow they sat in. "So I'll break the chain at my own link. It's the only thing I have that they can't take. It's the only thing I have that they *need*."

Wren was silent for a long time. The wind filled the silence, and beneath the wind, so faint Cyrus might have dreamed it, the mountain groaned—Ashvane turning in her dying, or the Undercourt shifting its weight below, hungry and patient.

"There's a way it fails," Wren said at last, very quietly. "You know there is."

"I know."

"Then we plan for it." And something in her face changed—settled, the way a blade settles into a whetstone's groove. She was, Cyrus remembered, a court mage still, whatever they'd done to her for trying to unmake a contract once before. She had been made for exactly this: the finding of flaws in binding words. "If we're going to do the impossible, we're going to do it *properly*."

* * *

*Wren*

They planned by the light of a shielded lantern in the lee of a shrine to a god whose name had worn off the stone, which Wren thought was fitting. Everything that mattered tonight would be nameless.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. The exact words you'll use."

"'I, Cyrus Voss,'" Cyrus recited, "'being of sound mind and free will, strike my name from the ledger of the Undercourt, and with it every pact, pledge, debt, and bargain ever written against the house of Voss, from the first to the last. Let the ink remember what it bound, and bind no more.'"

Wren closed her eyes and heard it the way the ledger would hear it: not as sound but as structure, as weight, as the particular gravity of a promise. She turned it over. Probed its joints.

"The law requires three things," she said. "Freely given. Before witnesses. With the ink still binding. The ink is the easy part—you sign the renewal, the binding takes hold, and in that heartbeat between signature and seal, the name is *live*. That's your door. But freely given—" she opened her eyes— "they'll test that. The herald will test it. She'll offer you things. She'll threaten things. She'll show you Maren's face, or mine, or the villages burning, and every one of those is a lever, and a levered choice is not a free one, not to a ledger. You have to want this *clean*, Cyrus. Not as a trade. Not as a sacrifice. Not even to save the dragon. Can you do that?"

Cyrus was quiet a moment. Wind worried the lantern's shutter; their shadow shivered on the shrine stone.

"When I was nine," they said, "my mother sat me down at the guild ledger—you remember, the crumbling one—and taught me to write my name in the back, in the family column. She said, *this is who you are, and who you are is who we owe.* I wrote it. And I remember looking at it and thinking it looked less like a name than like a hook waiting for a fish." Their mouth twisted. "I have spent my whole life being the last entry in a book I never asked to be in. Wren, I don't want to strike the name to save Ashvane. I don't want to strike it to free Elira, though I'd walk into the deep for that alone. I want to strike it because it was never mine. It was a debt wearing my face. I want to be—" they stopped, and Wren watched them reach for a word and fail to find one, and finally say, helplessly, "—*ours*. Just ours. Mine and yours. Nothing owed on it."

Wren's throat closed. She had buried one mentor already because he'd tried to do a small, clean version of this—unmake one contract, one soul, one small mercy—and the ledger had taken him apart like a knot pulled from wet rope. She had sworn she'd never stand in that room again. And here she was, on a mountain's shoulder, planning to do it a hundredfold, with the only person she—

She didn't finish the thought. She didn't need to. Cyrus was looking at her the way people look at a fire in winter.

"Then the witnesses," she said, because if she didn't start talking about procedure she was going to say the other thing, and there wasn't time, and there was never time. "The law says witnesses, plural. The herald and her wardens will count—they'll *want* to count, they'll want to be able to say they watched you damn yourself. That serves us. But we need a witness who isn't theirs. Someone the ledger can't dismiss."

"Odo's dead," Cyrus said flatly. "Maren's—"

"Alive." Wren said it gently, and watched the flinch. "Somewhere. If we could reach her—"

"No." Cyrus's jaw set. "The herald burned her inn to get to us. If Maren's alive, it's because she's hidden, and I'm not dragging a witness's summons across the mountain with the court listening through every stone. Who else? The ghost-merchant? He trades in the Hollow Fair—he's bound by its law, isn't he? If he witnesses it, the law is *his* law too."

Wren had already thought of this, and had already hated the cost, and said it anyway. "He is. And he'll come, for a price. He told us what he trades. Memories for warmth. But he told us something else, in the Fair, do you remember? *The ledger takes names.* He wasn't warning us. He was *advertising*. He's seen this tried before, Cyrus. Maybe not a whole house—maybe just one soul, one desperate ghost trying to strike its own line—and he'll know where the law bends."

"Then we pay him." Cyrus said it simply, and Wren's chest ached, because she knew what was left to pay. She'd watched Cyrus trade the memory of Maren's face to buy passage to the vault. She'd been furious for three days and had never once stopped understanding. What was left? A mother's voice, maybe. The smell of the inn's kitchen. The weight of the ledger-book in small hands. The court would strip Cyrus down to the last smooth stone before this was over, and Cyrus would let it, and Wren could not—

"Stop," Cyrus said softly.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to offer to pay it yourself. I saw you do the arithmetic." They took her hand. Their palm was still scarred from the gate, the cut that had opened the mountain, and Wren found herself pressing her thumb against it as if to hold the wound closed forever. "Whatever it costs, it comes out of me. That's not pride, Wren, it's *law*. The name being struck has to pay its own passage. If you pay, the strike is tainted—a gift to a contract voids the freedom of it. You'd undo us with kindness." A ghost of a smile. "For once, the rules are on my side. Let me keep the one thing I'm still allowed to give."

She wanted to argue. She was, by training and temperament, an arguer; she had argued with archmages and won. But Cyrus's hand was warm around hers, and the wind was cold, and somewhere below them a dragon was dying of a theft two generations old, and every argument she had was, at bottom, *I cannot lose you*, and that was not an argument. That was just the truth, standing there naked in the snow.

"Then we drill it," she said instead. "Every hour until the parley. The words, the timing, the door. The heartbeat between signature and strike. And the failure case." She made herself say it. "If the ledger refuses the law—if it takes your name and keeps the debts—you'll be nameless *and* bound. The court will own a nothing it can carry anywhere. So we need a blade at that moment. Something that makes refusal more expensive than obedience."

Cyrus looked at her. "The heartflame."

"The heartflame." Wren nodded slowly. "It's still in the vault, still caged beside Elira's lantern, still burning with everything the court stole to feed its starving halls. If the ledger refuses—if the law fails—then I take the flame and I walk it up the spire's throat to Ashvane whether the court likes it or not, and every ward they've built to hold it becomes a cage around *them*. They know it. It's the one reason they haven't simply taken you by force. You're the key, but the flame is the *fire*, and fire is the one thing a starving court cannot fight." She met their eyes. "So we walk in under parley, you sign, you strike, and if the world breaks wrong, I burn my way out with a dragon's heart and you find me on the other side of it. Agreed?"

"Agreed." And then, because it was Cyrus, because it had always been Cyrus: "But you hold the flame only if I fall. Not before. Not because it's clever. Because I'm not spending you to save the world, Wren. I've had enough of people being spent."

* * *

*Cyrus*

They slept in shifts, and Cyrus's shift was the second, and they did not sleep.

They sat with their back against the shrine stone and watched the caldera breathe its failing light, and took stock of themselves the way a smuggler takes stock of a hold before a dangerous run: what's left, what's sound, what's worth carrying.

The map was blank now—had been fading since the vault, its roads dissolving one by one as if the mountain were forgetting its own memory. But Cyrus kept it anyway, wrapped in oilcloth against their heart. Elira's last gift. *The road only traitors know.* It had gotten them out of the deep once. It would not get them out again; the herald had sealed the way behind them. Whatever happened in the ledger hall, they would leave through the front door or not at all.

They thought about their name.

It was a small thing, really. Two syllables. A family word, worn smooth by generations of mouths. Cyrus Voss. Voss, who ran the passes. Voss, whose pardon was bought with a soul. Voss, whose name was written in a ledger under the world in ink that moved. They tried to imagine themselves without it and found—this surprised them—that they could. Not an emptiness. A clearing. A field after a wall comes down. They thought: *I could be the person Wren knew before she knew my name. The one who cut her cage open in Greyhollow market because a person was in it, not because a name was on it.*

The wind shifted. Footsteps in the snow, light and certain, and Wren came back from her turn at the rim and folded down beside them without a word, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while they just watched the mountain die by inches.

"I keep thinking about the pardon," Cyrus said at last. "The elders traded Elira for it. Their own freedom, their own guild, their own *names* kept clean above the law—and it cost her everything. And when I strike the line, that pardon goes too. Every descendant of every elder loses the shelter their grandfathers bought with my grandmother." They turned the thought over, looking for the guilt in it, and found only a cold, clean anger. "Good. Let them feel the wind for once. Let them learn what she paid."

"And you?" Wren asked. "What do you lose?"

"Nothing I was using."

Wren huffed—almost a laugh—and leaned her head against their shoulder. "Liar. You lose the guild. The last of it. The thing you've been dragging alone up and down these passes for years, the thing you hated and kept alive anyway because it was *hers*."

Cyrus was quiet. The caldera light pulsed, faint as a sleeping pulse.

"The guild was a chain," they said finally. "Beautiful links. I loved the links. But a chain's only purpose is to bind, and every link was forged in someone's blood." They reached up and touched the oilcloth over their heart, where the blank map lay. "She hid a road inside the map that only traitors could walk. Do you understand what that means? At the end, she *knew*. She knew what she'd signed, and she built a door out for whoever came after, and she hid it in the one place the court would never think to look: in the gift itself. She was a smuggler to her bones, Wren. Right up to and past the end of her soul. And I think—" their voice caught, and they let it— "I think if I could ask her, she wouldn't say *avenge me*. She'd say *unload the hold*. She'd say *travel light*. She'd say the road's only weight is what you choose to carry on it."

"So carry me," Wren said. "I'm light."

Cyrus laughed—really laughed, for the first time since the candle burned down—and pulled her in, and they sat like that while the mountain groaned and the stars wheeled and the failing fire below them dimmed another shade toward dark.

* * *

*Wren*

Dawn came up the color of old brass, and with it the sound that had been gathering at the edge of hearing all night: the Undercourt, moving. Not rising—the wards still held, barely—but *stirring*. The spire's black glass caught the early light and threw it back wrong, and Wren, who had been raised in a court of marble and law, recognized the gesture for what it was. An appetite, stretching.

"They'll call the parley at dusk," she said. "Starving courts negotiate at dusk. It's when the light favors them."

"Then we have the day." Cyrus was already moving, buckling on the last of their gear with the economy of long habit. "Words first. Again."

They drilled until the sun stood high and the shadows were short. The words of the strike, fifty times, a hundred, until Cyrus could say them asleep, until Wren could hear a single syllable out of place from across a burning room. The timing—the heartbeat between the ink taking hold and the seal descending, a gap the width of a moth's wing, and Cyrus practiced the quill-stroke on snow, on stone, on the back of Wren's hand with a charred stick, a single slashing line through an imagined name, until the motion lived in their wrist the way a knife-fight lives there.

Then the failure case. Then the signals. Then what to do if the herald separated them, if the wards came down between them, if the ledger *whispered*—and it would whisper, Wren knew; it had been whispering to Cyrus in dreams for days, and dreams were only its first language.

At midday they ate the last of the Saltcinder bread, hard as slate, and shared the last of the water, and Wren found she was memorizing it. The bread. The water. The way Cyrus's hair stood up in the mountain wind and the exact gray of their eyes when they were afraid and refusing to be. She had lost a mentor to a small version of this. She had sworn off large versions forever. And here she sat, planning the largest one ever attempted, and the strange thing—the thing she would remember all her life—was how *calm* it felt. Not peace. Peace was for afterward. This was something older: the stillness of two people who have decided, together, and whose decision has burned away every other question.

"Cyrus." She said it without meaning to. "After. If it works. If we're both on the other side of it and you're nameless and the dragon's burning and the world is absurd—what then?"

Cyrus looked at her across the dead fire ring, and the wind pulled at them both, and they smiled—the crooked, tired, absolutely certain smile she had first seen through the bars of a cage in Greyhollow market, on the worst and best day of her life.

"Then I'll need someone to call me something," they said. "Since no ledger will. I thought you might have ideas. You're good with words."

Wren laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, and she crossed the dead fire ring and kissed them, there in the full light of day with the enemy's spire watching, which was its own kind of signature. Its own kind of law.

When they broke apart, the light had changed. The brass dawn had gone to iron, and the wind had stopped—not gentled, *stopped*, the way air stops in a throat—and from the spire below came a single clear note, like a bell struck in a cathedral of bone.

The parley. Early. Of course. Starving things do not wait for dusk when the meal is walking toward them.

Cyrus straightened. Drew the oilcloth-wrapped map from their coat and, after a moment's thought, left it—left it there in the shrine's shadow, weighted with a stone, because the road it held was spent, and what they carried into the spire tonight should be nothing but themselves.

"Together?" Cyrus said.

"Together," Wren said.

And hand in hand, under a flag of parley they had sewn themselves from a torn cloak—white, because neither of them owned anything clean—they walked down toward the mouth of the risen spire, where the herald waited in the last of the light, and behind her, in a hall built of shelves and silence, the ledger lay open to a page with one name left on it.

Cyrus Voss.

Waiting to be written. Waiting to be crossed out.

Waiting to learn what it was worth.
