## 31. Descent to the Spire

They came down to the spire in the grey hour before dawn, when the ash on the caldera's rim had not yet warmed and the mountain held its breath between one heartbeat and the next.

Cyrus had dressed for it the way their grandmother must once have dressed for a hanging: slowly, deliberately, in her best. The coat was Maren's—recovered from a chest in the shrine at Kestrel Shelf, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and lavender gone to dust—and its lapels were embroidered with the Voss mark, the thorned key, stitched in thread that had once been silver and was now the color of old moonlight. They had not worn it since Saltcinder. They had sworn not to wear it again. But tonight they walked into a court that dealt only in names and inheritances, and so they wore the name like armor, like bait, like a wound opened on purpose.

The heartflame rode against their chest in a lantern of dragon-glass, and it was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm—not hot, exactly, but *alive*, pressing back against the ribs with each pulse. It had dimmed since the vault. It knew where it was going. Cyrus tried not to think about what that meant.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said beside them. "Where you breathe like you're counting."

"I am counting."

"What number are we on?"

"Four hundred and six."

"Good," she said. "Only four hundred more to go." Her hand found theirs in the dark, brief and hard, and then let go, because there were things that could not be done hand in hand, not yet, not where the road could see them. The mountain's roads still breathed around them, slow and tidal, following the memory of Elira Voss—and Elira Voss had walked this last stretch alone, every time, because some doors admit only one grief at a time.

Ahead, through the thinning pines, the spire stood up out of Thornfell's shoulder like a needle drawn from a wound.

It had risen three days ago, punching through granite and glacier ice with a sound like the world's longest scream, and it did not look built so much as *grown*—a tower of black stone veined with channels of pale light, tapering to a point lost in low cloud. Frost climbed its flanks in spirals. Where the surface courts raised walls to keep things out, the Undercourt raised something more like a throat. Cyrus could feel it swallowing when the wind moved: a slow peristalsis in the stone, drawing warmth down and down toward whatever hunger waited below.

At its base, where the rock had split open to receive it, a road led in. And standing on that road, exactly where the herald's message had promised, was a single figure holding a lantern aloft.

Under the lantern hung a flag of parley—a strip of white cloth, though even at this distance Cyrus could see the white was wrong. Not dyed. Bone-scraped. Thin enough to read your hand through.

"They'll honor it," Wren said. She wasn't asking.

"The Undercourt honors its law above all else." Cyrus adjusted the strap of the lantern, felt the flame shift against their sternum like a second heart learning the rhythm. "That's the whole problem. Law is how they eat you politely."

* * *

They walked down together, and the mountain watched them go.

Cyrus felt it in the trees first—the pines leaning, very slightly, all in the same direction, like a crowd turning its head. Then in the snow, which had stopped falling somewhere behind them, as if the sky itself were unwilling to interrupt. The wards were failing; everyone knew it; Ashvane's fire guttered in the caldera above them like a candle in a draft, and the cold came down off the peak in sheets, and yet here, along this one road, the air was almost warm. Warm the way a mouth is warm.

The herald waited beneath its bone-white flag, and up close it was beautiful, and the beauty was the worst part.

It wore a long coat of grey silk that moved without wind, and its face—if face it was—was smooth and ageless, carved like something meant to be admired rather than known. Its eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone. When it inclined its head to Cyrus, the motion had the grace of a dance learned over centuries, and Cyrus understood suddenly why the ghost-merchant in the Hollow Fair had warned them, why Odo had died in an alley rather than be taken alive, why the riders wore masks: because the Undercourt did not need terror anymore. Terror was for things that might escape. This was for things already caught.

"Heir Voss." The voice was soft, resonant, like a finger run round the rim of a glass. "You honor us. You cannot know how long we have hoped to say those words."

"I can guess." Cyrus kept their voice level. Four hundred and seven breaths. Four hundred and eight. "You sent terms. I've come to answer them."

"Not here." The herald gestured, and the road ahead lit—not torches, but veins in the black stone waking one by one, a spine of pale light running up the spire's flank. "Terms are read by candlelight, or they are not truly read. Will you walk with me? Your—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—*companion* is welcome. Parley extends to all who come under the cloth."

Wren stepped forward before Cyrus could answer, chin up, and Cyrus saw the herald's gaze slide over her and *recognize* her—saw the water-colored eyes narrow with something that might have been appetite or might have been memory.

"The mage of the flooded quarries," it said. "We wondered where the crown's castoffs went. Come. Both of you. We have been so hungry for company."

It turned and began to climb, and the light rose ahead of it, and Cyrus looked at Wren, and Wren looked at Cyrus, and between them passed everything that needed no words: *this is the trap, we always knew it was the trap, walk into it anyway.*

Wren squeezed their wrist once. Then she took her place at Cyrus's left shoulder—guarding the sword side, the old smuggler's habit—and they followed the herald up into the spire.

* * *

Inside, the spire was a cathedral that had been starved.

That was the only way Cyrus could hold the sight of it. The hall they entered through the base gate was vast beyond reason, its ceiling lost in darkness shot through with drifting motes of pale light, and everywhere—everywhere—was beauty laid out like a feast that no one had been allowed to touch for a hundred years. Tapestries worked in thread that glowed softly of its own accord, depicting courts and hunts and dances in colors that made Cyrus's eyes ache. Chandeliers of glass like frozen fountains. A floor of polished stone so dark and deep it seemed less a floor than a lake you might fall upward into. Music hung in the air somewhere, faint and endless, a melody with no source and no end, played on instruments that did not exist.

And under all of it, the rot. The tapestries' glowing threads flickered like dying coals. The chandeliers were dusty, half their branches dark. The music skipped, sometimes, the way a tired heart skips, and in those gaps Cyrus heard the true sound of the place: a silence so total it had weight, pressing on the eardrums, pressing on the chest.

Starving beauty. Beauty that had been magnificent once and now spent its last strength pretending.

Figures stood among the splendor—thin, elegant shapes in high-collared robes, faces smooth as the herald's or hidden behind masks of ivory and jet. As Cyrus passed, heads turned. Not quickly. Slowly, the way flowers turn toward light, except these turned toward *warmth*, and Cyrus carried the warmest thing in the whole hungry dark strapped to their own beating chest.

The heartflame pulsed. The nearest figures leaned, just slightly, like grass in wind. Someone made a sound—small, involuntary, almost human—that Cyrus would hear in dreams for the rest of their life.

"Don't look at them," Wren murmured.

"They're looking at me."

"I know. Don't look back. Looking back is how they learn what you're afraid to lose."

So Cyrus fixed their eyes on the herald's grey-coated back and climbed, and counted breaths, and felt the court's attention slide across them like cold water finding every seam in their clothes.

The stair wound upward inside the spire's throat. Through tall windows of smoky glass, Cyrus glimpsed the world outside falling away—the caldera rim, the glacier, the tiny distant lights of villages huddled in valleys that would die within days if Ashvane's fire failed. Below, far below now, the base of the spire vanished into a glow of pale veins spreading outward through the mountain like frost through a windowpane. The Undercourt rising. The wards failing. All of it converging here, in this tower, on this night, on the name stitched in old silver thread on Cyrus's collar.

"You carry your grandmother's work," the herald said without turning. Its voice floated back up the stair, unhurried. "Elira's map. Elira's blood. Elira's debt. Do you know, heir, that we remember her? The last Voss who walked these stairs. She wept at this landing." It paused on a step, gracefully, letting them draw level. "She bargained well, for a frightened woman. Better than any soul we have bought before or since. There are those in this court who still speak of her with something like love."

"She sold herself to save her family," Cyrus said. "There wasn't much love in it."

"There is always love in it," the herald said. "That is why the contracts hold. No one signs away their soul for gold. They sign for a daughter. A guild. a mountain full of strangers they will never meet. Fear opens the door, heir. Love walks through it. You of all people should understand—you are climbing our stairs right now, with the dearest thing in the world burning against your heart, and you tell yourself it is duty, or strategy, or defiance. But we can smell it on you. You came because you could not bear to let it go out." Its smooth face tilted, courteous, terrible. "We do not blame you for it. We were built on it."

Cyrus said nothing. Four hundred and sixty. Four hundred and sixty-one.

But the words went in, the way cold goes in, and they did not stop being true just because Cyrus refused to answer them.

* * *

Higher, the character of the court changed.

The grandeur fell away—or rather, was consumed—and what remained was older and stranger: corridors of unpolished stone where the veins of light ran thick and slow as honey, and the masked figures grew fewer but watched harder, and the music gave way to a sound like pages turning somewhere far off, endlessly, patiently, a sound Cyrus had been hearing in dreams for weeks.

The ledger. They were getting close to the ledger.

Wren felt it too. Cyrus saw her fingers curl, saw her lips move through the opening syllables of wardings she'd taught them on the pass—*hold-fast, mind-your-own-name, what-is-freely-given-cannot-be-stolen*—the small magics of protection that were all she had left after burning herself thin in the collapsing halls. She had slept two hours in three days. She was running on stubbornness and tea and, Cyrus suspected, on looking at them, the way a lamp runs on oil.

"Whatever happens in that room," she said quietly, as the herald drew ahead around a bend, "remember the plan. Remember the law. He'll try to rush you. Hunger makes them fast. The law is the only thing they move slow for."

"I remember."

"And if it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes *wrong*, Cyrus—"

"Then it goes wrong for both of us," Cyrus said, "because I'm not leaving this mountain without you, and I'm not leaving it without the flame, and I'm not leaving it as anyone's heir. That's the whole plan, Wren. Everything else is embroidery."

For a moment, in the honey-slow light, Wren smiled—not the wry guard-the-sword-side smile, but the other one, the rare one, the one from the geode cavern where trapped lightning had lit them both blue and brave. "Embroidery," she repeated. "I'm going to remember that. If we live, I'm putting it on a sign."

"A road-mending partnership," Cyrus said. "No names asked, none given. Embroidery optional."

The herald's voice drifted back down the stair, silken and patient: "Heir Voss. The hall awaits. Your grandmother kept us waiting too, at the end. It seems to be a family habit."

They climbed the last steps side by side, under the flag they no longer carried, into the light of candles that had been lit for this reading a hundred years ago.

* * *

The ledger hall opened around them like a held breath finally released.

It was round, and vast, and its ceiling was the underside of the mountain itself—raw granite ribbed with veins of pale light, all of them flowing inward toward the center of the room the way rivers flow to a sea. And at the center stood the desk.

Not a desk. An altar. A slab of black stone on which lay a book the size of a door, bound in something that was not leather, its pages thick and soft and faintly translucent, glowing from within with the light of ten thousand names. Around it burned candles—hundreds, thousands, wax pooling and never overflowing, flames standing perfectly still, as if even fire here had agreed to wait.

The herald took its place behind the altar and folded its grey hands.

"Terms," it said, "read by candlelight, as promised."

And as it spoke, the entire court came in—not marching, not crowding, but *arriving*, the way fog arrives, filling the galleries above the hall, the shadowed arches, the spaces between candles, until every tier of the great round room was lined with the smooth-faced and the masked, hundreds upon hundreds of them, silent, patient, all that ancient starving beauty gathered to witness a signature. The heartflame pulsed against Cyrus's ribs, faster now. It knew. Somewhere in its dying embers, the stolen piece of Ashvane's heart remembered this room, and wanted, desperately, to leave it.

"The offer stands as written," the herald said, and drew from its coat a single sheet of parchment, and laid it on the altar beside the book. "Renew the contract of Elira Voss. One signature, freely given, in the blood-line of the signer. In exchange: the Undercourt withdraws beneath the seal of the rekindled wards, forever. No rider crosses. no debt is called. The villages sleep safe. The dragon burns. All of it, heir—all of it—for the price of a name."

Its water-colored eyes lifted to Cyrus, and in them, for just a moment, beneath the courtesy and the patience and the centuries, Cyrus saw the hunger plain and naked and vast as the sea.

"We are so tired," the herald said softly. "You cannot imagine how tired. Sign, and end it. Sign, and be merciful. Your grandmother was. Be, at the last, a Voss."

The quill lay waiting on the altar, black-feathered, its nib already wet.

Somewhere above them, muffled by miles of stone, the mountain groaned—the wards failing by another degree, another hour shaved off a thousand villages' worth of tomorrows. The candles did not flicker. The court did not breathe. Ten thousand names glowed in the book like a city seen from very far away at night.

Cyrus stepped up to the altar, and reached out, and took up the quill.

Behind them, quiet as a vow, he heard Wren begin the warding words—the ones that would hold the ledger's attention, burn her thin, buy the seconds the plan required. The herald's eyes flicked toward her, then back, satisfied, certain, watching the heir of Elira Voss bend at last over the page.

Cyrus touched the nib to the parchment, and signed.
