## 34. Ashvane Wakes

The spire's throat was a chimney of black glass, and it breathed.

Wren felt it in her teeth before she understood it with her mind—a slow, rhythmic pull of air from below, warm and foul-sweet, like the exhale of something vast sleeping badly. The walls around them were not stone at all. They were fused ash, centuries of dragon-fire poured down the mountain's bones and cooled into a substance that caught the light of Cyrus's lantern and gave back nothing. It was the most beautiful dead thing she had ever seen, and she had seen the Undercourt's halls.

"Up," Cyrus said, and there was no argument left in either of them.

The heartflame rode in its lantern against Cyrus's chest, wrapped in three layers of Elira's smuggler's silk—cloth woven to carry fire without feeding it—but Wren could feel it anyway, a second heartbeat pressed to her partner's ribs. It was heavier than flame had any right to be. It was heavy with everything it had been taken from: two hundred years of wards, of warm winters, of villages that had never once needed to learn the word *frostbite*.

They climbed.

The stairs were cut for longer legs than theirs—or no legs at all—and spiraled upward through the dark in long switchbacks, each landing marked with soot-handprints pressed into the glass by hands long gone. Wren counted them as she climbed because counting kept her from thinking about what waited below, where Cyrus had stopped walking, turned around, and smiled at her with a face that was already saying goodbye.

*Not goodbye,* she told herself, gripping the wall. *A delay. A smuggler's trick. Elira's tricks always worked.*

Elira's tricks had also gotten her soul sold to a court of hungry ghosts, said a smaller voice, and Wren shut that voice away like a door on a draft.

Below them—far below, down through the breathing halls, through the market of memories and the ledger hall with its scorched wound in the vellum—the Undercourt was screaming. They had heard it start when the ink dried. Every contract unraveling at once, every soul-lantern bursting free like a bird from a snare, and beneath all of it one voice, the herald's voice, rising over the ruin of his master's work with a sound that was not anger so much as *hunger denied*.

He would come. He was coming now. And Cyrus Voss—who was legally no one, who had struck their own name out of existence to save a grandmother they'd never met—stood alone in the dark between the herald and this stair.

"You're slowing us down," Cyrus called up, cheerful and awful, and Wren realized she had stopped moving.

"I'm deciding whether to go back and strangle you."

"That's not very courtly of you."

"I'm not courtly anymore. That was the whole point." But she climbed, and Cyrus laughed—the short, sharp laugh of someone spending fear like coin—and the spire's throat swallowed them both, breath by breath.

* * *

Cyrus stood on the landing where the stair bent back on itself and listened to the herald climb.

That was the thing about the deep roads, the true smuggler's inheritance: you learned to listen. Not to footsteps—the Undercourt's riders made none—but to the pressure of a body displacing air, to the way silence changed shape when something filled it. Twenty years of running contraband through passes that could hear a heartbeat at fifty paces had taught Cyrus that silence was never empty. Silence was just a room waiting to be read.

The silence below was full of bone masks and cold purpose, rising fast.

*Good,* Cyrus thought, and did not examine too closely how much of that was courage and how much was the simple animal relief of having something to fight instead of something to lose.

They worked quickly. Elira's border-notes, scrawled in the map's margin in a hand like frost-ferns, had described the old guild signals: chalk-marks and wire-triggers, mirror-flash codes, the little treacheries of geometry that let four people hold a pass against forty. Cyrus had grown up on these the way other children grew up on lullabies. Aunt Maren singing the flash-code while kneading bread. Uncle Tam drawing tripwire patterns in spilled flour. A whole guild of ghosts, teaching a child how to make the world hurt the people chasing them.

"I'm sorry," Cyrus murmured—not to Maren or Tam, but to the herald, in a strange sideways honesty that felt right for the occasion. "You're about to have a genuinely terrible evening."

The first trick was simple: a length of copper wire strung ankle-high across the stair at knee-bend height, anchored to a shard of the spire's glass wall. It would do no damage. It didn't need to. It only needed to *sing*—a high, thin note that would tell Cyrus exactly where the first rider's foot came down, and exactly when.

The second trick was less kind.

The spire's throat breathed. In, and out, and in—a rhythm as fixed as tide. And anything that breathed had a rhythm, and anything with a rhythm could be *interrupted*. Cyrus wedged the lantern-shutter open a crack, angled a sliver of polished glass to catch its light, and braced the whole arrangement against a chip of flint with a hair-trigger of wax. When the wire sang, the shutter would fall, the light would flash once up the throat of the spire—

—and every rider behind the first would see, reflected in a hundred facets of black glass, a light that wasn't where the light was.

It was an old trick. Older than Elira. Smugglers had blinded toll-wardens with it since before the guilds had names. But the Undercourt's children had spent centuries in the dark, and eyes that live in the dark trust light absolutely. Light was *law* to them. Light meant direction, meant truth, meant prey.

Let them chase a ghost.

Cyrus crouched in the shadow of the landing's lip, knife loose in their grip, and watched the darkness below begin to fill with the pale gleam of bone.

They came the way water comes into a sinking ship—patiently, totally, from everywhere. First one mask, then five, then a dozen, pale ovals floating up the spiral like drowned moons. The lead rider's foot found the wire.

The note rang. Pure and small as a temple bell.

The shutter fell. Light flashed—and died, and flashed again off a facet forty feet higher, and the riders *surged*, all of them at once, toward the lie. Masks cracked against glass. One went through a reflection entirely, into whatever space lived behind the spire's skin, and the sound it made was brief and terrible.

And the herald—he came last, and he did not surge. He stopped at the wire. He looked down at the singing thread with the expression of a man reading an old letter, and then he looked up, directly, precisely, at the shadow where Cyrus crouched.

"There you are," he said gently. "The nameless child."

"The nameless *adult*," Cyrus said, standing. No point hiding now. The trick had bought what it was bought to buy: minutes. Maybe ten. Maybe six. "And I'm not your child. I'm nobody's. I checked."

"You are Elira's blood." The herald drifted up the last few feet of stair, robes whispering like paper, his bone mask tilted with something almost like tenderness. Behind him the riders milled, disoriented, snapping at reflections. "Do you know what she smelled like? We kept her scent in a jar for sixty years. Cold iron and river-water. We are very sentimental, we of the deep. It is the one appetite we cannot eat."

"You're stalling," Cyrus said. "I'm stalling. Isn't it nice that we agree on something?"

"It is," said the herald, and moved faster than anything wearing robes should move.

Cyrus met him with the knife and the landing and twenty years of dirty fighting, and it should not have been enough, and it was—barely, viciously—because the herald did not want to kill them. That was the horror of it. Every strike pulled. Every blow turned aside at the last instant, herding rather than harming, driving Cyrus backward up the stair toward some door the herald wanted opened. He fought the way a fisherman plays a trout, and Cyrus let themselves be played, let the retreat look real, because every step the herald took upward was a step away from Wren, and every second was a second bought at the price of blood—Cyrus's, thin lines of it, opening along forearm and cheek where the mask's edges kissed skin.

"You cannot win this," the herald murmured, almost sorrowful, pressing them back another turn. "You erased yourself. There is nothing in you for us to take. Do you understand what you've done? You've made yourself *empty*, little ghost. And empty things cannot bargain."

"Empty things can't be owned, either." Cyrus grinned through bleeding lips. "I did the arithmetic. I'm at peace with it."

Then the mountain shuddered.

Not the spire—the *mountain*. A groan rolled up through the black glass, deep as a whale's grief, and every rider below froze mid-motion, masks turning as one toward the peak, toward the source, toward the place where a dying heart had just heard footsteps.

Ashvane knew they were coming.

*Hold on,* Cyrus thought, pressing a hand flat to the trembling wall, to the old fire sleeping under the ash-glass. *Hold on, old one. She's bringing it home.*

* * *

The caldera rim was a crown of ice, and Wren reached it half-dead and wholly certain.

Her legs had stopped negotiating somewhere below the cloud line; they simply obeyed now, one after the other, mechanical as a clockwork she was winding down. The air at the top was thin and razor-cold and tasted of sulfur and snow, and the sky above Thornfell's peak was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges—dawn coming whether anyone deserved it or not.

And in the bowl of the caldera lay the dragon.

Wren had read about Ashvane in the closed stacks of the court library, in books chained to shelves because the knowledge itself was dangerous: *the Last Ward, the Fire Beneath, eldest of the line of Vellithrax*. The books had given her a size. Nothing had given her the truth of it—that a dying dragon is not smaller than a living one, only *heavier*, as if the weight of all that failing fire pressed down on the world through its body. Ashvane lay coiled in the caldera's heart like a mountain range that had decided to grieve, scales the grey of banked coals, each one big as a barn door, and between them ran veins of dull orange light that pulsed slower with every breath. Slower. Slower.

Frost furred the scales near her spine. Frost, on a dragon.

"Oh," Wren said aloud, and her voice cracked in the thin air. "Oh, you poor enormous creature."

The eye nearest her opened.

It was the size of a millpond and the color of deep amber, and it regarded her with a weariness so ancient it circled all the way back around into kindness. When Ashvane spoke, her voice did not come through the air so much as rise through the ground, through Wren's boots, through her bones—a voice like a landslide that had learned gentleness over two hundred years of practice.

*Little thief of the deep ways.* A plume of steam, more grey than gold. *You smell of my fire. You carry what is mine.*

"I'm returning it." Wren slid down the scree toward the great head, falling twice, not caring. "I need to know how. The lantern—I have the vessel, but the books say a stolen flame must be *given back freely* or it burns the hand that holds it. Is that true?"

Something moved in the amber depths that might have been amusement. *Your books are right. Your court wrote them after they helped steal me. Ask your next question carefully.*

Wren's hands were shaking—from cold, from altitude, from the plain animal terror of kneeling on a dying god's cheekbone—but she made herself say it plainly. "Then I'm asking. Take it back. Freely given, by me, Wren of the Ninth Circle, disgraced and unrepentant. I give you your own heart."

*And the price?* The great eye did not blink. *There is always a price, mage. You know this better than most. What will you pay to hand me back my fire?*

"Nothing," Wren said. "That's the point. That's the *whole point*. Nobody pays. Not me, not Cyrus, not the villages below, not you. The paying stops here. Tonight. With us." Her voice broke and she let it. "Please. I am so tired of everyone paying."

The silence that followed lasted three of Ashvane's failing heartbeats, and in that silence the dragon studied the small broken creature kneeling on her face, and found—against two hundred years of evidence to the contrary—something worth believing in.

*Then give,* said Ashvane, and opened her jaws.

Wren unwrapped the silks with numb fingers, lifted the lantern, and walked into the dark of the dragon's mouth as calmly as if she were entering a library—which, she supposed, in the end, she was: the oldest archive of warmth in the world.

She tipped the heartflame out.

It fell upward.

That was the only word for it—the flame rose out of the lantern like water running backward, streaming into the cavernous dark, and the dark *drank*, and the drinking was loud, a sound like a forest fire remembered by a forest, and the veins along Ashvane's scales lit vein by vein by vein, orange to gold to white, frost flashing to steam, steam to thunder—

The dragon's heart caught fire, and the mountain caught fire with it, and Wren was thrown flat on the scree by a wind of pure heat and heard, above the roar, above everything, the last thing she expected:

Laughter. Deep and rolling and *young*, impossibly young, two hundred years of age burned away in a single breath.

*TELL THE NAMELESS ONE,* Ashvane thundered to the whole waking world, *THAT THE DEBT IS REFUSED. TELL THEM THE FIRE REMEMBERS WHO CARRIED IT HOME.*

New fire flooded the mountain's bones. Far below, in the halls of the Undercourt, wards that had been starving for two centuries suddenly ate their fill—and began, with the slow inexorable patience of stone, to close.

* * *

Cyrus felt the rekindling come up through the soles of their boots like a second sunrise.

One moment they were pinned against the spire's glass, arm burning, knife gone, the herald's hand closing on their collar with the gentle inevitability of gravity—and the next, heat roared up the spire's throat like the mountain inhaling a furnace, and the black glass walls blazed gold, and every bone-masked rider on the stair screamed as the light they trusted became the light that burned.

The herald did not scream. He released Cyrus slowly, reverently almost, and turned his masked face upward toward the distant bloom of dawn-fire, and stood very still while his court's long hunger came apart around him.

"Well," he said softly. "Well done."

"Don't—" Cyrus coughed, hauling themselves upright against the blazing wall. "Don't you dare sound proud of me."

"You mistake me." The herald turned back, and though the bone mask showed nothing, Cyrus heard the smile in his voice, and it was the saddest sound they had ever heard. "I am the first elder of the Voss guild. I built the first bargain. I have served the debt for nine generations, hoping, always hoping, that some heir would be clever enough or stubborn enough to end it—and none ever was. They all paid. You are the first to refuse." The mask tilted. "You have freed me too, little ghost. Do you understand? The debt is void. I am only an old man now, in a mask, in a mountain that is closing."

Riders fled past them down the stair, masks streaming smoke, fleeing toward the deep places as the wards drank new fire and squeezed. The spire itself groaned, its glass skin crazing with golden cracks, beginning the long collapse back into ordinary rock.

"So what happens to you?" Cyrus asked.

"What happens to any traitor whose war ends?" The herald stepped backward off the edge of the landing—off into the shaft, into the rising light—and fell upward, dissolving into sparks even as he fell, an old man's shape unravelling into embers that streamed away toward the surface like a river of returning souls.

From somewhere in the blaze, faintly, came a voice like a landslide learning joy: *THE NAMELESS ONE AND THE MAGE ARE EXPECTED AT THE CALDERA. SHE SAYS TO TELL YOU THE CLIMB IS EASIER NOW. SHE HAS MADE THE MOUNTAIN HOLD OUT ITS HAND.*

Cyrus looked up. The spire's throat, which had been a chimney of dead glass, now glowed from within, and the stairs—impossible, wonderful—had begun to smooth and shorten, the mountain reshaping its own bones, building a road home for the two people who had carried its fire back.

Cyrus laughed until they cried, and started climbing, because somewhere at the top of all that light, Wren was waiting—and below, far below, the last sound the Undercourt made as the wards sealed was not a scream at all.

It was the sound of a ledger, closing.
