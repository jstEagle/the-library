## 37. The Court Above Comes Calling

The summons arrived the way the crown did everything: wrapped in silk, sealed in gold, and entirely too late.

Wren found it on the doorstep of the waystation at dawn, three weeks after the spire fell, pinned beneath a river stone so it wouldn't blow away. The courier who had delivered it was already gone—she could see the churned hoofprints in the frost, a rider's impatience, a courtier's distaste for the mud of the border roads. The envelope itself was pristine. Cream vellum, unblemished by ash or weather, as if the paper had traveled through some cleaner world than this one.

She stood in the doorway with her breath steaming and read it twice.

*By order of Her Radiant Majesty, Queen Isolde III, Cyrus Voss is summoned to the Court of Thorns-and-Laurels, there to receive the gratitude of the realm: to wit, the Barony of Greyhollow, its mills, tolls, and tenancies; the Order of the Mountain Star, first class; and a seat at the Queen's own council, with all privileges pertaining.*

*The Crown acknowledges with thanks the resolution of the Thornfell Crisis.*

*Crisis.* Wren turned the word over like a coin she suspected was clipped. Three thousand souls had risen out of the Undercourt's vaults like sparks off a bonfire. A dragon older than the kingdom had opened one amber eye and set the mountain alight again. Odo was dead in an alley in Saltcinder. Maren's inn was a black square of foundation stones. And the crown—whose soldiers had chased them through flooded quarries, whose magisters had signed Wren's exile, whose taxes and tariffs had squeezed the smugglers' guild until selling a soul to the dark seemed like good bookkeeping—that crown wished to express its *thanks*.

She carried the letter inside. Cyrus was at the hearth, mending a harness strap, their hands moving with the absent competence of someone who had done such work since before they could write. They looked up when she said nothing for too long.

"What is it?"

"The Queen has decided you're a hero." Wren dropped the letter in their lap. "Congratulations. You're a baron."

Cyrus read it slowly. Wren watched their face do something complicated—the same expression, she thought, that they'd worn in the ledger hall, when the ink bound and the quill went through the Voss line. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of someone seeing an old trap rebuilt in new timber.

"They want to give me Greyhollow," Cyrus said. "Greyhollow, where their magistrates kept you in a cage with a sign around your neck."

"Where I *was* in a cage," Wren corrected. "The sign said *property of the Undercourt, transit pending*. Which, in fairness, was legally accurate. That was rather the problem."

"Wren."

"I know." She sat down across from them, knees nearly touching in the small room. "I know what it is. But we should talk about what it *is*, because there are two answers, and only one of them is safe to say out loud."

Cyrus set down the harness. Outside, the wind came down off Thornfell carrying the smell of warm stone—the mountain still breathed heat these days, Ashvane's fire working through old veins of it—and somewhere below the ridge, the morning bell of the refugee camp rang thin and clear.

"All right," Cyrus said. "Tell me both."

* * *

They walked up to the caldera rim that afternoon, because Cyrus thought better with the mountain under their feet, and because Wren had learned that arguments went easier when there was something vast to be small against.

Ashvane lay along the far lip of the crater like a hillside that had decided to dream. The dragon's scales, which had been dull as slag when they'd first seen her dying, now caught the low sun and threw it back in slow ripples of copper and ember-red. Her breathing moved the whole air of the caldera—in, and the wind drew toward her; out, and warmth rolled over the rim and down the passes, melting frost in long advancing lines. Every few minutes her tail shifted, and the ground hummed.

"She's stronger," Wren said. "The wards are holding without effort now. I can feel them from here—clean, like a struck bell."

"Mm." Cyrus was watching the dragon, but Wren could tell their mind was elsewhere. "Say the two answers."

"The flattering answer," Wren said, "is that the Queen is grateful. That the crown saw a disaster coming and wants to honor the people who stopped it. That a barony and a council seat are a kingdom's way of saying *thank you*."

"And the true answer?"

"The true answer is that the crown spent three weeks doing nothing while the wards failed, and the moment the crisis resolved itself, it moved to own the result." Wren crouched, picked up a shard of dragon-glass from the rubble of the fallen spire—it crumbled to glittering sand between her fingers—and let the wind take it. "A barony isn't a gift, Cyrus. It's a leash with land attached. The moment you hold Greyhollow, everything you did belongs to the crown. The miracle gets a royal seal on it. The dead get a line in a chronicle written by court scribes. And you—" she looked at them, and gentled her voice despite herself, "—you spend the rest of your life in a council chamber, being thanked, which is how power says *shut up*."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Below them, past the caldera's shoulder, the refugee camps strung along the valley like lanterns: the villages that had emptied when the cold came, the free-town folk who'd fled Saltcinder after the riders, all of them waiting to learn whether the mountain would let them come home.

"When Maren found us," Cyrus said at last, "after the spire fell. She wept. Do you remember what she said?"

"You told me. She said, *you gave away the only thing we ever had, and it was the right thing, and I don't know how to forgive that*."

"No. She said—" Cyrus smiled faintly. "She said, *your grandmother would have hated this, and then she'd have done it herself*. That's the inheritance I kept. Not the name. The judgment." They turned to face Wren fully. "If I take the barony, I'm signing something. Another contract. And every contract I've ever seen signed in a fine hand cost somebody underneath it everything."

* * *

The court came to them anyway, because crowns do not wait to be refused; they arrive pre-celebrated.

They came a week later, up the eastern pass: forty riders in white and gold, banners snapping, trumpets announcing themselves a full mile out so that no one could claim they hadn't been properly heralded. At their head rode Lord Chancellor Aldery Vane, whom Wren remembered from her years at court—a narrow man with a narrow face, silver at the temples, hands folded on his saddlebow like a man perpetually about to applaud. Behind him came a herald with a scroll case, six honor guards, and, absurdly, a wagon bearing a carved oak chest that Wren would later learn contained the actual regalia: a chain of office, a charter with ribbons and wax the size of a fist, a sword of ceremony that had never cut anything but air.

The refugees came out of the camps to watch. That was the thing about spectacle—even hungry people will stand in the cold to see power perform itself.

Vane dismounted at the waystation with the grace of a man stepping onto a stage he owned. His eyes found Cyrus first, catalogued the patched coat, the burn-scarred hands, the total absence of anything worth taxing, and did not so much as flicker. Then they found Wren, and flickered considerably.

"Magister Wrenvale." A pause, delicately weighted. "Or should I say—no, I believe the matter of your standing remains under review. How fortunate that you've kept such distinguished company."

"How fortunate," Wren agreed pleasantly. "Lord Chancellor. You've brought a great deal of gold leaf up a very muddy road."

"The Crown spares no expense in honoring its heroes." He turned to Cyrus, and his voice lifted into the register men use when they know the crowd is listening. "Cyrus Voss! Last of that storied name. Kneel, if you would, and receive the gratitude of Her Radiant Majesty."

"I'll stand, thank you," Cyrus said. "I've done a lot of kneeling lately. It didn't suit."

A ripple through the crowd. Vane's smile held. He gestured, and the herald stepped forward with the scroll, unrolling it with a crack of ribbon, and began to read in a voice trained to fill cathedrals: the barony of Greyhollow, the mills and tolls, the Order of the Mountain Star, the seat at council, *in perpetual gratitude for the resolution of the Thornfell Crisis, and in recognition of services rendered to the Crown and realm*—

"Stop there," Cyrus said.

The herald stopped. Vane's eyebrows rose a precise quarter-inch.

"*Services rendered to the Crown*," Cyrus repeated. "That's not right. I didn't do this for the crown. With respect—and I mean that, Chancellor, because you climbed a mountain in winter to be here—but if you're going to put words in the air over those people's heads, they should be true ones."

"My lord Voss—"

"Not a lord. Not yet, anyway, and never, if I have any say." Cyrus raised their voice—not shouting, Wren noted, just letting it carry the way smugglers learn to carry it, pitched to reach the back of a crowd without insulting the front. "You all saw what happened here. Some of you were in the valley when the cold came. When the fires went gray. When the riders crossed the ice and took people in the night. So let me tell you plainly who ended it.

"It wasn't the crown. The crown's soldiers chased us through the quarries. The crown's magistrates had caged my friend and sold her passage to the Undercourt because her research embarrassed them. The crown knew the wards were failing for years—we found the reports, Chancellor, in your own archives, stamped and filed and ignored—and the crown did nothing, because the mountain's troubles were poor people's troubles, and poor people's troubles don't reach the throne until they start costing tax revenue."

Somewhere in the crowd, a woman made a sound—not quite a cheer. More like a door opening.

"What happened here," Cyrus went on, "was paid for by the dead. By Elira Voss, who sold her soul twenty years ago to keep a guild alive, and whose name I struck from the ledger so she could go free. By Odo Ferrin of Saltcinder, who died in an alley holding a door so two strangers could run. By the ghost-folk of the Hollow Fair, who traded their last warm memories to warn us what the ledger costs. By a dragon named Ashvane, who burned for centuries to keep this border intact and asked nothing of anyone—not the crown, not the courts, nobody—and who nearly died for it."

They turned back to Vane. Their voice came down soft, and somehow that was worse than the carrying voice.

"You want to give me Greyhollow. Its mills, its tolls, its tenancies. Chancellor, those tenancies are the families in those camps. If I take that barony, I become their landlord before they've even buried their dead. No."

"Lord Voss," Vane said, and now the silk had thinned enough to show wire beneath, "the Crown's gratitude is not accustomed to—"

"I'm declining," Cyrus said. "Publicly. Formally. Write it down." They nodded at the herald's scroll. "And while you're writing, correct the record. The credit belongs to the dead. All of it. Put *that* in the chronicle. Elira Voss. Odo Ferrin. The nameless of the Hollow Fair. And Ashvane, the last dragon, who is alive and awake and lying four hundred feet above your head, and who I would strongly advise the crown to treat with more respect than it treated her fire."

The silence afterward had weight. Wren felt it press against her eardrums. Forty riders sat very still. The chest of regalia gleamed on its wagon, absurd, unclaimed.

Then, from the back of the crowd, the woman who had made the door-opening sound began—to Wren's astonishment—to sing. It was an old pass song, a mourning song, the kind sung at burials when there wasn't money for a stone: *the mountain keeps what the mountain loves, and gives back only light.* Two voices joined it. Then ten. Then the whole camp, ragged and off-key and utterly unstoppable, singing the dead up the mountainside while the crown stood in the middle of it holding a charter no one wanted.

Vane's face went through several small adjustments and settled on gracious defeat. "The Crown will, of course, respect your decision," he said, each word laid down like a card he hoped no one had counted. "History will note your... modesty."

"History can note whatever you write," Cyrus said. "But the people here will remember what actually happened. That's the difference between a chronicle and a memory, Chancellor. One of them belongs to you."

* * *

They left at dusk, the column winding back down the pass with its trumpets muted and its regalia unbestowed, and Wren stood at the waystation window watching the torchlight shrink into the dark.

"He'll be back," she said. "Not with gifts next time. Men like Vane don't record refusals, Cyrus. They record debts. In his ledger, you've just cost the Queen a barony and a very useful hero. That's not modesty to him. That's insubordination with a soundtrack."

"I know." Cyrus was banking the fire, their face lit orange and shifting. "Let him keep his books. I've seen what happens to ledgers that think they own people." They glanced up, and there was something in their expression Wren hadn't seen since before the spire—something lighter, almost startled by itself. "It felt good. Saying no out loud. I've been saying yes to duty since I was born. Yes to the guild, yes to the name, yes to the debt. Saying no to a lord chancellor in front of three hundred people may be the freest thing I've ever done."

"You realize," Wren said, settling beside them on the bench, shoulder against shoulder, "that you're now legally no one, politically inconvenient, and famous throughout the borderlands. There are perhaps a dozen people in the world in your exact position, and most of them end up either saints or cautionary tales."

"Which are you betting on?"

"Neither." She laced her fingers through theirs. The burn scar on Cyrus's palm had healed to a pale seam where the mountain's gate had drunk their blood. "I'm betting on the road-mender. I've seen your work. Very thorough."

Outside, the wind turned, and came up from the caldera warm.

It was near midnight when the knock came—not the confident knock of authority but the furtive double-tap of someone who had learned, recently and painfully, to be quiet. Cyrus opened the door on a figure swathed in a traveling cloak gone gray with road dust, hood back, face hollowed by grief and distance.

Maren.

But it was not Maren's arrival that made Wren rise slowly to her feet, heart beginning to hammer. It was what Maren carried, wrapped in oilcloth and held out in both hands like something rescued from a fire:

A scroll case. Scorched at one end. Familiar.

"There's another map," Maren said hoarsely, pushing past them into the firelight. "Elira drew two. And Cyrus—the second one isn't a road into the mountain." Her eyes, red-rimmed and fierce, went from Cyrus to Wren and back. "It's a road *out*. For all of them. And someone else has been looking for it—and they're closer than the crown ever was."
