## 38. A Guild of Two

*Cyrus*

The mountain breathed wrong for most of a year.

Cyrus learned its new rhythm the way they'd once learned tide-tables and guard rotations: by walking it. Thornfell's shoulder had been punched through where the Undercourt's spire rose and fell, and what remained was a wound of glassed stone and slag that wept heat on cold mornings and cold on warm ones. The passes below had shifted too—old roads swallowed, new cracks opening like mouths—and the folk displaced from the villages beneath the caldera came trickling along those broken ways in ones and twos and, later, in whole caravans, carrying what they could save.

Somebody had to know the roads.

It turned out that somebody was two somebodies, and one of them couldn't stop talking about drainage.

"You can't just fill the washout with gravel," Wren said, crouched at the edge of a collapsed switchback above Hollowmere, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and grey mud to both of them. "The meltwater will undercut it by spring. You need a culvert. Stone-lined. You—" she pointed a muddy finger at a burly quarryman who had been filling baskets with cheerful incompetence all morning, "—you're going to dig me a channel three feet deep from that seep to the bend, and you're going to line it before you cover it, or I will personally haunt you."

The quarryman looked at Cyrus. Cyrus looked at the sky, which was doing something lovely and unhelpful with pink clouds over the caldera rim.

"She means it," Cyrus said. "She haunted a man once."

"I did not *haunt*—"

"There were witnesses."

Wren threw a pebble at them. It bounced off their shoulder with the accuracy of a woman who had spent a year learning exactly how far she could throw things at the person she loved, which was to say: precisely.

They worked through the afternoon. Cyrus hauled stone because hauling stone was simple in a way nothing else had been for a long time—the weight honest in the arms, the placement obvious, done or not-done. Wren drew the water-ways with chalk and certainty, muttering about gradients, and when a child from the caravan wandered too close to the drop-off, Cyrus caught him without thinking, swung him up onto their shoulders, and carried him back to his mother as though their arms had always been made for two purposes: smuggling and this.

That night, camped above the finished stretch of road with the caravan's cook-fire smoking down the slope, Cyrus took out the map.

They still called it the Ember Map, out of habit, but there was no ember left in it. The vellum lay pale across their knees, blank as fresh snow, blank as an unwritten page. Once it had crawled with living ink, roads that moved like lungs breathing, their grandmother's hand guiding them through the dark of the world. Now it was only skin. They had tried, in the first weeks, to feel grief about that. What they felt instead was something closer to gratitude—a debt paid so completely that even the paper had been released.

"Still blank?" Wren asked, settling beside them with two cups of tea that tasted mostly of pine needles and optimism.

"Still blank." Cyrus ran a thumb along the border, where Elira's confession had once hidden itself in cipher. Smooth now. Quiet. "I keep waiting for it to want something."

"Maybe it's done wanting." Wren blew on her tea. "Wouldn't that be nice."

Cyrus laughed—not the sharp bark they'd used in market squares and escape tunnels, but something slower, rustier, a sound they were still relearning the shape of. "Nothing we touch is ever done wanting."

"No," Wren agreed, and leaned her head against their shoulder. "But this might be close."

* * *

*Wren*

Wren had been a creature of courts once. She remembered it clearly now, the way you remember a fever: the marble corridors of the surface palace, the ledgers of favor and obligation, the way every kindness arrived pre-invoiced. She had thought, fleeing it, that she was leaving structure behind. That freedom would be formless.

Nobody had warned her that freedom would look like a woman arguing about culverts at dawn.

But here was the truth she kept discovering, small and startling each time: the work had its own liturgy. Rise before the light to check the frost-line on the pass. Boil water. Walk the road you built yesterday and find the flaw in it. The rhythm held her the way the old rituals never quite had, because this liturgy ended in something visible—a family crossing safely, a cart not foundering, a child asleep instead of lost.

Today's liturgy involved a bridge.

The old Kestrel crossing had rotted decades ago; they'd nearly died on its bones once, and Cyrus still went quiet whenever they passed the shelf where it had hung. But refugees kept coming up the valley, and the valley needed a crossing, and so Wren spent three weeks doing arithmetic on rope-tension and stone piers while Cyrus negotiated with the Saltcinder ropemakers—negotiated being a generous word for what happened when the last Voss, name struck from every ledger in creation, explained that payment would be in road-mending, escort-work, and the occasional discreet recovery of stolen goods.

"They love you down there," Wren said one evening, watching Cyrus come up the trail with rope-coils over one shoulder and a sack of dried apples someone had refused to let them pay for.

"They remember my grandmother."

"They remember *you*. There's a difference." Wren tested the first pier-stone with her heel. Solid. "Odo used to say the Voss name opened doors. Turns out it wasn't the name at all. It was the person standing behind it, holding it out like a lantern."

Cyrus set down the coils and looked at her with an expression Wren had learned to read slowly, over months: not the smuggler's mask, not the heir's wariness, but the face underneath, the one that still seemed surprised to be allowed tenderness. "You've been talking to Odo's ghost again."

"He'd have liked that. He'd have charged us for it." The joke landed soft, the way their jokes about Odo always did now—grief worn smooth by handling, no longer cutting. Wren crossed to them and straightened the collar of a coat that didn't need straightening, because sometimes her hands wanted an excuse. "Bridge is sound. We can open it tomorrow."

"Tomorrow we open it," Cyrus said, and kissed her forehead, flour-dust and cold air and home.

* * *

*Cyrus*

The guild register was Maren's idea.

She'd turned up in early autumn—alive, half-starved, furious, and weeping, in roughly that rotation—after months presumed dead in the burning of her inn. She had walked out of the fire through the same cellar tunnel she'd shoved Cyrus into, and had spent the time since moving between refugee camps, cooking for hundreds, and pretending her hands weren't shaking. When she finally reached their camp above Hollowmere, she stood at the edge of it a long while before Cyrus saw her, and neither of them spoke for a full minute, and then Maren hit Cyrus's arm hard enough to bruise and then held on like the mountain was falling again.

Now she ran the camp kitchen and issued opinions on everything, including, one gray morning, the matter of the ledger.

"You should start one," she said, slapping down a battered account-book she'd salvaged from somewhere. "A proper register. Names of everyone you guide, debts owed and paid, the works. A guild without a ledger is just vagrancy with better intentions."

"We're not a guild, Aunt Maren."

"You feed forty people a week. You mended the Kestrel crossing. You've got a ropemaker in Saltcinder who extends you credit and a quarryman who'd die for the mud-witch. Call it whatever you like. Write it down anyway." She pushed the book across the trestle table. Her eyes, when Cyrus met them, were gentler than her voice. "Names matter, girl. You of all people should know what happens when nobody writes them down."

Cyrus looked at the ledger. Blank pages. Clean binding. A thing waiting to become a weight.

They thought of the vault under the mountain, shelves of soul-lanterns each burning with a name. They thought of ink binding, quills driven through entries, debts voided and souls freed. They thought of their own signature, once offered to renew an ancient hunger, and the other thing they'd done with the pen instead.

"Maren." They slid the book back, gently. "If I write names in that book, someone will eventually come to collect against them. That's what ledgers are. That's all they've ever been, in my family."

Maren's jaw worked. "Then write debts, not names. Write what's owed and what's given. Leave the rest blank."

And that—Cyrus had to admit—was very like her. Very like Elira, too, if the stories were true; the woman who hid roads inside maps and confessions inside borders. So they compromised, the way smugglers do. They kept the book. They wrote in it every evening, sitting by the fire with Wren reading over their shoulder: *Two sacks barley, given freely, repaid in passage. One mended roof, owed to the Widow Anthe, due before snowfall. One pair of boots, taken from Odo's stores, never repayable.* No names but the dead's, no signatures, no bindings. Debts and gifts, tracked like weather.

Maren pretended not to be pleased. She was terrible at pretending.

* * *

*Wren*

Winter came down off the peak early that year, and with it came a rumor: a column of refugees on the far side of the mountain, thirty souls or more, trying to cross the high shoulder before the snows closed the passes entirely. Among them, the messenger said—breathless, frost-burned—a court mage, exiled, carrying a child who wasn't hers.

Wren knew the exile's name. Everyone in her old circle had known it. Serah Vail, third chair of the College of Wardens, cast down for the crime of asking what happened to the wards' fuel. Wren had sat two benches behind her for six years and never once spoken to her.

"We don't have to go," Cyrus said, already pulling on their pack.

"I know."

"It'll be the north shelf. In winter. With the ice-bridges gone soft since the fire changed."

"I know."

"The child slows them."

"Cyrus." Wren laced her boots. "I spent ten years learning magic in a place where knowing a suffering existed and choosing not to act was considered prudence. I'm not doing it anymore. Get your rope."

They went up through three days of white silence, following the wind-scoured spine of the pass, and found the column huddled in the lee of a fallen watchtower with two of their number already frozen and the rest past fear into that terrible calm. Serah Vail recognized her instantly—of course she did; disgraced mages recognize each other the way soldiers do—and her cracked lips formed Wren's name like an accusation.

"Wren of the Court. Here."

"Warden Halloran," Wren corrected, and heard herself say it, and did not take it back. "Formerly of nothing. We're getting you down the mountain. All of you."

It took four days. Cyrus led the column down routes that existed nowhere on any map anymore—routes that lived only in their memory now, in their blood, in the mountain's strange affection for the last person whose grandmother had loved it enough to sell herself for it. Wren walked the rear and taught the frightened and the frostbitten the small magics that keep bodies alive: warmth pooled in cupped hands, breath timed to steps, the trick of not panicking when the wind screams. And on the last day, descending into the green shadow of the low valleys, the child—the one who wasn't Serah's—fell asleep against Wren's back in the sling, trusting completely, and Wren stood very still in the falling snow and understood that this was what power had actually been for, all along, and that no one in either court had ever told her.

That night, in the camp, Cyrus found her checking the child's fingers for frostbite a fourth unnecessary time.

"You're crying," Cyrus said quietly.

"The snow." Wren dashed her sleeve across her face. "Wind."

"Mm." Cyrus sat down beside her and put an arm around her shoulders and said nothing else, because they had learned that about her too—that she would tell the truth when she was ready, and that the readying went faster when nobody stared at her.

"Serah asked me tonight," Wren said finally, "if the College would take her back. If she groveled. If she brought proof of what's under the mountain." She watched the fire. "And I realized I don't care. Not cruelly. I just—there was a time when that question would have eaten me alive. Would I be restored? Would I matter to them again? And now there's a child breathing on my back and a road that exists because we built it, and the question just... slides off."

"Do you miss it? The court?"

"Sometimes. The libraries. The certainty." Wren leaned into them. "Then I remember the certainty was load-bearing lies, and I come back to the mud and the culverts, and I'm fine."

Cyrus's laugh rumbled through her shoulder. "*Fine,*" they repeated. "High praise, from a warden of Thornfell."

"From a *warden*, yes." She tilted her head to look up at them, firelight and snowlight mixing in her eyes. "Serah asked what to call us. What title to use when she tells the camps help is coming. I told her: wardens of the passes. Two of us. Ask no further."

"Guild of two," Cyrus murmured.

"A guild of two," Wren agreed, "with no ledger of names, no colors, no oath but the roads." She paused. "And one map that won't show anything."

As if summoned—as if the mountain, with its odd humor, had been listening—the flap of Cyrus's pack shifted, and the edge of the Ember Map showed pale in the firelight. Wren reached over and drew it out, half expecting the usual blankness.

The vellum caught the light.

For a moment—one breath, no more—she thought she saw lines stirring beneath the surface. Faint. Ghostly. Roads, perhaps, or the memory of roads, drawn in ink the color of dawn. Then she blinked, and it was blank again, smooth as sleep.

"What?" Cyrus asked, watching her face.

"Nothing," Wren said slowly, folding the map away. "Old habit. Checking the roads."

Outside, the snow fell soft on a mountain that was healing, and somewhere far below, behind glassed stone and rekindled fire, something ancient turned over in its dreams—and for the first time in a year, Wren thought she heard it turning toward the light.
