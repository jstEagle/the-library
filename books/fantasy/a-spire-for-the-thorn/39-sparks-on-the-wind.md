## 39. Sparks on the Wind

One year to the morning.

Cyrus knew it without counting. The body kept its own ledger, and theirs had never learned to lie. They woke before dawn in the small stone house below the caldera's eastern shoulder, in the narrow bed they shared with Wren, and lay listening to the wind come down off Thornfell like a long exhalation—the mountain breathing again, easy and deep, the way it had not breathed for a hundred years before that night.

The hearth was warm. Wren had risen already; he could tell by the smell of pine resin catching, by the kettle set near the coals, by the fact that her boots were gone from where she always left them, crooked, one upright and one on its side, as if she'd stepped out of them mid-thought. She did that when a day mattered. She got up early so as not to be caught unprepared by it.

Cyrus dressed slowly. On the hook by the door hung the old smuggling coat, mended now more times than they could count, salt-stained at the cuffs and scorched across the shoulder where a bone rider's blade had kissed it on the Kestrel Shelf. They put it on. Some days it felt like a costume. Today it felt like skin.

Outside, the world was the color of ash held up to light—grey going to rose along the ridgelines, frost furring the bunchgrass, the air sharp enough to bite the back of the throat. And there was Wren, standing in the dooryard with two tin cups steaming in her hands, looking up.

She didn't say anything. She just tilted her chin toward the peak, and Cyrus followed her gaze, and forgot how to breathe for a moment.

Thornfell stood clear of cloud for the first time in weeks, and along its western flank, thin as wire in the pre-dawn dark, burned a line of gold.

"Early this year," Wren said quietly. "Last year it didn't start until full dark."

"They're eager." Cyrus took the cup she offered. Their voice came out rougher than they expected. "Or the door's worn smoother."

They drank their tea standing up, shoulder to shoulder, watching the line of light thicken. It would take all day. It always did. The river of souls rose out of the deep ways—from the shattered mouth of the spire, from every crack and chimney the unraveling had opened—and wound up the mountain's bones like water climbing against its nature, spark after spark after spark, thousands upon thousands of them, freed the night Cyrus Voss drove a quill through their own name.

Most nights, the river was faint. You had to know where to look, and believe in what you saw. But on the anniversary, when the veil between what was owed and what was paid wore thin, it came up bright enough to read by. The villages on the southern slopes called it the Lantern Night now. They lit candles in their windows and set out bowls of milk and honey on their sills—for the travelers, they said, though no traveler ever touched them. In Saltcinder they floated little paper boats down the harbor channel, each one carrying a name written in charcoal. Names given freely. Names remembered.

Cyrus had gone to Saltcinder for the first anniversary of the battle, three months after, and had stood in the market square where Odo died holding the alley, and had said nothing while the boats went by, because there was nothing in them yet that hurt to see. Now there was. Now Odo's name went by every year in a boat painted with a gull, and Maren's handwriting on it—*for the man who held the door*—and Cyrus had learned that grief was not a debt you paid once. It was a toll road. You walked it as often as it asked.

But today they were not going to Saltcinder. Today they were climbing higher than anyone else dared, up to the caldera rim, because today the river carried someone specific, and Cyrus intended to be waiting.

* * *

The climb took most of the morning. The paths were good now—Wren had seen to that, over the years, warding the scree fields and bridging the bad ravines, working the kind of quiet magic that asked nothing of anyone—but good paths still climbed, and Thornfell had not grown any shorter.

They stopped at noon in the shelter of a leaning boulder and shared bread and hard cheese and a pear each from the tree behind the house that stubbornly refused to die at that altitude. Wren ate with the map rolled in her pack beside her, though the map was blank vellum now and had been for a year, and though they both knew it. She carried it anyway. "Habit," she said, when Cyrus raised an eyebrow. Then, more honestly: "Insurance. If it ever wakes up again, I'd rather know before it does."

"It won't."

"No," she agreed. "Probably not." But she checked the wrapping anyway, the way Cyrus checked the loads on the old chute behind the house, the way smugglers do, forever.

Above the shrine of the pass—they paused there only a moment, laying a spruce sprig on the cold altar where pilgrims had begun leaving tokens for Ashvane—the trail bent upward through country where nothing grew but stone and lichen and a kind of fierce yellow moss that thrived on warmth rising from below. Dragon-warmth. The whole upper mountain was threaded with it now, veins of heat that hadn't existed before Ashvane's rekindling, so that snow fell on the peak and melted into a hundred small springs, and the springs fed the valleys, and the valleys fed the villages, and the whole slow economy of the mountain ran on the heartbeat of a creature who had nearly died keeping strangers safe.

"She'll be there," Wren said, as they came up under the caldera's rim in the long amber light of late afternoon. "She's always there, on this day."

"I know."

"Do you find it strange? That I can feel her moods now, through the flame-work?" Wren flexed her fingers; faint gold traceries moved beneath the skin of her wrists, the marks the heartflame had left on her the night she'd run the spire's throat with it cupped in both hands, burning herself thin and refusing to stop. "My masters would have called it contamination. Unsanctioned resonance."

"Your masters," Cyrus said, "are currently drafting their fourth petition to get a shard of glass from the fallen spire for their reliquary, and Ashvane calls them *the crows*. So perhaps contamination improves things."

Wren laughed—that low, surprised laugh that still felt, after everything, like a thing Cyrus had been given rather than earned—and shouldered her pack, and they climbed the last stretch together, hand finding hand on the broken rock without either of them looking, an old choreography by now.

The caldera opened before them like a bowl of dusk.

And there, coiled in its center on the black glass of her own making, lay Ashvane.

She was larger than Cyrus remembered, though that was impossible. But fire had filled her out; the great ribs no longer showed like shipwreck timbers, the wings lay full and heavy along her flanks, and her scales—dull as old iron on the night they'd found her dying—had taken on a deep ember sheen, banked and patient. Her eyes opened as they came down the scree into the caldera, twin slits of molten copper, and her regard settled over them with the weight of weather.

*The Voss,* she said. Her voice was not sound so much as heat, arriving directly behind the sternum. *And the crow-fingered mage. Come to watch the river go by.*

"We come every year," Cyrus said.

*Yes.* Something moved through the vast frame, a settling, that might have been a shrug or might have been pleasure. *That is why I said it. It is good to say known things aloud. It makes them truer.*

They made camp on a shelf above the caldera floor, close enough for her warmth, far enough to respect the perimeter of heat she breathed out in her sleep—a courtesy she had once explained with a dry remark about the flammability of mortal concerns. Wren built a small fire more from ritual than need. They waited.

The sun went down behind the western crags and the sky went the color of a fresh bruise, then a wound, then a mercy: violet, then ink, then strewn hard with stars. And then, out of the broken throat of the dead spire at the caldera's northern edge, the river began to rise.

It came slowly at first—one spark, then three, then a dozen—drifting up out of the darkness below like embers loosed from some immense buried bonfire. They rose without hurry. They rose the way bubbles rise, inevitable and unhurried, each one a soft gold point of light that pulsed faintly, and if you looked closely—not with your eyes, Wren had taught him, but with the place behind your eyes where names lived—you could almost make out shapes in them. A bend of shoulders here. A tilt of a head there. The river thickened. It became a current, a flood, a slow-motion torrent of the freed dead pouring upward through the night air, curving around the caldera walls in a wide silver-gold spiral, ascending toward the stars in silence.

The villages below, Cyrus knew, would be seeing it too—the glow over the peak, the lights streaming—and would be standing in their doorways with candles lit, murmuring names. But here, at the source, there was only the sound of the wind and the vast slow respiration of a dragon, and the light passing overhead so close that sparks drifted down onto their blanket like warm snow, dissolving into nothing an inch above their skin.

Cyrus sat at the edge of the shelf with their knees drawn up and watched the river, and watched it, and watched it.

Looking for one spark among ten thousand.

*You will know her,* Ashvane had told them, that first anniversary, when Cyrus had confessed—haltingly, ashamed of the smallness of it amid all that vastness—that they didn't know how to find one grandmother in a flood of freed souls. *You carry her roads in your blood. She will recognize the shape of herself coming home.*

And she had been right. Last year, Elira had come up out of the spire's ruin like a struck match, brighter than the others, faster, and had slowed as she passed the shelf where Cyrus sat, and had circled once—once, a full slow orbit, close enough that Cyrus had felt her the way you feel sunlight through glass—and had gone on.

No words. Souls that have finished their debts don't carry many words anymore. Just the circling, and the brightness, and then the long climb into the dark, joining the river of lights going up and up until Cyrus could no longer tell which one had been her.

This year Cyrus waited, hands loose in their lap, heart doing its stubborn work, and tried to think of what they wanted to say, and knew, as they'd known last year, that saying was not really the point.

* * *

The hours turned. Wren slept a little, curled against Cyrus's side with the map-roll for a pillow, and woke, and made more tea over the small fire, and sat with her shoulder against theirs, saying nothing, which was its own language now, fluent after years of practice. Ashvane watched the river with her ancient eyes half-lidded, and once remarked, apropos of nothing: *I knew some of them. When the courts were young. There goes a king of the third dynasty. He owes me an apology.* And a spark drifting past seemed, absurdly, to wobble.

It was past midnight when Cyrus felt it.

Not saw—felt. A change in the temperature of the air, a brightening behind the breastbone, the sensation of a door opening in a house you know every creak of. They sat up straight. Wren woke instantly, hand half-raised, wards gathering gold at her fingertips, then subsiding as she followed Cyrus's gaze.

There.

Rising out of the spire's shattered throat, smaller than Cyrus remembered—or maybe just quicker, surer, a soul that had done this twice now and knew the way—came one spark brighter than all the rest. It did not drift. It climbed with intent, threading the river's current like a fish that knows its water, and it came up the caldera wall, and it came toward the shelf, and Cyrus stood up so fast they nearly knocked the teapot into the fire.

The spark stopped three paces away. It hung in the air at the height of a person's heart, pulsing gently, gold shading toward white at its center, and the warmth coming off it was not dragon-heat, not mountain-heat, but something older and particular: the smell of pipe smoke and tallow candle, the feeling of a kitchen in winter, the memory-touch of a hand smoothing hair back from a forehead.

"Grandmother," Cyrus said.

The spark pulsed. Brightened. Did not come closer, and did not leave.

"I—" Cyrus's prepared words, the ones they'd composed on the climb and rehearsed against the wind, dissolved entirely. What remained was plainer and true. "I'm sorry. For being angry at you. For most of my life, I was. At the guild, at the name, at you—for selling whatever you sold, for leaving me holding a ledger with one line in it and everyone else gone or lying. I didn't know what you'd bought. I didn't know what it cost." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they let it. "I know now. I've walked your roads. All of them. Even the one you hid inside the map for us. Even the Smuggler's Road."

The spark drifted a fraction nearer. Warmth bloomed against Cyrus's cheek like a hand.

"You bought a hundred lives with yours. I understand that arithmetic now. I hate it, and I understand it, and I'm done letting it decide what I am." Cyrus swallowed. "So I wanted to say goodbye properly. Not the way I said it at the vault—shouting at a lantern, bargaining with a guttering flame. Properly. Like people do. Like family does."

Behind them, quietly, Wren began to sing. It was an old passes-song, a road-mender's song, the kind sung over fences between villages to keep travelers company in the dark—a simple tune about walking and weather and reaching home before the lamp burns out. Cyrus didn't look back. But their spine loosened, and their breath came easier, and they found the end of what they needed to say.

"The name Voss is gone. I struck it out myself, and I'd do it again. But not everything in it was a contract. Some of it was just you—teaching Maren to read star-paths, salting the winter stores, the way you always left a coin on the sill for whoever came next even when you couldn't spare it. That part wasn't debt. That part was gift. And gifts don't burn."

They reached out—not touching, never quite touching; the one time a soul-spark had brushed bare skin, Wren had spent three days easing the numbness from their fingers—and cupped their palm in the air beside the light, an open hand offered to a shape of warmth.

"Thank you. For the roads. For the map. For the escape you hid inside the ending." Their voice dropped to almost nothing. "Goodbye, Elira Voss. Go well. Go up."

The spark hovered. Pulsed—twice, slow, deliberate, unmistakable, the way a hand squeezes twice to say *yes, I heard, yes.*

Then it rose.

It rose unhurried, the way all the others did, but Cyrus watched it the whole way, and Wren's song carried it part of the distance, and Ashvane lifted her great head and sent a single thread of flame curling up into the night like a lantern held high for a departing guest. The spark climbed past the rim of the caldera, past the crags, up into the strewn stars, and for one moment it flared—bright, bright, brighter than anything else in the sky for that single breath—

—and then it was simply one light among the lights, indistinguishable, home.

Cyrus sat down slowly. The grief was there, of course. Grief was the toll road; you walked it when it asked. But tonight, walking it, they found the load lighter than expected, and understood, somewhere around the second verse of Wren's song, what had changed. Last year they had watched the spark rise and felt robbed. This year they watched it rise and felt—paid. Paid in full. Both directions. Gratitude where anger used to live, like a spring filling a bed that had run dry.

"She heard you," Wren said softly, when the song ended.

"Twice-pulse," Cyrus agreed. Their eyes were wet and they made no effort otherwise. "That's yes where I come from."

Ashvane lowered her head back onto her forepaws with a rumble like distant rockfall. *A good closing of accounts,* she observed. *I have watched ten thousand years of mortals fail to say goodbye. It is the commonest failure of your kind. You have failed less than most.* A pause, and then, with something dangerously close to draconic amusement: *The mage taught you the twice-pulse. She has been practicing it on me for months. It is very difficult to remain dignified when someone pulses affection at your scales daily.*

Wren had the grace to look only slightly unrepentant.

They slept a little, in the caldera's warmth, under the diminishing river. Near dawn the last sparks rose, fewer and fewer, until the sky held only stars again, and the mountain exhaled its long slow breath, and somewhere far below, in the deep places where a starving court had once kept its ledger, there was silence—which was, Cyrus had learned, what justice sounded like when it was finally finished.

* * *

They came down off the mountain two days later, taking the long switchback trail through the yellow-moss country, and found the world waiting at the bottom of it, as the world insisted on doing.

There was a party at the trailhead.

Not for them—never for them, they'd made sure of that, two anonymous wardens of Thornfell who asked no names and gave none—but for the road itself. Refugees, a dozen families, newly arrived over the southern passes and camped in the shelter of the old customs station, and a knot of villagers from the valley with carts of food, because the wardens had sent word down that a large group was coming through and the valley had learned what the wardens' word was worth. Children everywhere. A goat, for reasons never adequately explained. Someone playing a fiddle badly and with tremendous conviction.

Cyrus and Wren worked the rest of the day without ceremony—checking the refugees' route south, re-marking a flooded ford, arguing amiably with a carter about axle-widths—and when evening came they sat at the edge of the firelight sharing a jug of cider with an old woman who had walked out of the war-torn east with nothing but a granddaughter and a recipe for plum brandy, and who did not ask their names because the wardens of Thornfell asked none, and this was known now, all up and down the passes. It had become, in a strange way, their reputation. The ones with no names. The ones who owed nothing and collected nothing and fixed the roads anyway.

"Strange thing, though," the old woman said, in the way of someone arriving at the actual point of a long circumlocution. "Folk coming through from the far side o' the mountain. East of it. Where nobody goes, on account of the wastes. They're coming through now, see. Since the wards healed. Saying there's green on the other side, first time in living memory. Green and water." She squinted at the fire. "And they're saying—now this is fireside talk, mind, I put no stock in it—they're saying there's *someone* out there. Past the wastes. Someone who knew this was coming. Left markers on the far trails, waymarks, safe-camps, cut and stacked firewood in the shelters. Years' worth of it, some say. Whoever it is, they knew the mountain would open, and they got ready for the folk who'd come through after."

Cyrus went very still.

Beside them, he felt Wren do the same.

"Fireside talk," Cyrus agreed, when they trusted their voice. "Who could such a person be?"

"That's just it." The old woman leaned close, firelight deepening the canyons of her face, and her eyes—sharp, miss nothing—moved from Cyrus to Wren and back. "They leave the same mark at every site. Burned into the wood, or scratched in stone, always the same. My granddaughter drew it for me in the ashes, and I've not forgotten it, though I've no notion what it means."

She reached out and drew it with one stick-thin finger in the packed earth between their feet: a circle, crossed by a single diagonal line.

An old sign. A guild sign. The mark the Voss smugglers had chalked on safehouse doors for four generations, meaning *provisioned — friend ahead — walk on.*

A mark no living person should have known. A mark Cyrus had last seen chalked inside the border of a map, in a dead woman's handwriting, at the edge of the hidden road.

The fiddle played on. The children shouted. The goat ate someone's hat. And Cyrus Voss and Wren looked at the mark in the dust, and then at each other, and neither of them said a word, because there was a road east of the mountain now, green and water and waiting, and somebody out there had been preparing it for years—

—and every road Cyrus had ever walked had taught them one thing above all others:

No one prepares a road unless they intend, someday, to walk it back.
