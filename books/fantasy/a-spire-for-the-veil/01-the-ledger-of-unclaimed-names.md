## 1. The Ledger of Unclaimed Names

The trouble with keeping ledgers for people who officially never existed, Quentin Nystrom had learned over nineteen years, was that they never stopped wanting to be written down. Living people, dead people, people who had been carefully unwritten from every census and parish roll in the country—they all came through the workshop door eventually, asking him to make them real again, if only in ink.

"Ink's cheap," he'd tell them. "Being remembered is the expensive part."

He said it now, absently, to no one at all, because the workshop was full and warm and loud in the particular way that meant everyone was pretending not to eavesdrop on everyone else.

Morning light came through the high windows of the Spirework—the guild called it that because the building leaned, very slightly, like a spire that had heard about spires secondhand—and fell across a dozen workbenches where the bone-carvers plied their trade. Fine dust hung in the light like it had nowhere better to be. Somewhere near the kilns, Maret was arguing with her own left hand, which happened when she'd been carving too long; the hand wanted to keep going and the rest of Maret wanted breakfast.

"Quentin." Maret didn't look up from the rib bone she was incising—a love token, by the look of the filigree, though who paid for love tokens carved into human ribs was a question Quentin had learned not to ask before noon. "Your apprentice is in the counting room eating your biscuits."

"She's *restocking* my biscuits," Quentin said.

"She's eaten four."

"That's inventory management."

Sefa, from the doorway of the counting room, raised the offending biscuit in salute. She was sixteen, sharp-elbowed, with ink on her fingers in six colors because she hadn't yet learned which inks were for which ledgers and had decided, in true Veil fashion, that this was nobody's official business. She had been with the guild two years—plucked off a street where, officially, she had never stood, in a city ward that appeared on no map—and Quentin had never once regretted teaching her figures. He occasionally regretted teaching her confidence.

"Master Nystrom," she said, "the Ashwell account doesn't balance."

"The Ashwell account never balances. The Ashwells are memory-brokers. Their whole trade is things people paid to forget. Of course it doesn't balance."

"You say that every month."

"And every month it remains true. Consistency, Sefa, is the soul of bookkeeping."

She grinned and disappeared back into the counting room, and Quentin allowed himself the small, private pleasure of watching his world run exactly as it should. The workshop hummed. Hesper at the far bench was fitting a knucklebone into a brass setting for a client who wanted to hear her mother's laugh one more time. Down in the memory-broker's alcove, old Tam was conducting a transaction in the low murmuring voice he used for prices—half auctioneer, half priest. The kettle on the stove had just begun its opinion.

This was the Veil: forty-odd artisans in a leaning building in a street that wasn't there, trading in impossible things for clients whose names they took great care never to learn twice. Bone-carvers who could set a dead voice singing in carved relic. Memory-brokers who bought your worst afternoon and sold you someone else's wedding. And Quentin, ledger-keeper, who made sure that all these transactions—every name, every debt, every small resurrection—were recorded in books that existed in exactly one copy each and would burn, per guild law, rather than be seized.

Officially, none of it existed. That was the point. That was the *protection*. In a country where visible things were conscripted, consumed, or killed, the Veil had survived three hundred years by the simple discipline of being nowhere on paper. Quentin's ledgers were the single exception to erasure, kept because someone had to remember what was owed to whom, and hidden because remembering was the most dangerous work of all.

He settled at his desk with the morning's entries and dipped his pen.

*Hesper, one setting, brass, commissioned, paid in advance against the Ferrow estate.*
*Tam, purchase of one memory (drowning, shallow), resale pending.*
*Maret, materials, two ribs, ethically sourced, do not ask.*

Routine. Warmth. The soft scrape of gravers on bone, the smell of beeswax and lamp oil. He was halfway through the third entry when he noticed the sound.

At first he took it for the kettle. Then for Tam, muttering prices. But it was coming from his own desk—from the tray of unclaimed relics that sat beside the ink pots, waiting for owners who had died before collecting.

Quentin knew the voices of carved relics the way a choir master knows his singers. Every piece the Veil made carried something: an echo, an impression, a residue of the hand that carved it or the life it commemorated. Usually they were quiet unless held. Usually they whispered only to the person they were made for.

The knucklebone in the corner of the tray—the small, yellowed one, carved with a spiral so fine it looked like a fingerprint—was whispering to the whole room.

No one else seemed to hear it. The gravers kept scraping. Maret's hand kept arguing with Maret. But Quentin heard it plainly, the way you hear your own name across a noisy market, and what it said chilled him down to the ledger-keeping part of his soul, which is to say, all the way down.

It was praying.

*"Keep us. Keep us. Old one under the hill, old one under the river, we were promised back. We were promised. Answer, answer—"*

A child's prayer. A begging prayer, the kind street children still mumbled into cupped hands even though every priest in the country would tell them, kindly or cruelly, that nothing was listening. The gods had gone silent centuries ago—that was history, that was fact, that was the foundation everything else stood on. The old gods did not answer. They had not answered since before the Veil was founded. Whole guilds had risen and fallen in the silence.

The prayer stopped. And then, in the little spiral-carved knucklebone, something answered.

Quentin did not have a word for what it felt like to hear that answer, except that it was like standing in a dark house and feeling, rather than seeing, someone very large turn toward you. Not words. Attention. The bone grew warm against the tray's felt lining—warm enough that Quentin saw, distinctly, the faint curl of vapor rising from it, as if it had been held in a closed fist.

"*Answered*," the child's whisper said, wondering and afraid. "*You answered.*"

And beneath it, vast and slow, like a bell rung underwater:

"*Seen.*"

Quentin put down his pen with great care, because his hand was not steady, and looked around the workshop. Nobody had heard. Nobody had felt the temperature of the room change, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere in the building—no wind, just pressure. Sefa's laughter drifted out of the counting room over some joke with Tam. Hespér fitted her brass setting. The world went on being warm and ordinary and officially nonexistent.

Only the bone was different. Only the bone lay in its tray radiating a heat that had no source, whispering now in two voices—one small and grateful, one enormous and *interested*.

He picked it up. It should have been cold; relics always were until held, and even then they held only the warmth of your own hand returned to you. This one was hot as a stone left by a fire, and through the contact Quentin felt the attention swing onto him—not hostile, not kind, merely *noticing*, the way a cart horse notices a fly, the way a king notices a new coin in his treasury.

Gods collect what they can see, said a thought in his head that was not quite his own, arriving with the flavor of old ivory and river silt. He couldn't have said where it came from. He couldn't have said it wasn't his.

"Quentin?" Sefa stood in the counting room door, biscuit forgotten, watching his face. "You've gone gray as unbleached ledger paper. What's wrong?"

He looked at the knucklebone in his palm. The spiral carving seemed, in this light, less like decoration and more like a record—a mark someone had made trying to write down something too large for any page he owned.

"A relic's talking," he said. "And not to me."

"To who, then?"

Quentin hesitated. Nineteen years of discretion warred with nineteen years of honest accounting, and honest accounting won, as it always did, at a cost.

"To something that answers," he said. "Sefa—fetch Guildmaster Orla. Tell her the old gods are listening again."

He set the bone down on the ledger page, and where it rested, the paper began very faintly, patiently, to scorch.
