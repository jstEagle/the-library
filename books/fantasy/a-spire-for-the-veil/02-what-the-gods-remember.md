## 2. What the Gods Remember

The morning the god answered the child, Sefa was carrying a tray of tea through the Veil's workshop and thinking about nothing more dangerous than whether Master Ivo had put too much clove in it again.

She nearly dropped the whole tray when the shouting started.

Not angry shouting—celebration, spilling up from the street below in waves, punctuated by bells that nobody in Ashfall had rung in living memory. The temple bells. The ones everyone said were decorative. Sefa set the tray down on Ivo's workbench, between a half-carved femur and a jar of teeth, and leaned out the window.

The street was full of people. Not the usual market crowd, either—people were standing still, which in Ashfall meant something extraordinary had happened, because standing still got you noticed, and getting noticed was how you ended up conscripted into one labor levy or another.

At the center of it all, on the steps of the old dry fountain, stood a girl. Eight years old, maybe nine, barefoot, with the particular ferocity of a child who has been hungry long enough to stop being polite about it. She was pointing at the fountain with one hand. Water was coming out of it. Clean water, silver-bright in the morning sun, rising out of stone that had been dry since before the war, before the treaties, before the country decided to pretend its own gods had packed up and left.

"She prayed," someone in the crowd was saying, over and over, like a man learning a new word. "She knelt down and she *prayed*, and it answered."

Sefa's stomach did something complicated.

In the three years since Guildmaster Orla had fished her out of a gutter and told her, "You have steady hands and no history, which makes you perfect for us," Sefa had learned exactly one rule above all others: attention is a debt collector. It always comes eventually, and it never accepts payment in kind.

A god answering prayers publicly was not a miracle. It was an invoice arriving early.

She found Quentin already at the big ledger table, and he was not calm, which frightened her more than anything else, because Quentin was the calmest person she knew. He balanced accounts the way other men breathed. She had once watched him discover a discrepancy of two coppers in a ledger six months old and not sleep until he'd found where they'd gone—and when he found them, tucked in the wrong column by a dead carver whose hands had trembled at the end, he'd wept a little, quietly, and entered a correction with a footnote about dignity.

Now his finger was pressed flat against the ledger page as if holding down a bird that might fly off with their names.

"Say it back to me," he said without looking up.

"A god answered a prayer. In public. In daylight." Sefa pulled her apprentice stool around and sat. "In front of at least two hundred people."

"Which prayer?"

"How should I know? Water, presumably. She was pointing at the fountain."

Quentin finally looked up, and his eyes were doing the thing where he read you like a column of figures. "It matters. If it was water, it's a small god, or a bored one, or a merciful one testing the door. If it was justice—" He stopped himself, jaw tight. "If it was justice, then every prayer anyone has ever muttered under their breath just became audible."

"That's why we run." Sefa kept her voice level. She'd been turning the thought over all morning while the city celebrated, and now she laid it out the way she'd seen him lay out accounts: plainly, itemized, no sentiment. "Today it's a fountain. Tomorrow some god decides to look at the whole city properly, and what does it find? Us. We don't exist, Quentin. That's not poetry, that's bookkeeping. There is no record of any of us. Which means when a god goes looking through the world for things to see—" she tapped the ledger, "—we are the blank pages. Blank pages get filled in. By whoever gets there first."

"You want to leave the workshop."

"I want us to leave before we're *made* to leave. There's a difference."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was the expression he wore when she'd made a mathematically correct argument he didn't like. "And go where? Every road out of this country is watched by people who keep very good records of who passes. We'd be trading invisible for legible. At least here we know the walls."

"The walls won't help if something looks *through* them."

"Sefa." Quentin closed the ledger gently, the way he did everything, even now, even with bells ringing outside. "Do you know why Orla built the Veil? Not why the guild works—why it exists. Because after the war, the people who survived were the ones nobody wrote down. The burned, the landless, the renamed. My grandmother spent forty years being officially dead. She carved bone under a false name in three different cities, and every time a clerk came asking questions, she simply wasn't there. Erasure kept her alive. You're proposing we give that up because of one wet fountain."

"One wet fountain and every bell in Ashfall ringing at once."

"Bells rust," said Quentin. "Gods forget. It's practically their defining feature—that's why the ledgers matter. Somebody has to remember what heaven won't."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, and something in her chest cracked, quiet as a hairline fracture in porcelain. Because he believed it. That was the terrible thing—he believed that staying, keeping the books, tending the guild's warm cluttered life, was loyalty. And maybe it was. But she had been invisible once, truly invisible, gutter-invisible, and she knew the difference between being unseen by choice and being unseen because no one bothered to look.

"We're not your grandmother," she said softly. "We're the grandchildren. And grandchildren get to be scared in ways grandparents can't afford."

Before he could answer, the workshop door banged open and Master Ivo came through it at a speed Sefa had never previously witnessed from a man his age, still wearing his carving apron, a bone dust ghosting his sleeves.

"It's not stopping," Ivo announced breathlessly. "The fountain. The water keeps coming. And there's a priest—well, a woman dressed as a priest, though there haven't been priests in—anyway, she's writing everything down. Names. She's asking the crowd for names. Who saw it, who heard the prayer, who the child belongs to." He caught his breath. "Attention, lad. Attention is a debt collector."

"That's my line," Sefa said.

"Great minds," Ivo wheezed, dropping onto a bench. "Guildmaster wants everyone upstairs within the hour. Full assembly. She's had the shutters painted."

"The shutters are already shuttered," Sefa said.

"They're being painted *again*," Ivo said grimly. "With the pattern that doesn't hold the eye. Whatever's looking at this city, Orla means for our windows to bore it."

* * *

The assembly was held in the long room above the workshop, the one where the ceiling beams were carved with the faces of every member the Veil had ever lost—not names, never names, just faces, so that grief could be recorded without becoming evidence. Forty-some artisans sat crammed among memory-brokers' shelves and bone-carvers' lathes while Orla stood at the front with her arms folded and her gray braid pinned up like a woman preparing for weather.

Sefa had expected panic. What she got was worse: order. Orla spoke calmly about doubling the erasure rotations, about moving the most visible members to safehouses, about the new priest-woman going door to door with her list. Calm, procedural, survivable. And underneath it, Sefa realized with a cold slide of understanding, the entire room was agreeing to do the thing they had always done—shrink, hush, become furniture again—and calling it strength.

When Orla finished and opened the floor, Quentin stood.

"With respect, Guildmaster—the plan is sound but incomplete. The child at the fountain gave her name to the priest. I checked. A neighbor confirmed it. The god knows her name now, and through her, the street, and through the street, this district." He paused. "Someone should watch the child. Someone should learn what the god wants with her. Running blind helps no one."

"And running *sighted*?" Sefa was on her feet before she'd decided to stand. Her voice came out sharper than intended and she didn't soften it. "You want to assign someone to stand next to the only person in Ashfall a god is currently *looking at*. Quentin, that's not watching, that's volunteering."

"Knowing what it wants is survival."

"Knowing what it wants is how you end up *in its ledger*!" Her own voice cracked and she hated it. Around them, the guild had gone very quiet, the way rooms go quiet when a private argument stops being private. "Every one of us exists because somebody refused to be written down. And you're proposing we write ourselves into a god's book on purpose. That's not loyalty, that's—"

"Loyalty is precisely what it is," Quentin said, and his voice stayed gentle, which was somehow the worst part. "These people fed you when you were nine years old. They taught your hands everything they know. I'm not asking anyone to die for a ledger. I'm asking us not to abandon the first name on the god's list just because she's small and poor and convenient to leave behind. That child is where we were, Sefa. All of us. Every last erased one of us started as someone the world declined to notice."

The silence afterward had weight. Orla's eyes moved between them, unreadable, and Sefa felt the crack in her chest widen—not anger, exactly. Something sadder. The first clear sight of the fault line running straight through the middle of the only family she had: Quentin, who would stay and keep accounts until the sky fell; herself, who would rather burn every page than let the pages decide when she died.

Orla raised one hand, and the room settled.

"The child will be watched," she said. "By rotation, briefly, and at distance. No one speaks to the priest. No one writes anything down near the district—we carry nothing legible until this settles." Her gaze rested on Quentin a moment longer than necessary, and Sefa would spend many nights afterward wondering what passed between them in that look. "Meeting's done."

* * *

That evening, Sefa sat on the workshop roof with her knees drawn up, watching the fountain-glow flicker gold against the clouds across the rooftops. People were still gathered there. Praying, probably. Queuing, certainly—Ashfall folk knew a queue when they saw one, even for miracles.

Quentin climbed up beside her with two cups of tea, handed her one, and said nothing for a while, which was his way of apologizing without spending words she might need later.

"I'm not leaving," she said at last. "I want that understood. I argued for running because someone had to say it out loud. But I'm not leaving you, or Ivo, or the old bats downstairs. I'm just—" she searched for it, "—keeping a second ledger. In my head. Of exits."

Quentin snorted into his tea. "Of course you are. You'd balance accounts against the apocalypse."

"Somebody has to remember what hope won't," she said, echoing him, and he laughed properly then, tired and real, and knocked his cup gently against hers.

Below them, across the city, the fountain sang its silver song, and somewhere in the dark beyond the rooftops, Sefa could not shake the feeling that something vast and patient had opened a very old eye—and that it was reading.

What she could not have known—what none of them could—was that the reading had already begun much closer than the fountain. That night, in the workshop below her feet, a small carved knucklebone in Quentin's ledger drawer began, very softly, to whisper.
