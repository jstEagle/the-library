## 3. Bone Whispers

The knucklebone had been whispering for three days, and Quentin Nystrom was losing patience with it.

He kept it in the third drawer of his workbench, wrapped in wool, the way one keeps a wasp in a jar—contained, observed, and not to be trusted near the jam. It was a small thing, a child's finger-bone by the size of it, carved with a pattern of interlocking spirals so fine they blurred under lamplight. Some long-dead artisan had loved this piece. You could always tell. The dead left their fingerprints in the work, and the bone remembered them the way cloth remembers shoulders.

"Third drawer," Quentin told it firmly, closing the wood over its murmuring. "We've discussed this."

The drawer whispered anyway. It whispered through the wood, through the wool, through the general dignity of the workshop below the chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, where the Veil did its invisible business among vats of bone-glue and racks of half-finished reliquaries. It whispered through Quentin's supper, which was herring, and through his attempts to balance the week's ledger, which was going badly even before the dead got involved.

The problem was this: relics did not whisper. They *spoke*, if you knew how to listen—Quentin had spent thirty years learning how, and his ears had gone strange and patient in the process—but they spoke in the voices of their makers, and makers said maker-things. *The chisel slips here. She never knew I loved her. Mind the grain.* A finished piece was a finished sentence. It did not go on and on in the dark of a drawer like a man muttering through a keyhole.

Unless, of course, someone had answered it.

Quentin put down his pen. He looked at the drawer. The drawer looked back, in the way of drawers, which is to say it did nothing at all and made him feel foolish for expecting otherwise.

"All right," he said. "But if you wake Sefa, I'm carving you into a button."

He took the bone out, unwrapped it, and held it to his ear the way a physician holds a watch, and this time he listened properly, with the whole of his strange patience, and what he heard made the hair on his forearms stand up and salute.

It was a prayer. A child's prayer, decades old, worn smooth as a river stone by repetition: *keep me, keep me, keep me.* And beneath it, threaded through it like a second voice singing a round, something vast had answered. Something with the texture of deep water and the attention of a lighthouse. Something that had said, in a voice Quentin's bones understood before his mind did:

*Seen.*

He wrapped the bone again, very carefully, and sat with his hands flat on the desk for a long moment.

The gods had been gone from this country for four hundred years. Everyone agreed on this. The priests agreed, the historians agreed, the tax collectors agreed most enthusiastically of all, since divine attention had historically been very bad for commerce. The gods had withdrawn, the country had erased itself from their sight, and the Veil had been founded in that great blank space—a guild of artisans who traded in impossible things precisely *because* no god was watching to object.

But the bone said *seen.* The bone said it in a child's voice and a god's voice braided together, and it had been saying it for three days, and Quentin was beginning to suspect that the blank space on the divine map was not blank at all. It was merely unattended. And unattended things, like unattended ovens and unattended children, had a way of demanding attention eventually.

He put the bone in his pocket instead of the drawer, because a man should know where the wasp is, and went to bed, where he did not sleep so much as lie down adjacent to it.

* * *

The bone led him north.

It did not lead him dramatically—relics rarely did. It warmed, very slightly, when he walked in one direction and cooled in another, like a cat deciding whether you were worth following. Quentin followed it through the morning markets, past the fishmonger who owed the Veil three favors and the midwife who owed eleven, out through the Tanners' Gate, and up into the hills where the city kept its dead in terraces like vineyards.

The Bonefields, the locals called them, without any of the reverence the name deserved. The city's oldest dead lay here, from before the erasure, from the years when the gods still watched and the country had still been on maps. The graves were marked with carved stones, and every stone whispered as Quentin passed, a low tide-murmur of maker-voices: *mind the grain, mind the grain, she never knew.*

The bone warmed hottest at the very top of the hill, where the terraces ended and an older, stranger cemetery began—one that predated the city's records, which was saying something, because the city's records went back to a fire that had destroyed everything before them, which Quentin had always privately considered a suspiciously convenient fire.

There was a single marker there. It was not a stone. It was a ledger.

Someone had carved an entire ledger-book out of grave-grey rock, pages and all, each page fanned open, and the whole impossible sculpture stood taller than Quentin and said, in a voice like a landslide deciding to be polite:

"You're late."

Quentin did not jump. He was proud of that. His knees jumped for him, but he himself remained dignified.

"Madam," he said, "I wasn't aware we had an appointment."

"Four hundred years," said the ledger. "Give or take. I've been waiting four hundred years for a keeper with ears good enough to hear me, and you spent three of your days dithering over a herring supper."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the dead spoke, but it had a shape to it—dry, sharp, amused, the voice of someone who had been dead long enough to find death funny. And Quentin knew that voice. Every keeper of the Veil's ledgers knew it, because every ledger in the workshop was kept in the founder's hand, and her hand had a personality: precise, impatient, with a downward slash on the descenders like a woman stabbing time itself.

"You're the Founder," he said.

"I'm Maren Vael," said the ledger, "when I had a name. Names are currency, boy. Spend yours carefully."

"I—" Quentin's mouth, which could talk its way past customs officers and tax assessors and one memorable dragon-shaped misunderstanding, went briefly blank. "The ledgers say you died in the Year of Ash."

"The ledgers say a great many things. I wrote most of them." The stone pages rustled, though there was no wind. "You've been reading the wrong pages, Quentin Nystrom. You've been reading the ones I let the guild find. There are pages in that workshop that only open for a keeper who's heard a god answer. And you have. I felt it through your pocket. Tickles, rather like a sneeze."

"The bone," Quentin said. "The child's prayer. Something answered it."

"Something old. Something waking. The country erased itself from their sight four centuries ago, and gods, boy, are not patient creatures. They are beginning to look for us. And here is the thing your Guildmaster will not tell you, because Orla is a good woman and good women hoard bad news like a miser hoards a coin she cannot bear to look at." The pages turned, all of them at once, a sound like a thousand small wings. "The ledgers contain a door."

Quentin's heart did something unprofessional. "A door."

"Out. Out of the country, out of the erasure, out of the sight of anything with the attention to look. The Veil was never only a guild, Nystrom. It was an *exit*, dressed as a guild, because no one watches a tradesman's ledger the way they watch a temple. Every name we've ever erased, every record we've ever unwrote—it was all practice. Four hundred years of practice at making one specific thing invisible." The founder's voice dropped, and the hillside seemed to lean in to listen. "A door out of a country that officially does not exist."

The temptation arrived in Quentin's chest like warmth from a hearth, and he hated how quickly it arrived, how *at home* it made itself. He thought of Sefa, seventeen and sharp-tongued, who had argued three nights ago that they should run before the gods finished waking. He thought of Orla, whose hands shook now when she thought no one watched. He thought of the workshop smelling of bone-glue and herring, of the found, unlovely family of it.

And then he thought of everyone the door would not hold. The fishmonger. The midwife. The street children the Veil fed through the chandler's back door. The city itself, four hundred thousand people, erased and invisible and *safe*, or so they had believed, and now the gods were turning over in their long sleep and reaching for them.

"Everyone," Quentin said. "The door takes everyone?"

The ledger was quiet for a moment. Dead things, Quentin had learned, were quietest before their worst truths.

"The door takes who fits through it," Maren Vael said. "It is a door, boy, not a miracle. It will open for the guild. For the guild and what the guild can carry. It will not open for a city."

"Then it's not an exit. It's a lifeboat, and I'm meant to decide who drowns."

"I'm meant to decide nothing. I'm a rock on a hill. You're the keeper." The stone pages turned again, gentler now. "I built the Veil to save what could be saved. I erased my own name doing it—gave it to the gods as a down payment, you might say, on the door's construction. They have been holding it in escrow for four hundred years. They collect what they can see, Nystrom. That is the whole of divine law, the first and only commandment. What is seen is owned. What is owned is consumed. Your country understood this, once, and made itself invisible, and called the making a victory."

"And now they can see again."

"Now they are learning to see again. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a door locked and a door being picked." The founder's voice went very dry. "You have perhaps a season. Less, if the child's prayer spreads. Prayers are like rats, boy. Where you find one, there are forty you haven't."

Quentin looked out over the city below, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, the chandler's shop a smudge among smudges, the workshop invisible inside it the way a heart is invisible inside a chest. He thought: *I could carry the ledgers out. I could carry Sefa and Orla and the bone-carvers and the memory-brokers, and we could go through, and we could be safe, and every night for the rest of my life I would hear the city behind us.*

He thought: *Orla will never agree. Orla would burn the ledgers first.*

He thought: *Orla knows something. Orla has known something for years, and her hands shake, and she has been feeding the ledgers false pages, and I have been too loyal or too cowardly to ask which.*

"Keeper," said Maren Vael, and there was something almost kind in the landslide of her voice. "You're doing the arithmetic. I can hear it. Dead men hear arithmetic the way dogs hear whistles."

"Then hear this," Quentin said. "I'm not choosing yet. I'm going to ask Orla why her ledgers don't balance."

The ledger laughed. It was a terrible sound, stone grinding stone, and it went on rather longer than politeness required.

"Oh, you *are* my successor," said the founder of the Veil. "Go home, Quentin Nystrom. Read the pages that only open for you. And when Orla lies to you—and she will, and it will be a good lie, the best lie, the lie of a woman who loves you—remember that the gods are waking, and that they collect what they can see, and ask yourself what *else* she's been keeping visible to keep the rest of you hidden."

The bone in Quentin's pocket went cold as riverbed stone.

He walked down through the terraces of the dead, past the whispering graves, through the Tanners' Gate, into the city that officially did not exist, and every step of the way he did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic did not balance, and somewhere behind him on the hill a stone ledger turned its pages in the dark, waiting to see what its keeper would do.
