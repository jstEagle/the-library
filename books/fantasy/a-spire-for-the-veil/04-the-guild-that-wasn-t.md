## 4. The Guild That Wasn't

Orla Vess had been Guildmaster of the Veil for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about leadership: never let them see you count the money, always let them think the tea was your idea, and keep at least one secret so heavy that carrying it keeps you honest.

She was carrying four.

The heaviest lived in a locked drawer beneath her workbench, wrapped in oilcloth that had once shrouded a dead man's hands. She thought about it every morning while she ground her coffee, and every night while she pretended to sleep, and several times in between when someone in the workshop laughed too loudly and she flinched like a woman expecting a bill.

Tonight the workshop smelled of beeswax and bone dust and Sefa's disastrous attempt at spiced buns, which were still cooling on the windowsill and still, against all reason and chemistry, smoking faintly. Orla had eaten one anyway. Loyalty had a flavor, and it was mostly charcoal.

"Guildmaster." Quentin stood in her doorway with the particular stiffness of a man who has rehearsed an argument on the stairs. He held a ledger against his chest like a shield. Behind him, the founder's map — or whatever it truly was — pressed its weight through the leather binding. Orla could feel it from across the room, the way you can feel weather coming in a bad knee.

"Quentin." She gestured to the chair across from her desk. "Sit. Have a bun. They're terrible."

"I know what they are," he said, not sitting. "Sefa told me they're terrible. She said it proudly."

"That child has no fear of failure. It's going to make her either a great artist or a public menace." Orla poured two cups of tea anyway, because some rituals held a room together better than any argument could. "You've been to the Hollow Marches' edge. I can see it on you. You walk like a man who's been listening to something I told everyone to stop listening to."

Quentin set the ledger on her desk. Opened it. Turned it toward her.

There, in his careful hand — the hand that kept the guild's true accounts, the names of people who had never officially drawn breath — was a copy of the pattern he'd found carved into the old knucklebone. And beside it, in her own handwriting, dated eleven years ago, the same pattern.

He'd been in her drawer. Or he'd remembered. With Quentin it was impossible to say which was worse; the man's memory was a vault with opinions.

"You knew," he said quietly. "Before I did. You've known about the door for eleven years."

Orla looked at the tea. Looked at the bun. Considered, briefly, the strategic value of pretending to go deaf, an affliction she had successfully performed twice before during tax assessments.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

The trouble with secrets, Orla reflected as Quentin's voice climbed, is that they are never alone. One secret invites others in like stray cats. Feed one and by winter you have six, all yowling at your window at inconvenient hours.

"—eleven years, Orla. Eleven. We lost Marla to the conscription carts. We lost the Fenwick twins. We lost—" His voice cracked on the last name, and he hated that it did, and she loved him for hating it. "We lost people. And you sat on a door out."

"I didn't sit on it." Orla set down her cup with great care, because if she didn't give her hands a job they would shake, and Guildmasters do not shake. "I fed it."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," she said, "that a door is only useful if the things hunting you don't know where it is. And gods, Quentin, are not stupid. They're worse than stupid. They're attentive."

She rose, went to the locked drawer, and brought out the oilcloth bundle. Unwrapped it on the desk between them like a surgeon laying out instruments.

Inside lay a row of small carved tokens. Bone, each one. Each one bearing a name.

Not the guild's names. Not quite.

"These are prayers," she said. "Little ones. Carved in the old style, the way the founder taught — the way you taught yourself, before I ever showed you a single knot of it. A prayer made solid enough to be heard. I carve one every month. I leave it somewhere the old gods will find it. A shrine. A well. Once, memorably, a magistrate's hat."

Quentin stared at the tokens. "You've been praying to them."

"I've been feeding them." She tapped the first token. "A widow in the Salt Quarter, praying for rain. This one, a boy who wanted his dog back. This one—" her finger paused, and her voice went carefully flat, "—a man named Aldous Rennick, who prayed to be seen."

"Aldous Rennick doesn't exist. You invented him."

"He exists now," Orla said. "Thoroughly. The god of thresholds found him very interesting. Spent the better part of two years looking for him. Two years, Quentin, in which that god's attention was on a name I made up out of a dead man's knucklebone and my own bad conscience, instead of on the four hundred living people under this roof."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Sefa laughed at something, bright and unguarded, and both of them flinched.

"You picked names," Quentin said slowly. "Out of nothing. And gave them to gods."

"I picked names out of *nothing*, yes. That was the mercy." Orla finally sat, because her knees had opinions of their own these days. "At first. But attention is a hunger, and hunger grows teeth. Three years ago the tokens stopped being enough. The gods started wanting specificity. So I gave them specificity. Small truths. Real streets. Real griefs, borrowed from our own dead — people already gone, already beyond the reach of conscription or cart or knife. I figured the dead had already paid. Let their names buy a little more time for the living."

"And the founder?" Quentin's voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when he handled a relic that was still warm. "Did she know? Is this her design?"

"The founder," Orla said, "gave the gods her own name four hundred years ago, and I did not learn that until after I'd started, and when I understood what she'd done — really understood — I sat in this chair until dawn and then I got up and carved another token, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping would kill us faster than any god." She met his eyes. "I am not proud of it. I am not asking you to be proud of me. I'm asking you to understand that everything I have done for eleven years, I did so that you would never have to know I was doing it."

"That's the problem," Quentin said. "Don't you see? That's exactly the problem. You decided we were safer ignorant. You decided *for* us. That's not protection, Orla. That's just another kind of erasure. We're the guild that erases itself — but you erased *us* from our own story."

Orla opened her mouth. Closed it. Nineteen years of leadership, and the boy had found the one argument she had no counter for, probably because it was true.

"Well," she said at last. "That's the trouble with keeping secrets. You get so good at hiding things you hide the point."

* * *

They argued for another hour, but it was the good kind of arguing, the kind where two people are actually walking toward the same door from opposite ends of the room. By the end of it, the tea was cold, the buns were gone (Quentin had eaten two, which Orla chose to interpret as diplomacy), and the oilcloth bundle lay open between them like a confession neither of them needed anymore.

"So the map works," Quentin said. "The door is real. You've simply never used it."

"Using it means leaving. Leaving means the Veil ends here, in this country, as a footnote nobody wrote." Orla rubbed her eyes. "And there are four hundred people in this building, and maybe half of them would survive the Marches, and fewer than that would fit through whatever toll that door charges. I did the arithmetic years ago, Quentin. It's the one sum I've never been able to balance."

"Then we change the sum."

"With what?"

Quentin looked down at the ledger — his ledger, the true one, the one with every unclaimed name in careful rows — and something moved behind his eyes that made Orla's stomach drop, because she recognized it. It was the same look she saw in the mirror eleven years ago, the look of a person about to do arithmetic with themselves as the currency.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But the dead do. And lately, they've been very talkative."

"Quentin—"

"Eleven years, Orla." He said it without heat now, which was somehow worse. "You carried it alone. Nobody should carry it alone. That's the whole point of a guild — that's the whole point of *us*. So the next secret anyone finds in this building, they bring to the table. All of us. Even the ugly ones." He almost smiled. "Especially the ugly ones. Sefa will want the ugly ones. She'll probably frame them."

Despite herself, despite everything, Orla laughed — a short, cracked thing, but real. "She'll want to carve them into buns."

"Gods preserve us."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. Not the alarm. The third bell — the one that meant someone, somewhere in the city, had prayed aloud in public and been answered.

Orla and Quentin looked at each other over the open bundle of carved names, and the warmth drained out of the room like water through a cracked cup.

"They're getting closer," Quentin said.

"They were always going to get closer." Orla began wrapping the tokens back in their shroud-cloth, her fingers steady now, steadier than they'd been all night. Decision, at least, was a kind of rest. "The question was only ever whether we'd be ready."

"And are we?"

Orla tied the knot, and thought of Sefa's terrible buns, and Marla's laugh, and the four hundred souls asleep above and around them, and the door in the ledgers waiting like a held breath.

"No," she said. "But we're awake. Fetch the apprentice. If the gods want to start collecting, they can bloody well wait until we've finished the accounts."

Above them, the bell rang on, and on, and did not stop.
