## 5. A Country Erased

The map in the ledgers did not look like a map. It looked like a mistake—a smear of ink where a cartographer had fallen asleep, or perhaps wept. Quentin had spent three nights holding it up to candlelight before he found the trick: the roads were drawn in the spaces *between* the ink, the way the Veil itself lived in the spaces between records.

"Officially," Quentin said, tapping the blank western quarter of the page, "this is nothing."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it's the Hollow Marches, and it's where we're going."

Sefa had argued, of course. Sefa always argued; it was how she loved people. But the argument had ended the morning a god's attention slid across the workshop like a searchlight through gauze, and every carved relic on the shelves had sung at once—high and terrified, like wine glasses feeling a note they couldn't bear.

So now there was a mule.

Her name was Turnip, which Sefa had chosen because naming things was a form of protection, and because Quentin had refused to name her at all. "If you name it, you're attached," he'd said. "If you're attached, you grieve." To which Sefa had replied that by that logic he should also avoid naming his own hands, and he'd gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet when she'd won but he wasn't ready to say so.

They walked west out of the city at dawn, when the fog was thick enough to make even visible things invisible, and Sefa pretended not to notice that Quentin kept checking over his shoulder for gods the way other travelers checked for bandits.

* * *

By the third day, the world began to forget them.

Not all at once. The milestones came first—they stopped having numbers, then stopped having words, then stopped being milestones and became just stones, squatting in the grass with an air of having resigned from their jobs. The road thinned to a track, the track to a suggestion. The villages they passed had no names on any map Sefa had ever seen, and their inhabitants watched the two travelers with the flat, patient stare of people who had long ago decided that anything arriving from the east was probably a rumor.

"You feel that?" Sefa asked on the fourth evening, as they made camp under a rowan tree that grew, impossibly, out of a wall.

"The cold?"

"The—" She waved a hand vaguely at herself. "The thinness. Like I'm being... edited."

Quentin looked up from the fire he was coaxing. He had that expression he got when a ledger balanced and he didn't want to jinx it. "That's what erasure feels like from the inside. Out here, the country's official history says nothing exists. So the country is... obliging. It's forgetting this place, and everything in it, including us, a little more each day." He fed the fire a stick. "It's why the founder chose it. Nothing to see here means no one looking."

"That's supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be true. Comfort is extra."

But Sefa noticed he didn't sleep well that night either, and that he slept with the whispering knucklebone wrapped in wool inside his coat, against his heart, as if it were a small animal that might bolt.

The knucklebone had been talking more, lately. Sefa couldn't hear it—only Quentin could hear the dead through what they carved—but she could see him listening: head tilted, lips moving slightly, like a man taking dictation from someone rude.

"She says we're close," he reported at midday on the fifth day, as the track climbed into hills that had no names and didn't seem to miss having them. "She says—" He paused, listening harder. His face did something complicated. "She says we should hurry."

"Why? What happens if we're slow?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer, and Sefa found herself hurrying without deciding to.

* * *

The dead, it turned out, were terrible navigators.

"They say left here," Quentin said, at a fork where both paths went right.

"There is no left here."

"They're aware. They say the left path used to be here. They say a lot of things used to be here." He dismounted and pressed his palm flat against a leaning stone, eyes half-closed. Sefa waited. Waiting for Quentin to finish listening was a skill she'd been developing the way other people developed calluses.

When he straightened, he was pale in a way that had nothing to do with the wind.

"What?"

"There were villages here," he said quietly. "Whole towns. The dead remember them—the baker, the ferryman, a woman who kept bees. They're still standing at their posts, Sefa. Still doing their work. They just don't have anywhere to do it anymore." He looked out at the empty hillside, where grass grew over something that might once have been foundations, or might just be lumps. "The country erased them so thoroughly that even the ground gave up. But the people stayed. You can't erase someone out of themselves."

Turnip snorted and stamped, and Sefa realized her own hands were shaking. She thought of the ledgers back home—all those names recorded nowhere else, all those lives held together by ink and bone and Quentin's stubborn bookkeeping—and felt, for the first time, the full weight of what would happen if the Veil ever stopped writing itself down.

Erasure wasn't safety. She'd known that intellectually since the god had turned its face toward the city. But standing in a place where erasure had *finished*, where it lay over the land like snow, she understood it in her body at last: erasure was not a wall that kept danger out. It was a slow tide that came in and never went out again.

"We keep moving," she said, and was proud that her voice only cracked a little.

* * *

They found the first ruin on the seventh day, and Sefa knew before Quentin said anything that it had not always been a ruin.

It sat in a valley like a cupped hand: walls of pale stone, a great arched gate, workshops with their roofs fallen in, dormitories with bunks still bolted to the floors. And everywhere—everywhere—carvings. Bone carvings, hundreds of them, thousands, laid out on every surface with a care that made Sefa's throat hurt. Little figures. Names scratched into lintels. A child's drawing of a dog, cut into a doorframe at knee height.

"It was a guild," Quentin said. His voice had gone strange and soft, the way it did in the workshop among the relics. "A memory-brokers' guild, maybe older than ours. Look—the mark above the gate. That's a ledger-clasp. They traded in remembered things." He walked slowly through the archway, and the air around him seemed to fill with voices only he could hear, though Sefa could almost believe she heard them too, the way you can almost hear your name called in surf.

"How many?"

"I don't—" He stopped in the middle of the main hall. Sefa watched him count, watched the counting go wrong, watched him arrive at a number he clearly didn't want. "All of them," he said finally. "Everyone who stayed. They didn't escape. They didn't even try, maybe. Or they tried and the door wouldn't open for them." He crouched beside a carving near the hearth—a woman's face, serene, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather. "She's still here. She says they thought being forgotten would protect them. They thought if the gods couldn't see them, nothing could touch them." His hand hovered over the worn face without touching. "She says the forgetting ate them anyway. Slowly. From the edges inward. First their names, then their faces, then—" He swallowed. "Then whether they'd ever been real at all."

The valley was very quiet. Even Turnip stood still, ears forward, as if listening.

"They starved," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. "Of memory."

"Invisible things in this country get conscripted, consumed, or killed," Quentin recited—the old guild catechism, the one every apprentice learned before they learned to carve. "Nobody mentions the fourth option. Nobody mentions just... fading. Because it doesn't happen to us. We have each other. We write each other down every day. We say each other's names over breakfast." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, bright and brittle. "We've been keeping ourselves alive this whole time and calling it accounting."

Sefa sat down on a fallen beam beside him. Below them, in the ruined hall, the bones lay in their careful rows like a congregation that had been waiting a very long time for a service no one would ever hold.

"Quentin." She made herself say it plainly, because plainness was a kind of mercy. "Is that going to be us? If we don't find the door?"

He was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he didn't look at her; he looked at the worn-smooth face of the dead broker, and his hand finally came down to rest, gently, on the stone beside her.

"No," he said. "Because we know now. Knowing is the difference between drowning and swimming. They never knew. They thought the water was the whole world." He stood, and brushed off his knees, and when he turned to her his jaw was set in the way she'd learned meant he had already decided something and was merely waiting for the rest of the world to catch up. "We find the door. We open it. And then—" He hesitated, and Sefa saw something flicker behind his eyes, some private arithmetic he didn't share. "And then nobody fades. Not one of us. Not ever."

It was a good speech. It was exactly the speech she needed. She believed almost all of it.

What she didn't say—what she carried instead, heavy in her chest as they led Turnip out of the valley of the faded—was the thing the knucklebone had told him that he hadn't told her. She'd seen it in his face when he listened. Doors, in every story she'd ever heard, took payment. And Quentin Nystrom had never once in his life let anyone else pay his debts.

* * *

Two days farther on, the dead began pointing.

Sefa could tell because Quentin stopped arguing with them about directions and started simply walking where they indicated, quick and certain, his breath smoking in the deepening cold. The hills had given way to a high grey moor where nothing grew except a kind of pale moss that glowed faintly after dark, so that the whole landscape seemed lit from beneath, like a page held up to a candle.

On the evening of the eleventh day, they crested a ridge and Sefa stopped so suddenly that Turnip bumped into her.

Below, in a bowl of moorland ringed by standing stones, lay a second ruin—but this one was different. This one was *recent*. The walls still stood. The gate still hung, half-open. And carved above it, in letters deep enough to read across the distance, was a single word in old guild-script that Sefa knew from her first year of study:

*Threshold.*

"That's it," Quentin breathed. "That's the door. The founder's map ends here." He started down the slope, and the knucklebone, wrapped against his heart, was audible even to Sefa now—not as words, but as a sound, a low urgent hum, like a struck bell that would not stop ringing.

And then Sefa saw what else lay in the bowl below, scattered between the standing stones, and grabbed his sleeve.

"Quentin. Wait. Look."

He looked. She watched the certainty drain out of his face.

There were people at the threshold. Dozens of them—figures seated, kneeling, lying in the moss-glow, arranged with the same terrible care as the bones in the valley. But these were not bones. These were bodies that had not finished fading. Some still had faces. One, nearest the gate, still had eyes, and they were open, and they were fixed on the door they had reached and never opened.

And every single one of them was looking at Quentin.

The bell-hum rose to a whine. Quentin's hand went to his coat, to the knucklebone, and Sefa heard him whisper—not to her, not to the dead, but to whatever waited behind that half-open gate:

"Oh. You're still hungry."
