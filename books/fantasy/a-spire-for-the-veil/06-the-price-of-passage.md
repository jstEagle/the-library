## 6. The Price of Passage

The door was not a door.

Quentin had expected—hoped for, honestly—a door. Something with hinges, perhaps a knocker shaped like a patient face, the kind of thing a bone-carver could appreciate. Instead, what the dead voices led him to in the ruined heart of the Hollow Marches was an archway of black stone standing alone in a field of white grass, with nothing behind it but more field, and nothing through it but a shimmer like heat off a summer road, except that it was autumn here, and cold enough that his breath came out in little ghosts of its own.

"It's a ledger," he said aloud, because he had learned by now that saying things aloud made the dead answer faster.

"You're getting better at reading," said the voice from his satchel—the carved knucklebone, which had been whispering prayers for three weeks and complaining about the quality of Quentin's packing for two of them. "Most living people look at that arch and see an arch."

"Most living people haven't spent their lives balancing books for people who don't exist." He set down his pack and flexed his fingers, stiff from three days of walking through country that officially contained no roads, no villages, and no reason for him to be in it. "It's an entry. Look at the proportions. The stone is dressed like vellum. The grain runs vertical, like columns of script. Someone built this the way I'd build a page."

"And you're certain that's not just your trade talking?"

"My trade is the only part of me that's never lied to me."

The knucklebone made a sound that might have been a sigh if bones could sigh, and probably could, and did, constantly, around Quentin.

He circled the arch slowly, the way he approached any entry he didn't trust. There were marks along the inner curve—not carved, exactly, but worn, as though generations of fingertips had traced them. He put his own hand against the stone.

The dead rose up like water finding its level.

Not the small dead this time, not the murmuring chorus of relics he carried. This was one voice, old and dry and amused, the way a very long book is amused at being opened again.

*You read well, carver.*

Quentin kept his hand on the stone. His heart was doing something undignified, but he'd learned to let his body panic while his mind took notes. "You're the founder."

*I am what's left of her. Which is, if we're honest, mostly her handwriting.* A pause, and then, wryly: *They called me Maren Voss when there was anyone left to call me anything. You may call me the arch. It's shorter.*

"Maren Voss." He said the name carefully, tasting it. It was in the ledgers—or rather, it wasn't, which was how he knew it mattered. The Veil's books recorded everyone who officially never existed, and every one of those entries had a hole where the founder should have been. A guild with no origin story. He'd assumed the erasure had been done *to* her.

He was beginning to suspect she'd done it herself.

"The map in the ledgers," he said. "The one hidden in the margins. It leads here."

*It leads through here. The distinction will matter shortly, so let's attend to it now, while you're still brave. People are always braver before they understand prices.*

"That's a comforting thing to say to someone standing inside a doorway."

*I've found comfort is mostly a failure of information.* The voice settled around him like dust settling, patient, unhurried. *Touch the third mark from the bottom. No—your left. Your other left. There.*

Under his fingertip, the stone warmed. And the shimmer in the archway resolved—not into another country, not yet, but into something like a counting room seen through fog. Figures moved in it. Or figures had moved in it once. It was hard to tell whether he was looking across space or across time, and the arch seemed to consider the question pedantic.

"This is the way out," Quentin said. "Out of the country. Out of the gods' sight."

*Out of their sight, yes. That part works. It has worked for four hundred years, and it will keep working, because I built it properly and I resent the implication otherwise.* A beat. *But nothing passes through unseen without something being given to be seen instead. Attention is a current, carver. It doesn't vanish. It reroutes.*

Quentin lowered his hand. The fog-thin counting room faded. The white grass whispered around his ankles, and somewhere behind him, a long way behind him, Sefa was minding the camp and pretending not to worry, which meant worrying loudly at the fire.

"A price," he said. "Of course there's a price. Nothing in the ledgers is free. We charge for impossible things; why shouldn't doors?"

*Now you're thinking like a guild member.* Pride, in the old voice, unmistakable and oddly warm. *The toll is memory. One memory, given wholly—not copied, not shared, *given*. It leaves you entirely and goes to the gods on the other side of the threshold. They hold it. They look at it. And because they are looking at it, they are not looking at you. You walk through carrying everything else you are, and they hold the one piece you paid, forever, like a pawnshop ticket they never intend to redeem.*

"For how long?"

*Forever is the standard term.*

"And what happens to the memory? In their hands?"

*They experience it,* Maren Voss said simply. *Over and over. Your joy becomes their weather. Your grief becomes their music. You will be, in one small corner of their vast attention, permanently felt. It is not destruction, carver. It is—* she searched, and the searching itself told him something— *it is being remembered by something enormous, at the cost of remembering it yourself.*

Quentin stood very still.

He thought about the ledgers. That was his mistake, or his habit, or possibly his whole self: whenever the world grew frightening, he thought in entries. And the entry forming in his head now was ugly. A person walks through the door whole except for one piece. The gods hold the piece. The gods therefore hold a *claim*—a lien against the soul, registered in perpetuity. Every guild instinct he had screamed at the terms. Never sign anything where the counterparty keeps the original.

"There's no version," he said slowly, "where I pay and get it back."

*No.*

"No version where I pay and they forget me afterward."

*That would defeat the entire mechanism. If they stopped looking at the payment, they'd start looking at the payer.* The old voice gentled, which was somehow worse. *I'm sorry. I know how it sounds. I knew how it sounded when I first heard it, too, standing where you're standing, wearing boots very much like yours.*

"When you—" He stopped. Turned the thought over. Set it down again, the way you set down a relic whose weight isn't right. "Maren Voss. The ledgers have no entry for you. Not erased. *Absent.* Like a column that was never filled in."

The silence from the arch lasted long enough for the wind to make two full circuits of the field.

*You do read well,* she said finally.

"You gave a memory here. Four hundred years ago."

*I gave my name.*

The words landed in Quentin's chest like a dropped ledger—all the weight, none of the padding. He gripped the edge of the archway, and the stone was cold again, ordinary, just rock, just ruin, and yet he felt he was holding onto the oldest account in the country.

"Your name," he repeated. "Not a memory *of* something. The memory of being *you*."

*A name is only the handle on the bucket,* said Maren Voss. *But oh, what's in the bucket. Every morning I woke and knew myself by it. Every choice I'd ever made answered to it. When I walked through, I remembered that I had been a founder, a carver, a liar, a builder of doors—but the thread that stitched all of it into *me*, the word my mother used to call me home to supper—that went through the arch ahead of me and the gods caught it in both hands and they have been turning it over, all these centuries, like a coin too precious to spend.*

"So when people say your name—"

*Nobody does. That's rather the point. There is no name to say. I didn't lose it the way you lose a glove; I paid it the way you pay a debt, and the debt-holder keeps the receipt.* A pause, and then, with humor so old it had gone soft at the edges: *Though I'll confess a vanity. Sometimes, on certain nights, when the gods are restless, I think I can feel them saying it. To each other. Over and over. Whatever it was. It must have been a good name. They seem to enjoy it immensely.*

Horror rose in Quentin, sharp and complete—and then, strangely, turned over, the way a bone turns under the carver's blade until the grain shows true.

Because he understood. Not the horror; the *design*. She hadn't been robbed. She had looked at a country where visible things were conscripted, consumed, or killed, and she had built a door, and the door needed a hinge, and the hinge was a self. She had unscrewed her own name from her own soul and hung the door on it, and then she had stayed—four hundred years in the stone, listening to footsteps, waiting for someone who could read margins—to explain the terms to whoever came next.

Not a trap. A trust.

"You stayed," he said quietly. "You could have gone through. You'd already paid."

*And leave the door unexplained? Leave some poor carver to press his hand against my ribs and learn the price after he'd stepped halfway through?* The amusement cracked, just slightly, showing the thing underneath it. *I was a ledger-keeper, Quentin Nystrom. We do not send people into contracts unwarned. It's the whole of our ethics and most of our personality.*

He laughed—a short, wet sound, unexpected, and he wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist and pretended the wind was responsible.

"I have people," he said. "Back at the fire. An apprentice who argues with me. A guildmaster who lies to us lovingly. A workshop full of people who officially never existed and who I—" He stopped, recalculated, decided the arch had earned honesty. "Who I love. All of them. I want to bring them through. All of them. Does the toll take one memory per person, or can it be—"

*One per soul,* said Maren Voss gently. *There are no group rates. I checked, four hundred years ago, quite thoroughly, and bargaining with gods is even less productive than bargaining with the tax office.*

"Then I need to know everything. Every term, every exception, every margin note you ever wrote. Because I am not paying for myself alone, and I am not letting my people sign anything unwarned, and if the gods want a memory from the Veil—" He straightened, and heard his own voice go flat and clear, the voice he used when an account finally balanced. "Then they should know exactly what they're buying. All of it. Everything we were."

The arch was silent.

Then, from far away—from the direction of the fire, from the direction of home—came a sound that did not belong to the white grass or the wind: a horn, deep and brazen, blown from beyond the hills. Once. Twice.

The gods' attention, moving.

*Carver,* said Maren Voss, and for the first time in four hundred years, the old voice sounded afraid. *They're coming to the door early. You have until nightfall to decide what you're willing to give them—and I think, from the sound of that horn, they may already have decided for you.*
