## 7. Ledger-Burning

Orla Vess had spent forty years learning how to be forgotten, and in a single morning the whole country had decided to remember her.

She felt it first as an itch between her shoulder blades, the old carver's instinct that something was watching. Then the shop bell rang, and a customer looked directly at her—looked, and *saw* her—and said, "Good morning, mistress," as though she were a person who existed.

"Morning," Orla said, and sold him a memory of his late wife's laugh for three coppers, because panic was no excuse for bad business. But when he left she stood in the doorway a long moment, watching him walk away without once glancing back at the shop that should have slid off his eyes like rain off wax.

The gods were back. Everyone had said so for weeks—the street preachers, the drunkards, the children who now prayed openly at crossroads shrines instead of whispering into their cupped hands like thieves. Orla had told herself it would pass. Gods were weather. You waited them out.

But weather did not look at you. Weather did not *remember* you.

"You're doing the face," said Pip, the youngest of the bone-carvers, appearing at her elbow with a tray of unfinished knucklebones. Pip was nineteen, freckled, and constitutionally incapable of worry, which Orla privately considered the guild's most valuable asset after her own ledgers.

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding which of us to feed to something."

"I only make that face about the ledgers."

"You make it about everything." Pip set down the tray. "Quentin's not back yet. From the Marches. It's been nine days."

"I can count, Pip."

"Can you? Because you've re-inked the same entry four times this morning, and it says 'Marta Henn, one dream of flying, paid in full' every time, and Marta Henn has been dead eleven years."

Orla looked down. The ledger page was indeed a smudge of black ink, the same line written over itself until the paper shone through. She closed the book with more force than necessary.

"Go sand something," she said.

* * *

The betrayal came at dusk, delivered by a child too young to know what she carried: a folded scrap of cheap paper, no signature, pressed into her hand outside the tannery with the words "for the lady who isn't there."

Orla read it standing under the eaves while the evening market emptied around her. The handwriting was elegant, unhurried, the hand of someone who had never needed to hide.

*Mistress Vess. The Gilded Hand extends its compliments. We have always admired the Veil's work. We have taken the liberty of describing your workshop, your members, and your methods to those who are currently listening very carefully. Visibility is the new currency, we find. We have purchased some. You may purchase yours back, if you wish. The price is reasonable. Come to the Salt Exchange before the moon fills.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it, put it in her mouth, and chewed it to pulp, because Orla Vess had survived forty years by never letting evidence survive, and because chewing gave her something to do with her teeth besides scream.

The Gilded Hand. Rival brokers of luck and small fortunes, flashy where the Veil was quiet, gaudy where the Veil was invisible. She'd always assumed they'd die of their own ostentation eventually. Instead they had found the one commodity the returning gods made valuable: other people's existence.

She walked home the long way, cataloguing. The Salt Exchange was two districts east; she could be there within the hour. The price would be names—hers, or Quentin's, or Sefa's, or sweet doomed Pip who still thought worry was a spice you could choose not to add. The Gilded Hand would sell them one visibility at a time, like meat off a carcass, and the gods would collect what they could see, and the Veil would end not with a battle but with a transaction.

Unless.

Unless there was nothing left to see.

* * *

The false ledgers filled three shelves in the back room: forty years of careful fabrications, entries for guild members who had never existed, trades that had never happened, debts owed to people who had never been born. Every guild kept them. It was the oldest rule of the trade—you maintain a visible record so convincing that anyone who looks finds exactly what they expect to find, and stops looking. A forged history is armor.

But armor only works against enemies who want to hurt you. Against gods who wanted to *know* you, a forged history was worse than nothing. It was an invitation. Find the lie, and they would dig past it to the truth beneath—and the truth beneath included the true map, the door, the way out, hidden in plain sight inside the ledgers themselves, stitched into the margins in Quentin's cipher. If the Gilded Hand handed the false ledgers to the gods, the gods would read them the way gods read everything: completely, all the way down, to the last marginal line.

Orla stood before the shelves with a lamp in one hand and a fire-poker in the other, and discovered that her hands were shaking.

It wasn't fear of the burning. She'd burned ledgers before—a broker's life was full of ash. It was what the burning would cost, and she understood the cost precisely because she had helped write the rules.

The Veil's records were not just paper. They were woven. Every member contributed a thread of memory to the guild's collective recollection—that was how the erasure held, how customers forgot the shop, how officials' eyes slid past the door. The false ledgers were anchored in that weave. Burn them improperly, burn them *outside* the proper rites, and the weave tore. And the weaver who tore it took the damage into herself.

There was a rite for retiring records cleanly. It required three senior members, a night and a day, and the consent of everyone named within. Orla had perhaps half a night, alone, with a rival guild's messenger already counting down to the full moon.

So she did what she had done her entire life, which was the arithmetic nobody else wanted to do.

"Forgive me," she said—to the shelves, to the absent Quentin, to the guild entire. "This will hurt me more than it hurts you, which is the only kind of sacrifice I've ever trusted."

She pulled the first ledger down and fed it to the lamp flame.

* * *

The pain began at the third book.

Not in her hands, though they blistered from pages pulled free too fast. Not in her lungs, though the smoke was thick with burning ink and something older underneath, the smell of forty years of deliberate forgetting. The pain came from *inside*, from the weave tearing along its anchor-points, and it felt exactly like being unwritten.

Names went first. She reached for Pip and found a freckled blur, a shape with no face attached. She reached for the workshop and found a building-shaped absence, rooms she knew she knew but couldn't picture. She gripped the edge of the workbench and repeated aloud, like a prayer or a spell or both: "I am Orla Vess. I am Guildmaster of the Veil. I am doing this on purpose. I am doing this *on purpose.*"

Because that was the trick of it, the thing she'd learned from watching the old founder's ghost-stories pass down through generations of keepers: you could survive the tearing if you held one thread and refused to let go. One true thing, clung to with everything you had. The rite normally used a shared name, spoken by three voices. Orla had only her own voice, and only her own name, and so she spent it like a miser spending her last coin, over and over, feeding the fire and feeding herself the same syllables, *Orla Vess, Orla Vess,* while the guild's memory bled out of her in long silver strands she could almost see, unraveling from her chest into the smoke.

By the tenth ledger she had lost her mother's face. By the fifteenth, the taste of the plum wine she'd drunk at her own induction, forty years ago tonight, come to think of it. By the twentieth she was laughing, because somewhere in the tearing she had found the memory of young Quentin Nystrom arriving with his dead man's knucklebones and his terrible posture, asking if the guild took apprentices who talked to bones, and she had answered, "We take apprentices who balance books," and he had said, "Then I'll learn," and she had loved him from that moment with the fierce unsentimental love of a woman who does not say such things—and that memory was going too, fraying strand by strand, his face going soft and featureless as wax left in sun—

"No," she said, and held that one. That one she held. Whatever else the fire took, she would keep the shape of that boy bent over his ledgers, even if she had to invent the face afterward, even if the invented face was wrong. Some things you kept by choice or not at all.

The last ledger was the thickest, forty years of beautiful lies, and it fought her, the weave clinging to its anchors, and she pried it apart page by page with her blistered hands and fed it in, and when it caught, the tear went through her like a hook through cloth, and Orla Vess sat down hard on the floor of her own back room amid the ashes of her guild's armor, holding one thread in both hands.

*I am Orla Vess.*

Except—

She tested it, the way you test a tooth. Reached for herself. Found... a woman. Sitting in ash. Who knew she was Guildmaster of something. Whose name was—

Her name was—

The thread held. Barely. A name worn thin as old cloth, translucent, a name you could see light through. Enough. Perhaps enough. Perhaps a name that thin would slip past even gods, or perhaps it would simply be the first thing they collected.

Outside, the moon was nearly full. Somewhere east, the Salt Exchange lamps were being lit for a sale that would find no goods. Somewhere south, in the Hollow Marches, Quentin was walking toward a door with a map she had just finished making unreadable, and she hoped—I hope, she thought, I hope, that's what hope feels like, I'd forgotten—he was fast enough.

Pip found her at dawn, sitting in the ashes, and looked at her with the blank polite stare of a stranger, and Orla's heart cracked along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing.

"Mistress?" Pip said. "Are you all right? Only—only I can't seem to recall why I'm here."

Orla opened her mouth to answer, to gather the girl in, to begin the long slow work of rebuilding a guild out of ash and choice—and then stopped, because across the ruined room, faint as breath on glass, the air had begun to shimmer.

Something was looking at the place where the Veil used to be.

And finding, for the moment, nothing—but circling. Circling like a hawk over a field where the grass had been cut, knowing something had been here, waiting for anything to move.

Orla Vess, whose name was wearing thin as smoke, drew the last unburned scrap of ledger parchment toward her, turned it over, and began, with a shaking hand, to write the only thing she could still remember clearly enough to save:

*A boy who balances books. His name begins with Q.*
