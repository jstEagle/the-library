## 8. Truth Before the Gods

The door was not a door.

That was the first thing Quentin understood about it, standing in the ruin of the Hollow Marches with Sefa's hand tight around his wrist and the founder's ghost humming at his shoulder like a kettle deciding whether to boil. The door was a gap in the world shaped like a doorway—the way a footprint is shaped like a foot but is not one—and through the gap he could see nothing at all, which was somehow more inviting than seeing something. Unnamed land. Unrecorded land. Land that had never been entered into any ledger anywhere, and therefore could not be taken away.

"It's smaller than I imagined," Sefa said.

"Everything is," said the ghost of Maren Voss, who had founded the Veil four hundred years ago and had been giving unsolicited opinions ever since. "You should have seen the country before they erased it. Enormous. Terrible poetry everywhere."

Quentin set down the satchel of ledgers. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had expected them to shake. He had spent three weeks walking toward this moment—through the Marches, past the ruins of guilds that had not escaped, past the bone-fields where erased people went to be forgotten properly—and he had assumed that at the end of it he would be trembling.

Instead he felt the particular calm of a man balancing a column that finally adds up.

"The toll," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be argued with. "A memory given wholly to the gods. They see you forever after."

"Yes," said Maren.

"And you gave yours. Your name. Four hundred years ago."

"I gave my name." The ghost's voice did something complicated on the word *name*, the way a floorboard does something complicated under weight. "And they have watched me ever since. Every hour. Every thought. That is what ownership looks like from the inside, Quentin Nystrom. It looks like being a room someone else keeps clean."

Sefa made a small sound. Quentin looked over. The apprentice was seventeen, sharp as a paper cut, and currently wearing the expression she wore when she had figured out an answer before the adults and was deciding whether to be generous about it.

"You're not going to do it," she said.

"I'm going to do something."

"You're going to do something *worse*," she said, with dawning delight. "I know that face. That's your 'the ledgers are wrong and I'm right' face."

"They're not wrong," Quentin said. "The terms are clear. A memory, wholly given. And here's what nobody says plainly: if you give a god a memory, you make it *yours*. Yours to keep, yours to hoard, yours to hold over you forever. You turn yourself into collateral." He crouched and began unpacking the ledgers, all nine of them, their spines cracked, their pages soft as moth wings. "But there's another word for giving something to many parties at once. There's a whole section of the old trade-cantons devoted to it."

Sefa frowned. "What word?"

"Publication."

* * *

The gods came at dusk, because attention in this country had always kept rustic hours.

They did not descend so much as *arrive*, the way weather arrives—one moment the sky was empty, the next it was crowded. Quentin counted seven presences, though counting them was like counting reflections in moving water. The oldest one, the thing that had first answered the street child's prayer back in the city, settled above the ruined marketplace of the Hollow Marches and looked down at him with an attention that had actual weight, like a hand pressed flat against his skull.

It had been watching him for weeks. He could feel the shape of its regard now, familiar as a scar.

"Here's my problem," Quentin said to the sky, conversationally, because if you were going to commit blasphemy you might as well be comfortable doing it. "You want a memory. One memory, wholly given, and then I'm yours. Watched forever. Kept. Like a relic on a shelf."

The oldest god's attention tightened. Encouragement, or impatience. With gods it was hard to tell the difference, which Quentin suspected was also true of guildmasters.

"But I've read the contracts," he went on. "All of them. I keep the ledgers—I've read every clause your priests ever negotiated, every bargain struck since the Veil was founded. And here's the thing none of you ever put in writing, because you didn't think we'd notice." He held up one finger. "You can only own what is *hidden*. Attention makes things legible, yes—but legible isn't owned. Legible is just *known*. Ownership requires secrecy on one side. That's why you collect us. We were invisible, so we were collectible."

Maren Voss drifted closer, her translucent form flickering. "Quentin," she said carefully, "what are you doing?"

"What you should have done four hundred years ago instead of paying the toll." He lifted the first ledger. "I'm making you witnesses."

He opened to the first page and began to read.

* * *

He read the history of the Veil aloud to the assembled gods, and it took until well past midnight.

He read the founding: how Maren Voss, a bone-carver's daughter, had carved her dying mother's last words into a knucklebone and discovered that the dead spoke through what they left behind. How she'd gathered the other invisible trades—memory-brokers, unlicensed menders, forgers of documents for people fleeing conscription—and sworn them into a guild whose first rule was *we do not exist on paper.*

He read the middle years: the ledgers of unclaimed names, thousands of them, people the official histories had swallowed. The tailor Jorin Hale, erased for sheltering deserters, whose hands Quentin knew intimately because Quentin had carved his knuckles into prayer-beads and heard him complaining about the cold for eleven years. The midwife Ashka Vell, who delivered four hundred children no census ever counted. The boy Tam, who had prayed to a dead god for rain during the drought and gotten it, and whose answered prayer had started all of this—because the old gods had heard him, and hearing is the beginning of seeing, and seeing is the beginning of owning.

He read the betrayals, too. Orla feeding names to the gods one by one to keep their attention scattered—a protection that looked like betrayal and cost her more than any of them would ever fully understand. The rival guild selling them out. The burning of the false ledgers. He read all of it, plainly, without varnish, because a witness who flatters is not a witness.

And the gods listened.

That was the terrible, wonderful thing. They *listened*. Seven vast attentions bent over one tired man reading by the light of a lantern Sefa was holding with both hands, and they did not interrupt, because gods, it turned out, were exactly like everyone else: they could not resist a story they were in.

"You see it," Quentin said, somewhere around the third ledger, his voice gone rough. "You're in it now. All of you. Every name I read, you've heard. Every crime, every kindness—you've witnessed it. And a witness—" he tapped the page— "a witness cannot own the story. A witness is *obligated* to it. You wanted a memory? Here. Take all of them. Take the whole guild, whole and entire, given freely, publicly, to seven witnesses at once. Not owned. *Known.*"

The oldest god's attention pressed down harder. Somewhere in the pressure Quentin felt the shape of a question, vast and slow, like a whale turning over.

*Why would we accept?*

"Because you already have," Quentin said. "You listened. You're listening still. And besides—" and here he allowed himself the smallest smile, the one Sefa called his *ledger-balanced* smile— "you're collectors. And I am offering you the single most complete collection in the history of this country. Every erased name. Documented. Attributed. Witnessed by yourselves. No god in any pantheon has anything like it." He spread his arms. "Take the collection. Let the collectors go."

Silence. Or whatever passes for silence when the sky is thinking.

Then Maren Voss laughed—a bright, startled, four-hundred-years-overdue laugh—and said, "Oh, you *magnificent little accountant*."

* * *

The door opened at dawn.

There was no drama to it. The gap in the world simply became a passage, the way a held breath becomes an exhale, and beyond it lay green hills under a sky with no gods in it at all—or different ones, at least; Quentin chose not to look too closely, having had quite enough of divinity for one lifetime.

But the toll was paid. Of course it was. Nothing in this country was free, and gods did not do charity, even moved as they clearly were.

They took his name.

Not violently. That was the strange part. Quentin felt it lift out of him the way a splinter leaves—there was a place where it had been, and then there wasn't, and the skin of his mind closed over the absence so smoothly he almost couldn't find it. He reached for *Quentin Nystrom* and found a shape with nothing in it, a hook with no coat, a ledger line with the entry scraped away.

He knew he had a name. He simply couldn't remember what it was. He suspected, distantly, that this would bother him later. At present it felt oddly like setting down a heavy bag after a long walk.

"Well," he said, to the hills, to Sefa, to whoever he apparently was now. "That's inconvenient."

Sefa was crying and furious about it, which was very her. "You *idiot*. You brilliant, impossible—do you have any idea—" She stopped, wiped her face with her sleeve, and straightened. "Fine. Fine. Then I'll just have to fix it, won't I?"

"Fix what?" Quentin asked, genuinely.

She looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted from grief to resolve, the way iron shifts in a forge.

"Nothing," she said. "Never mind. Come on—they'll be waiting. Orla needs help walking, and somebody has to carry the ledgers, and apparently that somebody is still you, name or no name." She picked up the lantern and stepped through the door into the unnamed morning, then paused. "Well? Are you coming, or do you need me to write it down for you?"

Behind him, faint as breath on glass, the dead laughed.

Quentin—whoever he was—picked up the ledgers, and followed.
