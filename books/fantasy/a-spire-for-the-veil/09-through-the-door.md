## 9. Through the Door

The door did not look like a door. It looked like a mistake in the air—a place where the light of the Hollow Marches forgot what it was supposed to do and simply gave up. One moment I was staring at a shimmer the shape of nothing; the next, Quentin's hand was on my shoulder, and the world on the other side of that shimmer was green.

I went through carrying Orla's boots. Someone had to. Her feet kept catching on the tall grass of the new country, and she was in no state to mind them herself, being unconscious and slung across Tam Vetch's back like a sack of very important, very bleeding turnips.

"Grass," Tam said, wonderingly, when we were all through. He knelt and pressed his palm flat against it. "Actual grass. I'd forgotten grass had a smell."

"It doesn't have a smell," said Merrin, the youngest of us and the most committed to arguing about everything.

"Everything has a smell. You just stop noticing it when it's trying to kill you."

The old country's grass had, admittedly, mostly been trying to kill us. This grass smelled like rain and crushed stems and something sweeter underneath, like a promise made quietly. I took one breath of it and then another, and only on the third breath realized I was crying.

Nobody mentioned it. That is one thing the Veil always understood: some accounts you let pass uncollected.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of a hill that had no name, because nothing here had names yet, which meant everything was still possible. Tam built a fire from wood that burned without hissing or muttering or, in two cases, screaming. Merrin unpacked the good cheese we'd carried for three hundred miles against precisely this occasion. I sat with Orla's head in my lap and dabbed at the burn along her collarbone where the burning ledgers had marked her—she had reached into her own fire, Quentin told me later, to pull out the true map before it fed itself to the flames. The wound was ugly but clean. She was alive. Alive, and breathing with the deep, stubborn rhythm of a woman who had spent sixty years refusing to be legible and wasn't going to start by dying politely.

Quentin sat apart, as he had since the door.

He had gone through last. Of course he had—he was the sort of man who counted everyone else through a doorway before himself; it was in his bones, which was fitting, because his bones were practically an accounting office by now. And when he stepped through, the gods took their payment. His name. Not just from the records. From *him*.

I watched him now in the firelight. He had a stick and was scratching marks into the dirt beside his knee—little tally strokes, quick and automatic, the way other people hummed.

"What are you counting?" I asked.

He looked down at the marks as if surprised to find them there. "I don't know," he said. "Nine." A pause. "There are nine of us."

"Eight," I said gently. "You're not counting yourself."

He frowned at the dirt. Then, slowly, deliberately, he drew a tenth stroke, set slightly apart from the others, and did not label it. I felt something in my chest crack open, warm and terrible, and I looked away before he could see my face.

* * *

Merrin distributed the cheese with the solemnity of a coronation. "One wedge each. Except Tam gets less, because Tam ate half the rind on the road and claimed it fell."

"It *did* fall," Tam said. "Into my mouth. Tragic accident."

"Cheese-related tragedy," I agreed. "We'll note it in the ledger."

And then the silence came down, the way it does, the way it had been doing all day whenever anyone said *ledger*. Because the ledgers were ash. Because the guild they recorded had never officially existed, and now the false books that proved its nonexistence were burned too, and the only true record left in all the world was the one Quentin had read aloud to the assembled gods at the threshold—and the gods did not keep records. They kept *trophies*.

"He's still doing it," Merrin whispered to me, nodding at Quentin. "Counting."

"Of course he's still doing it."

"But he doesn't remember why. He doesn't remember the Veil, or the workshop, or—" Merrin's voice wobbled. "He asked me this morning who taught him bone-carving. He thought maybe his mother. Sefa, his mother sold fish."

"I know."

"And the dead don't talk to him anymore. Have you noticed? He keeps picking up stones and bits of wood and turning them over in his hands, waiting, and nothing answers. He doesn't even know what he's waiting for."

"I've noticed," I said.

It was true, and it was the worst of it. Quentin Nystrom had given the gods his name, and the name had been the thread that stitched him to everything—the guild, the ghosts, the ledgers, the whole architecture of who he was. The dead who had guided us through the Hollow Marches had fallen silent the moment he crossed. Whatever claim they'd had on him had been paid out, settled, closed. An account, once read aloud in full before gods, cannot be reopened.

But the habits remained. That was the strange mercy and the strange horror of it. He balanced plates at dinner without being asked, shifting weight so nothing tipped. He knew exactly how many days of food we had and how far the water source was and that Tam owed Merrin a sock. He did not know his own birthday. He did not know that the scar on his thumb came from his first carving knife, given to him by a founder who had died four centuries before either of us was born and who had loved him, in her cold and patient way, right up until the end.

"Do you think he's happy?" Merrin asked.

I considered it honestly, because Merrin deserved honesty. "I think he's lighter," I said. "And I think he doesn't know what the lightness is missing. Which is either the saddest thing I've ever heard or the kindest."

* * *

Orla woke at dusk.

She surfaced the way she did everything—without apology, mid-sentence, as if consciousness were a meeting she'd merely stepped out of. "—and if the fire took the west ledger too, then the map's location dies with me, so someone had better write it down, and it had better not be in code, because none of you can—" Her eyes found mine. She blinked. Took inventory: sky, unfamiliar stars, grass, fire, cheese. "Ah," she said. "We made it, then."

"You made it," I said. "Don't move. Your shoulder—"

"My shoulder is a ruin and has been since the second decade of this disaster. Help me sit up anyway."

I helped her sit up. Some arguments are not worth having; Guildmaster Orla Dunmore had won every argument she'd ever entered by simply continuing to exist louder than the opposition. She surveyed our ragged circle—Tam gnawing his reduced cheese, Merrin guarding hers, me, the unnamed hill, the enormous unmarked night—and something crossed her face that on any other person I would have called grief, except that on Orla it looked more like an invoice finally paid.

"Where is he?" she said.

"By the stream. Checking the water."

"Checking the water," Orla repeated softly. "He doesn't remember me."

"He remembers that someone should check the water. He remembers that everyone should eat. He remembers—" I stopped.

"Go on," Orla said. "I'm an old woman sitting in a new country with a hole in her memory where her guild used to be and a hole in her shoulder where her principles used to be. I can bear whatever you say next."

"He remembers how to be good," I finished. "That's what stayed. Everything else burned away and that didn't."

Orla was quiet for a long time. Firelight moved on her face. "Then the gods took worse payment than they knew," she said at last, "because they collected a name and got a conscience instead. Bad trade. I'd have haggled."

Despite everything, I laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in my chest, and for a moment the grief and the relief sat together inside me like two travelers sharing a bench, neither willing to leave first.

* * *

I found Quentin at the stream after full dark. He was crouched at the bank, and he had, I saw with a pang, gathered a row of smooth stones and lined them up along a flat rock—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. Eight stones. He was staring at them like a man doing sums that wouldn't balance.

"That's us," I said, sitting down beside him. "In case you wondered. That's the whole family."

"Is it?" He turned a stone over in his fingers. His hands were beautiful hands, scarred and sure, hands that remembered every craft their owner had forgotten. "I keep arranging things. I don't know why. There's a—it's like an itch behind my eyes. Like something should be written down and isn't."

My heart did the painful lurching thing it had done all day. Because I had decided something, back at the fire, and now was the time.

"There is," I said. "Something that should be written down. And I'm going to write it. Tomorrow, when there's light enough. A new ledger. First page."

"For what? We don't even have ink."

"We'll use berry juice. We'll use blood if we have to. We'll use charcoal and bad handwriting." I took a breath. "The first entry is your name."

He went very still.

"I don't have a name," he said. "I've been trying all day to—there's a shape where it ought to be. Everyone looks at me like they're about to say something and then don't."

"Yes. That's the shape of it. I know."

"How do you know it, if nobody does?"

"Because I'm keeping it," I said. "You gave it to the gods, Quentin. All of it, forever, so the rest of us could walk through a door into a country with grass that doesn't want to kill us. But a name isn't only a thing that gets taken. It's a thing that gets *kept*. Given. Spoken. So starting tomorrow morning, I'm going to say your name out loud, every day, and write it down every week, and teach it to every child this guild ever raises, until there are so many copies of it scattered through so many living memories that no god alive could ever collect them all."

The stream ran. Somewhere an owl—or this country's equivalent of an owl, which might, for all I knew, sing opera—made a soft sound in the dark.

"I don't remember it," Quentin said at last, and his voice was very small.

"You don't have to. That's the whole point. That's what an apprentice is *for*." I put my hand over his scarred ones, over the stone he held. "You kept everyone's names for twenty years. It's my turn. Consider the account transferred."

He looked at me for a long moment. And though he couldn't have known why, his eyes filled, and he laughed—a wet, bewildered laugh—and said:

"That sounds like something I would do."

"It is *exactly* something you would do," I said. "It's the most you thing there is. Now come back to the fire. Merrin's telling the story of the cheese incident and giving herself a much better role than she deserves."

He rose, and paused, and set the eighth stone carefully back at the end of the line where it belonged. Then, after a moment's thought, he picked up one more stone from the bank—a ninth, small and pale and ordinary—and placed it apart from the others, at the head of the row, the way you would place a marker. The way you would begin a ledger.

Behind us, in the grass, something rustled that was not wind. And from somewhere beneath the ground of the new country—faint, patient, approving—I heard, or almost heard, the sound of many dry voices murmuring in unison, like readers turning a single page.

The dead, it seemed, had followed us after all.

They had come to see how the story ended.

And they had brought, unless my ears deceived me, a request.
