## 10. One Person Remembers

The new country had no name, and that turned out to be its finest quality.

Sefa learned this on the fourth morning, when she went down to the harbor to buy fish and the fishmonger asked her name, plainly, in front of everyone, the way you'd ask about the weather.

"Sefa," she said, and then stood there like a fool, waiting for the sky to fall in.

It didn't. The fishmonger—a broad woman with flour on her apron for reasons Sefa never did establish, since it was a fish stall—just nodded and wrapped up two mackerel. "Sefa," she repeated, tucking the name away the way people here tucked away names: carefully, but without fear. "You're the one who's been fixing the old custom house roof. Good work. That slate's been singing in the wind for twenty years."

"Someone had to shut it up," Sefa said, and the woman laughed, and that was that. A transaction. A name exchanged. Nothing consumed, nothing conscripted, nobody followed home by divine attention.

She carried the mackerel back through the morning streets feeling like a person carrying something far more fragile than fish. Behind her the town woke up in increments—gulls arguing over the harbor, a bell with no particular job ringing anyway because the ringer liked the sound, smoke rising from forty chimneys belonging to forty families who were all, officially and gloriously, *real*.

The Veil had taken over the old custom house because it was enormous, drafty, and had the word CUSTOMS carved over the door in letters big enough to be ironic. Orla had looked at it on their first day and said, "We'll never hide in this," with the expression of a woman falling in love.

"We don't have to," Sefa had said.

"I know," Orla said. "I'm just enjoying the terror of it."

* * *

Three months on, the terror had mellowed into something more like vertigo, and the custom house had become a workshop in the open air sense of the word: doors unlocked, windows unshuttered, bone-dust drifting out into the street where children sneezed at it and laughed.

Hesper ran the memory-brokerage out of what used to be the tax office, which everyone agreed was poetry. She took memories in trade and gave them back improved—or at least reorganized—and she had stopped flinching every time a client said her name out loud. Mostly stopped. Old habits died hard, and Hesper's habits were older than most.

Tam and the other carvers worked along the long south wall where the light was good, and their pieces sat in the front window now instead of behind cloth. Little saints. Little animals. Little knucklebones that whispered, though here they only whispered ordinary things—the carver's mood while shaping them, mostly, and once, memorably, a recipe for pickled herring that Tam swore he'd never known and refused to claim.

Orla held court in the middle of it all from a chair she'd dragged from somewhere, one arm still bound from the fire, supervising everything by the simple method of having opinions about all of it.

And Quentin—

Quentin sat by the north window with the ledgers, because some things survived even the loss of the name attached to them.

That was the part Sefa hadn't expected. She had braced herself for a hollow man. She'd rehearsed grief on the crossing, prepared speeches, prepared silence, prepared for the possibility that whatever came through the door wearing Quentin's shape would be a stranger with familiar hands.

Instead: Quentin, who no longer knew his own name, still balanced accounts. Still knew that Hesper took her tea bitter and Tam lied about how much bone-dust he used and Orla's arm ached before rain. Still rose first and banked the fire. Still had a way of looking at Sefa across a room when something was funny, as if to say *you saw that too*, which was its own kind of memory, the kind that lived in the body and not the ledger.

He just didn't know what to call himself.

They'd tried. Everyone had. The trouble was that a name spoken by someone else to a person who couldn't hold it slid off like water off wax. Quentin would nod politely—*yes, that sounds like a fine name*—and within an hour it had gone out of him again, leaving no more impression than a bird on a windowsill.

"It's not sad," Quentin had told her once, in that reasonable tone that made her want to throw a cushion at him. "I know what I am. I'm the one who keeps the books."

"The books aren't a name."

"They're a better one than most people get," he said, and went back to his columns, and Sefa had thrown the cushion, and he'd laughed, and she'd laughed, and neither of them had mentioned it again.

But she hadn't accepted it either. That was the difference between Quentin's surrender and hers. He had paid the toll and made his peace. She had paid nothing and intended to keep it that way.

* * *

On the morning of the fish, Sefa climbed to the loft above the workshop, where she slept among crates that smelled of someone else's vanished cargo, and took out the book she'd bought her second week in the new country.

It was nothing special. Good paper, good binding, bought openly from a stationer who had complimented her taste. It had cost actual money and been recorded in an actual shopkeeper's ledger, and Sefa had nearly wept at the counter, which the stationer had found very flattering for reasons she chose not to correct.

On the first page, in her careful apprentice's hand—the hand Quentin had spent two years correcting, and which had stubbornly kept its loops—was written a single entry:

*Quentin Nystrom. Ledger-keeper of the Veil. Born under a bad harvest moon; chose his own name at fourteen, which is why it fit him so well. Kept accounts for three hundred souls the world insisted weren't there. Gave his name so the rest of us could walk through a door. I watched him do it.*

Below that, smaller:

*He hums when the columns come out even. He pretends not to like Tam's terrible jokes. He is afraid of deep water and goes to the harbor anyway, every week, to look at it.*

Every morning, before anything else, she read the entry aloud. Every evening, she added a line. Small things. The way he'd stood in the rain last Tuesday to make sure the delivery cart didn't tip. The argument he'd had with Orla about whether the workshop needed a sign (he'd won; the sign read THE VEIL in plain letters, because Orla had decided that if the gods wanted to look at them here, they could jolly well look at a guild that wasn't ashamed of itself).

She didn't know if it worked. That was the thing nobody could tell her. Magic had gotten them across the border; magic had taken Quentin's name into the keeping of things older than borders. Whether a spoken name, repeated daily by one stubborn apprentice, could call anything back—whether memory kept alive by choice could do what memory kept alive by enchantment could not—there was no ledger for that. Not yet.

But there was this: every morning she said *Quentin Nystrom* out loud, and every morning the day felt slightly more defended. Like a door propped open. Like a lamp left burning in a window for someone who might not know the way home but would recognize the light.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Quentin said from the foot of the loft ladder, later that week. "The whispering."

"I don't whisper."

"You absolutely whisper. You've got a whole liturgy up there." He was holding two cups of tea, one bitter, and he climbed the ladder with the bitter one, which meant he'd brought her tea without being asked, which meant his hands remembered things his mind had lost, and Sefa had to look at the ledger for a moment until her face was steady.

"It's a ritual," she said. "Guilds are allowed rituals."

"What's the ritual?"

She weighed it. She had shown him the book once, early on, and watched him read his own entry with polite bafflement, like a man reading a stranger's eulogy. She hadn't shown him since. But he was here now, sitting on a crate, steam rising off both cups, patient in the way of people who had already given everything and were curious what was left.

"He's called Quentin Nystrom," she said. "He was our ledger-keeper. He heard the dead through bone, and he kept accounts for people who'd been erased, and when the toll came due he paid it with his name so the rest of us could pass. And somebody has to remember him, and the somebody is me, and I do it every morning, out loud, so that—" she faltered, then steadied, "—so that it can't be collected again."

Quentin was quiet for a while. Below them, the workshop clattered and murmured; Tam dropped something and blamed the cat, which did not exist.

"That's a good ritual," he said finally. "Very thorough accounting. He sounds like a man worth the ink."

"He'd argue with you about that."

"Would he? Sounds sensible to me."

"He'd say the guild mattered more than any one name in it." Sefa handed him back the bitter tea by mistake, realized, and swapped cups, and Quentin's mouth twitched like the error had told him something. "But he'd be wrong. I checked the arithmetic myself. One name, weighed against everyone he carried through—that's not a fair trade. It never was. We just let him believe it was because it was easier than watching him refuse."

"And you?" Quentin asked. "What do you believe?"

Sefa looked down at the ledger, at the growing column of small Tuesdays and bad jokes and harbor visits, at the name at the top of the page written in her stubborn looping hand.

"I believe remembering is a craft," she said. "Like carving. You don't get it right the first time. You keep working the material until something true comes loose."

Quentin drank his tea and looked out the little window at the unnamed country, at the roofs and chimneys and the gulls wheeling over water he was afraid of and visited anyway.

"Well," he said, "whoever he was, I hope somebody's keeping track of him."

"Somebody is," Sefa said. "It's going to take years. I've done the sums."

* * *

She added the entry that night by candlelight, after the workshop had gone quiet and the harbor bell rang its purposeless kind hour.

*Today he brought me tea without asking. He always makes mine wrong and then swaps the cups like I won't notice, which means some part of him knows exactly how I take it, which means the name isn't gone, it's only sleeping in a different ledger than the ones he kept.*

She paused, pen lifted, listening.

The old knucklebones had come across the border with them—smuggled openly, actually, because customs here consisted of a man named Pell who waved at boats. They sat in a dish on the sill, and most nights they said nothing, or said the ordinary weather of the dead, or recited pickling recipes.

Tonight, faintly, in a voice like bone dust settling, one of them spoke. Not a prayer. Not a warning. Just two words, approving, unhurried, the tone of an old craftsman inspecting an apprentice's joinery and finding it sound:

*"Well kept."*

Sefa smiled, blew out the candle, and in the dark said the name once more, softly, like locking a door against the night.

Somewhere below, the sea counted its waves. Somewhere beyond it, the gods kept what they'd collected. But here, in a loft over a workshop with its name on the door, in a book that anyone could read, one person remembered—and meant to go on remembering, morning after morning, for as long as it took.

Which, she had begun to suspect, looking at the way his hands moved and his mouth almost curved and his columns came out even, might not be forever at all.
