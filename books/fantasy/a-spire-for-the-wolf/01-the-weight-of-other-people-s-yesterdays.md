## 1. The Weight of Other People's Yesterdays

There were three hundred and eleven vials in the Mnemosyne Guild's counting room that autumn evening, and Noor Thackeray knew each of them by heart without remembering a single thing that had ever happened to her.

She was aware of the irony. She had been made aware of it, in fact, by Master Corvane himself, who enjoyed saying clever things twice so that his subordinates could laugh at them both times. *"You are a woman who keeps other people's yesterdays, Miss Thackeray, and has none of her own worth mentioning."* He'd said it four years ago at the winter audit, chuckling at his own wit while adjusting his silver-gray cuffs. The junior clerks had laughed. Noor had smiled the way one smiles at a stone: politely, and expecting nothing back.

The trouble was that he had been right, and everyone in the room had known it, and knowing it together had made it somehow official. It had gone into the ledger of her life — the invisible one, the one no one wrote in — under the heading *Noor Thackeray: keeper of accounts, unremarkable, unlikely to leave.*

She sat now at her desk in the corner of the counting room where the candlelight pooled like spilled honey and did not reach the high windows, cataloging the evening's intake. The Guild bought memories the way other merchants bought wool or salt: by weight, by quality, by the desperation of the seller. Grief sold heaviest. Joy sold light but fetched a better price per ounce, because joy was rare and nobles had discovered, somewhere in the last century, that they could purchase the *feeling* of a daughter's wedding without the inconvenience of ever having had a daughter.

Noor dipped her pen and wrote in her small, exact hand:

*Item 2,114. One vial, blue glass, wax sealed. Contents: summer rain on a tin roof, as experienced by a child of perhaps six. Seller: a debt-collector who wished to be rid of softness. Quality: excellent. Note: he wept while selling it and seemed relieved afterward.*

*Item 2,115. One vial, green glass. Contents: the taste of bread shared with a stranger during a famine year. Seller: an old soldier. Quality: worn thin, handled often. Note: he asked twice whether it would hurt the memory to sell it. I said yes. He sold it anyway.*

That last note she should not have written. Notes were for quality and provenance, not for sentiment. But the ledger had long since become the only place Noor allowed herself to say true things, and so the margins of Volume Forty-Seven were quietly crowded with observations no one would ever read: that the woman who sold her mother's funeral had laughed the entire transaction, horribly; that the young lord who purchased courage had hands that shook; that grief, bottled, looked exactly like starlight caught in oil, which was either beautiful or obscene depending on how late you had been working.

She worked until the candles burned down to their collars. She was good at this — unnervingly good, Corvane admitted whenever it suited him to admit anything. She could hold a vial up to the flame and read its contents from the way the light bent inside, the way a sailor reads weather in a rope. Memories had textures. Regret moved slowly, like sediment. Love shivered when the candle flickered, as if embarrassed to be observed. Hatred sat perfectly still and pretended it wasn't there.

Tonight's final crate held the day's stragglers: three vials in plain brown glass, the kind used for low-value stock. She lifted the first. A dog, barking at a mail carrier, forty years of Tuesdays compressed into one indignant afternoon. The second was a headache. Someone had paid the Guild to take away a *headache*, which struck Noor as cheating, somehow, though she couldn't have said why. You were meant to trade your yesterdays, not your this-mornings.

The third vial she almost didn't look at. It was small — smaller than standard, the glass cloudy, the wax seal cracked as though someone had opened it once and repented and closed it again.

She held it to the candle.

And stopped breathing.

Inside, the memory moved like nothing she had ever cataloged. It didn't shimmer or sink or sit still. It *sang*. There was no other word for it. The light passed through the liquid and came out the other side in slow, swelling pulses, and although the counting room was silent except for the tick of the case clock and the settling of the coals, Noor could feel a melody forming against her palms like warmth from a hearth she had stood beside once, in some life that wasn't hers, in a house she had never entered, held by arms she had never—

She set the vial down very carefully, the way one sets down something sleeping.

Her hands were not steady. That was the first strange thing. Her hands were always steady; steadiness was her entire personality, if she was honest, the way neatness is the whole soul of a ledger. The second strange thing was that her eyes were wet, and she had not authorized that either.

*A lullaby,* she thought. *It's only a lullaby.*

But it wasn't only anything. When she looked at it, a door opened somewhere behind her ribs — a door she had papered over so long ago she'd forgotten the papering, let alone the door. On the other side of that door was a voice. Low, unhurried, half-tuned, the way mothers sing when they think no one is listening and the day has finally gone quiet. Singing to someone small. Keeping watch over someone small.

It was not her mother's voice. Noor was certain of that, and the certainty itself was odd, because how would she know? Her mother had died when she was an infant — that was the story, the whole story, the beginning and end of the family chapter in the ledger of Noor Thackeray. Died, and left her to an aunt whose name she also could not recall clearly, and then to the Guild's orphan clerks, and then to this desk, this candle, these three hundred and eleven yesterdays that belonged to everyone but her.

So it was not her mother's lullaby. Obviously. It belonged to whoever had sold it — item 2,117, seller unknown, provenance unstated — and Noor Thackeray was a professional, and professionals did not stand in candlelight at half past eleven weeping over inventory.

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way one deals with weather.

*Quality: exceptional,* she wrote. *Note: lullaby, maternal origin. Handle with—*

The pen hovered.

*Handle with care.* True, but not permitted. She crossed it out with two clean strokes and wrote instead: *Handle per standard procedure.*

Then she sat there for a long moment with the crossed-out words staring up at her like a confession.

* *

The rules of the counting room were simple and had been posted on the door since before Noor's time, in Corvane's own looping script: *Inventory is sacred. Inventory is neutral. What enters the vault belongs to the Guild; what belongs to the Guild belongs to no one.*

Clerks did not touch stock beyond what cataloging required. Clerks certainly did not *open* stock, though every clerk Noor had ever known had, at least once, held a vial a moment too long — breathed against the cool glass, imagined the yesterday inside. It was understood the way small sins are understood in any workplace: universally committed, never named.

Pocketing stock was another matter entirely. Pocketing stock was theft from the god of forgetting himself, and the punishment was not dismissal. The punishment was that Corvane took your memories — all of them, neatly, legally, with your signature on the bill of sale — and set you out on the street with a head full of fog, unable to remember why you were crying.

Noor knew all this. She could recite it. She had, in her twenty-six years, never broken so much as the rule about running in the corridors.

So it came as a genuine surprise to her when, some minutes later, she found that the lullaby vial was no longer on the table, and that her right hand was in her apron pocket, and that through the wool she could feel the small hard shape of it against her hip like a second heartbeat.

She had no memory of deciding. That was the unsettling part — she, who remembered everything, who kept the ledgers, whose whole identity was the careful recording of what went where. Somewhere between writing *standard procedure* and blowing out the third candle, her body had staged a small revolution and neglected to inform her.

*I'll put it back,* she told herself. *First thing tomorrow. Before the morning count. No harm done.*

Her hand did not move toward the table.

The truth — and Noor was, above all things, an honest bookkeeper, even in her own head — the truth was that she wanted it. Not the way one wants cake or rest or revenge, which are wants with reasons attached. She wanted it the way a locked room wants wind. Something in her leaned toward that little clouded vial like a plant leaning at a window, and no part of that leaning consulted her first.

*It's a mother's lullaby,* she reasoned with herself, walking the long dark corridor toward the dormitory wing, her footsteps swallowed by carpet worn thin by other people's important business. *Some mother sang it to some child, and the child grew up and fell on hard times, and the Guild bought the song for six coppers, and someday some lonely noblewoman will pay sixty gold to feel it for an hour and call it love. That's the trade. That's the world. I keep the accounts of the world.*

*And I want it anyway.*

She stopped walking.

The corridor stretched ahead, dark and orderly. Behind her, the counting room door stood shut, and beyond it the vault where three hundred and ten vials slept in their velvet slots, each one somebody's worst or best hour, all of them safe, all of them counted, all of them *neutral*.

In her pocket, the lullaby hummed. Or she imagined it hummed. Or some door behind her ribs had come open and wouldn't close again no matter how much paper she pressed against it.

"Sixty gold," she said aloud, to nobody, and her voice sounded strange in the empty hall — younger than she expected, less certain. "Sixty gold, and I'd steal it for nothing."

*Not for nothing,* said something in her, in a voice that was her own and yet pitched lower, warmer, like the singing. *For me.*

Noor Thackeray stood in the dark corridor of the Mnemosyne Guild, invisible as always, unremarkable as always, and committed — quietly, irrevocably, in the manner of a person signing a ledger line — her first theft.

She walked on. The vial rode against her hip all the way to her narrow room, all the way through her cold supper of bread and cheese, all the way through the washing of her one good dress and the banking of the fire. She hid it in the flour jar, which was absurd because Noor Thackeray baked perhaps twice a year, and then she moved it to the hollow under the floorboard by the bed, and then she took it out again and put it under her pillow, because apparently she was now the sort of person who slept with stolen lullabies, and there was nothing to be done about it.

She lay in the dark listening to the Guild settle around her — the pipes ticking, the night clerk's cough two floors up, the vast patient silence of three centuries of secrets kept in glass.

From beneath her pillow, faint as breath, the lullaby rose and fell. Rose and fell. And just before sleep took her, Noor realized she was humming along — perfectly, note for note, without hesitation, without ever having heard the song before in her life.

She woke with the certainty that she had missed something, the way you wake when a sound stopped just before you noticed it.

The bell. Someone was ringing the Guild's night bell, the iron one by the service door that was only rung in emergencies, and it was still dark, and the vial under her pillow had gone strangely warm.

Noor dressed in the dark, tucked the lullaby deep into the flour jar where even she might forget to look, and went downstairs with her ledger-clerk's face already on — calm, neutral, unremarkable — past clerks stumbling into corridors in their nightshirts, toward the yellow smear of lamplight around the service door.

Master Corvane was already there, fully dressed, which was its own small alarm. And on the flagstones between him and the door lay a courier's satchel, torn open and empty.

"Miss Thackeray," Corvane said pleasantly, without turning around. "Wake the archivist. It seems we've lost an inventory."

* * *

By the time the bells of the dawn shift rang, Noor had learned three things, none of which she wrote down.

First: the missing courier was called Wren Halloway, senior grade, trusted, unmarked, the sort who carried only the most valuable stock. Second: Wren Halloway had failed to make her delivery round two nights running, and the Retrievers had found her lodging emptied, her route ledger burned, and blood on the stair — her own, by the scent-splints, and a great deal of it. Third: Master Corvane was not frightened, because Master Corvane was never frightened, but he had ordered the gates watched and the ledgers pulled, and when he said *ledgers*, his eyes had rested on Noor for exactly one heartbeat longer than pleasant.

"A thief inside our walls," he'd said to the assembled clerks, smiling his audit-day smile, the one that reached his mouth and stopped there like a visitor refused entry further. "Someone carrying what isn't theirs. Do any of you know anything about carrying what isn't yours?"

Thirty clerks had looked at their shoes. Noor had looked at her shoes. Inside her, below the steady drumbeat of terror, something else pulsed — small, warm, absurdly serene — and she thought of the flour jar, and the song under the flour, and the door behind her ribs standing open in the wind.

She walked home through streets going gray with morning, past shuttered stalls and the lamplighter snuffing the last of the wicks, and told herself the day could not get stranger.

Which was when she saw the blood on her own doorstep, still wet, leading inward.
