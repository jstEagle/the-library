## 2. The Courier at the Door

Noor Thackeray's lodgings sat above a chandler's shop on Wick Lane, which meant two things: her rooms always smelled faintly of tallow and honey, and she never once had to stumble home in the dark. The chandler, a widow named Mrs. Abernathy with forearms like ship rope and opinions to match, kept the street lamps outside burning until Noor was safely up the stairs, though she'd never once said so aloud. She simply left them lit and grumbled about the cost of oil where Noor could hear her, which in the grammar of Mrs. Abernathy amounted to a declaration of love.

It was, Noor reflected as she climbed those stairs that evening, the closest thing to tenderness in her life. Twelve years in the counting room of the Mnemosyne Guild, cataloging other people's yesterdays by candlelight, and her nearest thing to family was a woman who expressed affection through complaints about lamp oil.

The vial was still in her pocket.

She hadn't taken it out once during the walk home, but she felt it there the whole way, a small cold weight against her thigh, like a coin she'd stolen or a stone she'd swallowed. *A mother's lullaby, hummed twice.* She had no idea why she wanted it. That was the trouble. Wanting things — wanting anything — was not a habit she permitted herself. Wanting led to reaching, and reaching led to being noticed, and being noticed, for a woman whose entire survival rested on being forgettable, was very nearly fatal.

She let herself into her rooms, hung her coat on the peg — the third hook from the left, because the first two were decorative and she was nothing if not consistent — and lit the single lamp on her table. The room was small and neat as an audit: one bed, one chair, one table, one window looking down on Wick Lane's crooked rooftops. A shelf of blank ledgers she'd bought at discount, waiting for a life with enough in it to record.

She set the vial on the table and looked at it the way one looks at a letter one is afraid to open.

"Oh, don't be absurd," she told herself, in the brisk tone she used for such conversations. "You're tired. You counted four hundred and six memories today and your eyes are crossing. Sleep, and tomorrow you'll return it to the vault and log the discrepancy yourself, and no one will ever—"

Someone knocked on her door.

At half past nine, on Wick Lane, at Noor Thackeray's door specifically — this was so far outside the ordinary run of events that her first thought was that it couldn't be happening. Her second thought was that Mrs. Abernathy had finally come up to complain about something. Her third thought arrived at the same moment as the sound of a body sliding down the other side of the door, all fabric and weight and a soft, terrible thump.

Noor opened the door, and the stranger more or less fell into her arms.

He was young — younger than her, certainly, twenty perhaps, with courier's leathers gone dark with rain and something else, something thicker than rain. His face was pale as ledger paper. There was blood on his collar, his cuffs, his hands, everywhere she looked, and when she got an arm around him she felt how wet his back was through the leather, and understood.

"Don't," he said. "Don't shout. Please."

"I'm not going to shout," Noor said, and found, to her mild astonishment, that it was true. Some deep-buried part of her — some strata of self she hadn't known she possessed — had simply taken over, calm as a clerk balancing a column. "I'm going to help you inside, and then you're going to tell me who did this."

"Stairs were too much," he said, which she took for an answer to none of it. "Couldn't climb to the Guild. They'll be watching the Guild."

"You came here instead?" She got him onto her bed, which immediately ruined the neatness of it beyond repair, and thought, absurdly, *Mrs. Abernathy will have opinions about the mattress.* "Why? Who are you?"

"Wick Lane." He said it like it explained everything, and for a horrible moment Noor feared he was delirious. Then his eyes focused on her with sudden, awful clarity, and he added: "Your name. It's written on the route. Wick Lane, third house past the chandler, top floor, the woman who keeps the ledgers."

"The Guild sent you to me?"

"The Guild wants you dead." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a wet cough that spattered red across his knuckles. "Or Corvane does. Same thing now, maybe. Listen. Listen, I haven't got long and I've been rehearsing this the whole way across the city and I refuse to die before I've said my piece, that would be unforgivable."

Noor pressed her hands against his back, which was the only first aid she knew, and which she suspected was roughly as useful as pressing hands against a sinking boat. Blood welled up between her fingers, warm and shocking. She had handled a thousand bottled memories today. She had held grief and joy and a mother's lullaby in glass, and none of it had ever felt like this — this hot, this immediate, this unstoppable.

"My piece," the courier repeated. He fumbled at his breast pocket. Noor helped him, because his fingers wouldn't work, and drew out a package wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with grey wax stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize: a spiral, coiling inward, like water going down a drain.

"What is it?"

"A map." He closed her fingers around it with both of his bloody ones, and his grip was stronger than she expected. "The Sunken Spire. The drowned way in. Nobody living has the route except me and one other person, and the other person is Vess Corvane, so you can see the arithmetic."

"I don't understand any of this."

"No," he agreed, gently, almost kindly. "But you will. That's rather the point of you."

*The point of you.* The words went through Noor like a splinter finding its depth. Before she could ask — before she even knew what she'd ask — he caught her wrist.

"He'll send Retrievers. Not tonight, they don't work at night — lamplighters burn what they need, superstitious lot — but at dawn. Maybe sooner if he's frightened, and he is frightened, Noor Thackeray, he is more frightened than he has been in three hundred years." His voice was going quieter, each word costing him visibly. "Take the map. Go under. Find people the Guild doesn't watch. Lamplighter, if you can find one — Quinn, Sable Quinn, ask anyone who lights lamps badly. And the child, if the child finds you first, feed her and don't ask questions."

"This is insane," Noor whispered. "I keep books. I count other people's memories and file them alphabetically. I am nobody. I have made myself nobody very carefully for twelve years, and you've come to the wrong—"

"Vess Corvane killed me an hour ago," the courier said, "and I am spending my last hour arguing with a woman about whether she matters. Isn't that just like the Guild. Isn't that exactly like us."

She stopped arguing. Partly because his voice was failing. Mostly because of what he said next.

"He'll tell you that you're no one. He's been telling you that since before you could talk. It's the first lie they ever wrote in your ledger, Noor, and it's the only one you ever believed." He smiled, and it cracked something open behind his eyes, some exhaustion and relief tangled together, the relief of a man setting down a weight carried too many miles. "But your name isn't Noor Thackeray."

"I—" Her mouth was dry. "Of course it is. I've signed it ten thousand times. I sign it every morning in the attendance ledger, I sign it in triplicate—"

"That's the cage," he said. "Not the bird."

And then, with the last of his breath, with his hand still closed around her wrist like a man passing judgment or a blessing, he said:

"*Yevunneh Ashkain.*"

The name went into Noor like cold water into a burn.

She had never heard it before. She was certain of that — certain the way she was certain of sums, certain the way she was certain of the alphabet, and she was the most certain person alive. She had never heard it, and yet it *landed*, it settled somewhere behind her ribs with the unmistakable rightness of a key turning in a lock she hadn't known her chest contained. Yevunneh Ashkain. Four syllables, and every one of them hers, the way a bone is yours, the way a heartbeat is yours: without argument, without paperwork, without any signature at all.

The courier watched her face while it happened. Whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy him completely.

"There she is," he murmured. And died.

* * *

For a long time — she would never know how long; five minutes, an hour — Noor Thackeray sat on the edge of her own bed beside a dead man and did not move.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, Mrs. Abernathy snuffed the street lamps, one by one, and Wick Lane went dark, and still Noor sat, holding a map she dared not unwrap and a name she dared not say again, listening to her own heart do its stubborn, unremarkable work.

Here was the shape of her life, laid out suddenly and horribly plain: twelve years of perfect, invisible routine, built brick by brick out of small consistencies. Third hook from the left. Attendance in triplicate. Never late, never noticed, never asked to dinner, never asked for anything at all. She had thought of it as safety. Sitting there with blood drying on her hands, she understood it for what it had actually been: a hiding place. And hiding places only work when no one knows where you are.

Someone had known. Someone had known enough to write her address on a courier's route in dying-man's handwriting, and someone else — Corvane, the courier said, *Corvane wants you dead* — had known it too.

She looked down at the courier's face. In death he looked younger still, and faintly pleased, like a man who had finished a difficult sum and gotten the answer he expected.

"I don't even know your name," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the little room, roughened, like it belonged to someone with a far more eventful life. "You knew mine. Both of mine, apparently. And I don't know yours."

The room, naturally, offered no reply.

So she did what Noor Thackeray did best. She organized.

It took until nearly midnight. The chandler's cellar had a coal hatch opening onto the alley behind the shop, and Mrs. Abernathy slept like the dead on the far side of the house, and Noor — slight, methodical, unnoticed Noor — managed the whole grim business with a rolled carpet, a wheelbarrow borrowed without asking and returned dripping, and a shovel she found leaning in the alley as if left there for precisely this purpose, which she chose not to think about. She buried him in the disused ground behind the tannery, where the earth had seen worse, and said over him the only words she could find:

"You knew my name. I hope somebody knows yours."

Then she went home, scrubbed the blood from her hands and her floorboards, remade the ruined bed with military precision, hung her coat on the third hook from the left, and sat down at her table across from the oilcloth package, and allowed herself, at last, to be terrified.

Because here was the trouble with a carefully sealed life: seal it well enough, for long enough, and the smallest crack lets in a flood.

She unwrapped the map with steady hands. Grey wax split cleanly under her thumbnail — the spiral sigil, coiling inward, water going down a drain — and the oilcloth opened around a sheet of cured hide, dense with ink. Streets she knew. Streets she didn't. And there beneath the river's blue hatching, rendered in faded lines like veins under skin: the drowned quarter, and the Sunken Spire, and a route threading between them marked in a careful hand, annotated in the margins in the same script.

One annotation, near the Spire itself, circled twice:

*She won't remember why it opens for her. Tell her anyway.*

Noor sat very still. Below her floorboards, Mrs. Abernathy turned over in her sleep. Above the rooftops, unseen, the moon was climbing toward dawn, and dawn, the courier had said, was when the Retrievers came.

*Yevunneh Ashkain,* said something deep in her chest, quiet as a lullaby, patient as a lock.

"Noor Thackeray signs the attendance ledger in triplicate," she said to the empty room, and reached for her coat, and a satchel, and the small cold vial she had never returned to the vault.

By the time the sky went grey, the third hook from the left hung empty, and Wick Lane's most invisible woman was gone.
