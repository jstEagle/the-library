## 3. Ledgers and Lies

The Mnemosyne Guild opened its doors at the seventh bell, the same as it had for three hundred years, and Noor Thackeray was there before them, as she had been every morning for six of those years.

She let herself in through the counting room's side entrance with her own key—the only key she'd ever been trusted with—and stood a moment in the dark, breathing the familiar smell of tallow smoke, old paper, and the faint copper tang that clung to everything in a building where memories were bottled by the gross. Most people found the smell unsettling. Noor had long ago stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. It was simply the smell of *here*, and *here* was the only place she had ever been certain of anything.

She lit the lamps one by one. There were eleven of them along the counting room's length, each with a green glass shade to keep the light from spoiling the vials on their shelves, and she lit them in order, left to right, because order was the whole architecture of her life. Ledger open. Pen inked. Blotter squared against the desk's edge. The day could now begin, and nothing in it would surprise her.

That was the arrangement she had made with the world, more or less. She would be precise, punctual, unremarkable, and in exchange the world would leave her alone. It was not a bad bargain, as bargains went. She had seen what the Guild charged people for worse ones.

By half past the seventh bell the clerks were trickling in—Marrow with his cough, Odile with her gossip, young Fenwick who still flinched when a vial chimed against its neighbors—and Noor was already three pages deep into yesterday's reconciliations. Twelve bottles of a fisherman's first storm, sold to a merchant's son who wanted to feel brave without the drowning. One bottle of a wedding, returned unsatisfied by the bride herself, who said the memory had gone flat "like wine kept past its grief." Noor catalogued the return, noted the refund, and did not wonder—as she once might have—what it meant to outlive your own happiness.

She had gotten very good at not wondering. It was, if she was honest, her finest professional skill.

The lullaby vial sat in her apron pocket. She had checked four times since waking that it was still there, which was absurd, because where would it go? But her fingers kept finding the curve of the glass through the fabric, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. A mother's voice, singing to a child in a language the donor hadn't spoken since girlhood. Noor had told herself she'd taken it to catalogue properly in the morning—a labeling error, the seal number transposed, an honest clerk's correction. Both halves of that sentence were lies, and she knew it, and knowing it made her feel like a stranger wearing her own hands.

She was mid-entry—*Item 4471, joy of a first snowfall, provenance verified*—when the counting room went quiet in a particular way.

Noor knew all the ways a room could go quiet. There was the quiet of a dropped bottle, followed by shouting. There was the quiet of closing time, gradual and comfortable as a tide going out. And then there was this: the quiet that happened when Master Vess Corvane came down the stairs.

He didn't announce himself. He never did. He simply appeared among the shelves the way weather appears, and the clerks bent lower over their work, and the lamps seemed to burn a shade greener, and Noor felt the familiar cold settle into her shoulders like a well-fitted coat.

"Thackeray."

"Master Corvane." She rose, as protocol demanded, and inclined her head precisely the correct amount. Not so little as to seem proud; not so much as to seem afraid. She had practiced the angle years ago in a cracked mirror and had used it perhaps two hundred times since.

Corvane was not a large man. People expected him to be, and were always faintly disappointed, as though the Guild's master ought to come with more architecture about him. He was instead narrow and neat, with silver at his temples arranged as carefully as everything else about him, and eyes the color of pond water over stone—you couldn't see the bottom, but you knew there wasn't any mud either. He wore kindness the way other men wore jewelry. Beautifully. Never once convincingly.

"I'm told," he said, drifting along the shelf of recent acquisitions, trailing one finger just above the vials without touching them, "that we are short a courier."

"We have forty-one couriers on roster, Master Corvane."

"And yesterday we had forty-two." He smiled at a bottle of someone's childhood dog. "Wren Ovid. Do you know him?"

Noor's pen was still in her hand. She set it down across the ledger's spine with great care, aligning it with the edge, because her hands wanted very much to do something else and she could not allow them.

"No, sir. I don't know the couriers. They don't come through the counting room."

"Mm." He picked up a vial now—actually touched it, which made Fenwick across the room go rigid—and held it to the green lamplight. "No, I suppose they don't. You keep such tidy accounts, Thackeray. I sometimes think you're the only person in this building who knows exactly where everything is." He turned the vial. Light moved inside it like something swimming. "Everything," he repeated, softly, as if tasting the word.

There it was. That was the thing about Corvane that six years of careful invisibility had taught her: he never asked a question he didn't already know some answer to. His questions weren't questions. They were doors he held open to see whether you'd walk through.

"Wren Ovid missed his evening delivery," Corvane continued, setting the vial back in its exact former position—Noor noted, with a small mad part of her mind, that he got it right to the finger's width. "His lodgings are empty. His route book is missing. And no one at the Guild has seen him since the dusk bell." He looked at her then, directly, with all the warmth of a man asking after a mutual friend's health. "You left early yesterday, I understand. Unusual for you."

"My head ached, sir."

"Of course. And on your way home?"

"On my way home I went home." *Home.* The word sat wrong in her mouth, because her lodgings above the chandler's shop had never been home, they were the place where she stored her body overnight, but she had never before needed the distinction to hold weight. "I took willow bark and slept until morning."

"You didn't pass anyone in the street? See anything unusual? The undercity has been restless lately. Retrievers report strangers moving through the drowned quarter." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man remarks on weather he intends to make use of later. "A courier goes missing the same night our steadiest keeper takes to her bed early. Coincidence is such an untidy thing in ledgers, don't you find? I do hate untidy things."

Here it was, then. The door, held open.

Noor had lied before, of course—in small social ways, the way everyone does. *I'm fine. I didn't notice. What a lovely hat.* But she had never lied to Corvane, and she suspected, in the place where her heartbeat lived, that lying to Corvane was a different category of act entirely, the way a kitchen fire and a house fire are both called fire.

And yet.

She thought of Wren Ovid's blood on her floorboards, scrubbed twice and still ghosting pink in the grain. She thought of the map beneath her mattress, wax seal unbroken, heavier than any object she owned. She thought of the name he'd whispered with his last breath—a name that was not Noor Thackeray, a name that had settled into her chest like a coal and had not cooled since—and she understood, with terrible clarity, that the truth would kill her faster than any lie.

So she lied.

"I'm sorry about the courier, sir. But I saw nothing. I was asleep by the eighth bell and woke at dawn." Her voice came out level. Steady. Almost bored. Somewhere inside her, a girl who had spent six years being furniture screamed *what are you doing*, and Noor stepped on the scream the way you step on a spark.

Corvane regarded her for a long moment. The lamp between them hissed. Across the room, Marrow's quill scratched and stopped and scratched again, a small nervous metronome.

"Well," Corvane said at last, and his smile widened by a degree that somehow contained multitudes. "Then I'm sure it's nothing. Couriers are careless creatures. They lose themselves the way they lose packages." He began to move toward the stairs, then paused, one hand on the banister, and spoke without turning around. "Oh—one more thing. The inventory. Have you completed the audit of the sealed archive?"

"The sealed archive is restricted, sir. I've never had cause to enter it."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. But you count everything else so faithfully. I'd like the sealed archive counted too. Beginning tomorrow. Consider it a promotion of sorts." He glanced back over his shoulder, and the pond-water eyes caught the lamplight and gave nothing back. "Unless something about that troubles you?"

Every instinct she possessed rang like a struck bell. *Say yes. Say of course. Say thank you.*

"Nothing troubles me, sir," said Noor Thackeray, liar. "I'll begin tomorrow."

"Splendid." He climbed two steps, stopped again—Corvane was a man who believed in arriving at conclusions slowly—and added, almost as an afterthought, in a tone of genuine, terrible gentleness:

"Do take care on your walk home tonight, Thackeray. The city's been strange lately. People see things in the dark that aren't there. And other people—" the smile, over his shoulder, "—see things that are."

Then he was gone up the stairs, and the counting room exhaled, and the clerks bent back to their work with the studied casualness of people pretending a wolf had not just walked through the sheepfold.

* * *

Noor lasted until the noon bell before her composure finished dying.

She managed it through sheer routine: the morning deliveries logged, the returns shelved, Odile's gossip about the Retriever captain's divorce absorbed and discarded without retention. She even ate her lunch—bread and hard cheese at her desk, as always, crumbs brushed into her palm and deposited in the bin, as always. It was only when she reached for the ink to begin the afternoon entries that she noticed her hand was shaking, and the shaking had a rhythm to it, and the rhythm spelled out the shape of what she'd done.

*I'll begin tomorrow,* she had said. The sealed archive. The one part of the Guild she had never been permitted to see, in six years of seeing everything else. He was giving her access the day after a courier died whispering secrets in her arms. Either Corvane had lost his mind—and Noor would have bet the building he hadn't—or he was setting a trap with her name on the bait.

Or.

Or he already knew. Or he had known since the dusk bell yesterday, and this entire gentle morning had been theater, and every hour she spent here being precise and punctual and unremarkable was an hour spent standing in a river while the water rose.

She thought about Wren Ovid. She had known him after all—not by name, but by face; he'd delivered to the counting room a dozen times, a quick-footed young man with ink-stained cuffs who'd once complimented her handwriting and meant it. He had run, bleeding, through half the city to reach *her*, of all people, and pressed a map into her hands, and died saying a name that lived behind her ribs like a second heart.

*They'll come for the vessel,* he'd said. *They always come for the vessel.*

She had assumed he meant the map.

Sitting in the candlelit counting room with her perfect ledgers and her stolen lullaby, Noor admitted to herself, slowly and with the sensation of pulling a splinter from her own palm, that she had never once believed that.

She closed the ledger. Squared the blotter. Capped the ink. Around her, the Guild hummed its ordinary hum, and somewhere upstairs Vess Corvane was doing whatever Vess Corvane did with his afternoons, and none of it—none of the tallow smell or the green lamps or the two hundred rehearsed inclinations of her head—felt like safety anymore. It felt like a cage she had mistaken for a shape, all these years, because it fit so well.

Noor Thackeray gathered her shawl, her satchel, and the lullaby in her pocket, and walked out of the Mnemosyne Guild at half past noon, breaking a schedule she had kept for six years without fail.

Behind her, in the counting room, Fenwick watched her go and reached for the bell-pull that rang in the master's study.

Ahead of her, the streets of the undercity waited, crowded and dim and full of corners, and Noor had one thought, repeating with every footstep like a pulse:

*Don't go home. Don't go home. Don't go home.*

It was, she reflected grimly, ducking into the first alley she found, the first genuinely sensible plan she'd had in days. Which meant, given how the week was going, that someone was probably already waiting there anyway.
