## 4. The Lamplighter's Debt

The lamps of the undercity were Sable Quinn's to light, and had been for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once been thanked.

He didn't mind. Or rather — he minded, but he'd made a comfortable little house out of the minding and lived in it. It had a hearth of routine and windows that looked out on nothing much, and it kept the rain off the part of him that still remembered wanting things.

That was the trouble with selling a decade of your memories to the Mnemosyne Guild: you got paid well enough to stop being hungry, but you never quite knew what you'd traded away. The Guild's brokers had been very professional about it. *Ten years, sir, cleanly excised, no ragged edges.* They'd let him choose which decade, which was like asking a man which finger he'd prefer to lose. He'd chosen the one from seventeen to twenty-seven, on the theory that those were the years he could best afford to be without.

He had since revised this theory. Those were, it turned out, the years a man learned who he was. Sable had skipped straight from a gawky apprentice with his mother's laugh to a lamplighter of thirty-odd with a hole where his heart's history should have been, and he'd spent the years since trying to fill the hole with work.

Work was good. Work had rules. You climbed the ladder, you trimmed the wick, you fed the lamp its oil, you struck the match. The undercity ran on six hundred and forty-one lamps — he'd counted twice, because counting was something his hands remembered how to love — and every dusk he walked his rounds and lit them, and every dawn he walked them again and put them out, and in between he slept in a room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and old rain.

It was on the dusk round, at lamp four hundred and twelve — the crooked one outside the fishmonger's on Drownlane, the one that leaned like a drunk leaning on a friend — that he saw her.

A woman, running.

Now, people ran through the undercity all the time. They ran from creditors and constables and mothers-in-law, and Sable had long ago developed the professional courtesy of pretending not to see any of it. But there was running and there was *running*, and this woman was doing the second kind. She ran the way a hare runs when the hawk's shadow has already touched its back — flat-out, ears back, no thought in it but *away*.

She came around the corner of Brackish Row with her skirts hiked in both fists, took the steps down to Drownlane three at a time, and slammed shoulder-first into the very lamp post Sable was standing beside.

"Oof," said the woman.

"Beg pardon," said Sable, who hadn't moved, and then, because she looked up at him with the wild eyes of something cornered, added, "You're alright?"

"No," she said, with great precision, as though he'd asked her a question on an examination paper and she intended to get full marks for honesty even if it failed her. "No, I'm not."

She pushed off the post and kept going. And Sable would have let her go — truly, he'd have stood there trimming his wick and minding his own considerable business — except that as she passed, the light of his half-lit lamp caught her face, and something in his chest went off like a dropped lantern.

Not recognition. He didn't know her. That was the whole cursed problem; he didn't know half the people he ought to. But some door in him that had been painted shut for eleven years blew suddenly open, and a wind came through it carrying a smell — tallow and rain and someone saying *thank you* in a voice like hers — and Sable Quinn heard himself say:

"Wait."

She didn't wait. Of course she didn't wait. She went down the lane like water finding a drain, and Sable stood there holding his lighting pole like a fool, listening to the door in his chest creak on its hinges.

*Thank you,* said the memory-that-wasn't-a-memory. *Thank you, lamplighter.*

He'd sold ten years. Whatever lived in that gap had known this woman, or someone with her voice, or someone who owed her, or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome — someone she'd saved.

Sable looked up at lamp four hundred and twelve, which was now lit and doing its modest best against the dark, and then down the empty lane, and made the first decision in eleven years that wasn't on his rounds.

He snuffed the lamp and followed her.

* * *

The undercity after dark belonged to other people. Sable knew this the way a dog knows property lines. By day it was his — the lamps, the lanes, the ladder-scars on his palms — but by night the drowned quarter's alleys filled with the kind of folk who preferred not to be lit, and a big man with a lighting pole was neither invisible nor welcome.

He tracked her anyway. Not by skill — he had none — but by damage. She left a trail like a startled pigeon: a knocked-over crate of eels (the eels were fine; they were enjoying themselves), a torn scrap of grey wool snagged on a nail, a beggar on Tallow Steps pointing wordlessly downward with the serene authority of a man who charged a copper per direction and had been paid.

He caught up with her at the mouth of the Weep, where the undercity's oldest stairwell descended into the flooded tunnels, and where she had stopped — finally stopped — pressed against the wet stone, breathing like a bellows with a grudge.

"I'm not going to hurt you," Sable said, from a respectful distance, hands visible, pole lowered. "I just want to say something, and then I'll leave you alone, and we'll both pretend this never happened, which is my usual policy regarding everything."

Her head turned. In the dark her eyes were the color of ledger ink, and they went over him with the quick arithmetic of a woman deciding what he'd cost her.

"You're the lamplighter," she said. "From Drownlane."

"You remember me?"

"No." A pause. "I remember everyone. It's a professional failing. I keep accounts." She straightened, and he watched her assemble composure the way he assembled a lamp — piece by piece, wick and glass and flame, until it stood and gave light whether or not there was anything behind it. "Did you follow me?"

"Yes," Sable admitted. "And I want to be clear that I don't do this. Following people. Talking to people. Last week I had a conversation with a cat and it was the highlight of my month, and the cat started it."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile, or it might have been indigestion. "Why did you follow me?"

And here was the thing Sable had rehearsed the whole way down Brackish Row, the speech about how she seemed troubled and the undercity was dangerous at night and a citizen should help a citizen, and every word of it evaporated now, because she was looking at him with those ink-dark eyes and lying to her felt like writing in someone else's ledger.

"I owe you," he said. "Or someone does. Someone I used to be."

Her breath caught — small, but he heard it. "What do you mean?"

"I sold ten years of my memory to the Guild. When I was young and stupid and needed the money more than I needed myself." He turned the lighting pole in his hands, watching the brass fittings catch the distant glow of lamp four hundred and thirteen, which he had, he realized, abandoned mid-round for the first time in eleven years. "I don't know what's in those years. But just now, when you ran past me, something in me knew you. Not my head. Lower down. The part of a man that remembers even after the Guild has tidied it away." He shrugged, feeling enormous and awkward, a wardrobe trying to apologize. "So whoever I was, he owed you a debt. And I'd like to pay it. Because—" and here his voice did something embarrassing, so he pushed through it, "—because I've spent eleven years wondering what kind of man could look at ten whole years of his own life and think, *that, I can live without.* And I've decided he must have owed somebody something terrible, and run from paying it, and sold the years so he wouldn't have to remember running."

The woman was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere below them, in the Weep, water dripped with the patience of stone.

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me," she said at last, "and I work for the Guild, so the competition is stiff."

"You work for—" Sable's stomach dropped through the floor, through the stairs, into the flooded dark below. "The *Guild?*"

"I keep their ledgers." Her chin came up, defiant, as though daring him to make something of it. "I count bottles. I don't make them."

"A clerk," Sable said faintly. "The Guild sent a clerk."

"The Guild didn't send me anywhere." And then she flinched, glancing over her shoulder at the black throat of the stairwell, and all the composure she'd built flickered like a bad wick. "They're looking for me. There was a courier. He's dead. He gave me something before he died, and Master Corvane will want it, and Corvane doesn't send clerks for things he wants. He sends Retrievers."

Sable had seen a Retriever exactly once, from a distance, and had gone home and drunk himself to sleep over it, which for Sable was a significant event involving two full glasses.

"Right," he said. "Right. Well." He rubbed his face with a hand rough as lamp-brass. "There's a place. Down past the Weep, third landing, behind the old pump-house. A woman named Odji lets rooms to people who don't ask questions, mostly because she charges extra for answers. I can take you there. It's not far, and I know which stairs are missing."

"Why?" she said. Simply, directly, the way she seemed to do everything. "You don't know me. Your debt might not even be mine. Whoever you were, whatever he owed — it could have been to someone else entirely. Someone with a similar voice."

"It could," Sable agreed. "But here's the thing about a debt you can't remember. You spend your whole life feeling it, like a stone in the boot, and you can't take the boot off because you don't know which foot it's on. Eleven years I've felt it. Tonight the stone moved." He met her eyes. "Let me pay something toward it. Even if it's the wrong account. Call it a deposit."

The woman studied him for a long moment, and Sable had the distinct sensation of being entered into a column somewhere, weighed and totaled.

"Noor," she said finally. "My name is Noor Thackeray."

"Noor Thackeray," he repeated, and the name went into him strangely, like a key into a lock that had been rusted shut — not opening anything, exactly, but *fitting*, which was somehow worse. "Sable Quinn. Lamplighter, fourth district. Six hundred and forty-one lamps, and I'll thank you to remember that number, because it's the only impressive thing about me."

"Six hundred and forty-one," Noor Thackeray repeated, and he could see her filing it, and absurdly, that pleased him.

Then her head snapped toward the stairwell, and every line of her went taut as wire.

Footsteps. Below. More than one set, unhurried, the particular unhurried tread of people who are paid to be unhurried.

"They're already here," she breathed. "How are they already—"

"How many?" Sable whispered.

"Three. Maybe four. Heavy boots. Lanterns with shutters — I can hear the slats."

Retrievers. Sable's mouth went dry as snuffed wick. Every sensible instinct he owned — and he owned several, kept them in a drawer, rarely used — told him to step back, tip his hat, return to lamp four hundred and thirteen, and let the Guild collect its runaway clerk in peace.

Instead he heard himself say, in a low, calm voice he barely recognized as his own: "Third landing's no good anymore. There's a way down through the pump-house cellar — old maintenance crawl, runs under the Weep, comes up by the tanneries. Smells like sin and lasts about forty minutes, but Retrievers won't fit. Most of them." He was already moving, pulling a stub of candle and a striker from his coat. "Can you run quietly?"

"I can do anything quietly," said Noor Thackeray. "I've been invisible for thirty years."

"Lucky you," said Sable Quinn, striking the candle alight, "I've been unforgettable for eleven and it's done me no good at all. This way. And madam — when we're through this, you and me are going to sit down with a pot of tea, and you're going to tell me everything the Guild ever wrote down about a lamplighter named Sable Quinn. Because I've got a feeling—" the stone in his boot shifted, settled, pointed down into the dark, "—it's a longer entry than they told me."

Behind them, the shuttered lanterns came up the stairs, patient as tide.
