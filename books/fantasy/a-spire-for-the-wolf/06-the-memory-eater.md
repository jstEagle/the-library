## 6. The Memory-Eater

There were three things Sable Quinn knew for certain about the undercity. First: never trust a door that opens before you knock. Second: the mushroom sellers on Gutter Row watered their stock with worse than water. Third: if a child watched you from a rooftop for longer than ten seconds, you were already in trouble.

The child had been watching them for four minutes.

"Is that normal?" Noor whispered. They were pressed into the shadow of a collapsed aqueduct, and Sable was doing his best impression of a man who belonged there, which mostly involved slouching and looking disappointed.

"Pip?" He glanced up at the small shape crouched on the broken archway, silhouetted against the bioluminescent fungus that gave the undercity its sickly green twilight. "Yeah. That's her version of a welcome."

"You know her?"

"I know *of* her." Which was true in the way most of his knowledge was true these days—secondhand, borrowed, missing the middle bits where understanding ought to live. "She's called Pip. She lives around here somewhere. Nobody knows exactly where, because nobody who follows her ever finds out anything except regret."

The child tilted her head. Even at this distance, Sable could feel the weight of her attention like a hand on the back of his neck.

"Why is everyone whispering about her?"

"Because she eats memories."

Noor turned to look at him fully for the first time since they'd started running. In the green light her face was all sharp angles and exhaustion, and there was something underneath the fear—something that looked almost like hunger. "That's not possible."

"No," Sable agreed. "And yet here we are, hiding from a Guild Retrieval squad under an aqueduct, so I've decided to stop being picky about what's possible." He nodded upward. "She's deciding whether we're worth the trouble."

"How does she decide?"

"Honestly? I don't think she does. I think she just gets bored."

Pip got bored. One moment she was a statue on the arch; the next she dropped straight down—a fall of twenty feet that ended in a crouch so soft it barely stirred the dust—and stood in front of them, close enough that Sable could see she was maybe nine years old, or maybe twelve, or maybe some age that didn't bother applying to children who lived on fungus and shadows. Her hair was a matted dark cloud. Her eyes were the pale gray of old rain. She wore a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves torn off, and around her neck, on a string, hung a dozen bottle caps, a fishhook, and one small brass key that gleamed like it mattered.

She looked at Sable. She sniffed, once, delicately, the way a cat sniffs something it has already dismissed.

"Hello, Pip," Sable said.

"You smell like burnt yesterday," she said. Her voice was hoarse, as though she didn't use it often. "You burned your last one this morning. Stupid."

"Yes," Sable said. "It was."

Then she turned to Noor, and everything changed.

Sable had seen people meet royalty. He'd seen beggars meet bread. But he had never seen anyone look at another person the way Pip looked at Noor Thackeray—like a starving man looking through a bakery window, like a drowning woman looking at a rope, like a child looking at a door marked *home* in a language she couldn't read but recognized anyway.

The child took one step forward. Then another. Noor went very still, the way you do when a strange dog approaches, except Pip wasn't a dog, and whatever was happening here, Sable suspected stillness was the correct response.

"You're full," Pip said wonderingly. She reached up—Noor flinched but held—and pressed two grimy fingers flat against Noor's sternum, right over the heart. "You're so *full*. There's something in you that isn't yours. It's big. It's—" Her face scrunched up, hunting for a word. "It's *old*. Older than the pipes. Older than the lamps. It's older than the word for older." She looked up, and her rain-gray eyes were shining. "Can I taste it?"

"No," said Noor and Sable, simultaneously.

"It wouldn't hurt," Pip said, wounded. "I'm very careful. Mostly."

"She ate a Retriever's whole childhood once," Sable offered. "Left him standing in the street crying about a dog he couldn't remember."

"He deserved it," said Pip. "He kicked a cat."

"The cat was hers," Sable explained to Noor. "Or she'd decided it was. Hard to say."

Noor crouched down slowly, bringing herself level with the child, and Sable watched her do the thing he was beginning to realize she did—the careful, deliberate act of taking someone seriously, of treating a feral memory-eating child under an aqueduct with the same gravity as a ledger entry. "My name is Noor. This is Sable. We're being hunted by bad men, and we need somewhere to hide until morning. Can you help us?"

Pip considered this with the solemnity of a magistrate. Then she looked at Noor's chest again, and her expression did something complicated—want and wariness tangled together like fishing line.

"You have a secret," she said. "A big sleeping secret. If I help you, can I keep watching it? Not eating. Watching."

Noor's hand drifted, without her seeming to notice, to her own sternum—the exact spot where Pip's fingers had rested. "You can watch," she said quietly. "For now."

That seemed to satisfy some ancient treaty neither of the adults understood. Pip spun on her heel. "This way. Walk quiet. The walls near my house are thin and they gossip."

* * *

Her house, it turned out, was a dead lamplighter's maintenance shed wedged into the roots of the aqueduct, no bigger than a generous coffin, lit by a single jar of glowworms and cluttered with the archaeology of a childhood assembled from other people's discards: a chipped teapot, forty-three buttons arranged by color, a birdcage holding nothing but folded paper stars. It smelled of moss and candle wax and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath—something that made the back of Sable's skull ache in a way he'd learned to recognize.

Memories. Residue. The shed was soaked in them.

"Cozy," he said, ducking through the doorway and immediately banging his head on a shelf of jars labeled in crayon: *WORMS (FRIENDLY)*, *BUTTONS*, *DO NOT EAT*, and one simply marked *MUM?* in letters that wobbled.

Pip ignored him. She was dragging a blanket—actually a sailcloth tarp—into a pile in the corner and patting it, arranging it, fussing over it with an intensity that made Noor exchange a glance with him over the child's head.

"She's nesting," Sable murmured. "Congratulations. You've been adopted."

"I don't—" Noor began.

"You have," Pip said firmly, not turning around. "I've decided. Sit."

They sat. The tarp was lumpy and smelled of pond, and Sable's knees complained about the floor, and none of that mattered because Pip then produced—from a hiding place Sable chose not to investigate too closely—three cups of mushroom tea, served in mismatched tin mugs, and sat down cross-legged between them like a small empress holding court.

"So," she said to Noor, staring at her with open, unblinking fascination. "Tell me about your secret."

"I don't actually know anything about it."

"That's the best kind," Pip said, delighted. "Those are the tastiest. When I find one I don't know, it's like—" she waved her hands, searching, "- like a present wrapped in more presents wrapped in a riddle. Most people are boring inside. Same memories, same shapes. *Mother, bread, winter, sad.* Yours is..." She frowned, pressing her palm flat against her own chest now, as if listening to something echo back. "Yours is like standing at the edge of a really deep well and hearing something breathe at the bottom."

Sable felt the hair rise on his arms. "Pip. Has anyone else ever told you Noor smells like that? Anyone from the Guild?"

Pip shrugged. "Guild men smell like locks and vinegar. They came down here last month asking about a courier. I bit one."

"You *bit* one?"

"On the ankle. He cried." She said it without pride or apology, simply as fact, and Sable decided that he liked this child enormously and would die for her without further discussion.

Noor set down her tea. "Pip, do you remember how you got down here? To the undercity?"

The change was immediate and total. The delight drained out of the small face, leaving behind something hollow—not sadness exactly, but the space where a memory should have been, the phantom itch of an amputated past. Pip looked down into her mug.

"No," she said. "I remember everything I eat. Every single one, all jumbled up like a drawer full of string. But my own ones—" She touched the little brass key hanging at her throat. "Gone. Someone took them before I could eat them myself. Or I ate them and forgot I did. I don't remember which, which is the worst part." She looked up, defiant, daring them to pity her. "So I eat new ones instead. Fill the hole. Doesn't work. Keeps me busy."

The silence stretched. Sable, who had sold a decade of his own life to the Guild for reasons he could no longer recall—who had, just that morning, burned his final unspent memory to save two strangers—knew exactly the size and shape of that hole. He knew it lived in him like a tenant who never paid rent.

"What's the key for?" Noor asked gently.

"Dunno." Pip smiled suddenly, bright as a struck match. "That's the second-best kind of thing to have."

* * *

Later, when Pip finally slept—curled against Noor's side with the unselfconscious entitlement of the very young, one fist closed in the fabric of Noor's sleeve—Sable kept watch by the glowworm jar and thought about doors.

He'd spent thirty years lighting lamps above ground and the last few below it, after the Guild took his decade and his reputation with it. He remembered the ledger clerk who'd processed the sale: a gray little office, a gray little man, and a woman across the desk whose face he could no longer picture even though he knew—he *knew*, the way you know a scar—that she had mattered. That whatever he'd traded away, he'd traded for her sake, or her safety, or possibly because she'd asked him to and he'd loved her enough to say yes. The debt-shaped feeling in his chest had led him to Noor Thackeray through half the undercity without his understanding why. Debts, he'd learned, remembered better than debtors.

He looked at Noor now, sitting rigidly upright with a sleeping child against her ribs, clearly unsure whether to move, breathe, or surrender entirely.

"You're good at this," he said quietly.

"At what?"

"People keep choosing you. The courier died rather than let go of you. Pip adopted you in under a minute. I followed you through a flooded tunnel based on a feeling." He shrugged. "Invisible people don't collect debts they never incurred. Something about you pulls threads."

Noor looked down at the child. In the worm-light her face was unreadable, but her hand had come to rest, very lightly, on Pip's wild hair.

"When she touched me," Noor said, "she said there's something inside me that isn't mine. Something old." A pause. "Sable, the courier whispered my name when he died. My real name. And I recognized it. Do you understand what that means? I recognized a name I've never heard." Her voice stayed level, ledger-clerk steady, but her knuckles had gone white where they gripped her mug. "What if whatever's inside me is why Corvane wants the map? What if the map is only half of it, and *I'm* the other half?"

"Then we get to the Spire first," Sable said, "and we find out together."

"You don't even know what's in the Spire."

"No. But I know what's in this room, and it's more than I had this morning." He tapped his temple. "Turns out a man can lose every memory he owns and still know a good thing when it sits down next to him."

Pip stirred in her sleep and mumbled something. Noor's hand stilled on her hair.

Then the child's eyes snapped open—fully awake, instantly, no groggy middle—and she sat up so fast her head cracked against Noor's chin.

"Ow—"

"*Listen,*" Pip whispered. Her rain-gray eyes had gone wide and fixed, aimed at the wall, at the world beyond it. Somewhere far off, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Sable had hoped not to hear again for days: boots. Many boots. And beneath them, threading through the waterlogged silence of the undercity, the high cold note of a Retrieval whistle.

"They're coming," Pip breathed. She grabbed the brass key at her throat and held it like a talisman, and her voice, when it came again, was smaller than Sable had ever heard it. "Not for me this time. For *her*. They're singing her name—the wrong name, the cage-name—but it's her. Noor Thackeray, Noor Thackeray." She looked up, terrified and certain all at once. "They brought a *hollow man* to carry her home."
