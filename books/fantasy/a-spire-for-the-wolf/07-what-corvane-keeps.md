## 7. What Corvane Keeps

The thing about breaking into the Master of the Mnemosyne Guild's private vault, the ghost pointed out, was that Noor had a key.

Metaphorically.

"Figuratively," Wren said — because that was her name, the courier's name, Wren Calloway, and she had been saying it inside Noor's skull for three days now like a song stuck behind a wall. "You said metaphorically. I'm correcting you. It's figurative. A metaphor compares things. You're just... sneaky."

*I'm lying in a laundry cart,* Noor thought back at her, *so let's not split hairs.*

The cart was real enough, piled with table linens from the Guild's evening meal, and Noor was folded into it in a way that would have been humiliating if anyone could see her. Nobody could. That had been true her whole life — invisibility not as a talent but as a condition, the way some people were nearsighted and some people were Noor Thackeray, whom one remembered only while directly looking at her and forgot before the door clicked shut. It had made her perfect for the ledgers. Ledgers wanted someone who left no impression on the ink or the room.

Tonight it made her perfect for theft.

The laundry cart trundled down the east service stair, pushed by Marla the washerwoman, who had worked for the Guild forty years and had spoken to Noor perhaps nine times, all of them about soap. At the landing by the cellar arches Marla stopped, stretched, muttered something about her knees, and wandered off to fetch a second basket, exactly as Noor had paid her two silver marks to do.

Noor climbed out of the linens, straightened her collar, and stood in the underbelly of the only building she had ever belonged to.

"You're stalling," Wren observed. Her voice came from nowhere and everywhere, a candle-flame of a voice. Being dead seemed to have improved her conversational confidence considerably.

"I'm orienting myself."

"You've been orienting yourself for four minutes."

"Ledger-keepers orient thoroughly." Noor looked down the cellar corridor. Three doors: salt stores, wine, and at the end, iron-banded and utterly unremarkable, the door every employee of the Guild knew about and no employee of the Guild had ever opened. Corvane's vault. The place where, presumably, he kept whatever a man keeps when he has spent two hundred years — everyone said two hundred years, though no one said it loudly — buying and selling the pasts of an entire city.

"And what exactly," Noor asked, both aloud and in her head, because she hadn't yet learned which way Wren heard her, "am I hoping to find? He asked me about you today. Gently. He poured the tea himself. He knows something, Wren. He may already know you're dead. He may know I have your map." She swallowed. "If he knows all that, why am I not running for the coast?"

"Because," Wren said, "you looked at that map and recognized the route before I ever showed it to you. Because when I said your name, you didn't hear a stranger's name. You heard yours." A pause, softer. "And because you pocketed a mother's lullaby out of the inventory last month and still haven't uncorked it, and I don't think that's discipline, Noor. I think it's fear of wanting it too much."

Noor did not answer. The lullaby vial was sewn into her hem right now, a small cold weight against her ankle, and she had not uncorked it, and Wren was right, and being right gave her an insufferable air even without a body.

She crossed the corridor, took out her picks — ledger-keepers kept picks; you never knew when a drawer would be locked against the very people who balanced it — and set to work on a lock older than the city's current name.

It took eleven minutes. Somewhere around minute seven she began humming, quietly, the way she always hummed when nervous, and realized mid-note that she was humming the lullaby. She had never uncorked the vial. She had never heard the song.

She stopped humming so fast she nearly bit her tongue.

*That,* said Wren, very gently, "is why we're here."

* * *

The vault was not full of gold.

Noor had expected gold, or memories — thousands of vials racked like wine, the concentrated wealth of the Guild. There were vials, yes, one whole wall of them, glowing faintly the way bottled memory does, blue-green and patient, like drowned lanterns. But it was the other walls that stopped her.

Ledgers.

Floor to ceiling. Hundreds of them. Leather spines cracked and re-cracked, some swollen with damp, some so old the leather had gone black and shiny as beetle shell. Each bore a date on a small brass plate. She walked the rows slowly, trailing her fingers, reading dates backward into time. The oldest shelf held volumes from before the printing of anything else she had ever seen; their plates were hand-engraved, and the earliest of all read a year that made her stop and do the arithmetic twice, because it was four hundred years ago, and the Guild's own public histories claimed two hundred.

"Somebody's been lying in the front-of-house books," she murmured.

"That's rather the theme," said Wren.

Noor cleared a space on the great reading table — there was a reading table, lamp and magnifying glass and everything, which told her Corvane spent hours here, alone, among the receipts of centuries — and opened the oldest volume first.

Her professional instincts woke at once, warm and familiar, like slipping into a bath. This was work she understood. Columns. Dates. Entries. Except—

Except the entries weren't sales. The earliest pages recorded withdrawals. Large ones. Memory drawn from living subjects in quantities that should have killed them, marked not with names but with designations: *Vessel Candidate IV — unsuitable, fractured.* *Vessel Candidate VII — rejected, too old.* *Vessel Candidate IX—*

The page after IX had been torn out. Torn carefully, at the gutter, but torn.

Noor stood very still.

*Vessel,* she wrote in the margin of her own mind. *Candidates.* She turned pages. The designations continued for decades, then thinned, then stopped, replaced by a different kind of entry entirely — maintenance records. Sedative dosages. Sealing procedures. And then, beginning some thirty years ago, in a hand she recognized with a lurch, because it matched the hand on every memo that ever crossed her desk:

*Subject remains stable. Seal holds. Subject unaware.*

*Subject relocated. New identity implanted: clerk's daughter, orphaned. Name chosen for dullness.*

*Subject employed within the Guild. Ideal. Watches nothing, is watched by none.*

Noor sat down. Not gracefully. Her knees simply resigned.

"He watched you," Wren said quietly. "Your whole life. He built it."

"He *wrote* me." Noor's voice came out strange, flat, the voice she used for balancing columns that refused to balance. "I thought I hired on here at nineteen. I remember applying. I remember the interview. I remember—" She stopped. She examined the memory the way she'd examine a suspect coin, biting it, weighing it, checking the edge for seams. *Clerk's daughter, orphaned.* She could see her childhood home if she tried. She could see a gray street, a thin mother, coughing at night, a funeral in rain.

She could not, no matter how hard she pressed, remember her mother's face.

She could not remember her mother ever once calling her anything but *Noor*, and even that felt recent, like a coat worn so long it had become skin.

"I remember a funeral in the rain," she said aloud. "I can feel the wet hem of my skirt. I can't tell you what my mother looked like. Wren, I've inventoried ten thousand memories. I know what real ones look like on the page. Fabrications have gaps where the emotional entries should be." She laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "My childhood reads like a badly kept ledger."

*Oh, love,* Wren began, and then stopped, because Noor had gone rigid at the table.

Because she had seen it.

At the far end of the vial wall, apart from the others, on its own small stand like an altar or an accusation: a single vial. Older glass than any of the rest, hand-blown, slightly uneven, its wax seal black and stamped with a sigil she had seen exactly once before — pressed into the first page of the map hidden under her floorboard. And tied to its neck with wire, a label in that same careful hand, the hand that had written *subject* for thirty years.

The label said:

**VESSEL SEAL — ORIGINAL. DO NOT OPEN. DO NOT SELL. DO NOT DUPLICATE.**

And beneath it, the name. Her name. Not *Noor Thackeray*. The other one. The one a dying courier had pressed into her hands along with a map, the one that had fit like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known her chest contained.

Dated sixty-two years before Noor Thackeray, clerk's daughter, orphaned, had supposedly been born.

* * *

She didn't remember crossing the room. One moment she was at the table; the next her fingers were on the vial, trembling so badly the glass chattered against the stand.

"Don't," said Wren sharply — the sharpest Noor had ever heard her. "Not open. Read the label again. *Do not open.*"

"It's mine."

"It's a seal, Noor. Look at the wax. That's not storage wax, that's warding wax. Whatever's in there isn't a memory someone paid to forget. It's a memory somebody *imprisoned*, and the label says vessel, and you—" Wren hesitated, and Noor understood, with a vertigo like standing at the top of an endless stair, that Wren had known more than she'd said. Had perhaps died knowing it. "You're not just a girl who was given a false name. You're the *lock*, Noor. Maybe the door. And this is either your heart or your cage, and I don't know which, and neither do you, and we are in *his* vault with his lamp and his chair and his rules."

Noor's hand hovered over the vial.

The strangest thing, she thought distantly, was how little shocked she felt, down beneath the shaking. Some sealed room in her had apparently always suspected. All those years of watching other people buy and sell their yesterdays — the widow selling her wedding joy, the soldier buying back one hour of his brother laughing — and Noor had never once been tempted to trade her own past, and had prided herself on detachment, on contentment, on being a creature of the present tense.

You don't gamble a currency you know is counterfeit.

"I'm not going to open it," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "I'm going to copy the label. The date. Everything. And then I'm going to take the map and the ghost and the lullaby I apparently already know the tune of, and I am going to find out who decided that I was a *subject* before I was a person."

She copied quickly, hands steadying themselves through sheer habit of precision. Date. Sigil. Designation. And then, because she was who she was — because some part of her had spent a lifetime reading other people's accounts and could no sooner leave a ledger unread than stop breathing — she pulled down the most recent volume on the maintenance shelf and turned to the last entries.

The final line, dated six days ago, in Corvane's immaculate hand:

*The courier reached her. As anticipated. Vessel moving toward the Spire of its own accord. No intervention required. The lock walks to the door. — V.C.*

Below it, fresher, ink still sharp:

*Prepare the vault for her return.*

*Return.* Not arrival. Return. As though she had merely stepped out and would sensibly come back, the way tools come back, the way ledgers come home to be balanced.

The lamp on the table flickered.

At first Noor blamed her own movement, her breath. Then she saw the flame lean — steadily, deliberately — toward the vault door, the way a candle leans toward a draft. Toward footsteps. She heard them now, muffled through old iron: slow, unhurried, accompanied by a soft clink she knew from a thousand mornings — the sound of Master Vess Corvane's ring of keys, coming down the cellar stairs.

"Noor," Wren whispered. "The light's under the door. He'll see—"

There was nowhere to go. The vault had one door and no windows and shelves that went up past the reach of the lamplight, and Noor Thackeray, who had passed unseen through twenty-nine years of rooms, was for the first time in her life standing in a small bright box with her name written on the inventory.

She snuffed the lamp, pressed herself into the dark gap between two ledger cases, and pulled the newest volume off the shelf with her — not for courage, exactly. For evidence.

The keys reached the door.

A pause. The particular pause of a man noticing that a lamp is warm when it ought to be cold.

Then the lock turned, and the door swung wide, and Vess Corvane stood silhouetted in the doorway holding a second lamp, and said, in the gentle voice he used for difficult accounts:

"There's no need to hide, my dear. You're home early — but then," he sighed, stepping inside and setting the lamp on the table beside the single waiting vial, "I suppose you always were going to read it eventually. Everyone does, in the end. It's the one entry in this entire building nobody ever balances." He turned toward her shadow between the shelves, and smiled the smile of a man greeting a ledger that had learned to walk.

"Come and sit, child. Let's discuss what you're worth."
