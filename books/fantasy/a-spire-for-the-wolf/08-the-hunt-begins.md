## 8. The Hunt Begins

Sable Quinn had spent thirty years learning to read the dark, and tonight the dark was reading him back.

He stood in the mouth of Lamplighter's Row, where the old lamp-posts leaned like tired old men gossiping about the weather, and he watched the Retrievers come down the undercity stairs in a line as neat as funeral mourners. Six of them. Grey coats, grey hoods, grey gloves, and that particular way of walking that said *we have never once been in a hurry, because everything is always already ours.*

They carried lanterns with shutters on them, which told Sable everything. Ordinary folk carried lanterns to see by. The Guild's Retrievers carried lanterns so they could decide who else got to.

"Right," Sable muttered, and pulled his cap low. "Right, right, right."

The trouble with being a lamplighter — even a disgraced one, even one who'd sold ten years of his own head to the Guild for reasons he could no longer remember — was that you knew every way into and out of the undercity, because lighting lamps meant walking all of them, twice a night, in all weathers, for decades. The undercity had more doors than it had walls. And Corvane's men were coming through the front ones like they owned the deed.

Which, Sable supposed sourly, they did.

He moved along the Row with his shoulders hunched and his hands in his pockets, past the tallow-seller's shuttered stall, past the fountain nobody had cleaned since the Flood of Old Marrow, past the doorway where Pip slept when she remembered to sleep at all. He'd left her there an hour ago with half a meat pie and strict instructions: *Stay put, eat your pie, don't follow the nice man with the crooked leg.* She'd looked at him with those enormous dark eyes and eaten the pie and he'd been a fool to think that settled anything, because Pip treated instructions the way rain treated umbrellas — acknowledged, then ignored entirely.

The doorway was empty except for crumbs.

"Oh, wonderful," Sable said to no one. "Oh, that's just lovely."

* * *

Finding Pip in the undercity should have been like finding one specific rat in a city made entirely of rats, but Pip had a habit — a talent, really — of leaving herself findable when she wanted to be found, and lately she'd decided she wanted to be found by exactly two people: Noor Thackeray, whom she called *the ledger lady* with an odd reverence, and Sable, whom she called nothing at all, mostly, though sometimes she tugged his sleeve and studied his face with an expression he couldn't name and didn't ask about.

He found her crouched on a rooftop over Tallowman's Walk, eating something that had once been a pigeon and watching six grey figures move through the street below with the focused interest of a cat watching a slow bird.

"Pip." He hauled himself up beside her, knee complaining. "Inside. Now."

She pointed down.

"I can see down. I've got eyes. Two of them, very fine ones."

She pointed again, insistent, and then touched her own temple, then her chest, then made a gesture he'd learned to read weeks ago: *something wrong, something wrong below.*

Below, the Retrievers had stopped. They'd fanned out around the door of Widow Cratchit's boarding house, where Noor had been sleeping these past few nights, ever since she'd turned up white-faced and shaking with a dead courier's map and a dead courier's ghost rattling around behind her eyes. Sable didn't know all the details. He knew enough. He knew the Guild wanted what she carried, and he knew that Master Vess Corvane smiled like a kind uncle and kept vaults full of other people's yesterdays, and he knew — in the place where his missing decade used to be, in the hollow ache of it — that the Guild took things from people and called it commerce.

One of the Retrievers knocked on Widow Cratchit's door. Politely. That was somehow the worst part.

"She's not there," Sable breathed. "Tell me she's not there."

Pip shrugged, which meant she had no idea, which meant Sable had to assume the worst, which meant—

The door opened. A shape slipped out the back and went over the fence like a startled cat, and Sable's heart did something complicated and painful, because the shape moved like Noor Thackeray moved — carefully, apologetically, as if apologizing to gravity itself for taking up space.

"There's my girl," he whispered. "Go on, love. Go on."

But the Retrievers weren't fools. Two of them peeled off after her, unhurried, terrible, and the other four spread through the Walk and began doing the thing Sable had seen them do only once before, years ago, in another life he couldn't remember: going door to door, lantern-shutters opening, light spilling into faces, and wherever that light touched, people forgot things. Small things first. The name of a neighbor. Where they'd put their keys. Then bigger things, if the Retriever judged it necessary.

"They're sweeping," Sable said. "They're doing a sweep. In my city."

Pip made a sound low in her throat that wasn't quite a growl and wasn't quite fear, and Sable realized with a lurch that she was looking not at the Retrievers but at their lanterns with an expression of pure, ancient hatred.

"What is it?" he asked. "Pip. What do they keep?"

She looked at him then, and her eyes were wet, and she pressed both fists against her temples and shook her head hard, hard, hard — *I don't know, I don't know, I ate it and I don't know* — and something in Sable's chest cracked clean down the middle.

Because he understood that. Oh, he understood that better than anyone alive. Somewhere in this city was a child who had eaten her whole self, and somewhere in the Guild's vaults were the pieces, labeled and shelved, and Vess Corvane probably dusted them on Sundays.

"Right," Sable said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt. "New plan. We get everyone out. You, me, the ledger lady, and if there's time, my dignity, though I've not seen that in years."

* * *

Getting ahead of a sweep was simple arithmetic: the Retrievers worked door to door, and doors came in order, and order could be predicted by anyone who'd walked a route for thirty years. Sable cut through the wash-yards and the coal-chutes, Pip flowing after him like smoke with opinions, and they caught up with Noor in the tunnel behind the old bathhouse, where she stood pressed against dripping brick with her hand over her mouth, listening to the Retrievers' polite knocking echo down the corridor.

"Sable—" she started.

"No time, no questions, yes I'm limping, it's the damp." He grabbed her wrist. "Widow Cratchit's is gone. They'll be at the tannery in ten minutes and the fish-market in twenty, and after the fish-market they close the sluice-gates, and after the sluice-gates, dearheart, there is nowhere in this undercity that isn't a pocket."

"The sluice-gates," Noor repeated faintly. "You know this how?"

"Thirty years of lamps, love. You learn where the dark keeps its doors." He was already moving, hauling her along, Pip darting ahead like a small ferocious scout. "There's a way out through the drowned quarter's edge — not in, mind, I'm not mad, just the rim of it, the old pilgrim stairs. Nobody sweeps the pilgrim stairs. Even Retrievers have superstitions."

Noor stumbled, caught herself, and said, in a small voice, "Why are you helping me? You barely know me."

And Sable, who had asked himself the same question every night since he'd spotted her fleeing through the undercity with terror written all over her careful face, gave the only honest answer he had:

"I owe you a debt I can't remember taking on. My whole missing decade, love, it's got your fingerprints on it somewhere — or someone's, and whoever they belong to, they were worth selling ten years for. So until I remember why, I'm going to act like a man who owed somebody something enormous. It's the only religion I've got left."

Behind them, further back than it should have been, a lantern-shutter clacked open, and light bloomed in the tunnel like a bruise.

* * *

They almost made it.

That was the thing Sable would carry afterward, the thing that sat in him like a swallowed stone: *they almost made it.* They'd reached the pilgrim stairs — forty-one steps of wet ancient stone climbing out of the undercity's throat toward the rim of the drowned quarter, where the air tasted of river and old grief. Pip had gone up first. Noor was halfway. Sable was at the bottom, turning to check the tunnel one last time, because thirty years of habit dies hard.

The lantern-light caught him full in the face.

"There he is," said a voice like oiled hinges. A Retriever stepped out of the dark, and behind him two more, and their leader — a broad woman with a scar through one eyebrow and a shuttered lantern held at hip height like a dueling pistol — smiled at Sable with genuine warmth.

"Sable Quinn," she said. "Formerly of the Lamplighters' Guild. Formerly of most things, I'm told. Master Corvane remembers you."

"Lucky him," Sable said. "I don't."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Funny thing, that. Ten years bought and paid for, and here you are, standing between us and Guild property." She tilted her head toward the stairs, toward Noor frozen on step nineteen like a rabbit on a church wall. "Step aside, Quinn. There's no debt in it for you. There never was. Whatever you sold us, you sold it for bread and rent, same as everyone. Sentimentality is just forgetting with better posture."

And that — *that* — was the moment Sable understood something he hadn't let himself understand before. Because the woman was lying, or she wasn't, and either way she knew something about his missing decade that he didn't, and she dangled it like bait, and the horrible truth was that he *wanted* it. He wanted those ten years the way a starving man wants bread. He dreamed about them. He woke reaching for names that weren't there.

But Noor was on the stairs. And Pip was at the top, watching him with her fists pressed to her temples, and the debt he couldn't remember was still a debt, and debts were the last religion he had.

"Here's my counteroffer," Sable said. "You let them walk, and I come quiet."

"We were always going to take you too, Quinn."

"Well." He wiped his nose. "Then here's my real offer."

His hand went to his breast pocket, to the little stoppered vial he'd carried for eleven years without ever once deciding whether to drink it. Every soul who sold memory to the Guild got to keep one unspent vial — the last drop, the dregs, whatever the decanters couldn't draw. Most people drank theirs the first night, greedy for any scrap of themselves. Sable had carried his like a coin he couldn't spend, because spending it would mean admitting the rest was truly gone.

Inside it, he'd always believed, was something precious. Something *his*. The last unguarded room in a house that had been emptied.

He uncorked it and drank it in one swallow, before the wisdom could catch him.

It tasted like rain on hot stone. It tasted like —

Like a kitchen. A small warm kitchen, and someone laughing — a woman laughing, head thrown back, flour on her hands, saying *you great useless hero, you'll be the death of me* — and a table with three bowls set out, and morning light, and the smell of bread, and the laughter again, and the words *come home safe, Sable, come home* —

The memory rose in him, whole and golden, and he held onto it with both hands for one full second, memorizing the fact of it if not the details, thinking: *someone waited for me. Someone loved me enough to say come home safe. I was loved. I was loved, I was—*

Then it burned away, spent, spent buying what it bought, and the woman's face went with it, and the kitchen, and the words, leaving only the shape of the feeling behind, like a dent in a pillow after the sleeper's risen.

What it bought was this: the lantern-light bent. All three Retrievers staggered as a wave of raw released memory rolled off Sable and struck the lanterns' workings — because that was the secret at the heart of the Guild's trade, the secret the lamplighters had always known and the Guild had paid good money to bury: memory and light were the same current, and an unspent life, freely given, could flood any lamp ever built. The Retrievers' lanterns flared white, shrieked like kettles, and died. The scarred woman screamed and clawed at her own head as ten years of her colleagues' collected stock backwashed through her — Sable didn't stay to watch what she forgot.

"UP," he bellowed, up the stairs. "UP THE STAIRS, BOTH OF YOU, NOW."

They went. God help him, they went — Noor seizing his arm and half-carrying him, Pip ahead, all three of them bursting out onto the rim of the drowned quarter where the river wind hit them like a blessing, while below, in the dark they'd left, the Retrievers groped blind and shouting among lamps that would not light for them anymore.

* * *

They walked until the undercity noises faded, and then they walked more, because Sable didn't trust quiet yet. Finally, in the lee of a ruined pilgrim shrine whose saint's face had been worn smooth by centuries of rain, they stopped. Noor sank down against the stone. Pip curled against her side like it was the most natural thing in the world, and Noor — after one startled heartbeat — put her arm around the girl and left it there.

Sable sat down heavily and found, to his mild surprise, that he was crying. Not sobbing. Just leaking, quietly, like a lamp with a bad seal.

"Sable?" Noor said softly.

"Don't mind me, love. It's the wind."

"It isn't the wind."

"No." He wiped his face with his sleeve and laughed, a cracked, astonished sound. "I had someone. Back then. Before. I don't know who — I spent the knowing, that's the joke of it — but I know *that much.* Somebody set three bowls on a table and told me to come home safe." He stared out at the black water of the drowned quarter, glinting far below. "And I can't tell you her face or her name or whether she's even living still, but I can tell you this: I know what it felt like to be loved, and I'm not giving that feeling back to Corvane or anyone. It's mine now. The only thing I've got that's truly mine. Bought it fair."

Noor's hand found his in the dark and squeezed it. Her fingers were cold and trembling, and her voice, when it came, was smaller than he'd ever heard it.

"He knows my name," she whispered. "Not Noor. My *other* name. The courier said it as he died, and now the ghost says it in my head every night, and tonight — Sable, one of the Retrievers looked straight at me and said *the vessel comes quietly or she doesn't,* like I was cargo. Like I was inventory." She laughed, high and thin. "I've spent my whole life making sure nobody noticed me. And now I'm the most noticed thing in this city."

Pip stirred against her, lifted her head, and did something she had never done before. She reached up and touched Noor's cheek, deliberately, gently, the way you'd touch something breakable and holy. Then she tapped her own temple twice — *I know, I know what's inside you* — and laid her head back down on Noor's shoulder, closing her eyes with the fierce finality of a guard dog claiming its post.

Sable looked at the two of them — the ledger lady and the memory-eater, holding each other up in the ruins — and felt the dent in his pillow, the shape-of-the-feeling, settle warm in his chest.

"Right," he said thickly. "Well. If we're all being dramatic, I'll add mine: they'll come again. That woman backwashed a decade of Guild stock; Corvane won't forgive that. He'll send more. He'll send *all* of them, and next time they'll bring lamps we can't drown." He pushed himself upright, knees crackling like green firewood. "So we go where their lanterns can't follow. Down, love. All the way down."

Noor followed his gaze to the drowned quarter — to the black water, and beneath it, far beneath, the faint suggestion of a spire's crown breaking the surface like a tooth.

"You want us to go into the Sunken Spire," she said flatly.

"I want us to go anywhere Corvane's light doesn't reach. It just happens that everywhere is down." He offered her his hand, and after a moment she took it, and Pip took hers, and the three of them stood in the ruined shrine like the world's smallest, dampest army.

From far below the water — so far it might have been imagination, might have been the wind, might have been a god turning over in its sleep — came a sound.

A long, low, patient howl.

Pip's head snapped up. Her eyes went wide and bright, and she grinned — a wild, gap-toothed, remembering grin — and pointed down at the water with one small finger, and said her first word in the whole time Sable had known her:

"*Wolf.*"
