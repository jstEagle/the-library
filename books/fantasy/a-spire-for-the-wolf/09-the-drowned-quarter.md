## 9. The Drowned Quarter

The stairwell behind the tannery had not been used, by the look of it, since the city had decided to forget it existed. Noor went first, holding the map in one hand and a hooded lantern in the other, and the stairs went down and down and down, each step slick with a damp that seemed less like water and more like patience.

"Tell me again," Sable said behind her, "why we couldn't take the nice road. The one with air in it."

"The nice road has Retrievers on it," Noor said.

"Ah. Yes. I remember now." He shifted Pip higher on his back; she rode him the way a barnacle rides a hull, arms around his neck, chin on his crown, utterly silent. "I've decided I hate the undercity. All of it. Every brick."

"You haven't seen the drowned quarter yet."

"I've seen enough of it to know I'll hate it more."

Pip said nothing, but she made a small sound against his hair — the ghost of a laugh, maybe, or just breath. She'd been making those sounds all day. Small approvals. Small disapprovals. A whole vocabulary of almost-words that Noor was beginning to be able to read, which frightened her more than Corvane did, if she was honest about it. Corvane wanted her body. Pip seemed to want something harder to give.

The ghost, meanwhile, had been quiet for an hour, and Noor had learned that this was worse than when she talked.

*Left at the landing,* the courier's voice said at last, arriving inside Noor's skull like a cold draft under a door. *There should be an archway. It will look wrong.*

"It will look wrong," Noor repeated aloud.

Sable stopped. "I'm sorry?"

"The archway. It looks wrong." Noor turned left at the landing and there it was: an arch of black stone set into a wall of grey brick, so precisely out of place that the eye kept trying to slide off it, the way a tongue worries a chipped tooth. Someone had built it to be noticed and then built everything around it to make noticing painful.

"That," Sable said, "looks wrong."

"Yes."

"Wonderful." He ducked through it anyway, because he was that kind of man — the kind who complained the entire way into the dark and then went anyway. Noor had known him for four days and she had already catalogued this the way she catalogued everything: *Sable Quinn, lamplighter, disgraced, complains as breathing, goes regardless.* It was, she suspected, the most honest entry she'd ever made.

Beyond the arch, the stairs ended, and the drowned quarter began.

Noor had grown up — or been placed, or been assembled, the thought still caught like a fishbone in her throat whenever she reached for it — above ground, among ledgers and candle-smoke and the soft rustle of other people's yesterdays. She had imagined the flooded ruins beneath the city as a cellar. What they were, in fact, was a sky turned upside down.

The lantern light found water first: a vast black sheet of it, still as glass, stretching away into darkness in every direction. Out of that water rose the tops of things — a bell tower with only its crown above the surface, a weathervane frozen mid-turn, the upper windows of a street that had once been a street and was now a reef. Doors opened onto nothing. Chimneys stood like reeds. And everywhere, threaded through the water, grew pale white branches — some kind of coral, or bone, or memory given root — that glowed faintly when the lantern passed near them, the way a cat's eyes catch a flame.

"Oh," Sable said quietly. All his complaining drained out of him at once, which somehow made Noor's chest ache worse than any wail would have. "Oh, that's — someone lived here."

"Thousands of someones," the ghost said, and now her voice came with a pressure behind the eyes, grief-shaped. "The Guild called it the Reclamation. Fifty years ago. The quarter wouldn't sell, so the quarter drowned. They told everyone it was the river's doing."

Noor wrote none of this down. There was no ledger here. That was the thing about the drowned quarter, she was beginning to understand: it was what happened to places after the ledgers were closed.

* * *

They walked along a submerged parapet — the top of an old sea wall, wide enough for two abreast if one of them was a child riding piggyback — with the black water close enough to touch on either side. The glowing branches thickened as they went, and where they grew thickest, the water gave back reflections that didn't match.

Noor looked down at one point and saw herself looking up: same face, same lantern, but standing in dry streets, under a sun, beside people whose faces she almost knew. She looked away quickly.

"Don't stare at the reflections," the ghost advised. "They're not yours. Nothing down here is yours. That's rather the point of it."

"Whose are they?" Pip asked — her first full word in hours, delivered with the flat practicality of a child asking why the sky was up.

"The drowned ones'. The ones the Guild bought cheap and shelved." The ghost's voice did something complicated. "Memories sink, you know. Nobody tells you that. Bottled memories, spilled memories, memories sold by desperate people in bad winters — they seep down. The water remembers what the city pays it to forget."

Sable walked a little straighter at that. Noor noticed. She noticed everything; it was her curse and her trade. He was thinking about his decade — the ten years he'd sold to the Guild to pay a debt he could no longer name, the hole in the middle of his life shaped exactly like himself. He was thinking about whether any of it had seeped down here, into the black water, glowing pale on a branch somewhere, waiting.

She didn't say anything. Some ledgers you keep closed out of kindness.

They made camp that night — if night meant anything a hundred feet below a city — in the belfry of the drowned bell tower, which rose from the water like an island. Sable got a fire going from lamp-oil and driftwood with the ease of a man who had spent his life coaxing light out of unwilling things. Pip ate half a heel of bread and then sat at the tower's edge, legs dangling over the black water, watching the pale branches pulse below like slow heartbeats.

Noor took out the map.

By firelight it was stranger than ever. The wax seal had cracked on the second day, and now the parchment seemed almost to breathe, ink shifting like silt in current. The route to the Sunken Spire wound through the drowned quarter in a long silver thread, and at certain points along it, small marks — a hand, an eye, a bell — glowed faintly warm when her finger passed over them.

"She's guiding us," Noor said. Not a question. She was getting used to talking about the ghost in the third person while the ghost listened from inside her own head, which was the sort of arrangement that would have driven her to drink if she were a drinking woman.

"I'm guiding us," the ghost corrected, from inside her own head. "You're carrying me. There's a difference, and it matters."

"Why does it matter?"

"Because carriers get a vote." A pause, and then, gentler: "And because you keep saying *she* like I'm not here. I'm very here. I'm so here I'm practically furniture."

Despite everything — the water, the dark, the Retrievers somewhere above them combing the undercity, the name dying on a courier's lips three days ago — Noor laughed. It came out rusty, a hinge unused for years, but it came.

Sable looked up from the fire. "What did she say?"

"She says she's furniture."

"Sensible woman." He fed the fire another stick. "I knew a courier once. Before. Back when I still had a before." He said it lightly, the way men say heavy things when they're tired. "She used to say the city was just a story the streets told about themselves. I thought it was nonsense. Now I'm sitting in a bell tower in a drowned neighborhood listening to a dead woman navigate, and I find I miss her nonsense."

"What was her name?" Noor asked.

He was quiet a moment. "That's the trouble. I don't know. She's part of what I sold." He smiled, and it was a good smile even worn thin. "Funny. You spend ten years knowing someone and then one afternoon you sign a slip of paper and she's gone, and what's left is just — the shape where she was. Warm shape, though. I remember it being warm."

Pip had come back from the edge and wedged herself between them without asking, the way cats and children do. She looked at Noor with those flat grey eyes that saw too much.

"You're sad," Pip announced.

"I'm tired," Noor said.

"You're sad *and* tired." Pip considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she reached out and patted Noor's knee twice, precisely, like stamping a document. "It's fine."

It was such a small thing. It undid her completely.

* * *

Later, when Sable slept and Pip slept curled against his side like a comma against a sentence, Noor sat at the tower's edge with the map across her knees and let the truth come up the way drowned things come up — slowly, reluctantly, trailing weed.

The ghost waited. She was good at waiting. Dead women usually were.

"I don't know who I am," Noor said finally, to the water.

"You're Noor Thackeray. Keeper of ledgers. Terrible liar. Worse singer — yes, I heard you humming earlier, don't make that face."

"Noor Thackeray is a name on a Guild intake form. I checked, in Corvane's vault." Her hands were steady. She'd trained them to be steady through years of balancing accounts that could ruin families; they obeyed her now out of habit. "There's no record of me before the age of nineteen. No parents. No town. No school fees, no illnesses, no debts. Everyone has debts. Even orphans have debts. I have *nothing*, and I work for the people who keep everyone's everything, and I never once wondered why until a dying man said a name to me that felt like—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Like a key turning in a lock I didn't know I had."

The water breathed against the old sea wall. Below, the pale branches pulsed their slow light.

"And the name he said," the ghost said carefully. "The one you recognize."

"Aurelia." Saying it aloud was like opening a window in a sealed room; something moved in her chest that had never moved before. "I don't know it. I *know* it. There's a difference."

"There is," the ghost agreed. "I died knowing it, whatever else they burned out of me. Hold onto it. Names are anchors. Especially down here."

Noor wrapped her arms around her knees. "When this is over — whatever's at the end of the map — I won't have anything. Did you think about that? If Noor Thackeray is a fabrication, then everything I am is a fabrication. My ledgers. My neat little room. My careful life. Twenty-some years of being a person, and it might all be a cage someone built, and I don't even know how much of me is real enough to walk out of it."

She hadn't meant to say all of it. It came out the way floodwater comes through a door — all at once, having waited long enough.

Behind her, a voice said, sleep-ragged and unimpressed:

"Neither do I."

She turned. Sable was propped on one elbow, firelight catching the grey in his stubble, watching her with the level calm of a man who lit lamps in fog for a living and knew all about seeing only a few feet ahead.

"Sold my decade, didn't I," he said. "Ten years. Gone. I don't know who I loved. I don't know what I was like. I don't know if I was kind or cruel or funny or insufferable — well, I know I was insufferable, some things survive anything." Pip, asleep, snorted. "Point is, I wake up every day and I have to *decide* who Sable Quinn is, because nobody handed me the answer. And you know what? It's fine."

"It's fine," Noor repeated. "That's your philosophy."

"It's my entire philosophy. Learned it the hard way, cost me everything, very exclusive education." He sat up properly, and his voice lost its joke-shape. "Here's the thing nobody tells you. When you lose your past, you don't become nothing. You become *available*. Everything you do after — every choice, every kindness, every stupid brave thing — that's all yours. Firsthand. Not inherited, not assigned, not written in somebody's ledger. Yours." He shrugged. "So you don't know who you are. Good. Means nobody's written it yet. Means you get to write it yourself, and I'll tell you a secret about writing, since you lot love your books—"

"I keep ledgers."

"Same thing, smaller ambitions. The secret is: the first draft is allowed to be terrible." He lay back down. "Get some sleep, Aurelia. Or Noor. Whichever one's on duty."

Above the black water, in the drowned dark, Noor sat a while longer. The ghost said nothing, which from her was a kind of blessing. Pip's small hand had found the hem of Noor's coat in her sleep and held it, the way you hold a ledger closed against the wind.

*Fine,* Noor thought, testing the word. *Neither do I, and it's fine.*

It didn't feel fine. But it felt, for the first time in her careful fabricated life, like hers.

* * *

They broke camp before the water-light woke fully — the ghost said the branches brightened with the tide, and the tide down here kept its own hours — and followed the silver thread of the map deeper into the drowned quarter. Twice the route took them through buildings, along upper corridors that had once been homes: a nursery with a rocking horse fused to white coral, a kitchen where copper pots hung in a chandelier of barnacles. Pip touched nothing, but she looked at everything, and once she stopped before a child's drawing nailed to a doorframe — a crayon wolf, huge and gentle, standing over a tiny stick-figure house — and stared at it for a long, long time.

"Pip," Sable said gently. "Come on, sprout."

"He's nice," Pip said, meaning the crayon wolf, and would not say anything else for an hour.

Near midday — or the undercity's idea of it — the map's silver thread began to glow in earnest, pulling taut toward a distant point where the water gathered itself into a slow spiral, turning around something far below. And the ghost, who had been humming inside Noor's skull without realizing it — an old song, a lullaby, one Noor had pocketed in a vial a lifetime of days ago and still carried against her heart — went suddenly silent.

Then, very quietly:

*Aurelia. The gate wards. Look at the gate.*

Ahead, where the spiral turned, a structure broke the black water: a ring of black stone, vast as a cathedral door lying on its side, carved over every inch with script older than the Guild, older than the quarter, older perhaps than the city's story of itself. And as Noor's lantern light touched it, the carvings began to move — waking, reading, searching —

—and every line of them turned, at once, toward her.

The water around the gate went still as held breath.

"Well," said Sable. "I don't suppose it's looking at Pip."

"No," said the ghost, and her voice trembled like a struck bell. "It's looking at her blood. It knows what she carries. Aurelia — the Spire knows you're here. And I think—" she hesitated, and Noor felt, for the first time, fear in the dead woman's voice, "—I think something in there just woke up to check."
