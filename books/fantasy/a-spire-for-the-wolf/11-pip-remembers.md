## 11. Pip Remembers

Pip tastes the air before the door.

That's what she does. That's what she *is*, sort of, when she bothers to think about it, which is not often because thinking is loud and tasting is easy. The air down here in the drowned dark is old water and old stone and a little bit of Sable's fear-sweat, which tastes like copper pennies left too long in a pocket. Noor's fear tastes different. Noor's fear tastes like cold tea and someone else's name said wrong.

But this air—

This air tastes like *before*.

Pip stops walking. Sable bumps into her back and says "oi" and she doesn't answer because her mouth is full of the past.

* * *

Here is how remembering works for Pip, when it works at all, which is almost never: it is like swallowing a whole plum without chewing. You don't taste the plum. You just know, somewhere inside you, that there was a plum, and that it had a stone, and the stone is still in you somewhere, sitting heavy, waiting.

Most of what Pip has eaten sits like that. Stones. She ate a baker's memory of kneading dough once, off his sweaty wrist in a market crowd, and for three days after, her hands moved like they knew flour, and she didn't know why, and she cried about it a little, quietly, into a bread roll she stole from that same baker, which felt fair somehow.

She ate a soldier's last morning. She ate a wedding. She ate a funeral by accident, from a grieving man who grabbed her hand too hard, and that one sat in her chest for a year like a wet coat.

She ate hundreds of things. Thousands maybe. And she remembers none of them, none of the shapes or names or faces, only the stones. Only the weights.

Except now.

Now the Spire's breath is in her mouth, and every stone in her is turning over at once, like birds startled up off a field.

* * *

The first thing she remembers is small, so it comes first:

A woman named Odette who smelled of lavender and lies. Odette used to feed pigeons on her balcony and tell them secrets because she thought pigeons couldn't talk. Odette's memory tastes of crumbs and guilt. Pip ate it off a stolen glove two winters ago. *Odette poisoned her sister's husband,* the stone says suddenly, clear as a bell, *and told the pigeons instead of a priest.* Pip laughs out loud, right there in the flooded dark, a high bright sound that echoes weird against the drowned walls. Sable says, "What? What's funny?" and Pip says "a lady and some pigeons" and he says "kid, I swear—" but he's smiling, she can hear him smiling, and then his smile falls off because the Spire's breath keeps coming and Pip's knees go soft as porridge.

The second thing she remembers is bigger:

A harbor town called Brindlewick. Fish guts and gulls and a boy named Tam who could whistle through his fingers so loud it hurt teeth. Pip ate Brindlewick whole — not the town, obviously, towns are too big to eat, but a fisherman's forty years of it, off a rope he'd held since he was nine. She'd been so hungry that day. Hungrier than hungry. The kind of hollow where your own self starts dissolving and you have to fill it with somebody or you stop being anybody.

Forty years of Brindlewick unfolds in her like a paper flower dropped in water. The storm of '61. Tam's whistle. The smell of low tide. A dog named Captain who was afraid of the sea, which everyone found hilarious and sad.

"Oh," Pip says softly. "Oh, that's nice."

And then the third thing comes, and the third thing is not nice at all.

* * *

The wolf's memory doesn't unfold. It *detonates*.

It's been in her longest — no, wait, that's wrong, it can't be, she only met Noor weeks ago — but it feels oldest, oldest of everything, older than Brindlewick, older than Odette's pigeons, older than any of the stones, because it isn't really hers, it never was, it's been riding along inside her like a passenger who paid no fare, and now the Spire's breath reaches into her chest and *wakes it up.*

Pip falls down.

Sable catches her, mostly. Her knees hit shallow black water anyway and she doesn't feel it because she is standing in a war.

* * *

A war.

Not a war like soldiers-and-drums. Pip has eaten soldiers; she knows those wars, muddy and loud and full of letters home. This war is older than mud. This war is fought in a city that is not this city, or maybe it is, maybe it's this city when it was young and its bones were above water.

There is a wolf in it.

Not a wolf like dogs-with-long-noses. A wolf like weather. A wolf whose body is made partly of the idea of winter, whose eyes are two moons, whose voice doesn't come through ears but through the ground itself, up through the soles of feet, and what the voice says — over and over, patient, enormous — is:

*Remember. Someone must remember.*

Because the wolf is dying. Not dying like stopping. Dying like *spilling*. It has lived so long and loved so much — and gods love strangely, hugely, they love whole harvests and whole griefs at once — that its memory has grown too big for its body, the way a fire grows too big for a hearth, and it is coming apart at the seams of itself, forgetting its own name from the edges inward.

So it asked for help.

That's the part that makes Pip whimper, later, when she can whimper again. It *asked*. It didn't take. It went to the people of the young city — the ones whose ancestors it had sheltered through famines, whose children it carried across rivers in its mouth like kittens, gentle, so gentle — and it asked them to hold pieces of its memory. Just pieces. To keep them safe until it healed. It split itself up among trusted hands, and it lay down beneath the city to sleep and mend, and it trusted.

And the founders of the Guild —

* * *

Nine of them. Pip sees them the way you see things in eaten memories, all edges and feeling and a few terrible details burned sharp. Nine men and women in gray robes, standing around a table, and on the table, glowing soft as hearth-coals, the wolf's memories in vessels of glass and clay and bone.

They were supposed to be keepers. That word is in the fragment, worn like a coin rubbed smooth: *keepers, keepers, we swore it, keepers.*

One of them — the tall one, the one whose face Pip can't see but whose *hands* she can see, long clever fingers, fingers that look horribly like Master Corvane's hands, though that's impossible, that's centuries ago — one of them stands and says words that taste like ice sliding down the back of your collar:

"Why should we give it back?"

And the others are shocked, Pip can feel their shock, eight hearts going quiet — but underneath the shock, Pip can feel something worse. Something interested. Something that wants.

"We could wake it slowly," the tall one says. "We could wake it *grateful*. Or..." and here the hands spread over the glowing vessels, protective, covetous, "...we could keep it asleep forever, and hold what it dreams."

Two of the nine say no. Pip feels them say no with their whole spines. One of them is a woman with ink-stained fingers — a ledger-keeper, the first keeper of ledgers, and her refusal tastes like iron and tears — and the other is a young courier-type man with restless feet.

Those two are dealt with. Pip doesn't see how. She's glad she doesn't see how. But she feels the shape of it, the cold shape, and she understands suddenly, in a way that makes her want to throw up everything she's ever eaten including actual food:

The Guild was never a shop. The Guild was a cage. Every bottle sold in that candlelit counting room, every noble buying pretty forgettings and pretty rememberings, every ledger line in Noor's neat hand — all of it built on top of a theft. All of it built to hide a theft. Centuries of bookkeeping stacked over a sleeping god's stolen heart like blankets over a birdcage.

And the wolf, down under the water, dreaming — the wolf doesn't even know it was robbed. It sleeps and mends and waits for its keepers to bring its memory home, and its keepers became owners, and its owners became merchants, and the merchants' latest master wants to wake it *grateful* after all, just like the tall one with the clever fingers said, so long ago.

Grateful. As if you could steal a god's heart and then expect thanks for giving it back.

* * *

"Noor," Pip says.

Her voice comes out cracked and tiny. Sable is crouched next to her in the black water, holding her shoulders, saying her name — "Pip, Pip, kid, stay with me" — but she pushes at him, scrambles upright, dripping, shaking.

"Noor," she says again, louder, and Noor turns from the front where she's been walking with the ghost-light flickering around her head like a halo made of trouble. "Noor, I remembered. I remembered *everything*, I remembered the war, I remembered—"

"Pip." Noor is kneeling in front of her now, hands on Pip's face, and her palms are cold but her eyes are scared in a way that means she'll listen. "Slowly. What did you remember?"

"The wolf," Pip says, and the word comes out of her huge, filling the drowned corridor, and somewhere far off in the deep water something enormous shifts its weight, listening. "The wolf asked them to help. It *asked nicely*. And the first Guild people took its memories and said they'd keep them and they kept them instead, they kept them like *money*." Her breath hitches. "There were two who said no. A woman with ink on her hands and a man who ran. They tried to save a piece. They hid a piece. In a person. In a—"

She stops.

Everyone stops. Even the water seems to stop moving, which water should not do.

Noor has gone very still, in the way that Noor goes still, like a page that doesn't want to be read yet.

"In a vessel," Pip whispers. The stone of it settles into place inside her, the heaviest stone of all, clicking against all the others. "In a made person. With a fake childhood and a cage-name. The ink-woman's piece. The last free piece. That's what's inside me, that's what I tasted the day I met you, that's why I wouldn't leave—"

"*Pip.*" Noor's voice does something strange. It isn't angry. It's worse than angry. It's the voice of someone reading their own name in a ledger written by strangers. "You've known? Since the market? Since the first day?"

"I didn't *know*-know," Pip says, wretched. "I just knew it like you know a plum. There was a plum. I couldn't find the plum. The Spire found the plum." She wipes her nose on her sleeve, which is filthy, which is fine, everything down here is filthy. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't say. Saying things is hard. Things get heavy when you say them."

Noor pulls her in and holds her, hard, and Pip lets her, and over Noor's shoulder the drowned corridor stretches away into dark, and far below them, beneath fathoms of black water, something vast and wounded turns over in its sleep, and the ground hums with it, and the hum tastes like the beginning of a name.

Sable clears his throat. "Right," he says, in the voice he uses when he's pretending not to be terrified, which Pip knows very well by now, having catalogued all his voices like little stones of her own. "So the child's been carrying a god's grief this whole time, and our ledger-keeper here is apparently the world's most complicated hiding spot, and there's a madman with an army of Retrievers somewhere behind us. Is that everything? Anyone else got a bombshell? Now's the time."

Pip opens her mouth.

Because actually, yes. Actually, there is one more thing. The smallest, sharpest splinter of the wolf-fragment, the detail that burned brightest, the one she saved for last the way you save the best bite:

The tall founder with the clever hands — the thief of thieves, the first Corvane-shaped shadow — he wasn't talking only to the other eight at that table. He was talking to someone kneeling in the corner. Writing it all down. And the kneeling one's thoughts, which leaked into the fragment like ink through wet paper, were thinking a single sentence over and over, a sentence that has been waiting in Pip's chest for centuries without her knowing:

*He will do this again. Whoever wears his face will do this again. Someone must write it down where he cannot burn it.*

"I know where the real ledger is," Pip says. "The one he can't burn. The first one. The ink-woman's daughter hid it, and the hiding place is—" and she points, arm steady, finger aimed straight through the black water toward the heart of the Sunken Spire, "—*there*."

The water shivers.

Somewhere below, the wolf begins, very slowly, to open one eye.
