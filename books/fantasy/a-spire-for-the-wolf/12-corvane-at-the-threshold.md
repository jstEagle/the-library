## 12. Corvane at the Threshold

Sable Quinn had lit ten thousand lamps in his life, and he had never once been thanked for any of them. So he supposed it was fitting that the bravest thing he'd ever do would also go entirely unappreciated.

"Say it again," he said, "the part about how I'm a hero."

"You're a hero," Pip said, not looking up from the map she was chewing. Not reading. Chewing. The girl ate memories, and apparently the memory of the drowned quarter's layout had a flavor she found agreeable, because she'd been gnawing the corner of the courier's map for the better part of an hour while Noor traced their route with a fingertip gone white from pressing too hard.

"I didn't hear it the first time."

"I didn't say it the first time."

"Then we're agreed you're a liar, and I'm a hero."

Noor didn't laugh. That was the trouble with Noor — she carried her humor somewhere deep, like coins sewn into a hem, and you had to know where to press to hear them jingle. She was crouched at the edge of the sluice gate, the last dry threshold before the drowned quarter opened up into black water and older black stone, and her face had the particular stillness of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"It's coming down," she said. "The gate. Listen."

Sable listened. Under the drip and groan of the undercity there was a sound like a giant grinding his teeth — stone shifting against stone, slow and patient. The sluice gate was a relic of some engineer's ambition, a hundred tons of bronze-banded granite meant to hold back the flood when the river had been rerouted and the quarter below drowned on purpose. Somebody, a long time ago, had decided that a neighborhood was worth less than a reservoir. Sable had a professional appreciation for the cruelty of that kind of arithmetic.

"It's been coming down for two hundred years," he said.

"It's coming down *faster* now." Noor looked up at him, and in the lamplight her eyes did that thing they did — the thing where you felt seen by someone who had spent her whole life unseen, and so had gotten very, very good at seeing. "The Retrievers are in the upper galleries. Pip heard them."

"Heard them *smell* them," Pip corrected, spitting out a scrap of map. "Retrievers smell like cold iron and lying. Like a church that owes you money."

"And they're following us."

"They're following *you*," Sable said. "You're the one carrying a god in your ribs and a dead woman in your head. I'm just the man carrying the lamp."

"You're the man carrying the *light*," Noor said, with a small fierce precision that embarrassed him, and he turned away to fuss with the lamp in question — a good lamp, a lamplighter's lamp, brass and scarred glass, the only thing the Guild had let him keep when they'd taken his decade.

That was the thing about Sable Quinn that nobody, including Sable Quinn, liked to say out loud: he had sold ten years of himself to pay a debt, and he couldn't remember what the debt had been. He had a receipt for his own life and no memory of the purchase. Some nights he lay awake inventing the missing years — a wife, maybe, or a brother, or just a dog he'd loved — and every invention hurt worse than the last, because the not-knowing was a wound that kept deciding to be fresh.

But he remembered this: three years ago, before the disgrace, before the ledgers, a woman with a ledger-slate and a face like a closed door had found him weeping in the counting room corridor, drunk on the specific despair of a man who has just signed away his own yesterdays, and she had — what? Said something kind. Given him her scarf, maybe. He couldn't remember. That was rather the point. But he knew the debt was real, because debts to Noor Thackeray were the only kind he trusted anymore, and when he'd seen her running through the undercity with terror stitched into her shoulders, he had followed her without deciding to, the way water follows a slope.

So. Here he was. Holding a lamp at the mouth of the drowned quarter while a god's memory slept in his friend and a child who ate the past picked her teeth with a map.

* * *

They came at the fourth bell, though there were no bells down there, only the water keeping its own slow time.

Sable heard them the way Pip had promised — a wrongness in the echo, boots that fell too evenly, like men who had been trained out of the habit of being people. He counted six shadows in the gallery above, and then a seventh that didn't walk like the others. That one walked like a man reading a room he had already purchased.

Vess Corvane.

Sable had seen the Guild's master maybe four times in fifteen years, always at a distance, always in that grey coat that managed to be more expensive than finery by refusing to be finery at all. Up close, descending the gallery stairs with his hands folded like a man arriving slightly early for his own funeral, Corvane was worse than the stories. He wasn't cruel-looking. That was the horror of him. He looked *patient*, the way a ledger is patient, the way a debt is patient.

"Quinn," Corvane said, and Sable's stomach dropped, because the man knew his name. Of course he did. The Guild kept ledgers of everything, and Sable Quinn had been a line item. "The lamplighter. Ten years, sold at a fair price. I remember your signature. You had a good hand, before the drink ruined it."

"Before the *grief* ruined it," Sable said, "but who's counting." He lifted the lamp a little, so its light caught Corvane's face. Old habit. You always gave a man light when you wanted to see what he'd do with it. "You've come a long way to bully a lamplighter, Master Corvane."

"I've come for the vessel." Corvane's gaze went past him, down the dark throat of the sluice, to where Noor and Pip had already slipped into the water and the dark beyond it — because of course they had, Sable had told them to go, had told them with the full authority of a disgraced lamplighter that this was lamplighter's work, gate work, the work of a man whose entire profession was standing in the dark so other people could pass through it. "She has something that belongs to the city. To its *betterment*. You've no notion what's at stake."

"Never do," Sable agreed. "That's the beauty of it. You lot always need the stakes explained, and the rest of us just need the lamp lit."

Corvane studied him for a long moment, and then did the worst thing: he smiled. Gently. Like a man about to explain arithmetic to a child.

"Step aside, Quinn. I'll even write it kindly in the record. *Obstructed no one. A minor figure.* You've spent fifteen years being a minor figure. It needn't hurt."

And here was the thing — the thing Sable would think about later, in whatever cell they put him in, turning it over like a coin he couldn't spend: for one whole second, he wanted to. Step aside. Be a minor figure. Live.

Then he thought about Noor's face when she'd said *you're the man carrying the light*, and about the scarf he couldn't remember, the kindness he couldn't remember, the debt that was the truest thing left in his ledger. He thought about how Noor Thackeray had spent her whole life being nobody's entry, and how she was down in that black water right now, terrified, walking toward a truth that might unmake her, and she deserved — at minimum — a head start.

"Master Corvane," Sable said, "do you know what a lamplighter actually does? Everyone thinks it's lighting. It's not. It's *timing*. You light the lamps so the dark arrives on schedule. You make the night *punctual*." He reached up, and with the ease of fifteen years' practice, unscrewed the reservoir valve on the lamp's base. "And every trade has its tricks."

"What are you doing?"

"Overtime."

* * *

The trick was this: lamplighter's oil wasn't lamp oil. It was a Guild blend, volatile as a grudge, and a full reservoir, cracked and ignited, would burn hot enough to soften bronze. And the sluice gate was bronze-banded. And the gate, Noor had said, was already failing. And a hundred tons of granite, hurried, would come down across the sluice passage like a door slammed in the face of God.

Sable had explained all this to Noor an hour ago, and she had said *absolutely not*, and he had said *absolutely*, and they had settled it the way all found families settle things: he'd waited until she was in the water.

"Quinn." Corvane's voice had changed. The patience had gone out of it, and underneath was something older and hungrier. "Whatever you're — *stop him*."

The Retrievers came down the stairs fast and silent, six of them, cold iron and lying, just as advertised. Sable backed toward the gate mechanism, lamp held out like a torch, and did the arithmetic he was best at: six Retrievers, one lamplighter, one valve, one gate, and maybe forty seconds.

He could live forty seconds. Probably. The word *probably* had carried him this far.

The first Retriever reached him and Sable swung the lamp low and hard, catching the man across the knee — the knee, always the knee, a lamplighter learned early that you didn't fight people, you fought their architecture — and the man went down with a sound like a dropped toolbox. The second got a handful of Sable's collar, and Sable let him have it, let himself be hauled close, because close was where the lamp's glass wouldn't shatter on anything he needed intact.

"Tell Noor—" he started, and then laughed, because tell her what? Thanks for the scarf? Sorry about the decade? *You were the best thing I found in the years I can't remember?* "Tell her the lamp's paid off."

He smashed the reservoir against the gate's bronze band.

The oil went up with a *whump* he felt in his teeth, a flower of white-gold fire that turned the whole sluice chamber noon-bright for one impossible second, and in that second Sable saw everything: the Retrievers recoiling, Corvane's face lit and furious and — for the first time — *afraid*, and above them all the great gate shuddering as its bronze bands softened, the granite beginning its long-delayed fall.

Someone grabbed him. Arms like dock rope. He was hauled backward, off his feet, the fire roaring, the gate groaning its two-hundred-year groan at last finding its ending, and Sable did the only reasonable thing left to a man in his position.

He held on to the gate chain.

It was a stupid thing to hold. It was bolted to the falling stone; holding it was like holding the tail of a comet. But the chain ran through an iron ring bolted to the *wall*, and if he wrapped it twice around his forearm and threw his weight against the ring — if he was the last small stubborn thing between a hundred tons of stone and the passage where Noor had gone — the gate would jam, catch, hesitate, buy her another minute, two minutes, whatever a life weighed in minutes.

"Quinn!" Corvane, shouting over the fire. "Let go! You'll be crushed with it!"

"Occupational hazard," Sable said, and wrapped the chain.

The gate came down. Not all at once — stone that old doesn't fall, it *surrenders*, in lurches and shrieks, and each lurch dragged the chain through the ring and the ring through Sable's grip and the grip through what remained of his dignity. He got one arm free enough to feel it tear. He got his feet braced against the ring's mounting and pushed, a lamplighter's push, the push of a man who has spent his life holding ladders steady for other people's benefit, and the gate *caught*, jammed in its ruined frame, half-fallen, a crooked door of granite sealing the passage to a crack.

Through the crack: black water, and silence, and no Retrievers, and no Corvane, and no Noor, which meant she was gone, which meant it had worked.

Sable slid down the wall, laughing, which he realized distantly was because he'd cracked a rib, or several, and the laugh was the body's way of filing a complaint. The fire was guttering. The Retrievers were regrouping on the far side of the jammed stone, and after a while — a long while, long enough for Sable to think about the scarf, and the dog he'd maybe owned, and the name he'd maybe been called by someone he'd maybe loved — they pried a gap wide enough for a man to come through with a pry bar, and then a lantern, and then Corvane himself, stepping over the rubble with his grey coat immaculate, which seemed frankly rude.

The Guild's master crouched in front of him. Up close, in the lantern light, Corvane's face was doing something complicated — fury and respect having a quiet argument behind his eyes.

"You bought them an hour," Corvane said. "Perhaps two. It won't matter. The drowned quarter has one other entrance, and I've held its key for twenty years."

"Then I bought them an hour," Sable said, "which is more than I bought myself, most weeks."

Corvane studied him. "Why? You don't remember her. You don't remember *anything*. You're a man defending a debt he can't even recall incurring. It's sentimental nonsense. It's — " and here his voice did something almost wondering, almost wounded — "it's *unledgered*."

Sable grinned through the blood in his mouth. "Aye," he said. "Best kind."

They took the lamp first. Of course they did. A Retriever picked it out of the rubble with strange gentleness, checked the wick, and handed it to Corvane, who turned it over in his hands like a man reading a signature.

Then they took Sable Quinn, and the last thing he saw of the drowned quarter's threshold was his own lamp going dark in a stranger's hands — and the strange, stubborn comfort of knowing that somewhere down that black water, in the dark he'd made punctual, Noor Thackeray was walking toward her name, and she was not walking alone.

*Hold the gate*, he told himself, as they marched him up into the iron-smelling dark. *Hold the gate. It's what you're for.*

He only hoped somebody would tell her he'd been punctual about it.
