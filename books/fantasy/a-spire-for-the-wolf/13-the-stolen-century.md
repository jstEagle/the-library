## 13. The Stolen Century

The heart of the Sunken Spire was not what I expected.

I had imagined something vast and terrible—a cathedral of drowned stone, maybe, or a pit where the god-wolf's bones curled like a sleeping serpent. Instead the ghost led me through a doorway of black coral into a room no larger than the Guild's counting room, round-walled and dry as paper, lit by a soft gold light that came from everywhere and nowhere. The air tasted of ink. Old ink, the kind that has sat in a pot too long and gone bitter.

Shelves lined every wall from floor to ceiling. On them stood vials.

Thousands of them.

My hand went to my pocket before I could stop it—to the lullaby vial I'd stolen from my own counting room a lifetime ago, back when I was still a woman who catalogued other people's yesterdays and told herself she wanted nothing. The glass there was warm. It had been warm for days.

"This is it," I said. "This is what he wants."

"Not all of it," said Wren.

Wren—the courier's ghost, who had lived behind my eyes for two weeks now like a lodger who never paid rent—stepped out of the shadow of the door. She could do that here, take shape at the edges of things, a woman made of candle-smoke and salt water. In the flooded quarter she'd been only a voice. In the Spire she was almost solid, and looking at her almost-solid face did something painful to my chest, because she looked tired in a way the dead shouldn't be able to look.

"The shelves are the archive," she said. "Guardians' records. Ten centuries of watch-notes, oaths, names. But the room isn't the point." She turned slowly, and the golden light bent around her like water around a stone. "You're standing on the point."

I looked down. The floor beneath my feet was a single slab of pale stone, and carved into it—so worn by age that I'd mistaken it for decoration—was a spiral. A wolf's eye, drawn as a spiral, drawn as a door.

"Oh," I said, very quietly.

"Sit down, Noor," Wren said. "Or don't. What I have to show you works either way. But you should know it's going to hurt."

"You've mentioned."

"I'm going to mention it again." Her smoke-face did something that might have been a smile, on someone who still had the equipment for smiling. "Because you're the kind of person who thinks being warned about pain is the same thing as being prepared for it, and I've been in your head long enough to know better."

I almost laughed. That was the terrible thing about ghosts—they got familiar. Two weeks of sharing a skull and Wren knew me the way no one had ever bothered to know Noor Thackeray, keeper of ledgers, invisible woman, thief of one small vial of a mother's lullaby.

"I sat down," I reported, folding myself cross-legged beside the spiral door. Above me, through the drowned shaft we'd climbed, the city groaned. Somewhere up there Sable was holding a gate with his bare hands and his stubborn, half-erased heart, and Pip was—somewhere; Pip was always somewhere, the way weather is somewhere—and Corvane was coming. I could feel him coming the way you feel a storm in your teeth.

"Good," Wren said. "Now. Close your eyes. And whatever you see—remember it's true. That's the part people forget."

* * *

She didn't show me pictures, exactly. She showed me *witnessing*.

It began with cold. A cold so old it predated winter, predated the word for winter, a cold at the bottom of the world before the world had finished deciding what it was. And moving through that cold: something enormous, and warm-hearted, and *awake*—a mind like a landscape, like a country that loved itself. The wolf-god. Not a beast. A presence. It walked the seabed when the sea was young and its memory was the memory of everything it saw, and everything it saw, it *kept*, because keeping was its nature. It was the world's rememberer. When a mountain eroded, the wolf remembered the mountain. When the first fish died, the wolf remembered the fish. Grief and wonder, held together, forever.

I felt my own eyes sting. I wasn't crying. Something in me was.

"Then they came," Wren said, her voice arriving from very far away and very close, both at once. "The first settlers. The founders' founders. They found the wolf's pool—the place where it slept between its walkings—and they were afraid. Not of the wolf. Of the memory. Do you understand? They weren't afraid a god would eat them. They were afraid that if the god ever woke fully—if it remembered everything at once—it would grieve so completely that the grief would drown the coast. Every death it had ever witnessed, released in a single tide."

"That's—" I began.

"A reasonable fear," Wren agreed. "That's the awful part. It was reasonable. So they did the reasonable, unforgivable thing. They took the memory out."

The witnessing turned. Cold became a knife of light, became hands—human hands, careful, reverent, *guilty*—and the great warm country of the wolf's mind was poured, drop by drop, century by century, out of the sleeping god and into vessels. Human vessels. People made for the purpose, raised for the purpose, their own histories hollowed out and replaced with a fabricated childhood, a fabricated name, a life that fit neatly in a ledger—

"No," I said. Out loud. In the dark. "No, I know where this goes."

"Yes," Wren said gently. "You do."

The founders built an order to guard the vessels. An order of keepers and counters, sworn to protect the memory until the day it could be safely returned—or safely borne forever. They kept ledgers. They bottled memories for the grieving and the desperate, because a guild that guards memory learns to trade in it, and because the work paid for the guarding. For nine hundred years they were faithful. I watched the faithful centuries pass like weather: robed figures lighting candles, carrying keys, teaching each vessel-child to read, to count, to be nobody, to be safe.

And then, three generations ago, the ledgers changed hands.

I felt the corruption the way you feel a floorboard give way—not a dramatic break, just a slow, quiet rot, a decision made by comfortable men in a comfortable room: *the memory is safer with us than in anyone else's hands.* And then, a decade later, softer, more poisonous: *the memory is wasted, asleep. Think what it could do. Think what it could be worth.* And then, finally, the sentence that made my teeth ache with fury: *think what we could do with a god.*

"They stopped being guardians," Wren said, "and started being owners. Vess Corvane is not a corruption of the founding purpose, Noor. He is its logical conclusion. He read the old ledgers—all the ones you've spent your life filing—and he understood them perfectly. He understands exactly what was stolen, and from whom, and why. He simply decided theft was a business model."

The witnessing ended. I opened my eyes in the gold-lit room with my fists pressed against my thighs and my breath coming hard, and the first coherent thing I managed to say was:

"He's going to feed it to them."

"Yes."

"At the Concord. The winter Concord, when every noble house sends a representative to the Guild to buy forgetting for the year's scandals. He's going to pour the wolf's memory into the wine."

Wren's smoke-face regarded me with something like respect. "Two weeks ago you couldn't lie to a man without your ears going red."

"I've had practice lately." My voice sounded strange to me. Level. Cold. Like Corvane's voice, and I hated that, and I let it stay anyway because the alternative was screaming. "Think it through with me. He doesn't want to wake the wolf himself—he can't. The memory burns out any vessel that releases it; you told me. He knows it would kill him. So he doesn't take it into himself. He *distributes* it. A sip for each house. Each lord and lady wakes with a fragment of the god in their blood—a fragment of grief older than their family name. And every one of them will owe the man who gave it to them. Every one of them will feel, forever after, that he holds something inside them that they can't reach without him."

"And the wolf?"

"Wakes scattered," I said. The arithmetic assembled itself in my head the way ledger-columns always had, clean and damning. "A god remembers in pieces, through a hundred frightened aristocrats who each think they're special. It wakes *owned*. Divided into shares. Corvane doesn't get to rule the wolf. He gets something better—he gets to rule everyone who carries it. The god becomes collateral." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "He'll call it stability. He'll call it stewardship. He'll stand up at the Concord with his gentle voice and say he's saved the city from the wolf's grief, and he'll even believe it, because he's read ten centuries of ledgers and learned that the worst things are always done by people who keep excellent records."

Wren was quiet a moment. "There it is," she said softly. "Righteous fury. I was wondering when you'd get around to it. You've been so polite about everything—hiding the body, fleeing the Retrievers, breaking into the vault. All very measured. But fury looks good on you, Noor. It fits. Which makes sense, given whose memory you're carrying."

I looked down at the spiral door under my crossed legs. The wolf's eye. The door. The place where a god's stolen self lay coiled inside a woman who had been manufactured to hold it and told she was nobody.

"It was never going to be destroyed, was it," I said. "In the old days. The faithful centuries. They never planned to give it back."

"Some did. Some tried. There are entries—" Wren gestured at the endless shelves, "—guardians who argued for return, generation after generation, always voted down. Always the same reason: too dangerous, too soon, not yet. Nine hundred years of *not yet*. The wolf slept on, emptied, walking its dreams at the bottom of the sea, missing the only thing that made it a god instead of an animal. Its memory. Its *keeping*." Her voice frayed. "Do you know what it is, to witness a creature lose its remembering? I carried the map for six years. Six years of watching Corvane's ledgers from the inside. But this—" she touched her own translucent chest, "—this is the first time I've seen the beginning. And I find that I am angry too, and I have been dead for two weeks, and I didn't think I had any anger left to spend."

We sat together in the gold light, a living woman full of a god's grief and a dead woman full of her own, and for a while neither of us spoke, and it was, absurdly, a companionable silence. Found family comes in strange shapes. Mine currently included a ghost and a feral child and a lamplighter holding a door somewhere above us with his shoulders and his sins.

Then I stood up, because my legs insisted, and walked to the nearest shelf, and looked properly at the vials there for the first time. Guardian notes, Wren had said. I pulled one at random. Inside the glass swirled a grey-gold light that moved the way snow moves behind a window—slow, patient, endless. A label, in a dead guardian's careful hand:

*Year 412 of the Keeping. The vessel Ede sleeps soundly. The wolf dreams of green hills that no longer exist. Note for the future: she smiled in her sleep tonight. Whatever we have taken from the god, some part of it is glad.*

I put the vial back with both hands, carefully, the way you handle something sacred, and discovered my vision had blurred.

"They wrote it down," I said. "They *knew*. Century after century, some keeper on night-watch noticed the gladness, the grief, the wonder leaking through—and they wrote it down and shelved it and went back to bed." I wiped my face with my sleeve, furious at myself for crying, furious at the crying for being right. "That's the whole Guild in one shelf, isn't it. Everything true, recorded perfectly, and none of it ever *acted upon*. We were a religion of bookkeeping. We worshipped memory by refusing to touch it."

"And now you understand what Corvane is," Wren said. "Not the opposite of the Guild. The Guild, grown honest about its appetite."

Above us, through miles of drowned stone and dark water, something boomed. Long, deep, rhythmic. Not the city settling. A gate—Sable's gate—taking blows.

I closed my eyes and listened, doing the arithmetic of distance and collapse, and arrived at a number I didn't like.

"How long?" I asked.

"If he brought the ram-wards? Hours. If he brought the Meridian Key?" Wren's form flickered, guttered, steadied. "Less."

"Then I need to decide," I said. "Now. Before he arrives. Because he can't force me to release it, and he can't force me to destroy it, and I promised a ghost a favor and a child a home and a fool a rescue, and I am *done*, Wren—I am done letting other people write my ledger. Nine hundred years of guardians deciding *not yet*. Corvane deciding *mine*. Everyone deciding for the wolf, and for me, and never once asking either of us." I opened my eyes. The gold light had begun, very faintly, to pulse—slow, deep, rhythmic, matching nothing in the room except, I realized with a shiver, the beat of my own heart. "So I'm going to ask."

Wren went very still. Even ghosts can go still.

"Ask *it*?" she said.

"The wolf. The memory. Whatever's down there wearing my heartbeat." I knelt and laid both palms flat on the spiral door. The stone was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been warm for days—it had been warm since the courier died saying my true name, since before that, probably, since always, since the first night some guilty keeper had poured a god's gladness into a sleeping vessel-child and written *she smiled in her sleep tonight*. "Everyone in ten centuries made decisions about this memory," I said. "I'd like to be the first one to consult it."

The spiral under my hands began, very slowly, to turn.

Far above, the booming stopped—which was somehow worse than the booming—and through the gold-lit quiet I heard, thin as thread, carried down the drowned shaft on water and stone: the sound of many boots, and a gentle, reasonable voice, giving orders.

Corvane had arrived early.
