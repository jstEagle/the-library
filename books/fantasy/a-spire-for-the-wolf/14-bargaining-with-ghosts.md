## 14. Bargaining with Ghosts

The Spire did not echo. That was the first thing Noor had noticed, and the thing she still could not get used to. Every other ruin she had ever entered—the drowned quarter below, the collapsed arcades of the old merchant district—had announced her presence back to her in a hundred small voices, drips and skitters and the groan of settling stone. The Sunken Spire simply absorbed her footsteps and gave nothing back, as though the building itself had been taught discretion.

She sat in a chamber the ghost called the Antechamber of Accounts, because of course it was called that, because everything in this place sounded like it had been named by a Guild clerk with delusions of grandeur. Water ran somewhere out of sight, a sound like a patient whisper. Light came from no lamp she could find—pale, sourceless, the color of memory just before you lose your grip on it.

"You're brooding," said Wren, from inside her skull. "I can tell because your breathing does that thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you breathe like someone who is trying very hard to be calm about breathing."

Noor huffed—a laugh, mostly. "You're inside my head. You could at least have the courtesy to pretend you're not taking notes."

"I'm always taking notes," Wren said. "It's what I am now, apparently. A courier who died mid-delivery and got filed away in somebody's brain instead of the afterlife. Honestly, of all the misdeliveries." A pause, and then, gentler: "I didn't mean to stay, you know. When I pressed the map into your hands. I meant to go on. Wherever it is we go on to."

Noor looked down at her hands. In the sourceless light they seemed made of the same pale stuff as everything else, as if she were already halfway to being a ghost herself.

"I know," she said.

"I felt it, when the wards read your blood. Like a door opening that had been waiting for me specifically. And then—" The ghost's voice did something complicated, the way voices do when the person speaking them is trying not to feel something enormous. "Then I saw what was under this city. What *you* are. And I thought, well. Someone has to see her through it."

"Someone has to," Noor agreed. "Though I notice you've never actually asked whether I wanted an audience."

"You talk to yourself constantly. I assumed you'd welcome the company."

"That's slander."

"It's testimony from an eyewitness."

Noor laughed properly this time, and the laugh bounced off nothing and died in the absorbing dark, and for a moment the silence afterward felt less like erasure and more like privacy—like the Spire was letting them have this one small unguarded thing.

* * *

They had been in the Spire three days. Or what she guessed were days; time down here moved like honey off a cold spoon. Pip slept curled against the outer gate, having refused absolutely to come further in—"the walls are too *loud*," the child had said, hands over her ears, and Noor hadn't argued, because Pip's ears heard things Noor's couldn't, and because the gate chamber was defensible, and because Sable was there.

*Sable.* She turned the thought over carefully, the way you'd turn a cracked egg. Sable Quinn, disgraced lamplighter, man of exactly one coat and infinite stubbornness, was standing watch between them and Corvane's Retrievers with a lantern, a pry-bar, and the particular cheerfulness of a man who has decided that worrying is a luxury he cannot currently afford. He'd kissed her forehead before she came down into the deep halls—quick, awkward, like a man signing a document he wasn't sure he had the authority to sign—and said, "Come back up before the tea goes off. I found actual tea. Don't ask me how. Lamplighter secrets."

She had promised she would. She was beginning to suspect promises were heavier things than she'd been raised to believe.

"Wren," she said now, to the empty air, because she'd learned that the ghost preferred to be addressed aloud even though the words were unnecessary—it gave their conversations shape, edges, a feeling of two people rather than one person and her own cleverness. "Tell me again what happens when the memory goes free."

"Ah." A long silence. Somewhere in the deep water, something shifted its weight. "No."

"No?"

"I've told you twice. You made that face both times. The face where you go very still and start doing arithmetic behind your eyes, except you're bad at arithmetic, so really you're just doing worry with extra steps."

"I am *excellent* at arithmetic. I kept the Guild's ledgers for six years."

"Ledgers," Wren said, "are arithmetic with the interesting parts removed."

"The interesting parts being the numbers?"

"The numbers being the parts where you find out what things cost."

Noor stood. Pacing helped; she'd discovered that the ghost tracked her by her movement through the pale light, a faint brightening wherever she walked, like frost forming in the shape of her steps. "Wren Ashgrave," she said—using the courier's name deliberately, because names mattered down here, because every name spoken in the Spire seemed to land somewhere and take root—"you carried this map across half the country with Retrievers on your heels and a wound in your side that should have killed you a mile sooner than it did. You died on my floor rather than let them take it. Do not tell me you're shy about the cost now."

Another silence. Longer. When Wren spoke again, all the levity had drained out of the voice, and what remained was very quiet and very old, older than the three days Noor had known her.

"When you release it," the ghost said, "the memory will need somewhere to go. It has spent four hundred years folded up inside a human vessel, pressed flat and small so it wouldn't burn the world awake. Unfolding it takes a hand. It takes a vessel. It takes—" the ghost hesitated, and Noor realized with a strange, floating clarity that the hesitation was not reluctance but grief, "—it takes someone standing at the door when it opens, holding it open, while everything that was kept pours through."

"And the someone burns out," Noor said. Not a question.

"Vessels that carry it break. That's what *vessel* means. That's why they hid it in a person instead of keeping it in the vaults—because a vault can be robbed, but a life wears out on its own schedule, and the founders told themselves that was mercy." The ghost's voice hardened. "It wasn't mercy. It was accounting."

*Accounting.* The word went through Noor like a splinter finding its old channel. Six years of ledgers. Six years of writing down what people paid and what they received and calling the difference fair. Six years of bottling mothers' lullabies and first loves and last words, and stamping each vial with a number, and telling herself the numbers were neutral, that the ledger didn't judge, that keeping the accounts clean was its own kind of virtue.

"I pocketed a lullaby once," she said, to no one, to the pale air. "Early on. A mother singing to a child. I don't know whose. I don't know why I took it. I just—couldn't stand the thought of it sitting on a shelf."

"I know," Wren said softly. "I've been living in your head, remember. I've heard you hum it."

"I hum it wrong."

"You hum it like someone who only ever got the outside of the song. Which is, I think, the whole tragedy of you in one sentence, and also the whole hope of you."

Noor stopped pacing. The light of her settled around her feet like a held breath.

"So if I open the door," she said, "I die."

"If you open the door alone, almost certainly. The memory isn't cruel, Noor. It's just *true*, and truth at that volume scours. Four centuries of a god's grief, a god's joy, a god's memory of running under stars that haven't existed since before this city had a name. Any single vessel that tries to hold it while it passes through—" Wren didn't finish. Didn't need to.

* * *

She should have been afraid. She catalogued herself, the way she catalogued everything, checking the inventory of her own chest—and found fear there, yes, but underneath it, shockingly, something else. Something that felt almost like relief.

"Do you want to know the terrible part?" she said. "Part of me is glad."

"Noor—"

"Hear me out. I was *made*. You know that now. We both know it. Some clerk in some century decided the wolf's memory needed a hiding place that could walk and eat and keep books, and they built me, and they wrote me a name, and they gave me a childhood like a receipt for a purchase." Her voice stayed level. Practiced detachment; six years of it, worn smooth as river glass. "Everything I am was filed by someone else. If I open that door and burn out, then at least the burning is mine. The one entry in the ledger written in my own hand."

The silence that followed had texture. When Wren finally spoke, the voice trembled in a way Noor hadn't heard before, and it took her a moment to understand that a ghost trapped in another woman's mind could cry, and that apparently it could, and that it was doing it now.

"That," Wren said, "is the stupidest, bravest thing anyone has ever said inside my skull, and I once delivered memories to a warlord who narrated his own poetry."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment."

"I'm choosing to take it as one."

But the ghost wasn't laughing. "Listen to me. Please. Before you go building cathedrals out of your own ashes—you are not a filing error. You are not a receipt. I watched you hide a bleeding stranger's body and lie to Vess Corvane's face with your heart hammering and your voice steady. I watched you take in a feral child who eats memories and give her the bigger share of the food without ever once making it a transaction. I watched a lamplighter follow you into a drowned city for reasons he can't even remember, and I'll tell you what those reasons are, Noor: they're *you*. Whatever the Guild built, whatever they wrote in their ledgers—what grew in the cracks of it is real, and it will not fit in any vault, and it will not burn out, because it was never stored. It was lived."

Noor sat down slowly on the cold stone. The sourceless light gathered close around her, warm-ish, the way candlelight is warm-ish, the way memory is warm-ish, the way anything is that stands near enough to a fire to catch its color.

"You said 'almost certainly,'" she said at last. "When I asked. If I open the door alone, it's almost certain. That's not 'certain.'"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. "You did the arithmetic."

"I told you. Excellent at arithmetic."

"Yes," the ghost admitted. "Almost certainly. The memory wants to be given, not taken. If it's given—if it's opened by someone who means it as a gift rather than spending themselves as payment—I think there's a version of the account that balances. I think the vessel can pour and survive the pouring. But I think it requires the one thing the Guild never once managed in four hundred years."

"Which is?"

"Letting go without keeping score."

* * *

That was when the ghost said the other thing. The thing Noor had been circling for three days without naming, the way you circle a wound because you already know what's under the bandage.

"There's something I have to ask you," Wren said. "And I need you to hear all of it before you answer, because I've watched you answer questions halfway through hearing them your entire life. It's why your ledgers balanced and your conversations didn't."

"Charming."

"When the memory goes free—when it's loose in the world, in everyone, unownable—I won't be needed anymore. I was the delivery. The map, the warning, the name. All of it was to bring you here. And a courier who's completed the delivery..." The voice caught. Steadied. "A courier who's completed the delivery gets to stop walking."

Noor's breath went out of her.

"You want to go," she said. "When it's done. You want to be released."

"I *am* tired, Noor. I've been dead for longer than I was alive, and dead in the worst way—not gone, just *paused*, a letter stuck in a slot. I have people waiting for me. Maybe. Probably. There's a woman in Harrowmere who used to leave her window lit for me, and I never once told her I knew, because couriers aren't supposed to keep the letters they carry, and I thought—" the ghost laughed wetly, "—I thought there'd be time after this route."

The silence stretched. Noor found she was gripping her own hands hard enough to hurt, and made herself loosen them, finger by finger.

"I promise," she said.

"Noor."

"I promise. When it's done—when the memory is free and the wolf is awake and Corvane is finished—I'll let you go. I don't know how, exactly, but I kept six years of ledgers for a guild full of thieves and liars, and every one of them balanced. I can balance this." Her throat tightened. "I'll miss you. Terribly. You're the rudest person I've ever had living in my head, and I include myself."

"The feeling," Wren said, and now the ghost was definitely crying, and Noor was too, and neither of them pretended otherwise, "is mutual, and I include you twice."

They sat together in the pale light for a long time—two people sharing one body and no future guaranteed to either of them, and finding, absurdly, that it was enough. That it was more than enough. That acceptance, it turned out, was not a stone wall you braced against but a door you leaned your shoulder into, and it swung easier than you feared, and what waited beyond it was not darkness but simply *elsewhere*.

"Hey," Wren said eventually, when the tears had done their work and dried salt-stiff on cheeks that weren't entirely either woman's. "One more thing. About the releasing. There's a way to do it that spreads the weight—opens the memory not as a flood but as a tide. Slow enough to live through. I felt it in the wards when they read you. But you'd need to be at the heart of the Spire, and you'd need—" the ghost paused, listening to something Noor couldn't hear, and Noor felt her own heartbeat stumble, because she had lived with this presence long enough now to know what a pause like that meant, "—oh," Wren said. "Oh, no."

"What?" Noor was on her feet. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"Lantern-light," the ghost said. "Above us. At the outer gate. A lot of it—dozens of flames, moving in formation, and the wards are singing like struck wire." A beat. "Noor. They're not Retrievers. Retrievers come quiet. This is Corvane himself, and he's brought the whole hunt."

And far above, muffled by forty feet of ancient stone and black water, Noor heard it anyway—or imagined she did, or perhaps the Spire carried it down to her deliberately, a message from the building itself:

A crash. A shout. Sable's voice, clear and furious and utterly unsurprised, ringing through the gate chamber like a lamp flaring in a high wind:

"Pip, RUN!"
