## 16. What Noor Thackeray Forgot

The heart of the Sunken Spire was a library that had never been meant for books.

Noor stood at its center, in a round chamber where the flooded quarter's water pressed against high arched windows like a congregation at glass, and the light came down green and gold and trembling. Shelves spiraled up the walls—not for volumes but for vials. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one glowing faintly with the soft interior light of a life: a first snowfall, a wedding argument, the smell of a bakery that had burned down forty years ago. The Guild's oldest vault. The Guild's first sin.

"You're stalling," said Wren, from inside her own head.

"I am *orienting*," Noor said aloud, and her voice came out strange in the vast room, swallowed by all those murmuring memories. "There's a difference. Stalling is cowardice. Orienting is a professional practice. I've kept ledgers for eleven years; I know the difference between stalling and orientation."

"You're standing in exactly the same spot you were standing in when you asked me what 'orientation' means as an excuse."

"That was four minutes ago and it was a fair question."

Wren laughed—that ghost-laugh of hers, which Noor felt more than heard, a warmth behind her sternum like tea going down. The courier had been dead for over two weeks now, and living rent-free in Noor's mind for every day of it, and somewhere in that time she had stopped being an intrusion and become something closer to a sister. A bossy, dead sister who was right too often.

"Your name is on the third shelf," Wren said, gently now. "The one you've been not-looking-at since we walked in."

Noor looked.

Third shelf, eye level, because of course it was—Corvane's people had never been subtle once you got past the door. There, among vials that shimmered amber and rose and river-green, sat a cluster of six, sealed with black wax, labeled in a hand she knew intimately because she copied it every day in the ledgers.

*Vessel: N.T. Intake, Year of the Bell 1173.*

She had been born—in whatever sense she had been born anything—in 1161.

Twelve years before her birth.

"Noor," Wren said.

"I see it."

"There were things I couldn't tell you. Things I could only show you if you chose to look. That was the seal's design—it wasn't cruelty, or—" the ghost paused, reconsidering, "—well. It was somewhat cruelty. It was mostly caution. If you'd known too soon, Corvane would have felt it break. The seal is tied to your heartbeat, Noor. It has been listening to your heart your whole life, waiting for you to be ready."

"Ready," Noor repeated. Her hands, she noticed, were perfectly steady. Eleven years of cataloging other people's worst days had given her that, at least: steady hands in the presence of catastrophe. She picked up the first vial. It was warm, warmer than the others, warm as skin. "How does one orient oneself toward this?"

"Drink," said Wren, "and hold my hand."

"Ghosts don't have hands."

"Then drink and hold the memory of mine. It's nearly as good. I had excellent hands. Very capable. I could carry six bottles and open a door with my elbow."

Noor laughed—a wet, cracked sound—and pulled the wax.

It didn't taste like anything. That was the first betrayal. Twelve years of watching clients weep over bottled childhoods, and she had built up a private theology of memory as something thick and syrupy, honey and iron. This went down like cold water.

Then the chamber went away.

---

*She is small. She is perhaps three years old, though she doesn't know numbers yet. She is sitting on a counting-room floor—the counting room, candlelit, the great brass scales—playing with corks while adults argue above her head.*

*"It won't hold," says a woman's voice. Noor knows that voice. She has heard it every day of her working life, aged and silk-soft, from the mouth of Master Vess Corvane. But this is Corvane young, Corvane thirty years younger, Corvane with fear in it. "The wolf's memory is tearing through vessels in months. We've lost four. Four people, Ilse. Not instruments. People."*

*"Then we stop making people into jars," says the second voice.*

*"And let it wake? Let it rise under eight districts and drown them looking for the rest of itself? It isn't a jar, it's a wound. Somebody has to hold the wound closed."*

*"Then somebody should volunteer. You can't keep building children for it."*

*A silence. In the silence, small-Noor holds up a cork to be admired. A hand—Corvane's hand, she knows the ring now, the signet she has watched turn keys for eleven years—takes the cork gravely, examines it, returns it.*

*"She's good at corks," little-Noor reports.*

*"She's perfect," Corvane says, in the tone of a man describing a bridge that must not fail. "That's the horror of it, Ilse. Look at her. She's not a child to me, do you understand? She's nothing to me. That's what they made me remove. And it worked. Gods forgive me, it worked."*

---

The Spire came back. Noor was on her knees. She did not remember getting there. Green water-light moved across the shelves like slow fish.

"He took it out of himself," she said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "Whatever he felt about me. He cut it out before I could ever be a person to him."

"Yes," Wren said softly.

"And I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "Eleven years I worked under him. Eleven years, and some nights I used to lie awake inventing reasons. Some tragedy. Some debt. Some version of the story where he was kind once and it went wrong, where there was a version of me he grieved. I built whole ledgers of excuses for that man. Balanced his books for him in my head."

"I know."

"You *knew*?"

"I knew what the vials would show. Not how it would land." The warmth behind Noor's sternum tightened. "I'm sorry. I would have taken this memory for you if I could. I tried, actually, the night I died—I ran with the map because I thought if I got far enough from the Guild, I could find another way to free the wolf without ever putting this weight on you. Then I was dying in your doorway, and there wasn't time anymore, so I did the only thing left. I gave it to the vessel. I'm sorry, Noor."

Noor discovered she was holding the second vial already. She had no memory of picking it up. Her hands were doing their steady work without consulting her.

"Intake, 1173," she read. The year of her making. The year of her birth, if you believed the fabricated parish records she had once paid a clerk to fetch, back when she still hoped. "This one's mine. The real one."

"It's the last one," Wren said. "The others are context. This is the cage."

Noor broke the seal.

---

*She is seventeen. She knows this because she has just been told, formally, by a woman with a clipboard: "You are seventeen, your name is Noor Thackeray, your parents died of marsh fever in Fennwick parish, you were apprenticed to the Guild at eleven. Do you understand?"*

*"Yes," says seventeen-year-old Noor, and means it. That's the terrible thing. She means every word. There is a hole in her where a childhood should go and the story pours into the hole perfectly, snug as a cork in a bottle—*

*(corks, she thinks, from very far away, she was good at corks)—*

*and she signs the ledger with her new name and feels pride. Pride! She has been given a name. She has been given a history. She believes she is lucky.*

*Above her, on the gallery, Corvane watches, and where a man might feel satisfaction there is only vigilance, worn smooth as river stone, and underneath the vigilance, deep down, in a place he cut out of himself with a knife made of procedure: a scar shaped exactly like a three-year-old holding up a cork.*

---

Noor came back to herself sitting on the floor, back against the shelf, and she was laughing.

It alarmed even her, the laugh—too loud, too jagged, bouncing around the drowned chamber. She pressed her sleeve against her mouth and laughed anyway, because the alternative was screaming, and she had learned from ten thousand client intake forms that grief will take whichever door you leave open.

"The name," she managed. "Wren. The name. He *gave* me the name. Like a uniform. 'Here is your history, here is your parish, here is your grief about your parents you never had.' I grieved them, Wren. On my birthday, some years, I lit a candle for two marsh-fever graves in Fennwick and cried, genuinely cried, and there was never anybody in them. I mourned an empty plot. I was the empty plot."

"I know," Wren said again, helplessly. "Noor—"

"No, wait. Wait. There's more, isn't there. There's the funny part." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, still half-hysterical. "Do you know what the truly funny part is? He couldn't make me invisible completely. The seal needed a vessel that functioned. So they built me a personality with a flaw in it, a little crack, so the memory could breathe—do you know what the crack was?"

"What?"

"Yearning." She said the word like spitting out a stone. "I thought it was loneliness. I thought everyone felt it, this ache, this reaching—for the lullaby vial, for mothers' memories, for anything warm. I thought I was just a lonely woman who liked other people's yesterdays. But it was the wolf, Wren. It was twelve years of a god's homesickness leaking through the seams of me, and I filed it under *personal failing*, and I charged myself a handling fee."

Wren was quiet a moment. When she spoke, the humor was gone, and what remained was fierce.

"Listen to me, vessel-girl. They built the cage. They wrote the name. They decided which feelings fit through the bars. And then you—do you know what you did? With a cage built by the finest memory-smiths in three centuries? You still loved. Badly-built love, misfiled love, love with the wrong labels on it—but you loved. You pocketed a mother's lullaby because something in you refused, absolutely refused, to let the ache be sanded smooth. They left one crack in you and the whole sky got in through it."

Noor breathed. The green light moved. Somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms of water and stone, the city groaned—the faintest tremor, like a sleeper turning over.

"That's started, then," Noor said.

"That's started," Wren confirmed. "The closer you come to unsealing yourself fully, the more the wolf stirs. Which means we're on a schedule, and the schedule belongs to an earthquake. Are there more vials?"

"One." Noor turned the last of the six in her steady, traitorous, magnificent hands. This seal was different—older wax, a fingerprint pressed into it that she recognized with a lurch as her own. Three-year-old her. Cork-holding her. *They let me seal my own cell,* she thought, and put the thought down carefully before it cut anyone.

"This is everything," she said. "The intake was the lie. This one is the truth. What's in it?"

"The night they poured the wolf's memory into you," Wren said. "And the reason the map exists. And the names of the people who founded the Guild to guard the wolf instead of farm it—and what Corvane's grandfather did to them. After this, you'll know everything. There's no coming back to not-knowing. I want you to choose it wide awake. That's all I've ever wanted for you. To choose."

Noor looked at the vial a long moment.

Then, because she was still, at bottom, a ledger-keeper, she did the accounts aloud. "Cost: every story I have about myself. Every candle lit at an empty grave. Eleven years of believing I was small and unlucky and merely lonely. Benefit:" She paused.

"Go on," Wren said. "Benefit."

"Benefit: the truth. However it cuts." Noor smiled, and it was a real smile, cracked and crooked and hers—hers, the first thing in twenty years that was entirely hers, made by nobody, assigned by nobody, entered into no book. "Also benefit: I hear Sable's boots in the corridor, which means he and Pip fought their way down through a collapsing gate to reach me, which means I am apparently the sort of person people walk through hell for, and I would like to be that person *on purpose*."

The door burst open in a spray of green water. Sable stood in it, soaked, singed, one lamp still burning in his fist like a stolen star, with Pip perched on his shoulders gnawing a Retrievers' gauntlet and grinning around it.

"Noor!" Sable bellowed. "Gate's down, Corvane's coming, we've got minutes—why are you on the floor, are you drunk, is that a *vial*, please tell me you're not drinking the archive—"

"Sable," Noor said, rising, uncorking the last vial with her thumb, "if this goes strangely, catch me."

"Strangely how—"

She drank.

And the world peeled open like a page, and Noor Thackeray—who was not Noor Thackeray, who had never been Noor Thackeray, who was about to learn what she actually was—felt the first true memory of her life rise up to meet her:

and at the bottom of it, opening one enormous golden eye,

something woke.
