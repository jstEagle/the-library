## 19. Everything the Light Touches Remembers

**Noor**

The memory did not leave her the way she expected. She had braced for tearing, for the sensation of a tooth pulled from the jaw of her soul. Instead it left the way dawn leaves a room—gradually, generously, touching everything on its way out the door.

She stood at the heart of the Spire with her hands open and her eyes closed, and she felt the wolf's century pour through her like light through water. It did not go *into* the wolf. That had been the old design, the founders' design: memory to god, god to leash, leash to Guild. Noor had looked at that design—had felt it humming in the stones around her, patient as a trap—and had said, in the small firm voice of a woman who had spent her life being told she didn't exist: *no.*

Instead she gave it away. To everyone. To no one. To the water, the stone, the air, the ten thousand sleeping lives stacked above her like crockery.

*You cannot do that,* Corvane had screamed, or perhaps he was screaming it now; time had gone soft at the edges. *It belongs—*

*It doesn't,* she had said. *That's the whole point. That was always the whole point.*

And then the Spire had begun, gently, majestically, to come apart.

Noor opened her eyes. The ghost was beside her—or the idea of the ghost, the last translucent suggestion of the courier who had died at her door and lived a little longer in her head. The courier was smiling. It was the first time Noor had seen her smile, and it was so unguarded, so young, that Noor's chest ached.

"You're going," Noor said.

"I'm going." The ghost's voice was already thinner, a note held past its length. "You kept your promise. That's more than most people manage with me."

"Thank you," Noor said, and meant the whole impossible shape of it—the map, the name, the dying at her doorstep, the months of whispering in the dark of her skull. "For choosing my door."

"Wasn't choosing," the ghost said. "Was the ledger. It said you'd be the one." The smile tilted. "It was right. Ledgers usually are, if you keep them honestly."

Then the light came up through the floor like water through a cracked hull, and the courier went with it, and Noor was alone in the heart of a waking god with a collapsing building and, distantly, the sound of Sable Quinn shouting her name in the particular tone of a man who has recently been a hostage and would very much like to stop being one.

* * *

**The city**

In a bakery on Coppersmith Row, Marta Ellery woke at her kneading table with her hands covered in flour and her heart full of an enormous, wordless grief that belonged to no one and therefore, somehow, to her. She wept into the dough without knowing why, and when her son came down to ask what was wrong, she could only say, "Something old is sad," and he nodded, because he felt it too, and they stood together in the flour-dusted dark like two people at a funeral for someone they'd never met, and it was—strangely—the closest they had been in years.

On the bridge over the Canal Street lock, a toll-keeper named Bram stopped mid-transaction and stared at the water. The water was glowing. Not the sickly green of Guild-light, but a deep warm gold, like lamplight seen through honey, and in it he saw—or remembered, or was given—a wolf the size of a hill walking through a forest that had not existed for a thousand years, and the wolf was *lonely*, and the loneliness went through Bram like a bell being struck. He forgot to collect the toll. He forgot it for the rest of his life. He gave the money back, eventually, to everyone he'd ever overcharged, because once you have felt a god's loneliness, a shilling seems like a very small thing to be greedy about.

In the Mnemosyne Guild itself, in the candlelit counting room where a woman named Noor Thackeray had once catalogued other people's yesterdays by the hour, the clerks looked up from their ledgers all at once, as if a door had opened somewhere. The vials on the shelves began to sing—softly, each with its own small voice, a mother's lullaby, a first kiss, a betrayal, a birthday—and every clerk who heard them remembered, unbidden and unasked, a piece of something vast and grieving and beautiful, and more than one of them sat down on the floor and wept into the accounting books, and the books, it must be said, were improved by the tears, being dry and dishonest things before.

In the palace district, a duke woke screaming, having dreamed of a war he had never read about, fought over a betrayal he now understood was his great-great-grandmother's doing. He would spend the rest of his life trying to be worth the dream. He would mostly fail. But he would try, which was more than the dream had expected of him.

And in the undercity, in the drowned quarter, in the dark wet throat of the world, the water itself remembered, and rose, and sang.

* * *

**Sable**

Sable Quinn had held collapsing gates before. Well—once. Once, and it had gone badly, and he had lost a decade of his life to the Guild because of it, and here he was again, braced against a stone door the size of a cathedral's conscience, buying time for a woman he'd known a season and a child he'd known a fortnight, and if anyone had told him a year ago that this was where his debts would lead him, he'd have called them a liar and gone back to his bottle.

The gate groaned. Water sheeted through the gaps in silver ropes. Somewhere behind him, Corvane's Retrievers were screaming, though whether at the gate or at what was happening in the world above, Sable couldn't say. The whole city was shaking. The whole city was *glowing*. He could feel it through the stone under his boots, a great slow pulse like a heartbeat the size of a municipality.

"Oi," said a small voice at his elbow.

He looked down. Pip was there, of course. Pip was always there, in the way of small feral children who have decided you belong to them. She was soaked to the ribs, wild-haired, and looking up at him with an expression he had never seen on her face before, because it was an expression that required memories, and Pip had never had any.

"You're sad," she said.

"I'm holding a door, love. Sadness comes later."

"No." She frowned, concentrating, and Sable realized with a lurch that she was *remembering*—that the golden light in the water had filled her up with everything she'd ever eaten, and she was now the only person in the world who could say what a god's grief tasted like. "You're sad *under* the holding-the-door. You're sad about the fire. The one you sold." She patted his arm with a small wet hand. "It wasn't your fault. I remember it now. It wasn't your fault."

Sable's arms nearly gave out. Not from the door.

"Pip," he managed, "you can't just—that's not—you can't just *say* things like that."

"Why not?"

"Because—" He laughed, or sobbed, or did the thing that is both and is therefore the truest thing a man can do. "Because I've been carrying it thirty years and you've had it for four minutes and you're already putting it down."

"Someone should have," Pip said reasonably, and then the gate shuddered and the moment was over, and Sable set his shoulder and pushed, and the door held, and held, and held.

It held long enough. That was the miracle of it. When the last of them were through—when he heard Noor's voice calling from the high passage, and felt Pip's hand ripped from his sleeve, and saw the water coming like a golden tide with a wolf running inside it—Sable Quinn did the arithmetic every lamplighter knows: one door, one man, one choice. He let go.

The water took him the way the light had taken the memory: not cruelly. Almost kindly. The last thing he thought, going down into the gold, was that he had finally, *finally* paid what he owed, and that it had turned out to be worth every farthing.

* * *

**Corvane**

Vess Corvane had spent two hundred years preparing for this moment, and it is a particular cruelty of the universe that the moment arrived and immediately stopped belonging to him.

He had planned for the vessel to refuse. He had planned for her to flee, to fight, to bargain, to die clutching the memory like a dragon on its hoard. He had planned for everything except the thing she actually did, which was to open her hands and give it away—to *everyone*—to the fishwives and the dukes and the clerks and the lamplighters, to every soul in the city, so that no man, no Guild, no god could ever hold it again.

He stood in the flooding heart of the Spire and felt it go. He felt it leave him the way a miser feels a coin leave his hand, except it was not one coin, it was *everything*, and it did not go into his vault, it went into the *world*, and the world lit up with it.

"No," he said. It came out very small. Two hundred years, and it came out very small.

The memory had passed through him on its way out. Of course it had. He was standing in its path. And the memory of a god is not a gentle thing when it moves through a man who has spent two centuries hoarding grief he never intended to feel.

It showed him the wolf. It showed him the founders, young and earnest, swearing their oath. It showed him every ledger entry he had ever written—the vials, the prices, the desperate nobles, the children sold by the hour—and it showed him what he had told himself all along, which was that someone had to keep it safe, and then it showed him the truth underneath, which was that keeping things safe and keeping things *owned* had become, somewhere in the second century, the same word to him, and that this was how every theft in history begins.

"I was guarding it," he whispered.

"You were *counting* it," said the memory, in the voice of every person he had ever bought a yesterday from, and then it finished passing through him, and what was left of Vess Corvane sat down in the rising golden water, and was not, strictly speaking, unmade, but was rather *corrected*—the way a ledger is corrected, the false line struck through, the true one written beneath in a careful hand.

What remained after was a very old man with no memory at all, sitting in the flood, looking around at the glowing dark with the mild bewildered interest of a child. He would be found, eventually. He would be given soup. It would be, by every account, the first honest thing anyone had ever done for him.

* * *

**Pip**

Pip remembered everything now, and it was too much, and it was wonderful, and she was carrying it all the way up the high passage with her arms full of god the way other children carry cats.

She remembered the war. She remembered the founders. She remembered a lullaby that a mother had sung in a kitchen in Coppersmith Row, and a duke's great-great-grandmother burning a treaty, and a wolf walking under the stars, and Sable's fire, and Noor's whole false childhood, and a hundred thousand small yesterdays that people had paid good money to lose.

And she remembered—this was the strange part, the part that made her stop on the stair and laugh until she coughed—she remembered *Noor*, from the inside, from the vial Noor had pocketed a lifetime ago. A mother's lullaby. Noor had stolen a lullaby she couldn't explain wanting, and Pip had eaten it off her once, by accident, in the dark, and now here it was again, coming home.

Pip decided, on the spot and with the full authority of a child who is temporarily a god's grief-bearer, that Noor was going to keep it. Properly, this time. Not in a pocket. In a life.

Then the passage shook, and the water roared up from below, and Pip heard the gate go, and she screamed Sable's name into the dark with a voice that had a wolf in it now, and the dark, which was full of gold and memory and everything that had ever been forgotten, heard her.

* * *

**Noor**

She found them at the water's edge, or they found her—there was no real difference anymore, there hadn't been since the drowned quarter. Pip hit her like a small furious cannonball, soaked and sobbing and *remembering*, chattering a flood of things no child should know, and Noor held on to her and looked past her at the water and understood what was missing from it.

"Sable," she said.

Pip's face crumpled. "The door. He held the door. The water took him and he *let* it, he let it, he said—" and here Pip's voice did something impossible, something borrowed, something remembered, and became for one moment the rough warm voice of a disgraced lamplighter: *"—worth every farthing, tell the ledger-keeper she was the best debt I ever had—"*

Noor stood at the edge of the golden water with her found family one short and her city shaking above her and her own name—her *real* name, the one she was only just learning—caught in her throat like a stone.

"No," she said. It came out very small. Then, louder, with the voice of a woman who had spent her whole life invisible and had just finished becoming the opposite: "*No.* I have had enough of things being taken. I have had enough of ledgers with losses in them."

The water glowed. The wolf—somewhere in the water, in the light, in the world now, everywhere and owned by no one—the wolf turned its attention toward her, and it felt, unmistakably, like being looked at by something that had known her before she was made.

"You owe him," Noor told it. "You owe all of us. He held your door."

The gold pulsed. The Spire groaned around them, coming down in slow majesty, stone remembering it was only stone. And in the flood, at the edge of Noor's sight, something moved—something large and dark and *laughing*, being hauled up through the glowing water by a current that had apparently decided, in its last official act as a god's memory, to balance one final account.

The Spire came down. The three of them ran. And the last thing the Sunken Spire did, before it became rubble and then a story and then, eventually, a shop with a good light in the window, was to open its hands the way Noor had opened hers, and give Sable Quinn back.
