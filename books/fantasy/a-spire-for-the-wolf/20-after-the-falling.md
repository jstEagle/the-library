## 20. After the Falling

The dust took three days to settle, and Sable Quinn spent every one of them digging.

Not that he was good at it. Lamplighters were not, by trade, a digging profession. They climbed ladders and trimmed wicks and, in Sable's particular case, drank too much and fell off ladders. But the Spire had come down across the drowned quarter like a god's dropped toy, and somewhere under forty feet of ancient masonry and newer rubble was Noor Thackeray, who had once—though he couldn't remember when or why, because he'd burned that memory for kindling—owed him nothing at all, and whom he owed everything.

So he dug.

Pip dug with him, which was to say Pip burrowed. The child went through gaps no grown body could follow, wriggling into the dark like an otter after fish, and came back out each time with her pockets full of things she'd eaten on the way down and could now describe in unsettling detail. She'd eaten a memory off a dead Retriever's coat on the second day—a soldier's memory of his mother's bread—and spent an hour afterward chewing air and saying "warm" over and over, as though the word itself tasted good.

"Bread," she informed Sable solemnly, spitting into her palm and offering him the memory like a coin. "You have some."

"I'm all right, sprout."

"You're sad-sad." She patted his arm with a hand still grimy from the rubble. "Sad-sad is heavy. Bread helps."

He almost told her that what he felt wasn't sadness exactly. It was something bigger and more frightening than sadness, sitting in his chest like a lit lamp he was afraid to look at directly. Because three days ago he'd watched Noor Thackeray stand in the heart of a waking god's house and pour a century of stolen memory out of herself like water from a jug, and he'd been certain—certain as he'd ever been of anything—that she would die doing it. That they all would. And instead the world had simply... changed. Everyone in the city had woken up two days ago with someone else's grief behind their eyes and someone else's wonder, and nobody could say whose it was, because it belonged to everyone now.

And Sable had woken up with it too, and found that in among the wolf's vast, cold sorrow there was a small warm coal that wasn't the wolf's at all. It was his. It was the shape of a debt he'd never been able to remember incurring, and now he could see it whole: a night eleven years ago, rain, a girl with ink-stained fingers kneeling beside a drunk lamplighter in the gutter outside the Guild, paying his fine out of her own wages so the Retrievers wouldn't take the last ten years of his life in a bottle. A ledger clerk named Noor Thackeray, who had never once done anything reckless in her careful invisible life, committing her first crime for a stranger who wouldn't remember it.

He remembered it now. He remembered everything, except the decade he'd sold, which was gone forever, and the memory he'd burned to buy their escape from the Retrievers, which turned out to have been his wedding day. He'd learned that from the dispersal too—the wolf's memory had passed through everyone, and everyone's fragments had brushed against everyone else's, and somewhere in that great mingling Sable had caught an edge of his own lost years reflected in a stranger's mind. A woman with flour on her hands. Her name wouldn't come. But the day came, and the way she'd laughed, and the terrible sweet weight of knowing he'd had that and traded it without ever choosing to.

So no, not sad-sad. Something else. But he let Pip press the bread-memory into his hand anyway, because arguing with Pip about food was a war no one won.

* * *

On the fourth day, the city above them started putting itself back together, and the strangest part was how gently it did it.

Sable went up for supplies—candles, rope, a wheelbarrow with only one broken wheel—and found the streets full of people standing around looking at each other like long-lost cousins, which in a sense they were. A fishwife was weeping openly at her stall while customers waited patiently, patting her shoulder. Two rival gang bosses sat on the same bench outside the magistrate's court, sharing a flask, wearing identical expressions of bewildered peace. Somebody had chalked a wolf on the fountain in Tanner's Row, and somebody else had left flowers in its jaws.

And on the doors of the Mnemosyne Guild—sealed since the falling, its master unmade, its Retrievers wandering home with empty hands and haunted faces—somebody had nailed a notice:

*BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL COUNCIL: ALL LEDGERS OF THE MNEMOSYNE GUILD TO BE OPENED FOR PUBLIC INSPECTION. ALL BOTTLED MEMORIES TO BE RETURNED TO THEIR RIGHTFUL OWNERS OR THEIR HEIRS, WITHOUT FEE.*

Somebody had written underneath, in different chalk: *about time.*

Sable stood in front of the notice for a long while. Eleven years ago he'd signed a contract he couldn't read through his tears, and a clerk with careful handwriting had slid his decade into a drawer, and that had been that. Now the drawers were being pulled open one by one, and people were walking out of the Guild holding little glass vials to the light like they held birds.

He didn't go in. His decade was ash; he'd known that going on four years now. But he stood there until an old woman came out clutching a vial against her chest and crying, and she saw him looking, and she said, "It's my mother's voice, young man. I sold it when I was starving. I'd forgotten I even had a mother." And then she'd hugged him, this total stranger, because apparently that was what the city did now, and Sable had walked back toward the rubble with his eyes stinging and blamed the dust.

* * *

They broke through on the fifth day.

Pip found it—or rather, Pip smelled it, which was worse. She'd been quiet all morning, sitting on a slab of fallen Spire-stone with her knees drawn up, and then she'd said, "She's there. Under the big flat rock. She tastes like sleep but not like stopped." And then she'd looked at Sable with those enormous feral eyes and added, "Hurry hurry hurry," in a voice much smaller than usual, and Sable's heart had gone down through the floor of him.

The big flat rock was a section of the Spire's outer wall, black stone shot through with veins of silver that still faintly glowed, like embers refusing to admit the fire was out. It took six hours, two borrowed levers, and the help of nine strangers who appeared out of the crowd the moment Sable started shouting—because that was the other thing the city did now; you shouted for help and help came, blinking and embarrassed, as if everyone had just remembered how.

When the gap was wide enough, Pip went in first, of course. She always went in first. Sable heard her voice echo back, thin and high: "Noor. Noor Noor Noor. Wake up, your bread's getting cold."

Then Sable squeezed through after her, candle held high, and saw her.

Noor Thackeray lay on her back in a pocket of clear space beneath the fallen wall—as if the stone had made room for her, as if the old wards had spent their last strength tucking a blanket around her instead of letting the mountain crush her. She was gray with dust. Her lips were cracked. Her hands, folded on her chest like a sleeping effigy, were stained with ink that had never washed out and never would.

"Noor?" Sable's voice came out wrong, half a whisper. He knelt and touched her shoulder, and she was warm, warm, thank every god including the recently freed one, she was warm. "Noor. Come on. You've slept through five days of the most interesting civic chaos this city's ever seen. You'll want to see the chalk wolf. Very flattering likeness."

Her eyelids fluttered. Moved. Opened.

For a moment she just stared at the candle flame above her, and Sable watched her try to remember where she was and saw the attempt come up empty—not confused-empty, the ordinary fog of waking, but hollow-empty, like a room swept clean to the boards.

"I don't—" Her voice was rust and paper. She swallowed, tried again. "I don't know who you are."

The words landed in Sable's chest like a dropped lamp.

"That's all right," he said, and was surprised to find it mostly true. "Neither do I, most days. Name's Sable. Sable Quinn. Lamplighter. Formerly. Currently employed as a shovel-adjacent professional."

"I don't know who *I* am," she said, and now there was fear under the rust, rising fast. She pushed herself up on her elbows and looked at her own ink-stained hands as if they belonged to a stranger, which, Sable supposed, they did. "There's nothing. There was—I know there *was* something, I can feel the hole where it was, but it's all—it's all gone. The wolf. The—" her face worked, groping for words that weren't there anymore, "—the ledgers. Corvane. The name the courier said. All of it. I gave it away and it took me with it."

Pip climbed into her lap without asking, the way Pip did everything, and put both grimy hands on either side of Noor's face.

"You're Noor," Pip said firmly. "I remember you. If I can remember you, you're real. I forget everything and I still remember you, so you're extra real."

Something in Noor's face cracked—not breaking, Sable thought, but cracking open, the way ice cracks on a river in spring. She wrapped her arms around the child and held on, and then her shoulders started shaking, and Sable very carefully did not watch, because some griefs deserved privacy even in a collapsed ruin.

"The courier," she whispered into Pip's hair, after a while. "The ghost. I promised her I'd free her. Did I? Do you know?"

"You did," Sable said. "Whole city saw her go, near enough. Brightest thing anyone ever saw, Pip says. Went up through the water like a lantern swimming."

"And Corvane?"

"Gone. Unmade. The memory didn't care for him much." Sable hesitated. "There's more. The ledgers are open. Everything the Guild ever took is going back. There's a shopkeeper on Bellows Street who got her childhood returned yesterday and hasn't stopped cooking for strangers since. The whole city's eating too much. It's a crisis."

That earned him a wet, disbelieving huff that might, in better light, have been a laugh. Noor wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a streak of clean skin through the dust, and looked around the little pocket of ruined dark as though seeing it for the first time.

"I have nothing," she said slowly, testing the words like loose teeth. "No past. No name but the one on the map. No memories that are actually mine." She looked down at Pip in her lap, then up at Sable, and something steadied in her eyes—something tired and scorched and, impossibly, beginning to be glad. "Except this. Whatever this is. The three of us in a hole."

"Aye," Sable said. "Congratulations. It's called a family. Terrible pay, long hours, frequent collapses. You get used to it."

Pip raised her head. "Is there bread in a family?"

"There can be," Noor said, and her voice wobbled and firmed. "I think I'd like there to be bread."

* * *

Getting her out took another hour, most of which was Pip narrating the entire process in advance whether anyone wanted her to or not. When Noor finally emerged into daylight—real daylight, gray and drizzly and utterly ordinary—she stopped dead on the rubble slope and stared at the city.

From the drowned quarter's rim you could see most of it: the rooftops, the smoking ruins of the Spire's crown, the harbor, the hills beyond. And everywhere, everywhere, chalk wolves. On walls and doors and cobbles and shutters, hundreds of them, thousands, drawn by every kind of hand—some crude, some beautiful, one so enormous on the side of the granary it could be seen from the far hills. People had died in the falling; that was true, and there would be mourning for weeks. But the city had decided, with the stubborn collective instinct of cities, that what it had gained outweighed what it had lost, and it had marked the gain in chalk.

"What is all that?" Noor asked quietly.

"That's you," Sable said. "Or near enough. Everyone woke up with a piece of what you let go. Nobody knows quite what to do with it, so they're drawing wolves about it. It's as good a plan as any."

Noor stood in the drizzle a long time, saying nothing, her ink-stained hand tight around Pip's smaller one. Then she said, in a voice like someone reading the first line of a brand-new ledger:

"I'm going to need a new book. To write things down in. Before I lose them again."

"Shop on Bellows Street sells them," Sable said. "Though fair warning, the woman will feed you until you beg for mercy."

But Noor wasn't listening anymore. She was looking at the granary wolf—at the huge chalk lines, and at something else, lower down, near the base of the wall where the crowd hadn't gathered yet. Fresh writing, in careful, familiar handwriting, chalked that very morning by someone who'd clearly climbed up on a barrel to reach it:

*NOOR THACKERAY — LEDGER OPEN. FIRST ENTRY PENDING.*

Below it, smaller, in letters that wobbled just slightly, as if the writer's hand had shaken:

*Take your time. We'll wait. — S. & P.*

Noor read it twice. Then she laughed—a rough, rusty, astonished sound, the laugh of someone hearing their own voice for the first time—and pulled a stub of chalk from Sable's pocket before he could protest, and reached up on her toes, and wrote beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand that trembled only a little:

*First entry: stayed.*
