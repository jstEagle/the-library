## 22. The Wolf in the Window

The shop had no sign yet. Sable kept threatening to carve one, and Pip kept vetoing his suggestions, and so the little storefront on Candlewick Row went on announcing itself the way good shops do—by smell and by rumor. It smelled of beeswax and old paper and the lavender oil Noor rubbed into the counter to keep it from complaining about the damp. The rumor, meanwhile, said this: *the Thackeray woman gives them back for free. She doesn't sell anything. She just gives them back.*

Which was true, mostly, though it undersold the paperwork. There was always paperwork. Noor was, after all, still a ledger-keeper at heart, and she had come to accept that some things survived even the unmaking of everything you thought you were.

It was morning on the first anniversary—the anniversary of falling spires and waking cities and a name written in a hand that shook—and Noor was alone in the shop because Sable had gone to the market "for provisions," by which he meant gossip, and Pip had gone with him "for supervision," by which she meant stolen apples. The kettle was on. The sun came through the big front window in a warm slab, catching all the dust motes Noor hadn't swept because she'd decided, on reflection, that dust motes in sunlight were one of the few uncomplicated pleasures left in the world.

She was mending the spine of a ledger with thread and patience when the light changed.

Not dimmed—*changed*. As though something vast and quiet had passed between the sun and the street, the way a cloud does, except clouds do not have shoulders. Noor looked up.

A shadow moved along the cobbles outside the window. It was wolf-shaped. Not dog-shaped stretched tall, not smoke pretending—it was unmistakably, anciently *wolf*, long-muzzled and heavy through the chest, moving with that particular unhurried gait of a creature who has never once needed to hurry for anyone. It flowed up Candlewick Row past the window, and then—

It paused.

Noor did not breathe. The shadow's head turned, or seemed to; there was nothing solid to turn, only darkness on sunlit stone, but she felt the attention of it settle on her like a paw placed gently on her sternum. Through the glass, through the morning, through the whole thin membrane of the ordinary world, something looked at her and *knew* her.

And then—this was the part that undid her, then and every time she thought of it after—then it simply moved on. Unhurried. Unbound. It slid down the row and around the corner by the cooper's yard and was gone, leaving the sunlight whole and the dust motes dancing and Noor sitting very still with a darning needle in her hand and tears running freely down her face, which annoyed her considerably, because she'd promised herself she wasn't going to be sentimental today of all days.

"Right," she said aloud, to nobody. "Well."

Her voice wobbled. She let it.

* * *

A year ago she had not known what morning was.

That sounded overwrought—she knew it sounded overwrought—but it happened to be true. The vessel that had been called Noor Thackeray had experienced mornings before, certainly: gray ones in her lodgings above the Guild counting room, candle-lit ones in the ledgers' margins. But a morning belonged to someone. A morning required an owner, someone for whom the light arrived as a gift rather than an administrative fact. For thirty-one years she had been furniture in her own life—sturdy, useful, unremarked-upon—and then she had opened her hands in the drowning dark of the Spire and given away the last thing anyone had ever put inside her, and woken up three days later under a collapsed ceiling with Sable's dirt-streaked face above hers saying, "Don't you dare, don't you *dare*, I traded half my remaining past for a message and you are not going to waste it," while Pip sat on his back eating a pear and nodding solemnly, as if concurring with medical authority.

She had been empty when they dug her out. That was the price, and everyone had warned her, and she would pay it again before breakfast without blinking. The wolf's memory was gone from her—not destroyed, not hoarded, but *loosed*, poured into the water table of the world, and now it lived everywhere at once and nowhere entirely, which meant it could belong to no one, least of all Vess Corvane, whose unmaking had been swift and strange and witnessed by four hundred people who still couldn't describe it without their voices dropping.

But empty was not how it had turned out. Empty was what she'd expected—a vessel drained, a ledger with the pages torn out. Instead she'd found the opposite. With the god's stolen century gone, what remained was small, and unremarkable, and hers: a fondness for lavender, a talent for arithmetic, an ache in her left knee when it rained. A loneliness that, once named, turned out to have been loneliness *for specific people* all along—two of whom were currently at the market deciding whether provisions included candied ginger.

The bell over the door jangled. Pip came in like weather, hair full of leaves, holding an apple in each hand and a third already in progress.

"Sable says the cooper's wife fainted," Pip announced, "but Sable lies when he wants attention."

"Does he." Noor wiped her face quickly, badly.

"You've been crying." Pip observed this the way she observed everything—with total honesty and zero alarm. Since the Spire, since tasting the air of that place and remembering everything she'd ever swallowed, Pip remembered more than she used to. Not everything. But she kept a running inventory, and she reported it like a ship's log. "Happy crying or sad crying?"

"I'm not sure. A wolf looked at me through the window."

Pip considered this seriously, chewing. "Was it rude?"

"No. It just... looked. And then it went on."

"That's polite," Pip decided, and climbed onto her stool behind the counter, which was *her* stool in the way that the moon is everyone's but mostly hers, and began arranging the morning's intake: three vials, a locket, a folded letter somebody's grandmother had died holding. Things people brought to be returned to their owners—which sometimes meant themselves, which was the point, which was the whole mad beautiful point of the place.

"The cooper's wife didn't faint," Pip added, mouth full. "She remembered something. Everybody remembers things lately. Hers was a flood, when she was six. She cried in the street. Sable held her basket."

Noor smiled at the ledger in front of her. That was how it worked now, all across the city. Fragments of the wolf's grief and wonder had settled into ten thousand minds like silt into a riverbed, and people woke some mornings with a memory that wasn't theirs and somehow was—of running under an older sky, of a betrayal in a high tower, of loving a city before it was built. Some wept. Some wrote songs. One enterprising playwright had made a spectacle of it that Noor refused on principle to attend, on the grounds that grief should not be sold for ninepence, *she had strong feelings on the subject.*

And the wolf itself—or its shadow, or its echo, or whatever walked now that its memory belonged to everyone—passed through the city sometimes. Always at a distance. Always pausing, occasionally, outside windows. Remembering, presumably, in the only way left to it: secondhand, scattered, borrowed from the people it moved among.

Free, and unowned. Which was, Noor reflected, a description she was working toward herself.

* * *

Sable arrived an hour later, arms laden with actual provisions and also a wooden plank, which he set on the counter with a thump.

"A sign," he said. "I'm doing it today. The suspense is killing the customers."

"We have customers."

"They're confused customers. Mrs. Aldery stood in the doorway for ten minutes yesterday trying to work out if we were a pawnshop, an apothecary, or a cult."

"What did she decide?"

"She bought a candle and joined the cult, apparently." He was already carving. His hands were steadier than they'd been a year ago—he'd stopped flinching at lamplight, mostly, though Noor knew better than anyone what he'd burned to buy their escape in the drowned quarter, and knew too that he'd made peace with it in his own grumbling way. The decade he'd sold to the Guild as a young man was still gone. The scraps he'd traded in prison were still gone. What remained was enough. "Right. *Sable Quinn's Emporium of—*"

"No," said Pip.

"*Establishments,*" Sable tried. "*Fine Bottled Memories, Honestly Sourced—*"

"No," said Noor and Pip together, with feeling.

He grinned and kept carving anyway, because provoking them was one of his remaining pleasures and Noor wouldn't have taken it from him for the world. He'd stayed, was the thing. After everything—after the collapsing gate and the prison cell and the bargain that cost him the last scraps of a childhood he'd already half-forgotten—he had helped dig her out of the rubble of the Spire, and then he had simply never left. He fixed the shutters. He argued with the kettle. He carved signs nobody approved and hung lanterns he'd sworn off making, and every evening he lit the shop's single lamp himself, with great ceremony, standing where he'd stood in another life watching flames he was no longer allowed to tend.

Some debts, he'd told her once, aren't owed backward. They're paid forward. He'd never remember what he owed her for. He paid it anyway, daily, in small coin.

Noor opened the new ledger.

It was a fresh one—cream paper, good binding, purchased from the stationer on Cordwain Street who still got nervous around her because everyone knew she'd once broken into the Master's vault, though the details grew more operatic with each retelling. In a year of business she had filled two volumes with returns: names, dates, descriptions of memories going home to their owners. Every entry a small act of restitution against everything the Guild had ever done. Corvane's ledgers had been seized and opened to the public months ago—any citizen could walk in and read what had been taken from them, and claim it back, and the reading room smelled now of dust and outrage and, increasingly, reunion.

But this third ledger was different. This one she'd been saving.

She dipped her pen.

On the first page, in her neat counting-room hand, she wrote the heading she'd chosen weeks ago and rehearsed in her head at odd moments, embarrassed by her own ceremony: *Register of Names Claimed.* Because that was the real trade now, she'd realized. Not memories, exactly. Ownership. People came in carrying other people's yesterdays and asked her to help them give the yesterdays back, and what they left with, every time, was the same thing: a little more room in themselves to be somebody.

The first entries came easily. *Mrs. Odile Aldery, cordwainer's widow—one flood, age six, returned by her own request, witnessed and wept over.* Pip insisted on being entered too, though her line read, in Pip's own dictated phrasing, *Pip — has eaten forty-one memories and given back six and remembers seven and that is plenty for now,* which Noor transcribed verbatim because rules were rules. Sable demanded his entry be short. It read: *Sable Quinn — lamp lit, debt paid.* She had argued that it wasn't descriptive. He had said neither was life, mostly.

Now she turned to the next blank line and sat with her pen hovering, the way she'd sat with it hovering for a year.

Because there was one name left. One return outstanding. The oldest theft in the book.

Noor Thackeray, keeper of ledgers, had been made, not born—raised in a Guild nursery on fabricated years, given a name like a cage, emptied and fitted and sealed. All of that was gone now: the false childhood, the vessel's purpose, the aching yearning that had lived beneath her detachment like groundwater. She missed none of it. But the vacancy it left had needed filling, and filling took time, and she had learned to be patient with herself in a way no one had ever modeled for her.

She knew who she was now. It had happened slowly, the way a photograph develops, out of mornings and arguments and apple cores and mended ledger-spines and a shadow with shoulders passing kindly by her window. She was the person Sable teased and Pip trusted. She was the woman who gave memories back for free and had strong opinions about playwrights. She was the last vessel of nothing, the first owner of herself, and her name—the true one, the one the dying courier had whispered into the dark a year ago tonight, the name she'd recognized without knowing, the name that had surfaced like a drowned thing until it broke the surface and breathed—

She knew it. She'd known it for months. She had simply wanted to write it properly.

Noor set the pen to the cream page and wrote, in careful letters, unhurried:

*Nour. Called Noor. Returned to self, this first day of the year, witnessed by a lamplighter, a child, and one free wolf. Nothing owed. Nothing owned. Ledger open.*

She blotted it, and looked at it, and found that her eyes had gone hot again, and decided that was allowed.

"Well," said Sable, peering over, sawdust on his sleeve. "About time. Does this mean you'll finally let me hang the sign?"

"It means," Noor said, "that the shop needs a name, and I know whose job that is."

Pip raised her hand. "Mine."

"Yours," Noor agreed.

Outside, the autumn light lay thick and gold along Candlewick Row, and somewhere beyond the cooper's yard a shadow with shoulders went on its unhurried way through a city full of borrowed wonder—remembering, and remembered, belonging to everyone and therefore, at last, to itself.

Noor closed the ledger softly, and left it open on the counter anyway. Just in case anyone else came in needing to claim a name. There was room. There was, she had finally learned, room for all of them.
