## 1. The Ink That Remembers

The bells of Vell rang six, and Ines Ishikawa was already lying to his first customer of the day.

"Riverstone," he said, turning the small grey pebble between his fingers so it caught the lamplight. "Carried downstream for a hundred years. Good for calm dreams. Keeps the nightmares from finding your door."

It was riverstone, that much was true. The rest — the hundred years, the dreams — was the kind of comfortable fiction that sold charms in a city where magic had been outlawed for three generations and hungered for it anyway, the way a starving man hungers for bread he's been told is poison.

Maren Tull, the cooper's widow, pressed two coppers into his palm and clutched the stone to her breast like an infant. "You're a good boy, Ishikawa. Your sister used to say so."

Ines kept his smile fixed and did not let anything move behind his eyes. "She did."

Three years since Mira had vanished. Three years since the constables had shrugged and the magistrates had stamped *unsolved* and the city had closed over her absence like water over a dropped coin. Maren meant no harm. No one in Vell ever meant harm; they only meant to forget.

He watched her out the door of the shop — a narrow slot of a place wedged between a chandler and a pawnbroker on Candlewick Row, its single window crowded with trinkets: knotwork rings, hagstones threaded on leather, bottles of colored sand labeled with virtues. Protection. Luck. Sleep. None of it worked. That was the point. A charm that worked would be sorcery, and sorcery was ash.

What actually worked was hidden beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, in forty-one volumes of his own hand.

* * *

When the last customer had gone and the dusk had thickened enough to trust, Ines knelt and pried up the board behind the counter. He worked by feel as much as sight, though he needed little light for what came next, because the ink he drew with did not need light either.

The ink remembered.

That was the secret at the center of him, the gift he'd carried since childhood and learned to hide the way other children learn not to wet the bed. Ordinary ink sat on paper. His ink sank through, and further, and further still, until it touched the true shape of things. When he drew a street, the ink found the street. When he drew a coastline, the ink grew restless until the coastline agreed with it. And when he drew places that were not supposed to exist — the ink sang.

They called themselves the Veilways, those places. Ines had never found another word for them, and no mapmaker's guild had ever named them because no mapmaker but Ines could see them: the fold-paths between streets, the doors that opened onto weather from elsewhere, the stairwells in Vell that went down farther than Vell had depth. They drifted like silt in the corners of the world, real as rain and twice as deniable. For three years he had mapped them by night — the Gullet under Saltmarket, the Long Stair behind the tannery, the door in the aqueduct wall that breathed warm air smelling of pine and snow. Forty-one volumes. Every one of them treason, if anyone ever read them. Every one of them proof of exactly what the crown said did not exist.

He unrolled tonight's page and dipped his pen.

The ink went eagerly, the way it always did, threading off the nib and down into the paper as if the paper were a window and not a surface. He was mapping a stretch of the Veilway called the Undertow — a passage that surfaced, he believed, somewhere past the city walls — when the ink hesitated.

It had never done that before.

Ines held the pen still and watched. The line he'd drawn wavered on the page, then bent — not smearing, but *turning*, curving southward against his hand, the way iron filings turn toward a lodestone. Something south of him was pulling at the ink. Something with weight.

He wrote three more lines, testing, and all three leaned the same direction, gentle and insistent, like grass bending before wind that hadn't arrived yet.

His mouth had gone dry. He capped the inkwell and sat back on his heels among the trinkets, listening to the ordinary sounds of Candlewick Row: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the chandler's dog complaining at nothing. Above the pawnbroker's, someone was arguing about money. The world was being stubbornly, aggressively normal, and still the image of that bending ink stayed in his mind like a splinter.

*Something is pulling,* he thought. *South. Down.*

He told himself he would think about it tomorrow. This was a skill Ines had perfected in three years of hiding — the art of postponing fear until morning, morning after morning, forever.

Then the drums started.

* * *

He heard them first from the east, from the direction of Crown Square: the deep ceremonial pulse of the king's heralds, the rhythm every child in Vell knew the way they knew their own heartbeat. Proclamation drums. Nobody could remember the last time they'd been sounded for anything but a coronation or a war.

By the time Ines reached the square, it was packed shoulder to shoulder, torchlight jumping off the white faces of the crowd. Three heralds stood on the steps of the assize court in silver-and-black livery, and the middle one unrolled a scroll long enough to hang to his knees.

His voice carried without effort — heralds' voices always did, trained to slide under doors and into dreams.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Protector of the Realm: Let it be known that the poison of the gift has taken root among us. Sorcery walks our streets wearing honest faces. It sells you luck in bottles and blessing in knots." Ines felt the crowd around him shift, a slow ripple, heads beginning to turn. "Let the Edict of Ash therefore be read: every gifted person in Vell shall surrender themselves to the crown within nine days, confessing their crime and receiving the crown's mercy — the pyre made quick. Those who conceal themselves shall be hunted. Those who shelter them shall burn beside them. There is no third road. So it is written. So it is ash."

A boy near Ines whispered, "Gifted people. You mean there really are—"

"Course there are," his mother hissed. "Quiet."

But Ines barely heard them. He was watching the herald's scroll, watching it the way other men watched a knife coming. Because the Edict was wrong in a way that mattered, wrong in its bones, and Ines alone in that crowd understood how: the crown did not believe magic existed. The crown had outlawed something it insisted was superstition for three generations. You do not hunt what you do not believe in.

Unless you know exactly what you're hunting.

And unless you want it.

*Something is pulling,* the ink had said. *South. Down.* And now the crown, on a random autumn evening, sets drums rolling and fires lit. Coincidences were for people who hadn't spent three years learning how the world's seams fit together. Ines turned and walked home fast, not running, running being memorable, his heart keeping time against his ribs like a second pair of drums.

* * *

They came four nights later, an hour past midnight, when Candlewick Row was nothing but shuttered windows and guttering lamps.

Ines woke to the smell of pitch and the sound of boots — many boots, moving with the unhurried coordination of men who had done this often and expected no trouble. He was out the back window with his satchel before the front door came down, which was the only reason he lived. From the shadow of the chandler's alley he watched witchfinders in grey hoods pour into his shop like smoke running backward, and he watched the torch go into the shelves.

Trinkets burn quickly. Knotwork rings, hagstones, bottles of sand — the fire took them in minutes and stood up roaring out the window like a thing satisfied. Ines pressed himself against cold brick and made himself watch, because a man who hides must learn everything about what happens to the things he can't save. His shop died in twenty minutes. His whole visible life, the three-year mask of a harmless charm-seller, went up in sparks that rose and rose and refused, in defiance of all law, to come down.

The witchfinders raked the ashes till dawn. Ines watched them find the floorboard — of course they found it, they always find it — and he felt something in his chest tear loose when they pulled up the boards where forty-one volumes should have been.

Should have been. He had moved them, months ago, after the night the ink first bent. He'd told himself it was caution. Now he understood it had been foresight, the gift working through him whether he willed it or not. The volumes lay in a rented crypt under the mason's yard on Gravel Lane, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as seeds.

The witchfinders found the empty space, argued, and left as dawn came up grey. Ines waited until the street had yawned and stretched and begun pretending the night hadn't happened. Then he climbed through his own burnt doorway, past the constable's chalk scrawl on the frame, and knelt in the ashes.

Under the counter, half-buried, was something that had not been there before the raid. A book. Small, black, bound in leather gone stiff with age, lying face-down as if dropped in haste. It was not his. He knew every object he owned the way a priest knows scripture, and this book was a stranger.

He turned it over. The cover bore no title, only a stamp he recognized from documents he had no business recognizing: the seal of the Office of Founding Charters, the branch of the crown that concerned itself with the oldest laws of Vell — the laws from before the kings, from the founding, from whatever bargain had built the city.

His hands were shaking. He opened it.

It was a ledger. Names in columns, hundreds of names, each with a date and a payment entered beside it in faded brown ink that Ines suspected had never been brown. Deeds, mostly. Houses signed away. Debts forgiven. Titles granted. And beside each transaction, in a smaller hand, a word that recurred so often it stopped looking like a word:

*soul — received.*

He turned the pages faster. Founding-era entries. Grain contracts paid in souls. A bridge charter. A well. And then, near the middle, an entry dated seventeen years ago — the year of the great plague, the year his parents died, the year the city forgave itself for everything —

*Ishikawa, Ren & Sana — two souls — received — debt of founding discharged.*

The ledger slipped from his hands into the ash.

His parents. Not plague. Never plague. And if his parents —

He grabbed the book back up and tore through it, page after page, ash rising around him like grey snow, until he found it. Near the end. Fresh ink, barely three years faded.

*Mira Ishikawa — one soul — received — sold by the debtor's own hand — conveyed to the court beneath.*

*The court beneath.*

Ines knelt in the ashes of his life and read the words until they stopped being words and became simply true. Somewhere below Vell — below the sewers, below the cellars, below the deepest foundation — there was a court. And it held his sister's soul in its ledgers the way he held maps: carefully, completely, and meaning to keep it.

Around him the morning came on, indifferent, the neighbors sweeping their steps, the Edict drums already beating again in the distance for the next proclamation, the next name, the next harvest. Nine days, the heralds had said. Surrender or be hunted.

Ines closed the ledger gently, the way he closed his atlases, and stood up out of the ashes of his old life. His dread had finished hardening into something else — something with edges.

They wanted the gifted. Very well. He was the most gifted man in Vell, and he intended to introduce himself.
