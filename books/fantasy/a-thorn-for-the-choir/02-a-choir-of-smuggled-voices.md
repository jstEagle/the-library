## 2. A Choir of Smuggled Voices

The rain in Vell fell gray, the way everything did now that Ines knew what he knew. He had walked out of the ashes of his shop with a ledger under his coat and no plan beyond *keep moving*, and the city seemed to sense it — the lamplighters hurrying their rounds, the shuttered windows, the wet cobbles reflecting nothing back at him.

He was three streets from the ruin when the hand closed on his arm.

"Walk," said a woman's voice, low and even. "Don't run. Running is a confession."

She wore a plain oilskin cloak, hood up, but beneath it he caught the collar — the high, severe collar of a crown inquisitor, stripped of its silver chain. The rank without the office. Her grip was not cruel; it was simply immovable, the way a doorframe is immovable.

"You're Ishikawa," she said. "The charm-seller on Candle Row. Your shop burned an hour ago."

"Tragic accident," Ines said.

"The heralds don't have accidents." She turned him down an alley he had never mapped — which was itself remarkable, because Ines Ishikawa had mapped every alley in Vell, most of them twice, once for the crown and once for himself. "My name is Serah Duvane. Three years ago I refused a writ. The writ named six children, the eldest nine. I was told refusal was treason. I have been learning what treason feels like ever since."

"And you want?"

"To show you something before they show it to you themselves." She glanced back at the mouth of the alley, where the rain stitched silver threads through the dark. "You found a ledger in your shop. Don't lie to me; ash leaves ink legible if you know how to read it. You found your sister's name in it, and a price, and a buyer. Say the word *court* and watch your own face."

Ines said nothing. His face, apparently, said enough.

"Good," Serah Duvane said. "Then come meet the people who've been singing about it for twenty years."

* * *

Serah Duvane had spent eleven years as the crown's hound, and she still dreamed in the grammar of interrogation: the small stone rooms, the ledgers, the careful questions. She had been good at it. That was the wound that would not close — not that she'd been forced, but that she'd been *good*, that children had wept in front of her and she had written down their answers in her beautiful clerk's hand.

So when she pulled the cartographer through the drainage arch beneath the old tannery and heard, rising out of the dark like heat off stone, a human voice singing a note that made the wall forget it was a wall — she watched his face instead of listening, and what she saw there was recognition.

"You can see it," she said. "The way it opens."

"It doesn't open," Ines murmured. "It *remembers*. The song reminds the stone it used to be something else." He stepped forward, and she saw his eyes move in that particular way she'd learned to dread in suspects — tracking something that wasn't there. Except with him, she suspected, it *was*. "There's a road behind this wall. It runs downhill. It has been running downhill since before Vell had a charter."

"That's the Veilway," said a new voice, warm and rough as wool. A broad woman emerged from the dark, gray-streaked hair cropped short, hands like a dockworker's. "And you're either the most dangerous man in Vell or the most useful one. I'm Mother Casserly, though nobody living calls me mother. This is the Choir."

The cavern beyond the remembered wall was lit by lanterns the color of honey, and it was full of people. Dozens. Some sat tuning instruments that looked ordinary until you noticed the strings were gut from animals Ines could not name. Others simply waited, in the patient posture of people who had been waiting a long time.

"We smuggle voices," Casserly said. "That's all. When the Edict came down thirty years ago — the first one, the quiet one, before your heralds started nailing paper to doors — anyone whose gift lived in the throat came to us. Singers who could unseal. Speakers who could talk locks into forgetting. We moved them out of Vell through ways the crown sealed after the founding, and we thought we were saving them."

"But?" Ines asked.

"But the mountain takes a toll," Serah said quietly. She had given this speech before, to families, to the desperate, and every time it tasted worse. "Every way opened by song opens both directions. And the court beneath — whatever signed your city's charter, whatever the founders called *partner* — it developed a taste. Not for bodies. For what the singers carry. Souls travel light, Mr. Ishikawa. They slip through a song like water through a net."

Ines had gone very still. "The hunt. The Edict of Ash. The heralds collecting the gifted—"

"—aren't collecting them for trial," Casserly finished. "Trials are theater for the mob. The condemned go to the mountain. Every soul the crown burns or hangs or 'transports,' it pays downward. Interest on the charter. The witch-hunt is a harvest, and the solstice is threshing day. Whatever they've gathered by then gets consumed. Eaten. Gone past any calling-back."

"How do you know the date?"

"Because we lost two of ours last month," Casserly said, and her voice did not waver, which cost her visibly. "A boy of nineteen who could make ropes unknot. A girl who spoke to river water. Taken in the second sweep. We've been planning to go down after them since, but going down requires opening the deep gates, and the deep gates require—" she gestured around the cavern, at the thin, tired, frightened excellence of them, "—more voice than we have left. And a map. And someone who can see the roads while standing in them."

All eyes turned to Ines.

"I'm a trinket-seller," he said.

"You're a man who just described a Veilway's nature in one sentence," Serah said. "I've spent my life around liars, Mr. Ishikawa. Liars are slow to answer and quick to smile. You answered fast and didn't smile at all."

* * *

They gave him a cot in a side gallery that smelled of tallow and old rehearsals, and sleep declined to visit. Instead Ines lay in the dark doing what he always did when the world grew too large: drawing maps in his mind. The Veilway downhill. The branches he could half-perceive through the limestone, roads braided like roots. And somewhere at the bottom of all of them, a court with his sister's name on a shelf.

*Mira.* Three years missing, and he had told himself exile, accident, the sea. He had built a whole architecture of gentler explanations, and tonight it all came down like his shop had, and underneath was only the ledger line: *Ishikawa, Mira — sold to the court beneath.*

Near dawn he heard footsteps and found Serah Duvane sitting against the far wall, awake, sharpening a knife she clearly hadn't used in years.

"You should hate me," she said, without looking up. "I signed the writs that emptied half the quarters. I told myself the law was the law. Then one day the law was a nine-year-old girl crying, and I discovered the law had no answer for that, and neither did I."

"I don't hate you," Ines said. "Hating you would require believing you mattered more than the thing that aimed you."

Serah laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse. "Casserly thinks you'll run."

"Casserly's right to think it. Running is the sensible act." He sat down across from her. "But my sister is below, and the solstice is—" he counted, hating the arithmetic, "—nineteen days away. So I need to know one thing, Duvane, and I need you to answer like an inquisitor, not a penitent. Can the Choir actually open the deep gates? Or are these good people walking me into the mountain to die beside their own dead?"

Serah set down the knife. For a long moment she studied him with the flat assessing look he realized she must once have turned on the guilty and innocent alike.

"Honestly? I don't know," she said. "Their songs have opened shallow ways for decades. The deep gates were sealed by the founders themselves, with the court's own consent — and consent, once given to that thing, may be the only key. But there's a verse in the Choir's oldest song that none of them will sing alone. Casserly says it needs a full choir and a true map and a reason. We have two of the three as of last night."

"And the third?"

"You," she said. "Your reason. Which is the strongest kind there is."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone and street, the bells of Vell rang the hour, and beneath the bells — so faint that Ines might have invented it — came another sound, rising from somewhere very far down. A great many voices, singing in unison, in a language that predated the city that had sold them.

Serah heard it too. He watched her hear it.

"Nineteen days," she said.
