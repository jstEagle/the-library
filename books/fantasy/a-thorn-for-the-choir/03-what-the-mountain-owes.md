## 3. What the Mountain Owes

The Veilway opened like a held breath.

Ines had mapped a hundred thresholds in three years of secret work — the seam behind the tannery wall, the stair that went down from a cellar that had no cellar, the door in the alder grove that only existed on fog mornings. But he had never drawn one like this. The Choir's song peeled the mountain's skin back the way a hand peels an orange, and beneath it was not stone but *distance*, a corridor of folded dark where his gift flared so bright behind his eyes that he had to press his palm against them to keep from weeping.

He could see it all. Every branch. Every depth. The mountain was not a mountain; it was a knot of ways tied around a single room, and at the center of that room something old and patient was waiting to be fed.

"Stop looking," Serah Duvane murmured beside him. She walked with her inquisitor's blade still at her hip, though the Order had struck her name from its rolls and would strike her head from her shoulders if they found her here. "I've seen gifted folk stare too long at the deep ways. They come back wrong."

"I'm not staring." He lowered his hand. "I'm reading."

"Same thing, down here."

Ahead of them the Choir moved in single file, nine singers wrapped in smugglers' grey, their voices bound in scarves of waxed silk until they were needed. Their leader, a broad woman called Mother Cass with a voice like a landslide given grace, raised one fist and the column halted. The way before them forked into passages that did not exist in any geometry Ines trusted — one sloping down into warmth, one rising into cold that smelled of iron and old snow.

"The warm one," Ines said quietly. "It goes to the court."

Mother Cass looked back at him. "You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell everything from here." His voice cracked on it. "That's rather the problem."

They took the warm passage. And as they descended, the walls began to speak.

* * *

Mira Ishikawa had learned to count the meals.

Three a day, always the same: pale bread that tasted of nothing, broth that tasted of less. The food kept her body going the way oil keeps a lamp burning, but it gave nothing back. Nothing here gave anything back. That was the whole architecture of the place — a court of tall, courteous figures who smiled with too many teeth and called her *guest*, who fed her just enough that her soul would ripen slow instead of spoiling fast.

She had been there four years. She knew because she scratched marks into the underside of her cot, and because every solstice the court grew louder, hungrier, and one more name was called from the ledger and never answered again.

Tonight the court was restless. She could feel it through the floor like a storm coming up through the roots of a house.

She pressed her thumb against the newest scratch and thought of Ines. Not with hope — hope was a luxury the court taxed heavily — but with the plain stubborn love that had gotten her through four years of pale bread. *If you're alive, brother, stay small. Stay hidden. Don't come for me.*

She had taught him to swim by throwing him in the canal. She should have known he'd do the same to himself someday.

* * *

The charter was carved into the throat of the mountain.

That was how Ines thought of it, standing in the vast antechamber where the warm passage ended: a hall whose walls were covered floor-to-vault in script, dense and looping, written in a metal that gleamed dull gold and was not gold. His gift read it before his eyes could — the way it read coastlines and river-courses, the true shape of things beneath their painted surfaces.

"It's a contract," he said. His own voice sounded far away. "The founding charter of Vell. It's not law. It's a *bill of sale*."

Serah stepped closer to the wall, and he watched her face as she read what she could. Her lips moved. Stopped. Went white.

"'Signed in souls,'" she quoted softly. "'One hundred and eleven souls, delivered at each solstice, in perpetuity, as consideration for the city's continuance.' Gods. The Edict of Ash — the hunts — they're not piety. They're *collections*."

"Not collections." Ines's finger traced a clause near the bottom, and his gift translated the signature block with terrible clarity. "Payments. Look. The first payment. The founders didn't buy Vell with coin."

He read the names aloud because he could not stop himself, because his gift had already read them and silence would have been a lie:

"Ishikawa. Tomas Ishikawa. Lien Ishikawa."

His parents. Sold with the city's founding, forty years before Ines was born, their names inked into the bedrock of the place he'd grown up thinking of as home. He heard Serah say his name once, gently, and did not answer. Somewhere above them, in a city built on this paper, people were lighting candles against witches while the candles themselves were made of the rendered dead.

"They're still here," he said. "Their names are still legible. If a contract can be broken—"

"Contracts with this court don't break," said Mother Cass grimly. "They're renegotiated. Or they're paid."

"And that," said a voice like cool water over stones, "is why we are pleased you came."

* * *

Mira felt the change before the doors opened.

The hunger in the air turned, the way a dog turns when its master whistles. The court was excited. In four years she had never felt the court be *excited*.

They came for her at the hour between hours, two of the tall courteous figures, and walked her not to her cell but upward, through galleries she'd never been permitted to see, to a hall where a dais stood empty and a table had been set for one.

"A guest of consequence arrives tonight," said her escort, in the tone of a steward announcing weather. "Your brother. The cartographer. You will sit across from him and smile, little soul. Your face is part of the setting."

Her heart did not leap. Four years had taught her that much discipline. But underneath the discipline something small and fierce turned over, like a fish under ice, and she let herself feel it for exactly three heartbeats before she buried it where the court could not taste it.

*He came anyway,* she thought. *Of course he came anyway. I threw him in the canal.*

* * *

The court received them in a chamber that Ines's gift refused to map.

That had never happened. He had mapped the inside of storms, the undersides of dreams, the fold of space behind his own eyelids. But this room slid off his perception like water off waxed cloth, and the thing seated at its center — vast, robed in the dark between stars, wearing a crown of unlit candles — existed only as a pressure, a presence, a voice.

"Ines Ishikawa." The voice came from everywhere, including, horribly, from inside his own mouth. "Son of Tomas. Son of Lien. We remember your line. We hold their signatures still."

"You hold them *captive*," Serah said, and her hand was on her blade though both of them knew steel meant nothing here.

"We hold them *securely*. There is a difference, inquisitor, though your Order never learned it." The darkness inclined, courteous. "You have come for the sister. Mira. A fine soul, nearly ripe. We confess we anticipated you. The harvest is richer when the fruit walks in willingly."

Ines's hands were shaking. He made them stop. "Name your price."

"Mira's freedom. Full release, her name struck from our ledger, her soul returned whole." A pause, savoring. "In exchange for your gift. The cartographer's eye. The sight that reads what is unwritten. With it, we could chart the Veilways entire — every realm, every threshold, every soft place in the world's skin. We have been confined to this mountain since the charter was signed. You would give us the world."

The temptation did not arrive as fire. It arrived as arithmetic. *One life for a gift. One sister for a sight. And if I refuse, the solstice comes in nineteen days and she is consumed forever, and my parents' names rot in this rock until the sun burns out.* He thought of Mira teaching him to float on her palms in the canal. He thought of the ledger in the ashes of his shop, her name sold *to the court beneath*.

"Yes," he heard himself say.

"No—" Serah started.

"Alone," Ines said. "I sign alone. The Choir withdraws. The inquisitor withdraws. No tricks, no songs at the threshold. You have my word."

Behind him, Mother Cass made a sound low in her throat. And behind *her*, one of the nine — Pellam, the young tenor with the burn-scarred hands who had sung the mountain open — shifted his weight, and Ines saw, with the terrible clarity of his gift, the shape of the man's loyalty bent like a compass needle toward the crown above.

Pellam had been reporting since the alder grove.

"You're generous," the court said, delighted. "We accept—"

"No," said Pellam, stepping forward, silk scarf torn from his throat, and his voice when he sang was not a song but a *summons* — a herald's cry, the Edict's own cadence, and the chamber filled with light that was not light and soldiers that were not quite soldiers, crown-wrought things of ash and oath. "By authority of the Edict of Ash, these persons are seized. The gift is claimed *for the crown* — which holds the charter's other end, little cartographer. Did you think the hunt served the mountain? The mountain serves *Vell*. And Vell serves whoever holds the pen."

Chains of cold closed on Ines's wrists. Serah fought — he heard her blade ring, heard Mother Cass's roar, heard the Choir scatter like startled birds — and then a blow took his knees and the floor rose up and the last thing his gift showed him, unbidden, merciless, was a map of the chamber after all, one thin unmapped thread running beneath the dais, down past the ledger-hall, to a small cell where a woman sat scratching marks into the underside of a cot.

*Mira.*

The court leaned close, and its breath smelled of extinguished candles.

"Nineteen days," it whispered. "Do try to be worth the wait."
