## 1. The Arithmetic of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, Ulyana Okonkwo had learned in her first month at the Gilded Thorn, was that they refused to stay counted.

A bottle of memory did not weigh the same on the day it was sold as it had on the day it was corked. Grief lightened a bottle; joy made it heavier, though no one could say why, and the guild's scales — brass, honest, older than the building — registered the difference to the grain. Borrowed luck came back diminished if the borrower had wasted it, and swelled strangely if they'd needed it desperately. And one honest lie, the rarest item in inventory, gained value every year it went unsold, like wine, or resentment.

So every night, Ulyana sat in the counting room above the vaults with a candle, an inkpot, and three ledgers, and she made the numbers agree. Not because they wanted to. Because she made them.

Tonight the candle was down to its last thumb-width and the autumn rain was working at the shutters like a patient creditor, and Ulyana dipped her pen and wrote, in her small exact hand:

*Inventory reconciliation, 14th of Harrowmonth. Discrepancy noted: one (1) jar, last summer, contents diminished by approx. two spoonfuls.*

She underlined *two* twice.

Someone had been eating the summer.

It was not theft, exactly. Nothing in the Gilded Thorn was ever exactly anything. The jar of last summer sat on the third shelf of the second vault, squat and green-glassed, full of a July afternoon from nine years back: heat shimmering off cobbles, cut grass, a dog barking somewhere, the particular gold light that made even ugly streets look forgiven. Customers bought spoonfuls of it when winter got into their bones. Vespera allowed it, priced by weight, and the ledger recorded each withdrawal, and the jar was supposed to hold out until spring.

But the withdrawals logged totaled eleven spoonfuls, and the jar's weight said thirteen were gone.

Ulyana sat back and looked at the discrepancy the way other people looked at a stranger loitering near their door. She did not jump to conclusions. Conclusions, like debts, had to be earned.

Possibilities: the seal had failed and the summer had evaporated. Possible; summers were volatile stock. Or someone with vault access had taken two spoonfuls without logging them, which meant either carelessness — unforgivable — or sentiment, which was worse. Sentiment was how ledgers died. She had seen it happen at her mistress's cottage, before: a hedge-witch's book of accounts gone soft, favors written in the margins, debts forgiven because someone's eyes were pretty, until the whole book was fiction and the witch who kept it owed more than she owned.

Ulyana had left that house with nothing but a grimoire and a talent for arithmetic, and she had sworn, on the long cold road to this city, that she would never again be the person who let kindness cook the books.

She wrote beneath the entry: *Investigate. Quietly.*

Then, because she was thorough rather than cruel, she added: *If Maren took it for her coughing, dock my wages instead.* Maren was the guild's bottler, sixty years old, lungs like wet paper, and last summer was the only medicine that touched her. Ulyana docked no one's wages for breathing. But the ledger would show the loss accounted for, because a loss unaccounted for was a lie, and lies compounded.

That was the whole of her philosophy, and it fit in one line: *everything is owed.*

Vespera had said it to her on her first night, four years ago, when Ulyana had turned up half-frozen at the guild door with a stolen book under her coat and a story about being good with numbers. The guildmother was a tall woman with iron-grey hair coiled like a rope and eyes that weighed you before you finished speaking. She had read Ulyana's trial sums in silence, set them down, and said: "We trade in impossible things, girl. Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. Second chances, at a markup. There is one rule older than the guild itself, and it is not *be kind*, and it is not *be clever*. It is *everything is owed*. Every gift is a loan. Every mercy accrues interest. Keep that in your bones and you will do fine here."

Ulyana had been seventeen and starving, and the words had sounded like cruelty. Four years later they sounded like comfort. That was the trick of growing up, she suspected: the same sentence, new music.

*

The counting room sat directly above the first vault, and there was a speaking-tube of polished copper that ran between them, because Vespera believed a ledger-keeper should be able to hear her inventory breathe. Most nights the tube delivered nothing but the faint sound of shelves settling and, once an hour, the chime of the ward-bells that confirmed nothing had escaped.

At midnight the bells rang, all seven, clean and true. Ulyana checked them against the schedule. In order: the memory vault, the luck cabinet, the dream drawers, the weather closet, the name-boxes, the reliquary of small mercies, and — last, always last, with a note in the margin that predated her employment — *the shelf we do not discuss*.

She had asked about the seventh bell in her first week. Vespera had said, "There are things we hold that we did not choose to hold," and had changed the subject with the smoothness of a card sharp, and Ulyana, who knew better than anyone what it was to be held against your will, had decided not to press. Some doors you left shut until you were strong enough to open them. That was arithmetic too.

Tonight, after the bells, the tube crackled and Maren's voice floated up, papery and amused. "You still up there, little auditor?"

"Balancing."

"It's midnight."

"Midnight is when the numbers tell the truth." Ulyana leaned toward the tube. "Maren. The jar of last summer is short two spoonfuls."

A pause with a smile in it. "Is it now."

"You wouldn't know anything about that."

"I know I'm an old woman with bad lungs and you're a girl who writes down other people's sins for a living." A cough, then, softer: "I also know somebody docked wages tonight in advance for something nobody's confessed to. Word travels in the walls, child. You've got a soft streak a mile wide under all that ink."

"I have a balanced column," Ulyana said. "The softness is incidental."

Maren laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into the sound of her shuffling away through the vaults, and Ulyana returned to her work and absolutely did not smile, and if the candlelight caught her face at a kind angle, that was between her and the candle.

*

The ledgers themselves deserved description, because they were the closest thing Ulyana had to a country.

There were three. The Green Book held inventory: everything the guild owned, from the trivial (a phial of courage, diluted, for job interviews) to the considerable (the deed to a man's regret, purchased at auction, still unopened). The Red Book held obligations: what the guild owed and was owed, and it was the thicker of the two, because the Gilded Thorn had been lending impossibilities for a hundred and forty years and interest never slept. And the Black Book — small, locked, chained to the desk — held the exceptions. The entries so old or so strange that they predated the guild's own founding, inherited with the premises like ghosts in the walls.

Ulyana had reconciled the Green and Red Books to the coin within her first year. The Black Book resisted her. Its early pages were in scripts that predated the city's current alphabet, and its later pages drifted into handwriting that made her eyes slide off, the way a dream slides off at waking. Vespera said the Black Book was "historical" and "not operational," and Ulyana, who trusted arithmetic more than reassurance, had privately resolved to read every page of it before she died.

She was a third of the way through. Some nights that felt like plenty. Some nights it felt like standing at the edge of a well, reading the graffiti on the top stones and wondering about the bottom.

Tonight she worked the Red Book. This week's obligations, entered fair and neat:

*Borrowed luck, 3 days' worth, to Ostrec the roofer — due back within the season, plus one favor, unspecified, callable.*
*Memory of a mother's voice, rented to a sailor departing on the tide — returnable in person only. (They always cry at this one. Note: raise price. Grief should cost what it's worth.)*
*One honest lie, held in trust for the widow Callas, unsold these six years. Appraised value: rising. Do not disturb.*
*Second chance, partial, to a boy who cheated at cards — terms: he must spend it honestly or it reverts with penalty. Watch him.*

And beneath those, the ordinary business that paid for the extraordinary: rents collected on the three shops the guild owned, interest on loans of actual money to actual merchants, the bread account, the candle account, the wages — hers modest, Maren's generous, the enforcers' retainer sitting unused because the guild hadn't needed enforcing in two years.

Everything balanced. Everything always balanced, because Ulyana made it balance, and there were nights — most nights, if she was honest, and honesty was the one luxury she permitted herself free of charge — when balancing felt like enough. More than enough. It felt like safety.

She had grown up in a house where the numbers never agreed, where her mistress's accounts were a web of favors and fury, where a child could be told she was family on Tuesday and collateral on Thursday. Here, in this room, with this candle, the world obeyed rules she could verify. Two and two made four. Debts came due. Nothing was given that was not recorded, and nothing was recorded that could not be found again. If her whole life was a ledger, then at least it was *her* ledger, kept in her own hand, and no one could rewrite an entry she had witnessed.

Wary contentment, she thought, dipping her pen. That was the honest term for it. Contentment with a lock on it.

She was writing the closing line — *Books balanced, 14th Harrowmonth, all columns agree, discrepancies noted and pursued* — when the candle did the thing.

*

Later, she would try to describe it to Davor Kestrel, and fail, and try again, and fail better. But in the moment there was no describing. There was only the fact of it.

The candle flickered.

Candles flicker; that is their profession. But this was not wind — the shutters were latched, the flue drew steady, the counting room was sealed as tight as a purse. And it was not the ordinary stutter of a wick drowning in its own wax. It was a *lean*. The flame bent sideways, toward the window, toward the street below, the way a dog leans toward a door a heartbeat before the knock comes. Then it stood up straight again, burned blue at its heart for the space of one heartbeat, two —

— and guttered out.

Not blown out. *Guttered*, the way a candle does when it has finished saying something.

Ulyana sat in the dark with her pen still wet and her heart going like a debt collector's knuckles, and she did the thing she always did with the inexplicable: she counted. Ten heartbeats. Twenty. The dark held. The rain continued its patient work on the shutters. Somewhere below, the ward-bells did not ring, which was somehow worse than if they had.

She relit the candle with hands that were almost steady — almost was acceptable; perfectly steady would have meant she hadn't understood what she'd seen — and she looked at the flame, and the flame looked like any flame, small and obedient and made of chemistry.

Prayer candles were not supposed to move. That was the whole point of them. The Gilded Thorn sold votives among its lesser stock — cheap tallow ones, blessed by nobody in particular, guaranteed to carry a wish upward for as long as they burned. Three centuries ago, everyone knew, the old gods had gone silent, and prayers had become a dead letter trade, wishes posted to an empty house. The guild's candles were sentimental stock, essentially decorative. Ulyana stocked them, priced them, and believed in them exactly as much as she believed in the jar of last summer's ability to make anyone truly happy: technically, commercially, and not at all.

Her candle was not a prayer candle. Her candle was tallow from the chandler on Cooper's Lane, two coppers the pound, no blessings attached.

And it had leaned toward the window like something listening.

Ulyana reached for the Red Book, then stopped. What entry would she even make? *Discrepancy noted: one (1) candle, behaved strangely.* Under which column? Assets? Omens?

Instead she pulled a scrap of paper toward her and wrote, in the private shorthand she used for things too strange for the official record:

*Candle moved. No wind. Toward the street. Blue at the core. As if answering.*

She looked at the words *as if answering* and disliked them immediately, because they presumed a question, and questions implied askers, and askers implied —

Below her feet, muffled through floor and stone and shelf, very faint, the seventh bell gave a single chime. Out of turn. Off schedule. The bell for the shelf they did not discuss.

Ulyana sat very still.

Then she blew out her candle — deliberately, herself, on her own terms, because if the dark was coming she preferred to sign for it — and picked up her lamp and her ring of keys, and went down to count the thing that could not be counted.

* * *

The stairs to the vaults were stone, worn concave by a century of feet, and Ulyana took them at the pace she took everything: measured, deliberate, refusing both hurry and hesitation. Hurry invited mistakes. Hesitation invited fear. She permitted herself neither, and if her grip on the banister was firmer than usual, the banister was discreet.

The first vault opened onto cool air and the smell of glass and old paper and, underneath it, something green and far away, like a meadow remembered by someone else. Shelves rose to the ceiling in ranks, thousands of bottles and jars and boxes, each labeled in the guild's careful hand, each holding its piece of somebody's life. Memories mostly, in colors: griefs gone grey-green, joys a suspicious gold, one entire shelf of first kisses in shades of dawn-pink that Maren swore sold best at midwinter. The luck cabinet stood apart in its cage of iron filigree, humming faintly, the way luck did when it was fresh. The dream drawers breathed slow and sweet.

Nothing was wrong. That was the first thing she verified, lamp raised, walking the aisles with her keys clicking softly against her hip. Seals intact. Labels legible. Weights — she checked eleven at random against the reference scale — correct to the grain. Whatever had leaned her candle toward the window had not disturbed the inventory.

Which left the seventh bell.

The shelf they did not discuss lived at the vault's far end, past the reliquary of small mercies, behind a curtain of grey wool that Vespera had hung there before Ulyana's time. Behind the curtain: a single shelf, and on it a handful of items that appeared in no catalog and answered to no price. Ulyana had seen them exactly once, in her first month, when she had followed the wrong corridor and Vespera had found her there and said, in a voice with no anger in it at all, which was frightening in its own way: "Some things are held, not owned. We keep them because someone must. Go back to your numbers, girl."

She had gone back to her numbers. For four years she had gone back to her numbers.

Now she stood before the grey curtain with her lamp held high, and the seventh bell's single chime still seemed to hang in the air like a struck note refusing to decay, and she thought about the candle leaning toward the window. Toward the street. Toward the world outside, where three hundred years of unanswered prayers lay stacked like undelivered letters.

*Toward the window,* she thought. *Not toward the shelf.* Whatever had come — whatever had stirred — it hadn't come from below. It had come from beyond. From the empty house where the gods used to live.

From the desk above, faint through the floor, came the sound she had been waiting for without knowing it: the ward-bells ringing the hour. All seven, in order, clean and true.

Except that the seventh rang twice.

Ulyana stood in the dark aisle with ten thousand bottled lives glittering around her like a frozen crowd, and she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the candle went out. Candles did not lean. Bells did not ring twice. Jars of summer did not lose spoonfuls on their own. The world she had built her safety on was a system of columns, and tonight, for the first time in four years, the columns did not agree.

Something had changed in the ledger of the world. Something old had come due.

She pulled the grey curtain aside, and looked at the shelf they did not discuss, and saw that the smallest object on it — a candle, white, ancient, unlit for as long as anyone could remember — was burning.

Its flame stood straight up. Perfectly still. Patient as an unpaid debt.

And it was burning blue.
