## 2. What the Guild Sells

The vaults of the Gilded Thorn opened at dawn, which was a lie Vespera told everyone. They opened whenever Ulyana unlocked them, and Ulyana rose before dawn because she had learned, in six years of keeping other people's accounts, that the hour before sunrise belonged to whoever wanted it badly enough to be awake for it.

She came down the spiral stair with her ledger under one arm, a stub of tallow candle in the other, and the keys on a ring that hung heavy as a manacle from her belt. Forty-one locks. She had counted them her first week, when counting things was still a comfort rather than a profession. Now it was simply true: forty-one locks between the street and the deepest shelf, and every one of them hers to open.

The first vault smelled of cold stone and beeswax and something older underneath — a sweetness like fruit going soft in a dark room. Ulyana lit the wall sconces one by one, left to right, because the inventory began at the left wall and she did everything in order. Order was the only prayer she trusted.

"Shelf one," she said aloud, to no one, to the room. "Bottled memories, first pressing."

There were two hundred and six bottles on shelf one. She knew this the way she knew her own hands. Each bottle held a memory someone had sold — not lost, sold; the distinction mattered enormously to the guild's legal position and not at all to the people who wept at the counter afterward — and each had been catalogued by hand in a fair copy that lived upstairs, while the working ledger in Ulyana's grip recorded only movement: bought, sold, pawned, recalled.

She walked the shelves with her candle, checking seals. Most memories kept well. Grief soured fastest; joy went thin and watery after a decade; love was unpredictable, sometimes perfect for fifty years, sometimes curdling into something you didn't want to be holding when the customer asked for a taste. The guild's rule was that you never opened a bottle to test it. You trusted the seal, the wax, the date, and the arithmetic.

Ulyana trusted the arithmetic most.

"Lot fourteen," she murmured, pausing at a squat green bottle with a chipped neck. *A mother's voice singing in a kitchen, 40 seconds.* The seller had been a cooper's widow who needed rent money and could spare nothing else. Ulyana had written the entry herself three years ago and had thought about it more times than was professional. Some entries stayed in the head like splinters.

She made her marks. Moved on.

The second vault held the weather. Not weather exactly — seasons, moods of air, the particular quality of light on certain afternoons that rich clients paid obscene sums to revisit. A jar of fog off the harbor, harvested by a guild agent with a net and remarkable patience. A phial of thunder, which rattled faintly if you walked past it too fast, and which Ulyana had never once looked at directly because the last clerk who had was deaf in his left ear until he died.

And there, on its own padded shelf with its own lock, the jar of last summer.

It was the guild's crown jewel and its great embarrassment, depending on whom you asked. An entire season, compressed: the heat of it, the long gold evenings, the smell of cut grass and river water and someone frying onions at dusk. A farmer had sold it during the famine year to buy seed grain, and the guild had resold tastes of it ever since — a spoonful of July to a dying woman, an hour of August to a man about to emigrate. Vespera called it the finest asset in the house. Ulyana called it what it was: a whole year of somebody's life, decanted.

She checked the seal. Intact. She wrote the date. Her pen moved in small, exact strokes, each numeral closed like a door.

"You talk to them."

Vespera stood in the doorway of the second vault, and Ulyana did not jump, because jumping was for people who hadn't spent six years learning where the guildmother's footsteps fell, which was nowhere. Vespera moved like a debt arriving: suddenly, and always eventually.

"I talk to the inventory," Ulyana said. "It keeps me honest."

"It keeps you strange." Vespera came down the aisle, silver-haired, straight-backed, dressed in grey wool that probably cost more than the building. She was sixty if she was a day and carried herself like thirty, and she had never once told Ulyana her age, which Ulyana respected the way she respected all good bookkeeping: information withheld is information owned. "Inventory doesn't answer. That's rather the point of it."

"Then I'm safe."

Vespera smiled the smile she used on customers who were about to spend money they'd regret. "Come. Walk the deep shelves with me. It's audit day, and I want your eyes on the old stock."

*Audit day.* Ulyana's stomach performed a small, unauthorized calculation. Audit day meant the oldest ledgers, the ones chained to their lecterns, the ones written in hands dead before Ulyana was born. She loved them and dreaded them in equal measure, the way you might love and dread a large dog.

They walked back through the first vault together, past the memories, past the shelf of borrowed luck — small clay tokens, each one a finite quantity of good fortune drawn out of a life like blood from a vein — and past the iron box at the end of row nine that held the guild's rarest item.

One honest lie.

Nobody knew whose lie it was anymore. It had come with the founding, or so the story went, sealed in iron because an honest lie was a paradox and paradoxes needed containing. Vespera touched the box lid once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and walked on. Ulyana wrote *verified, sealed* in the ledger and did not ask. In six years she had learned which questions Vespera answered. This was not one.

* * *

The deep vault was beneath the others, reached by a stair that went down farther than the building above it plausibly allowed. Ulyana had stopped wondering about the geometry years ago. The guild traded in impossible things; it was fitting that the guild itself refused to fit inside its own footprint.

Down here the candles burned slower. Down here the air tasted of dust and time and, faintly, of the jar of last summer, though that was probably her imagination lending the dark a kindness it hadn't asked for.

"The founding stock," Vespera said, sweeping her hand along a shelf of items so old their labels had gone brown and illegible. "Before my time. Before my predecessor's time. We don't sell these. We hold them."

"Hold them for whom?"

"For the account," Vespera said, which was not an answer, and both of them let it stand as one, because that was how they worked.

Ulyana set her candle on the reading ledge and unchained the oldest ledger — the First Book, the clerks called it, though never within Vespera's hearing, since Vespera maintained there was nothing mystical about an old accounting document, and she was right, and also she was wrong, and Ulyana had never resolved which.

The First Book was bound in leather gone black as tar, and it was large as a paving stone, and its pages were vellum fine as onion skin. The early entries were in scripts Ulyana couldn't read — she'd taught herself three alphabets since coming to the guild and these defeated all of them. Later pages settled into trade-tongue, then into modern hand. The guild was three hundred years old, give or take a war. The First Book was older. Nobody said by how much. Nobody liked the number when they estimated it.

"We'll start at the current century and work backward," Vespera said. "I want the provenance of lot ninety-one confirmed. There's a buyer in the capital asking questions I don't like."

"About a memory?"

"About a season." Vespera's mouth thinned. "Rich men collect summers the way boys collect beetles. Pin them to a board and they stop being alive. Confirm the chain of custody and we'll decide whether to sell."

Ulyana nodded and turned pages. This was the work she was best in the world at: following a thing backward through paper, entry by entry, sale by sale, until it stood naked at its origin. Provenance was just a story told in numbers, and Ulyana had never met a story she couldn't audit.

Lot ninety-one. Sold twice, pawned once, recalled once under clause twelve — she noted the clause with approval; whoever had drafted it had understood collateral. Back another fifty years. Another hand, another ink, the same careful chain. Back another hundred. The script grew spikier, the abbreviations stranger. She worked with her lips slightly parted, the way she did when the numbers were talking.

Vespera drifted toward the far shelves, checking seals among the founding stock, her shadow long and patient on the stone.

Ulyana turned another page, and another, and reached the section where the ledger stopped being the guild's history and started being something before it. The paper changed here — older, creamier, ruled in a grid so faint it might have been worn into the vellum rather than drawn. The entries were sparse. A name. A sum. A term. Over and over, in different hands across what must have been centuries:

*Soul of [name], pawned against [obligation], term [number] years.*

Page after page of it. Souls, listed like stock. Bottled, shelved, held against debts she couldn't parse — obligations owed to whom? There was no counterparty named. Just *the Court*, capitalized, in every entry, the way you'd write *the Crown* or *the Church*, institutions too large to need surnames.

Her quill slowed.

It wasn't horror, yet. It was professional unease, the feeling of a column that won't sum, of a total that sits two coppers off no matter how many times you run it. She read the entries the way she read everything — mechanically, thoroughly, looking for the error — and the names passed through her like water through a sieve. Aldric. Maren. A child listed only as *the cooper's girl*. Terms of twenty years, fifty, a hundred. All held by the Court. All outstanding.

She turned the page.

The handwriting changed.

That alone would have stopped her — new hand, new era, fine. But it wasn't the age of the writing that stopped her. It was the shape of it. The tall ascenders with the slight forward lean. The way the numerals closed their loops tight, like doors. The little hook she put on her sevens because a mistress once beat it into her — *a seven without a crossbar is a lie waiting to happen, girl.*

Ulyana knew that handwriting.

She saw it every day. She saw it in the working ledger under her own hand, in the margin notes, in the fair copies, in the shopping lists pinned to her door.

The entry read:

*Soul of Ulyana Okonkwo, pawned against obligation unnamed, term: life of debtor plus interest. Held by the Court. Status: outstanding.*

She did not move for a long time. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere above, faint through forty-one locks' worth of stone, the city was waking — carts, gulls, a bell — the ordinary world going on with no idea what was written in the basement.

"That's not possible," she said quietly. Her voice sounded very loud.

"What isn't?" Vespera, from the far aisle. Not alarmed. Curious, in the mild tone of a woman who had heard impossible things described in this room for decades and found most of them negotiable.

"This entry." Ulyana's finger hovered over the page without touching it, the way you hover over a snake. "It's in my hand."

Silence. Then Vespera's footsteps, unhurried, crossing the vault. She leaned over Ulyana's shoulder and looked, and Ulyana watched her face in profile, and what she saw there was not surprise.

It was recognition. Old, settled, wearied recognition — the look of a woman finding a bill she'd known was coming.

"Yes," Vespera said. "It is."

"I've never written in this book. I've never *seen* this book before today — I mean, I've seen it, but I've never—" Ulyana stopped herself. Breathed. Did what she did. Went back to the facts. "This entry predates me. The vellum is older than I am. Ink doesn't lie about drying, Vespera. Whoever wrote this wrote it decades ago, and they wrote it in my handwriting, which means either I wrote it in some future I don't remember, or—"

"Or," Vespera said, "someone wrote it in a hand they knew would one day be yours."

The vault got colder. Or Ulyana did.

"Whose?"

Vespera straightened. For a moment — a long moment, long enough for Ulyana to count four breaths and hate herself for counting — the guildmother looked at the page instead of at her. When she finally turned, her face had closed like a ledger at day's end: everything entered, nothing readable.

"Who taught you your letters, Ulyana?"

The question landed strangely, sideways, in a place that wasn't the conversation they were having. "You know who. My mistress. The hedge-witch I—" She stopped again, because she heard it now, the shape of the trap assembling itself around the words. The hedge-witch. The woman whose cottage she'd fled at seventeen with a stolen grimoire and a talent for sums. The woman who had beaten the crossbar into her sevens. The woman who had—

"*No,*" Ulyana said.

"She was a careful woman," Vespera said gently, which was the worst possible thing to say gently. "Careful with her letters. Careful with her contracts. And careful, I think, to teach you a hand she could recognize anywhere — even here, even now, even in a book older than both of us." She touched the page, just beside the entry, not on it. "Your soul has been in this ledger since before you could write. Someone simply waited for the hand that would match."

Ulyana looked down at the entry. At her own name in her own writing in a book she'd never touched. *Status: outstanding.* The words sat there with the terrible patience of all entries, waiting to be reconciled.

Somewhere above them, faint and ordinary, the city bell rang the hour, and half a street away a woman was lighting a prayer candle at a shrine that had been silent for three hundred years.

But Ulyana didn't know that yet. She only knew that her whole self was a line item, and the audit had just begun.

"Tell me everything," she said.

Vespera looked at her for a moment longer — pity and arithmetic in the same expression, which Ulyana would learn was the most honest face the guildmother owned — and said:

"Not here. Upstairs. Bring the book."
