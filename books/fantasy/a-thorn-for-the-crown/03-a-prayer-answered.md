## 3. A Prayer Answered

The trouble with impossible things, Ulyana had learned, was that they kept books too.

She'd been at the Gilded Thorn for two years now, long enough to stop flinching at the inventory lists, long enough to read *one honest lie, condition: buyer must not recognize it as a lie until spoken* without smiling. Long enough to know that the guild's ledgers balanced the way a knife balances on its edge — perfectly, and only because someone was always holding their breath.

She held her breath every night, in fact. That was the job.

Candlelight, ink-stained fingers, the great ledger open across her knees in the counting room above the vaults. She worked by column and candle because Vespera insisted the accounts be reconciled nightly — "the gods forgive many things, Ulyana, but they do not forgive an unbalanced page" — and Ulyana had come to love the ritual the way she'd come to love most things here: suspiciously, and then completely.

*Two bottles of borrowed luck, returned by the widow Hask, one-third used.* *Bottled memory of a first kiss, sold to a man dying of forgetfulness, payment deferred against his estate.* *One jar of last summer, unopened since the solstice, storage fee accruing.*

Everything owed. Everything entered. The iron rule of the Thorn, older than its walls: nothing given freely, nothing taken without price, and no debt left to rot on a page. It should have been cold work. It wasn't. There was a warmth in it that Ulyana couldn't have explained to anyone who hadn't grown up keeping accounts for a hedge-witch who charged orphans by the tooth. Here, at least, the arithmetic was honest. Here, if you were owed, you were *owed*, and the book said so in ink that could not be argued with.

She was halfway through the luck column when the prayer candle flickered.

It sat on the corner shelf — a stub of tallow the guild kept lit for customers who wanted to pray before they traded, a courtesy that cost nothing and, per Vespera, "implied obligation." It had been burning steadily all evening. But this flicker was wrong. Not the gutter of a draft. The flame *bent*, leaned toward the window like a dog hearing its name called from three streets away, and then stood back up straight, and burned a shade too gold, a shade too bright, like something behind it had drawn a breath.

Ulyana put down her pen.

She looked at the ledger. She looked at the candle. In her experience, when those two things disagreed about reality, the ledger usually won, and the times it didn't were the times she'd ended up running through hedgerows with a stolen grimoire under her arm.

"Candle," she said aloud, "you are not on my books."

The candle did not answer. But downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, she heard the vault door sigh — the deep brass groan it made when something inside shifted weight. Inventory did not shift. Inventory was catalogued, shelved, warded, and *still*.

Ulyana closed the ledger, marked her place with the ribbon, and went down to see what the night had decided to owe them.

* * *

The street outside the Gilded Thorn was called Candlemakers' Row, which was a lie twice over, since no candlemaker had worked there in forty years and the row sold everything except candles. By day it was fishmongers and fortune-tellers and a man who repaired clocks that had never been broken. By night it belonged to the guild's clients: people who needed things that couldn't be bought anywhere else, walking fast, hoods up, hands full of coin they'd rather not explain later.

At half past eleven, a woman came out of the crowd and stopped in front of the Thorn's dark windows, and began to pray out loud.

Ulyana watched her from the doorway. This was normal enough. Desperate people prayed at the Thorn's threshold all the time; there was an old superstition that prayers spoken on the guild's doorstep got heard faster, because the guild had a reputation — half true, wholly useful — for having connections everywhere, including places with no address. Usually Vespera would open the door and offer to sell the woman something, because a prayer offered for free is a debt with no paper, and free debts are the kind that ruin families.

But tonight Vespera was upstairs with the oldest ledger, doing whatever she did with it — Ulyana had never once seen its pages, only the way Vespera's hands moved over the binding, gentle, the way you touch a sleeping animal you don't entirely trust — and so nobody opened the door. And the woman prayed on.

Her name, Ulyana learned later, was Marta Voss, and her husband was a fisherman lost nine days off the Graywater shoals, and she had been to every temple in the city and been told by every temple what everyone had been told for three hundred years: *the gods sleep; your prayers go nowhere; buy our candles anyway.* Nine days. Two children at home. And somewhere in her, past despair, past dignity, was the animal place where a person will say anything to anyone, and so she stood on the doorstep of the Gilded Thorn and said it:

"Please. Please, whoever's listening — anyone — bring him home. I'll give anything. *Anything.* I'll pay any price—"

And the sky over Candlemakers' Row opened.

Not rain. Ulyana would remember this precisely for the rest of her life, because precision was the only weapon she had ever owned: it was not rain, it did not fall the way water falls, in drops and sheets. It fell in *fish*. Silver, thrashing, whole and living fish, dozens of them, hundreds, tumbling out of a cloudless black sky and slapping onto cobblestones, onto awnings, onto the shoulders of a screaming fishmonger whose stock had just, technically, doubled. They came down like coins from a torn purse. They came down like an answer.

Marta Voss stood in the middle of it with her arms raised and her face wet and her mouth open, catching fish in her skirts, and laughing, and crying, and saying *thank you, thank you, thank you* to nobody visible, while half the street poured out of doorways to stare upward into empty air and catch dinner from the gods.

Ulyana did not move. Ulyana counted.

It was reflex, pure and shameless. Even as wonder hit her like a wave — even as some young, buried part of her, the girl who'd believed in the old stories before the hedge-witch priced them out of her — even as that girl rose up in her throat singing, *they're real, they're real, they answered* — the auditor in her was already working. Fish falling in a street: mass, roughly, of several hundred pounds. Value, at market: considerable. Cost: not yet entered anywhere. And the word that made her blood run cold was the word the woman had shouted into the dark, the word every desperate soul shouts, the word the temples had spent three centuries teaching people was safe again:

*Anything.*

"You shouldn't have said that," Ulyana whispered, to a woman who couldn't hear her. "Oh, you poor fool. You shouldn't have said *anything*."

Because Ulyana had read contracts. Ulyana knew what happened to words like that, spoken aloud, unwitnessed, unwritten, with no counterparty named and no terms set. Somewhere, something had just accepted them. Somewhere, a page had turned. And whatever had caught that prayer and answered it with a skyfall of silver fish had not done it as a gift.

Nothing was ever a gift. That was the first law of the universe, and the second law of the Gilded Thorn, and Ulyana had built her whole careful life on both.

The fish-rain lasted four minutes. When it ended, the street lay silvered and stinking and stunned, and Marta Voss knelt in the middle of it holding a fat gray mullet against her heart like a child. People were already gathering fish in buckets and aprons. Someone started cheering. Someone else started weeping. A boy ran toward the temple quarter, shouting that the gods were awake, THE GODS WERE AWAKE, and Ulyana felt the words go through her like a splinter, because she had grown up on the edge of the last generation that remembered what that meant, in her mistress's cottage, where the hedge-witch had kept her little gods fed and priced and furious.

"Inside, everyone," said a voice, silk over steel, and Vespera was suddenly there in the doorway, and Ulyana had not heard her come down. The guildmother stood in her gray travel cloak with the hood back, surveying the street the way a banker surveys a fire. Her eyes found the fish. Found Marta. Found the sky. Something moved behind her face that Ulyana had never seen there before, and it took Ulyana a moment to name it, because it was so out of place on Vespera Underhill, who had once stared down a debt-collector without blinking.

It was fear.

"Get the doors barred," Vespera said quietly. "All of them. Wake Tam and put him on the vault stairs." She turned, and her gaze snagged on Ulyana, and for one strange moment the guildmother looked at her ledger-keeper as though checking an entry. "Ulyana. The books. Tonight, everything gets entered *twice*. Once as recorded. Once as—" her mouth thinned, "—as outstanding."

"Outstanding to whom?" Ulyana asked.

Vespera looked up at the clean, starry, innocent sky, from which no more fish were falling, and said, with the weariness of a woman reading a bill she'd hoped was misdelivered:

"To whoever's collecting."

* * *

They didn't sleep, either of them. Ulyana sat in the counting room making double entries until her eyes burned, while below her the vault hummed with a sound she'd never heard it make before — a low, resonant tone, like a struck bell too deep to hear properly, coming up through the floor and through her chair and into her teeth.

Because the vault was answering. That was the horror she pieced together somewhere past midnight, pen frozen mid-column. Every impossible thing the Gilded Thorn had ever bottled, brokered, or bartered — every memory, every scrap of luck, every honest lie — none of it had been created by the guild. The guild *traded*. It moved goods between hands, took its margin, kept its books. The goods themselves came from somewhere else. From the old currents, the old sources, the sleeping machinery beneath the world that had gone silent three hundred years ago, forcing the guild to trade in leftovers, dregs, secondhand miracles bought low and sold high.

And now the source had woken up. And everything the guild had ever handled was, in some sense, *on loan*.

She tested it. She went down to the vault with a lamp and Tam shuffling nervously behind her, and she pulled the ledger of current holdings, and she checked the jar of last summer against its entry. The entry said: *contents stable, sealed, one summer, quality: perfect.*

The jar was fine. The jar was beautiful. The light inside it still moved like afternoon through wheatfields.

But when she held the lamp close, she saw that the seal had changed. The wax bore the guild's mark, the twisted thorn, pressed there decades ago. Beneath it, fresh, thin, like writing under frost, a second seal had appeared — a spiral, coiled tight, turning slowly, very slowly, when she wasn't looking directly at it.

Someone had countersigned the guild's property.

"Tam," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Fetch me every jar, bottle, and reliquary in the east racks. We're going to check the seals."

By the third hour it was beyond doubt. Eleven items, eleven countersignatures, the same slow spiral appearing under the guild's wax like mold under paint. Bottled memories, borrowed luck, a vial of courage distilled from a soldier's last stand — all of it marked, all of it claimed, all of it, in the cold language of the new seals, *outstanding*.

Ulyana sat down on a crate among the glowing shelves and laughed, once, without humor, at the sheer audacity of it. Three centuries the guild had traded in these waters, skimming the surface, selling the dregs, telling itself the deep was dead. And the deep had kept books the whole time. The deep had *always* kept books. And now the deep was awake, and calling in margins.

"Wonder curdling into dread," she murmured to herself, not knowing she was summarizing her own life. "That's the whole trade, isn't it. That's all any of it ever was."

She thought of Marta Voss, kneeling in fish, thanking the dark. Whatever that woman had promised — *anything* — was already on its way to becoming an entry somewhere. And there was no auditor for her. No one to check the terms, contest the rate, argue precedent. Just a desperate woman and a listening dark, and a debt being written in a hand she'd never see.

Ulyana climbed back to the counting room as the sky went gray toward dawn. Vespera was there, the oldest ledger open before her at last, and the guildmother's face in the early light was terrible and calm, the face of someone who has just finished reading a letter they've been dreading for thirty years.

"Ulyana," she said. "Sit down."

"I'm standing."

Vespera's mouth twitched — almost a smile, gone instantly. "Of course you are." She turned the oldest ledger around and pushed it across the table, an act Ulyana understood, dimly and with rising alarm, to be unprecedented. "Before I show you this, tell me what you saw tonight. Precisely."

"A prayer answered on the doorstep. Fish. Hundreds. Witnessed by half the Row." Ulyana swallowed. "Countersignals on eleven items in the east racks. A spiral seal. And a vault that's humming."

"Humming." Vespera closed her eyes briefly. "Yes. It does that now." She tapped the ancient page in front of her, and Ulyana leaned over it — and forgot how to breathe.

The handwriting was hers.

Not similar. Not a forgery she could catch, and she could catch forgeries, it was her trade. It was *her hand* — her particular cramped ascenders, her habit of crossing sevens, the little hook she put on the tail of a *y*. Writing she had never written, on a page older than the guild itself, recording figures in a currency she didn't recognize beside a name she did:

**Okonkwo, U.**

"What," said Ulyana Okonkwo, very carefully, to the oldest ledger in the world, "is this?"

"That," said Vespera, "is why I need you sitting down."
