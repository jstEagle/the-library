## 5. Ledger of Souls

The oldest ledger in the Gilded Thorn did not want to be read.

I had suspected this for three days. It sat in the deepest drawer of Vespera's private cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of tallow and something older — grave-dirt, maybe, or the inside of a sealed room. The binding was not leather. I had pressed my thumbnail into it once and it had given like skin, warm, faintly resistant, and I had pulled my hand back as though scalded.

Three days I told myself it was cataloguing work. Three days I told myself a guild that traded in impossible things needed its impossible things counted, and an uncounted asset was a liability, and liabilities were my business. That was the whole of my employment, after all: I made the impossible legible. Bottled memories got shelf numbers. Borrowed luck got interest schedules. The one honest lie sat in a lead-lined box with a valuation note I had written myself — *depreciating; do not open* — because even a lie, if you were honest about it, was just a debt waiting to be called in.

Everything is owed. That was the first rule of the Gilded Thorn, chalked above the counting-room door in Vespera's own hand, and I had built my life on it the way other people built theirs on scripture. If everything is owed, then nothing is arbitrary. If every miracle has a price, then no one can take anything from you without your having agreed to it somewhere, in some clause, on some page. I found that comforting. I have always found arithmetic comforting, which should have told me something about the childhood I'd come from.

On the fourth night I unwrapped the book.

* * *

It opened to my touch, which I did not like. Old ledgers resist; this one sighed, the way a door sighs when someone has been standing behind it a long time, listening.

The paper was wrong for its age. Pages from two centuries past go brown and brittle at the edges, foxed with rust-colored blooms. These pages were pale as fresh-cut linen, and cold. Not cool — cold, the way stone is cold in a cellar in summer, a cold with intention behind it. And the ink. I held the first page to the candle and the ink did not reflect the light so much as drink it, each letter a small absence shaped like a word.

The script itself was beautiful. A looping, confident hand, the kind taught by tutors who charged by the hour, and every few pages the hand changed — a generation's worth of keepers, each one trained in the same school of letters, each one adding entries in the same tongue. Not Lowmarket, not High Cant, not any trade-language I knew. But ledgers are ledgers. Numbers are honest in any alphabet, and after an hour of cross-referencing against the guild's founding glossary — Vespera kept one, of course she did, tucked behind a false spine labeled *Sermons, Dull* — I began to make out the structure.

It was a pawnbook.

Not a ledger of sales. Sales end things. This was a ledger of pledges: items taken into keeping against future redemption, terms noted, interest accruing, collateral held. The entries ran back further than the guild's founding by a century at least, which meant the Gilded Thorn had inherited it, or been founded as a front for it, or — and this thought arrived cold and stayed colder — been founded *by* it.

The collateral column was what stopped my breath.

Souls.

Not souls the way preachers say it, vague and shining. Souls the way we said *memory, luck, shadow, name*: inventory. Each entry had a description field, and the descriptions were precise in the way only accountants and gods can be precise. *One soul, female, aged nine years, disposition cheerful, defect: trusts too easily.* *One soul, male, soldier, half-spent already, remainder sound.* *One soul, twin of the above, sold separately, do not reunite.*

Generations of them. Page after page. Some marked *redeemed* with dates and sums. Most marked *held*. A handful marked *called* — and next to those, in a different ink, a red that was not quite red, the notation *delivered below*, and below, in this context, needed no glossary.

I am not a woman who weeps over books. I have entered dead men's debts without blinking; grief is just a figure you carry forward. But I sat in that cold room with a candle guttering and read four generations of people reduced to line items, and my hands shook, and I told myself it was the chill.

Then I turned to the page written in my own handwriting.

* * *

I knew it was mine before I knew what it said. You know your own hand the way you know your own gait — the slight forward lean of my numerals, the way my sevens cross twice because Mistress Aldessa beat the habit into me and then, when I wouldn't stop, beat it back out again, leaving me with a sevens that crossed once but hesitated, a small scar in every number I've written since.

There it was. My hesitation-sevens. My lean. An entry in Ulyana Okonkwo's handwriting, dated eleven years before I learned to write.

The ink was still wet.

I did not touch it. Some instinct older than arithmetic kept my fingers flat on the desk, away from the page, the way you keep your hand from a dog that has gone quiet and still. I read instead. The glossary was open beside me now, bristling with slips of paper, and I translated the way you pull a splinter — slowly, knowing it will hurt, unable to leave it in.

*Received into pledge:* one soul, female. *Description:* apprentice, hedge-craft, aged zero years, term of gestation not yet complete. *Pledged by:* A. Aldessa, mistress of the craft, her mark here rendered as a thorned circle I had seen burned into the lintel of her cottage a thousand times. *Pledged to:* the Court Beneath the Mountain, care of the Gilded Thorn, holding agent. *Term:* the natural life of the named party, plus interest. *Interest schedule:* compounding annually from date of birth. *Redemption permitted:* no.

*Redemption permitted: no.*

Most entries allowed redemption. That was the whole architecture of pawning — the door left open, however narrow, however expensive. Mine had been closed at the drafting. Not a loan. A sale wearing a loan's clothes, filed among loans so that anyone reading quickly would pass over it, and anyone reading carefully would understand there had never been anything to buy back.

And in the margin, in that same impossible handwriting of mine, a note. Small. Almost kind.

*She will be good with figures. Charge accordingly.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. The candle burned down twice; I lit a third from the second's stub without looking at it. Somewhere above me the guild slept — Tam in his loft over the bottling room, the twins in their corner bunks, Vespera behind her door with its lock that clicked at odd hours. The building breathed around me, familiar as my own pulse, and for the first time in six years it felt like a thing I was inside of rather than a thing that held me up.

Mistress Aldessa had sold me before I was born.

No. Be exact. Exactness was the only weapon I had ever owned. She had pledged me before I was born, to the court beneath the mountain, through the agency of the guild whose books I balanced, and the entry stood in my own hand because — and here my mind skated sideways off the thought, twice, three times, before I forced it — because the ledger wrote its entries through its keepers. Because the hand on these pages was not any keeper's hand. It was the hand the ledger gave you, the way a lock gives your key its teeth. I had not written those words. I had been *going* to write them, someday, or some version of them, and the book had reached backward down the wire of my life and taken the shape of my intent for its own use.

Or simpler, and worse: the book simply used whoever held it, and someday I would sit at this desk and write that entry myself, and the wetness of the ink was just time being flexible about direction.

Both explanations were unbearable. I chose neither. I chose the third thing, the accountant's thing: verify.

I went back through the pledge-book page by page, and I looked for Aldessa's mark. Thorned circle, thorned circle — there, 1741, a boy child, redeemed never. There, 1768, a girl, *description: sings*, delivered below. There, 1802, a man who must have been her own brother, pledged against a cure that the ledger notes he did not survive long enough to need. Four generations of the desperate and the foolish and the loved, all passing through one hedge-witch's thorned circle, all flowing downhill like water finding its level, downhill being beneath the mountain.

She hadn't sold me out of malice. That was the vertigo of it, worse than cruelty would have been. She had sold me the way she sold everything — as part of a running balance, a standing arrangement, a *practice*. I had spent six years telling myself the story of my escape: the stolen grimoire under my coat, the road at dawn, the clever girl outwitting the wicked witch. And the whole time the truth had been sitting in a vault beneath my desk: she wasn't holding me. She was holding my *paper*. I hadn't fled her. I had walked out of the cottage and straight into the collateral department.

Every kindness of six years re-priced itself while I sat there. The bed they'd given me. The wage, fair by any market. The way Vespera had said, my first week, *you'll do, girl, you have the right sort of mind for our inventory* — I had glowed at that. I had *glowed*. The right sort of mind. A soul described as good with figures, charge accordingly. I had been hired to keep the books on my own lien.

The candle went out. All three stubs, at once, in air that had not moved.

In the dark, the ledger's pages turned themselves — softly, once, like someone shifting their weight in the next room — and settled open to my page.

* * *

I relit the candles with hands I made steady through pure administrative discipline. Breathe. Count. One flame, two flames, three. The world reassembled along its seams. The ledger lay where it had lain, open, patient, obscene.

Here is what I knew, and knowing it felt like standing at the edge of a high roof:

One. My soul was held by the court beneath the mountain, against a term of my natural life, plus interest, non-redeemable. Whatever that meant — and I had seen enough of the guild's trade to guess, and the guessing was a cold walk down a long stair — it meant that everything I called mine was mortgaged. My memory of my mother's face. My talent for sums. The specific weight of my attention, the way I could fall into a column of figures like a swimmer into still water. All of it, borrowed goods. I had been living my whole life on credit extended to a corpse-to-be.

Two. The Gilded Thorn was not my refuge. It was my *holding facility*. Someone — Vespera? the ledger itself? the arrangement Aldessa had made before my birth? — had ensured that the collateral would be kept close, kept safe, kept *productive*. Charge accordingly. I had been an appreciating asset this entire time, and assets, in my experience, were exactly as beloved as their yield.

Three. The old gods were awake. Everyone in the city had felt it since the fish-rain — prayers answered in the street, miracles blooming like mold, the guild's stock soaring while sensible people locked their doors. And if the gods were awake, then the court beneath the mountain was awake, and courts awake meant collections resumed, and collections meant someone would come, sooner or later, with my page copied fair and a polite request for delivery.

Four. I had perhaps until midwinter. Ledgers have seasons. Every collection cycle I had ever administered peaked at the year's turning, when accounts were squared and debts called in under the old law of the turning year. Midwinter was when the court would open its books.

I looked at my page a long time. At the thorned circle of Aldessa's mark. At the margin note in my stolen handwriting — *she will be good with figures* — and I found, underneath the terror, underneath the vertigo, something harder and more useful taking shape, the way a number takes shape out of noise when you finally find the pattern.

She had been wrong about one thing. The note said *redemption permitted: no.* But I had spent six years in the Gilded Thorn, and if I had learned one doctrine down to the marrow it was this: no contract is final. No contract has ever been final. Every instrument ever drafted has a flaw in it, because contracts are written by hands, and hands are imperfect, and somewhere between the intent and the ink a gap always opens — a clause mis-set, a condition unstated, a power granted too broadly or too narrowly. The guild lived on those gaps. We bought flawed debts cheap and worked them like ore.

Somewhere in that page, in that book, in that court's accumulated mountain of paper, there was a gap with my name in it.

I would find it. Not because I was brave — I sat in that cold room shaking like a struck bell, and I want that recorded honestly, whoever reads this, whatever this becomes. But because betrayal, properly processed, is just information, and information is just inventory, and inventory is mine. That was the one thing they had never thought to pawn: the part of me that read the fine print.

I closed the ledger. It resisted, then didn't. I wrapped it in its oilcloth, returned it to the deepest drawer, and locked the cabinet with Vespera's spare key, which I had copied in my second month and never had reason to use until tonight.

Then I sat back down at my own desk, in my own pool of candlelight, and pulled the guild's current ledger toward me, and began — because the books do not keep themselves, because routine is a rope you cling to in deep water — to enter the day's ordinary figures. Two memories bottled. Luck drawn down against the harvest festival. Interest accrued.

My pen hesitated on its sevens.

Above me, through the floorboards, I heard the front bell of the Gilded Thorn ring — once, twice, insistent, at an hour when no honest customer knocks — and then voices, low and urgent, and Vespera's door unlocking with its dry click, and her footsteps crossing the counting-room floor toward the stairs toward me, unhurried, deliberate, the footsteps of a woman coming to see whether her asset had finished reading.

I put down the pen. I folded my hands. And I waited for the guildmother to come and tell me, in whatever words she had prepared across all these careful years, exactly how much I was worth.
