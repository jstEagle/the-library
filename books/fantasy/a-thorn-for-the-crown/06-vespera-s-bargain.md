## 6. Vespera's Bargain

Vespera sent for Ulyana at the hour when the counting room went quiet — not midnight exactly, but the dead pocket of it, when even the city's drunkards had run out of coin and argument. The note was delivered by a junior clerk with ink-stained fingers and the particular stiffness of someone who had been told to say nothing on the way.

*The vaults. Come alone.*

Ulyana read it twice, then a third time, because reading things three times was how she did everything now. It was how she'd survived six years in the Gilded Thorn: assume nothing is simple, assume every kindness has a footnote, count twice before you sign once.

She brought her ledger anyway. Not out of defiance — though she told herself it was that too — but because she did not trust herself to stand in front of Vespera Underhill empty-handed. A ledger-keeper without a book was a soldier without a blade.

The guildhouse at night was a different animal than the guildhouse by day. By day it clattered and murmured with clerks and appraisers and the occasional customer weeping quietly over a jar they couldn't afford. At night the corridors held only candlelight and the soft tick of inventory settling, as if the impossible things themselves breathed slower after dark. She passed the shelf where the bottled memories slept in their rows of colored glass. Passed the cabinet where the borrowed luck was kept, each charm humming faintly like an insect against a window. Passed, without looking directly at it, the small iron box where the one honest lie lived — it had been sold eleven times and returned ten, and no one at the Gilded Thorn liked to discuss the eleventh.

The stairs down to the deep vault were narrow and worn hollow in their centers by two hundred years of feet. Ulyana counted the steps out of habit. Forty-one. The same forty-one there had always been, though tonight each one seemed to take longer.

Vespera was waiting in the oldest chamber, the one at the bottom, where the walls were not dressed stone but raw mountain rock veined with something pale and glittering. Salt, most people assumed. Ulyana had once scraped a sample and tested it. It wasn't salt.

There was a table. On the table, a single candle. And beside the candle, open, lay the ledger she had found her own handwriting in — the ledger older than the guild itself, older than the guildmother's title, older, if its binding was anything to go by, than the guildhouse built around it.

"You read page one," Vespera said. It was not a question. She sat with her hands folded, silver hair pinned back with the same plain bone comb she wore every day, looking for all the world like a woman about to discuss quarterly figures. "I know you read it because you're the only person in this building who would have understood what they were looking at, and you've avoided my eye for nine days."

"Ten," said Ulyana.

"Ten." Vespera almost smiled. "Sit down, Ulyana."

Ulyana did not sit. "I'd rather stand."

"Yes. I imagine you would." Vespera turned the old ledger so it faced outward, toward her, and drew the candle closer. "Then stand, and listen. I'm going to tell you how the Gilded Thorn was founded, and I'm going to tell you the truth of it, which means I'm going to tell you the parts I have never written down anywhere."

"I thought everything here was written down," Ulyana said. "That's supposed to be the rule. *Everything is owed.*"

"The rule is everything is owed," Vespera agreed. "Not everything is recorded. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a business and a confession." She touched the ancient cover with one fingertip, gently, the way you touch a wound you've stopped hoping will heal. "This book predates me. It predates the woman who trained me, who inherited the guildmother's chair from the woman who trained *her*. Six generations. Each of us has kept it, added to it when required, and told no one what it actually says. You broke that chain simply by being able to read it."

"Somebody should have locked it better."

"Somebody should have," Vespera said, "if locking it had ever worked. But the court beneath the mountain doesn't require secrecy, Ulyana. It requires *witnesses*. That's why the book sits where it sits, in the one vault every guildmother must walk past daily. So that we never forget we are watched."

The candle guttered. In the draftless room, with no door open, with no window within forty feet of stone — it guttered anyway, leaning sideways as if something exhaled.

Ulyana felt the cold arrive ahead of understanding, the way winter arrives ahead of snow. "Tell me," she said.

*

"The year the guild was founded," Vespera began, "there was a famine. Not the polite kind — not bad harvests and thin soup. The kind where the prayer candles burn all night in every window and the gods do nothing, because the gods had already gone quiet. Three centuries of silence, remember. Everyone knew the old gods didn't answer anymore. Everyone had made peace with praying into a well."

"But?"

"But a merchant named Aldous Thorn — the name is real, the gilt came later — discovered that silence is not the same as absence." Vespera's voice stayed level, unhurried, the voice of a woman reading terms aloud. "He found a door. Under the mountain, where the salt tunnels go deeper than any map admits. And behind the door, the old gods were not gone. They were *sleeping*, and their court was awake, and the court had learned to live on what the sleeping gods could no longer spend. Prayer, mostly. Worship, in its older forms. And when those ran short—" she paused, "—other currencies."

"What other currencies?"

"You already know. You read page one." Vespera turned the ledger around again and pushed it across the table, an inch, no more. "Read it properly this time. Read it as an auditor."

So Ulyana stepped forward, because her feet decided before her fear did, and she bent over the old pages by candlelight, and this time she did what she had refused to let herself do nine days ago: she followed the arithmetic all the way down.

Page one was the founding contract. Aldous Thorn's signature, cramped and desperate. Below it, a column of terms in a hand that was not a human hand — too regular, each letter identical to its siblings, as if written by a press rather than a pen. The guild received capital: luck, memory, miracle, stock for trade. The guild would prosper beyond any mortal venture.

And below that, the consideration. Not coin. Not worship.

*"In perpetuity, the Guild of the Gilded Thorn shall hold in trust one soul per generation, tendered by the sitting guildmother, redeemable upon terms to be determined by the Court Beneath the Mountain."*

One soul per generation. Every twenty or thirty years, a new name entered the book, and the guild went on trading in impossible things, and nobody above ground asked why the guildmother never seemed to age past a certain point of careful, or why the succession was always so smooth.

Ulyana's eyes moved down the column of names. Six of them. The last entry, the freshest ink, though the ink itself looked decades old:

*Ulyana Okonkwo. Tendered by Maren Vashti, hedge-witch, in settlement of outstanding debt. Term: indefinite. Interest: accruing.*

"There it is," Vespera said softly. "Now you know what I've known since the day I succeeded to this chair, which is the day I learned I hadn't succeeded to a business at all. I'd succeeded to a debt collection route with nice carpets."

Ulyana straightened up very slowly. Her hands were steady — she made them be steady, the way she made columns balance, through sheer administrative will. "You knew. When you took me in. When you gave me the ledgers, the room, the wage — you knew my name was already in this book."

"I suspected before you arrived. I confirmed it your first week." Vespera met her eyes without flinching, which Ulyana hated, because flinching she could have worked with. "Your mistress came to me before she came to you. Did you think it was chance? A runaway apprentice with a stolen grimoire and a genius for sums, wandering into the one guild in the city that needed exactly that? Maren Vashti owed this guild a great deal, and she settled part of it by pointing you at our door like an arrow."

"She *sold* me. Before I was born, the premise of it — she pawned my name, my—" Ulyana stopped. Her voice had climbed higher than she allowed it to climb in front of other people. She brought it back down. "You let me believe I'd escaped her."

"You had escaped her. That much was true." Vespera rose, unhurried, and walked to the vein-lit wall, where the pale glitter caught her face and made it briefly unreadable. "I have spent thirty years keeping this guild solvent inside a contract I despise. Do you understand what happens if I default, Ulyana? If the guild fails to tender a soul when the court calls? The court doesn't send bailiffs. It repossesses *everything the guild has ever sold.* Every bottle of memory poured back into the wrong head. Every borrowed luck reversed, doubled and inverted. Every miracle recalled mid-working. Half this city has bought from us. Half this city would come apart like wet paper."

"That's not my problem."

"No," Vespera agreed. "It isn't. Which brings me to why you're here."

She came back to the table. From her sleeve she produced a second document — not the old ledger, but a fresh sheet, written in her own precise hand, with blank spaces left where numbers belonged. Ulyana recognized the format instantly. It was a standard guild instrument. An offer of employment, structured as a lien.

"Two options," Vespera said. "I'll give them to you plainly, because you deserve plainness even if you won't thank me for it. Option one: you serve out your term. The court takes possession of your soul at whatever hour it calls, and in exchange, the Gilded Thorn continues, the city keeps its memories and its luck, and I keep buying time until I find a way out of this that doesn't end in fire. Your term might be long. The court is patient when it's fed."

"And option two?"

"Option two is the one I hate myself for offering, which you may file as evidence that I am not entirely lost." Vespera laid the document flat and turned it to face her. "Find a substitute asset. The contract specifies *a soul tendered by the sitting guildmother.* It does not specify whose. If you can identify another soul of equivalent weight and secure its lawful transfer—" her jaw tightened, just slightly, the only crack in thirty years of polish, "—the court will accept substitution. It has before. Once. Two hundred years ago. The records of that transaction are in this book, and I recommend you never read them, because the man who arranged it did not sleep afterward, and neither, I suspect, did his victim."

Silence filled the vault, thick as the candle smoke.

"You want me to sell someone else's soul," Ulyana said, "to buy back mine."

"I want you to *choose,*" Vespera said, and for the first time something moved underneath her voice, some old exhausted current. "That is the entire cruelty of this office, Ulyana. The court never forces anyone. It presents terms. It lets the guilty choose who pays. I have chosen, every year, to keep choosing — to protect four hundred people who work under this roof and ten thousand who trade with us, at the cost of one name per generation. That is the arithmetic. I don't expect you to love it. I expect you to *do it*, because you are the best auditor I have ever employed, and because the alternative is watching you get taken apart by something that has been patient for three hundred years and can afford to wait out your outrage."

Ulyana looked down at the document. The blank spaces waited for numbers. They always did. Every trap she had ever seen wore this exact shape: a form, a field, a place to sign.

"You said the court accepts substitution," she said slowly. "Once. Two hundred years ago."

"Yes."

"Then the precedent exists. Precedent cuts both ways." She lifted her eyes. "If the court will take a substitute soul from *me*, it will accept new terms from *anyone*. The contract isn't fixed. It's negotiable. You've just told me so yourself."

Something flickered across Vespera's face — not quite alarm, not quite hope. Something older and wearier than either. "Careful."

"No. I mean to be *careful*. Careful is the whole point of me." Ulyana picked up the pen from the table — Vespera's own pen, silver-nibbed — and instead of signing, she wrote in the margin of the offer, in small exact figures, a calculation: the guild's total assets against the founding debt, compounded over two centuries at the rate the court's own ledger implied. The number was obscene. The number was also, she noted, *not infinite*. Debts that grow forever are not debts; they are stories. This one had edges.

"You've spent thirty years paying interest on a principal you've never once audited," Ulyana said. "You don't know what the court thinks it's owed. You don't know what it *legally* holds. You know what this book says, and this book was drafted by frightened men in a famine year, in front of a counterparty that wrote in a nonhuman hand. I have read forged contracts before, Vespera. I grew up copying them for a witch who forged them professionally." She set the pen down. "I'm not serving my term. And I'm not finding you a substitute. I'm going to find the flaw."

"The flaw," Vespera repeated.

"In the fine print. There's always fine print. There's always a clause somebody signed in panic and nobody has read since." Ulyana closed the old ledger with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin or a door on a sleeping child. "You offered me a choice between being collateral and becoming a creditor. I decline both. I'm going to do what no one in six generations of guildmothers has apparently thought to do."

"Which is?"

"*Check the arithmetic.*"

For a long moment Vespera said nothing. The candle flame stood perfectly still now, as if listening. Then she laughed — one short, unlovely syllable of it, surprised out of her like blood from a split lip.

"Audit the court beneath the mountain," she said. "Of course. Of course that's what you'd say. That's exactly what I would have said, at your age, before I learned what this chair costs." She gathered the offer document and folded it away, and when she looked up again the guildmother was back, the polished surface restored, but something behind it had shifted a degree off true. "Then hear my final term, since you've rejected my first two. You may pursue your audit. But you will not speak of this to Kestrel, who arrived yesterday with trouble stitched into his coat, nor to anyone else. And you will not leave this building. The moment your name moves, the court notices. It has *always* noticed. That's what witnesses are for."

Ulyana tucked the old ledger under her arm — Vespera didn't stop her, which told her something important — and walked to the stair.

At the foot of the forty-one steps she paused, without turning around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I believe you hate this. I believe you've hated it for thirty years, and I'm sorry for what that's cost you. But you asked me to choose who pays, and I won't. Not me. Not a stranger. Not anyone." She started up the stairs. "That's not arithmetic, Vespera. That's the whole point of learning it."

She heard no reply. Only, faintly, from the depths of the vault, the sound of a candle going out — and beneath that, far beneath, in the mountain's stone bones, something turning over in its long sleep, the way a debtor turns over when he hears footsteps on the stair.

Upstairs, the guildhouse was dark except for one lamp burning in the entrance hall — and a figure seated beneath it on the hard bench where petitioners waited, coat still dusty from the road, refusing the bed that had clearly been offered and just as clearly not trusted.

Davor Kestrel looked up as she came out of the vault stair. His eyes went to the ledger under her arm, then to her face, and whatever he saw there made him stand.

"You look," he said, "like a woman who's just read something she shouldn't have."

"Yes," said Ulyana. "And I need to tell someone about it. Immediately. Before I lose my nerve."
