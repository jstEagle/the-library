## 7. The Debt-Collector Who Quit

Davor Kestrel counted his money by feel, in the dark, the way he'd learned to do it as a boy. Eleven coins and a bent nail that had once been lucky. He laid them out on the tavern table under his palm so no one could see how few they were, and he ordered the cheapest thing on the board, which was hot water with a rumor of barley in it.

The Gilded Thorn was three streets away. He could smell it from here, faintly, the way you can smell rain before it comes — candlewax and old paper and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft in a cellar. Impossible things. He'd spent nine years collecting on impossible things for people who could not afford them, and he knew the smell of a debt the way other men knew the smell of their mothers' cooking.

"You're sitting in my chair."

He didn't look up. He knew the voice the way he knew his own pulse — Ilya Voss, who had shared his route for six years, shared his fire for four, and once pulled an arrow out of Davor's shoulder with a fishhook and a prayer neither of them believed in anymore.

"I didn't see your name on it," Davor said.

"No," Ilya said. "You wouldn't have."

Davor looked up then. Ilya hadn't changed much. Same long face, same gray eyes that always looked like they were doing arithmetic on you and finding you short. But there were new lines around the mouth, deep ones, carved by something that wasn't laughter. And he wore the under-court's colors now — not the guild's gray-and-gold but a black sash shot through with silver thread that moved when it shouldn't have, rippling against the wind instead of with it.

"You took the court's badge," Davor said.

"Somebody has to keep the books honest." Ilya sat down across from him without asking, which was also the way he used to do things. "You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping rough."

"You've been sleeping guilty. There's a difference. One of them you can fix with a roof."

* * *

They had been partners the way two oxen are partners — yoked, not friends, at least at first. The under-court farmed out its collections to men who could walk the mountain roads without flinching at what lived in them, and Davor and Ilya had walked those roads together through three winters and one war that never got declared. They collected years from the old, luck from gamblers, memories from the desperate, and once — only once, and Ilya still didn't know — a name.

It was good work if you didn't think about it. That was the first rule of collecting: don't think. The debt existed before you did; you were just weather moving across a landscape of obligations. You knocked, you read the terms aloud, you took what was owed. What the debtor felt about it was between them and the contract.

Davor had been good at not thinking for a long time. Then came the Vell child.

* * *

Her name was Mira. She was seven. Her grandmother had pawned the girl's first word — *mama*, naturally, the most valuable single syllable in any language — to buy medicine through a hard winter. The medicine worked. The grandmother died anyway in the spring, which is the sort of joke the ledgers love to play, because interest doesn't care whether you live or die; it only cares that time passes.

By the time the debt reached Davor's route, the compounded term said: one first word, plus penalties, plus a substitution clause if the original asset was unavailable. The original asset was unavailable. The girl had never learned to say it. She was seven years old and she did not know her mother's name — knew her face, knew her hands, knew the lullaby hummed behind the missing word, but the word itself slid off her tongue like water off glass. She'd stand in front of her mother's door mouthing sounds that wouldn't come.

The substitution clause let the collector take anything of equal value. A memory of comparable weight. A year of life. A talent.

Davor stood in that doorway with the writ in his hand and looked at the child, who was chewing a piece of straw and looking back at him with the flat, unimpressed stare of children everywhere, and he found that he could not think, which was the job, and he could not not-think either, which was the problem.

"She can't pay," he told Ilya later, at their fire.

"Then she defaults, and the default terms apply."

"The default terms are worse."

"They're always worse. That's what makes them terms." Ilya turned the spit. "You want me to take this one?"

And here was the thing Davor had never told anyone, the thing that had been sitting in his chest for two years like a swallowed stone: he had almost said yes. He had opened his mouth to hand over the writ, because Ilya was better at the not-thinking, because Ilya's hands didn't shake, because it would be easier — and then the girl had come to the door of her house behind them and offered them the straw she was chewing, solemnly, the way you offer hospitality to strangers, because her grandmother had raised her right even if the ledgers had raised her wrong.

She'd held out a chewed piece of straw to two grown men who'd come to take the voice out of her mouth.

Davor had taken the writ back and torn it. Not defied it, not appealed it — torn it, physically, which every collector knows is the one unforgivable act, because the paper isn't paper, it's the debt itself wearing a coat of ink, and when you tear the debt the debt tears you back. It had gone into his record. *Asset destroyed. Collector compromised.* They cashiered him in a room that smelled of cold iron, and the clerk who stamped his dismissal couldn't meet his eyes, and Ilya had stood in the corner and said nothing, which was the loudest thing anyone had ever said to him.

* * *

"You heard about the fish," Ilya said now, in the tavern.

"Half the street saw the fish."

"And the snow in harvest month. You've seen it."

"Snow in harvest month," Davor agreed.

Ilya leaned forward. Up close, Davor could see that the silver thread in the black sash wasn't thread at all but writing, tiny script flowing through the fabric like water through cloth, and it made his eyes hurt to try to read it. "Then hear me, Kestrel, because I'm saying this once and I'm saying it as the last friend you've got. Walk away. Out of the city. Over the passes, past where the roads have names. The gods woke up hungry and everything the guild ever sold is coming due, all of it, every bottle and every bargain, and when the court finishes its collections it will go looking for loose assets. Men who tore writs. Men whose names are flagged in the margin ledger in red ink that doesn't dry."

"I tore one writ. Two years ago."

"The margin remembers." Ilya tapped the table, twice, and where his finger struck, frost bloomed and died. "You think they cashiered you? That was mercy. That was the court deciding you were worth more walking around than rendered down. That arithmetic changes when the gods need stock. Winter is coming out of season, Davor. Do you understand what that means? The gods stopped being paid in worship three hundred years ago, and now they're taking payment directly, and they have three centuries of arrears to work through, and they will work through it alphabetically if they have to. Your name is in the middle of that list. K comes early."

Davor drank his barley-flavored water. Outside, someone laughed too loudly, and somewhere further off a dog started barking at nothing, which dogs had been doing a lot lately.

"Why come tell me?" he asked. "If I'm flagged, if I'm stock, why warn the stock?"

Ilya was quiet for a moment. In the quiet, Davor watched him decide something — watched the decision move across his face like weather, the same way it had moved across his face outside the Vell house two years ago, the day Davor tore the writ and Ilya said nothing and walked away first.

"Because I'm the one who filed the flag," Ilya said finally. "Standard procedure. Compromised collector, name entered in the margin for review. I wrote your name into that book myself, and I have regretted my handwriting every day since." He stood, dropping a coin on the table — more than the meal cost, Davor noticed, more than Davor owned. "Walk away tonight. Don't go near the Thorn. Whatever shelter you think you'll find there, it's a trap with candles in the windows. The guild's founding debt comes due this winter, and the court means to collect it whole, and anyone standing inside those walls when the collectors arrive gets itemized."

"Ilya."

"Don't." 

"The child. Mira Vell. Is she—"

"On the list?" Ilya paused at the tavern door, his hand on the frame, and for a moment the silver script in his sash stilled, as if even it was listening. "Everyone's on the list. That's what a list is." And then he was gone into the dark, and the cold came in behind him like a debt arriving.

* * *

Davor sat with his eleven coins and his bent nail and thought about walking away.

It would be easy. East over the passes, where the winter was only ordinary winter, where the gods were names in stories and nobody had seen a miracle except in a fever dream. He had legs. He had — barely — money. He had the particular survival skill of a man who knows exactly what he's worth on an open market, which is to say he knew how to become worthless quickly: drink, dirt, bad company. A man can vanish into himself if he digs deep enough.

He thought about Mira Vell holding out her chewed straw.

That was the trouble with refusing a debt. Everyone thinks the refusal frees you. It doesn't. The debt stays with you; it just changes shape. He had torn a writ two years ago and ever since he had been carrying the default — not the court's default, his own. Every morning since, some small voice in him had asked what happened to the girl, and every morning he had answered *she defaulted, the terms applied,* and every morning the answer tasted like the bottom of a well.

He should have gone back. He'd meant to go back. He'd told himself a hundred times he'd find a way to buy out her substitution clause quietly, off the books, the way you feed a stray — and then the seasons had turned and the road had gotten long and the shame had gotten heavy, and shame is excellent at making you late.

The snow was falling harder now. Harvest month, and the flakes came down fat and unhurried, settling on the windowsill in little drifts that glowed orange under the tavern lamp, and each one that landed whispered something just below hearing. He listened. He wished he hadn't. It sounded like tallying.

Eleven coins and a bent nail. Against that: one flagged name, one torn writ, one child on a list, and a city full of people who had bought miracles on credit and were about to learn what credit costs when the creditor is a god with three hundred years of hunger.

He put the coins away.

Then he picked up his pack, and left the bent nail on the table — payment, or a tip, or a promise, he honestly couldn't have said which — and went out into the falling snow, and turned his feet not east toward the passes but toward the smell of candlewax and old paper and something sweet going soft.

Toward the Thorn.

Because here was the thing Ilya had gotten wrong, the thing the whole under-court had gotten wrong, the thing the entire architecture of debt rested on and would one day choke on: you cannot collect a man who has already given himself away. The court could itemize his body, flag his name, render down his years. But the part of him that mattered had been spent two years ago in a doorway, torn up with a writ, given to a seven-year-old with a chewed straw — and what's given freely doesn't come due. It was the one transaction in all creation that no ledger could hold.

Let them send collectors. He'd been ruined already. Ruined men make poor collateral and worse prisoners, and best of all, they make excellent allies — because a man with nothing left to lose has finally, at long last, got something worth fighting with.

The snow thickened around him as he walked. Somewhere behind, a bell began to ring — harvest month bells, alarm bells, the sound of a city understanding all at once that winter had arrived eight weeks early and intended to stay. Ahead, the Gilded Thorn's windows burned gold through the white, warm and certain and doomed, and Davor Kestrel squared his shoulders, and went to offer it the only thing he had left.

His service. Unencumbered. Free and clear.

Behind him, unseen in the dark, a figure in a black sash followed at a distance — not following him, Davor would realize later, but watching the Thorn, counting its windows, marking its doors. Writing something down.

The snow kept falling. The list kept growing.
