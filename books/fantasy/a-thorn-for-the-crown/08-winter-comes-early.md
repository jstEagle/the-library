## 8. Winter Comes Early

The first snow fell on the twenty-third of Harvestmonth, which was wrong the way a ledger balancing to the wrong number is wrong — technically correct, entirely false.

Ulyana noticed it because she was at the window of her counting room, checking the light for candle purposes, and the light went strange. Not dim. *Whitened.* She looked up and the sky was shedding itself in slow fat flakes onto rooftops still strung with drying peppers, onto market stalls piled with apples, onto children who had run out into the street shrieking with delight before their mothers hauled them back inside with faces already going gray.

Snow in harvest month. Snow while the granaries were still open and the last barges were coming down the river heavy with grain that would now have nowhere dry to go.

She stood there long enough for the window glass to fog around her breath, doing the arithmetic she could not stop herself from doing: three days of snow like this and the river ices early. Five days and the outlying farms lose what's still standing. Seven days and the price of bread triples, and half this city starts praying to gods who haven't answered in three hundred years.

Except they had started answering. That was the problem.

* * *

By midmorning the Gilded Thorn's front room looked like a church that had learned commerce. The queue ran out the door and down the steps into the slush, and Vespera stood behind the counter with the serene expression of a woman watching interest compound.

"Borrowed luck," she said to a fishwife whose hands were blue with cold. "One week's worth. Standard rate."

"How much?"

"For you? Your mother's recipe for fever-wine. It's a good one. I can tell it's good by how tightly you're holding it."

Ulyana, at her desk in the corner, wrote the entry and hated her own neat hand as she did it. *One week borrowed luck against family recipe, fever-wine, irreplaceable.* The woman took the little brass token and pressed it to her forehead like a coin to a saint and went out into the snow, and Ulyana watched through the window as a cart that should have crushed her foot slid instead on the ice and missed by a finger's width, and the fishwife laughed, high and wild, and crossed herself to no god in particular and then, apparently thinking better of it, crossed herself toward the mountain.

"Business is good," Vespera observed, appearing at Ulyana's elbow without having visibly moved.

"Business is obscene."

"Those are not mutually exclusive." Vespera's eyes were on the queue, cataloguing it the way Ulyana catalogued numbers. "Desperate people pay honestly. It's the comfortable ones who haggle. You'll want to double-check my valuations tonight; I'm being generous, and generosity is a rounding error we don't carry."

"You raised the rate twice since dawn."

"The market raised the rate. I merely read it aloud." Vespera smoothed her sleeve. Outside, another flurry came slanting down between the buildings, thick enough to blur the far side of the square. "Three hundred years the gods slept, Ulyana, and we built something durable in the quiet. Now they've woken up cranky and generous at once, and every prayer they answer makes people need us more. Do you know why? Because a god's miracle is a loan too. Nobody reads those terms either."

"What terms?" Ulyana asked. "What do the gods charge?"

Vespera smiled the smile she used on customers whose debts were about to come due. "Ask the rain of fish."

* * *

Everyone in Saltmarket still talked about the rain of fish. It had happened nine days ago, before the snow, when an old man named Corvo had knelt in the gutter outside his ruined stall and prayed — loudly, drunkenly, hopelessly — for his livelihood back. And the sky over Saltmarket had opened and rained silver fish for a full quarter hour, live fish, flopping fish, fish that filled his barrels to bursting while the street cheered and wept and filled its hats.

Corvo was rich now. Corvo was also, Ulyana had learned from a customer's gossip, unable to sleep. He said the fish whispered. He said they all had the same face. He said that when he closed his eyes he could feel a great patience leaning over him like a creditor at a counter, waiting.

The gods were answering prayers again. But they had been asleep for three centuries, and anyone who has ever woken a debtor after three hundred years knows what comes first: not forgiveness. Reckoning.

* * *

The second cruelty arrived on the fourth day of snow, and this one Ulyana saw herself.

A dyer named Anselm had a daughter with lung-fever, and he had spent everything, and so he did what desperate men in this city had done for generations: he came to the Gilded Thorn and bought bottled luck, and when that failed, borrowed courage, and when that failed, he went out into the square and knelt in the snow where everyone could see him and prayed to whatever was listening.

Something listened.

The fever broke. Ulyana watched it happen — or rather she watched the aftermath, because the girl walked out of the house on the corner that evening, pink-cheeked, laughing, carrying a tray of cups to the neighbors as if to prove it. The whole street celebrated. Anselm wept into his dye-stained apron and shouted thanks at the sky until his voice cracked.

And then, two streets over, the well water turned to ink.

Not poisoned — the city alchemist tested it, later, frantically — but *changed*, black and cold and smelling faintly of deep stone, undrinkable, unboilable, unfilterable. Forty households lost their water in the same hour a child was healed. No pattern. No reason anyone could find. Just an entry somewhere in some cosmic ledger: *one miracle disbursed; offsetting debit applied.*

"That's not mercy," Ulyana said that night. She and Vespera were alone in the front room, the lamps low, the snow ticking against the shutters. "That's bookkeeping. Someone is moving numbers around."

"Of course they are." Vespera was counting the day's intake — memories in wax-sealed bottles, years in stoppered vials, the honest lie in its lead box, humming faintly, as always. "What did you think a miracle was? Something from nothing? Nothing is the one thing even gods can't make. Every gift is a transfer. Every answered prayer is someone else's unanswered one, dressed up in light." She sealed the strongbox. "The old gods kept accounts too, dear. Ours are just tidier."

"And the winter? Is that a transfer too?"

For just a moment — less than a moment — Vespera's hands went still on the strongbox lid. Then they resumed, smooth as ever. "The winter is weather. Even gods get those wrong."

But Ulyana had spent two years learning to read Vespera the way she read ledgers, and she knew what a hesitation looked like. She filed it. That was what she did. She filed things, and waited, and watched the interest grow.

* * *

On the sixth day the granary district burned.

No one ever proved it was set. The official story was a lamp, spilled oil, bad luck. But Ulyana stood in the crowd at the cordon and watched half the city's grain go up in orange ruin under the falling snow, and she thought about the fishwife's fever-wine recipe, and the dyer's ink-black well, and she understood with a nausea that rose up her throat like bile: *the gods aren't answering prayers cruelly. They're answering them accurately.* People were asking to be saved, and salvation was being issued at cost, and the cost was being billed to everyone who hadn't asked.

The panic that followed the fire was worse than the fire. By nightfall the queue outside the Thorn stretched past the fountain. They wanted luck. They wanted warmth. They wanted bottled summers and borrowed strength and anything, *anything* that wasn't prayer, because prayer had stopped being safe.

"We should close," Ulyana said. She found Vespera upstairs, in the map room, studying charts of the mountain passes by lamplight. "Half these people can't pay what they're asking. We're selling drowning men rope at interest."

"They can pay," Vespera said mildly. "People can always pay. They simply don't yet know what they have that we want." She didn't look up from the maps. "Besides. If we close, they'll pray instead. Would you rather sell a grandmother a jar of last summer, or watch her kneel in the snow and have the gods take her granddaughter's name to balance the entry?"

"That's blackmail dressed as kindness."

"Yes." Vespera looked up then, and her face was calm, and tired, and very old in the lamplight. "Most of civilization is. The difference is that we print our rates on the door." She rolled the map closed with a snap. "You may take a portion of tonight's proceeds to the relief fund at the Temple Steps, if it salves you. Book it as charity. Charity is deductible."

"I don't want it to be deductible. I want it to be—"

"To be what? Clean?" For a heartbeat something moved behind Vespera's eyes — not anger. Recognition. A woman looking at her younger self across thirty years and a counter. "Nothing in this trade is clean, Ulyana. The best we manage is *honest*. Write your books straight, keep your rates posted, never sell a person something they don't understand. That's the whole of our virtue, such as it is." She turned back to the dead hearth. "Now go count the till. And Ulyana — the entry for the relief fund goes under losses, not gifts. We are not sentimental here."

* * *

But the thing that kept Ulyana awake that night was none of it — not the fire, not the queue, not even Vespera's maps of the mountain passes, which she turned over and over in her mind like a coin she'd caught out of the corner of her eye and couldn't put down.

It was a small discrepancy. Two lines, actually, in the oldest ledger — the one older than the guild, the one with her handwriting in it that she had never written. She had taken to keeping it locked in her desk, and taking it out at night when the house slept, reading it the way you'd probe a sore tooth.

Tonight, by candle, she found that the page had changed.

Where before there had been columns of pawned souls — dates, names, terms, the court beneath the mountain noted in a spidery script — there was now, at the bottom of the page, a fresh line, in fresh ink, though no pen had touched it:

*Interest accruing. Winter is the invoice.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in handwriting that made her stomach drop because it was hers, unmistakably hers, down to the way she crossed her sevens:

*Balance due at midwinter.*

She sat very still and let the candle burn and did not look at the window, where the snow came down without sound, without wind, without end — patient, orderly, falling like figures into a column.

Outside, somewhere in the white dark, a bell began to ring. Then a second bell. Then a dozen, all across the sleeping city, ringing no alarm she knew — a rhythm she felt in her teeth rather than heard, old and slow, like knocking on a door from underneath.

And in the wall behind her, faint but distinct, came a sound the vaults made only when inventory woke up unhappy: glass, singing softly, all down the shelves.

Someone — or something — was scratching at the cellar door.
