## 9. The God's Forgotten Daughter

The bell above the vault door rang at the hour when no honest person was awake and no dishonest one was working.

Ulyana knew this because she had memorized the guild's rhythms the way other people memorized prayers — which was to say, better than the people who claimed to believe in them. The Gilded Thorn kept three shifts: Vespera's, which ended at midnight; the porters', which began before dawn; and the dead hours between, when the vaults were supposed to empty themselves of everything but inventory. Ulyana worked all three, because ledgers did not sleep and neither, lately, did she.

The prayer candles were still flickering. They had been flickering for six days now, unlit, guttering with a light that had no source she could find in any book. She had stopped writing it down in the margin of the day-ledger. Some things you recorded; some things you survived.

The bell rang again. Twice, then a pause, then once — not the pattern any member used, not the triple ring that meant a customer with coin or the long scrape that meant someone dragging something heavy to pawn. It was the ring of someone who didn't know there was a right way.

Ulyana set down her pen. She considered, briefly, the iron poker by the hearth, and then considered what Vespera would say about damaged goods, and took the poker anyway.

The vault stair was cold under her bare feet. She had meant to put on shoes. She hadn't. There was a version of herself, three years ago, who would have stood at the top of those stairs and shouted for help. That girl had learned arithmetic since, and arithmetic said: one intruder, two exits from the lower vaults, zero guards between midnight and the porter's bell. Whatever came up those stairs would come up to meet her or it wouldn't come up at all.

She rounded the last shelf of bottled memories — *a first kiss, lightly used*; *the smell of your mother's kitchen, guaranteed authentic* — and found a child crouched in the aisle like a bird that had flown through a window and couldn't find the glass again.

She was perhaps eleven, perhaps fourteen; some faces refused the question. Thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a coat cut for someone twice her size, dark hair cropped short and unevenly, as if she'd done it herself with a knife and a grudge. Her eyes were the problem. Ulyana had catalogued enough impossible things to know impossible eyes when she saw them: they were grey the way deep water is grey, and they held light that wasn't in the room.

"Don't," the girl said. Her voice cracked on it. "Don't scream. I'll go."

"You'll go," Ulyana agreed. "After you tell me how you got past the door."

"The door let me in." The girl said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "It does that. Sometimes."

Doors did not do that. Doors in the Gilded Thorn especially did not do that; Vespera had them warded twice over and paid for both wardings. Ulyana looked at the girl's hands — small, grimy, knuckles split from cold — and then at the shelf beside her, where a jar of last summer sat glowing faintly gold, and where, she now noticed, every bottle within arm's reach had turned to face the child like flowers finding sun.

"Who are you?" Ulyana asked.

"Nobody."

"Nobody doesn't make doors open. I've read the contracts. Nobody is precisely what doors are built against."

The girl's jaw set. Under the grime and the defiance there was something else, something Ulyana recognized with an unwelcome lurch: the particular stillness of a person deciding whether to run. Ulyana knew that stillness from the inside. She had worn it for a year after leaving Mistress Havel's cottage, flinching at footsteps, sleeping with the stolen grimoire under her cheek like a stone she could bite.

"I'm looking for something," the girl said finally. "It's mine. It was taken."

"A great deal of what's in here was taken," Ulyana said. "We prefer the word *acquired*. What is it?"

The girl looked down the aisle, at the shelves of memories in their bottles, each labeled in Vespera's elegant hand. Her impossible eyes moved across them the way a starving person looks through a baker's window.

"My father," she said. "A memory of my father. He sold—" Her voice snagged. "He traded our names. Mine and my brothers'. For something. Nobody will tell me what. And when he traded the names he lost—" she made a frustrated gesture at her own head— "*this*. The part that remembers us. He walks past me in the street now. He smiles because I look like someone worth smiling at, but he doesn't know why." She swallowed. "There's supposed to be a memory in here. Of him holding me. Before. I heard it from a man who sells secrets behind the fish market. He said the Thorn had it. Bottle number—"

She stopped. Looked at the shelf. Found it.

Ulyana watched her find it, and felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath her professional composure.

Bottle 4471. *A father's joy, unnamed child, circa the godswar's end. Provenance uncertain.* It had come into the guild's keeping thirty years ago, per the ledger, in a lot purchased from a temple being deconsecrated. It had never sold. Bottled joy almost always sold. This one had sat on its shelf through three inventory audits while everything around it turned over, and Ulyana had noted its stagnancy more than once as an anomaly without explanation.

Now she had the explanation, standing in an oversized coat with light in her eyes that wasn't in the room.

"You're his daughter," Ulyana said quietly. "You're a god's daughter."

"I'm *nobody's* daughter," the girl said, too fast, too loud. "That's the whole point. He gave away the part of himself that knew me. The name was the hook the memory hung on. Take the name out and everything falls off it. Everything." Her breath shuddered. "I just want to see it. Once. I want to know I was real before I was a debt."

And there it was. Ulyana stood in the cold aisle of the Gilded Thorn, holding an iron poker she no longer had any intention of using, and understood that she was going to be in trouble.

Because the rule of the guild was simple and total: *everything is owed.* Nothing left these shelves without payment. Vespera had said it to Ulyana on her first day, tapping the ledger with one lacquered nail: *The moment we give anything away, girl, we become a charity, and charities die, and the people who depended on them die faster.* Ulyana believed in the rule. The rule had fed her, clothed her, given her warm rooms and work she was good at and a life built on careful books instead of a witch's whims. The rule was fair. Fairness mattered to her more than almost anything.

But the rule required payment, and Ulyana looked at this child — this ragged, furious, impossible-eyed child who wanted nothing but proof of having been loved — and ran the numbers, and the numbers came back wrong. There was nothing in the girl's pockets. There was nothing the girl could earn in a year of honest labor that would cover even the handling fee on a bottled memory of a god.

Which meant that by the books, this child could stand in this aisle until she froze, and the answer would still be no.

"Give me your hand," Ulyana said.

The girl tensed. "Why?"

"Because if you're going to steal from the Gilded Thorn, you should know what stealing costs, and I'm the only one here who can show you the entry." She kept her voice level, auditor-flat. "I'm not going to hurt you. I keep the ledgers. Hurting people isn't in them."

Slowly, suspiciously, the girl extended one small hand. Ulyana took it — cold fingers, a recent burn scar across the palm, the calluses of someone who'd been climbing things she shouldn't — and turned it palm-up, and set the bottle in it.

Bottle 4471. *A father's joy.*

"What—" the girl started.

"Hold it. Don't uncork it. If you uncork it in here, the wardings eat the memory and you'll have risked everything for a mouthful of nothing. You take it somewhere quiet, somewhere the walls don't have opinions, and you open it there, and it pours into you like warm rain. Understand?"

The girl stared at her. At the bottle. Back at her. "This is a trick."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "It's a trick I'm playing on myself. Hold still."

She was already composing the entry in her head, because if you were going to commit a crime against the only institution that had ever been kind to you, you should at least do the paperwork. *Item 4471, removed by U. Okonkwo, keeper of ledgers, against no consideration received. Reason: pending.* She'd figure out *pending* later. She had a talent for making later arrive.

The girl's fingers closed around the glass. Something happened to the light in the aisle — the bottles dimmed slightly, as if a lamp had been carried out of a dark room, and the jar of last summer sighed like a sleeper turning over.

"It's warm," the girl whispered. "It's warm like—"

Like a hand on your head, Ulyana thought. Like someone saying your name kindly. She remembered warmth like that from exactly one place and one person, and that person had burned their bridges behind her with the rest of the cottage, so she said only: "Go. Out the way you came. And don't come back — if Vespera catches you, no ledger trick I know will save either of us."

The girl got three steps toward the door.

Then the prayer candle on the wall — unlit, flickering, six days flickering — went out. All of them went out, down the whole aisle, a wave of darkness rolling toward the vault door, and in the sudden black the girl's eyes glowed like deep water catching moonlight, and a voice that was not entirely the girl's voice, or perhaps was entirely the girl's voice and had never been allowed to sound like itself, said:

"*Sable.*"

The name hung in the air. The girl went rigid.

"That's not—" she whispered. "That's not my name anymore. He sold it."

"He sold it," Ulyana said slowly, her mind moving fast now, the old hedge-witch training waking up in her blood like a dog hearing a whistle, "to someone. And whoever holds it just used it. Through you. Which means the contract is *active*, which means—" she stopped. Started over. "Child. When did the old gods wake up?"

"Six days ago." The girl's voice was very small now. "Everyone says so. The fish-rain. The man in Copperlane whose shadow started obeying someone else. My da—" she caught herself— "the man who used to be my father, he went to the shrine for the first time in thirty years and the statue opened its eyes."

Six days. Six days of flickering candles. The same night the candles began, the same night the ledgers had begun showing anomalies Ulyana had filed and re-filed and told herself were transcription errors — dividends posting themselves, debts accruing interest in accounts that had been dormant for decades, entries in a hand older than the guild.

Every miracle the Gilded Thorn had ever sold was coming due. And now, standing in the dark with a god's forgotten daughter clutching a stolen memory, Ulyana understood that the awakening had a shape, a direction, a *purpose* — and that purpose had something to do with the oldest ledger, the one written in her own handwriting she had never written.

"Come with me," she said.

"No. You said go—"

"I said go, and now I'm saying stay, and the second order supersedes the first because I'm the one holding the ledger pen. Come with me. Now. Quietly."

* *

She hid her in the counting-room, in the windowless inner office where Ulyana slept during the busy seasons, with the false wall behind the shelving that Vespera believed was structural and Ulyana knew was not, because she had built it herself two winters ago for reasons she'd told herself were about securing documents and now recognized, with uncomfortable honesty, had been about having somewhere to hide.

She brought bread, cheese, the good tea she hoarded. She brought lamp oil and a second blanket. She brought, after a moment's internal argument with herself, the day-ledger, and sat cross-legged on the floor outside the false wall with the candle shielded, and talked to the gap in the shelving while she worked.

"His name," the girl said from the darkness. "My father's. Do you know it? Properly? Not the shrine-name everyone uses — his real one."

"Minor gods keep their true names close," Ulyana said. "Even after they've sold everything else. A true name is the last thing anyone trades, because it's the only collateral that can't be forged." She wrote, crossed out, wrote again. "What is he god *of*?"

"Small things." A pause. "Doorways that stick. Lost buttons. Finding things people gave up on. He used to say the big gods got the harvests and the wars and he got whatever fell between the cracks, and he'd laugh like that was the best bargain in creation." Another pause, longer. "Then the silence came. Three hundred years ago. And the prayers stopped being answered and the offerings dried up and he got smaller and smaller and I suppose eventually small wasn't small enough."

"The silence ended six days ago," Ulyana said. "Gods are answering prayers again. Cruelly, mostly. The city's half-mad with it." She tapped her pen. "Your father woke up with the rest. And the first thing a debtor does on waking is check what he still owes."

"And what he still *has*," the girl said bitterly, "is a memory in a bottle in a thieves' pantry."

"Guild," Ulyana corrected automatically. "We're a guild. We pay taxes. Occasionally."

From the dark, unexpectedly, a laugh — short, cracked, real. Ulyana found herself smiling at the shelving like a fool, and stopped.

"Sable," she said. "Is that truly your name? Before?"

Silence. Then: "Sable. Yes. He called me Sable because I was born at dusk and he said dusk-colored children belonged to no one's daylight and no one's night." The voice wavered. "I haven't heard it said aloud in four years. Hearing it felt like — like putting on a coat that fits."

Ulyana looked down at her ledger, at the neat columns, at the entry she had drafted for the theft: *pending, pending, pending.* She thought about names. She thought about the page in the oldest ledger, in her own handwriting, dated before her birth, ink she had never touched. She thought about Mistress Havel's kitchen, and the apprenticeship papers she'd signed at seven years old without being able to read them, and the way the witch had smiled and called her *my investment.*

Two orphans of other people's bargains, sitting on either side of a false wall.

"Sable," she said. "The memory you're carrying. When you open it — and you should, tonight, somewhere safe, because bottled joy sours if it sits uncorked too near grief — when you open it, listen to what he says. Not to you. Around you. Gods talk in their sleep, and yours has been asleep a long time, and if he woke up owing debts, he may have said something in that memory about *to whom*."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because," Ulyana said, and her pen had gone still on the page, and her heart had gone very quiet and very cold in the way it did when the numbers finally lined up into something terrible, "because six days ago every dormant account in the Gilded Thorn's books woke up screaming, including one with my name on it that I never opened. And I think your father's debts and my debts may have been filed in the same drawer."

From the darkness, the girl's voice, small and steady: "The court under the mountain."

Ulyana's pen slipped. "What did you say?"

"The secret-seller behind the fish market. He talks about it when he's drunk. Says the old gods didn't vanish three hundred years ago — they moved. Went under the mountain with all their paperwork and all their creditors, and they've been down there ever since, sleeping off the silence. And now they're awake." A breath. "And collecting."

Outside, far above, the prayer candles flickered back to life, one by one, down the aisles of the vaults — and this time Ulyana could have sworn they flickered in sequence, like a knock. Like a count. Like something patient taking attendance.

She closed the ledger. She sat in the dark with her back against the false wall, listening to a god's forgotten daughter breathe on one side and the guild's careful silence on the other, and began, methodically, to plan a burglary.

Not of the vaults. She'd already committed that crime, and it was the lesser one.

Of the mountain.
