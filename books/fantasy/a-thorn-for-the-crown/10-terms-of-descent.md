## 10. Terms of Descent

The maps came out at midnight, because that was when the vault was coldest and the candles burned slowest and no one could pretend this was anything other than what it was: theft.

Ulyana spread them across the long counting table in the guild's back room — three of them, borrowed from Davor's old kit without his permission, which she suspected was the only way anyone ever got anything from Davor Kestrel. They were beautiful things and terrible things at once. Inked on salt-cured hide, annotated in a hand she now recognized as his: cramped, impatient, with little crosses marking every place he'd nearly died.

"You went through my kit," he said from the doorway.

"You were asleep in a chair in the common room. You sleep like a man who expects to be woken by violence, so I chose not to provide it."

He grunted and came to stand over the maps, arms crossed, looking at them the way a man looks at an old scar. "These are collector's routes. Not tourist maps."

"I know what they are." She tapped the largest one, where the mountain's roots were drawn in cross-section like a dissected animal. Tunnels branching off tunnels, each labeled in his hand with tolls, hazards, and — her favorite touch — small marginal notes in a different ink, added later. *Don't trust the third fork. The wights lie about distances. Bring more light than you think.*

"Then you know," he said, "that nobody goes down there for free."

"Everything is owed," Ulyana said. "Yes. It's the first rule of the guild. It's apparently also the first rule of hell."

"It's not hell."

"It's a court beneath a mountain where souls are held as collateral against debts incurred before my grandmother drew breath. I'm comfortable calling it whatever gets me angriest fastest."

Sable, curled in the corner chair with her knees drawn up and a blanket around her shoulders like a small gray ghost, spoke without lifting her head. "You're both doing the thing where you argue instead of plan."

"We *are* planning," Ulyana said.

"You're planning out loud at each other." The girl finally looked up. She had been living in the vaults for six days now, hidden between the shelf of bottled memories and the jar of last summer, and she had lost some of the feral edge she'd arrived with but none of the watchfulness. "My father used to do that. Argue at people instead of talking to them. Look where it got him." She gestured vaguely downward, encompassing the entire mountain, the court, and several centuries of bad decisions.

Ulyana felt that one land somewhere under her ribs. She let it sit there and turned back to the map.

The route was simple to describe and impossible to do. Down through the salt tunnels under the eastern quarries — two days' walk if the lamps behaved. Past the toll-wights at the First Gate, who took payment in years and were famously flexible about whose years. Through the Market of Small Mercies, where prayers changed hands like counterfeit coin. And then into the archive itself, the frozen cathedral where petitions hung like icicles and souls sat in glass reliquaries on shelves organized, according to Davor, by nothing so kind as alphabetical order.

"Debt seniority," Davor said. "Oldest claims deepest in. Yours—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Yours will be far down."

"Of course it will." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. Three hundred years of compounding interest. Her soul wasn't just pawned; it had *appreciated*. Somewhere below the mountain, in a glass case, the flame that was Ulyana Okonkwo had been quietly growing rarer and more valuable every year she stayed alive, the way wine does, the way grudges do.

"So we go down," Sable said. "We find your fire. We take it. We walk out."

"And the guards?" Ulyana asked.

"What guards?"

"The bailiff-spirits. The wardens. Whatever passes for law down there when the gods themselves are the creditors."

Sable considered this with the gravity of a child who had recently learned the world contained paperwork. "I'll set something on fire."

"You will not set anything on—"

"I'm a god's daughter. Fire is practically a family heirloom."

* * *

That was when the door opened and Vespera came in, and the temperature of the room changed the way it always did — not colder, exactly, but more *accounted for*. The guildmother moved through the world like an entry in her own ledger: precise, weighted, expecting balance.

She looked at the maps. At Davor. At Sable, whom she did not acknowledge, because officially the girl did not exist and unofficially she was a liability the guildmother had not yet priced.

"An interesting evening's reading," Vespera said.

"We weren't hiding it," Ulyana said, which was true, though they hadn't been advertising it either, and they both knew the difference mattered to Vespera the way the difference between a loan and a gift mattered to a moneylender.

"No," Vespera agreed pleasantly. "Hiding would have required effort, and you wanted me to find it. You want me to approve it." She drifted along the table, fingertips trailing over the salt-hide, reading Davor's marginalia upside down with the ease of a woman who had spent forty years reading other people's handwriting for profit. "A descent into the under-court. An unauthorized retrieval of a soul the guild holds secondary claim on — don't look surprised, Ulyana, you read the page yourself; you know how lien priority works. A stolen grimoire. A fugitive enforcer. And a child of divine blood we are sheltering without charging her board." She paused at the largest map. "This is either the bravest or the stupidest venture ever planned at this table, and I have planned ventures at this table for longer than you've been alive."

"Which is it?" Davor asked.

"Both. That's usually how the profitable ones start." Vespera straightened. "Very well. The guild will fund the descent. Provisions, lamps, salt-ward charms, the good rope. In exchange, the Gilded Thorn takes a cut."

Ulyana's hands went flat on the table. "A cut of what?"

"Of whatever you bring back."

"There is nothing to bring back but me. My soul isn't divisible, guildmother. There's no percentage interest available. I've checked the arithmetic."

Vespera smiled, and it was the smile she wore when a customer tried to renegotiate terms — patient, faintly amused, absolutely immovable. "Souls aren't divisible. But *claims* are. If you succeed, you'll walk out of that court holding a release document. A signed discharge, witnessed by the court itself, stating that Ulyana Okonkwo owes nothing to anyone beneath or above the earth." Her finger came down on the table like a stamp on wax. "That document has value. Enormous value. A clean soul-deed, freely transferable, is worth more than everything in our vaults combined. The guild takes thirty percent."

"Thirty percent of *me*."

"Thirty percent of the paper. Which is you. Yes." Vespera said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "You misunderstand if you think this is greed, child. This is survival. The guild's founding debt runs to that same court. Every year the debt stands, the interest compounds, and every year the court grows stronger and the guild grows smaller. A discharge document in our possession — even partial — gives us leverage. Leverage is the only currency the under-court respects. You of all people should understand that. You keep our books."

"I keep your books," Ulyana said slowly, "and here is what they say." She pulled the current ledger toward her, opened it to the marked page, and turned it around. "The guild's founding debt, principal and accrued interest, calculated last night while the rest of you slept. Read it."

Vespera read it. Ulyana watched her face do something very small and very fast — a flicker, gone in half a heartbeat, but Ulyana had spent years learning to catch exactly those flickers, because catching them was the job.

"That number is wrong," Vespera said.

"The number is right. You just didn't know it was that large." Ulyana kept her voice level, almost gentle, which was the cruelest tone available to her. "The debt doubles again in eleven months. At that rate, within four years, the guild's total assets cannot cover it. You aren't negotiating for leverage, guildmother. You're negotiating for time. And I am offering you the only exit in existence, and you're asking me to sell you part of the door."

Silence. Sable had stopped breathing theatrically in the corner. Even Davor had gone still, in the way of men watching two blades pass close to someone's throat.

"The number could be restructured," Vespera said at last, quieter.

"Yes. That is literally what I am proposing. One audit, one discharge, one precedent — and then the court can't hold the guild's founding debt either, because the whole rotten book comes apart at once. You taught me that. Lesson one, my first week behind this desk: *a flaw in one entry is a flaw in the ledger.*"

Something moved behind Vespera's eyes — not warmth, Ulyana thought, nothing so simple, but recognition. The look of a chess player realizing the opponent had learned her opening.

"Fifteen percent," Vespera said. "And the guild keeps the document until the founding debt is discharged. Then it burns. Full release. Yours entire."

"Five percent," Ulyana countered. "Held in trust, same terms, and the guild pays for the rope."

For a moment Ulyana genuinely believed Vespera might laugh. Instead the guildmother inclined her head a precise half-inch — the smallest concession she had ever seen her make — and said, "Done. Witnessed." She glanced at Davor. "You. You're the only one of these lunatics who's been past the First Gate and returned. You'll lead."

"I wasn't asking to."

"You weren't asked. Note the difference. It matters down there." Vespera turned for the door, then paused, her hand on the frame, and without turning around said: "The hedge-witch's contract made you collateral. My terms make you a partner. Try to remember, someday, which one of us gave you a choice." And she was gone, footsteps fading down the vault stair, leaving the candle flames leaning after her like they wanted to follow.

* * *

They worked until the fourth bell. Davor talked and Ulyana wrote, converting his memory into columns: distance, hazard, toll, contingency. It was, she realized somewhere past the second hour, the most intimate conversation she'd ever had with another person — not because of what he told her, but because of how he told it. Everything in costs. Everything in risks weighed against returns. He thought like a ledger. She'd never met anyone else who did.

"The First Gate tolls in years," he said. "Wights take them off you like coins from a purse. You feel lighter after. Most people don't notice what they've lost for weeks — a spring morning here, an afternoon in summer there. You can't choose which ones they take. Don't try. Trying is how they get greedy."

"How many years did you pay, last time?" Ulyana asked, pen poised.

His jaw worked. "Doesn't matter."

"Davor. I need the number. It goes in the column."

"Three days." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage, and looked at the map instead of at her. "First trip. Second trip was five days and a favor I haven't finished repaying. Third trip I walked out owing more than I carried in, and that's when Ilya started calling me a bad investment." A pause. "He was joking. Mostly."

Ulyana wrote *3–5 days, per crossing, plus variable* in the toll column and hated the neatness of her own handwriting.

"And if we refuse the toll?"

"Then we don't pass. Or we pass as payment." He tapped the map where the tunnel narrowed to a black thread. "There's a reason collectors travel alone, Ulyana. Every extra body is extra toll, and the wights can smell who's carrying the most. With you—" he glanced at her, quick and uncomfortable, then away "—with a soul already claimed by the court, you'll ring like a struck bell down there. Everything under that mountain will know exactly what you're worth and exactly where you are."

"Wonderful."

"It's also useful. Things that know what you're worth sometimes negotiate."

From the corner, Sable's voice, drowsy: "My father used to say the mountain remembers everyone who walks it. He said it like it was a nice thing."

"Was it?" Ulyana asked.

"He said it right before he traded away our names, so." The girl pulled the blanket higher. "Draw your own conclusions."

They fell quiet after that. The candles burned down. Ulyana finished the last column, totaled it, and stared at the sum: provisions, tolls, contingencies, the cost in years of three lives walking into the dark. It fit on one line. That was the horror of it. The entire price of stealing herself back — three days here, five days there, a god-daughter's fire, an enforcer's scars — compressed into figures that would fit in a margin.

"Ulyana." Davor's voice, low. He was looking at the oldest map, the one with the second-hand annotations, and his finger rested on a mark near the surface entrance she hadn't asked about yet. A small cross. The kind he put on places where he'd nearly died. "Before we go down. There's a stop we have to make first."

"Where?"

"The hedge-witch's cottage." He said it plainly, the way he said everything that hurt. "Your mistress filed the original contract. Court copies are worthless — I've seen the clerks shred them for amusement. If we're going to argue your case down there, we need the source document. Her seal, her hand, her errors." His finger lifted from the cross. "It's two days east. It's on the way."

Ulyana looked at the mark on the map and felt the candlelight go strange and thin around it. She had not been back since the night she'd run. Eight years. She had built a life out of careful books and borrowed safety precisely so she would never have to see that door again — and now the door stood between her and her own soul, and there was no route around it that didn't add a week to the descent.

"She'll be there," Ulyana said.

"Maybe."

"She'll be there. She always knows when someone's coming to settle accounts." Ulyana closed the ledger, squared its edges, and set the quill down in the groove worn by eight years of her own hand. Above the table, the prayer candle that no one had lit flickered once — twice — and steadied, burning without flame, casting a light that was somehow colder than the dark.

All three of them looked at it.

"Pack tonight," Davor said. "We leave before dawn, ahead of the snow."

Outside, high over the city, the first flakes of the out-of-season winter had begun to fall — and somewhere east, in a cottage at the edge of the moor, Ulyana would have bet every asset in the Gilded Thorn that a kettle was already going on the stove, and a woman who had sold her before she was born was setting out two cups, and waiting.
