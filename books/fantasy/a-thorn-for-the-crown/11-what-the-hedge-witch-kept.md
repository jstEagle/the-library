## 11. What the Hedge-Witch Kept

Ulyana told herself it was a supply run.

That was the lie she drafted at her desk the night before, in the tidy hand she used for anything she wanted to believe: *Travel requisition — one keeper, three days, provisions for two.* The second name was Davor's, because Vespera had stopped pretending not to know what Ulyana was planning, and because roads east of the city had grown strange since the winter started falling out of season. A man who had walked debt-routes through bandit country for fifteen years was a reasonable line item. She filed him under *security*. It was easier than filing him under anything else.

The real requisition was written smaller, on a page she kept folded against her ribs: the old ledger entry that had started all of this. Her own handwriting, from before she owned it.

*Okonkwo, Ulyana. One soul, pawned. Holder: Mother Ash of the Fen Road.*

She was going home to audit her childhood.

Davor met her at the Salt Gate an hour before dawn, dressed like a man who expected weather to have opinions about him. Snow had been coming down for two days — soft, wrong snow, falling in harvest month while apples still hung on the trees, and the market women had taken to wrapping their scales in wool as if the cold were something that could be bargained with. He carried a pack, a short sword he wore the way other men wore bad news, and a flask.

"You didn't have to come," Ulyana said.

"You didn't have to put it in writing," he said. "We going to stand here or walk? Standing is how you freeze."

The Fen Road ran east out of the city through country that got flatter and wetter the further they went, reed-beds stiff with frost, drainage ditches skinned with ice thin as communion glass. Ulyana had walked this road at eleven years old, clutching her mother's hand with one hand and a bundle of everything they owned with the other, delivered to Mother Ash because there had been four children and a failing farm and a hedge-witch who took apprentices in trade for debts forgiven. Her mother had cried. That was Ulyana's whole memory of the transaction: her mother crying, and Mother Ash standing in her doorway smelling of smoke and river mud, saying, *She'll do. Look at her counting the chickens already.*

She'd been counting the chickens. Even then, arithmetic was the only prayer she trusted.

"She'll know we're coming," Ulyana said, when they stopped at midday to share bread. "She always knew things. Which cow would sicken, which neighbor would cheat us, when the river would rise. I used to think it was magic. Now I think most of it was just paying attention, and calling the rest magic so people owed her."

"Where'd she get the grimoire?" Davor asked. He asked it mildly, the way he asked everything, but Ulyana had learned that mildness was where he kept his sharpest questions.

"Theft," she said. "Or inheritance, depending which of us you ask. It was hers before it was mine. She never let me touch the first hundred pages. I used to think that was discipline." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "It was probably bookkeeping."

They walked on. The sky did something low and yellow that Ulyana distrusted on principle, and by late afternoon the light had gone the color of old paper, and then they crested the last low rise above the fen, and Ulyana stopped walking, and did not start again for a long time.

The cottage was burned.

Not recently — the walls had fallen inward weeks ago, black beams lying like snapped ribs, the thatch reduced to a grey mat gone soft with snow. Weeds of frost furred every charred surface. Whatever fire had done this had been thorough and patient and had left the outhouse, the well, the fence, untouched. Only the house. Only the heart of the thing.

"Huh," said Davor, softly, in the voice of a man adding figures.

"That's it," Ulyana said. "That's your reaction? *Huh?*"

"My reaction is that fires don't burn like that." He was already moving down the slope, careful-footed on the frozen ruts. "Roof fires spread outward. Chimney fires go up. This went *in*. Every wall fell toward the hearthstone. See it?" He crouched at the ruin's edge without touching anything. "Somebody drew the fire in and shut the door behind it."

Ulyana stood in the snow and looked at the place where she had spent nine years of her life, and found, to her surprise, that she could not feel the thing she was supposed to feel. Grief was what the beat called for, she thought absurdly — grief, or rage, or the sweet clean relief of a prison burned down. Instead there was only a terrible inventory instinct running: *roof beams, gone; herb-drying rack, gone; the crooked table where I ate, gone; the corner shelf where the grimoire lived, gone; my pallet under the north window where I read by stolen candlelight, gone, gone, gone.* As if the cottage were a set of accounts and someone had struck through every line.

"She's gone ahead," Ulyana said.

"To the mountain."

"To collect interest." Ulyana heard her own voice go flat, which was what it did instead of shaking. "Three centuries the gods slept and the moment they wake, she moves. She didn't burn her own house down out of spite, Davor. She burned it down the way you cancel an account. Nothing left outstanding."

"Except," Davor said, and pointed.

At the center of the ruin, exactly where the hearth had been, sat a small square object, pristine, unmarked by ash or snow. From this distance it looked like a slate. Or a ledger.

Neither of them moved for a moment. Then Davor said, "Booby-trapped?"

"With her? Probably. But she wants me to take it. Traps are for people whose attention you want to punish. This is bait."

"Comforting," said Davor, and stepped over the threshold-stones anyway, because he was the kind of man who walked through doors first, and Ulyana followed because following him had begun to feel less like risk and more like policy.

The object was a contract. Her contract. Not a copy — the original, the true one, the document that had existed somewhere in the world's great filing system since before she could read it. It was written on skin. She knew it was skin the moment she touched it, because it had the faint animal warmth of skin, even here, even in the snow, and because the ink moved slightly when she wasn't looking directly at it, like veins under a sleeping person's skin.

Her name was at the top. Below it, terms. Below the terms, signatures: her mother's, laborious and pressed too hard; Mother Ash's, a smooth closed loop like a noose seen from above.

And below that, in the margin, in a different hand — small, dry, economical — a note she had not been meant to see:

*Third substitution. Papers hold. — V.*

The fen wind came across the ice and found the back of Ulyana's neck and she barely noticed it, because the letters were rearranging themselves in her mind into sentences she did not want to read.

*Substitution.* In guild language it had one meaning. An asset swapped for another asset of equivalent value, so the books stayed balanced while the underlying reality changed hands.

"Third," she said aloud.

Davor read the margin over her shoulder. He was quiet for a moment. "Vespera forged these papers," he said. "Your apprenticeship. Before you were born, maybe. Your mother didn't sell you to a witch — the witch bought a debt that never existed, and Vespera wrote the paperwork."

"Why would she—" And then Ulyana stopped, because the answer arrived the way answers always arrived for her: not as a feeling but as a number. Vespera had known about the soul-pawn for years. Had known, perhaps, since before Ulyana was born. Had guided her — recruited her, sheltered her, promoted her, kept her close among the ledgers where her own name waited in an older book — the way you keep an asset close that you intend to liquidate.

"I'm the substitute," Ulyana said. "I'm what she's been holding in reserve. When her own account comes due, she pays with me." She laughed once, and hated the sound. "I thought I ran away from one woman who owned me and built a life on careful books. I ran from one cage straight into the counting-house of another, and I *organized the shelves*."

Davor said nothing for a while. He was good at silence, better than anyone she'd known; he deployed it like shelter. He picked his way back to the fallen lintel and sat, and after a moment she sat beside him, the contract on her knees, warm as a living thing.

"Tell me what you're actually thinking," he said. "Not the ledger version."

"The ledger version is accurate."

"It always is. That's why I'm asking for the other one."

So Ulyana looked at the burned house and tried. "My mother signed this," she said. "Pressed too hard. She was ashamed. All those years I thought — I built my whole self out of the idea that she gave me away because I was worth giving. Four children and a failing farm. But look at the date." She turned the contract toward him. "This is dated eleven months before I was born. She couldn't have signed my soul away at birth. She signed a promise made on credit — a debt she was told already existed. Someone told her the debt was real. Someone made sure a girl named Okonkwo would be born owing." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, once, briefly, the way you crack a window in winter: enough to keep the air moving, not enough to freeze.

"And now?" Davor said.

"Now I'm thinking that everything Mother Ash kept — the grimoire, the contracts, nine years of my life measured out in chores and lessons and the flat of her hand — all of it was storage. She was the vault. Vespera was the bank. And I was the deposit." Ulyana folded the contract along its old creases, carefully, the way you handle any irreplaceable document. "And I'm thinking that both of them assumed I'd stay a line item forever. That I'd be legible. That I'd balance."

"And will you?"

"No," Ulyana said. "I've decided to be an error."

* * *

They searched the ruin until dark, by lamplight, methodically, because Ulyana could not leave a site unaudited and Davor understood that about her without needing it explained. Most of what they found was ash and rust and the fused green glass of a window that had melted like a sweet. But in the collapsed pantry, under a shelf that had protected it like a hand cupped over a candle, Davor found a strongbox with its lock burned open, and inside the strongbox, wrapped in oilcloth, Mother Ash's working papers: forty years of fen-country contracts, loans, charms sold on installment, babies warded, cows cured, all of it cross-referenced in a cramped hand that Ulyana recognized as the ancestor of her own.

And tucked inside the oldest folder, a map.

Not a map of anywhere. That was Ulyana's first reading, anyway: the geography made no sense, rivers running in circles, mountains drawn as spirals, distances marked in units she didn't know — until she saw the notation key at the bottom, in Mother Ash's hand, and realized the units were *years*, and the rivers were routes of payment, and the spiral mountain at the center of the page was labeled in a language older than ink, except for one word added later in plain Trade-Tongue:

*The court takes delivery at midwinter's end.*

"Midwinter's end," Davor repeated. "Same day your trial falls. Same day every outstanding soul in that court reverts to the house." He tapped the spiral. "She's not going down there to gloat. She's going down there to settle up before the whole market crashes. And she'll want the original contract presented — proof of title." His eyes went to the skin-paper in Ulyana's hands. "Which you're holding."

"Then I hold the leverage," Ulyana said slowly, turning it over. The warmth of it had not faded. It pulsed very faintly against her palms, like something asleep. "If the court requires this document to process her claim — to process *any* claim on me —"

"Don't say it."

"— then whoever carries it sets the terms of—"

"Ulyana." Davor's voice was quiet and absolutely level. "Every person in this story has looked at you and seen an asset. Your mistress. Vespera. The gods themselves. Don't you dare start doing it to yourself. You are not collateral that happens to have opinions."

The rebuke landed harder than anger would have, because it was true, and because he was the one person entitled to deliver it — the enforcer cashiered for refusing to treat a child as a sum. Ulyana sat back on her heels amid the ashes of her childhood and felt the two halves of her life grind together: the girl who had fled with a stolen grimoire and a talent for sums, and the woman who audited empires of impossible things. For nine years in this house she had been property learning to read. Everything since had been property learning to count.

Enough. Let the property file a complaint.

"We go down," she said. "Into the mountain. We take Sable, because her father's name might open doors that prayers can't. We take the contract, the map, and every page of these working papers, because Mother Ash kept records of *everything*, and if Vespera's forgery is in here — if there's one scrap proving the papers were false before the fact — then I don't need the court's mercy. I need their clerks." She stood, brushing ash and snow from her skirts, and heard her own voice arrive somewhere new: not flat, not shaking. Level as a ruled line. "She burned her house down and left me the deed because she thinks documents are traps and I'm still the girl who flinched at her hand. Fine. She taught me everything I know about contracts. She should have taught me less."

Davor shouldered his pack and looked up at her with something that was almost, but not quite, a smile. "There she is," he said.

They camped that night in the outhouse, which had somehow survived, and Ulyana lay listening to the wind work at the fen and the snow ticking on the roof, the contract wrapped in oilcloth against her chest where it warmed her like a second heartbeat. Somewhere under the world, a woman who had raised her was walking toward a court of sleeping gods to spend Ulyana's soul like coin. Somewhere above it, a guildmother who had hired her was keeping books that ended in the same place.

Both of them, Ulyana thought drowsily, had made the same mistake. They had each believed that a soul, once pawned, stays pawned.

But she had read the fine print of the world now, all the way down, and there was one clause neither of them had accounted for: *the holder must present the instrument.* And instruments could be contested. Instruments could be audited. Instruments, if you were very careful and very stubborn, could be torn up in front of everyone.

Near dawn, half-asleep, she heard Davor outside talking — to himself, she assumed, until she caught the words. He was standing in the snow facing east, toward the mountain, and what he said, quietly, over and over, like a man settling an account of his own, was: "I'm coming this time. Whatever it costs. I'm coming this time."

When they broke camp, the sky over the eastern hills had cleared for the first time in days, and the mountain stood above the white morning like a judge above a courtroom, and the road toward it was already marked with footprints — a single set, small and dry and unhurried, leading straight down the fen road and out of sight, pressed into the snow so lightly it might have been made by a woman who weighed nothing at all anymore.

Mother Ash had left them a trail.

Either an invitation, Ulyana thought, tightening her pack straps, or a test.

With her luck — which, knowing the Thorn's inventory, might literally be borrowed — it was both.
