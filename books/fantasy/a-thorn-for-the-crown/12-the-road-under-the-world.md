## 12. The Road Under the World

The mountain's mouth was a crack in the world disguised as an old mine entrance, half-swallowed by bramble and the first mean snows of the early winter. Davor stood before it with a lantern in one hand and a coil of chalked string in the other, looking like a man about to enter his own grave and mildly annoyed at the wait.

"Rules," he said, without turning around. "Three of them. You break them, I can't save you. You follow them, I still might not be able to save you, but at least I'll die knowing you tried."

"That's very reassuring," Sable said.

"I'm told it's why they paid me."

Ulyana hugged her coat tighter. She had packed like an accountant: rations counted to the day, candle stubs weighed against each other, a spare nib wrapped in oilcloth. The grimoire she'd left behind at the guild — Vespera had watched her pack it with an expression Ulyana couldn't read and hadn't liked trying to. What remained in her satchel were the essentials: ink, paper, and the copied page of her own contract, folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth.

"Rule one," Davor said. "We don't eat anything down there that offers itself to us."

"Offers itself?"

"You'll know. Rule two: if the lamps turn blue, we stop walking until I say otherwise. Blue means something holy died here, or something holy is sleeping, and neither kind appreciates being stepped on. Rule three—" he finally turned, and the lantern light caught the gray stubble on his jaw, the scar through one eyebrow that Ulyana had never asked about "—nobody pays for anything. Not a coin, not a favor, not a word you don't want heard. The road under the world runs on payment. We starve it instead."

"And when it demands?" Ulyana asked.

Davor smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had spent eleven years collecting debts and had opinions about the process.

"Then we run."

* * *

The salt tunnels began forty paces in and went on forever.

They were not natural caves. Ulyana realized this within the hour, by lamplight, running her fingers along walls carved in long smooth flutes, like the inside of some enormous throat that had swallowed a cathedral. Salt glittered in the stone everywhere, catching the lantern light and throwing it back in small dishonest sparkles. Everything looked precious. Nothing was.

"The old roads," Davor said. His voice behaved strangely down here, arriving flat and close no matter how far ahead or behind him he walked. "Before the gods went quiet, pilgrims came under the mountain instead of over it. Cheaper tolls. You could buy passage with a song, once. A true apology. Things that used to have value because people believed they did." He snorted. "Then the gods stopped answering, belief got scarce, and the toll-keepers got hungry. Hungry things get creative about what counts as currency."

"Years," Ulyana said. "You mentioned years before."

"Did I." It wasn't a question. He kept walking.

Behind them both, Sable's lamp was doing something wrong.

Ulyana noticed it the way she noticed everything wrong with a ledger — late enough to be annoying, early enough to matter. Their three lamps burned ordinary tallow-yellow, guttering in the tunnel draft. But every hundred paces or so, as they passed some alcove or side-shaft or niche in the salt wall, Sable's flame would lean toward it and change color: a wash of blue, deep and cold, like the sky remembered by someone who had never seen it.

"Rule two," Sable whispered the third time it happened, stopping obediently. "Blue. We stop."

"We stop," Davor confirmed, raising a hand. He studied the alcove where the blue light reached. Inside it, worn nearly smooth by centuries of salt air, was a carving: a woman with six arms, all of them empty, palms turned upward in offering or supplication. Someone had left a rusted iron ring at her feet, and dried flowers that might have been centuries old, and a child's shoe.

"She was a hearth goddess," Davor said quietly. "Vessa-of-the-Six-Hands. People prayed to her for enough bread. Just enough bread. That was the whole religion." He touched the shoe without picking it up. "When the prayers stopped getting answered, her altars went cold first. Small gods starve fastest."

Sable stared at the carving with an expression Ulyana couldn't name, and then did name, because she had seen it in mirrors of her own making: recognition. The look of a person finding family they'd been told didn't exist.

"My father traded my name," Sable said. "But somebody down here remembers hers."

"Lamps up," Davor said gently. "Keep walking."

But Ulyana noticed, for the next mile, that Sable carried her lantern higher, and that wherever the blue light brushed the walls, she turned her face toward it like a plant toward sun. And Ulyana noticed something else, something that made the accountant in her sit up and take notes: where Sable's blue light fell, the frost on the tunnel floor melted. Only in those small circles. Only where a god had lived.

The mountain, it seemed, knew its own blood.

* * *

They camped at the first junction where Davor pronounced the air safe — meaning it moved, meaning something somewhere still breathed. They ate hard bread and harder cheese, counted out by Ulyana into exact thirds, and Sable slept first while Ulyana took the watch with the ledger-copy open on her knees, reading the contract language again by lamplight, hunting for the flaw she could feel but not see.

"It's in the phrasing," she murmured, mostly to herself. "Hedge-witch contracts are always verbal-first, written-second. If the written version contradicts what was spoken—"

"You're doing arithmetic in your sleep," Davor said from across the fire. He wasn't mocking her. He was watching her with the particular attention of a man reassessing inventory.

"I'm auditing. There's a difference. Arithmetic tells you what happened. Auditing tells you who lied about it."

"Hm." He fed the small fire another stick. Down here even the flames seemed muted, burning with less conviction, as though the dark taxed them. "Ilya would have liked you. My partner. He has a mind like yours — sees the shape of a thing underneath its words."

"You've mentioned him twice now. Once more and I'll assume he's dead."

"He's worse than dead." Davor's jaw worked. "He's employed."

Ulyana let that sit. In the silence, Sable turned in her sleep and murmured something that sounded like a name — not her own; a smaller name, spoken the way children speak names they've invented for things that frighten them.

"Tell me about the child," Ulyana said.

She saw him decide, visibly, whether to lie. She saw him lose.

"There was a girl in Millbrook. Eight years old. Her father drank, then gambled, then borrowed — and when he couldn't pay, the debt passed down. That's standard practice in the under-court. Generational collateral. The father signed, the daughter owed." Davor stared into the fire. "My orders were to collect. Standard collection for a soul-debt on a minor: you don't take the whole soul, you take the interest-bearing portion. Ten years of her life, off the end, where she wouldn't miss them until she did. She'd die at sixty-seven instead of seventy-seven, and never know why her last decade felt thin."

"And you refused."

"I put my seal down on the contract marked *unpaid*, which is the single most expensive thing an enforcer can do. It made me personally liable for the debt. So I bought it myself — ten years of my life, transferred, gone. And then the court cashiered me anyway, because a collector who refuses once will refuse again, and unreliable instruments get retired." He shrugged, the shrug of a man describing weather. "Worth it. She's fourteen now. Growing up in a house with a sober father, because losing his daughter's years scared him straighter than any bailiff ever did. I check on her sometimes. She thinks I'm her uncle."

"Davor." Ulyana closed the ledger-copy. "That wasn't a refusal. That was a purchase."

"Don't romanticize it. I've done a hundred collections I didn't refuse. I just stopped counting the ones I could live with and started counting the ones I couldn't, and the second column got shorter every year until it was one girl long." He looked at her across the fire, and his eyes were very tired and very steady. "Your turn. Why does a ledger-keeper walk into hell for her own soul? You could serve your term. Vespera offered you the easy road."

"The easy road was paved with somebody else's body. It always is. That's the whole business model." Ulyana pulled her coat around her shoulders. "Besides. I read the fine print for a living. And I have not yet found the clause that says I have to accept what was done to me."

Something changed in Davor's face then — not warmth exactly, but the thing that comes before warmth, the ground thawing. Trust, Ulyana thought, is just arithmetic done slowly between people who keep checking each other's work. Somewhere in the last three days, their sums had started agreeing.

"Sleep," he said. "I'll take the watch."

For once, she obeyed without auditing the instruction.

* * *

The wights came for them on the third day, at a place where the tunnel widened into a hall.

Ulyana knew it was coming before Davor did, which she counted as professional pride. The signs were bookkeeping signs: the frost patterns on the walls had become regular, rhythmic, like ruled lines on a page. The salt crystals grew larger, more organized, arranged almost like columns of figures. And Sable's lamp had stopped flickering blue entirely — it burned steady and deep and cold, the way a flame burns when it is paying attention.

"Stop," Ulyana said.

Davor stopped. Then he smelled it too — or felt it: the specific pressure change of a space that contained more mouths than bodies.

"Toll-wights," he said softly. "Stay behind me. Say nothing. Offer nothing."

They came out of the salt walls the way figures come out of fog. They had been standing there all along, Ulyana realized — the tunnel had simply declined to show them. There were five, then seven, then she gave up counting because the number kept revising itself upward like a bill with hidden fees. They were tall and thin as unlit candles, their bodies crusted in salt bloom, their faces smooth except for mouths: wide, patient, unhurried mouths, the mouths of creatures who had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more.

The middle one spoke. Its voice was dry paper turning pages.

"*Passage costs,*" it said.

"We're not paying," Davor said.

"*Everything passes. Passage costs.*" Another wight drifted closer, and its mouth opened, and inside it Ulyana saw — she would swear afterward she saw — a tiny hourglass, sand falling. "*Pay in coin. Pay in blood. Pay in breath. Pay in years.*"

"How many years?" Sable asked, before Ulyana's hand closed on her arm.

"Sable. Nothing."

"But—"

"*The little god-child asks,*" the wight said, and all the mouths smiled at once, and the sound of it was salt grinding on salt. "*God-blood weighs heavy. Her years would buy passage for fifty.*"

The cold that went through Ulyana then was not the tunnel's cold.

"They don't touch her," she said, and her voice came out flat and hard and completely unfamiliar, the voice she imagined her handwriting would have if handwriting could speak. "Nobody touches her. Nobody prices her. She is not an asset on your books, and neither am I, and neither is he. So make your demand of me or move."

"*Bold,*" the wight observed. "*Boldness costs extra.*"

And then the hall erupted into negotiation-by-violence, which is to say the wights stopped pretending to be patient.

Davor moved like the professional he'd been — low, fast, driving his shoulder into the nearest wight and shattering it into a burst of salt crust and shrieking vapor. But there were too many, and they didn't fight fair, because they didn't fight at all: they *levied*. One wrapped around Ulyana's arm and she felt it *counting* her, a horrible crawling sensation like fingers running down a column of figures, taking inventory of her marrow. Another seized Sable's lamp hand, and the blue flame bent sideways, screaming silently.

"RUN!" Davor roared. "The side shaft — left — NOW—"

Ulyana ran. She grabbed Sable's wrist and hauled her toward the narrow fissure Davor had marked hours earlier as *flood route, unstable, last resort*. Behind them Davor held the line alone, and she heard him grunt, heard salt shatter, heard — worse — the dry-page voices chanting numbers, numbers, *years*, and she understood with sick clarity that they weren't trying to kill him.

They were invoicing him.

She shoved Sable into the fissure and turned back. Davor was on one knee. Three wights had their hands — their *pages* — pressed to his chest and temples, and his face had gone gray, and his lips were moving, counting backward from something.

Ulyana did the only thing her whole strange life had trained her for.

She picked up the fallen lantern, walked straight into the middle of them, and held the flame up to the tallest wight's blank face.

"Read my terms," she said.

It paused. Everything paused. Even the falling sand in its mouth seemed to hang.

"This tunnel charges tolls," Ulyana said, and the auditor's voice came out of her like drawn steel, "which makes it a business, which makes it *bound by the law of accounts*. And the first law of accounts is disclosure: no charge may be levied that was not posted in advance." She pointed the lantern at the wall behind the wights — at the ancient carved flutes, at a section of salt surface worn nearly blank. "Where is your tariff? Where is your posted schedule of rates? Show me the sign that says what passage costs, or by every law from here to the sleeping judge, you are collecting *fraudulently* — and fraudulent collection voids the debt."

Silence. The wights' smooth faces regarded her. Somewhere in the dark, Davor stopped counting.

Then, slowly, the tall wight's mouth opened, and the hourglass inside it turned over — and the sand began falling *upward*.

"*Posted,*" it rasped, and gestured with one salt-crusted limb at the blank wall, where letters surfaced like drowned bodies rising: ancient script, faded, half-eaten. A tariff. Centuries old. Rates in coin, in song, in apology, in years. "*Old rates. Before the quieting.*"

"Then honor them." Ulyana's heart was slamming against her ribs but her voice held. "Coin, song, or apology. Those are your lawful tolls. Years aren't on the schedule anymore — the schedule predates generational collection, I can *read*, and I'm telling you your own books disagree with your practice." She dug in her pocket and produced the last of their silver — three coins, counted precisely — and set them on the frozen floor. "Two passengers' toll, posted rate. Paid in full. Now *release my friend*."

The wights looked at the coins.

The wights looked at each other.

And then, one by one, like candles pinched out, they folded back into the salt walls — and Davor collapsed forward onto his hands, breathing hard, alive, whole, minus nothing that Ulyana could see.

She was at his side in four steps. "What did they take? Look at me. What did they *take*?"

"Nothing." He coughed salt dust. "Nothing. You — that was—" He stopped, and stared at her, and something in his face reorganized itself permanently, the way a ledger balances after long error. "Eleven years I worked for the court. Eleven years, and I never once saw anyone beat a toll-wight with *bookkeeping*."

"It's not bookkeeping." Ulyana helped him up. Her hands were shaking now that it was safe to shake. "It's auditing. There's a difference."

Sable appeared at the fissure mouth, lantern high, blue light washing the walls, and behind her — impossibly, absurdly — the frost on the tunnel floor had melted in a perfect ring, and in the ring, tiny white flowers were opening. Salt-flowers. Blooming where a god's daughter stood.

All three of them looked at the flowers.

"Rule two," Sable said weakly. "Does this count as blue?"

"It counts as something," Davor said. He picked one of the flowers, careful as a man handling evidence, and tucked it into his breast pocket, and did not explain himself, and Ulyana decided she would let him have that.

They walked on. The tunnel sloped down now, steepening, the air growing warmer in a way Ulyana trusted even less than the cold. And a half-mile on, Davor stopped so abruptly that Sable walked into his back.

Ahead, the passage forked. And wedged into the fork's apex, planted upright in the salt like a knife in a table, was a marker none of them had placed:

A strip of pale parchment, pinned by a black iron nail, bearing a seal Ulyana recognized from a thousand collection notices.

Davor's partner's seal. Ilya's seal.

Beneath it, in a familiar careful hand, a single line:

*They let you pass. I didn't. — I.*

Ulyana looked at Davor. Davor looked at the note for a long, long moment, and then said, very quietly, in the voice of a man reading his own obituary:

"He knows our route."
