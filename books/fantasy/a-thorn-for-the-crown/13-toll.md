## 13. Toll

The lamps had been burning blue for an hour before Davor admitted what that meant.

"Gods walked here," Sable said, holding hers up to the tunnel wall. The salt glittered under the flame like crushed stars, and where the light touched, faint shapes moved beneath the surface — footprints, maybe, or the ghosts of footprints, pressed into the stone by something vast and patient a very long time ago.

"Gods walked here," Davor agreed. "Which means we're close enough to be noticed. Which means from here on, everything wants a toll."

Ulyana looked up from her ledger — she still kept it, even down here, walking with the book open against her forearm like a soldier's shield. "We have almost nothing. I spent my last coin on the locket."

"We don't pay in coin." Davor didn't stop walking. "Down here you pay in what you are."

"That's not an answer, that's a riddle."

"It's a warning." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it used to go flat when he stood on a debtor's doorstep and knew what he was about to take. He hated that voice. It followed him into the dark anyway.

The salt tunnels ran under the mountain like veins under skin. Some were wide enough for carts; some forced them sideways, shoulders scraping crystal, breathing air that tasted of old brine and older secrets. Sable's lamp threw their shadows huge against the walls, three tall thin giants marching toward something none of them could name without flinching.

It was Ulyana who finally asked the question he'd been dreading.

"Davor. When you say toll — what did they take from you? Before. When you collected for them."

He counted eleven steps before answering. "Years."

* * *

They made camp in a widening of the tunnel where an ancient shrine niche held nothing but a bowl-shaped depression in the stone, worn smooth by centuries of offerings that had long since rotted or been taken. Davor built no fire. Fire attracted attention down here, and attention wanted payment.

They ate cold travel bread and hard cheese and drank water that seeped from the walls, tasting faintly of salt and, oddly, of summer. Ulyana noticed it too — he saw her frown at her flask.

"Memory," Sable said quietly. "Water remembers passing through places people loved."

"You say things like that as if they're normal," Ulyana said.

"Nothing about me is normal. You knew that when you hid me."

"I hid you because breaking into our vaults takes initiative."

Davor let them talk. Their bickering had become a kind of music over the weeks of descent — the small domestic sounds of people who had decided, without ever saying so, that they belonged to each other. He'd heard that sound once before, in another life, in the barracks of the guild enforcers, men and women bound by shared work pretending it was the same thing as shared love. It wasn't. This was closer.

"So," Ulyana said, turning to him, and he knew the tone: auditor's tone, ledger-open, pen uncapped. "Years. Explain."

"There's nothing to explain."

"Try me. I read contracts for a living. I promise you cannot bore me."

He looked at the dark beyond the lamplight instead of at her face. "The toll-wights stand at certain crossings. Old crossings, where roads used to meet before the mountain swallowed them. They don't rob you. Robbery implies you get to keep something. They weigh you — what your time is worth, what your days would fetch — and they take passage accordingly."

"And how much did they take from you?"

"Ilya and I crossed the Wither Gate six times in nine years of collecting." Eleven steps' worth of silence again. "First crossing, they took a month between us. By the last, we'd learned to arrive with good posture and clean consciences, and it was still two years."

Ulyana set down her bread. "Two *years.*"

"Split. A year each. It comes out of the middle somewhere — you don't feel it happen, you just find gaps later. Birthdays you can't account for. Winters you were told you lived through but can't remember." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than the telling. "You learn to stop auditing yourself."

"I will never do that," Ulyana said, with such ferocity that Sable laughed, one short bright note that died fast in the tunnel dark.

"No," Davor agreed. "I don't suppose you ever will."

* * *

They heard the wights before they saw them, which was backwards from every story, because wights were supposed to be silent.

It was a sound like abacus beads, thousands of them, clicking and sliding somewhere ahead in the black. Then a smell like cold iron left out in rain. Then the crossing itself: a place where four tunnels met under a vaulted ceiling ribbed like the inside of a chest, and standing at its center —

Davor had forgotten how tall they were. Or perhaps they grew, fed on all the years they'd eaten. Three figures, grey as unlit candles, their bodies less bodies than accumulations — layers upon layers, like wax drippings, like sediment, like debt compounding. Where faces should have been there were smooth hollows, and inside the hollows something turned slowly, endlessly, like coins in a counting-house drawer.

"Toll," said the first, and its voice was every receipt ever torn in half.

"Toll," said the second, in his mother's voice, which meant it was reading him, which meant it had found the memories worth pricing.

"Toll," said the third, in a voice Davor didn't know but Ulyana clearly did — he saw her go white and grab the strap of her ledger like a talisman. The hedge-witch's voice. Of course the wight would know it. Debts remembered their makers.

Sable stepped forward first, lamp high, and the blue flame made all three wights lean back a fraction — god-blood, even diluted, even forgotten, still read as *creditor*. But then the foremost wight extended one grey finger toward her and clicked its bead-teeth, and whatever it said passed between them in silence, and Sable's chin dropped.

"What?" Ulyana demanded. "What did it say?"

"It says my blood buys my own passage," Sable said, flat and careful. "But not yours. My father's name covers a daughter. It doesn't cover strangers."

"Then it can cover me," Davor said, and stepped past her before either woman could move.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd done in the dark while they slept, the sums he'd run and re-run until they balanced: three travelers, one crossing, and only two ways through. Sable's blood, or someone's years. And Ulyana could not spend years — her days were already spoken for, mortgaged to the court beneath them, and if she arrived short, if her soul-flame came due even an hour early, everything they'd walked through the dark for would gutter out in a glass reliquary before she could argue the fine print. Sable could not spend years either, though none of them fully understood why — divine accounting, Davor suspected, worked on principles no honest man should try to audit.

That left him. That had always, if he was honest, left him. He had quit collecting because he refused to weigh other people's lives on the guild's scales. Fine. Then let him put his own weight on, freely given, and see if the scales sang any sweeter.

"How much?" he asked the wight.

The hollow face tilted. Something inside it rattled like a strongbox shaken once.

"For three," the wight said, in the mother-voice, in the iron-rain voice, patient as interest accruing. "For the god's daughter and the pawned girl and the man who quit. For safe passage and no further tolls at this gate, ever, in either direction." The beads clicked. "Three days."

Davor exhaled. Three days. He'd braced for decades. Somewhere behind his ribs a tight knot he'd been carrying since the Wither Gate last time loosened by a single turn.

"Done."

"Davor—" Ulyana started.

"Wait outside the crossing," he told her. "Both of you. It has to be witnessed alone. That's how it works — the payer stands in the scale and everyone else stays off it, or the price climbs."

"Davor, wait—"

"*Off the scale,* Ulyana."

She went. He heard her boots retreat, heard Sable murmur something to her, heard the two of them arguing in whispers at the tunnel mouth. Good. Let them argue. Arguing was how those two loved people; he'd figured that out weeks ago.

The wights arranged themselves around him, three grey towers, and the crossing filled with a pressure like deep water. He felt the scale descend through him — not pain, exactly, worse than pain, more like an auditor's finger running down a column of his days, ticking, ticking, finding entries.

A morning on a rooftop watching gulls fight over bread. Gone.

His sister's wedding, the whole of it, the dancing and the terrible singing and his father crying into his cup. Not gone — he'd keep the fact of it — but the color drained out of it, the warmth, the way his feet had ached from dancing. A wedding reduced to a line item.

A night in a tavern after his first collection, drinking until he stopped shaking, and the bartender's kindness, and the stranger who bought his third round and told him nobody has to do this work forever. That one hurt going out. He felt it leave like a tooth.

Three days, the wight had said. But days weren't equal, down here. Days were weighed by what they held.

The beads stopped clicking. The pressure lifted.

"Passage," said the wights, in unison, in three voices, and the crossing was empty, and Davor stood alone under the ribbed ceiling feeling strangely lighter, which was the trap of it, wasn't it — that's what losing things always felt like at first.

He straightened his coat and walked toward the lamp-light and the two women waiting in it, and he put on his best shrug before he got there, so it would be ready.

* * *

Ulyana hit him before he finished the shrug.

Not a blow — a grabbing, both fists knotted in his lapels, hauling him down so they were eye to eye, her breath quick and furious, her eyes wet with a rage that hadn't decided yet whether it was grief.

"How many," she said. "How many days. Tell me the number."

"Three."

"Don't you *dare* lie to me with arithmetic, Davor Kestrel. I watched your face when you came out of that crossing. I've watched faces come out of negotiations for two years. Nobody walks away from paying *three days* looking like they buried a dog."

He considered lying again. Considered the shrug, the easy deflection, the enforcer's armor of not-telling. But her fists were in his coat and her eyes were full of him, actually full of him, in a way almost nothing in his life had ever been, and lies curdle fast in that kind of attention.

"The price was three days," he said. "What they took was heavier. Days aren't equal. They took the weightiest ones they could find. I don't even know all of it yet — you find the gaps later. There's a wedding I'll never dance at twice. There's a night in a tavern with a kind stranger that's just... gone now." His voice did something traitorous on the last word and he clamped down on it. "It's done. It balances. We're through the gate, all three of us, and no further tolls at this crossing ever. That was the deal."

"*No further tolls at this crossing*" — she released his coat to jab a finger at the dark behind them — "is not the same as no tolls, is it? There are more crossings. You said so yourself. And next time you'll do the same thing, won't you? You'll do the sums in your head in the night, and decide we're the assets that mustn't be spent, and you'll walk into the scale alone and come out smiling that horrible shrug—"

"Yes," Davor said.

The word landed in the tunnel like a dropped coin. Sable, standing a few paces off with her blue lamp, went very still.

Ulyana stared at him. "*Yes?*"

"Yes, I'll do it again. Next crossing, the one after, every one between here and the court." He held her gaze because looking away now would be unforgivable in a different way. "I did the same sums you're doing, Ulyana. Your days are collateral. Yours are *owned*. If you come up short — one hour short — the court collects early and everything we've suffered down here is waste. Sable's blood opens doors for her alone; it doesn't stretch. Mine is the only currency among us that's free and clear. Unencumbered. You taught me the word. So yes. I'll pay. Every time. And I'll tell you after, because if I tell you before, you'll argue, and you're right, and I can't afford for you to be right."

"You can't afford— " She made a sound halfway between laugh and sob. "You think this is about *affording?* Davor, you're spending yourself. That's not sacrifice, that's — that's slow suicide with extra steps, and you're dressing it up in bookkeeping language because you think it makes it noble. It doesn't. It makes it *accounting*, and I am so tired of everything terrible in this world wearing accounting's clothes!"

"Then what would you call it?"

"Mine!" The word tore out of her raw. "If it's going to be spent, it should be *mine* to spend! That's the whole point of everything — of the ledgers, of the court, of my soul in its glass jar — the whole rotten system runs on deciding what other people's lives are worth and taking them without asking! And you — you *good* man, you *kind* man — you're doing it too, you're doing it gently, you're doing it with a shrug, but you're still doing it!" Her voice cracked. "You don't get to decide my debts are yours. That's not love. That's just a nicer cage."

The tunnel was quiet except for water remembering summer somewhere in the walls.

Davor found he couldn't hold the shrug anymore. It fell off him entirely, and underneath it he was just a tired man in the dark who had lost a wedding and a kindness and couldn't remember what else yet.

"What's the alternative?" he asked, and hated how small it sounded.

"You tell us the price *before*. All three of us. We weigh it together, in the open, like people, not like accounts. And sometimes — sometimes — we let it be uneven. Sometimes you let Sable's blood open a door partway. Sometimes you let me owe a little more to a court I'm already planning to burn down. Sometimes the fair division is the one where everybody loses something and nobody loses everything, and you don't get to be the hero who absorbs it all, because heroes who absorb it all leave the rest of us standing around with nothing to give, and *that* is its own kind of theft, Davor. Did you ever think of that? Refusing our help is stealing from us."

Behind them, Sable spoke softly. "She's right. I've been stolen from my whole life. Don't do it too."

Davor looked at Ulyana — at the fury and the fear braided so tightly together in her face that he couldn't tell which was holding up which — and understood, with the slow inevitability of a figure finally arriving at the bottom of a column, that she was not angry about the toll.

She was terrified of a world where he wasn't in it.

"All right," he said.

"All right *what?*"

"All right. Before, not after. Together. In the open." He rubbed a hand over his face. "I've spent nine years making collections and one year learning to refuse them. Apparently I still don't know how to receive. Give me time. I'm told I have somewhat fewer days than previously, so I ought to use them efficiently."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's *not*—" But her mouth was doing something against her will, and the fury was draining out of her posture the way heat leaves a stone, and she turned away sharply to hide whatever her face was doing and busied herself furiously with her ledger, opening it, closing it, opening it again.

Sable drifted over to Davor while Ulyana composed herself. The girl's lamp burned steady blue between them.

"She'll want to check you," Sable murmured. "For the gaps. She'll make a list of your missing days and interrogate you about each one and write everything down. It's how she loves people. Like you shrugging. Like me knowing things."

"I know."

"Do you?" Sable looked up at him, and in the blue light her eyes were old, old, old. "My father sold my name before I could walk. Everyone who's supposed to love me has always paid for me in ways I didn't agree to. I know exactly what it costs to be someone's settlement." She touched his sleeve, briefly. "Don't make her a widow of choices she never made. That's all."

She walked back to the lamp. Davor stood a moment longer in the dark, taking inventory of himself, running fingers down his own interior columns the way Ulyana had taught him without ever teaching him.

Somewhere in him, a wedding had gone grey. Somewhere, a stranger's kindness was a blank page. And somewhere else — he could feel it, a smooth absence shaped like a memory he'd never get back — something about the child. About the door he'd refused to knock on, the winter he'd carried a small warm bundle away from the collectors' route, the reason he'd thrown away his badge and his wage and his name in the guild rolls.

He reached for it and found fog.

The panic that rose in him then was unlike anything the wights had wrung out of him. Because that day — that choice — was the load-bearing wall of everything he was. He had built his entire self on top of refusing that collection, and now the foundation was gone, and the whole structure above it was swaying, and he couldn't remember the child's face, or the street, or why, *why*—

"Ulyana." His voice came out wrong. She looked up. "Check me. Now. Tonight. Make your list. Start with—" He swallowed. "Start with a child. There was a child. I need to know if I still know why."

Ulyana was already reaching for her pen.

* * *

They walked on through the blue-lit dark, three figures in the veins of the mountain, and far ahead — so far it might have been imagination — something answered Sable's lamp with a flicker of its own.

Not a lamp.

A flame in glass. Waiting.
