## 14. The Market of Small Mercies

The tunnel opened the way a mouth opens before it speaks.

One moment they were walking single file through salt that glittered like dirty sugar, and the next the dark ahead of them turned warm — amber, honeyed, lit from below — and Davor put out an arm to stop them all.

"Market," he said. "Rules first. Don't haggle unless you mean to buy. Don't say your true name aloud. And don't," he glanced back at Sable, "touch anything that hums."

"Why not?" Sable asked.

"Because things that hum want to be held."

Ulyana peered past his shoulder and felt her ledger-keeper's heart do something complicated. Below them, spilling down the throat of the mountain like spilled gold, was a bazaar. Not the sort she knew — no cobbled square, no awnings snapping in river wind — but a cavern the size of a cathedral district, terraced in rings, each ring hung with lanterns of different colors, and every lantern burning without flame. Stalls crowded the terraces: some built of bone-white salt, some of stacked prayer-books, one entire shopfront made of folded letters tied with red string, thousands of them, breathing faintly in a wind that had no source.

Sound rose up to meet them. Not shouting — murmuring. The murmur of ten thousand people whispering at once, and beneath it, threaded through it, voices saying *please*.

"What is this place?" Sable whispered.

"The Market of Small Mercies." Davor's voice went flat and careful, the voice he used for things he'd rather not feel about. "When the gods went quiet three hundred years ago, somebody had to keep the prayers moving. This is where they ended up. Prayers are bought here. Sold here. Resold. Bundled. A widow prays for rain; a merchant buys her prayer cheap because rain is coming anyway; he resells it during a drought at four times the price. The gods never heard most of these. They just circulate." He paused. "Like everything else."

"Everything circulates," Ulyana said quietly. It was the first law of the Gilded Thorn, carved over Vespera's desk: *Nothing is owned. Everything is owed.* She'd thought it a guild maxim. Standing here, looking down at a cavern built entirely out of other people's desperate hopes, she understood it was older than the guild. Older than almost anything.

"Stay close," Davor said. "And whatever you see me do — I'm working. Remember that."

He led them down the switchback stairs into the amber light.

* * *

The Market smelled of candlewax and cold iron and, strangely, oranges — a bright false sweetness that Ulyana couldn't place until she passed a stall where a woman was ladling spoonfuls of summer out of a clay pot. Actual summer. You could see it: heat shimmering off the surface, a dragonfly frozen mid-hover just under the skin, rising lazily when the ladle disturbed it.

"Last jar of the season," the woman called. "Harvested July, still sweet. One memory of a specific afternoon, or trade for a good night's sleep."

Ulyana thought of the vaults beneath the Gilded Thorn, of Vespera's gloved hands lifting a jar labeled LAST SUMMER, HARTSWELL DISTRICT, and felt the same moral nausea she'd been carrying since the snow began, except here it was worse, because here there was no guildmother between her and the trade. Here the trade was simply the air.

They moved through the terraces. Davor walked like a man wading upstream, shoulders set, eyes forward, refusing stalls with the discipline of a monk. But Ulyana watched him look. At a stall selling secondhand courage — small vials of it, cloudy, each labeled with what it had been spent on originally (*one charge, facing a brother*, read one). At a rack of borrowed luck, keys on a ring, each key opening nothing, each one humming faintly when you picked it up.

She herself kept her eyes down and her hands in her pockets, around the last coin she owned. A silver piece, worn nearly smooth, minted by a kingdom that no longer existed. She'd carried it since the hedge-witch's cottage. It had no value here — she'd checked the exchange rates in Davor's old route-notes twice — but she hadn't been able to leave it behind. Some things you keep not because they're worth anything but because they're yours.

Sable, meanwhile, had stopped walking.

It took Ulyana three steps to notice, two more to turn. The girl stood at the edge of a terrace, absolutely still, staring across the market floor at a stall near the bottom ring. Around her, the crowd flowed like water around a stone.

"Sable?"

"My father," Sable said. Her voice came out strange, stripped flat. "I can hear my father."

Ulyana followed her gaze. The stall at the bottom was modest by Market standards — a table, a canopy of grey silk, a vendor who looked like everyone else's grandmother, plump and shawled and kind-eyed. On the table, arranged on velvet gone grey with age, lay perhaps forty lockets.

Each locket was open. And from each one, so faint you had to strain, came a voice.

* * *

Getting down to the bottom ring took longer than it should have. The Market didn't like people hurrying toward things; the stairways rearranged themselves subtly, offering detours past tempting stalls, and twice Ulyana caught herself pausing over merchandise she had no interest in, wondering vaguely if she might like a bottle of someone else's patience. The Market sold by distraction. It was, she realized grimly, the Gilded Thorn with better lighting.

Davor broke the pull by grabbing both their arms. "Eyes on the stairs," he muttered. "Count them. Out loud if you have to."

So they counted — forty-one steps, thirty-eight, thirty-five — and reached the grey-silk stall flushed and annoyed at themselves.

The vendor looked up from a cup of tea and smiled as though they were grandchildren arrived early for supper.

"Three travelers," she said. "Or is it four? Sometimes there's a fourth." Her eyes flicked somewhere over Ulyana's left shoulder, and Ulyana decided very firmly not to check. "You'll be wanting to browse, or will I save us both the time? Everyone wants the same thing down here. To hear somebody again."

"We're passing through," Davor said. "Just need directions to the Court road."

"Everyone's passing through." The vendor sipped her tea. "Nobody passes through the Market, dear. The Market passes through you. Browse."

Sable was already at the table.

Ulyana watched her lean over the lockets and understood, with a lurch, exactly what this was. Forty voices. Forty fathers, maybe, or mothers, or brothers — the Market didn't care about the shape of a grief, only its weight. Each locket held a captured voice: a god's voice, in at least some cases, harvested back when the gods still spoke aloud, back before the silence. Resold ever since. A father's blessing, bottled. A father's anger. A father saying your name.

"Which one—" Sable started, and stopped, because her hand had already drifted to the third locket from the left, the way a compass needle drifts north, and when it touched the velvet the little brass case began to glow.

"Oh," said the vendor, setting down her tea. "Oh, that's an old one."

The voice came out thin, tinny, worn down by centuries of playback, but underneath the wear it was unmistakably what Sable said it was. A man's voice. Warm, tired, amused in the way of someone who has been patient a long time.

*"—told you, little shadow, names are heavier than they look. Carry mine carefully—"*

The recording frayed there, looped, began again. *"—told you, little shadow—"*

Sable made a sound Ulyana had never heard a person make. Not a sob. Something smaller and worse — the sound of someone finding one crumb of a meal they'd starved through.

"How much," she said.

The vendor named a price.

Ulyana did the arithmetic automatically and hated every number. It was, in honest terms, a fortune — more than the three of them could raise in a month of selling everything they carried, boots included. The vendor wasn't even gouging; Davor's tight face said he recognized the figure as fair, which somehow made it crueler. Fair prices could still be impossible. Most of them were.

"It's a famous voice," the vendor added gently, watching Sable's face collapse and doing nothing whatsoever to stop it, which was its own kind of cruelty, the professional kind. "Minor storm-god, northern pantheon, traded his children's names away in the settlement years. Very collectible. I've had offers."

"From whom?" Davor asked sharply.

"Collectors." The vendor smiled her grandmother smile. "The Court buys sentiment, you know. Lockets especially. They melt them down for the trials." She turned back to Sable with terrible kindness. "You could leave a deposit, dear. The Market honors deposits. Come back when you're liquid."

Sable's hand hovered over the locket. She didn't pick it up. Ulyana realized why a moment later: the girl was afraid that once she held it, she would never be able to put it down, and then the taking-away would kill her outright.

Ulyana had grown up poor under a witch who priced everything, including affection. She knew this exact moment. She had stood at a hundred market stalls in her childhood, wanting, while someone kind-eyed named a number that might as well have been the distance to the moon.

There was a coin in her pocket. Worn smooth. Minted by a dead kingdom. Worth nothing here.

She took it out anyway.

* * *

"I'll give you this," Ulyana said.

The vendor looked at the silver piece lying in Ulyana's palm and laughed — kindly, genuinely, the laugh of someone who has heard every pitch the road produces. "Sweetheart. That's not currency anywhere. That kingdom fell before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk."

"I know what it's worth," Ulyana said. "I'm not offering it as currency. I'm offering it as payment. There's a difference."

That got the vendor's attention. Down here, in a market built entirely on the difference between what things cost and what they cost *you*, difference was the whole trade. The grandmother-eyes sharpened into something appraising. "Go on."

"It's the last thing I own," Ulyana said. "Not the last thing of value — the last thing that's *mine*. Everything else I have, I hold on behalf of other people. Ledgers, wages, inventory, debts. This coin I stole from a woman who stole it from someone else, and I've kept it eleven years knowing it was worthless, because it was the only object in my life with no entry against it. No lien. No claim. Clean." She turned the coin over. It caught the lantern-light. "In a market of mercies, that should count for something. A wholly unencumbered asset. Ask your appraisers. They know how rare those are."

The vendor was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, a thousand whispers murmured *please, please, please.*

"You'd be giving up the last clean thing you have," the vendor said, "for a girl you've known a fortnight."

"Yes."

"For her *father*. Who isn't here. Who sold her name away and got himself collected like a bad debt."

"Yes," Ulyana said again, and found she meant it more the second time, which surprised her. "That's rather the point of unencumbered assets. You get to decide where they go."

The vendor studied her. Then Sable. Then, oddly, Davor, who was standing very straight with his jaw set like a man trying not to interrupt a miracle.

"Well," the vendor said at last, and sighed, and sounded for one moment much older than any grandmother. "Eleven years clean, and you spend it on love. They always do. That's why the Market exists, you know. Not greed. Greed we could manage." She scooped up the locket, closed it gently around the fading warmth of the voice, and held it out — not to Sable, but hovering between them. "The coin, then. And one condition."

"Name it," Ulyana said, hating conditions, hearing Vespera's voice in her own mouth.

"When you play it for her — and you will play it, tonight, I can tell — listen all the way to the end. Past where it loops. These old harvestings have deep grooves. There's more on this one than the traders ever bothered to hear." She pressed the locket into Sable's hands and closed the girl's fingers over it with her own soft ones. "Traders only ever listened for what they could sell. Listen for what nobody wanted."

Then she took the coin, bit it out of pure theater, and dropped it into a tea cup where it vanished with a sound like a small door closing.

* * *

They camped that night in an alcove off the Market's upper ring, behind a curtain of dried forget-me-nots that Davor bribed a stallholder to pretend didn't exist. The Market's amber light seeped through the flowers and lay across the floor in coins.

Sable sat cross-legged with the locket in both hands and didn't open it.

"It'll sound wrong," she said finally. "Worn out. Looping. He'll say six words and start over. I'll sit here and he'll say six words and start over forever, and I'll—" Her thumb traced the locket's hinge. "I used to imagine what he sounded like. Eleven years. Whatever comes out of this box is going to be smaller than what I imagined."

"Things usually are," Davor said. "Doesn't make them less real. Makes them realer, mostly. Imagining's the luxury version."

"That's supposed to help?"

"No," Davor admitted. "But I've noticed true things rarely are."

Ulyana sat down beside her. "Play it," she said softly. "All the way through. Like she said. What nobody wanted."

A long breath. Then Sable opened the locket.

The voice filled the alcove, thin and crackling, worn to a groove:

*"—told you, little shadow, names are heavier than they look. Carry mine carefully—"*

Loop. *"—told you, little shadow—"*

Loop. *"—told you—"*

Sable's eyes were wet and fixed on nothing. Davor had turned away, giving privacy the only way he knew, studying the curtain of dead flowers like it owed him money. Ulyana took Sable's free hand and held it and listened, really listened, the way she listened to ledgers — past the obvious entries, down into the margins, the fine print, the parts written small.

The loop stuttered.

*"—little shadow, names are heavier than they look. Carry mine carefully. If anyone asks after me — if anyone comes wearing my face, or my weather — remember: I did not sell you. I sold the* debt *of you. There's a difference, and someday someone clever enough will find it in the paperwork. Tell her—"*

Static. A long tearing static, the groove jumping, decades of careless playback chewing the words.

"—*tell her the account was never settled. Tell her I left something in escrow. Under the winter, in the—*" 

The voice degraded into a whisper, then a hiss, and then, barely, almost lost:

*"—her name is Sable. Write it down. Someone write it down—"*

Silence. The loop tried to restart and couldn't. The locket went cold in Sable's hands.

For a moment none of them moved.

"He remembers," Sable whispered. "At the end. He — that's not a message for me, that's a message for *whoever found it*. He hid it in the part nobody listens to. 'Someone clever enough will find it in the paperwork.'" She looked up at Ulyana, and her face was wrecked and blazing at once. "He means you. He means *that*. You found the flaw in my father's contract the way you found the flaw in yours — you read the fine print — Ulyana, he left something in escrow, under the winter, in the—"

"In the Court," Ulyana finished slowly. The pieces were clicking together with the particular pleasure of a balanced column. "Under the winter. In the archive, probably. Or the reliquary vaults." She was already, helplessly, sketching columns in the frost-dust on the stone beside her: *escrow — unreleased assets — Court holdings — search protocol.* "Your father didn't just sell your names and vanish. He structured the deal. He hid an asset the Court may not even know it's holding. An escrow release requires a claimant with standing and proof of name." She met Sable's eyes. "You have the name. I have the proof-of-claim training. We were always going to break into that court anyway."

Sable laughed — a wet, cracked, astonished laugh — and clutched the locket to her chest like a heartbeat.

Davor turned around. He was smiling, which on Davor's face was an event, like a solar eclipse. "An auditor, a runaway heiress of a minor god, and a disgraced enforcer," he said, "planning to audit hell."

"The Court beneath the mountain," Ulyana corrected primly.

"Same paperwork."

It was Sable who heard it first — footsteps in the corridor outside the forget-me-not curtain, unhurried, deliberate, the tread of someone who knew exactly which alcove they were in. Davor was on his feet with a knife out before the second step landed, one arm sweeping Sable behind him.

The curtain parted.

A tall man stood in the amber light, travel-worn, grey at the temples, with a debt-collector's badge hanging openly from his belt and a face Davor clearly knew — Ulyana saw it land on him like a blow.

"Hallo, Kestrel," the stranger said quietly. "We need to talk before the Court finds out you're in my market. And Kestrel — it's Ilya. Please put the knife down."
