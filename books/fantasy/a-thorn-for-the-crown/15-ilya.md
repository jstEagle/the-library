## 15. Ilya

The bazaar's noise faded behind them like a tide going out.

They walked the last stretch of the salt tunnel in a loose line — Sable first with the locket clutched in both fists against her sternum, then Ulyana counting their remaining supplies under her breath the way other people prayed, and Davor at the rear, watching the dark behind them out of habit that had once been a profession. His boots knew these tunnels. That was the problem. Every fork in the road ahead of them was a road he had walked in the other direction, carrying paper and chains, collecting what people owed.

He had told himself the knowledge was an asset. He had not let himself think about what it made him.

"Left at the pillar," Sable said. She didn't look back. The locket's chain glinted blue at her throat where the godlight touched it — fainter now, the lamps burning ordinary yellow as they moved away from the places where gods had once walked. She'd hardly spoken since the vendor's stall. She kept touching the locket the way you touch a bruise to check it's still yours.

"You remember it?" Davor said.

"I remember everything down here," she said. "It remembers me too."

He believed her. Three days under the mountain and he had stopped disbelieving things said calmly by children with divine blood.

Ulyana fell back a step to walk beside him. In the lamplight her face was all sharp angles and ledger-arithmetic, but there was something underneath it tonight, something softer than calculation. He had learned to read her the way she read contracts: the meaning lived in the margins.

"You're doing the thing," she said quietly. "Where you walk like a man being followed."

"I am a man being followed."

"We're three days past the toll-wights and nothing has come for us. If the court wanted us stopped—"

"The court doesn't chase," Davor said. "It waits. It lets debt do the chasing. You know that better than anyone."

She flinched. He hadn't meant it as cruelty; it came out as fact, because everything came out of him as fact these days. Facts were safer than the other things in his chest. He watched her decide not to be wounded by him, watched her fold the moment away into some column of her own keeping, and hated himself a little for how easily he was becoming someone people had to make allowances for.

Ahead, Sable stopped so suddenly that Ulyana nearly walked into her.

The tunnel opened.

It was one of the great junctions, the kind the old salt-cutters had hollowed out before the mountain learned to charge admission — a cathedral of pale crystal veined with seams of blue fire, tall enough that the lamplight couldn't find the ceiling. Pillars of rock salt stood in ranks down its length, worn smooth by centuries of wind that no longer blew. And at the far end, where the tunnel narrowed toward the descent to the court, stood a man.

He was leaning against a pillar with his arms folded, one boot crossed over the other, as if he'd been waiting through the whole history of the world and found it mildly diverting.

Davor's body knew before his mind did. His hand went to the knife at his belt and his heart went somewhere cold and small, because he knew the shape of that lean, knew the way the man tilted his head when he was deciding whether to be amused, knew him across thirty years and two hundred miles of dark.

"Ilya," Davor said.

"Davor Kestrel." Ilya pushed off the pillar. The blue light caught his face — older than Davor remembered, or maybe Davor had simply never looked properly before. There were new lines around his mouth, the kind carved by long work and short sleep. He wore the grey coat of the under-court's collection service, cut fine, buttons polished. On his chest hung the collector's seal: a small brass weight, balanced perfectly level.

Behind Davor, he heard Ulyana shift her weight. Heard the whisper of her hand finding Sable's shoulder and drawing the girl half behind her. Good instincts. Wrong instinct, this once. Or right. He genuinely did not know yet.

"You're supposed to be dead," Davor said. It wasn't true. He'd never heard that Ilya was dead. But it was the sentence his mouth chose, and later he would understand why: it was easier to grieve a man than to meet him standing here, wearing the coat Davor himself should have been wearing if he hadn't thrown it in a canal seven years ago.

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things." Ilya spread his hands. "Alive is the only one I've managed."

* * *

They sat on a fallen pillar while Ilya produced bread and hard cheese and a flask of something that smelled like it had been distilled from regret, and Davor understood that this was how Ilya negotiated: he fed you first. He had done it with defaulters, with widows, with men twice Davor's size who wanted to fight rather than sign. Feed them, sit them down, make the debt a conversation instead of an execution. Davor had learned half his craft watching Ilya do it, and the other half refusing to learn it.

Sable ate without taking her eyes off him. Children always saw through Ilya fastest, Davor remembered. They hadn't yet learned that charm could be load-bearing.

"So," Ilya said, pouring the flask's contents into a tin cup and handing it to Ulyana, who examined it like a suspicious contract before drinking. "Three fugitives, descending toward the court with stolen goods and stolen selves. One of them a soul the court holds paper on. One of them—" his eyes rested on Sable, and something in them changed, gentled, "—one of them a name I've seen on documents I wasn't supposed to read."

Sable's chin came up. "You've seen my name?"

"I file things," Ilya said. "Everything filed passes through me eventually. Your father's accounts. His forfeitures." He paused, and Davor watched him decide how much truth to spend. "There's a locket in the inventory lists. Lot forty-one, voice of a minor deity, condition poor. Someone bought it recently. Paid in the last coin the buyer carried."

Ulyana set down the cup. "You're the clerk."

"I'm whatever they need me to be. Clerk, courier, collector." Ilya smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that used to mean the night was going well, and Davor saw the effort it cost him to hold it up. "Mostly I'm the man who knows where everything is. Including, currently, you."

"Then you know we can't stop," Ulyana said. Flat, clear, no apology in it. Davor felt an absurd flare of pride. "Whatever they sent you to do, the answer is we keep walking."

"They didn't send me." Ilya turned the flask in his hands. "That's the joke. Nobody sent me. The court assumes I'll handle it — I'm in the region, I have history with the male fugitive, why waste a bailiff? But there's no order. No writ. Do you understand what that means?" He looked at Davor, and the smile finally came down. "It means they don't think it matters. It means they think you're already caught. Everything below midwinter belongs to the reckoning; anything moving through the tunnels is just interest accruing on its way home. They didn't send me because sending someone would imply you were worth the postage."

The silence after that had teeth in it.

"That's either very freeing or very insulting," Ulyana said at last.

"It's both. That's the house style." Ilya stood, brushed salt dust from his coat. "Walk with me, Davor. Just you. Ten minutes of your company before I ruin your evening."

"He doesn't go anywhere alone with you," Ulyana said.

"Ulyana." Davor put a hand lightly on her arm. Her jaw set; he watched her run the arithmetic — odds, exits, the knife in her boot — and arrive, grudgingly, at trust. It was a strange gift, being trusted by someone who audited everyone. "Ten minutes. If I shout, come fast. If I don't shout—"

"Don't say it," she said. "If you don't shout, I'll assume you're being sentimental, and I'll come anyway."

* * *

Ilya led him along the gallery to a seam in the crystal where the blue fire ran thick, and the two of them stood in its glow like men warming themselves at a hearth. For a while neither spoke. Below them, muffled by stone, they could hear the deep slow pulse of the mountain — the sound the toll-wights had called the court breathing.

"She looks like you," Ilya said finally.

"What?"

"The child. Sable. Not the blood — the stubbornness. The way she stands like she's daring the world to try something." Ilya's mouth twisted. "And the other one. The auditor. She watches you like you're a sum she hasn't finished checking, and she'll defend the total to the death. You collected for eleven years, Davor. Eleven years, and the first time you refuse a writ, you come out the other side of the mountain with people willing to die over your arithmetic." He shook his head slowly. "I've been trying all evening to work out whether that makes you lucky or makes me stupid."

"Both," Davor said. "House style."

That got a real laugh out of him, brief and cracked, and then it was gone and the serious face came back, the one Davor knew from every collection that had gone wrong.

"The girl in Harrow Lane," Ilya said.

Davor closed his eyes. Seven years, and the name still arrived like weather. "Don't."

"Mira Voss. Age nine. Debt inherited from her father, forty-one marks against a bottled memory of her mother, compounded quarterly." Ilya recited it the way he recited everything, perfectly, and Davor realized with a lurch that he had carried the same figures in his head for seven years, word for word, number for number. Two men memorizing the same crime. "You refused the writ. You gave her the forty-one marks out of your own pay and told the guild the account was already settled, and when they audited it — which they always audit, that's the whole religion of that place — you took the fall alone. Cashiered. Struck from the rolls. Walked out with one bag and your good name in tatters."

"You were there," Davor said. "You signed the audit."

"I signed it," Ilya agreed, "because if I hadn't, they'd have looked at my accounts next, and mine wouldn't have survived inspection any more than yours would have. Because I had debts of my own, Davor. Not money. The other kind." He pressed his palm flat against the blue seam in the rock, and for a moment the light pulsed brighter around his fingers, as if the mountain recognized him. As if it owned him. "Do you know what happens to a collector who quits? The court doesn't cashier us. We're not guild property, we're *theirs*. The day you stop collecting, you start owing. My term is twenty years. I'm fourteen years in. Every soul I bring home shaves a week off. Every soul I let slip adds a month." He laughed, softly, horribly. "I did the sums once. At the rate I'm going, I get out when I'm ninety-one. Assuming the winter doesn't extend everyone's terms, which — between us — it will."

Davor stared at him. All those years, all those collections side by side, the shared rooms and shared flask and shared silences, and he had built his defection on a foundation of sand: the belief that Ilya had chosen the work freely. That staying meant agreeing.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Would it have changed what you did?"

"No."

"There's your answer." Ilya took his hand off the wall. The light dimmed. "Knowing wouldn't have saved you, and it would have broken me sooner. I let you carry it alone because carrying it alone was the only clean thing either of us had left."

* * *

When they came back, Ulyana was on her feet and Sable was standing slightly in front of her, which Davor noted and filed away: the god's daughter protecting the auditor, or perhaps the reverse, hard to say anymore, and maybe it didn't matter which. Ilya raised both hands, palms out, the universal grammar of *no trouble*.

"I'm letting you pass," Ilya said.

Ulyana's eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because I'm fourteen years into a twenty-year sentence and I've forgotten how to want anything, and watching the three of you has been the closest thing to wanting I've felt since Harrow Lane. Because the court thinks you're already caught, so technically I lose nothing. Because—" he stopped. Looked at Davor. Started again, quieter. "Because seven years ago I signed a piece of paper I've been trying to unwrite ever since, and this is the only pen I've got."

Nobody spoke. Sable stepped forward, looked up at him with that unnerving divine directness, and said: "You're lying about part of it."

"Sable," Ulyana warned.

"No, it's fine, she's right." Ilya crouched to the girl's level, unhurried, unafraid. "What else do you see?"

"You're going to tell them anyway."

The words dropped into the tunnel like a stone into a well. Davor felt the bottom fall out of the evening. Ulyana's hand went to her knife.

Ilya didn't flinch. He held the girl's gaze, and his face did something complicated — grief and relief fighting over the same ground.

"Yes," he said. "I am. Not today. But I'll report this junction, your numbers, your direction. I'll mark your route for the court, because if I don't, someone worse will follow it — a bailiff-spirit doesn't feed you cheese and tell you the truth first. And here's the part that will keep you up tonight, auditor:" he straightened, turning to Ulyana, "when I mark your route, I'll mark it *wrong*. Half a day east of where you actually go. Through the flooded galleries, where nothing living can follow. It'll buy you a full day before anyone works out the error, and by then you'll be at the gates of the court, and whether they open for you or swallow you won't be my problem anymore."

"That's not letting us pass," Ulyana said slowly. "That's spending us."

"That's betrayal," Ilya agreed pleasantly, "that isn't quite betrayal. Welcome to working for the under-court. Everything down here is owed, even mercy. Especially mercy." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded square of paper — a route map, drawn in the fine precise hand Davor knew from a thousand collection notices — and held it out. When nobody took it, he set it on the fallen pillar between them and stepped back.

"Flooded galleries are marked in red," he said. "Avoid them. Everything else on that map is a lie. Memorize the lie, walk the red, and don't look back, because looking back is how they catch people. Not with chains. With *regret*." He buttoned his coat. The brass seal at his chest swung level, perfectly balanced, and Davor thought: fourteen years, and the man still keeps his weights honest. "One more thing. Free of charge, since I'm ruining your evening anyway."

"The trial," Ulyana said.

"The trial." Ilya nodded at her, almost approving — auditor to auditor. "Midwinter's end, Hall of Frozen Petitions, the Sleeping Judge presiding. Precedent weighs more than truth down there; don't bother arguing justice, argue *procedure*. And—" he hesitated. It was the first hesitation Davor had seen in him all night, and it was worse than all the certainty before it. "There's a fourth prisoner coming down the mountain behind you. Small. Alone. Pawned young, like you were. The court recalled her file this morning, and files only get recalled when the owner wants to inspect the goods personally."

Ulyana went white. "Who owns her file?"

Ilya pulled his collar up against a cold that came from deeper than the mountain, and gave Davor a look across thirty years and one unforgiven piece of paper.

"A hedge-witch," he said, "with excellent references. She's expected at the court by tomorrow's dusk. Whatever you're planning — plan faster."
