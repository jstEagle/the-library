## 16. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The tunnel stopped being a tunnel an hour before they knew it.

Ulyana noticed it first as a change in the arithmetic of the dark. For three days the salt passages had run in predictable ratios — forty paces of straight, a bend, sixty of descent, a chamber. But now the numbers went strange. Corridors opened into corridors at angles no surveyor would sign. Staircases descended into staircases. A passage they had walked once came back to them from the other side, as though the mountain were folding its own ledger to hide an entry.

"It's the court," Davor said quietly. "We're inside its jurisdiction. Space starts keeping books here."

Sable's lamps had been burning blue since the salt caves, and now they burned blue and *slow*, the flames leaning backward like reeds in a current that flowed toward something. Toward the center of everything.

"Keep the light low," Davor added. "We're past the toll-wights' territory, but jurisdiction isn't safety. It's the opposite. Down here, everything you do is on the record."

Ulyana touched the folded contract against her breastbone — the hedge-witch's original, the one flaw she was carrying like a knife into the heart of the mountain. "Good," she said. "I want it on the record."

They came around a final bend of black stone and the tunnel simply *gave up*, the way a clerk gives up pretending he understands the accounts, and Ulyana stood at the mouth of the under-court and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

* * *

It was a cathedral, if a cathedral could be built by someone who had heard of churches only secondhand and resented the comparison. It was vast — vaster than the mountain should have contained, and Ulyana understood dimly that it did not *fit* so much as *insist*. Columns of black salt rose out of sight, and every one of them was carved with writing, floor to vanishing point, in scripts that predated the alphabet the way bedrock predates soil.

But it was the petitions that stopped her.

They hung in the air. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Prayers — she recognized them the way you recognize a letter from home — frozen mid-ascent, suspended in the cathedral air like snow that had been told to wait. A fisherman's plea for his nets. A mother's bargain offered to any god who would take it: *her own hands, her own years, only let the fever break.* A child's scrawled request for a dead dog, addressed to no one in particular because the child had not known addresses mattered. They hung at every height, glowing faintly, each one a small cold star of wanting.

"Three centuries of silence," Ulyana whispered. "Where did they all go?"

"Nowhere," said a voice from below them — Ilya, who had caught them at the mountain's mouth and now walked with them, memorized procedures and all, because a man who betrays you twice in the same direction has made a strange kind of vow. "They accumulated. Prayer doesn't expire. It *accrues*. Interest, penalties, compounding." He gestured at the frozen multitude. "The gods slept through the whole debt. Now they're awake, and every one of these is due for an answer."

"And the winter?" Sable asked.

Ilya looked up at the petitions, and for once his collector's face was just a tired man's face. "You're standing in the answer."

They descended. The steps were cut for things larger than people — each one came to Ulyana's knee — and the air grew colder in a way that was not temperature. Cold like a courtroom. Cold like the pause after a question you cannot answer.

* * *

The floor of the cathedral was a floor only by courtesy. It was, Ulyana realized as they crossed it, a ledger — the oldest one, the original, the master book from which all other books were copies. The stone was scored with columns. Debit. Credit. Names in a script that made her eyes water to read, and she had learned to read contracts in three dead languages before she was nineteen. Between the columns ran the entries of the world itself: *one river, granted, term of four thousand years; one sun, leased, renewal pending; the shape of fire, lent to mortals, no collateral taken — an error.*

"An error," Davor read over her shoulder, and made a sound that was almost a laugh. "They wrote *error*."

"Gods make them too," Ilya said. "They just never have to balance for them. Until now."

And then Ulyana saw the gods.

They sat at the far end of the cathedral, and her first thought — her shameful, irreverent, *accountant's* first thought — was that they looked like a panel of magistrates who had been on the bench too long. There were nine of them, or perhaps nine was just the number her mind could hold, seated in a crescent of thrones carved from what looked like frozen lightning. They were not what the prayer-candles and the village icons had promised. They were not radiant. They were *tired*. One had her head in her hand. Another was reading a petition with the squint of a man checking a bill he suspected was padded. The largest of them — the one at the center, vast as a hill, wrapped in a robe the color of deep winter — was asleep, his chin on his chest, and his breathing was the slow grinding sound they had been hearing for the last hour, the sound they had taken for the mountain settling.

"The Sleeping Judge," Ilya murmured. "Don't wake him. Whatever happens, whatever they say — don't be the one who wakes him."

The other eight were awake, but awake the way old men are awake: present, and not glad of it. As Ulyana watched, a petition detached itself from the frozen thousands overhead and drifted down like a leaf. A goddess with a face like a cracked bell took it, read it, and spoke a single word. The word fell through the cathedral and somewhere far above, Ulyana knew, it landed in the world as weather. As the death of a cow. As a strange mercy. The gods were answering prayers the way an insolvent merchant answers creditors: at random, from whatever pocket still had anything in it.

"They're not judging us," Sable said softly. She had gone very still, and the blue light of her lamps had gone almost white. "They're judging *themselves*. Look at the docket."

Ulyana looked. Between the thrones stood a pillar of light, and in the light, cases were moving — slowly, one at a time — past the crescent of gods like coins being counted. And every case, every single one the eye could catch before it moved on, was a god's name in the debit column. *Owed: one answered vow, three hundred years past term. Owed: one harvest promised, never delivered. Owed: one child's name, taken as surety, holder deceased, asset unreturned.*

"They defaulted," Ulyana breathed. "The gods defaulted on *us*."

"And the winter," Davor said slowly, "is the repossession."

The word went through her like a cold wire. All her life she had thought of worship as a gift freely given — candles, prayers, the small coins of the desperate. But nothing was freely given, not anywhere, not ever. The old gods had taken the prayers as payment, and the prayers had been *advances* — credit extended by mortals against promises of protection, of harvest, of mercy. Three hundred years of silence. Three hundred years of taking the payments and delivering nothing.

And now the gods had woken into insolvency, and they were liquidating. The winter wasn't wrath. It was *collections*.

Ulyana had spent six years keeping the Gilded Thorn's books, and she knew the feeling that was climbing up her spine. It was the feeling of opening a ledger you trusted and finding, at the bottom of the page, a number that cannot be paid by anyone.

* * *

They crossed the ledger-floor in the shadow of a column, close as they dared to the wall, because Ilya had been explicit: unregistered souls in the court were contraband, and contraband that moved in the open got impounded. Ulyana kept her eyes down, counting her own footsteps the way she counted everything, and it was that habit — the downward eye of a bookkeeper — that saved her from walking past it.

There was a shelf against the column's base.

Not a grand thing. A shelf, of dark stone, like the shelf in Vespera's counting room where the high-value inventory waited under lock. And on the shelf stood reliquaries — glass, hundreds of them, each one the size of a lantern, each one holding a flame.

Not fire. She knew fire. These flames did not flicker, did not lean, did not consume. They *stood* in their glass like small upright souls, which was, she understood with a vertigo that buckled her knees, exactly what they were. Each reliquary bore a tag. Parchment tags, lead tags, tags of hammered gold for the old ones. Names. Dates of pawn. Terms. Interest accrued.

Souls. A wall of pawned souls, shelved like inventory, in the vault of the court beneath the mountain.

"Ulyana." Davor's hand closed on her arm. "Don't stop. Don't—"

But she had already found it.

She would like to say she found it by courage, or by destiny, or by the tug of her own stolen self across the dark. She found it the way she found everything: by reading. Her eyes moved down the shelf the way they moved down a column of figures, fast and merciless, and then stopped, snagged, because the tag on the third reliquary from the end was written in a hand she knew.

Her own handwriting. The handwriting she had never written — the hand from the ledger older than the guild, the page she had found in the vault a lifetime ago, in another life, before fish fell from the sky. *Ulyana Okonkwo. Pawned in utero by holder: Maren Voss, hedge-witch, against debt of 40 crowns and one favor unnamed. Term: upon majority, transfer to under-court. Interest: compounding.*

The reliquary was small. Smaller than the others. The flame inside it was small too — a thin gold sliver of light, standing very straight, the way she stood when Vespera reviewed her columns. It did not flicker. It did not know she was there.

She knew it was her. That was the terrible thing. There was no doubt to hide behind, no hope of being wrong. She knew it the way she knew her own signature. The flame stood the way she stood. It was *patient* the way she was patient. Someone had taken the thing she most essentially was, and shelved it, and it had gone on being herself in the glass, alone, for twenty-three years, waiting for an owner.

Her hand rose before she decided anything. Her fingertips touched the glass.

The flame turned.

Not toward her — *to* her. It leaned, a fraction, the way a candle leans toward a draft, the way a child leans toward a door opening. And cold as the cathedral was, Ulyana felt warmth against her fingertips, faint and stubborn, like a coal remembered rather than felt.

"Oh," she said. It was not a word. It was the sound a ledger makes when it finally balances.

"Ulyana." Davor's voice, low and hard. "Step back from the shelf. *Now.*"

She didn't. She stood there with her hand on the glass of her own soul, and she thought: *I have kept careful books my whole life. I have counted everything. And all this time, I was an entry.*

The vertigo came then, the real one, the one she had been holding off since the page in the vault. Every choice she had ever made — fleeing Maren Voss with the grimoire under her arm, talking her way into the Gilded Thorn, vouching for Davor, hiding Sable, walking down into the dark — all of it had been done by a girl who did not fully own her own yes. The hedge-witch had sold her before she was born. The court had held her since. And the worst of it, the part that made her press her forehead briefly against the cold glass, was that the flame looked *glad* to see her. It had been waiting, and it had not even learned to be bitter about it.

That, she decided, was unforgivable. Not that it had been stolen. That it had stayed kind.

She took her hand off the glass.

"We're taking it," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Not at midwinter. Not at trial. Now, if we can. And if we can't—" She turned and looked at Davor, at Sable, at Ilya, and she felt the fear and the fury fuse into something with a spine. "Then we make them wish they'd shelved a different soul."

"That," said a voice above them, vast and cracked and terribly awake, "can be arranged."

The goddess with the face like a cracked bell was looking down at them from the foot of her throne. She had not crossed the floor. There had been no footsteps. There was only the fact of her attention, and it fell on the four of them like a verdict.

Behind the crescent of thrones, with a grinding like a glacier changing its mind, the Sleeping Judge opened one eye.

* * *

The eye was the size of a lake in winter, and it was looking at Ulyana.

"An auditor," the god said. His voice did not boom; it *settled*, the way snow settles, on everything at once. "Down here. In my court. With her hand on the inventory." The eye narrowed, and somewhere above them, ten thousand frozen petitions shivered in their places. "I have been asleep for three hundred years, and the first thing that wakes me is arithmetic."

Ulyana's mouth was dry. Her legs were water. Her hand, without any permission from her, had gone to her breastbone, to the folded contract over her heart.

Good, she thought. Let him see it. Let him see it coming.

"I've come about an entry," she said.
