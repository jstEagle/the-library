## 17. Audience with the Sleeping Judge

The reliquary hall went quiet in the way that only very old places can go quiet — not silence but *attention*, a thousand frozen petitions leaning inward like a crowd at a hanging.

Ulyana felt it first as pressure behind her eyes, then as a sound: a groan from somewhere above, deep and mineral, the complaint of a mountain shifting its weight. Dust sifted down through the blue light. Somewhere far off, a bailiff-spirit dropped something and did not pick it up.

"Oh," said a voice, "what *now*."

The voice came from the dais at the end of the cathedral, where Ulyana had assumed the great frozen figures were statues. They were not statues. They were gods, seated in judgment of their own debts, and the one in the center — vast as a cliff face, robed in what looked like sedimentary rock layered over eons — was opening one eye.

It was an enormous eye, amber-shot, veined with red like a river delta seen from very high up. It took its time finding them. Davor's hand went to a knife he wasn't carrying anymore; they'd been relieved of everything sharp three halls back. Sable grabbed Ulyana's sleeve with both hands.

The eye found them. The god sighed.

The sigh moved dust across the whole cathedral. It rattled the reliquaries on their shelves. It stirred the small flame that was Ulyana's soul, flickering in its glass, and she had the absurd, vertiginous thought that her own soul had just flinched at a stranger's breath.

"Mortals," the god said. "In my court. Uninvited." He rubbed his face with a hand the size of a hay wagon, grinding stone against stone. "Do you know how long I slept? Do you know what it cost me to sleep that long? And now there's *praying*. Every night. All hours. Do you have any idea what praying sounds like from in here?"

"No," said Ulyana, because apparently her mouth had decided to keep working even though the rest of her wanted to be dead or elsewhere.

"It sounds," said the god, "like someone dragging a nail down the inside of your skull. Three hundred years of beautiful quiet, and then —" he made a noise like an avalanche clearing its throat — "*please, please, please.* Please for rain. Please for my mother's cough. Please let the harvest hold, please let my husband love me, please, please, *please*." He glared at them with both eyes open now, which was considerably worse. "And do you know what the worst part is? The worst part is that we *answer*. We can't help it. It's in the contract."

"What contract?" Davor asked. His voice was steady. His knuckles were not.

"The *contract*, enforcer." The god said the word like he'd been chewing it for centuries without swallowing. "You people wrote it. Or your ancestors did. Before the Silence, when everyone still believed, worship flowed uphill like water — freely, daily, in drops and rivers. Sacrifices. Festivals. First fruits. Songs sung badly at dawn. That was the payment schedule. And in exchange, small miracles, weather cooperation, the occasional smiting. A functioning economy." He gestured, and half the cathedral's blue light dimmed respectfully. "Then your clever mortals invented *other* ways to get what you wanted. Guilds. Brokers. Bottled luck. Borrowed fortune. You built an entire secondary market for grace, and every drop you bought from a broker was a drop that never reached us."

He leaned forward. The dais creaked.

"We were owed, understand? Three centuries of arrears. Interest compounding. And no one came to collect, because who collects from the collectors? So eventually the accounts grew so unbalanced that the ledger — *the actual ledger, the one under this floor* — tipped over. And when the ledger tips, the debts come due all at once. Every prayer gets answered. Every single one. Rain of fish. Snow in harvest month. Your grandmother's rheumatism cured, your neighbor's roof collapsed. We don't choose anymore; we *pay out*. Automatically. Like a dam breaking."

"That's why the winter," Ulyana said slowly. Working it aloud helped. It always had. "It's not a punishment. It's a —"

"A repossession," the god said, with something horribly like relief, the way a man feels relief confessing to a crime. "Thank you. Yes. Finally, someone says it. It's repossession. Worship stopped arriving, so we're taking collateral. Warmth. Growing seasons. The general goodwill of the sky. We'll take it all back if we have to, audit to zero, and then we can *sleep*."

Sable made a small sound. The god's gaze swung to her, and something in his vast face shifted — a flicker of recognition, or near-recognition, the way you squint at a stranger who has your brother's walk.

"You smell like somebody I used to know," he said.

"I get that a lot," said Sable, flat, and Ulyana's heart cracked a little at how practiced the flatness was.

* * *

They were given seats. This was somehow more unnerving than being eaten would have been: three chairs hauled forward by bailiff-spirits, set before the dais at a respectful distance, arranged with the fussy precision of clerks expecting a long meeting. The chairs were carved from black ice and were, Ulyana noted grimly, more comfortable than anything the Gilded Thorn had ever owned.

The chief god — and the lesser gods along the dais had names, she could see them etched into their thrones in letters older than any alphabet she knew, though most of them stayed resolutely asleep, faces turned away like creditors avoiding each other's eyes at a debtors' dinner — the chief god poured himself something from a jug the size of a well. It steamed. It smelled like thunderstorms.

"Do you want some?" he asked. "It's melted glacier. It's all we have. Everything else is spoken for."

"No, thank you," Ulyana managed.

"Suit yourself." He drank, and the drinking took a while, and during it Ulyana had time to look around properly. Above them, the frozen petitions hung in their thousands, each one a prayer caught mid-flight, glassed and shelved like the guild's inventory. She recognized the format instantly. She had spent six years cataloguing that exact format by candlelight.

"You've been *warehousing* prayers," she said.

"Of course. Where else would unanswered petitions go?" The god wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, causing a minor seismic event. "Your guild learned the practice from us, if it matters. We taught your founders everything. Storage, valuation, interest accrual, the ethics of selling what isn't strictly yours." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, but it was a tired one, and tiredness, Ulyana found, was harder to fear than menace. "We were the first brokers. Before there were mortals clever enough to intermediate, the gods ran the whole trade ourselves. Prayer in, miracle out. Very simple. Very clean." His face darkened, geologically. "Then your kind discovered *leverage*."

"Leverage," Davor repeated.

"Borrowed belief. Prayed-for outcomes secured against future devotion. Mortals promising us worship they hadn't earned yet, spending faith they didn't have, rolling the debt into new contracts before the old ones matured." He counted off on fingers thick as bridge pylons. "Your guild's founding charter is a refinance agreement. Did Vespera ever tell you that? Did she ever tell you what the Gilded Thorn actually is?"

Ulyana's stomach dropped through the ice floor and kept going.

"The Thorn is our collection agency," the god said. "Chartered by this court, three hundred years ago, to manage the transition after the Silence began. To keep the accounts tidy while we slept. To sell the impossible things — memories, luck, honest lies — at a markup, and remit a percentage uphill." He spread his hands. "The remittances stopped coming eighty years ago."

The word landed in Ulyana like a ledger slammed shut.

Eighty years. Vespera had been guildmother for sixty-two. Before her, old Magda Thorn, whose name was on the charter. Before Magda, a founder whose name nobody living remembered, because the oldest ledger didn't record names so much as *positions*.

"She's been skimming," Davor said quietly. "For decades."

"She's been *surviving*," the god corrected, but without much heat, and Ulyana heard in the correction something she'd heard in her own voice often enough: a bookkeeper defending a number she hated. "Skimming implies malice. What she's done is more ordinary than that. She paid the court what she could, and when she couldn't, she paid it in other coin. Collateral. Souls pawned against future remittance." He looked directly at Ulyana, and she felt the look land on the exact spot between her shoulder blades where she kept her worst knowledge. "You've read the page. You know how the arrangement works."

"My soul," Ulyana said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Was sold by a hedge-witch who forged my apprenticeship papers, to a guild that was itself a front for a court of sleeping gods who are now repossessing the weather because humanity stopped tithing." She paused. "Is that an accurate summary of the accounts?"

For a long moment the god said nothing. Then he laughed — a short, rusty, genuinely startled laugh, like a door opening in a house long shut — and two of the lesser gods stirred in their sleep at the sound.

"Accurate," he said. "Yes. You'd make a fine auditor, girl. Come to think of it—" and here he leaned forward again, and the humor drained out of him like light from a closing door, "—that's rather the problem, isn't it. You already are one. Your whole life, every sum you ever balanced, every entry you ever posted — that was court work. You've been working for us since the day you signed on at the Thorn. You just weren't told your employer's name."

The reliquary with her soul sat on its shelf thirty feet away, small and golden and burning steadily, indifferent to all of it. Ulyana looked at it and felt the vertigo come back, the sense of standing on a page and realizing the page was balanced on nothing.

"So what happens now," Davor said. Not a question. An enforcer's question — *state the terms*.

"Now," the god said, "we wait for midwinter's end. When the last snow falls, all outstanding accounts settle automatically. Pawned souls revert to the court. Debts convert. Including yours." He nodded at Ulyana, almost apologetically, which was the strangest cruelty yet. "Unless, of course, the account is shown to be irregular. Mis-filed. Forged. Entered in error." His eye glittered, and Ulyana realized — with the particular electric jolt she got whenever a column refused to foot, whenever the numbers almost worked but not quite — that he was telling her something. Deliberately. Carelessly, the way powerful beings are careless, but deliberately.

"Court procedure does allow for contested claims," he added, examining his nails, which were quartz. "If a debtor believes an entry is invalid, she may petition for review. In writing. With documentation. Prior to settlement." He shrugged, a shrug that loosened a glacier somewhere. "Nobody ever reads the fine print. That's rather been the whole tragedy of the last four thousand years. Nobody on either side reads it."

* * *

They were escorted out — politely, which was worse than chains — to a cell-block carved into the mountain's root, three rooms of smooth salt-glass with doors that locked themselves and windows that showed nothing but blue dark. Their escort, a bailiff-spirit with the face of a bored customs official, informed them that review hearings required petitioners to remain "within the jurisdiction," that meals would consist of "whatever the mountain has too much of," and that the petitioner should "come prepared."

"Prepared for what?" Ulyana asked.

"For your hearing," said the spirit, already leaving. "Bring your documents. All of them. Originals preferred. The judge hates copies." It paused at the corridor's turn. "Also, he hates being woken early, hates wet boots, and hates being thanked. If you must speak, speak in numbers. He trusts numbers. Numbers are the only thing either side never lied about."

Then it was gone, footsteps fading like sand through an hourglass, and the three of them stood in their salt-lit cells looking at each other through walls gone translucent with cold.

Davor spoke first. "Documents."

"The contract," Ulyana said. Her mind was already moving, fast, the way it moved over a ledger at month's end, line by line, hunting the discrepancy. "The original. My apprenticeship papers. Whatever Vespera holds from the hedge-witch — bills of sale, transfer records, the charter itself if we can get it." She pressed her palm flat against the wall between her cell and Sable's, and Sable pressed hers back. "Every piece of paper that touches my name. Because if there's a flaw — if that woman sold a soul she never legally held —"

"Then the account is irregular," Sable whispered. She'd understood faster than either of them. Of course she had; she was a god's daughter raised mortal, fluent in exactly this kind of arithmetic. "And irregular accounts don't settle."

"They get audited," Ulyana said.

Somewhere above them, muffled by a mile of stone, the chief god rolled over and went back to sleep, and the snow outside — the endless, terrible, answering snow — fell on and fell on, paying out the world one flake at a time, like a creditor emptying a vault it intended to leave bare.

Ulyana sat down on the salt floor, put her back to the wall, and began, in her head, to draft the petition.

She would need the originals.

She would need Vespera.

And she had until midwinter's end to get them.
