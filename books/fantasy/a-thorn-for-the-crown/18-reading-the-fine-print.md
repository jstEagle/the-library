## 18. Reading the Fine Print

They gave us a room.

Not a cell — a room, which in the under-court was apparently a distinction that mattered to the gods' sense of their own mercy. It sat off a corridor of the petition-cathedral, behind a door of black wood carved with scales, and it contained three narrow beds, a basin of water that refilled itself out of spite or habit, and a desk.

The desk was the important part.

It had been Sable's idea, delivered in her flat, stubborn way while the bailiff-spirits were still deciding what to do with us. "You can't audit a soul from a cell," she'd said, arms crossed, blue light still fading from her veins where the court had recognized her father's blood. "You need paper. You need light. You need her to think."

*Her*, meaning me. Meaning the auditor. Meaning the girl who had once balanced a guild's books on candlelight and believed, with a faith I had never examined, that every problem was a sum waiting for the right column.

The desk had paper. The desk had ink. The desk had a lamp that burned without oil, because everything down here burned without oil, and I tried not to think about what fuel fed a flame beneath a mountain.

Davor stood at the door with his arms folded, doing the thing he did where he pretended to be furniture. He was very good at it. Six feet of scarred enforcer, and he could make himself as noticeable as a coat rack when he wanted to, which was whenever someone might come looking.

Sable sat on the windowsill — there were no windows, but there was a niche that glowed faintly, and she had claimed it within an hour, the way cats claim things — and watched me work.

I spread the contract out flat.

* * *

You have to understand what the contract was.

It was not paper. Paper would have been a kindness. It was skin — vellum scraped from something I chose not to identify, thin enough to read through when I held it against the lamp, and the words were not written on it so much as grown into it, the way frost grows on glass. My name was in it. Not the letters U-L-Y-A-N-A, but the *weight* of my name, the way my name felt in the mouth of someone saying it over my cradle, pressed into the material like a thumbprint into wax.

Three centuries of contracts lived in the cathedral's archive, and the hedge-witch's deed of sale sat among them like a stone in a shoe. I had demanded to see it. To everyone's surprise, including mine, they had let me. The Sleeping Judge had even yawned and said something about *due process being the only prayer mortals still pay on time*, and then gone back to sleep with his chin on his fist, and a clerk-thing with too many elbows had fetched the document and left me alone with it.

Alone with it. That was the mistake. That was always the mistake, if you were the kind of creature who thought a contract was finished once it was signed.

I read it the way Vespera had taught me to read a ledger: first for the shape of it, then for the numbers, then — third pass, slowest, the one that mattered — for what wasn't there.

First pass. Shape. A deed of sale, one soul, from one Margit Voss, hedge-witch of the Ashfall Road, to the Office of Outstanding Claims, Court Beneath the Mountain. Consideration rendered: three favors, unspecified, redeemable at will. Term: in perpetuity. My stomach did its old trick of turning to cold lead at the word *perpetuity*. Perpetuity was a word people used when they didn't want you to do arithmetic on it.

Second pass. Numbers. Three favors, unspecified. Unspecified consideration was bad law anywhere above ground — Vespera had drilled that into me my first month: *an unpriced asset isn't sold, girl, it's stolen with paperwork* — but down here the gods ran on older customs, and maybe ambiguity was legal under mountain rules. I set it aside. Flagged, not fatal.

Third pass.

I read it again. And again. The lamp burned steady. Somewhere far off, the cathedral hummed with frozen petitions, ten thousand prayers caught mid-word in ice and glass, and Davor breathed at the door like a patient wall.

And there it was.

Not in the body of the contract. In the *recitals* — the little paragraph at the top, the throat-clearing before the song, the part nobody reads because nothing ever happens in it. *Whereas the said Margit Voss holds lawful title to the soul of the apprentice named below—*

Lawful title.

Held lawful title.

I put both hands flat on the desk to stop them shaking.

"Ulyana." Davor's voice, low. He'd noticed. Of course he'd noticed; he'd spent twenty years reading debtors' faces across collection tables, and I must have looked like a woman watching her own gallows turn into a door. "What is it?"

"The apprenticeship," I said. My voice came out strange, thin, like someone else speaking through me. "She filed papers. When she took me in. Every hedge-witch files them — it's how they bind an apprentice, it's how they're allowed to teach the craft, it's—" I stopped. Started again, slower. "It's how they're allowed to own the labor. The apprenticeship papers are the root of title. No papers, no ownership. No ownership—"

"No sale," Davor said.

He crossed the room in four steps and stood over the desk, and for the first time since we'd come down here, the wall of him bent forward, interested.

"You didn't sign anything," he said. "When you were a child. Tell me you didn't sign anything."

"I was seven." I laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "I was seven years old and she gave me a slate to practice my letters and told me to copy out a passage as penmanship exercise. I thought it was scripture. I thought it was *homework*." My finger found the line in the recital, traced the frost-grown words. "But that's not the point. Whether I signed or smeared porridge on it doesn't matter. Look at the date. Look at the filing date on the apprenticeship record she cited as proof of title."

Sable slid off the sill and came to look over my shoulder. Up close, the divine blood in her made the air around her feel like the moment before lightning.

"The apprenticeship papers are dated four years after this contract," she said. She read faster than anyone I'd ever met; it was the god-blood, maybe, or just her. "The contract cites them. Cites papers that didn't exist yet."

"She backdated the citation," I said. "Or misdated it. Or — no. No, look closer." I held the vellum to the lamp again, tilting it, and the frost-words threw their shadows long, and there — faint, the way a palimpsest shows through — the original date ghosting under the correction. "She altered it. She wrote this contract *before* the apprenticeship existed, citing the apprenticeship as her source of title, and then went back and fudged the date when the clerks questioned it. But she fudged it badly. She fudged it in a hurry. Because by the time she brought this here, I'd already run away."

Silence. The lamp burned. The basin refilled itself with a small, smug sound.

"So the chain of title breaks," Davor said slowly, tasting each word like a man testing ice. "She never lawfully owned you. You can't pawn what you don't hold."

"You can't *sell* what you don't hold," I corrected, and the auditor in me stood up straight inside my chest for the first time in days. "Which means the consideration is void. Which means the contract is void. Which means—"

My voice cracked. I hated it. I kept going anyway.

"Which means my soul has been sitting in a reliquary in your cathedral for eleven years under a defective instrument, and every favor she drew against it, and every interest payment this court booked against my account, was booked against an asset that was never validly conveyed."

Sable made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a cry. "You're going to audit them."

"I'm going to *read them the fine print*," I said, "in front of the entire court, and then I'm going to watch a god's face when he realizes his office has been holding fraudulent paper for a decade."

* * *

Here is what literacy buys you that money never will: the ability to catch a lie in its own handwriting.

I worked until the lamp dimmed — and down here, when a lamp dimmed, it meant the court wanted you to sleep, which told you something about whose house you were in — and then I worked some more by memory, reconstructing dates in the margins with charcoal stubs I'd charmed off a clerk-thing by promising to teach it double-entry bookkeeping. Everything has a price. Mine is cheap.

The problems revealed themselves the way problems always do: patiently, one at a time, like debtors forming a queue.

One: precedent. The Sleeping Judge had already dismissed my earlier objection — the forged signature, the coerced consent — with the magnificent indifference of an institution that had been winning arguments since before my language had grammar. *Precedent outweighs truth.* He'd said it kindly, almost gently, the way you tell a child that winter lasts as long as it lasts. A technical flaw in the instrument might fare better than a moral flaw in the transaction, but it might not. Courts love their precedents the way dragons love their hoards: irrationally, fatally, to the death.

Two: the trial was set for midwinter's end. When the long winter finished falling. When, if the judge was to be believed, all outstanding souls reverted to the court automatically — a mass foreclosure, cosmic in scale, with my little flame among the lot. Even a void contract might be swept up in a general reversion. I needed the flaw established *before* midwinter's end, not argued after.

Three: Margit. My mistress. Somewhere between the burned cottage and this mountain, ahead of us the whole way, drawing her three unspecified favors like a woman with a bottomless purse. If she learned I'd found the flaw, she'd have eleven years of mountain custom and three open favors to patch it with. Unspecified consideration cut both ways — it was voidable law above ground, but down here it might be *renegotiable* law, and renegotiation was exactly what a cornered witch with a god's ear would attempt.

So. Speed. Precision. And an audience.

Because here was the fourth problem, the one I turned over last, in the dark, listening to Davor breathe and Sable dream in a language with too many vowels:

A perfect argument presented privately dies privately. The court could simply amend the record, quietly, the way guilds amended ledgers, and hand me a corrected contract, and I would have traded one owner's paper for another's. No. The flaw had to be read aloud. Filed formally. Entered into the public account where amendment became scandal and scandal became leverage. Vespera had taught me that too, though she'd have flayed me for the use I was putting it to: *shame is the only collateral a bureaucracy truly fears.*

I lay in the narrow bed and listened to the cathedral hum its ten thousand interrupted prayers, and I thought about my mistress's kitchen, and the slate, and my seven-year-old hand copying out what I thought was holy writ in careful letters, being graded on my penmanship while signing away the only thing I owned outright.

She had taught me my letters. That was the joke of it. That was the whole joke, the one I would carry up the mountain and into the courtroom like a lit brand: she taught me to read so that I could transcribe her grimoires, and I used the reading to find the seam in her work, and the seam in her work was the seam in the world.

Hope is a strange arithmetic. It doesn't add. It multiplies — small certainties compounding in the dark until suddenly you have enough of them to stand on.

I had one certainty now, and it weighed more than a soul in glass.

* * *

In the morning — morning being a courtesy the lamps extended to us — a clerk-thing with too many elbows came to fetch our breakfast, which was bread that tasted faintly of petitions, and found me already at the desk, documents squared, charcoal sharpened, margins annotated in a fair hand.

It paused. Its elbows rearranged themselves into something like curiosity.

"You require the form," it said. Not a question. Things down here could smell intention the way sharks smell blood, and I had evidently been marinating in it all night.

"What form?" Davor asked, wary, moving off the door.

"The form for contestation of instrument," the clerk said. "Prior to reckoning. It exists." A pause, and then, in a tone I can only describe as bureaucratic mischief, the driest voice I'd heard since Vespera's: "It has existed for nine hundred years. No mortal has ever filed it. We keep it dusted."

Sable laughed out loud, bright and startled, and the niche behind her flared blue.

"Then get it," I said, and my voice did not shake at all. "Dusted, please. And bring me the court's schedule of proceedings for midwinter's eve."

The clerk's elbows rippled. "Why?"

"Because," I said, dipping my charcoal, "someone in this cathedral has been booking interest on an asset they never legally acquired, and I intend to say so out loud, in front of the judge, with citations."

The clerk looked at me for a long moment. Then it bowed — a deep, elaborate, multi-elbowed bow that I suspected, in the dialect of under-court etiquette, amounted to applause — and scuttled off down the humming corridor, and the sound of its going was almost exactly the sound of a gavel.
