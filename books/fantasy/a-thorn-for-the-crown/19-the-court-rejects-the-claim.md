## 19. The Court Rejects the Claim

Ulyana had spent her whole life believing that the truth, once written down plainly enough, could not be argued with.

She had believed it in the hedge-witch's kitchen, when she was nine and learning that two plus two made four no matter how much the witch insisted it made whatever the witch wanted. She had believed it over ten years of ledgers at the Gilded Thorn, where a column that summed true was a column that summed true, and no guildmother's frown could change arithmetic. She had built her entire self on the faith that if you could only get the numbers into the open — all of them, every forged line and hidden entry — then anyone with eyes would have to concede.

The Sleeping Judge looked at her flawless evidence for the length of one long breath, and dismissed it.

"That," he said, "is not a flaw. That is a footnote."

He did not even sit up to say it. He reclined on his throne of frozen petitions — thousands upon thousands of prayers turned to ice and stacked into something between a chair and a glacier — and he examined his own fingernails while Ulyana stood before him holding the hedge-witch's contract like a writ of liberation.

"Your Honor." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The apprenticeship papers were forged. Vespera Underhill has admitted as much. If Marguerite Okonkwo—" she would not use the witch's name; she used her own mother's name instead, the name the witch had given her mother's daughter, and let the court make of that what it would "—if Marguerite Okonkwo never legally owned my soul, then she could not pawn it. The contract is void ab initio. The asset reverts to me."

"Void ab initio." The judge tasted the phrase like an old sweet. "You've been reading our procedure manuals, little auditor."

"Ilya Voss memorized three of them on his way down the mountain. I read four."

That earned her a flicker of interest from the gallery of lesser gods — small deities of thresholds and chimney-smoke and the first frost, sitting in tiers like creditors at a bankruptcy hearing. Which, Ulyana reflected bitterly, was exactly what they were.

The judge sat up. It took a while. He was vast and gray and moved like a mountain deciding to be inconvenienced, and when he finally leaned forward, the cold coming off him frosted Ulyana's eyelashes.

"Let me tell you about precedent," he said. "Three hundred years ago, your guild's founders came to this court with a ledger and a proposition: that worship could be securitized. That prayer could be bottled, sold, resold, and collateralized against souls. We laughed. Then we checked their figures." His eyes, when they found hers, were the color of deep ice over deep water. "They were correct. Every figure. Your Gilded Thorn has run three centuries of perfect books, girl, and this court honors perfect books. The contract for your soul is entered in the oldest ledger, under seal, witnessed by two gods who have since been forgotten so thoroughly they no longer exist to be cross-examined. You are asking me to unwind a valid instrument because one signature on one ancillary document was false."

"The ancillary document established ownership!"

"And the court's own records establish transfer." He gestured, and a page of frozen prayer peeled itself off his throne and drifted down to hover before her. She saw the entry in the old script: *Received of the Gilded Thorn, one soul, unencumbered, per contract.* "Unencumbered, it says. Certified by your guildmother's own hand. Precedent outweighs truth, auditor. Truth is what happened. Precedent is what we agreed to pretend happened, consistently, until pretending became the law. A court that unwinds its precedents every time someone brings a truer truth is not a court. It is a market stall."

Davor shifted behind her. She heard the leather of his coat creak, the only sound in the cathedral of frozen petitions besides the slow tick of the judge's attention.

"There is another way to put it," Ulyana said. "A court that hides behind precedent when the truth is standing right here is not a court either. It's a debtor hiding from its creditors."

The gallery went very still.

Somewhere in the tiers, a god of door-hinges laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered. The judge's expression did not change, but the temperature dropped five degrees, and Ulyana understood that she had just done something either very stupid or very memorable.

"You have spirit," the judge said. "I'll note it in the margin. Here is what I will do, because your figures were also correct, and I honor correct figures. I will grant you a trial."

Hope leapt in her chest like a struck match.

"Midwinter's end," the judge continued, and the match went out. "When the long winter finishes falling. When the last snowflake of the season lands, every soul currently held in default by this court reverts to the crown. All outstanding accounts settle at once. Your flame, the child Ondine's, the forty-one others in the reliquary vault — all of it, gathered in, and the gods sleep again for another three hundred years, fed and fat on what you people failed to pay any other way."

"And if I win the trial?"

"Then your soul is returned before the settling, unowned, and you may go home to your candlelit ledgers and live out a mortal life arguing with tax collectors." He smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was an honest one, which somehow made it worse. "If you lose, everything proceeds as written. Trial begins at midwinter's end. Court adjourned."

He lay back down. Just like that. A god of cosmic bookkeeping, pulling a glacier over himself like a blanket, and the audience chamber began to empty of divinities the way a courtroom empties after a disappointing verdict — slowly, in murmuring clusters, none of them looking at the mortals.

* * *

They were escorted to cells. Of course they were. Glass cells, in a corridor of glass cells, each one exactly large enough for a bedroll of frozen moss and a person's regrets. Ulyana's cell faced Sable's across the corridor, and Davor's beyond hers, and through the blue-tinted walls she watched Davor lower himself onto the moss with the careful movements of a man whose knees remembered the toll-wights' bridge all too well.

"They took three days," he'd said afterward, shrugging, as if he'd given up a hat. Three days of a life, paid to things made of salt and hunger, and he hadn't told them until the passage was bought and irreversible.

She was still furious about it. She filed the fury away for later, in the drawer where she kept things too big to feel yet. Right now there was room only for the verdict.

She pressed her palm flat against the glass. Cold seeped in, but the cell held — held her, held her breath, held the shape of her. Like a bottle. Like inventory.

*Everything is owed.* The guild's iron rule. She had thought it meant debts between people, tracked and settled and forgiven. She had never considered that the rule ran upward too. That somewhere, the guild itself was owed nothing and owed everything, and she was a line item.

Through the wall of her cell she could see, far down the corridor where it opened into the great vault, the reliquary racks: hundreds of glass vessels, each holding a small burning light. Somewhere among them was a flame the exact color of her — she didn't know how she knew, but she knew, the way you know your own handwriting. Her soul. Sitting on a shelf. Catalogued.

She had spent ten years keeping other people's property safe in ledgers. Now she was the entry.

"Hey." Sable's voice came faintly through the glass, the way voices travel in places built for silence. The girl sat cross-legged in her own cell, knees drawn up, watching Ulyana with those eyes that burned faintly blue when lamps were near. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes all ledgered. Where you turn yourself into columns." Sable hugged her knees. "My father did that too, before he traded us away. Turned himself into entries. Said it hurt less."

Ulyana's throat tightened. "It does hurt less. That's the problem. Numbers don't grieve, so you become numbers."

"So don't."

"It's not that simple—"

"My father said that too. 'It's not that simple.' Gods love that phrase. It means *I'm about to do something terrible and I want it to sound complicated.*" Sable unfolded herself and crossed to her own glass wall, pressing her small brown hand flat against it, mirroring Ulyana. Between their palms: six inches of divine architecture. "You found the flaw. Nobody else has ever found a flaw. The vendor in the bazaar said so — said nobody reads contracts anymore, they just sign. You read. So we lost today, but you *read*, and that means we're going to do something nobody's ever done, and I want to be there when it happens."

"We're in jail, Sable."

"We're in *inventory*," Sable corrected. "There's a difference. Inventory gets moved. Inventory gets counted. And counting—" she smiled, and for a second Ulyana saw the god in her face, the forgotten father's blood showing through like lamp-light through skin "—counting is what you're *for*."

* * *

Night, or what passed for it beneath the mountain — a dimming of the ever-blue light, a lengthening of shadows along the corridor of glass. Ulyana lay on her bedroll of frozen moss and listened to the court breathe. It breathed like a building full of sleeping creditors: slow, heavy, patient. Somewhere above, she knew, snow was falling on the city. Snow in harvest month. The long winter, falling toward midwinter's end, toward the settling, toward the moment when every outstanding account closed at once and the gods slept fat.

Forty-three days, by her count. She had counted before the trial, during it, and now after it, because counting was what she was for, and because the alternative was feeling.

In the dark, she allowed herself one minute of despair. She timed it. Sixty seconds exactly, during which she let herself understand fully what the day had cost: that the flaw was real but worthless, that truth was a currency this court did not accept, that her whole life — the flight from the witch, the grimoire, the careful years at the Thorn, the friends she had chosen one stubborn act at a time — all of it might close out at midwinter's end like a bad position in a falling market. Sixty seconds. Then she sat up, wiped her face on her sleeve, and got back to work, because despair was a debt like any other and she refused to carry it past due.

Think, she told herself. The judge had honored correct figures. He had said so twice. *Precedent outweighs truth* — fine. But precedent was just old transactions, and transactions had terms, and terms had edges, and edges were where auditors lived.

The guild had defaulted first. She didn't know it yet, not as fact — she only knew the shape of the hole in the court's own accounts, the way you know a missing column by the gap it leaves in a sum. But she was beginning to suspect, lying in a glass cell beneath a mountain while winter fell on everyone she loved, that the judge's beautiful precedent rested on a foundation the court itself had falsified. That the gods had stopped being worshipped not because mortals grew careless but because the gods had stopped honoring their side of the oldest contract of all.

And if that was true — if the first default belonged to heaven—

Then precedent wasn't stone. Precedent was a ledger. And every ledger could be audited.

She lay back down. Through the blue glass, across the corridor, Sable was asleep with her hand still pressed to the wall, as if even dreaming, she refused to let go of the person on the other side.

Ulyana reached up and pressed her own palm to the glass opposite the girl's. It was a foolish gesture. It proved nothing. The glass was cold and thick and divine and entirely indifferent.

But she kept it there anyway, and slept that way, and dreamed of sums that finally balanced.

* * *

Morning — or the court's version of it — arrived with footsteps.

Not bailiff-spirits. Not the whisper of wights or the rattle of chains. Footsteps she knew: measured, deliberate, wearing down one heel slightly more than the other because of an old injury Davor had never explained. Except these footsteps stopped at the wrong cell. They stopped at the end of the corridor, where a fourth cell stood — the one holding Ondine, the child whose soul had been pawned like Ulyana's, the reason Davor had doubled back during the escape, replaying his oldest choice and making it right at last.

"Davor Kestrel." A voice like dry paper. Ulyana knew it instantly, and her stomach dropped through the floor of her cell, through the mountain, through the world.

Ilya.

She heard him stop outside Davor's cell. Heard the long silence between two men who had been partners, then strangers, then betrayer-and-forgiven, then something neither of them had words for.

"You look terrible," Ilya said at last.

"Three days shorter than I used to be," Davor answered. "Costs add up."

"So I hear. Toll-wights. In *my* tunnels." A pause. "You always did take the expensive roads."

"I always did take the right ones."

Another silence, longer. Ulyana sat up in her cell, straining, watching through the blue glass as two shapes confronted each other at the far end of the corridor — one broad and battered, one lean as a collection notice.

"Why are you here, Ilya?" Davor asked finally. "To mark our route again? To file a report? We're already in cells. There's nowhere left to mark us to."

"I'm here because the trial date moved." Paper rustled. "Midwinter's end isn't forty-three days out anymore. The winter accelerated. The judge pulled the settling forward — twelve days, Kestrel. Twelve days until every soul in that vault reverts to the crown, including yours, including the auditor's, including the kid's. And the court's advocates are already preparing the case, and they're good, and your little flaw-witch—" a dry pause, acknowledging Ulyana without looking at her "—is going to lose, unless someone who knows the court's procedures from the inside decides that some collections shouldn't be collected."

Ulyana's breath stopped.

"I memorized the procedures," Ilya said quietly. "All of them. Thirty years, Kestrel. I know every loophole in this mountain better than the gods do, because the gods haven't read their own rules since before your grandmother was born. I can't undo what I marked. I can't give you back the days the wights took. But I can stand next to you in twelve days and watch you tear this court's accounts open line by line — and maybe, if the auditor over there is half as good as her reputation, help her find the sum that doesn't balance."

Silence. The kind of silence that follows a man laying his whole career, his freedom, and possibly his soul onto a table.

"You quit," Davor said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Ilya said. "I finally collected on the only debt that ever mattered. Mine."

Across the corridor, Ulyana saw Sable rise slowly to her feet, hand pressed again to the glass, eyes gone wide and blue. And Ulyana felt the despair of yesterday shift, recalculate, roll forward into something new — not hope exactly, hope was too soft a word — but a deadline. Twelve days. An audit with a hard close.

Twelve days to find the flaw in heaven's books.

She pressed her forehead briefly against the cool glass, closed her eyes, and began, silently, methodically, to count.
