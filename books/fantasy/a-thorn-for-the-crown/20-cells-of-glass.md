## 20. Cells of Glass

They kept Ulyana in a cell made of glass that was not glass.

She had tested it the first hour — fingernail, hairpin, the heel of her boot — and found it gave slightly under pressure, like the skin on cooled milk, and then refused entirely. The walls were warm. That was the worst part. Not cold like a dungeon, not rough like stone. Warm and faintly yielding, the way something living might be if it were very patient and did not mind being a wall.

The cell was perhaps six feet square. There was no door she could see, though one must exist, because twice now a bailiff-spirit had oozed through the wall to leave a tray of food that smelled like bread and tasted like remembering bread. There was no window. There was light anyway, sourceless, the color of late afternoon in a city you were homesick for.

There was a bed, of sorts: a shelf of the same warm glass, softened with something like moss. And there was silence — until, on what she judged was the second day, someone knocked.

Three taps. Pause. Two taps.

Ulyana sat up so fast her neck cracked.

The wall to her left. She pressed her palm against it. The warmth pulsed once beneath her hand, almost like a greeting.

She knew this code. Of course she knew it. Every ledger-keeper who had ever worked the Gilded Thorn's night desk knew it, because Vespera insisted the vault staff learn it for exactly this situation — trapped behind a jammed warded door, unable to shout, needing to say *I am here* and *how many are with you* and *count backward from ten while I fetch the key*. Davor had learned it in his enforcement days; Sable had learned it from Ulyana herself over three evenings of stolen tea, as a game.

Three taps. Two taps. Then, after a pause: four. One.

*Davor,* she thought. Four-one was the old signal for *situation stable, do not act rashly.* Which was such a Davor thing to tap through a wall that she laughed out loud, alone, in a glass cell beneath a mountain, and the laugh came out wet.

She answered: two taps. Three. One. *I am here. Others present. I am well.*

A pause. Then five taps, slow and deliberate, spaced like a man choosing each word.

Five was not in the code. Five meant nothing. She stared at the wall.

Then she understood. Five taps, evenly spaced — he wasn't sending a signal. He was starting a sentence. He wanted to talk. Actually talk, in the only language they had left, which was rhythm.

Ulyana settled against the warm wall, drew her knees up, and answered with the only honest thing the code contained: one long press of her whole palm, held until her skin ached. *Listening.*

And Davor Kestrel, disgraced enforcer, began — at the speed of a man tapping out a confession one letter at a time through half an inch of enchanted glass — to tell her about the child.

*

He told it slowly. The code they'd adapted had no alphabet, strictly speaking, so he'd invented one as he went: taps for common words, sequences for others, and when he lacked a word he would spell it out in a counting system she recognized from guild inventory tallies — number the letters, tap the numbers, let her assemble them. It was agonizing. It took hours. It was the most intimate conversation of her life.

His partner had been Ilya. This she knew. But she had never known the shape of the debt that broke him.

It started, he said, with a widow in the Salt Quarter who had pawned her luck — actual luck, a measurable quantity, the kind of trade the Gilded Thorn handled weekly without anyone losing sleep. Her luck had come due. The under-court's collectors had come for the collateral, which was not the luck itself but its substitute: her son's name. Names were currency down here. A child's name, unclaimed, could be spent on anything.

The widow had hidden the boy. The court's arithmetic did not care. Debt finds its level, like water. If the named party could not be produced, the debt transferred to kin, and the widow had no other kin, so the court had simply begun to erase her instead — slowly, politely, a memory at a time. By the time Davor and Ilya arrived, the woman had already forgotten her own wedding day. She greeted them at the door with the smile of someone meeting pleasant strangers and offered them tea.

The boy was in the cellar. Seven years old. He had scratched a tally into the wall — days since his mother last said his name — and the tally had passed forty.

Davor had been ordered to collect. Standard procedure: take the name, file the transfer, escort the asset below. Clean. Humane, even, by the court's standards — better the boy serve a term in the archives than his mother dissolve into forgetting entirely. The books balanced either way. That phrase, *the books balance*, had been Ilya's whole philosophy, and Ilya had said it that day too, standing in the cellar doorway, hand already extended for the writ.

And Davor — who had collected for eleven years, who had taken names and years and memories and once, memorably, a man's shadow — had looked at the tally marks on the cellar wall and understood that he was looking at a ledger, and that every ledger he had ever respected was built on entries like this one, small ones, tidy ones, entries where the columns added up because somebody had decided whose life was the rounding error.

He had torn up the writ.

Not dramatically. That was the detail he tapped out longest, slower than all the rest, as if his fingers needed her to get it exactly right. He hadn't thrown a punch. He hadn't run. He had sat down on the cellar steps next to the boy, taken out his knife, and cut the name out of the contract — the physical name, inked in the court's script — and eaten it.

Swallowed it. So that it existed nowhere the court could seize, lodged in the body of a man who had decided, in that moment, that some columns should not balance.

It had cost him everything. His commission, his pension, his standing — cashiered in front of Ilya, marched out of the Salt Quarter in irons, formally struck from the rolls. And it had cost the boy nothing, which was the point, and saved the widow nothing, because the court simply resumed erasing her, which was the thing Davor had never managed to stop grieving. He had saved the child and failed the mother and broken himself, and the books still didn't balance, and he had learned that sometimes they weren't supposed to.

The last thing he tapped, before exhaustion or the glass stopped him, was a sequence she had to reconstruct letter by letter:

*I-N-E-V-E-R T-O-L-D Y-O-U B-E-C-A-U-S-E I-T I-S-N-T A S-T-O-R-Y T-H-A-T M-A-K-E-S M-E L-O-O-K L-I-K-E A M-A-N W-H-O C-H-O-S-E R-I-G-H-T. I-T I-S A S-T-O-R-Y A-B-O-U-T A M-A-N W-H-O C-H-O-S-E L-A-T-E. E-L-E-V-E-N Y-E-A-R-S L-A-T-E.*

Ulyana pressed her forehead against the warm glass.

*Y-O-U C-H-O-S-E F-I-R-S-T,* she tapped back.

A long silence. Then, softly: *N-O. I H-A-D A C-O-I-N A-N-D A K-N-I-F-E A-N-D N-O P-L-A-N. T-H-A-T I-S N-O-T T-H-E S-A-M-E A-S C-H-O-O-S-I-N-G F-I-R-S-T.*

*Y-E-S I-T I-S.*

She felt rather than heard the laugh — a shiver in the glass, transmitted through whatever the walls were made of, arriving against her palm like a pulse.

From the far side, fainter, a third set of taps joined in. Sable. She had been listening the whole time. She sent only one line:

*M-Y F-A-T-H-E-R S-O-L-D U-S B-E-F-O-R-E W-E W-E-R-E B-O-R-N. I W-O-U-L-D H-A-V-E E-A-T-E-N T-H-E N-A-M-E T-O-O.*

Then, after a pause, smaller: *T-E-L-L M-E A-B-O-U-T H-E-R. T-H-E B-O-Y. I-S H-E —*

Davor answered before she finished: *G-R-O-W-N. I F-I-N-D H-I-M O-N-C-E A Y-E-A-R. H-E B-U-Y-S M-E A D-R-I-N-K. H-E T-H-I-N-K-S I D-O-N-T K-N-O-W H-E K-N-O-W-S W-H-Y.*

Ulyana closed her eyes. Somewhere above them winter was finishing its fall, and somewhere beyond the walls the Sleeping Judge was dreaming of debts, and here, in three cells of warm glass, three people who had been strangers a season ago lay with their palms flat against a shared wall, telling each other the true shape of their losses at the speed of a heartbeat.

It was, she thought, the strangest audit she had ever conducted: no columns, no sums, just three accounts opened all the way down to their originals.

*

On the fourth day — or what she believed was the fourth day; the light never changed, and time down here was clearly another form of collateral — the trays changed.

The bread stopped tasting of remembering bread and started tasting of ash. The water arrived in cups etched with a seal she recognized from her own contract: the sigil of the trial. And with the evening tray came a slip of paper, thin as onion skin, on which stood a single line in the court's looping script:

**Trial convenes at midwinter's end. The accused may prepare a defense. The court reminds the accused that precedent is a form of mercy, and mercy is finite.**

Ulyana read it twice. Then she did what any auditor worth her salt would do with an official communication containing an implied threat and a procedural opening: she went back to the wall and started planning.

*T-H-E-Y S-A-I-D D-E-F-E-N-S-E,* she tapped. *W-H-I-C-H M-E-A-N-S T-H-E-R-E I-S A P-R-O-C-E-D-U-R-E. P-R-O-C-E-D-U-R-E I-S A D-O-O-R.*

*Y-O-U A-N-D Y-O-U-R P-A-P-E-R-W-O-R-K,* Davor tapped back, and even through rhythm-only she could hear the smile.

*S-A-B-L-E,* she sent. *H-O-W A-R-E Y-O-U.*

The answer came slower than usual. Slower, and then not in code at all — a single irregular pattern, arrhythmic, urgent, nothing like the clean grammar they had built.

Then again. Then again.

Ulyana pressed both hands to the wall. *S-L-O-W-L-Y. A-G-A-I-N. S-L-O-W-L-Y.*

Sable repeated it. Ulyana decoded it twice more to be sure, her stomach dropping with each pass.

*T-H-E-Y K-N-O-W. T-H-E-Y K-N-O-W W-H-O M-Y F-A-T-H-E-R I-S. A B-A-I-L-I-F-F J-U-S-T S-A-I-D M-Y N-A-M-E A-L-O-U-D. H-E S-A-I-D I-T L-I-K-E A T-I-T-L-E.*

Down in the dark of the under-court, in a cell of warm glass, the god's forgotten daughter had just been remembered — and Ulyana, who had spent her whole adult life believing that everything could be entered in a book and therefore controlled, felt the pages turn beneath her without her consent.

Because she had seen what happened down here to things the court remembered. They got appraised.

* * *

The summons came through the wall itself, at the hour the light pretended was dawn.

It did not open. The glass simply became permeable, the way water becomes permeable to a falling stone, and a bailiff-spirit slid through carrying robes of grey silk and a tablet of black slate on which stood one word:

***Come.***

Ulyana dressed. Across whatever distance separated the cells, she heard — felt — Davor's taps begin and stop, begin and stop, a man asking *what is happening* through a wall that no longer cared to answer. She sent back the only comfort the code allowed: four taps. One. *Stable. Do not act rashly.*

Then she lifted her chin, folded her hands like a woman arriving to inspect someone else's books, and walked into the glass.

The corridor beyond was lined with reliquaries — she counted them as she passed, because counting was how she prayed — hundreds of small flames in small containers, each labeled in the court's script. Souls. Collateral. Some of the labels were older than the guild, older than the mountain's name, and a few of the flames guttered as she passed, leaning toward her like flowers toward sun.

Her own flame was somewhere among them. Small. Patient. Waiting for midwinter's end.

The corridor ended at a chamber she had not seen before: round, domed, hung with petitions frozen mid-fall like icicles of paper. In its center stood a dais, and on the dais stood a figure she knew from the trial — a herald-thing of silver and ledger-leather, holding a scroll.

But it was not Ulyana the scroll was addressed to.

"**Sable, daughter of the Quiet God,**" the herald read, and its voice filled the dome like water filling a bell. "**Daughter of the voice in the locket. Daughter of the name that was sold. The court acknowledges your blood. The court extends its recognition.**"

The floor opened. Light rose. And Ulyana, watching from the edge of the room, saw Sable walk into it barefoot and unafraid — saw the light bend toward her the way flames leaned toward Ulyana's passing, saw the herald unfurl a second scroll, longer than the first, sealed with the mark of a throne.

"The court offers," the herald said, "the seat of her father."

Behind Ulyana, at the far end of the corridor, muffled by a hundred feet of enchanted glass, Davor Kestrel began hammering on his wall — fast, frantic, decoding nothing, just *her name, her name, her name* — and Sable turned, found Ulyana's eyes across the shining chamber, and reached for the scroll.
