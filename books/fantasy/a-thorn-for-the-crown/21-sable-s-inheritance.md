## 21. Sable's Inheritance

They came for Sable at the changing of the tallow-light, which was the under-court's way of marking hours without committing to any of them.

Ulyana heard it first as a sound like a ledger closing — a soft, final thump that traveled through the glass floor of the cells and settled in her teeth. Then the bailiff-spirits arrived, three of them, tall as doorways and thin as unpaid invoices, their faces smooth where faces should have been. They did not unlock the cell so much as agree to stop pretending it had a wall.

"Sable of the Ninth Name," one said. Its voice was the sound a quill makes crossing out a word.

Sable stood up from the cell's stone bench slowly, the way you stand when you've been expecting bad news your whole life and have finally saved enough for the down payment. She was fourteen. She had broken into the Gilded Thorn's vaults with a hairpin and a prayer, and now the oldest court beneath the world knew her name.

"That's not my name anymore," Sable said. "My father sold it."

"The sale has been reviewed." The spirit tilted its headless head toward Ulyana's cell — adjacent, separated by bars and six feet of frozen petition-stone — then to Davor's beyond hers. "The lineage is confirmed. Come."

"Don't go," Ulyana said, and hated how it sounded, how it came out not as advice but as the raw thing it was.

"They said come," Sable answered, with a ghost of her usual crooked grin. "In my experience, when something with no face says come, you come, and then you decide afterward what you think about it." She paused at the gap in the wall, and her hand found Ulyana's through the bars — cold fingers, a grip that pretended at carelessness and failed. "If they offer me anything, do I take it?"

"You listen," Ulyana said. "That's all listening costs. I checked. It's the only free thing in this entire building."

Then the spirits folded around her like paper closing over a flame, and she was gone, and Ulyana was alone with the hum of ten thousand frozen petitions and the sound of Davor, in the next cell, knocking once against the wall — their code for *still here*.

She knocked twice back. *Still here. Still counting.*

* * *

The cells of glass were built for isolation, but nobody had told the walls.

They had worked out the tapping code on the first night — Ulyana had designed it in twenty minutes flat, an arithmetic of knuckles, because there was nothing else to do and because designing a system calmed her the way prayer calmed other people. Three taps meant *yes*. One meant *no*. A long scrape meant *I'm frightened*, though none of them had used that one yet, each privately convinced the others needed them not to.

Through the bars, in the intervals when the bailiffs drifted elsewhere, they talked.

"Her father's seat," Davor said now, his voice coming low through the dark. He sat with his back against the shared wall, which was as close to sitting beside someone as the court allowed. "You know what that means?"

"I know what seats mean in courts. They're thrones with better posture." Ulyana pulled her knees up. The cell's glass floor showed, far below, the cathedral of frozen petitions — prayers hung in the air like insects in amber, three centuries of them, glittering. Somewhere down there her soul burned in its reliquary, a small defiant flame. She had seen it once and hadn't slept since.

"It means power," Davor said. "Real power. The kind the old gods hoarded before they went to sleep. If they're offering her a seat—"

"She's fourteen."

"I know she's fourteen. I'm telling you what it means, not what to do about it. There's a difference. I spent nine years confusing the two." A pause, and the creak of him shifting. "When I refused to collect on Tam's debt — the Rell boy — my partner told me the same thing. *It's just arithmetic, Davor. Somebody pays. Better her than the ledger.* And he wasn't wrong, exactly. That's the trap. The numbers are always right. It's the sum that's rotten."

Ulyana smiled despite everything. "You've been talking to me too long. That's almost a metaphor."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll deny it under oath."

Above them, the light changed. The tallow-flames that served the court for sky guttered blue, then white, then gold, and a sound rolled through the cathedral that Ulyana felt in her sternum: the Judge, waking further, or the court assembling, or both.

Then every bailiff-spirit in the place turned toward the cells at once, and the middle one spoke.

"The auditor will attend."

* * *

They brought her to the Nave of Standing Claims, which was what the court called its judgment hall, though everyone Ulyana had met simply called it the Room, in the hushed way people say *the bank* or *the war*. It was vast and vertical, a shaft of stone going up farther than light bothered to follow, and its walls were shelved floor to unseen ceiling with contracts — millions of them, bound in hides she declined to identify, breathing faintly, the way old paper breathes.

Sable stood on the Judgment Floor at the center, dwarfed by it. Around her in a wide arc sat the gods.

Ulyana had expected monsters. What she got was worse: tired people. The old gods looked like a family portrait taken during an argument — some enormous, some small, all of them wearing the particular exhaustion of those who have been woken up after three hundred years to deal with paperwork. At the center, on a dais of black salt, sat the Sleeping Judge, who was no longer entirely asleep. His eyes were open to half-mast. His beard pooled on the floor and kept going.

"There she is," the Judge said, and his voice was gravel poured slowly. "The daughter. Come here, child. Stand where your father stood."

Sable didn't move. "Where did he stand?"

A ripple went through the seated shapes. One of the smaller gods — a woman made mostly of river, water moving patiently inside the outline of a body — put her face in her hands.

"Direct," the Judge observed. "That's the blood showing. Very well. You are Sable, last-born of Harrow-of-the-Shallows, god of safe crossings and small boats, who sat in this court for four thousand years before—" he waved a hand the size of a haywagon, "—before the difficulties. His seat has stood empty since the Silence. Seats may not. It is law. An empty seat is an unpaid position, and we are, as I understand you mortals say, *in arrears*."

He leaned forward. The dais groaned.

"We have reviewed the sale of your name. Your father sold it — foolishly, desperately, in the last gray year before we slept, to buy passage for your mother and you across waters that were rising. We do not fault him. We fault the market he was forced into, which is a different and larger complaint, and one my colleague the auditor—" a nod at Ulyana, half-lidded, unsettling, "—has been making at volume. But the review stands. You are his. Blood answers to blood. The seat reverts to the line."

"He's alive?" Sable's voice cracked on it. Fourteen years old, and the word *alive* still fit inside her like it belonged there.

The Judge's expression did something complicated. "He sleeps as we slept. Whether he wakes as we woke depends greatly on how the next fortnight goes, which is a sentence I invite you to consider the full weight of." He straightened, and the room straightened with him, contracts rustling on their shelves like a field in wind. "Here is the offer, and it is offered once. Take your father's seat. Take his name back — it returns to you the moment you sit. Take his power, his portfolio, his crossings; every river will know you; every ferryman will owe you. In exchange, renounce your mortal associations."

"Renounce," Ulyana repeated, before she could stop herself.

The Judge turned his half-closed eyes on her. "The term is precise. A god's seat cannot be held by one who carries mortal claims upon her loyalty. Debts of love are still debts, auditor. You taught us that, in your way. The girl must be unencumbered. Her companions' claims on her — and hers on them — must be formally struck from every book that holds them." A pause, dry as dust. "Including yours."

"And if she doesn't?" Davor's voice, from the side, where he'd been brought in manacled and furious about it.

"Then the seat passes to the collateral line, the waters rise again someday, and the girl remains what she currently is: a pawned asset, scheduled for reversion at midwinter's end along with every other outstanding soul." The Judge spread his hands. "We are not cruel here. We are *solvent*, or trying to become so, which in the current climate looks similar. Choose, daughter of Harrow. The court grows tired, and it has only recently woken."

The silence that followed was the largest thing in the room.

Ulyana watched Sable's face and saw the whole argument happen there — saw her look up at the empty seat, which was visible now that Ulyada knew to look: a hollow in the air above the dais, boat-shaped, rocking very slightly, as if on water no one else could see. Saw her think about her father, whose face she barely remembered, whose voice she carried in a locket they had bought with Ulyana's last coin in a bazaar underneath the world. Saw her count, the way Ulyana had taught her without meaning to — count what she had, count what it would cost, count who'd pay.

Then Sable turned around. She found Ulyana in the gallery of bailiffs, and she found Davor, and something in her shoulders unlocked.

"No," she said.

The river-woman lifted her head from her hands.

"No," Sable said again, louder, and her voice carried the way small voices sometimes learn to carry in big rooms, thrown upward by sheer refusal to be small in them. "You can keep the seat. You can keep the name — he gave it away, and maybe that hurt him worse than it hurt me, and maybe taking it back would fix something, I don't know, I've thought about it every night since I learned it was gone." She was shaking. She did not stop. "But you said renounce. You want me to walk out of their books. Their debts. Their — their *claims* on me." She laughed, wet and furious. "Do you know what their claim on me is? Davor carried me three miles through a snow that fell wrong because I was too tired to walk and he never once said so. Ulyana spent her last coin on my father's voice without hesitating, and she counts *everything*, I've watched her, she counts the spoons. That's not encumbrance. That's the only inheritance I ever picked out myself."

"Sable—" the Judge began.

"You want to talk books? Fine. I'm in yours, and I want out, and I know exactly how hard that is because my best friend is an auditor and she's explained it to me *twice*. But I am not getting out of one ledger by signing another. I choose them. Loudly. In front of everyone. Write it down." She looked around at the assembled gods, at the breathing shelves, at the boat-shaped emptiness rocking above the dais. "Write it down in whatever survives longest."

Nobody moved.

Then, from among the seated shapes, the river-woman began — quietly, terribly, beautifully — to laugh. It sounded like water over stones. "Oh, Harrow," she said to no one living. "*Oh*, Harrow. Look at her."

The Judge closed his eyes the rest of the way. When he opened them again he looked older, and more awake, and sorrier — an combination Ulyana was learning was common among powerful things.

"So be it," he said. "Refusal is entered. The seat stands empty. The girl remains in reversion with the rest." His gaze slid to Ulyana, and held, and there was something in it that was almost — almost — respect. "Midwinter's end, auditor. You have until the last snow lands to find your flaw, your loophole, your miracle. The court has reviewed your little discovery about forged apprenticeship papers. Precedent outweighs truth, as you were told. But precedents can be *audited*." A pause, vast as weather. "So. Audit."

The bailiffs came to return her to her cell. As they led her out, Sable caught her eye, and grinned — tear-streaked, terrified, absolutely certain — and mouthed two words.

*Worth it.*

* * *

Back in the glass, the tapping started before the bailiffs had fully drifted away.

Three taps. *Yes.*

Then Davor's fist, low on the wall, a slow deliberate rhythm Ulyana translated with her palm flat against the cold: *She did well.*

Three taps from Sable's cell, farther off now, fainter. *Yes.* Then, after a moment, a new pattern — hesitant, self-conscious, one they'd never used. Ulyana worked it out letter by letter, heart climbing into her throat.

*T-H-A-N-K Y-O-U F-O-R T-E-A-C-H-I-N-G M-E T-O C-O-U-N-T.*

Ulyana pressed her forehead against the glass wall and knocked back the only answer that was true: *Y-O-U D-I-D T-H-E M-A-T-H Y-O-U-R-S-E-L-F.*

She sat back in the dark then, and let herself feel it — pride, terror, grief for the girl's father sleeping somewhere in these deeps, all of it at once, unbalanced, unreconciled. Because she had done the math too, sitting in that gallery, watching Sable refuse a throne. And the math said this: midwinter's end was eleven days away. The last snows were falling faster than the Judge's patience. Precedent outweighed truth, and truth was on their side, and neither fact helped.

Eleven days. Three prisoners. One soul in a reliquary, and a court full of insolvent gods who had just been publicly invited to audit themselves.

Unless, Ulyana thought, sitting up slowly in the dark as the idea unfolded itself page by page in her head — unless the problem had never been getting *out* of the cells.

Unless the trial was the door.

She raised her knuckles to the wall, and began, tap by careful tap, to teach her family the plan.
