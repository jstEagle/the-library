## 22. The Escape That Wasn't

The locks of the under-court were not made by locksmiths. They were made by accountants.

Ulyana understood this within two days of tapping her cell wall and listening to the way the sound came back. Stone answered differently where the mortar had been mixed with interest — she could hear the compounding in it, a faint dry tick like a clock counting someone else's hours. The cells of glass were held shut not by iron but by lien. Every door in this place was a contract with a hinge. And every contract, no matter how old, no matter how holy, could be read.

She had spent three days reading hers through the wall.

The tapped code had started as Davor's idea — three knocks for *yes*, one for *no*, a long scrape for *shut up, they're coming*. But Davor had been an enforcer, not a scholar, and his vocabulary ran to perhaps forty words before the grammar collapsed. Ulyana had extended it. She'd assigned numbers. Numbers became letters; letters became sentences; sentences became, on the second night, a full accounting of the cell block transmitted at a speed that made Sable laugh breathlessly against her own glass wall — a sound none of them heard, but all of them somehow felt.

*The doors are numbered,* she tapped to Davor. *Everything here is numbered. Do you understand what that means?*

A pause. Then: *It means you have a terrible plan.*

*It means everything here is owed,* she tapped back. *And anything that is owed can be called due.*

The trick, when it finally unspooled itself in her mind at some hour when even the under-court slept, was embarrassingly simple. The doors were contracts. Contracts had clauses. Clauses had conditions of release — performance bonds, sureties, collateral swaps. The court's own bookkeeping demanded that every debt be traceable, transferable, *auditable*. Which meant that somewhere behind each lock there lived a ledger entry, and somewhere in each ledger entry there lived a line that said, in effect: *this soul may be released upon presentation of equivalent value.*

Equivalent value.

Ulyana pressed her palm flat against the cold glass of her cell and smiled the smile she used to give Vespera when a column refused to balance.

She didn't need to pick the lock. She needed to *refinance* it.

* * *

On the fourth night — if night existed here, which was doubtful; the cathedral of frozen petitions glowed its pale amber forever, ten thousand prayers suspended mid-plea like flies in honey — the guard-spirit made its round. It was a thin thing, a bailiff made of folded parchment and patience, and it walked the corridor at intervals with the unhurried gait of something that had never once been surprised.

Ulyana waited until it passed her cell. She counted its footsteps to the corner. Forty-one. Then she began to work.

Her grimoire was gone — paid at the door of the under-court, or so they believed, traded for passage that had turned out to be an ambush and a trial date. But a hedge-witch's stolen book had taught her more than spells. It had taught her that magic was just arithmetic performed on things that hadn't been told they were allowed to be counted. And the under-court had told her exactly how it counted. It had told her in the toll-wights' demands, in the judge's sleepy precedent-mongering, in the very architecture of her imprisonment. Everything here was a figure in a column.

She whispered the release clause aloud. Not a spell. A recitation. The terms of her own contract, memorized down to the last archaic syllable — *held pending midwinter's end, redeemable per Section Nine upon presentation of equivalent value* — and then she offered the only thing she had left that the court valued: information. An error. A discrepancy she had spotted in the frozen petitions above the judgment hall, a prayer recorded twice, a double-entry in the gods' own books worth — she estimated, wildly, gloriously — several centuries of accrued worship.

The lock did not turn. Locks were beneath it. Instead, the door simply reconsidered itself. There was a sound like a very large sigh passing through stone, and the seal of her cell dimmed from gold to grey, and the glass swung inward on hinges that had always been there and had merely been waiting for the paperwork.

*Oh,* Ulyana thought, standing in the corridor with her heart hammering like an apprentice late for market. *That worked. That actually worked.*

She went right, toward Davor's cell, and recited the same lie against his glass. His door sighed open too, and then he was filling the corridor — huge, bearded, gray-eyed, smelling faintly of the salt tunnels still caught in his coat — and he grabbed her shoulders and looked at her the way he always did, as if checking her for damage first and miracles second.

"The child," he said. Not hello. Not *how*. "There was a girl two cells past mine. I've heard her crying at night. Little one."

"I know." Ulyana's mouth had already gone dry. "I heard her too."

Sable came next, slipping out of her own opened cell with the silent grace of someone who had grown up learning which floorboards reported to whom. She was fifteen and carried her divinity the way other girls carried a family resemblance — lightly, resentfully, impossible to hide entirely. Her eyes were doing the thing they did near god-stuff: catching light that wasn't there.

"Which way?" she whispered.

"Out," said Davor. "The archive stairs are unguarded between rounds. We go now, we go quiet, we're in the salt tunnels by—"

"No," Ulyana said.

They both looked at her. In the amber glow of the frozen petitions, her face must have shown something, because Davor's expression shifted from surprise to recognition to something older and sadder. He knew. Of course he knew. He had stood in exactly this place once before, in a rain-soaked street in the Shambles, with a debtor's child watching him through a window, and he had chosen, and the choosing had cost him everything — badge, partner, trade, name.

"Ulyana," he said carefully. "We have maybe forty steps of grace before the bailiff rounds again. If we take the girl, we aren't escaping. We're fleeing with stolen property. They'll hunt us through the whole court. Ilya will—"

"I know what it costs," she said. "I'm telling you the price anyway."

"You don't have to—"

"Yes," she said, "I do. Because you taught me the arithmetic, Davor Kestrel. You stood in the rain and refused to collect a child's debt, and everyone told you it was bad business, and you were *right anyway*. I've done the sums. I keep doing them. Every version of the ledger where I walk out of here clean and leave her behind — every single one balances wrong. It balances like Vespera's books. Perfect columns over a rotten foundation." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "My soul was pawned before I was born because adults found it convenient. So was hers. I am not leaving her in a jar."

The corridor hummed. Above them, in the great cathedral, ten thousand prayers hung frozen, and one of them — a small one, a child's one, *please let someone come, please, please, please* — seemed almost to warm.

Davor closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was smiling, and it was a terrible smile, the kind men wear when they've decided to lose something on purpose.

"Forty steps of grace," he repeated. "Well. We'll spend them badly."

* * *

The girl's cell was smaller than theirs, deeper in the block, behind a door whose seal burned a sicklier gold — an older contract, a worse one. Ulyana put her palm against it and listened, really listened, the way she'd learned to listen to ledgers: not to the words but to what the words were hiding.

The clause was different. This wasn't a holding-pending-trial lien. This was storage. Long-term. The kind of entry written by someone who expected the principal to be forgotten.

The girl inside was perhaps eight years old, small enough that the glass cell swallowed her, curled on a bench of stone with her knees to her chest. She had the look Ulyana recognized from orphanages and debt-collectors' waiting rooms and her own reflection in bad mirrors: the watchful blankness of a child who has learned that hope is a bill that arrives later with interest. Her hair was dark and cropped unevenly, prison-cut. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a lead token stamped with a number instead of a name.

When the seal dimmed and the door sighed open, she didn't move. She watched them with those blank eyes, and Ulyana thought: *she doesn't believe it. She thinks we're part of the dream.*

"Hello," Ulyana said softly, crouching to make herself small. "What's your name?"

The girl considered the question the way one considers a trap. Then, barely audible: "Pip. It used to be. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before they said my name was the payment." Pip's chin lifted slightly, and there — underneath the blankness — was a spark of pure mulish pride that made Ulyana's chest ache. "But it isn't. It's mine. I checked."

"You checked," Ulyana repeated.

"Mama said names matter down here. So I hold it. All day. Like a coin in my fist." The small fist in question tightened. "They haven't got it yet."

Davor made a low sound in his throat, half laugh, half wound. Ulyana looked up at him and saw that he was staring at the girl as though she were a ghost with excellent timing — the child from the Shambles, seven years ago, rain running off his fists while he told Ilya *no* for the first and last time. Except this time nobody was going to stand in a doorway deciding. This time the deciding was already done.

"Pip," Ulyana said. "We're getting out of here. Both of us. All of us. Will you come with us?"

"They'll count me missing," Pip said. "Then it'll be worse."

"It's already the worst," Sable said from the doorway, matter-of-fact. "It can only get different."

Something flickered across the child's face — the ghost of a grin, quickly suppressed, as if joy too might incur fees. Then she slid off the bench, crossed the little cell, and took Ulyana's hand with the fierce grip of someone who had decided, on insufficient evidence, to trust forever.

"Right," said Davor, turning toward the dark end of the corridor, rolling his shoulders like a man putting on an old coat. "Archive stairs. Forty steps of grace, minus about twelve. Stay close, stay low, and if anyone asks, we're a delegation."

"A delegation of what?" Sable asked.

"Debtors," said Davor. "Best disguise in this whole court. Nobody stops a debtor; they're the entire economy."

* * *

They made the archive stairs with nine steps to spare.

The stairwell climbed through shelves — of course it did; everything in the under-court was shelving in some form — towering ranks of contracts bound in vellum and bark and things less pleasant, stretching up beyond sight into the amber haze. Their footsteps echoed wrong, too loud, and Ulyana counted the rhythm of their ascent against her heartbeat and did not like the mathematics. Four sets of feet. One of them small. Small feet make small sounds, but small people also move slowly, and Pip's hand in hers trembled with the effort of keeping pace.

"Here." Davor scooped the girl onto his hip without breaking stride, and Pip stiffened, startled — then wrapped her arms around his neck and buried her face in his collar with the boneless trust of a child who has finally, after long audit, approved a guardian.

Ulyana watched them and felt something in her chest go strange and bright and dangerous.

They were thirty steps from the top of the stairwell — thirty steps from the archive galleries and the route Davor remembered from his collecting years, the maintenance crawl behind the Hall of Precedent, the long dark run to the salt tunnels — when the humming started.

It came from everywhere. From the walls. From the contracts themselves, ten thousand documents rustling on their shelves like leaves before a storm. Ulyana knew that sound. She'd heard it the day the fish fell from a clear sky over Copperlane. It was the sound of the old gods paying attention.

Somewhere far below, a bell tolled once — a deep, patient, bookkeeping bell, the sound of an entry being made.

"Oh no," Sable breathed. Her eyes flared blue-white. "They've noticed. The count is short. They're—" she cocked her head, listening to something in the stone, something divine and dreadful, "—they're reconciling."

Down the stairwell, from the direction of the cells, came light. Gold light, hard as coin, climbing fast.

"That's not the bailiff," Davor said grimly, setting Pip down behind him and planting himself on the stairs like a door that had decided to become a wall. "Bailiffs don't glow."

"Can we outrun it?" Ulyana asked, though she already knew.

"Not all of us. Not with a child." He glanced back at her, and in the gold-rimmed darkness his gray eyes were steady and almost amused. "You should go. Take the girls. I'll slow it down."

"You will absolutely not," Ulyana snapped. "I did not refinance four doors to leave you on these stairs like collateral."

"Then we run together," Davor said, "and we pray the archive maze is as confusing as I remember it being."

Above them, at the top of the stairwell, a different light bloomed — cold, silver, familiar. And a voice drifted down the shaft, echoing off ten thousand binding contracts, a voice Ulyana had last heard across a bazaar stall with regret all over it:

"Well," said Ilya. "Of course it's you. Of course it's *you*."

Ulyana looked up. Davor looked up. And somewhere in the deeps below, patient as compound interest, something began to climb.
