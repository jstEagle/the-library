## 23. Hunted Through the Halls of Debt

The child's name was Pip.

She told them this four times in the first hundred yards, breathless, skipping on the wet stone, because no one had asked her name in the eleven years since it had been pawned and she seemed to want to hear herself say it. *Pip. Pip. My name is Pip.*

Ulyana did not tell her to be quiet. She wanted to — every instinct honed by ledgers and locked doors said that a running fugitive should be silent as ink — but the girl had spent eleven years in a glass cell being nobody's asset, and Ulyana would not take her voice from her too.

"Left here," Davor hissed, and they went left, into an aisle of shelves so tall the tops vanished into dark.

The archive-mazes of the under-court were built like everything else down here: as a record first and a place second. Every corridor was a ledger-line, every shelf a column of entries, and the entries were not books. They were *debts*. Jars of owed hours. Bundles of unpaid vows tied with red string. A shelf of small brass bells, each one ringing faintly with the sound of a promise someone had failed to keep, and Ulyana felt them shiver as she passed, as if her own unkept things could feel her coming.

Behind them, far back through the stacks, something rang in answer.

"That'll be the bailiffs," Davor said. "They follow noise. And debt." He glanced at Pip. "And souls out of their cells."

"How fast?" Ulyana asked.

"Bailiff-spirits don't have legs. They have *claims*. Wherever there's an outstanding obligation, that's where they are. So we run where nothing is owed."

"And where is that?"

He didn't answer, which was answer enough. In the under-court, everything was owed.

* * *

They ran. The aisles branched like roots; Davor navigated by habits eleven years dead, turning left when the left looked wrong, counting junctions under his breath the way Ulyana counted sums. Sable carried Pip piggyback when the girl tired, and the blue light that lived in Sable's blood spilled off her skin and lit their way, which was a mercy and a catastrophe both — mercy, because the bailiff-spirits hated godlight and hung back from its glow; catastrophe, because anything that hunted them could see the glow for a mile of shelving.

"We need to douse you," Davor told Sable.

"You can't douse what I am."

"Then we need to get somewhere you can't be seen."

"There's nowhere I can't be seen," Sable said, and there was something raw under the flatness of it. Eleven chapters of her father's bargains sat in that sentence. Ulyana heard it and filed it away — later, later, there was always a later — and put her hand briefly on Sable's shoulder as they ran.

The bells rang louder behind them. Then a new sound joined: footsteps. Even, patient, human.

"Ilya," Davor said, without turning around.

"You don't know that."

"I know how he walks. He walks like he's doing arithmetic." Davor almost smiled. "We taught each other that."

* * *

They lost the bailiff-spirits at the Well of Forfeits.

It was an accident, or as close to one as this court allowed. Pip, wriggling down from Sable's back to walk on her own feet because she was eleven and proud, stepped on a loose ledger-stone, and beneath it was a round dark well-shaft breathing cold air, and down in the cold air floated the forfeited things — a wedding ring, a lock of hair, a wooden horse with one leg, drifting slow as snowfall in water. Bailiff-spirits poured toward the sound of breaking seal like ink toward a blot, and Davor grabbed all three of them and hauled them down a side passage while the spirits clustered over the well, drinking the scent of other people's losses.

They stopped in a niche between a shelf of defaulted oaths and a wall of frozen petitions — the great ice-cathedral's fingers reaching even here — and Ulyana knelt to look at Pip properly.

The girl was bleeding at the knee. Small cut. Nothing. But Ulyana tore the hem of her own dress anyway, wrapped it, tied it, and Pip watched her do it with the expression of someone memorizing a miracle.

"You didn't have to do that," Pip said.

"Yes I did."

"Why?"

Because no one bandaged me either. Because my mistress sold me before I could walk and called it provision. Because every kindness I ever received came with interest attached, and I am going to spend the rest of my life giving away the kind that doesn't.

"Because it's free," Ulyana said.

Pip frowned like a small auditor. "Nothing's free."

"Not here," Ulyana agreed. "But I'm not from here."

* * *

Ilya found them an hour later, and he found them alone.

Davor had gone ahead to scout the junction where the archive-mazes met the Processional — the wide frozen avenue that led, eventually, to the Door. Ulyana stayed with the girls in the niche. And then the candle-glow at the aisle's end resolved into a tall shape walking with arithmetic in its step, and Ilya stood before them with his collector's satchel open and his hand nowhere near a weapon.

"Don't shout," he said. "Don't run. I'm alone. The bailiffs lost your scent at the well, and I told them you went east."

"Why would you do that?" Ulyana kept herself between him and the children. Sable's hands had gone bright.

"Because I owe Davor a debt I've never entered in a book." Ilya's face was gray with something that wasn't fear and wasn't quite regret. "But listen to me, hedge-girl, because I won't say it twice. I marked your route. That wasn't betrayal and it wasn't kindness — it was procedure. The court doesn't send bailiffs after runners. It sends *terms*. Everything you touch, everyone who helps you, every door you pass through — it all gets added to your account. You're not fleeing your debt anymore. You're *compounding* it."

"Then we'll pay it," Ulyana said.

"With what? Look at her." He nodded at Pip. "You pulled a soul out of a cell. Fine. Noble. Now that soul has accrued eleven years of storage fees, handling charges, interest compounded quarterly since before she could talk. The court will take it out of whoever's nearest. Out of *you*." His voice dropped. "I've seen it done. A man once ran from a two-mark fine and by the end they owned his grandchildren. Put the girl back, Ulyana Okonkwo. Find another cell for her, sign her back in, and run clean."

"No."

Ilya closed his eyes briefly, like a man watching good money burn. "No?"

"She's not an entry. She's not collateral. She's a person whose name is Pip, and she's told us four times because nobody ever wrote it down, and I—" Ulyana's voice cracked and she let it crack, let it be heard, because she was done performing composure for men with satchels. "I am the guild's auditor. I have read ten thousand contracts, and every single one of them was written by someone who needed the debtor to believe the debt was real. So no. I will not put her back. If the court wants to add her rescue to my account, let it. Let it write the number. I'll read the number aloud in front of the judge himself, and I'll ask him to show me where in the fine print it says a child can be charged rent for existing. I have beaten better bookkeepers than this court, Ilya. I beat them every morning before breakfast."

Silence. Somewhere far off, a bell tolled — not an alarm, just the court keeping time, time being the one thing down here that never went unrecorded.

Ilya studied her for a long moment. Then he reached into his satchel and took out a strip of pale bark-paper and pressed it into her hands.

"The Processional is sealed. Has been since midwinter began. The Door only opens for settled accounts now — the judge locked it when the winter started falling. You can't leave the way you came." He tapped the bark-paper. "There's a service stair behind the Hall of Reckonings. Clerks use it. It comes up inside the ice cathedral itself, above the throne floor. It's not on any chart, because charts are assets and assets get stolen." He almost smiled. "So I memorized it instead."

"Why give us this?" Sable asked quietly. "What does it cost you?"

"Everything, probably." Ilya shouldered his satchel. "That's rather the point of this place, isn't it. Everything costs. I've spent twenty years believing that. Your friend Davor spent twenty years refusing to." He turned to go, then paused. "Tell him the child's fine. The one he saved. She's apprenticed to a chandler now. She makes candles that smell of oranges. He'll understand why that matters more than I can explain."

He walked away down the aisle, footsteps patient as arithmetic, and did not ring a single bell.

* * *

"He lied about the route," Pip whispered, when he was gone. "Didn't he. There isn't a stair."

"There's a stair," Ulyana said.

"But he marked our route for the court. You said."

"He did. Both things are true." Ulyana folded the bark-paper into her bodice, next to the grimoire's last surviving page. "That's what this place does to people. It makes every kindness double-entry. He helps us and betrays us in the same gesture, and he'll never be able to tell you which one he meant."

"Which one did he mean?"

Ulyana thought of the chandler's apprentice, candles smelling of oranges. Of Davor's face when he'd spoken of the child he refused to ruin. Of Vespera, alone somewhere above them with the oldest ledger, cutting deals in the dark.

"Both," she said. "That's the trap of debts, Pip. They make it impossible to do one pure thing. Which is why we're going to burn the whole system down." She stood, and offered the girl her hand. "Come on. We have a judge to audit."

They found Davor at the junction, and gave him Ilya's message, and watched him turn away and press the heel of his hand against his eyes for exactly three seconds — Ulyana counted — before he said, in a perfectly level voice:

"Oranges. Good. She always liked orange peel."

Then he pointed down the Processional, toward the vast glow of the frozen cathedral, where the Sleeping Judge sat enthroned among three centuries of unanswered prayer, and where — somewhere in those shining vaults above the throne floor — a small flame burned in a glass reliquary with Ulyana's true name etched on the base.

"Service stair's behind the Hall of Reckonings," Davor said. "Ilya's map or not, we move now. Midwinter's end is coming. When the winter finishes falling, every outstanding soul reverts to the court automatically." He looked at Ulyana. "Including yours."

Behind them, deep in the archive-mazes, the bailiff-spirits had finished with the Well of Forfeits.

They began, patiently, to follow the trail of a bandage torn from a green dress — a strip of cloth given freely, freely given, the one thing in all the under-court with no price on it, and therefore the most dangerous object in the world.

They would find it. They always did.

And they would bring terms.
