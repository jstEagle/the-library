## 25. Above Ground, Below Hope

The last ladder was iron, and it bit.

Ulyana climbed with her palms wrapped in strips torn from her own hem, the child—Mira, eight years old, soul pawned before her first tooth—strapped to Davor's back above him. Sable went first because her blood made the dark honest: wherever a god had once walked, her veins lit the stone blue, and near the surface the blue had thinned to almost nothing. Almost nothing meant almost daylight. Almost daylight meant they had made it.

Then her head broke through snow, and Ulyana understood that making it and being saved were not the same thing.

She came up into a graveyard of grain. The harvest fields north of the city should have been stubble-gold by now, cart-tracks and crows and the smell of cut straw. Instead the wheat stood sheathed in ice, every stalk a glass needle, bending under snow that fell in fat, deliberate flakes—the snow of a thing that had all winter long and knew it. The sky was the color of pewter left out in the rain. Somewhere ahead, muffled by white, the city bells rang noon, and the sound came out wrong, flattened, as if even the bronze were cold enough to lie.

"Harvest month," Ulyana said. Her voice cracked on it. "It's still harvest *month*."

Davor set Mira down in the lee of a collapsed well-house and shrugged off his coat for her without comment. He'd lost three days of his life to the toll-wights and gained a gray thread at each temple that hadn't been there before; he wore both like weather. "Bells are ringing," he said. "That's something."

"Bells ring at executions too."

He looked at her then—not the flat enforcer's stare but the other one, the one that cost him—and said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

Sable stood at the edge of the field with her arms wrapped around herself, staring back down the hole they'd come out of. The locket hung outside her shirt now; Ulyana could see it catching what little light there was. Inside it, bought with Ulyana's last coin in the Market of Small Mercies, a god's voice waited in an inch of amber. A father's voice. It had already said one sentence—that was how they'd tested it—and the sentence had been *who's there*, asked in wonder, as if no one had spoken to him in three hundred years.

"We can't go back," Sable said. Not asking. Just setting the fact down where everyone could see it, the way you set down something heavy so your hands are free.

"No," Ulyana said. "We go forward. Into the city. Where the guild is profiting from all this, if my arithmetic holds." She flexed her stiff fingers. "And it always holds. That's the problem."

Mira stirred against Davor's shoulder. "Is it much farther?" The girl's voice was small and steady, and it did something to Ulyana's chest that she didn't have time to examine. A child whose soul had been collateral. A child Davor had doubled back for when every rational ledger-line said leave her. They had carried her up through the salt dark on bread crumbs and tapped code, and now she asked about distance like any other traveler, as if her whole self weren't a disputed asset.

"Not far," Davor told her. "And after that, hot soup."

"You said that about the wights' bridge too."

"And I was right eventually."

Ulyana turned south and started walking, because if she stopped moving she would feel the cold properly, and if she felt the cold properly she would think about what waited in the city, and she wasn't ready yet. But the walk wasn't long enough. It never is.

* * *

They came over the last rise an hour later and saw the Gilded Thorn.

Even at this distance—even through snow—you could tell it from every other building in Vell's Crossing, because the Thorn glowed. Its windows burned amber and gold, warm as hearth-coals, and around its doors clustered figures in good wool moving crates marked with the guild's sigil: a rose with a thorn drawn through it, stylized until the thorn looked almost like mercy. Carts were loading, not unloading. Bottled summer going out to rich houses. Borrowed luck, decanted into vials fine as wedding rings, going to men who needed dice to fall their way more than other people needed bread.

The rest of the city hunched around it like beggars around a fire they couldn't enter. Smoke rose thin and gray from chimneys that should have been fat with harvest cooking. On the road below them, a queue stretched from the guild doors down the street and around a shrine—a shrine to one of the old gods, Ulyana noted, its statue newly scrubbed, its offering-bowl overflowing with teeth, hair, rings, things people had run out of coin and begun paying in flesh instead. Prayers answered cruelly since the gods woke. Snow in harvest month. And the guild selling warmth by the bottle.

"They're doing well," Davor said quietly.

"They're doing *obscenely*," Ulyana said, and heard her own voice go flat and cold, colder than the air. "Look at the queue. Look at the *shrine*. Half those people will pay with something they don't know they're paying. That's how we were taught to write it—*consideration rendered in kind*—so the debtor signs smiling." She had kept those books for six years. She had balanced those entries and slept well afterward on careful arithmetic, and she had called it contentment, wary contentment, a life built on clean columns. Standing here, seeing the clean columns from the outside, she felt something in her stomach turn over like bad coin.

Mira tugged Davor's sleeve. "Is that the place that owned me?"

The words dropped into the snow between them and lay there.

"No," Davor said, before anyone else could speak. "That's the place that *held* the paper. There's a difference, and we're about to make it true."

Ulyana wanted to believe him. She was very good at believing things that balanced. She started down toward the doors.

* * *

Vespera received them in the counting room, because of course she did. Not the parlor, not with wine; the counting room, among the ledgers, where she was strongest and everything else was just inventory. The guildmother stood behind the great desk with her silver-streaked black hair pinned up like a fortress and her hands folded on the oldest book, the one older than the guild itself, and her face was arranged into exactly the expression Ulyana had seen her wear over a hundred hard negotiations: regretful, reasonable, immovable.

"You look terrible," Vespera said. "All four of you. There's soup."

"There's a queue around the block," Ulyana said. "People paying with hair and teeth."

"People paying with what they have." Vespera didn't flinch. "You've been under a mountain, so let me give you the season's numbers. The winter began early and has not paused. The granaries are half what they should be. The old gods answer prayers again—badly, cruelly, but they *answer*, and frightened people will take a cruel miracle over silence. Every impossible thing in our vaults is suddenly the only currency that matters. We have sold bottled warmth to keep eleven families from freezing, and yes, the price was steep, and yes, I set it." She unfolded her hands. "Say whatever you came to say, Ulyana. You've been composing it since the salt tunnels."

Ulyana had. Six days underground, tapping code through cell walls, she had composed it in iambic fury. Now, standing in the warm room with the soup smell in her nose, she found the speech had gone out of her and left only the question underneath, the one she'd carried since the vaults beneath the mountain, since the page in her own handwriting she never wrote:

"The apprenticeship papers," she said. "For me. With Marza Thell, hedge-witch. Filed with the Guild Registry thirty years—" she stopped. Recounted. "Twenty-six years ago. Before I was born."

"Yes," Vespera said.

"*Before I was born.*" Ulyana's voice rose and cracked. "I found the flaw in my own contract in front of a sleeping god. Marza never legally owned me—my mother signed nothing, my mother was dead before the ink dried, someone *forged* the apprenticeship so the sale of my soul would hold water in the under-court. And forged papers need a registry stamp, and there is exactly one organization in this city authorized to witness contracts of that kind." She stepped forward. Her hands were shaking; she watched them shake as if they belonged to a stranger, some other auditor. "You stamped them, Vespera. You stamped them twenty-six years ago, when I was a *fetus*, and then you hired me, and put me over your books, and let me balance accounts with my own name in them."

The room was very quiet. Sable had gone pale. Mira had pressed close to Davor, who had gone very still in the way he went still before violence—but he didn't move, because Vespera was not a threat you could solve with hands.

"I did," Vespera said.

Not *it was necessary.* Not *you misunderstand.* Just *I did*, laid on the desk like a weight.

"Why." Ulyana heard her voice drop to nearly nothing. "Give me the entry. Give me the reason. I'll read anything, I'll read the whole cursed book—"

"You won't find it in a book." Vespera walked around the desk slowly, and up close, in the candlelight, Ulyana saw something she had never once seen in six years: the guildmother looked *tired*. Not old—she had always been old the way mountains are old, as furniture, as fact—but tired, worn in the way a tool wears, hollowed along its working edge. "Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"You always stand when you're about to cry. Sit down anyway."

Ulyana sat. She would deny it later.

Vespera remained standing, hands folded again, and spoke in the level tone she used for reading terms aloud—which Ulyana realized, with a lurch, was also the tone she used for confessions, and perhaps had only ever been the tone of confession all along, disguised.

"When the guild was young—before the name, before the vaults—I made a bargain. The founding debt, the one I told you about. My soul, pawned to the court beneath the mountain, as surety for the guild's charter. The under-court does not lend to the living for free. I signed because there was no other way to build what I intended to build, and I have been buying time ever since. Renewals. Extensions. Interest paid in odd currencies." Her eyes flicked to the oldest ledger. "A term comes due, Ulyana. Mine. And a term can be served either by the debtor or by an asset of equivalent weight."

Understanding arrived the way cold water arrives—not all at once but everywhere at once, soaking through.

"You needed a replacement," Ulyana whispered. "Equivalent weight. So you went looking for a soul young enough to serve a full term. And you couldn't buy one honestly, because souls aren't honestly for sale, so you—" Her breath caught. "Marza Thell. The hedge-witch was *your instrument*. The forged apprenticeship. The sale of a baby's name before she drew breath. You built me. Twenty-six years ago you built a girl out of paperwork specifically so she could grow up clever enough with numbers to matter, desperate enough to flee to you, and encumbered enough that she'd belong to the court." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You didn't hire a ledger-keeper. You *grew* one. In a garden you planted before I was born."

"Yes," Vespera said again. And then, quieter, the confession cracking open another chamber beneath it: "I chose the mother carefully. I chose the bloodlines. I told myself I was choosing aptitude. It took me nine years to stop telling myself that."

Ulyana stood up so fast the chair went over backward. Fury came up through her like floodwater, six years of wary contentment drowning in it—the candlelit nights balancing books, the pride in clean columns, the sense of having *earned* her refuge, all of it revealed as a crop, cultivated, harvested on schedule. "Every kindness. Every kindness had a line item. When you took me in off the street with nothing but a stolen grimoire—was that mercy or was that *delivery*? When you taught me the trade, was that mentorship or maturation of an asset? Did you ever once, *once*, look at me and see anything except a term coming due?"

Vespera met her eyes. That was the worst part. She didn't look away, didn't retreat into the guildmother's mask, and what was in her face was not guilt exactly—guilt would have been easier—it was grief, old and settled and lived-with, the grief of someone who did an unforgivable thing so long ago that the unforgivingness of it has become load-bearing.

"I saw a girl who hummed when she did sums," Vespera said. "I saw it every day for six years. And every day I told myself the contract would lapse, that I'd find another way, that the court would default first. Debtors are cowards, Ulyana. I built a life on the assumption that everyone defaults eventually—including me." She picked up the overturned chair and righted it, unhurried, hands steady. "But the gods woke. The court is calling in everything. And I am standing here telling you the truth because I am out of extensions, and because you deserve to hear it from my mouth rather than read it in a margin."

"That's not why you're telling me."

"No," Vespera agreed. "I'm also telling you because the vote is coming. The guild must decide what it is. And I would rather lose the vote to you knowing everything than win it by keeping you ignorant. Consider it—" the ghost of her old dry smile— "*full disclosure. Prior to audit.*"

Davor moved then, finally, stepping forward until he stood between the two women like a wall that had decided something. "You forged papers that sold a child."

"I did."

"And you've fed us and armed us and led us to her soul's cage and back, and I genuinely cannot tell if that's atonement or husbandry."

"Neither can I," Vespera said simply. "That is the honest answer, enforcer. Neither can I. Ask me again when the vote is done, and I may have learned which."

Sable spoke from the doorway, where she'd been standing with Mira's hand in hers. Her voice was thin but it carried. "My father traded away my name for less than this. For *nothing*—he doesn't even remember what he got. And everyone tells me he's a god, so it doesn't count, so I should understand, divine arithmetic is different." She lifted her chin, and the locket swung, catching light. "You're not a god, mistress. You don't get the different arithmetic. What you did counts."

Vespera looked at the girl for a long moment. "Yes," she said softly. "It counts. That is rather the point of the entire profession, child. Everything counts. Everything is owed." She turned back to Ulyana. "The question the guild faces is whether that rule binds the ones who wrote it."

Ulyana's fury hadn't cooled—it was banked now, coal-deep, structural, the kind that would burn for years—but beneath it, terribly, unwillingly, she understood. That was the beat she'd dreaded, the one walking up the mountain she'd refused to rehearse: not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but *comprehension*. She understood exactly how it happened. A cornered woman, an impossible lender, a choice between her life's work and a person who didn't exist yet. She understood it the way an auditor understands fraud: completely, precisely, and without one drop of excuse.

"I'm not going to forgive you," Ulyana said. "Understand that clearly. Understanding isn't absolution. I can read your books perfectly and still find you insolvent."

"I would expect nothing less from my student." Something crossed Vespera's face—pride, maybe, arriving at the worst possible moment, refusing to be scheduled. "The vote is in three days. Winter council. Full membership. The question on the table will be whether the Gilded Thorn surrenders you to the court, negotiates, or stands against it. I will cast my vote last, as guildmother. Whatever I cast, you should assume it serves the ledger."

"And if the ledger says surrender me?"

"Then I will say so out loud, and you will hate me cleanly, which is a better shape for hate than the one you're carrying now." Vespera gathered the oldest ledger into her arms—casually, the way a woman gathers a shawl—and Ulyana's eye snagged on it, on the familiar worn spine, and something ticked at the back of her mind, an entry not yet posted, a number that wouldn't sit straight. Why bring the oldest book into this room today? Why carry it at all?

"Rest," Vespera said. "Eat. Mira needs a physician and Sable needs sleep and you—" her gaze lingered, unreadable, "—need to decide what you're auditing next. The guild. Or me."

Ulyana stood in the warm room full of candles and clean columns and felt the whole edifice tilt, foundation and all, and understood that whatever happened in three days, the Gilded Thorn she had fled to, believed in, balanced books for—it was already gone. What remained was a room full of people deciding, one entry at a time, whether they were an institution or a family.

Behind Vespera, on the great desk, the newest ledger lay open to the current week. And at the top of the page, in the guildmother's precise hand, dated tomorrow, was a single fresh entry Ulyana was certain had not existed an hour ago:

*One (1) ledger, oldest volume — removed from archive. Purpose: pending.*

Pending. Vespera was already moving. And the blizzard outside had begun to howl like something with a name.
