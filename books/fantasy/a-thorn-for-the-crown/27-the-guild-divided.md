## 27. The Guild Divided

The bell above the counting-room door rang at dawn, and it did not stop ringing for three days.

Ulyana heard it through the floorboards of her attic room, a thin silver thread of sound stitching itself into her sleep, and by the second morning she understood what it meant: the guild's customers had come to collect. Not the goods they'd bought — those were long since delivered, bottled and shelved and traded away — but the *consequences*. The old gods were awake, and every miracle the Gilded Thorn had ever sold was coming due like interest on a loan no one remembered taking.

She came down the stairs on the third morning to find the counting-room full.

Not with customers. With guild members.

They stood in loose clusters between the counters, the way people stand when they've been arguing in whispers for hours and have run out of whisper. Old Fenwick the bottler, his hands stained permanently violet from decades of decanting memories. The twins, Ash and Sorrel, who ran the luck counter and finished each other's sentences even when they disagreed — especially when they disagreed. Marta of the candle-floor, soot to her elbows. A dozen more, faces Ulyana had passed in corridors for two years without learning their names, because names weren't entries in a ledger and she'd been careful not to keep books about people.

They all stopped talking when she appeared.

Vespera stood at the head of the room, behind the mother-desk that had belonged to every guildmother since the founding, her hands folded on its scarred oak surface. She looked, as always, like a woman who had already done the sums.

"Ulyana," she said. "Good. We can begin."

"Begin what?"

"The question," Vespera said, "of what we do with you."

* * *

It was Marta who laid it out, because Marta had never learned diplomacy and didn't intend to start now. She planted herself in front of Ulyana with her arms crossed, soot flaking off her sleeves onto the clean boards.

"The court sent word. Proper word, sealed, through the toll-gates." She said it flatly, the way you read an invoice aloud. "They know where your soul is kept. They know where *you* are kept. And they've offered terms."

"What terms?"

"Surrender," said Ash.

"You," said Sorrel.

"Or," Vespera said quietly, "we negotiate."

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well. Ulyana watched it fall through every face present — Fenwick's grimace, the twins' identical flinches, young Pell the runner looking anywhere but at her.

"Negotiate what?" Ulyana asked. "There's nothing to negotiate. My contract is flawed. The apprenticeship papers were forged — Vespera knows, I told her everything, the audit trail is—"

"I believe you," Vespera said. "That has never been the issue."

"Then what *is* the issue?"

Vespera unfolded her hands. For a moment — only a moment — she looked tired in a way Ulyana had never seen her look. Not the performance of weariness she sometimes wore to make a hard bargain seem reluctant. Actual tiredness, worn down to the grain.

"The issue," Vespera said, "is precedent. If we contest the claim and lose — if midwinter ends and the trial goes against us — the court doesn't just take your soul, girl. It takes the guild's standing. Every contract we hold becomes suspect. Every asset in our vaults becomes seizable. Two hundred years of careful work, dissolved in an afternoon, because one ledger-keeper wanted to argue fine print with gods."

"And if we surrender her," Davor said from the doorway, "we teach the court that forging papers works. That any child can be sold by anyone with a steady hand and a seal." He hadn't been invited. He came in anyway, moving to stand beside Ulyana, and she felt the room recalibrate around his presence the way water recalibrates around a dropped stone. "I did collections long enough to know how courts read kindness. As appetite."

"You're not guild," Marta snapped.

"No," Davor agreed. "I'm worse. I'm someone who's been inside their halls. Ask me what surrender buys you. I'll tell you: nothing. They don't release claims. They *roll them over*. You give them Ulyana, and within a year they're back asking who else in this building has paper on their name. And there will be someone. There always is."

Silence. The fire in the grate ticked.

Fenwick spoke first, slow as syrup poured in winter. "He's not wrong. That's the trouble. Nobody in this room is wrong. That's how I know we're ruined."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned down to stubs, and then Marta lit new ones, and they argued past those too.

Ulyana listened mostly. She had learned that in a room full of frightened people, the accountant is most useful quiet, tracking numbers while everyone else tracks grievances. And the numbers told a story.

Twelve guild members present. Of those, four — Fenwick, the twins, and old Hesta from the memory-vaults — leaned toward negotiation, which everyone understood was surrender wearing a better coat. Four held with her: Marta, Pell, the candle-boy Tam, and gruff Sister Quill from the receiving desk, who hadn't spoken a word all day but sat with her chair turned pointedly toward Ulyana's side of the room like a flag planted in territory. The remaining four drifted somewhere in the middle, terrified of both outcomes, which meant they would vote whichever way the wind smelled safer.

And Vespera. Vespera was the wind.

Ulyana caught her watching the room the way a card player watches a table — not the cards, the players. Reading tells. Running sums Ulyana couldn't see.

When the argument wore itself down to exhausted circling, Vespera raised one hand, and the room went still the way it always went still, two hundred years of habit compressed into a single gesture.

"There is a mechanism older than any of us," Vespera said. "Written into the founding charter, before my time, before my predecessor's time. When the guild cannot agree, the guild votes. Not the guildmother. Not the senior partners. Every sworn member, one voice each. And the question binds."

"Binds how?" Pell asked.

"Whoever the vote decides against must accept it. Or leave." Vespera's eyes found Ulyana's across the candlelight. "Those are the founding terms. Our founders wrote them into the charter when they signed their own bargain with the under-court. They understood that a trade-house divided is a trade-house dead."

"And if I refuse to accept the result?" Ulyana asked.

Vespera smiled, and it was the worst smile Ulyana had ever seen on her — gentle, almost maternal, and underneath it the cold arithmetic of a woman who had survived something terrible once and built an empire on the lesson.

"Then you are no longer guild, and the guild owes you nothing, and protects you not at all, and mourns you briefly." She spread her hands. "I would rather not learn what you choose. But the choice is real. It has always been real. That is what nobody in this room understands yet: I am not offering you cruelty, Ulyana. I am offering you the same terms I lived under when I was younger than you. Terms bind. Terms save. Terms are the only wall between people like us and the weather."

"The weather," Marta said, jerking her chin at the window, where snow fell thick and silent in what should have been harvest month. "You mean like that?"

"Yes," Vespera said. "Exactly like that."

* * *

The vote was called for the following evening, which gave everyone one day to campaign, scheme, or pray — and given the current state of the heavens, praying was the riskiest of the three.

Ulyana spent the day doing the only thing that had ever steadied her: she read.

She pulled the founding charter from the deep vault, blowing dust off vellum gone the color of weak tea, and read the guild's original bargain line by line while snow ticked against the shutters. The founders' signatures marched down the final page, seven of them, ink brown with age. Below the last signature, in smaller script, the voting clause — and beneath *that*, something she had missed in every previous reading, because she had never had reason to look:

*Should the guild ever vote to deliver up one of its own, the charter itself shall be considered breached, and the under-court's claim shall extend to all.*

She read it three times. Then she sat back, heart hammering, and made herself consider whether it meant what it seemed to mean. Charter law was like any other law: full of traps for the eager reader. But the phrasing was plain. The clause wasn't a warning. It was a *cost*. The founders had priced betrayal into the document itself. Deliver up one member, and the whole guild became collateral.

Which meant Vespera either hadn't read this far, or —

No. Vespera had read everything. Vespera forgot nothing. Which meant Vespera knew, and had called the vote anyway.

Ulyana closed the charter and sat for a long moment in the vault's cold green light, listening to the mountain of the city breathe around her.

Unless Vespera believed the clause wouldn't be enforced. Unless Vespera knew something about the court's current state — its desperation, its hunger, the way repossession makes creditors flexible — that suggested the old rules had gone soft. The gods had slept three hundred years. Institutions rot in the dark.

Two readings. Two ledgers, balanced to opposite totals. One of them was wrong, and tomorrow night would tell her which.

She carried the charter upstairs and did not show it to anyone. Not yet. A flaw in a contract is worth nothing until the moment of enforcement; showing it early just gives the other party time to amend.

She had learned that from Vespera.

* * *

Snow fell all day, and by evening it lay knee-deep in the streets, and still the guild members came — wading through drifts, stamping white off their boots in the entry hall, drawn by whatever strange gravity the vote exerted. Even members who'd left the city years ago. Even Corvin, who'd taken his cut and retired to a vineyard south of the salt marshes and swore he'd never set foot in the counting-room again. He arrived smelling of wine and fear, and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes.

Twenty-three sworn members in total. Twenty-one made it through the snow.

Vespera presided from the mother-desk. Davor stood at the back with his arms crossed, not voting, not allowed to vote, watching the room the way he'd once watched doors in his collecting days — cataloguing exits, reading shoulders.

Sable wasn't there. Ulyana had put her in the memory-vault's inner room with instructions to stay quiet no matter what she heard through the floor. But when Ulyana glanced at the ceiling, she imagined she could feel the girl up there, listening the way children listen: entirely.

"The question before the guild," Vespera said, and her voice carried without effort, the way it always did, as though the room itself were slightly afraid of her, "is whether to accept the under-court's terms regarding the ledger-keeper Ulyana Okonkwo — namely, negotiated surrender of her claim — or to refuse those terms and contest the claim at trial, bearing the full risk of failure."

"Say it plain," Marta demanded. "Do we hand her over, yes or no?"

"If you prefer. Yes or no. Surrender Ulyana Okonkwo to the under-court."

Hands would be raised. Ulyana had expected hands. But old Hesta stood up instead, ancient knees cracking, and said, "By secret ballot. Founding custom. No one should have to raise a hand against a friend in front of her face."

A few heads nodded. Vespera considered, then inclined her head — gracious, controlled. "Secret ballot. Tam, bring the box."

The candle-boy fetched the black lacquer box used for settling disputes over stolen inventory. Slips of paper went around. Ulyana watched her colleagues bend over their slips, hiding them with cupped hands, and thought: *this is what a family looks like when it stops being one.* Each person alone with a piece of paper, deciding alone, the way debt decides things — silently, privately, in the space between one number and the next.

She took her own slip. She looked at it for a long moment. Then, because she was who she was, she wrote on it, in her small precise hand:

*No — and see charter, p. 4, clause 9, before you sleep tonight.*

She folded it small and dropped it in the box with the others.

Tam collected the box and set it before Vespera. Vespera drew out the slips one by one and read them aloud — not the names, never the names, just the answers, her voice level as a plumb line.

"No."
"No."
"Yes."
"Yes."
"No."
"Yes."
"Yes."

Seven slips. Three yes, four no. Ulyana's stomach performed slow arithmetic.

"No."
"Yes."
"No."
"No."

Eleven slips. Five yes, six no. The room had stopped breathing. Even Vespera's reading slowed, fractionally, each answer landing like a stone on a scale.

"No."
"No."
"Yes."
"Yes."
"No."

Sixteen slips. Seven yes, nine no. Marta exhaled shakily. Fenwick closed his eyes.

Four slips remained. Vespera drew the seventeenth.

"No."

Eighteen. Eight yes, ten no. Two left.

Vespera reached into the box. Her fingers paused there — Ulyana saw it, saw the pause, saw Vespera's eyes flicker down at the slip she'd drawn as if checking something — and then she read:

"No."

Nineteen. Eight yes, eleven no.

One slip left. Vespera held it, unread, and for the first time all evening something moved behind her face — some calculation completing itself, some sum arriving at a total she had not predicted or had predicted and hoped wrong. She looked at the slip for three full seconds. Then she placed it, unread, back into the box, and closed the lid.

"Nineteen votes cast," she said. "Eleven against surrender. The motion fails. The guild contests the claim." Her voice never changed. Her hands, folding themselves on the desk, never trembled. Only Ulyana, who had spent two years learning to read this woman the way she read contracts, saw the tell: Vespera had not needed to count the last slip. Eleven was already decisive. Whatever was written on that nineteenth-and-twentieth voice, Vespera had chosen not to hear it.

Or chosen to keep it.

"The guild stands together," Vespera said, "and the trial proceeds at midwinter's end. Meeting concluded."

Chairs scraped. Voices rose — relief, argument, Marta laughing out loud and clapping Pell on the shoulder, Fenwick muttering about ruin in tones that suggested he'd already reconciled himself to being right later. The family, fractured all day, reknit itself loosely, imperfectly, the way a bone knits: stronger at the break or not, depending on the setting.

Ulyana stayed in her seat, watching Vespera.

"The last slip," Ulyana said quietly, when the room had half-emptied. "You didn't read it."

"No," Vespera said.

"Why?"

Vespera lifted the black box, opened the lid, and drew out the final slip. She did not unfold it. She held it a moment between two fingers — and then she fed it to the candle flame on the mother-desk and let it burn to nothing, dropping the ash onto a saucer like a woman disposing of evidence, which, Ulyana supposed, was exactly what it was.

"Because it said yes," Vespera said. "And mine was going to say no. And I find—" for one unguarded instant her composure cracked, showing something old and exhausted underneath, something that might have been relief or might have been grief, "—that after two hundred years, I did not want to know which of us the guild had truly chosen."

Then she gathered the box, and the charter lay open on the corner of the desk where Ulyana had left it that afternoon — page four, clause nine, deliberately visible — and Vespera's eyes rested on it for a moment, long and unreadable.

"So," Vespera said softly. "You found the founders' clause. Clever girl." She turned toward the door, then paused. "Read the rest of page five before you celebrate. There is an exception written there, in the founder Maghda's hand. An exception concerning guildmothers."

She left. The door clicked shut behind her.

Ulyana sat alone in the guttering candlelight, snow ticking at the windows, and reached slowly for the charter, turning to page five, where in faded brown ink a single line waited beneath the founders' signatures — a line that would change everything she thought she knew about the woman who had just burned her own vote:

*Excepting the guildmother, whose soul was pledged at the founding, and who may not be saved by any vote of the guild.*
