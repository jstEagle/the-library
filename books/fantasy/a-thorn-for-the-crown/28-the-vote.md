## 28. The Vote

The counting-room had never held so many people. Ulyana counted them out of habit — thirty-one guild members packed between the shelves, some standing on the iron rungs of the vault ladder, others perched atop ledgers they were supposed to be balancing. Candlelight caught the gilt thorn above the door and made it bleed.

She stood at the center table with the oldest ledger open before her, and felt like a figure being audited rather than an auditor. Every eye in the room was doing arithmetic with her: what was she worth, weighed against what she would cost?

Vespera stood at the far end of the table, hands flat on the wood, the way she did when closing a bad quarter's books.

"The motion is simple," Vespera said. Her voice carried the same evenness it used for announcing interest rates. "The under-court has named its claimant. Ulyana Okonkwo, soul pawned in her infancy against debts incurred by her mistress, debt transferred through three hands to the court beneath the mountain. The claim is valid by every rule we honor. If we shelter her past midwinter's end, the court will seize the guild's charter, our inventory, and every soul contract in this house — including," and here her gaze moved slowly across the room, "yours. All of yours."

A murmur ran along the shelves. Ulyana watched it move like wind through wheat.

"If we surrender her," Vespera continued, "the court takes one soul and leaves thirty intact. That is the arithmetic. I did not write it. I have simply read it aloud." She straightened. "I call the vote. Speak your number."

Every guild member had a number, assigned at induction, spoken at meetings so that votes could not be swayed by faces or friendships. Ulyana had always thought it cold. Tonight she understood it as mercy. It let people hide behind their numbers while they betrayed or saved her.

"One," said Master Ferro from the wine-dark corner where he kept the bottled luck. "One."

"Two," said the twins who mended memory-bottles, together, always together. Ulyana's stomach dropped a little. They had taught her to seal wax.

"Three," said old Marette, who had sold the guild her own happy childhood at forty and never once asked for it back.

The numbers climbed. Ulyana stopped keeping tally after twelve because her hands were shaking too badly to be trusted with figures, which had never happened to her before, and she hated it, hated that her body refused to treat her own soul as a ledger entry.

Then Davor spoke. He stood by the door with his arms crossed, not a member of the guild in any formal sense, no number assigned, and yet his voice went into the room like a coin into a well.

"I don't have a number," he said. "But I've collected debts for eleven years. I know what it looks like when people pay for something they didn't buy. I'm not watching it happen here."

Silence. Then Sable, from her seat on the stairs where she'd been hiding her face in her knees all evening: "Fourteen. I mean — whatever hers is. I vote hers."

"That's not how—" Ferro began.

"It's exactly how," Sable said, lifting her head, and there was something in her voice that hadn't been there before, something older than a girl breaking into vaults, a small echo of a father's thunder. The candles guttered toward her and then away, as if uncertain whom to obey.

Voting continued. Numbers rose into the twenties. When the last voice fell silent, Vespera drew the slate toward herself and chalked the count without expression.

"Sixteen for surrender," she said. "Fifteen for shelter." She looked up. "One abstention. Mine."

The room erupted — relief and fury in equal measure, twins shouting at Marette, Marette shouting back that she had children, Marette's children were grown, Sable's voice cracking over the noise — but Ulyana heard none of it clearly, because she was looking at Vespera.

"You abstained," Ulyana said.

"I called the vote fairly. That is what I owe you." Vespera set down the chalk. Her face was stone, but her thumb pressed white against the table's edge, the only crack in it. "You wanted the family to choose. It chose. Badly, I think. But it chose."

"Then it's done," Davor said. "We shelter her."

"It is done," Vespera agreed, and began gathering her papers with brisk, ordinary motions, as if the meeting were ending rather than the world. "Midwinter's end is nineteen days off. I suggest everyone spend them productively."

* * *

Ulyana found her afterward in the vaults, among the memories.

It was the coldest room in the guild and the most crowded — thousands of bottles racked floor to ceiling, each holding a swirl of colored light: a first kiss in green, a funeral in gray-blue, someone's entire wedding day burning gold in quart glass. Ulyana had catalogued this room twice and still found bottles she couldn't account for. She suspected Vespera liked it that way.

Vespera stood before the oldest rack, where the earliest acquisitions slept in hand-blown glass gone slightly crooked with age. She did not turn around when Ulyana entered. She never needed to; she tracked footsteps the way other people tracked debts.

"Sixteen to fifteen," Ulyana said. "By one voice. Yours didn't count either way, and it counted more than any of theirs."

"Yes."

"Why abstain?"

Vespera touched one bottle — a small one, blue-gray, unmarked — with the back of one finger, briefly, like touching a scar. "Because if I voted to keep you, and the court came, I would have chosen you over thirty people, and I am not certain I'm permitted to be that person anymore. And if I voted to give you up, I would have been the woman who fed a girl to the mountain to save her shop." A pause. "I have been both of those women before. Neither of them sleeps well."

"What's in that bottle?" Ulyana asked, though she knew better than to ask. Inventory discipline.

"Nothing you'll ever audit." Vespera turned. In the light of the memory-racks her face was lit from below, gold and gray, younger and older than her years simultaneously. "You think tonight was a victory."

"I think it was a choice. Those aren't always the same thing."

Something flickered across the guildmother's expression — surprise, maybe, or the particular grief of hearing your own cynicism spoken back to you by someone you'd hoped would stay innocent of it.

"No," Vespera said quietly. "They are not." She smoothed her apron, and the flicker was gone, banked like a covered fire. "Nineteen days, Okonkwo. Spend them learning everything about the under-court you can. And spend them saying goodbye to things you might lose. Both lists are longer than you think."

* * *

That night Ulyana worked late, because working was the only prayer she trusted.

She sat in the counting-room reconstructing the vote in her head, member by member, trying to understand the fifteen who had saved her and the sixteen who hadn't, and whether the difference between them was anything she could name. Marette had children. So did Ferro, though he never spoke of them except when drunk, and then only numbers. Fear wasn't cowardice, she told herself. Fear was just arithmetic done under pressure, with bad data and no time. She had spent her whole life believing arithmetic could be made honest if you were careful enough. Tonight it had come within one abstention of selling her.

The candle beside her flickered. Not the prayer candle — those had been behaving since the gods woke, flickering constantly, greedy for attention. This was an ordinary tallow stub, and it leaned sideways as if a draft had walked through the closed room.

Ulyana looked up.

Sable stood in the doorway, wrapped in a blanket, bare feet on cold boards.

"Can't sleep either?"

"They're dreaming loud," Sable said. She meant the gods. She had explained it once — that since waking, the old gods dreamed in their sleep the way dogs run in theirs, and their dreams leaked, and sensitive people got them secondhand. "Someone's dreaming about snow. Deep snow. Snow that doesn't stop." She hugged the blanket tighter. "Ulyana. Why did they save me? At the vote. I'm not even guild. I'm nobody's entry. I broke in and stole shelf space and you hid me anyway, and then half the room voted to burn their own books rather than hand over a thief and a stranger."

Ulyana considered lying — a kind lie, a tidy lie — and found she was too tired.

"Because Davor stood up first," she said. "And because you said *I vote hers* in a voice that shook the candle flames. And because some of them are better than their fear, and the rest needed someone to show them they could be." She pushed back her chair. "Come sit. You're letting the cold in."

Sable crossed the room and sat, pulling her feet up onto the chair, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the candle burned straight again.

"My father traded my name," Sable said finally. "Did you know that? Before I could walk. Not sold — traded. Swapped it for something he needed worse. That's why the court can't find me properly, why I slipped through their doors like water. I'm not fully on any of their books." She stared at the flame. "He didn't even remember doing it. The locket proved that. He remembered *me* eventually, but the trade itself was nothing to him. A rounding error."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be angry with me. It helps more." Sable's jaw set. "Sixteen people voted to sell you. Sixteen people looked at you and saw a cost. My father looked at me and saw a rounding error. I want to know what makes people do that, so I can make sure I never do it."

"Keep asking," Ulyana said. "That's most of it."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin, and with it the discovery.

It was Ferro who raised the alarm, bellowing from the top of the vault stairs, and Ulyana took them two at a time with her heart already knowing what she would find, because she had balanced enough books to recognize the shape of a missing line.

The oldest ledger was gone.

Not hidden — gone. Its chain was cut, cleanly, with a tool that left no burr. The dust on the shelf showed one rectangle of untouched air where a book had rested for longer than anyone could remember. Vespera's chair in her office sat empty. Her coat was gone from its peg. On the counting table, weighted under the inkwell, lay a single sheet in Vespera's precise hand:

*The vote bound the guild. It did not bind me. My soul has been collateral in this house since before any of you could write your names, and I will not let a girl with a grudge negotiate my ransom. I am going to the mountain to settle my own account. Do not follow. The terms I can get alone are better than any terms you would ruin.*

*Everything is owed. Including me.*

*— V.*

"She cut her own chain," Ferro said hollowly. "Thirty years I've known her. Thirty years she told us the chains were sacred."

"She told us they were necessary," Ulyana corrected. Her voice sounded very calm in her own ears, the way it did when the numbers went wrong somewhere deep. "There's a difference. I should have seen it sooner."

Davor appeared in the doorway, already dressed for travel, snow melting on his shoulders — he'd circled the building, found her tracks heading east toward the mountain road, already filling with fresh fall. "Blizzard's coming down off the peaks early. By nightfall her trail's gone. We leave now or we leave never."

"We," Ulyana said.

"You voted to shelter each other," Davor said. "All of you. That includes her, whether she wants it or not." He glanced at the note, read it twice, and his jaw tightened. "Alone, she'll cut a deal that costs someone else. That's what alone buys. I've collected from enough lonely people to know."

Sable came down the stairs with her boots already laced and the locket of her father's voice hanging outside her coat, glinting. "Snow dreams," she said simply. "She's walking into them. Someone who knows the way should walk with her."

Ulyana looked around the counting-room — at the frightened members clutching their numbers, at the racks of impossible things that suddenly seemed less like inventory and more like hostages, at the gilt thorn above the door catching the first gray light.

Then she went to the shelf, took down the newest ledger, and wrote the day's entry in a steady hand:

*Assets departed: one guildmother, self-removed. One ledger, eldest, stolen. Liabilities outstanding: everything.*

She closed the book, pocketed her gloves, and followed the others out into the falling snow.

Behind them, above the door, the gilt thorn gleamed like something waiting to be paid for.
