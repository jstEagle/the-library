## 29. Chasing the Ledger

The blizzard came down off the peaks like a debt being collected.

Ulyana had seen storms before — hedge-witches loved to lecture about weather as a form of accounting, snow falling on the just and unjust alike because the sky kept no favorites. But this was different. This was *directional*. The wind drove at their backs with the flat insistence of something that wanted them moving, wanted them at the mountain's mouth before midwinter's eve closed the books on everything they'd failed to save. Snow in harvest month had been an omen. Snow like this was a summons.

She pulled her scarf higher over her nose and squinted ahead through the white roar. Three figures and one small flame. Sable walked closest to her, hood up, gloved hand tucked into Ulyana's elbow, and every few strides the girl's breath came out faintly blue — not cold-blue but lamp-blue, god-blood blue, the color the tunnels had burned when they'd descended the first time. The storm seemed to part around her by half a step, which was either mercy or recognition.

Davor broke trail. He was built for it — broad shoulders turned into the wind like a plow-blade, boots finding the old collector's road under the drifts without looking for it. He'd walked this route for eleven years, he'd told her once, back when his job was finding people who owed things and taking from them until the arithmetic balanced. He knew every switchback the way Ulyana knew a ledger line: by its errors.

"Waystation ahead," he shouted over the wind, without turning. "Half a league. There'll be firewood. There won't be much else."

"There never is," Ulyana shouted back.

Sable tugged her elbow. "What if Vespera got there first?"

"She did get there first." Ulyana had done the arithmetic herself, three nights ago, standing over the empty shelf where the oldest ledger had lived since before the guild had a name. Vespera had left the same night she conceded the vote — no farewell, no note, nothing taken but the one book she was never supposed to carry out of the vaults. "That's why we're walking into a blizzard instead of sleeping."

They crested the last rise and there it was: the waystation, a stone hut crouched against the slope like a penitent, its chimney smoking.

Smoking.

Ulyana stopped so fast Sable bumped into her. "Davor."

He'd already seen it. His whole body had changed — weight dropping low, hands loose, the shift she'd watched him make once outside a debtor's door when he thought violence was coming. "Stay behind me."

"It could be travelers."

"Travelers don't light fires in a collector's waystation." He said it flatly, but Ulyana heard what lived under the words. Travelers didn't know about collector's waystations. There were maybe six people alive who knew the mountain roads well enough to find this hut in a whiteout, and Davor had been one of them, and Ilya had been the other.

* * *

The door opened before Davor could knock.

Ilya Marchetti stood in the frame with snow melting off his coat in gray rivulets, and he looked exactly as he had in the bazaar and exactly as he hadn't in Davor's stories: tired, competent, and carrying himself like a man who had made a decision some hours ago and was now enduring the wait to see if it killed him. His eyes went past Davor to Ulyana, then to Sable, and something in his face flinched.

"Your fire's lit," he said. "I didn't want you walking into the cold after me. Come in or don't."

Nobody moved. The blizzard filled the silence with static.

"You marked our route." Davor's voice was very quiet. "In the market. You let us pass and then you sold the map of how we passed. A child nearly died in those halls because the bailiffs knew where we'd be."

"I know." Ilya didn't flinch from it, and Ulyana found, to her irritation, that she respected that more than any apology. "I've had two weeks to know it. Ask me anything. Don't ask me to unsay it, because I can't."

"Why are we even talking to him?" Sable's voice cracked out sharp and young. "He works for them. He's worked for them his whole life."

"He quit," Davor said.

"I didn't quit," Ilya said. "There's a difference. Quitting is clean. What I did—" He exhaled. "Come inside. Please. If I'm going to betray you again, at least let it happen somewhere warm."

It was Ulyana who decided, though she suspected all of them would pretend otherwise later. She looked at the man in the doorway — at the way he held his hands open, palms out, a collector showing he carried no chains — and she thought about ledgers. About how an entry wasn't the debt itself, only the record of it. Records could be amended. It was practically the first thing she'd ever learned.

"Firewood's expensive," she said. "We're coming in."

* * *

Inside, the waystation was one room and honest about it: iron stove, plank bunks, a table scarred by decades of collectors' knives. Ilya had laid the fire high and hot, and there was a kettle on it, and tea, actual tea, which in a famine winter was worth more than most souls on the guild's books.

Davor took the chair facing the door. Old habits. Sable sat against the wall where she could see everyone, also old habits, newer ones. Ulyana stood at the table with her mittens still on, because she didn't trust her hands yet.

"Talk," she said.

Ilya poured four cups without asking who wanted one. "You know what I was. Eleven years collecting for the Thorn, then the Thorn's debts moved under-mountain and I collected for the court. Same work, worse lighting." He set the kettle down. "When Davor refused the Okonkwo child's debt, I covered for him. Did he tell you that? No? Of course not. He wouldn't. I filed the paper wrong on purpose so the refusal looked like clerical error and not principle. That's the kind of friend I was."

"I didn't ask you to," Davor said.

"No. That was the problem. You never asked anyone for anything. Made you very easy to love and very hard to protect." Ilya wrapped his hands around his own cup. "Then you were gone, and I kept collecting, and I told myself the work was the work. Then the gods woke up. And I saw what collection looks like when the creditor is a god."

He looked at Sable. "There's a cell block under the petition hall. Glass cells, for souls pending trial. Do you know who's in them?"

Sable's chin came up. "Children."

"Forty-one children whose names were traded before they could talk. Your father's arrangement, among others." Ilya didn't soften it, and Ulyana understood that too — this was a man paying down a debt of his own, and he meant to itemize every entry. "I've been walking out of that block every night for a month telling myself it's procedure. Two weeks ago you escaped past me in the archive maze and I marked your route, and the bailiffs swept the cells afterward, and they moved nine children deeper down, where the cold comes from." His voice finally frayed. "Where the cold comes *from*, Davor. I've heard it. It sounds like counting."

The stove ticked. Outside, the storm leaned on the roof.

"So I memorized the procedures," Ilya said. "All of them. Intake, trial, appeal, reversion. Every form the court can file, every deadline, every loophole the judge pretends doesn't exist because nobody's read the fine print in three hundred years. I have it all up here—" he tapped his temple "—because the court burns paper but they've never figured out how to burn a collector. We're too useful. Too much like them." He set the cup down and slid it across the table toward Ulyana. "And I'm coming with you."

"To do what?" Ulyana asked. Not cruelly. She genuinely wanted to hear him say it.

"To stand in front of the Sleeping Judge and recite, verbatim, the procedural grounds on which every soul-pawn entered into the court's custody — and watch his face when he realizes a mortal has his own rulebook by heart." Ilya almost smiled. It was not a warm expression. "You're the auditor. You found the flaw in the forged apprenticeship. But a flaw isn't enough if you can't file it correctly, and the filing requirements—" he shrugged "—are a nightmare. Deliberately. Written by gods who assumed no one would ever read them."

"And if we say no?" Sable asked. "If we leave you here?"

"Then I follow you anyway, at a distance, badly, and probably die of exposure, which will be embarrassing for everyone." He glanced at Davor. "I'd rather you said yes. But I stopped expecting fair treatment around chapter eleven years ago."

Davor hadn't spoken since the doorway. Now he stood, slow, joints cracking in the cold, and crossed to the table. For a moment the two men just looked at each other — eleven years of partnership, one betrayal, one rescue that wasn't quite a rescue, one child saved and forty-one children not.

"You covered for me," Davor said. "I never knew."

"You never needed to know. That was the point."

"That's also the problem." Davor rubbed his face. "You keep deciding what people need without asking them. Same as the court does. Same as—" He stopped himself, and Ulyana saw him decide not to say *Vespera*. "I don't forgive you. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But I'm not stupid enough to walk into that court without the man who knows its procedures."

Ilya nodded slowly, as if accepting a sentence rather than a pardon. "Fair terms."

"They're not terms. They're just true."

* * *

They slept in shifts. Ulyana took second watch, sitting by the stove while the others breathed in the dark, and Ilya came and sat across from her at midnight, uninvited, keeping his hands visible the way he always did.

"You hate me," he observed.

"I'm auditing you," she corrected. "Hate comes later, if warranted."

"What's the audit say so far?"

Ulyana fed a stick to the fire. "You betrayed us. You also gave us passage twice. You spent eleven years doing monstrous work competently and one month trying to stop. The ledger's mixed." She looked up. "But here's what I actually want to know. The route you marked. In the maze. Was it a betrayal or was it a test?"

Ilya was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "Both," he said finally. "I needed the court to believe I was still theirs. A marked route bought me another week of access. Another week meant nine more children I could move to the shallow cells where the thaw will reach first." He met her eyes. "I'm not good, Ulyana. I'm *useful*, and I'm trying to aim it right. If that's not enough—"

"It's not enough," she said. "But it's what I've got. Welcome aboard."

Something in his shoulders let go, some tension he'd probably been carrying since the bazaar. "You sound like Davor."

"No," Ulyana said. "Davor forgives people. I extend credit. There's a difference, and mine has interest terms."

For the first time, Ilya laughed — short, rusty, surprised out of him. From the bunks, Sable's voice floated up, dry as ash: "Keep it down, some of us are inheriting divinity in the morning."

* * *

They left before dawn, four now instead of three, and the mountain rose out of the dark ahead of them like a wall between worlds. The storm had slackened to a hard steady fall, and the air had changed — thinner, older, tasting faintly of salt and candle smoke. Somewhere beneath the snow, Ulyana knew, the tunnels waited, and below the tunnels the cathedral of frozen petitions, and below that the place where the cold came from, counting.

Vespera was up there already. Had been for days. With the oldest ledger, and whatever bargain she meant to cut with it, and Ulyana's name written in her handwriting in a book older than sin.

Ilya fell into step beside her as the slope steepened. "One thing you should know before we're underground," he said quietly. "The trial date. Midwinter's end. It's not a hearing."

"I know. All outstanding souls revert."

"That's not what I mean." He glanced up at the peak. "Midwinter's end is also when the court settles its *own* accounts. Once every three hundred years, the gods audit each other. It's the one day the judge can't refuse a properly filed motion — house rules. Whoever wrote the procedures built in a day when even gods have to answer questions." His breath smoked. "Vespera knows this. It's why she ran now. She means to file something first."

Ulyana stared up at the black shoulder of the mountain, at the thin thread of woodsmoke rising from a crack near its base where no traveler had any business being.

"What would she file?"

Ilya's voice was very gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than the storm had. "A claimant gets to propose a substitute asset. One soul of equivalent weight, offered freely, discharges the debt of another." He paused. "Vespera's soul was the guild's first collateral. She's spent sixty years making sure it stayed encumbered — because as long as it was pledged to the guild, the court couldn't take it, and neither could anyone else. But the guild dissolved. Her shield dissolved with it."

The woodsmoke thinned and vanished. The mountain waited.

"She's offering herself," Ulyana said.

"She's offering herself," Ilya agreed, "and she's going to beat you there by two days, and the intake clerk accepts claims in the order received."

Ulyana started to run.
