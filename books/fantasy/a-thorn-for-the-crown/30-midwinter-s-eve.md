## 30. Midwinter's Eve

The mountain's mouth had changed since they left it.

Ulyana noticed it first as a wrongness in the light — the blue glow that Sable's blood kindled in the lamps had spread, seeping out of the tunnels and into the storm itself, so that the snow falling around the entrance fell faintly luminous, each flake a small cold star going out. The drifts stood higher than a man. Somewhere beneath them, she knew, the court was waking its accounts.

"Three days," Davor said. He said it the way he said everything now, flat and economical, like a man counting what remained of himself. The toll-wights' three days sat on him strangely — not visibly aged, but *thinned*, as if someone had skimmed the cream off him and left the rest standing. "That's how long midwinter's end takes to arrive. Whatever we mean to do in there, we do it before the last snowflake falls."

"We know what we mean to do," Sable said. She had her father's locket clenched in her fist, and had had it clenched there for most of the climb, Ulyana had noticed, though she never opened it. Saving it. Hoarding one conversation the way a miser hoards a single coin. "We read her contract aloud. We find the flaw again. We make them hear it."

"They didn't hear it the first time."

"They weren't *made* to hear it the first time." Sable lifted her chin, and for a moment the god-light in the snow bent toward her, subtle as iron filings toward a magnet. "My father's seat is empty up there. Empty seats make courts nervous. They'll listen this time."

Ulyana said nothing. She had the oldest ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, strapped where a heart would be, and she had not let Davor carry it, or Sable, or anyone, through nine miles of blizzard. It was hers to carry. That was the whole shape of her life, if she was honest about it: things hers to carry because no one else would.

Ilya appeared at her shoulder out of the white, soundless as debt itself. He had been doing that for two days — materializing beside whichever of them looked most likely to break, offering nothing but his presence, which was somehow worse than commentary and better than comfort.

"The threshold's ahead," he said. "There'll be someone waiting at it. There always is, on reckoning eve. The court posts a doorkeeper to take final declarations."

"A doorkeeper," Davor repeated.

"Mm." Ilya's face did something complicated. "Except the doorkeeper isn't usually a person. So when I tell you there's a woman standing in the snow with her hands folded like she's about to pour tea, understand that I don't like it."

Ulyana's stomach dropped before her mind caught up. But she already knew. Some part of her had known since the burned cottage, since the contract lying unburnt on an anvil of ash, patient as a spider. Some debts walk ahead of you.

She pushed through the drifts anyway.

* * *

The hedge-witch stood exactly where the mountain's shadow began, at the line where snow stopped being weather and started being architecture. Behind her, half-glimpsed through the falling light, the doors of the under-court stood open — vast slabs of black stone carved with columns of figures, numbers worn smooth by centuries of petitioners touching them, hoping arithmetic could save them. The witch's hands were indeed folded. Her shawl was grey. Her face —

Her face was kind.

That was the unbearable thing. Eleven years in that cottage above the fen, and Ulyana had built her memory of Morwenna out of splinters: the back of a hand, the smell of burnt hair from failed hexes, the ledger kept in a child's blood because ink was expensive. But standing here, framed by the doors of the place that owned her, the old woman looked like every grandmother in every painting of homecoming. Warm eyes. A smile with real creases earned by real smiling, once, somewhere, for someone.

"Hello, little bird," Morwenna said. "You've grown into your sums."

"Don't." Ulyana's voice came out smaller than she wanted. She planted her feet. "Don't call me that."

"You hated it even as a girl. You'd correct me. 'My name is Ulyana, mistress.' Every time. Eleven years, and you never once let it go." The witch tilted her head. "Do you know why I kept using it?"

"I imagine you'll tell me whether I ask or not."

"Because a name is the only thing you ever refused to sell." Morwenna unfolded her hands, and Ulyana saw that they were trembling — actually trembling, the tremor of age or cold or something older than either. "Everything else you gave up without a fight. Your labor. Your sleep. Your childhood, which I took in installments and told myself it was tuition. But the name you held onto like a dog with a bone. I used to lie awake thinking about that. About what it meant that the one thing you defended was the thing that made you *you*."

Behind Ulyana, Davor shifted his weight — she heard the crunch of snow, felt him arranging himself between her and the witch without appearing to. Sable's hand found hers. Cold fingers, divine blood, human grip.

"I didn't come here for a eulogy of my childhood," Ulyana said. "You're posted at this door for a reason. Say it."

Morwenna sighed, and the sigh fogged the air between them, and in the fog — Ulyana saw it clearly, because she was the guild's auditor and could not *not* see it — hung shapes. Figures. Columns of numbers written in breath, in frost, in the particular grammar of contracts. The witch was not merely standing here. She was *presenting*. This whole meeting was a document.

"The court is about to call its reckoning," Morwenna said. "Midwinter's end. All outstanding souls revert to the house. Yours among them, little bird. In three days, the flame they keep in glass goes dark, and what is left of you belongs to the mountain forever. Unless." She paused, delicately, a merchant letting the customer feel the weight of the goods. "Unless the debt is *transferred*."

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I read the terms eleven years ago, in your kitchen, in a book I wasn't supposed to be able to read yet. That's the entire reason any of this has happened. Do you remember? I found my own name in your ledger. Everything since has been interest accruing on that moment." Ulyana stepped forward, out from behind Davor's careful positioning, because some things you had to say to a person's face. "So no. Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"It isn't for sale," Morwenna said softly. "It's being *given*."

And the fog-figures rearranged themselves, and Ulyana, who could balance any book, who had untangled the Gilded Thorn's impossible inventory down to the bottled memories and the jar of last summer, read the new arrangement in four seconds flat and wished to every silent god that she couldn't.

Vespera's name sat where hers should have been. Struck through. Beside it, in the space reserved for a substitute asset, her own name glowed like a coal.

"She ran," Ulyana said. "To cut her own deal. And the deal fell through."

"Deals with courts fall through when the collateral is already spoken for." Morwenna's voice gentled further, which was somehow more horrifying. "The guildmother offered herself. Noble. Foolish. The judge declined her — her soul is too entangled, too much the guild's own first debt; taking it would collapse the very ledger they mean to collect. But the court requires *a* payment, or the audit you're carrying up that mountain becomes an execution order instead. So they sent me. Because I am, formally, still the lienholder on one soul in this room." She opened her hands. "And I am permitted — this once, this eve — to release it. Conditionally."

The word *conditionally* hung in the air like the smell of a trap.

"Say the condition," Ulyana said.

"Take her place." Morwenna nodded back toward the dark of the court, toward whatever office or obligation Vespera had tried to claim. "The under-court needs a keeper of accounts, little bird. Someone mortal, someone incorruptible — well." The smile flickered, wry. "Someone whose corruption is *documented*, which the court finds reassuring. Vespera served them thirty years from the other side of the mountain and called it trade. You would serve from within. And in exchange—" the old woman's voice cracked, just slightly, on the hinge of it— "in exchange, you walk free. Soul returned. Debt discharged. Every year of your life after this one belongs to nobody but you."

The snow fell luminous around them. Somewhere behind the black doors, something vast turned over in its sleep and muttered in a language made entirely of overdue notices.

"And you?" Ulyana asked. "What does the court pay *you* for brokering this?"

"Nothing." For the first time, Morwenna looked away, out at the blizzard, at the world above where winter was eating the harvest month alive. "That's the joke, if you want it. I sold your soul before you were born for enough to buy one good winter. One. I have spent forty years discovering what a bad price that was. The court doesn't need to bribe me, child. Watching you choose is payment enough. It's the only part of the account I ever got to keep open."

Ulyana's throat hurt.

Because here was the thing — the terrible, unspeakable thing — and she made herself look at it straight, the way she looked at every ledger: it was a *good offer*. It was, by any honest arithmetic, the best offer anyone had made her in her entire life. Freedom, total and unconditional, in exchange for a job. Not torment, not servitude in chains — a position. Keeper of accounts in a cathedral of frozen petitions, doing precisely the work she was best at in all the world, for the only employer whose books mattered more than the Gilded Thorn's.

And she would be good at it. That was the seduction underneath the seduction. She would balance the gods' ledgers the way she'd balanced the guild's, find their errors, tighten their columns, and somewhere in the third century of it she would stop remembering there had ever been a fen cottage, a stolen grimoire, a girl who corrected her own name every time it was taken from her.

"That's what you did," Ulyana said quietly. "Isn't it. Someone offered you this once. A position. A way to matter. And you took it, and you told yourself the child was tuition."

Morwenna didn't answer. The silence answered.

"You're not offering me freedom," Ulyana went on, and her voice steadied as she found the numbers underneath the feeling, the true column beneath the false one. "You're offering me your life. The one you already lived. You want to watch someone make the same choice and have it come out differently this time — no. Worse. You want it to come out the *same*, so you won't have been alone in it. That's not motherhood, Morwenna. That's accounting. You're trying to move a loss into a different column so it stops showing red."

The witch's face did not crumble. That was the second unbearable thing. It simply aged another decade, right there in the snow, the kindness draining out of it until what remained was just an old woman with bad knees holding a contract she couldn't close.

"There were years," Morwenna said, very low, "when you slept by my fire because you had nowhere else. When I taught you letters because no one else would. Whatever I am, I was also — for a while —"

"For a while." Ulyana nodded slowly. "Yes. And I have carried that *for a while* for eleven years like a stone in my boot, and here is what I finally know, standing at the door of hell with my soul in escrow: both things are true. You sheltered me and you sold me. You taught me to read and you forged the papers that made me readable as property. I don't have to pick one truth and throw the other away. I'm an auditor. I can hold a contradiction in both hands." She reached up and unpinned the oilcloth bundle from her chest, holding the oldest ledger against her hip. "But I can't hold *this* offer. Because accepting it wouldn't save me. It would just change whose ledger I die inside."

Sable's grip tightened on her hand. Davor hadn't moved, hadn't breathed loudly, but she felt his attention on her like a wall at her back.

"So the answer is no," Ulyana said. "Not because I'm brave. Because I've seen your books, and yours are the worst ones I've ever audited."

For a long moment, the only sound was snow.

Then Morwenna laughed — short, cracked, genuine, awful. "Gods help me. You sound like her. Like Vespera, thirty years ago, telling the judge his terms were sloppy." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, and when she dropped her hands she was, impossibly, older and lighter at once, like a debt partially forgiven. "Well then, little bird. You refuse the doorkeeper's offer. Formally. On the record." She gestured at the fog-figures, and they rearranged one final time — Ulyana watched her own name settle back into the liability column with a sensation like a bone setting. "Understand what happens next. The court will not negotiate now. You've cost them their easy settlement, and judges punish auditors the way kings punish tax collectors. Whatever you're carrying in that ledger, you had better be planning to use all of it."

"I am," Ulyana said. "Every page."

"Even the pages about you?"

"*Especially* those."

Something moved behind the witch's eyes — pride, maybe, or grief wearing pride's coat. She stepped aside from the threshold, folding her hands again, resuming her post.

"Then go in," Morwenna said. "And when you stand in front of the Sleeping Judge and he asks who sent you, don't say the guild. Don't say the girl with the god's blood or the enforcer with three fewer days. Say: *an auditor comes, unbidden, to review the house's accounts.* Nothing frightens them more, and nothing protects you better. Courts fear only one thing in all creation."

"What's that?"

"Being read."

Ulyana walked past her. It took everything she had not to pause — not to say one more thing, ask one more question, reach for one more scrap of the *for a while*. At the last step she stopped anyway, because she was not her mistress, and she did not leave debts ambiguous.

"When this is done," she said, without turning, "if there's anything left of the world above worth returning to — I'm not forgiving you. But I'm not carrying you anymore either. Consider yourself struck from my personal ledger. Transferred to the column marked *closed*."

Behind her, very quietly, the hedge-witch said, "That's the kindest entry anyone's ever made about me."

* * *

The doors of the under-court swallowed them whole.

Inside, the cathedral of frozen petitions rose around them — Ulyana had seen it once, fleeing, and it was worse now. Midwinter's eve had filled every niche and gallery. Petitioners, ghosts, bailiff-spirits, minor gods in threadbare vestments, all pressed shoulder to shoulder beneath icicles of unpaid vows. The air hummed with the sound of a court clearing its throat.

At the far end, atop a dais of stacked contract-stones, the Sleeping Judge was waking. She could see the shape of him stirring, vast and irritated, like a bear disturbed in January.

And descending the steps toward them, flanked by bailiffs, moving with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had already made her arrangements — came Vespera.

The guildmother's eyes found Ulyana across the frozen crowd. There was no apology in them. There was, Ulyana realized with a lurch, something far more dangerous: a plan.

"Whatever she says next," Ilya murmured, "don't sign anything."

Vespera smiled, and raised her voice so that the whole court could hear, and said:

"Judge. Before the reckoning begins — the Gilded Thorn requests to file a motion."
