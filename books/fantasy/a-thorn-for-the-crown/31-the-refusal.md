## 31. The Refusal

The hedge-witch stood at the threshold of the under-court with snow in her hair and her hands folded like a woman at prayer, and she said, in the voice she had used to teach Ulyana letters by firelight: "Come home, little ledger. Take Vespera's place. The books are waiting."

Ulyana had rehearsed this moment for eleven years without knowing it. Every night of counting candle-stubs, every column balanced to the last copper, had been a rehearsal for the day someone offered her a life in exchange for a self.

"You burned your cottage," Ulyana said.

"I moved it." Her old mistress smiled, and the smile was the same one that had hovered over the forged papers, over the contract signed in ink mixed from Ulyana's first stolen blood. "You always did love a good inventory. Come inside. I'll show you what's yours — the ledgers, the seals, the keys. A whole court's worth of accounts, and no one to keep them but me, and my hands are old now."

Behind her, through the cathedral doors, the frozen petitions hung like chandeliers made of grief. Somewhere inside, the Sleeping Judge was waking properly for the first time in three centuries, and tomorrow was midwinter's eve, and every outstanding soul in the under-court would revert to its owner or be forfeit.

Vespera Underhill stood ten paces behind Ulyana, holding the oldest ledger against her chest like a shield. She had walked into a blizzard and down a mountain to cut her own deal, and here was the deal, being offered to the wrong woman, in front of everyone.

"She's lying," Vespera said quietly. "She'll take both our souls if we let her sort us into columns."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I've read her handwriting for eleven years. She never writes anything down unless it profits her twice."

The witch's smile thinned. "You were a bright child. That was the problem."

"No," Ulyana said. "That was *your* problem. Mine was believing that whoever kept the books must be honest, because the books were beautiful."

She stepped past her mistress then, past the threshold stone worn smooth by three hundred years of debtors' knees, and into the court itself, and she heard Vespera's breath catch behind her, and Davor's boots on the ice, and Sable's small hand finding hers without looking, because they had spent enough days in cells to know each other's movements in the dark.

* * *

The Court Beneath the Mountain was full.

Not with bailiffs — though there were bailiffs, ranks of them along the walls, their faces like unmarked coins. Not with gods — though the old gods sat in their high seats, drowsing and glittering, wearing their debts like jewelry they'd forgotten was borrowed. It was full of *accounts*. Every petition ever frozen mid-prayer hung from the vaulted dark, and every one of them was an entry, and Ulyana Okonkwo, who had once been a child sold before she was born, walked down the center aisle reading them as she went.

"Bottled memory, one summer, sold twice," she murmured. "Borrowed luck, compounded at interest until a man hanged himself with his own good fortune. One honest lie, resold as truth, four hundred times."

"Stop narrating," said the judge.

The Sleeping Judge sat at the far end beneath a crown of icicles that had never melted because melting would have been a form of forgiveness, and he was awake now, properly awake, and annoyed about it in the way of a man interrupted at the only task he still enjoyed, which was sleeping.

"You are not scheduled," he said. "Your trial is tomorrow. Midwinter's end reverts all assets. You will stand on the scales, and you will be weighed, and you will be found owed."

"I'm not here for my trial," Ulyana said. "I'm here to file an audit."

Silence. Even the frozen petitions seemed to lean in.

"A what?" said the god of small harvests, whose name had been eaten by time but whose appetite remained.

"An audit," Ulyana repeated, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her, because everything else in her was shaking. "A formal examination of accounts. It is the right of any party named in a ledger to demand review of that ledger. You wrote that rule yourselves — clause nine, article two, carved into the foundation stone of this court before the first winter. I read it on the way in. Sable's blood makes the old carvings light up; it's very convenient."

Sable lifted her chin. Blue light crawled along the floor from where she stood, tracing letters older than the alphabet, and yes, there it was, glowing faint and patient: *any party named may demand the reading*.

"The rule exists," the judge admitted slowly, "but no one has invoked it in—"

"Three hundred years," Ulyana finished. "Yes. I noticed. Which means your books have gone unread for three hundred years, and I am a guild auditor, and I have audited things far worse than gods." She set her satchel on the floor and began unpacking it with the calm of a woman laying out surgical tools: the grimoire, the original apprenticeship papers, the copied pages from the oldest ledger, Vespera's confessions written in her own hand. "Also, I brought witnesses. Also, I brought a former enforcer who has memorized your collection procedures. Also—" she nodded at Vespera, who hesitated, then stepped forward and laid the oldest ledger on the ice with a sound like a coffin lid closing, "—the founding records of the Gilded Thorn, surrendered freely, which under clause eleven makes them admissible."

Vespera's jaw was tight. "You're spending my confession as evidence."

"You gave it freely," Ulyana said. "Everything given freely is admissible. That's going to matter later."

The judge leaned forward on his throne of repossession. Around him the lesser gods stirred, and Ulyana watched them do the arithmetic she knew by heart — the arithmetic of creatures deciding whether they could afford to refuse her.

"You understand," the judge said, "that if you open these accounts, they cannot be closed again. Some of what you find will implicate you. Your own soul is collateral in a fraudulent contract. Fraud cuts both ways, auditor."

"I'm counting on it," Ulyana said. "Because if fraud cuts both ways, then the question becomes: who committed the *first* fraud? And I think when we follow the entries back far enough, we're going to find out that the answer isn't a hedge-witch, isn't a guildmother, and isn't a girl who was born owing. Read clause nine aloud, Davor."

Davor Kestrel stepped forward. He had been a collector for the under-court once; he knew the liturgy of debt the way priests know psalms. He put his hand flat on the foundation stone, where Sable's light still glowed, and he recited the old words in the old cadence, and the courtroom — cathedral, counting-house, tomb — listened to its own law spoken back to it by a man it had cashiered.

When he finished, the silence had weight. Snow sifted down from a crack in the vaulting, falling gently onto the frozen petitions, and each flake that landed on a prayer made it flicker, as if remembering something.

"Very well," said the Sleeping Judge, and there was something new in his voice — not respect, nothing so warm, but the wariness of a debtor hearing a knock. "Read."

* * *

She began with the small things, because she had learned long ago that the way to audit a great fraud is to start where the numbers are too neat.

"One bottle of last summer," she read, "pawned by the god of orchards to finance a temple he never built. Interest accrued: the entire concept of ripeness, transferred to creditors. This is why fruit has tasted like nothing for three hundred years. Entry confirmed by seven independent complaints filed between the years of the Grey King and the Long Frost, all ignored."

The god of orchards shifted on his seat. His fingers, she noticed, were stained the brown-green of rot.

"One answered prayer, rain of fish, granted free of charge in Harvestmonth last." She looked up at the assembly. "Free of charge. When did this court last do anything free of charge? Never, per the records. Therefore the fish-rain was not generosity. It was a payment. Someone paid the gods to answer prayers again, and I want to know who received the money, and I want to know what was purchased with it."

"Proceed to the relevant matters," said the judge.

"This *is* the relevant matter." Ulyana turned a page. "Item: the long winter. Filed officially as an act of divine displeasure. Cross-referenced against collection schedules: the winter begins precisely when the largest tranche of pawned souls comes due for forfeiture. Souls revert to their owners at midwinter's end — but only if claimed. Unclaimed souls default to the court. A winter that keeps mortals desperate, praying, dying young, and unable to travel to claim what's theirs..." She let the sentence hang. "That isn't displeasure. That's inventory management."

The courtroom froze — literally; frost raced outward across the floor from the throne, and the bailiffs along the walls went still as coins in a dead man's pocket.

"You dare," said the god of small harvests, rising.

"I calculate," Ulyana said. "Sit down. Item next: the Gilded Thorn's founding contract, executed between Vespera Underhill and the under-court, in which the guild's entire trade — bottled memories, borrowed luck, honest lies — is structured as interest payments on a single underlying debt. Read the underlying debt, Vespera."

Vespera's voice came out steady, which cost her more than screaming would have. "'One soul, female, unborn, of the line of Okonkwo, held as primary collateral against the court's forbearance.'"

"And whose soul was held as collateral *before* mine?"

Vespera closed her eyes. "Mine."

"Murmur it for the record, please. Louder."

"*Mine,*" Vespera said, and the word echoed off forty thousand frozen prayers, and somewhere high above, snow shifted.

"So the guild's first guildmother was herself a pawn," Ulyana said to the room. "And when she bought herself time, she needed a replacement asset, and she helped forge apprenticeship papers so that a hedge-witch could sell a child who was never legally hers. And the hedge-witch—" Ulyana turned to the doors, where her old mistress still stood, smiling her profit-twice smile, "—sold me knowing the papers were false, because false papers meant the sale could be unwound, which meant she retained leverage over both buyer and seller forever. Three women, three generations, each paying the last one's debts. That's not commerce. That's a chain letter written in souls."

"Your point," said the judge, "being?"

"My point is that every contract in this room descends from a default." She held up the oldest ledger, open to its first page, and Sable raised her hand, and blue light poured through the chamber and illuminated the page so that every god, every bailiff, every frozen petition could see the entry at the top, in handwriting so ancient it was nearly geology:

*Received of mankind: worship, freely given, in perpetuity. Paid: protection, miracles, presence. Account status: —*

There was no figure after those words. There was a smudge, as though someone had meant to write *current* and then thought better of it.

"Account status blank," Ulyana said. "For three hundred years, the gods stopped showing up, and mortals stopped paying, and neither side ever closed the account. And then, last autumn, someone started answering prayers again — cruelly, expensively, with fish-rain and early winters — and I asked myself: why would creditors resume collections after three centuries of dormancy?" She closed the ledger. "Because they're insolvent. Because the protection was never delivered, the miracles ran dry, the presence withdrew — the gods defaulted *first*, gentlemen, ladies, whatever you are — and everything since, every soul pawned, every guild founded, every winter sent, is not righteous bookkeeping. It is the frantic papering-over of an unpaid bill that came due three hundred years ago."

No one spoke. The frost on the floor creaked as it spread. High among the petitions, one of the frozen prayers cracked clean through and fell, shattering on the stone, releasing — briefly, poignantly — the sound of a woman's voice saying *please* into air that had never answered.

The Sleeping Judge rose from his throne. Ice fell from his shoulders like a shrugged cloak.

"You are a mote," he said, and his voice filled the whole cathedral of debt. "A decimal point. An error of scale. You stand in a court older than your language, surrounded by entities who remember when your ancestors learned fire, and you propose to tell us our own accounts are wrong."

"Not wrong," Ulyana said. "*Overdue.* There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and you know it, because you built your entire court on it. Wrong can be argued. Overdue accrues." She took a breath. "So here is what happens next. Tomorrow, at midwinter's end, when the trial convenes, I will finish the audit. Every god's debts. Every forged contract. Every entry where the payment side of the page is blank — and there are more blanks than figures; I've counted. I will read them aloud, in order, from the foundation stone forward, and clause nine says you must hear all of it. All of it, your worship. Do you know how long three hundred years of unanswered prayers takes to read aloud? Neither do I. But I have a talent for arithmetic, a voice that carries, and two friends who can tap out code in their sleep. We'll be here all winter."

"We *are* the winter," the judge said softly.

"Then," said Ulyana Okonkwo, ledger-keeper of the dissolved and dissolving Gilded Thorn, daughter of no one's bargain and author of her own entries, "you should have collected while you still had the credit."

The courtroom froze — the people in it, this time, gods and bailiffs alike, staring at the small woman with the satchel who had just told the oldest powers in the world that they were the ones in arrears.

And in the ringing silence, from the back of the hall, unhurried and terrible, came the sound of the hedge-witch beginning to clap.
