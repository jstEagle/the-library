## 32. Trial by Ledger

The silence after the last page was not a silence at all. It was the sound of three hundred gods doing arithmetic they had spent centuries avoiding.

I stood in the center of the under-court with my voice worn down to wire and my hands still steady — steady because I had made them steady, the way you make a column of figures balance when everything in you wants to shake. The audit lay open before the Sleeping Judge on his throne of frozen petitions, forty-one days of my life transcribed into the court's own script, witnessed by its own clerks, sealed with its own wax. They had given me the ink themselves. That was their mistake. Courts love procedure, and procedure loves paper, and I had come armed to the teeth with both.

"You have read," I said, "the first nine hundred entries."

"I have read them," said the judge. His voice came slow and vast, like a glacier deciding where to put a valley. Around him the cathedral of ice held its breath — tiers upon tiers of seated gods, some vast as weather, some small as hearth-smoke, all of them wearing the expressions of debtors watching a creditor work through a ledger line by line.

"Then you know what the ninth hundredth entry says."

"We know what you *claim* it says."

"It isn't a claim." I turned the great book around so its face caught the blue lamplight that Sable's blood made burn along every wall. "It's your own record. Kept by your own scriveners. In your own hand." I let that sit. Let them look at it. "Entry nine hundred: *Worship received, third century, aggregate — declining.* Entry nine hundred and one: *Offerings of grain, wine, blood, song — declining.* Entry nine hundred and two: *Prayers unanswered per protocol.*" I looked up. "You stopped answering. Three hundred years ago. You wrote it down yourselves. And then—"

"And then we slept," the judge said, "as was our right. Worship is not owed to us. It is given."

"Is it?" I opened the second book — the founding covenant, older than the Gilded Thorn, older than any guild, pried out of the court's own archive-maze with a writ of inspection I had argued into existence over six days of procedural trench warfare. "This says otherwise. This is the Concord of First Snow, signed between the elder gods and the mortal kings of the valley. Article four." I read it aloud slowly, so that even the smallest god at the back of the smallest tier could follow. "*In exchange for the keeping of seasons, the tending of harvests, and the warding of the mountain roads, mortals shall render worship, offering, and remembrance, in perpetuity, without lapse.*"

"In perpetuity," the judge repeated. Something in his tone had gone careful. A man who has heard his own contract quoted back to him always goes careful.

"Without lapse. Which means the obligation ran both directions. You kept the seasons. We kept the faith. It was a trade, Your Honor — the oldest trade there is, older than any guild, older than coin. You were the first merchants. We were your first customers." I closed the covenant. "And then, somewhere in the third century, your records show worship declining. Do you know why? Because you had already stopped delivering. Davor — tell them about the mountain roads."

Davor stepped forward from the tier where he sat beside Sable, and I saw him plant his feet the way he did before collecting a debt, except this time he was the witness and not the collector, and something in his face suggested he found the reversal satisfying.

"Fifteen years I walked those roads for the under-court," he said. "Collecting what was owed. And I'll tell you what I saw. Shrines collapsed because the roads to them collapsed, because nobody tended the passes anymore, because the road-wardens' prayers went up for a hundred years and got nothing back but snow. Farmers left offerings in the fields until the fields went sour and the offerings rotted, and no god came to take them or thank them or make the sour ground sweet. Fisherman's daughter in Greyharbor prayed eleven years for her father's boat. Eleven years. She kept count. She had a little tally carved into the doorpost." His jaw worked. "You want to know why worship declined? Mortals are not faithless. Mortals are *bookkeepers*. They gave, and gave, and gave, and you took, and took, and took, and delivered nothing. So they stopped paying. Any sensible merchant would have called that a breach and come to renegotiate. Instead you slept on it for three hundred years."

"And now," the judge said, "we answer again."

"Now you answer again," I agreed. "Cruelly. Out of season. Rain of fish in a market square; snow in harvest month; a widow in Copperlane prays for her sick son and gets him healed and her roof collapsed the same night, because you've been asleep so long that answering a prayer is like a man waking from a long illness and crushing whatever he takes hold of." I gestured upward, toward the surface world, toward the winter falling out of season over the whole valley. "You call this repossession. I've read the internal memoranda. This winter isn't wrath. It's an *audit of your own* — you're seizing worship because you believe mortals defaulted on you."

"They did default," said a voice from the third tier. A goddess of small hearths, or what had once been one — she was thin as smoke now, translucent at the edges, and when she spoke her voice crackled like a fire low on fuel. "Three centuries of nothing. Do you know what starvation is, little auditor? We are starving. Every god in this room who answered a prayer this winter did it the way a dying man spends his last coin. We are owed. We are owed *everything*, and we are taking it, and if taking it kills the valley then the valley should have paid when payment was asked."

The tiers murmured assent. I had expected it. Hunger makes its own arithmetic, and it is very bad arithmetic, and I had come here precisely to correct it.

"May I approach the record?" I asked the judge.

"You may not—"

"She may." The judge raised one enormous hand, and the objection died somewhere in the tiers. He was watching me now with what I recognized, after weeks among gods and guildmasters, as professional interest. "Show me what you would show me."

I carried the third book to the foot of the dais and set it down. "This is the reconciliation. Every entry in your receiving-ledgers for the past three hundred years, cross-referenced against every entry in your delivery-ledgers. Your scriveners kept beautiful books. I mean that sincerely — whoever ran your counting-house understood double-entry better than Vespera does, and Vespera is the best I ever saw. Which is how I know these numbers weren't lost. They were *recorded*. You knew exactly what you owed and exactly what you failed to deliver, season over season, century over century, and you recorded the shortfall in a ledger marked—" I opened to the tab. "*Deferred Obligations, Accruing.*"

The word went through the cathedral like a crack through ice.

"Accruing," the hearth-goddess whispered.

"Compounding," I said. "At the rate specified in the Concord itself — article nine, the penalty clause. Three centuries of deferred obligations, compounded annually. Shall I read the total?"

"No one needs to hear a total," said the judge quickly.

"Everyone needs to hear the total," I said, "because everyone in this room is on one side of it or the other, and I promise you the side you think you're on is not the side the arithmetic puts you on." I read it. I read it in the court's own numerals, digit by digit, while the blue light burned and the frozen petitions on the walls — ten thousand years of human pleading, pressed into ice like flowers — seemed almost to lean in and listen. "That is what the elder gods owe the mortal world. Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. *Contractually.* By your own law, which you enforce with bailiff-spirits and toll-wights and collectors like Ilya Vosk, who could recite your procedures in his sleep because I've heard him do it. By the same law under which my soul was pawned and my friends imprisoned and a child named Wren sits in a cell of glass right now because someone sold her before she could walk — by that law, the debtors in this courtroom are not us."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water.

"The winter," I said quietly, "is not repossession. It is a *default*. You seized worship you were no longer entitled to, because you could no longer afford to earn it. You are insolvent, Your Honor. All of you. Magnificently, catastrophically insolvent, and you have been for three hundred years, and you hid it the way every bankrupt house hides it — by lending against assets that were never yours." I turned, slowly, so the whole tiered dark of the cathedral could see my face, plain and tired and certain. "You lent against souls. Mine among them."

* * *

They tried, of course, to have me removed.

A bailiff-spirit unfolded itself from behind the dais — a tall grey absence shaped like a clerk, holding a writ that materialized wet out of the air. *Disorder in the court. Removal of the claimant.* Davor was on his feet before the ink dried, and Sable stood beside him, and the blue light along the walls brightened so sharply that several of the smaller gods shaded their eyes, and the judge said, in a voice that frosted the air:

"Let her finish. If we silence the auditor mid-audit, the finding stands as true and we merely confirm it in front of witnesses. Sit down, Hesperin. You've been insolvent longer than she's been alive; you can bear five more minutes of arithmetic."

So the bailiff folded itself away, and I finished.

"Here is what insolvency means, under your own code, section twelve, which Ilya can confirm because he's enforced it against mortal houses his whole career." I nodded toward the far archway, where Ilya Vosk stood — not guarding, not hunting, just standing, arms crossed, watching the trial he'd once been sent to end. "When a debtor cannot meet obligations, the debtor does not get to seize the creditor's property to cover the gap. That's not collection. That's theft with paperwork. When a debtor cannot pay, the debtor submits to *restructuring*: terms are renegotiated, debts are rescheduled or forgiven, and the insolvent party surrenders control of its accounts to honest oversight until solvency is restored. That is the law you wrote. That is the law you made Davor Kestrel enforce and cashiered him for refusing to enforce *unjustly* — and I note, for the record, that the court itself reviewed his case and found his refusal procedurally correct, which means even your own disciplinary record agrees that your law is sound and only its application has rotted."

"That application," the judge said heavily, "was necessary."

"Necessary." I laughed — I couldn't help it, a short ugly sound that echoed off ten thousand frozen prayers. "Your Honor, I grew up apprenticed to a hedge-witch who sold my soul before I could write my own name, and I will tell you the difference between necessity and appetite, because I learned it young. Necessity is bread when the granary's empty. Appetite is taking the granary, the mill, the miller's daughter, and calling it all bread. The child whose soul was pawned for *sixteen silver pieces* — sixteen! — that wasn't necessity. The bottled memories your market sells by the crate, harvested from people who never consented — that wasn't necessity. The guild's founding debt, the reason Vespera Okonkwo spent eighty years buying time with other people's lives — trace it back, as I traced it, through the oldest ledger, and you find it began with a loan the under-court made to desperate mortals at terms no solvent institution would offer, *because* the under-court wanted collateral it could hold forever. You didn't fall into the soul-trade. You invented it. You are not creditors gone hungry. You are a bank that ate its own depositors."

"Careful," said the hearth-goddess softly.

"No," I said. "You be careful. Because here is the choice, and it is the only choice, and I have written it out in triplicate." I lifted the final document — one page, plain, unsealed, the humblest thing I had carried down the salt tunnels and past the toll-wights and through the market of small mercies. "Option one: the court admits insolvency. Publicly, formally, entered into the great record. The elder gods acknowledge three centuries of default, surrender all claims on pawned souls as void consideration — you cannot collect on contracts funded by money you stole — and submit the court's accounts to independent oversight until such time as the obligations of the Concord are either honestly renegotiated or honestly forgiven. The winter ends, because the winter was never weather. It was a lie you told with the sky."

"And option two?" the judge asked. His vast voice had gone quiet in a way that made the whole cathedral lean closer.

"Option two is that you reject the audit, declare me a fraud, and attempt to enforce your claims by force." I met his eyes — old, old eyes, older than the mountain, and underneath the majesty of them I saw what I had seen in Vespera's face the night she confessed, and in Ilya's the night he let us pass, and in Davor's every time he spoke of the child he wouldn't ruin. Exhaustion. The specific exhaustion of carrying a debt too long alone. "And if you choose option two, I want everyone in this room to understand what happens next, because I've run the projection. You seize our souls — mine, Davor's, Sable's, Wren's, every flame in your reliquaries. And the mortals above watch their gods devour children to cover losses the gods themselves incurred, and they do what mortals always do when the arithmetic finally becomes undeniable." I let the pause stretch. "They stop paying forever. Not three centuries. Forever. Every shrine, every offering, every prayer candle in every window — guttered, all at once, everywhere, and this time not because you slept but because they watched you wake and saw what you were. You won't repossess worship, Your Honor. You'll kill the market. There is no restructuring after that. There is no anything after that. A god with no worshippers isn't a sleeping god. I've seen what's left of those, in the deepest tiers of your own archive. They aren't sleeping. They're *gone*."

The hearth-goddess made a sound like a fire going out.

"So," I said. "Insolvency admitted, and mercy negotiated like civilized parties. Or insolvency denied, and death chosen instead. Those are the terms. I didn't write them." I held up the covenant. "You did. Three thousand years ago, when you were strong enough to be fair. I'm only asking you to be fair while you're weak, which is harder, and matters more."

* * *

For a long time the judge said nothing, and the court did nothing, and the only motion in that cathedral of ice was the blue light breathing along the walls where Sable's blood woke it, and the slow drift of frost-fall from the vaults of the ceiling like snow falling inside a thought.

When he finally spoke, it was not to me.

"Clerk of the Record," he said. "Has the claimant's audit been received in full?"

A small grey figure at the dais's foot bowed. "Received in full, Your Honor. Forty-one days of proceedings. Cross-referenced. Witnessed. Sealed."

"Has any error been found in it?"

The clerk hesitated. I watched the hesitation. Clerks hesitate for two reasons: because they've found something wrong, or because they've found something right and dread saying so.

"No error has been found, Your Honor."

"And the reconciliation of the deferred obligations?"

"Arithmetically... consistent, Your Honor." The clerk's voice dropped. "Consistent with records we have held and not read aloud for three hundred years."

The murmur that went through the tiers then was not anger. I had braced for anger. But hunger, I was learning, curdles faster into fear than into fury, and what moved through that cathedral was fear — the fear of creatures who had told themselves for three hundred years that they were sleeping, and were discovering in public that they had in fact been starving, and worse, that they had known.

The hearth-goddess rose. She was nearly transparent now; the blue light passed through her like light through breath. "I move," she said, and her crackling voice filled the whole frozen dark, "that the finding of insolvency be entered."

"You move *what*?" Another god, vast and slow, a shape like a glacier given shoulders — one of the mountain's own elder powers. "You move that we confess before a mortal? Before a *guild clerk*?"

"I move that we confess before the truth," she said, "while confessing is still cheaper than the alternative. I have been burning my own substance to answer prayers since midwinter. I have perhaps two winters left in me. I would rather spend them renegotiated than spent denying a balanced book." Her smoky gaze found me across the distance. "Auditor. Your option one. The oversight — who holds it?"

"Whoever the court trusts and the mortals trust," I said. "Which is to say, we'll need to build it. I have ideas. I've had forty-one days to have ideas."

"And the pawned souls?"

"Released. All of them. Consideration returned. Wren goes home to her mother by spring."

"And the guild that brokered half these horrors?" the glacier-god rumbled. "The Gilded Thorn sold your neighbors' memories by the jar, girl. Will your precious audit forgive *them*?"

The question landed where it was aimed. I felt Davor shift behind me. Felt Sable's hand find the hem of my coat, the way she did when she wanted to hold on without making a scene.

"That matter," I said carefully, "is before this court separately. And its resolution will be proposed in due course. By me. When the time comes."

"When the time comes," the judge repeated. He rose from the throne of petitions, and rising, he seemed to take the whole cathedral's temperature down another ten degrees, and every god in every tier bent forward like grass under wind. "Then let the record show that the Sleeping Judge has woken, has heard the audit, and finds it—" he paused, and I understood that the next word was the hinge on which the world's winter swung, "—*entered*. Insolvency is admitted. Claims on pawned souls are suspended pending release. Restructuring negotiations shall commence at once, under the oversight the claimant described." His ancient eyes fixed on me, and there was no warmth in them, but there was something rarer: recognition. One bookkeeper acknowledging another across a gulf of three thousand years. "Understand me, Ulyana Okonkwo. You have not won. You have prevented a loss — which is the smaller and harder victory. The debts in this room are real. Our hunger is real. Forgiveness is not forgetting; the arithmetic remains, and arithmetic is patient as we are. Someday the question of what the gods are *for*, when the old trades are dead, will stand on this floor again. Pray — if any of you still pray — that someone better than us is holding the pen."

"Someday," I agreed, "someone will be. I intend to train them myself."

A sound went through the tiers that I took a moment to identify, because I had never heard it in this place. It was laughter — thin, rusty, astonished laughter, gods laughing like men coming up out of deep water, and beneath it, high overhead, a sound like a seam of ice letting go.

Above the mountain, though none of us could see it, the snow had begun to slacken.

But the judge was not finished. He sank back onto his throne, and the frost of his attention settled on me one last time, heavier than all the rest.

"There remains," he said, "the matter you deferred. The Gilded Thorn. Its founding debt — the original loan, the first collateral, the contract from which all the others descend. It is still outstanding, auditor. Still accruing. Still owed." He opened one vast hand, and upon the palm, small as a coal, burned a flame I knew. Not mine. Older. Dimmer. A soul that had been burning in pledge for eighty years. "The court requires settlement. Full settlement. And the asset offered must be—" the old formula, recited wearily, as if he hated the taste of it, "—commensurate."

I looked at the flame. Eighty years of Vespera, burning in a god's hand.

And I reached into my coat, and drew out the only paper I had left that mattered, and stepped forward to name a price no ledger had ever held.
