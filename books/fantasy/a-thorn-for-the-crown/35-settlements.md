## 35. Settlements

The trouble with giving away impossible things, Ulyana discovered, was that people kept asking questions.

"Madam," said the cooper's wife, standing in the door of the counting-house with a jar of last summer clutched to her chest like a rescued cat, "there's been a mistake. This was on my doorstep with no bill. Nothing has come to my house without a bill since my mother's day, and she owed the tanner her wedding shoes."

"No mistake," Ulyana said. "It's yours."

"But what does it *do*?"

"It does last summer. You open it in February and it is July inside your kitchen for as long as the jar lasts. Bread rises faster. Children stop quarreling. The light comes in at the angle it comes in at seven in the evening in a good year."

The cooper's wife looked at the jar. She looked at Ulyana. She looked at the jar again, and Ulyana watched a woman decide, in real time, whether she lived in a world where such a thing could be true.

"And I owe you?"

"You owe nobody. That's rather the point."

"I don't *like* owing nobody." The cooper's wife said it the way other people announced a death in the family. "Owing nobody means there's nothing to be even. My mother always said—"

"Your mother sounds like my old guildmother," Ulyana said. "Open it in February. If it doesn't work, bring me back the empty jar and I'll owe you an apology."

That seemed to satisfy something in the woman's ledger-keeping soul. She went out clutching summer like a secret.

Behind Ulyana, Davor laughed from his chair by the window, where he sat with his splinted wrist propped on a cushion and a list of seeds in his lap.

"You're going to be doing this all winter," he said.

"I am doing this all winter. There are four hundred entries in the dissolution inventory and every one of them is somebody's doorstep by spring."

"What's on mine?"

Ulyana consulted the book. "A bottle of borrowed luck. Three uses."

"Huh." He turned a seed packet over. "I always thought luck was just attention applied early."

"That," said Ulyana, "is why you were never a good customer."

* * *

The Gilded Thorn had died well, which was more than most institutions could say.

Its goods went out over eleven days, and Ulyana insisted on delivering them personally, or nearly personally — Sable took the harbor district because she could walk through a crowd without being noticed, a divine inheritance she used exclusively for errands; Ilya took the north quarter because half the debtors there still flinched when they saw him coming and he wanted, badly, to be seen delivering instead of collecting. He came back each evening looking like a man who had run a long way uphill and wasn't sure yet whether he regretted it.

The bottled memories went back to whoever had sold them, or to their children if the sellers were gone. The borrowed luck went out unmarked. The one honest lie — sealed in lead glass, worth a kingdom to the right buyer, worthless to anyone else — Ulyana carried herself to a widow on Tanner's Row who had once pawned her grief for coin and spent nine years unable to cry at her husband's grave.

"He didn't suffer," Ulyana told her, which was true, and the widow wept for the first time since the funeral, and thanked her, and asked what she owed.

"A visit, someday, when the tears aren't new."

Debts were struck from books in the square outside the old guildhouse. Ulyana did it publicly, page by page, reading each name aloud before tearing it free: *Marta of Cooper's Lane, paid. Osric the ferryman, paid. The parish of Saint Ellin-over-the-Water, paid.* Half the city turned out to watch, the way people gather for executions, except this one ran backwards. An old man wept so hard at hearing his name read that Sable had to hold his elbow, and when Ulyana reached him he pressed a heel of stale bread into her hands — the only payment he had — and she took it, because refusing a gift would have made the whole exercise a lie.

By the fourth day, people had stopped asking what they owed. By the seventh, they had started leaving things on the counting-house step anyway: eggs, firewood, a hand-knit scarf that fit nobody, a live hen with a belligerent expression whom Davor named Auditor.

"She bites," Sable reported.

"Good," said Davor. "She should have a trade."

* * *

On the ninth day, Davor took a shovel to the ground behind the old guildhouse, directly over the vaults.

"You can't garden there," Ulyana said. "There's forty feet of cellar under you. Bottled weather. A cabinet of second chances."

"Not anymore there isn't." He drove the shovel in. "Dissolved means dissolved. Everything returned to the world freely — your words, read aloud in front of a god with bedhead. The vault emptied itself. Went down yesterday to check. It's just earth down there now. Good earth, actually. Smells like it's been waiting."

She helped him dig anyway, mostly to see. Forty feet down, where the memory racks had stood, the walls had gone soft and root-threaded, as if three centuries of inventory had composted in a fortnight. At the bottom of the old strong-room, wedged where the founding ledger's cradle used to sit, they found a single unmarked stone, warm to the touch, that hummed faintly when Ulyana held it — the frequency, she thought, of a debt forgiven at scale.

They left it buried. Some things you don't inventory.

Davor planted beans first, because beans forgive beginners. Then onions, then a row of apple pips he'd saved from the fruit the city had left on their doorstep, which everyone told him would take seven years and which he planted with the satisfaction of a man making a promise to a future he intended to be present for.

His wrist knitted slowly. The wights' three days hadn't taken years off him exactly — the toll had been subtler than that, taking specific days, and Ulyana caught him sometimes staring at his hands trying to work out which ones were gone. He never talked about it except once, planting, when he paused mid-furrow and said, "I think one of them was the morning after the flood year. I remember it being good." And then he kept digging.

"You could have let me pay," Ulyana said, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"And miss watching you argue with a toll-wight? No."

"Davor."

He straightened, wiped his hands on his apron — he wore an apron now, dirt-stained, and it suited him better than the enforcer's leathers ever had — and considered her with those collector's eyes that had always been able to price a person at a glance but had lately stopped bothering.

"You want the honest arithmetic?" he said. "Three days against your whole soul. Against the kid we pulled out of that cell, whose name I still don't know and probably never will. Against—" he gestured at the garden, the counting-house, the hen currently terrorizing Sable on the step, all of it. "I've collected debts my whole life, Uli. Let me have paid one properly. It sits easier than anything else I own."

It was the most he'd ever said about himself at one time, so she let it stand, and handed him another furrow's worth of pips, and they worked until the light went long.

* * *

Grief arrived on schedule, the way grief does, three weeks after the victory, when there was finally quiet enough to feel it.

It came for Ulyana in the counting-house at dusk. She had gone to update the gift-book — the new one, the one that recorded kindnesses given and repaid — and found herself instead reaching to the shelf behind her, out of habit, for the oldest ledger.

Empty bracket. Dust shadow where the spine had rested for longer than she'd been alive.

Vespera had taken it up the mountain, and whatever Vespera had found there — freedom, judgment, snow — the book hadn't come back down. Neither had word. Ilya, who knew the court's procedures better than its own bailiffs, had made inquiries through channels that preferred not to exist, and come back shrugging: *no record of entry, no record of refusal.* Which meant either the court had absorbed her quietly, or she had walked past all its doors into somewhere the ledgers didn't reach.

"She'd have hated that," Ulyana said aloud to the empty room. "Being unrecorded."

Then, because she was who she was, she opened the gift-book and wrote, in careful figures: *Vespera Underhill — taught me that everything is owed. Paid forward, in full, by persons unknown.* It wasn't an entry in any system she'd been trained in. It was the only kind of accounting left.

Sable found her there an hour later, candle burned to a thumb's width, and didn't ask, just sat on the desk and swung her feet like the twelve-year-old she was whenever divinity wasn't watching.

"Do gods grieve?" Ulyana asked her.

"My father forgot my name for thirty years and I'm still here, so — slowly, probably. With a lot of paperwork." Sable picked a dried bean pod off the floor from Davor's pockets and turned it over. "The locket's almost empty, you know. His voice. One conversation left in it, maybe two short ones."

"Are you saving it?"

"I'm going to spend it at midwinter. When the court's asleep and he can't hear the other gods listening." She said it matter-of-factly, but her thumb pressed the bean pod flat. "That's the thing nobody tells you about gifts. They end. That's what makes them theirs."

Ulyana thought of the jar of last summer on the cooper's wife's shelf, finite, precious because finite. She thought of a garden planted over a vault. She put her arm around a god's daughter and let her be, for one evening, only a girl.

* * *

The bewilderment peaked at month's end, when the last major asset went out: the guild's stockpile of answered prayers, held in reserve against lean seasons, released loose into the world like doves.

Most landed gently. A fisherman's prayer for his son's safe return, warehoused twenty years prior when the son had drowned anyway, found its way to the man's surviving daughter and gave her a fair wind home from across the strait. A prayer for rain, sold twice by fraud and repossessed by the guild, fell as actual rain on the east barley fields while the rest of the sky stayed clear, causing the local priest to have some kind of episode.

One landed badly, in the way that made the week memorable.

A prayer for a neighbor's cow to die, purchased in a moment of spite forty years ago by a man now dead, matured on a Tuesday afternoon and struck a cow in the west pasture. The cow belonged to the dead man's granddaughter, who came down the hill to the counting-house with a pitchfork and a grievance, and Ulyana met her at the door with the full account open on the desk: original purchase, date, seller, price paid, and — newly entered in her own hand — *asset dishonored upon dissolution of issuer; compensation due.*

"We killed your grandmother's enemy's cow," Ulyana said, "forty years late, by accident, and it landed on you. Here's what your grandfather paid, with interest compounded the honest way, minus the value of one cow, and the difference is yours."

The granddaughter stared at the figures. Stared at the cow money. Lowered the pitchfork.

"My gran told me the Thorn bought prayers," she said. "I thought she meant it wicked."

"The Thorn bought everything. We're just deciding, lately, what it means to have owned it."

The girl took the money, and came back the next day with a pot of honey, because that was how the city was learning to speak the new arithmetic — not *what do I owe* but *what can I add* — and Ulyana logged the honey in the gift-book with more ceremony than some contracts had received.

* * *

Midwinter approached, and with it Sable's appointment with the locket, and Davor's first frost-tender seedlings under glass, and the anniversary of a vote held in a fractured hall where a found family had chosen, narrowly, to keep one of its own.

On the last market day before the solstice, Ulyana set up a table in the square with a sign she'd painted herself:

**FREE READING OF CONTRACTS. NO OBLIGATION. NO INTEREST.**

For six hours she read fine print for farmers and fishwives and one sheepish junior clerk from the magistrate's office who'd brought a promissory note he shouldn't have signed. She showed them where the traps lived — the clause folded into the seal, the interest that woke on the third year like a flea, the word *shall* doing heavy lifting in the dark. She taught them to read for themselves, and made them do it out loud, and charged nothing, and Davor sat beside her all day saying nothing, minding the pen pot, being witnessed.

Near dusk, a woman lingered after the queue had gone. Older. Travel-stained. Hedge-wise in the set of her shoulders, though her hands were empty — no grimoire, no satchel, nothing.

"You'd be Okonkwo's girl," the woman said. "The arithmetic witch. I knew your mistress, before the fire. Before you."

Ulyana's blood went slow and cold. "She's gone ahead, I heard. To collect."

"Gone further than that." The woman smiled without warmth. "But she left something with those of us who remember her. A message, for when you'd surfaced. She said you'd want it, and she was right, because she made you want things — that was always her method."

"Say it or don't."

"She said: *Check the apprenticeship papers again, little ledger. All of them. I forged more than one set, and one of the sets isn't yours.*"

The woman touched her cap and walked into the dusk, and Ulyana stood in the emptying square with the contract-table between her and the dark, hearing the sentence turn over like a page.

Not hers.

Someone else's forged papers existed. Someone else had been sold the way she'd been sold — or worse, someone else's soul might still sit in a reliquary beneath the mountain, unclaimed, unaudited, its owner walking around above ground not knowing they were collateral.

And midwinter — the eve of reckoning, when the court's books lay briefly open — was three nights away.

She gathered her pens slowly, and called for Davor, and went home to pull every record she had ever kept.
