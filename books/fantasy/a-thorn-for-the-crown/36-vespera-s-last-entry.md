## 36. Vespera's Last Entry

The ledger arrived on the morning the snow finally stopped falling, which was fitting, because Vespera had always had a talent for timing.

Ulyana found it on her desk in what remained of the counting-house — the Gilded Thorn's front room, stripped of its shelves now, its bottles and jars and little lacquered boxes of impossible things all given away or broken open in the great unburdening. The desk itself was the only piece of furniture she'd refused to surrender. It had been hers since her first week, when she was nineteen and terrified and Vespera had set her down behind it with a pot of ink and said, *Numbers don't lie, girl. People do. That's why we keep both.*

The ledger sat square in the center of the blotter. The oldest ledger. Bound in leather gone dark as dried blood, its corners worn to softness by three centuries of hands. She hadn't seen it since midwinter's eve, since Vespera walked out of the guildhall with it under her arm and into the blizzard, and Ulyana had spent a shameful portion of the weeks since wondering whether she would ever see it again — and a still more shameful portion hoping she wouldn't.

There was no note. There didn't need to be. Vespera had never been a woman for notes; notes were apologies, and apologies were debts, and Vespera Underhill — no relation, though the city had always enjoyed assuming one — paid her debts in person or not at all.

Ulyana sat down. She did not open the book at once. She made tea first, because that was what you did, that was the ritual, that was the small domestic armor against the enormous things. She warmed the pot. She measured the leaves with the little brass scoop that had come out of the guild's stores, one of perhaps six items in the entire inventory that had never been magic at all, just good brass, honestly bought.

Then she opened the oldest ledger to its final page.

*Paid in full.*

Two words. No date, though the ink was fresh enough to shine. No signature, though the hand was unmistakable — that upright, unhurried script, each letter standing like a soldier at attention, the hand of a woman who had spent sixty years writing down other people's obligations and had learned, somewhere along the way, to make even her alphabet look solvent.

*Paid in full.*

Ulyana read it eleven times. She knew because she counted; counting was how she prayed, how she grieved, how she did everything that couldn't be done honestly in words.

Because here was the thing about Vespera: she had tried to sell Ulyana. Not metaphorically, not almost — she had sat across this very desk and offered her a trade, serve or be substituted, cold as coin. She had forged apprenticeship papers thirty years before Ulyana was born, or helped forge them, or stood in the room while someone else did, threading an unborn girl's soul through the gears of a bargain she was too frightened to face herself. She had built the guild's whole glittering edifice of impossible commerce on top of a hole in the ground where her own soul used to be, and when the mountain came calling, she had looked at the newest clerk in her employ and thought, *there, that one will do.*

And yet.

And yet she had taught Ulyana everything worth knowing. That arithmetic was a form of courage. That every contract has a seam if you read it enough times. That the difference between a swindle and a settlement is mostly a matter of who does the sums. She had fed her, housed her, defended her against creditors and customs men and once, memorably, a minor demon with a grievance about interest rates. She had kept a scared runaway hedge-witch's apprentice off the streets for seven years without ever once asking what Ulyana had stolen from her mistress, or why, or whether the theft had been worth it.

She had voted, in the end, with her feet — walked up the mountain alone rather than let the guild buy Ulyana's freedom with anything more than its own dissolution. Whatever else could be said of Vespera — and Ulyana had spent several sleepless nights compiling the list, both columns, debits and credits — she had never once asked anyone to pay a price she wasn't standing beside.

Except the ones she'd already sold. Except the ones who'd never been asked at all.

That was the knot. That was the thing Ulyana turned over and over like a coin she couldn't decide was counterfeit. You could not audit a person. There was no column for *she saved my life* that balanced against *she damned me before I drew breath.* The books wanted resolution. Life handed you entries that simply refused to reconcile, and then it expected you to close them anyway and carry the imbalance forward, year after year, until it stopped being an error and became just — the shape of your accounts. The shape of your love, if you were foolish enough to call it that.

Ulyana finished her tea. She took out a fresh pen.

Below *paid in full*, in her own careful hand, she wrote:

*Received of Vespera Underhill: one ledger, one education, one life. Consideration acknowledged. Account closed.*

It wasn't forgiveness. She wasn't sure it was gratitude. But it was true, and true entries were the only currency she'd ever trusted, and she suspected that somewhere on a mountainside, a woman with a soldier's handwriting would have accepted nothing less.

* * *

They found Vespera's trail three days later, and by *they*, Ulyana meant Davor found it, because Davor found trails the way other men found taverns: instinctively and slightly against their better judgment.

"She went up the pilgrim road," he said, coming into the counting-house shaking snow from his shoulders like a large dog. "Old path, pre-quiet. Nobody's used it since the gods went to sleep. Her prints are a week old but the wind hasn't filled them."

"Are we going after her?"

Davor considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which was considerable, which was one of the reasons — Ulyana admitted this only in the privacy of her own head — she'd begun sleeping badly in ways that weren't entirely unpleasant.

"Do you want to go after her?" he asked.

"I want—" Ulyana stopped. Started again. This was another thing Vespera had taught her, inadvertently: that honesty required drafts. "I want to know if she made it. I want to ask her why. I want to tell her the account is closed. I want her to have heard it from me and not from the mountain."

"That's four wants," Davor said. "Which one's the real one?"

"The last one."

"Then we go." He was already turning back toward the door, pulling his coat straight, and he added, over his shoulder, with the terrible gentleness he reserved for exactly two people and one of them was currently a child asleep upstairs: "But Ulya. If she's gone past the point where the living can follow — we turn around. We don't pay the mountain twice."

Sable appeared at the top of the stairs, wrapped in a blanket three sizes too big, her dark eyes solemn above it. She was nine going on ancient, and in the weeks since the winter broke she had begun to grow into her inheritance the way a tree grows into lightning damage — slowly, crookedly, alive.

"I dreamed about her," Sable said.

Ulyana went very still. Since the court, since the reliquaries and the frozen petitions, Sable's dreams had developed a regrettable tendency toward accuracy.

"What did you dream?"

Sable came down the stairs one step at a time, arranging her dignity. "A door. A door in the rock, and it was open, and there was warm light inside. And she was standing in front of it, and she wasn't scared." A pause. "She was holding something. She put it down before she went in. Like you put down something heavy when you get home."

The silence in the counting-house was long and full of tea steam.

"Well," Ulyana said finally, and her voice was steadier than she felt. "Then we walk fast, and we bring her the reply, and we hope the door stays open long enough for a courier."

* * *

They left at dawn, the three of them — Davor with his old enforcer's pack, Ulyana with the oldest ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her back, and Sable between them because there had been no serious discussion of leaving her behind, only a brief negotiation conducted entirely in glares, which Sable won on points.

The pilgrim road climbed through country the winter had remade. Snow still lay deep in the high hollows, but the lowlands ran brown and dripping, and here and there the first green insistence of spring was pushing up through the mud like a debtor through a locked door. On the second day they passed a shrine — one of the old ones, pre-quiet, its god-statue weathered faceless — and someone had left fresh bread on the altar. Not an offering. Just bread, with a scrap of paper: *no charge. pass it on.*

The new arithmetic, working its way up the mountainsides.

On the third day the trail entered the rocks proper, and Davor stopped so abruptly that Sable walked into his back.

"There," he said quietly.

Vespera's pack sat against the cliff-face, squared neatly, buckles fastened. Beside it, her coat, folded. Her boots, side by side, toes pointed at the mountain.

Beyond them, in the rock itself: a door. It had not been there on any map, on any survey, in any of Davor's years of collecting along these roads. It was simply stone that had decided, recently, to also be a door — and it stood open, and from within came light the color of late afternoon through honey, warm and patient and utterly indifferent to whether anyone chose to enter.

On the flat stone before the threshold lay a single sheet of paper, weighted with a pebble.

Ulyana knelt and read it. It was dated the day the ledger had appeared on her desk — Vespera had written it before she sent the book down, before she climbed, before any of it. The hand was steady. The hand was hers.

*To the auditor of record:*

*I have reviewed my own accounts and find them insolvent. Sixty years of buying time with other people's collateral makes a poor balance sheet, whatever the quarterly figures say. I am told the door takes payment in truth, and truth is the one asset I have hoarded too long. I intend to pay what I owe and see what remains.*

*You will want to know if I am sorry. Yes. For the papers. For you. For every name I moved around a board because moving mine hurt less. You will also want to know if I would do it differently, and the honest answer — the one I am paying with — is that I do not know, and that not-knowing is the largest debt of all.*

*You wrote below my entry. I felt the ink dry. Old ledgers know when they are closed properly. Thank you for doing the sums, girl. You always were better at them than I was.*

*Do not follow. The door is not for creditors.*

*— V.*

Ulyana read it aloud, because Sable tugged her sleeve and asked, and because some documents deserve witnesses.

When she finished, nobody spoke. The warm light breathed in the doorway. Somewhere far below, meltwater ticked down the mountainside, keeping time.

"Heavy thing," Sable said softly, looking at the pack, the coat, the boots. "She got to put it down."

"Yes," Ulyana said. Her throat hurt. She let it. "She did."

Davor cleared his gruff throat. "Door's closing," he said. And it was — slowly, by fractions, the stone remembering what it was.

Ulyana unwrapped the oldest ledger. She turned to the last written page, beneath her own entry, beneath *received in full*, and she added one line more, quickly, in the last seconds of the light:

*Settled in kind. May the next creditor be kinder.*

Then she stepped back, and the door became rock, seamless and silent, and the mountain held what it held.

They stood a while. Then Davor shouldered Vespera's pack — someone should carry it home, someone always should — and Sable took Ulyana's hand, and they started down through the melting snow toward the valley, where spring was arriving late and stubborn and entirely unbooked.

Halfway down, Sable stopped walking.

"Ulyana," she said. "Look."

Down in the city, spread out gold-green in the afternoon sun, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, the river running bright and free of ice —

and on the roof of the old guildhall, catching the light, a figure. Small at this distance. Standing perfectly still. Watching the mountain.

Watching *them*.

"It can't be," Davor breathed. "We watched the door—"

But Ulyana was already counting — the figure's height, the angle of the light, the way it stood with its weight even on both feet, upright and unhurried, like letters standing at attention.

She knew that posture. She would know it anywhere, in any light, at any distance.

Someone was standing on the roof of the Gilded Thorn wearing Vespera's silhouette — and the oldest ledger, still wrapped against Ulyana's back, had grown suddenly, impossibly heavy.
