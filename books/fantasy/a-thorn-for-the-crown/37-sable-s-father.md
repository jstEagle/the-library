## 37. Sable's Father

The locket had not spoken since the night they bought it.

Ulyana had checked it three times in the weeks after the winter broke — held it to the lamp, tipped it, shaken it gently the way one shakes a purse to hear whether anything rattles inside. Nothing. The vendor at the Market of Small Mercies had sworn it held a god's voice, and gods' voices did not go stale like milk or luck. But the locket lay cold and dumb in Sable's palm, a coin-sized oval of tarnished silver on a chain too fine for anyone who had ever owned anything worth locking up.

"It's waiting," Sable said, when Ulyana asked if she wanted to open it yet.

"Waiting for what?"

"I don't know." Sable turned it over. Her thumb found the worn groove where a thumb had clearly rubbed it ten thousand times before hers — her father's, presumably, or whoever had sold his voice into that market in the first place. "For me to be ready."

Ulyana understood that better than she could say. There were pages in her own past she had avoided reading until she was braced for them. So she said nothing, and let Sable carry the locket in her pocket through the strange spring of the guild's dissolution — through the days of carrying bottled memories up from the vaults and pressing them into strangers' hands, through the evening Davor broke ground for his garden over the old vault site and got into an argument with a spade, through the mornings when Ilya stood at the counting-house door as if guarding something that no longer needed guarding, because there was nothing left to take.

It was nearly summer when Sable came to find Ulyana at the new ledger table, the locket closed in her fist, her face doing the thing it did when she had decided something and dared anyone to notice she was frightened of it.

"Tonight," she said. "After dark. Will you sit with me? Not — " a quick flare of pride — "not *for* me. Just near."

"Just near," Ulyana agreed.

* * *

They sat in the garden Davor was making, because Sable chose it and no one argued with her about sacred spaces anymore. The snowmelt had finally finished its long retreat from the mountain's shadow, and the soil was black and generous, and Davor had planted things with the same stubborn patience he'd once applied to collecting debts: beans, turnips, a row of sunflowers he claimed were for the birds but which everyone knew were for looking at. The guild's old sign — GILDED THORN, ALL THINGS OWED — had come down in the thaw, and the wall behind the garden still showed the pale rectangle where it had hung, like a scar healing white.

Sable sat on the low wall with the locket in both hands. Ulyana sat an arm's length away with a blanket neither of them acknowledged needing. Somewhere above, Ilya was on the roof out of habit; below, Davor was pretending very loudly to weed.

Sable opened the locket.

Inside there was no portrait, no curl of hair, no pressed flower. Just a shallow hollow, empty, and a faint smell Ulyana couldn't name — ozone, maybe, or the air after lightning, or the specific cold that lived under mountains. For a long moment nothing happened. The locket sat open on Sable's palms like a small mouth.

Then it spoke.

"*Is someone there?*"

The voice was not what Ulyana expected. She had imagined, without meaning to, something vast — a voice with thunder in it, a voice that made lamps burn blue. It was none of those things. It was a tired man's voice, warm at the edges and worn thin in the middle, the voice of someone who has been alone so long that hearing another person is half joy and half pain. It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do in dreams, and it made every hair on Ulyana's arms stand up even though it was, unmistakably, gentle.

Sable's breath went out of her all at once.

"Yes," she whispered. Then, louder, steadying herself against her own shaking: "Yes. It's me."

A silence. In that silence Ulyana felt the whole night lean forward — Davor's weeding stopped, Ilya's boots went quiet on the roof, even the wind seemed to hold itself.

"*Me,*" the voice repeated slowly, tasting the word. "*I don't — forgive me. My memory is not what it was. They took pieces of it for collateral, and the pieces they didn't take, I gave away myself, one name at a time, until — *" A pause, and then something moved in the voice that might have been a man sitting up straighter in the dark. "*Wait. Wait. Your voice. I know your voice.*"

"Do you?" Sable's voice cracked down the middle.

"*You used to sing. Badly. You sang everything flat and you refused to be corrected, and you sang anyway, all the time, because you said songs weren't for being right, they were for being sung.*" The voice was gathering speed now, stumbling over itself. "*You sang to the lamps. You sang to the river. You sang to — to — *"

"Sable," she said. "My name is Sable."

The locket was quiet for so long that Ulyana thought it had emptied already, spent its single coin on almost-remembering. Then:

"*Sable.*"

He said it the way a starving man says bread. He said it twice more, quietly, as if checking it against some internal ledger that had been missing exactly one entry for thirty years. And then the voice broke — a god's voice, breaking like any father's, small and human and helpless — and Sable was crying, and Ulyana put the blanket around her shoulders without either of them deciding it should happen.

"*They told me I traded your names,*" he said when he could speak again. "*That's what I remember last — being told. That I owed something too large to name, and that names were legal tender in the court beneath, and that I paid. I told myself it was mercy, that they would be raised by mortals who could keep them safe from what I owed. I told myself so many things. But I never remembered — I could never remember — whether I had loved you enough to feel it as a cost. Do you understand? I feared I had given you up the way one gives up a debt. Lightly. And now — *"

"Now you remember," Sable said.

"*Now I remember.*" The voice was fierce suddenly, blazing in its little silver prison. "*I remember your mother singing back at you, harmonizing badly on purpose to make you laugh. I remember you falling asleep mid-song once, in my arms, and I stood holding you for three hours because putting you down felt like ending something. I remember — Sable. I remember you. Whatever else they took, whatever I sold — that came back. Hearing you say it, it all came back.*"

Sable pressed the locket to her chest, then pulled it away to look at it, then held it close again, unable to decide between keeping him and seeing him.

"How much time do you have?" she asked.

The voice paused, and when it answered, it had done the arithmetic already. Gods were bad at many things, Ulyana thought, but apparently accounting ran in the family.

"*Enough,*" he said. "*Not enough. Enough for what matters. Ask me.*"

* * *

She asked him everything.

She asked about her mother, and he told her — a mortal woman from the salt country, sharp-tongued and unsentimental, who had married a minor god knowing exactly what that meant and finding it, mostly, funny. "She called me her worst investment," the voice said, fond as firelight. "She said loving a god was like lending money to the weather."

She asked why he had let himself fall into debt, and the answer was longer, and worse, and true: the old gods had been going under for centuries, their worship drying up, their accounts bleeding out, and her father had been one of the last solvent ones only because he was small enough to hide. When the great ones began defaulting, the creditors came down the list, and his number eventually came up, and he had signed everything they put in front of him because the alternative was watching his family be collected piece by piece. "The names were mine to give," he said. "So I gave them. I have spent every year since trying to remember whether that was love or cowardice, and tonight, hearing you — tonight I know it was love. It doesn't make it right. But I know."

She asked if he was still alive, in whatever way gods are alive, and he laughed — a rusty sound, a hinge unused. "*Barely. I am mostly memory now. Most of me is scattered across that market like loose change. This voice is perhaps a tenth of what I was. But a tenth of a god is still more than most men get, and I am spending all of it on you.*"

And then, near the end — because the locket was growing lighter in her hands, Ulyana could see it, could see the tarnish brightening toward silver as whatever powered it burned down — Sable asked the question she had carried longest.

"Why didn't you come back? After the debts were forgiven. After the winter broke. The court dissolved everything. Everyone's contracts burned. You could have — "

"*Could I?*" He said it gently, without reproach. "*Child, look at what is left of me. Even freed, even whole in law — what would have walked out of that mountain but a tenth of a father, wearing his voice? You deserved better than a ghost arriving on your doorstep. And you — *" the voice softened, and Ulyana heard him smile, actually heard it, an impossible thing she would carry to her grave — "*you don't need me. I can hear it. You have people. The one sitting beside you — ask her name, she's been holding her breath for an hour — you have a woman there who watches you the way I watched you sleep. You built what I was too cowardly to trust the world to build for you. You found your own ledger, and it isn't written in debts.*"

"She's Ulyana," Sable said, wet-eyed and laughing. "She's the best arithmetic in the world."

"*Then she can check my final figure,*" the voice said. "*Sable. Listen. The locket will empty soon. What's left goes back to the world — that's how it works, everything returns, I learned that late but I did learn it. Don't grieve the metal. I am not in the metal. I was never in the metal. I am in the fact that you exist, and you are brave, and you sing flat, and somewhere in this city there is a garden growing over a place where people used to trade in children's souls, and you helped plant it. That is my estate entire. You are my inheritance, and you are spending yourself beautifully.*"

The light was failing. Not the lamplight — the lamplight was fine. Something inside the locket was guttering, the way the prayer candles used to flicker unlit, the way impossible things announced themselves leaving.

"Dad," Sable said — and the word came out of her like a splinter coming free, thirty years under the skin — "Dad, I'm glad it was you. Even the parts that hurt. I'm glad you were real, and I'm glad you're — " her voice caught — "I'm glad you remembered me."

"*Sable,*" he said, one last time, saying it the way you set down something heavy and precious on a high shelf where nothing can knock it off. "*Sing something. Badly. One line. Let me leave to music.*"

She sang. Flat, defiant, perfect — a harvest song from the salt country, four words of it, her voice cracking and steady at once. The locket grew warm in her hands, warmer, and then — Ulyana saw it happen, and would swear it to anyone who asked — the last of the tarnish lifted off the silver entirely, so that for one heartbeat it shone like a small moon, like a coin of honest weight, like a thing newly minted rather than long spent.

Then it cooled, and went silent, and was only a locket.

* * *

Sable sat with it a while. The garden was very quiet. Davor had abandoned all pretense of weeding and stood by the fence with dirt on his hands and wetness he wasn't hiding particularly well; Ilya had come down off the roof at some point and leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man relearning what hearts were for.

Finally Sable stood, crossed to the corner of the garden where Davor's sunflowers wouldn't reach, and knelt. She dug a small hole with her fingers — no ceremony, no words, just work — and laid the locket in it, chain and all.

"You could keep it," Ulyana said quietly. "As a thing."

"It's not a thing." Sable patted the earth flat. "It's an account, and it's settled." She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and clear. "He said everything returns. So — let it return. Maybe it'll be somebody else's locket someday. Somebody else's voice in it." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, done. "Besides. I've got what matters. I've got the memory, and I've got the name, and I've got — " she gestured at the ridiculous collection of them, the enforcer and the collector and the auditor, standing in a garden over a dead guild — "all this. Nobody pawned *this*. This one's mine outright."

Davor cleared his throat, which was how he cried. "Sunflowers'll grow over it," he said gruffly. "Come summer. Face east. He'll get the morning."

"He'd hate that," Sable said, smiling. "He hated mornings."

"Then he can complain about it," Davor said. "Ghosts should have hobbies."

They walked back inside together, four people and a garden, and it might have been the end of the story, right there, if the world were kind about endings.

But at the door Ulyana glanced back once at the small turned earth in the corner — and stopped.

There was a flower growing there. One flower, out of season, out of reason: a single stem of white, blooming through soil that had been bare an hour ago, opening in the dark with the unhurried confidence of a thing that answers to no calendar. It was not any flower Ulyana knew, and she had catalogued stranger inventory than most.

She said nothing. Neither did Sable, who had seen it too, and whose hand found Ulyana's and gripped it hard.

Some inheritances, Ulyana thought, decline to stay buried.

And in the morning, when the envoy arrived at the gate — robed in mountain-grey, bearing a sealed crown upon his collar and a request for an audience with *the woman who audited heaven* — Ulyana would remember that flower, and understand that the world had noticed what she had done, and that noticed things always, always come due.

But that was tomorrow's ledger.

Tonight there was only the garden, and the small white bloom turning its face toward the mountain, and a girl who had gotten her name back walking inside to dinner with her family.
