## 38. The New Arithmetic

Spring came to Thornhollow the way forgiveness does: slowly, then all at once.

The snow went first from the rooftops, then from the gutters, then in long dark veins down the mountain's flanks until one morning Ulyana woke to birdsong and the smell of wet earth through a window she'd left open on purpose, just to prove she could. The frost-rot had taken half the winter crops. The granaries stood at their lowest in a generation. And yet when she walked to market that first true morning of thaw, people were arguing about seed prices — *arguing*, loudly, with hope in it — and an old woman was selling early radishes out of a basket like they were rubies.

The world, it turned out, could be rebuilt on arithmetic after all. Just better arithmetic than anyone had used before.

She found the building by accident, or told herself she had. It sat on Candlemaker's Row, two doors down from where the Gilded Thorn's old counting-room had been — a narrow-fronted shop with a bowed door and a sign-hook over the lintel that still held nothing. It had been a chandler's once, then a pawnbroker's, then nothing for three winters running. The rent was insultingly low because everyone knew the row was haunted by bad luck, which Ulyana considered excellent advertising for a place that intended to teach people exactly how bad luck got made.

She stood across the street for a quarter of an hour doing sums in her head before Davor found her there.

"It leaks," he said, coming up beside her with his hands in his pockets, looking at the shop the way he looked at most things: like something that might need carrying.

"The roof?"

"Everything. Floors, windows, my patience."

"We can fix it."

"I know." He glanced down at her. "I already bought the nails."

That was Davor's whole proposal, delivered in full: *it leaks, I bought the nails.* She had learned, over the years since the mountain, that this was how he said enormous things. That night he'd paid three days of his life to wights without mentioning it — that too had come out later, sideways, in a shrug. Some men built cathedrals. Davor bought nails.

They signed the lease that afternoon with the landlord, a nervous widower who kept glancing at the corner where Sable sat cross-legged on the counter reading a book upside down, purely to watch him sweat. When he asked what kind of business it would be, Ulyana said, "A counting-house," and when he asked what they counted, she smiled the smile that had once frozen a room full of gods.

"Contracts," she said. "We count contracts. We find out who owes whom, and we tell the truth about it."

The widower lowered the rent another tenth.

* * *

They opened on the first of Planting Month with no ceremony, which was itself a ceremony, given that half the city had expected fireworks and a blessing. There were no bells. There was no ribbon. There was a swept floor, four desks scavenged from three different bankrupt estates so that no two matched, a blackboard Davor had hauled across town on his back, and a sign painted by Sable in letters that crawled slightly if you looked at them too long:

**THE NEW ARITHMETIC**
*Contracts read free. Debts explained free. Lies in small print pointed out, also free.*

Beneath it, smaller, because Sable had insisted and Ulyana had lost the argument:

*No gods were consulted in the founding of this establishment.*

People came anyway. They came the way people come to a well in a dry season — cautiously at first, then in numbers that surprised everyone except Ulyana, who had spent a winter watching starving families sign themselves into servitude over harvest advances written in language designed to be unreadable.

The first customer was a cooper with flour on his sleeves who slid a paper across the desk as if it might bite. "The miller says I owe him my daughter's labor till she's nineteen. Says it's all here, plain as day."

Ulyana read it aloud to him. Slowly. Every clause. It took eleven minutes. By minute six the cooper had gone very still. By minute nine he had gone very quiet in a different way, the way a man goes quiet when he is deciding whether to be ashamed or furious and has chosen furious.

"That's not what he told me it said."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "It isn't."

"What do I do?"

"You go back to him," she said, "with me standing behind you, and you tell him the contract requires arbitration by the city magistrate under section four, and that you are invoking it. And when he blusters, you say the words *misrepresentation of terms* until he turns pale. Then you renegotiate. Your daughter stays home."

The cooper stared at her. "That's legal?"

"That's *reading*. Legal is just reading that someone finally bothered to enforce." She tapped the paper. "You could have done this yourself. Anyone could. That's the entire point of this room."

He came back three days later with a jug of cider and his daughter, who wanted to learn her letters. Ulyana put her at the desk beside Sable, and that was how it began properly: not a business, but a slow contagion of literacy spreading outward from four mismatched desks.

Davor taught evenings, though he refused to call it teaching. He called it "showing people where the knives are hidden in a document," and he did it with the same flat patience he'd once used walking debtors through their ruin, except now the lesson always ended the same way: *you don't have to sign.* He kept a tally on the blackboard's corner, chalked in his blunt hand — *signatures not signed:* and a number beneath it that grew every week. It was at forty-one by mid-spring. He never explained the tally to anyone. He didn't have to. Everyone in Thornhollow knew about the child he'd once refused to collect on, and they understood that the number was a kind of penance measured in prevented sins rather than committed ones.

Ilya arrived in the second week without announcing himself, the way he did everything. One morning there was simply a third man at the back desk, sorting documents into piles with a precision that made Ulyana's teeth ache with envy, and when she raised an eyebrow at Davor, Davor shrugged the shrug that meant *he'll explain or he won't.*

He explained eventually, in his own time: the under-court's procedures were still lodged in his memory like shrapnel, and half the predatory contracts circulating in the city were drafted on templates older than the guild — templates whose loopholes he knew by heart. "I spent fifteen years learning how to take everything from people," he said, not apologizing, because Ilya never apologized, he only reported facts like weather. "Seemed wasteful not to spend the next fifteen giving it back."

Sable, meanwhile, had appointed herself the schoolmistress of children, mostly because children weren't afraid of her. The divine blood showed less now — her eyes had settled into an ordinary brown most days, though in candlelight they still flickered blue at the edges, a leftover tide — but children sensed something anyway, the way dogs sense storms, and instead of fleeing it they clustered around her and demanded stories. So she taught them letters through stories: the alphabet as a descent into the mountain, each letter a toll-gate, each word a lamp burning blue. By summer's edge she had a dozen small students who could read their own names, which Ulyana privately considered a more durable miracle than anything the old gods had managed in three centuries.

* * *

On the evening the prayer candle appeared, Ulyana was alone in the shop, closing up.

It happened the way such things had begun happening again lately — quietly, at the edge of vision. She was squaring the day's papers when she noticed light where there should have been none: on the shelf by the door, where Davor kept a stub of ordinary tallow for working late, a flame stood upright in the cold wax. No match. No wick lit. The flame simply *was*, small and gold and patient, throwing a circle of warmth across the shelf.

Her hand went to the ledger at her belt out of pure habit. Old reflexes died hard; for three hundred years the Gilded Thorn had catalogued every impossible thing in the city, and part of her still reached for a pen whenever reality misbehaved.

But the guild was gone. Dissolved, given away, its inventory scattered back into the world like ashes from a pyre. There was no vault anymore, no entry to write, no column for this to belong to. Whatever the flame was — a god's small courtesy, a stray petition, a leftover miracle looking for a buyer — it wasn't hers to price.

She stood and watched it for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the walls, the spring rain was coming down soft and steady on a city that no longer traded in bottled luck. Someone, somewhere, was probably praying. Someone always was. Three centuries of silence hadn't cured mortals of the habit; it had only made them hoard their prayers like misers, spending them only in desperation, and desperation was the worst possible exchange rate.

The flame guttered. Steadied. Flickered again, leaning toward her like a question.

*Ask,* it seemed to say. *Ask, and it will be answered. You know now how cheaply it can be done. You know what the answer costs.*

And oh, she was tempted — that was the thing nobody warned you about peace, how much of it was just temptation declining slowly. One candle. One answered prayer. She could ask for the roof to stop leaking. For Davor's three days back. For Vespera, walking somewhere in the high passes with a ledger full of *paid in full*, to turn around and come home. She could ask for her mother's face, her father's voice, the hedge-witch's cottage unburned, the whole ruined inventory of a life sold before she was born. The gods were sleeping, but they slept lightly now, and the flame on the shelf was a door left ajar.

Ulyana picked up the candle stub, cupped her palm around the flame the way you'd shelter a match in wind —

— and blew it out.

The dark came back gently, the good ordinary dark of a shop at closing time, smelling of chalk dust and ink and the bread Ilya had left on the table. She set the candle back on the shelf, unlit, where it would stay. If someone needed a prayer answered, they could come inside and learn to read the contract first. That was the whole religion of the place, scrawled on a board above a crooked door: *nothing is owed that hasn't been counted, and nothing counts unless you understand it.*

The door opened behind her. Davor, with rain in his hair and nails — more nails — in a sack over his shoulder. He looked at the dead candle, then at her, and something passed between them that needed no words, because they'd both stood in the frozen cathedral beneath the mountain and watched what answering prayers actually looked like when the bill arrived.

"Roof leaks," he said.

"I know."

"Bought more nails."

She laughed, and blew out the lamp, and let the spring dark take the shop — no flames but the ones they lit themselves, no miracles but the slow kind, the kind you built plank by plank and clause by clause, the kind that stayed.

Tomorrow the cooper's daughter would arrive for her second lesson, and the tally on the blackboard would climb past fifty, and somewhere a miller was already drafting a new contract with fewer knives in it, because word had spread that this street checked. Tomorrow the work continued. But tonight there was only bread and rain and four desks waiting empty in the dark, and Ulyana Okonkwo, ledger-keeper of nothing, owner at last of her own small unencumbered soul, locked the door on a life that owed no one — and was owed by no one — anything at all.

Outside, unnoticed by either of them, the candle stub on the shelf gave one last flicker in its sleep — not a prayer this time, but something older and stranger, a light passing through toward some other door in some other city where someone else was learning, slowly, the same new arithmetic.

And pinned to the inside of the door, where only someone leaving would see it, a scrap of paper had appeared that had not been there at closing: a single line, in handwriting Ulyana almost recognized, almost remembered, almost trusted—

*The crown sends its envoy at dawn. Decide what you will ask.*
