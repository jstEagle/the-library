## 1. The Taster's Oath

The stew arrived at the ninth bell, and Petra Okonkwo was already tired.

Six years in the Ember Court taught them that tiredness came before everything else—the tasting, the waiting, the small theater of survival performed three times a day for a king who had never once looked at their face. Tiredness was the first ingredient in every dish served beneath the ember-crowned sigil, and it was the one thing no poisoner had ever thought to weaponize, because nobody poisoned a taster on purpose. A dead taster was a confession. You killed the king first and let the taster's corpse tell everyone how.

Petra stood at the sideboard in the antechamber off the royal dining hall, sleeves rolled to the elbow, watching Cook Aldous's boy set down the covered tureen with the particular care boys used when they wanted you to believe they weren't nervous. Steam curled from under the lid. It smelled of venison, juniper, the smoked salt that came down from Thornfell in winter—and underneath all that, faint as a rumor, something sweet.

Not spoiled-sweet. Not onion gone over or wine cooked too long. Sweet like crushed flowers. Sweet like a garden at dusk when the bees have given up.

Petra didn't move. That was the first rule, learned in the first month, carved into them by Master Taster Sefu's knotted cane: *When you find it, become furniture.* A taster who flinched told the poisoner two things—that they'd been caught, and that they were worth catching again.

"Something wrong?" the boy asked. He couldn't have been more than fourteen. His eyes went everywhere except Petra's face, which meant he'd been coached, because every kitchen hand in the Ember Court knew the tasters by now and knew that looking at them directly was somewhere between rude and unlucky.

"The lid," Petra said mildly. "I want it to rest longer. Flavor deepens."

It was nonsense and the boy probably knew it was nonsense, but nonsense was a language both of them spoke fluently. He bobbed his head and backed out through the service door, and Petra counted his footsteps—eleven, then the squeak of the stair, then nothing—before lifting the lid the rest of the way.

The steam rose thick and honest. Venison browned properly, bone stock reduced to silk, barley, juniper, thyme. A good stew. A *loving* stew. Someone had spent hours on this dish, which was the part that always made Petra's chest ache in a way they'd never found words for: the best poisons rode inside the best cooking. Nobody wasted nightshade on gruel.

They took up the silver spoon kept for this purpose alone, polished so bright Sefu joked you could check your teeth in it, and drew the first taste from the center of the tureen, where the heat pooled deepest.

Venison. Salt. Juniper blooming late on the back of the tongue. And there—third taste, small spoonful held against the roof of the mouth the way Sefu had drilled into them until the technique lived in their jaw instead of their memory—there it was. Belladonna. Nightshade. Not enough to kill a grown man outright, but plenty if the king's heart was already taxed, and everyone at court knew King Aldric's heart had been "delicate" since the plague years. A dose sized for a delicate heart. A dose sized by someone who understood the king's body better than most physicians did.

Someone inside.

Petra set the spoon down with a soft click against the silver tray and became furniture.

* * *

The thing about being furniture, Petra had learned, is that furniture sees everything.

From the sideboard they could watch the antechamber's single window, where afternoon light fell in a dusty column; the door to the dining hall, where the king's steward would appear at any moment to collect the cleared dish; the service stairs, which creaked in four places and sang in a fifth. They could hear the muffled sounds of the hall beyond—Aldric's court eating its own dinner, laughter with an edge of performance, the scrape of a hundred chairs. Somewhere in that noise was the person who had seasoned a king's stew with death, and they would be listening too, waiting to learn whether their work had survived the tasting.

Petra's hands were steady. That was the strangest part, the part they never got used to. Inside, some animal part of them wanted to bolt—to run down the service stairs and out through the kitchens and into Thornfell where the smugglers paid in coin and asked no questions—but the hands stayed still, because six years of swallowing other people's murder had built a wall between what Petra felt and what Petra did, and the wall was load-bearing.

Sefu appeared at the antechamber door before the steward did, as he always did when the timing mattered, though how he always knew was one of the quiet mysteries Petra had stopped asking about. He was a big man going soft at the edges, gray threading his beard, moving with the deliberate calm of someone who had made stillness a profession. His eyes swept the room once—the tureen, the untouched spoon, Petra's face—and stopped.

"Well?" he said.

"You'll want to look at the third taste."

He crossed the room without hurry. Took up the spoon. Held the mouthful exactly as long as Petra had, jaw tight, gaze fixed on the middle distance. Then he spat into the copper spittoon kept beside the sideboard for exactly this moment, and set the spoon down, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because saying it out loud made it real, and real things required action, and action in the Ember Court was a stone dropped into very deep water.

"Nightshade," he said finally. "Dosed low. Dosed clever." He looked at Petra, and there was something in his face Petra couldn't read—a flicker, gone too fast to name. "How long have you known?"

"Since the boy set it down. Before I opened the lid."

"The smell?"

"The smell."

Sefu grunted. He took a clean cloth from his sleeve and draped it over the tureen, covering it the way you'd cover a body. "You didn't signal me."

"I signaled you the only way I could. By not clearing the dish."

"A steward will be here within the minute expecting a cleared dish." Sefu's voice stayed level, almost gentle, the tone of a lesson rather than a scolding. "What do we do, little ember?"

*Little ember.* The nickname had been his since Petra's first year, when they'd arrived at the court half-starved and mute with grief, a smuggler's orphan with a tongue too sharp for their own safety and an immunity to poison that Sefu had discovered by accident—or claimed he'd discovered by accident, though Petra had long since stopped believing in accidents where Sefu was concerned. He'd fed them a leaf of foxglove on a wager with the old physician, watched them chew it and swallow and feel nothing, and gone white as whey, and then he'd smiled like a man who'd found a coin he'd lost twenty years ago.

That smile had raised Petra. Fed them, housed them, trained them, loved them in the sideways, undemonstrative way of men who keep ledgers of feeling and never let anyone audit them. Which was why the question hurt more than it should have.

"We do our oath," Petra said. "We report it. We say nothing else. We don't guess at who."

"And if the who is someone we love?"

Petra looked at him. The light from the window fell across half his face and left the other half in shadow, and for one absurd heartbeat they thought: *he knows who it is.* Then the thought passed, the way bad thoughts do, leaving only a bruise behind.

"Then we report it anyway," Petra said. "The oath doesn't have exceptions. You wrote it."

"I wrote it." Something moved behind his eyes again, quick and dark as a fish under ice. "Yes. I did."

The steward chose that moment to open the dining-hall door, and whatever else might have passed between master and apprentice folded itself away like letters into a drawer. The steward was a narrow man named Corvin with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent expression of someone doing arithmetic he disliked. He saw the covered tureen and stopped.

"The king's supper," he said.

"Spoiled," Sefu said smoothly, before Petra could draw breath. "Barley turned. It happens, even in the best kitchens, even in autumn. The dish will be remade. The king will dine late tonight, and Her Majesty's table should be informed so no one waits on him."

Corvin's expression did arithmetic. "Spoiled," he repeated.

"Fermented. You can smell it from here if you've the nose." Sefu lifted the cloth a fraction, releasing a gust of steam, and Corvin recoiled on cue, waving his hand. Whatever else Sefu was—and Petra was beginning to suspect the list was long—he had buried more scandals than the court had gravestones. "Take word to the undercook. New pot. Quietly."

"Quietly," Corvin echoed, and withdrew.

The door shut. The antechamber was silent but for the tick of the mantel clock and the sound of Petra's own breathing.

"Why did you lie?"

Sefu was already gathering his coat from its hook, unhurried, a man with an errand. "Because a poisoning reported loudly becomes a purge, and a purge in autumn becomes a winter of blood. Because whoever planted that nightshade wanted it found—one dose, low, detectable, in a dish I know you taste first. Think, little ember. If they'd wanted the king dead, they'd have dosed the wine, and they'd have dosed it heavy, and they'd have done it on a night when the second taster draws the duty." He settled the coat across his shoulders. "They wanted *you* to find it."

The words landed in Petra's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"They wanted me noticed," Petra said slowly.

"Or they wanted to see whether you'd notice. Or they wanted to see what you'd do once you noticed. There are more tests in this world than the ones with answers written down." Sefu paused at the antechamber door, hand on the frame, and looked back, and for a moment the mask slipped—not into menace, but into something worse: worry. Genuine, unarmored worry, aimed at Petra like a lamp. "Go home. Eat nothing you haven't made yourself. Lock your door."

"It's just nightshade in a stew—"

"Petra." He almost never used their name. It sounded strange in his mouth, formal and heavy, a coin of large denomination spent deliberately. "Lock your door."

Then he was gone, footsteps fading down the corridor, and Petra stood alone with a covered tureen full of murder and a head full of questions, and outside the window the light was failing over the spires of the Ember Court, turning the copper roofs the color of dying coals.

* * *

Home was two rooms above a chandler's shop in the Tanner's Ward, chosen because the stair was visible from the street and the roof was climbable and the chandler, a deaf old woman named Marga, asked no questions and remembered everything. Petra ate bread and hard cheese and an apple bought that morning from a fruit cart they'd watched for ten minutes, and while they ate, they thought.

Six years of invisible living, and the invisibility had rules. Rule one: be excellent, because excellence is expected and therefore boring. Rule two: be predictable, because predictability is a kind of camouflage—you become part of the architecture, and nobody conspires against a wall. Rule three, the one Sefu had whispered the first time he'd caught them slipping out through the postern gate toward Thornfell: *have no story anyone wants to steal.* No title, no land, no famous face, no debts worth collecting. Be a person to whom nothing that happens can matter.

And now someone had seasoned a king's supper with a dose calibrated to be found, and every rule Petra had built their life around sat cracked down the middle like a dropped plate.

Because here was the truth they turned over with the apple core, here was the thought that wouldn't die quietly: the dose hadn't been sloppy. It hadn't been greedy. Whoever had brewed it knew the king's constitution, knew the kitchen's rhythms, knew the tasting order—and had *chosen* the detectable path. Poisoners who want success hide their work. Poisoners who want attention leave it in plain sight, the way a hunter leaves a snare where the deer must pass.

And there was only one deer in the Ember Court whose passing mattered enough to bait a trap for.

Unless—

Unless the target had never been the king at all.

Petra washed the plate, dried it, set it in the rack. Through the floorboards came Marga's tread, slow and even, her day ending as it always ended. Outside, the Tanner's Ward lamps were being lit one by one, and beyond them the palace burned against the darkening sky like a coal that refused to go out—which was, the priests said, exactly what the crown's ember was: a fire that would not die, passed from reign to reign, keeping the kingdom warm and the king... 

Keeping the king something. Nobody talked much about what those twenty plague years had cost Aldric. Nobody talked about why the decrees came slower now, or why the king's signature had acquired that faint scorched edge, or why he dined alone more often than any monarch in living memory. The court had ways of not talking. Silence was the court's native language, and Petra had learned it young and spoken it fluently, and it had kept them alive.

But silence had also kept them alone. That was the trade, and they had made it at fifteen with their eyes open, and they had never once regretted it out loud.

Tonight, alone in the chandler's rooms with the door locked twice and a knife under the pillow out of habit rather than fear, Petra regretted it quietly, thoroughly, the way you regret a tooth you can't stop probing.

*They wanted you noticed,* Sefu had said. And the terrible, treacherous thought that surfaced as the candle guttered was not *who?* but *what if I'm glad?*

What if some starved corner of them—the corner that had swallowed poison for kings and carried contraband through mountain passes for coin and eaten dinner alone above a chandler's shop for six years—what if that corner had been waiting its whole life for someone to aim a trap at it, just to prove it existed?

Petra blew out the candle and lay in the dark and listened to the ward settle into sleep, and told themselves the shaking in their hands was cold.

It was near midnight when the knock came—three soft raps, then a pause, then two more, the smugglers' sign, the *all clear, open careful* rhythm Rook used on the rare nights he came down from Thornfell with cargo and gossip. Petra was at the window before the echo faded, knife palmed, watching the street below where a figure in a drover's cloak waited in the lamplight with his hood back and his grin already showing.

Rook never came at midnight. Rook came at dawn, drunk on victory or running from failure, never in between, and never quiet, and never looking up at the window like that—with urgency instead of mischief.

Petra opened the door, and Rook pushed past them into the dark room, breathing hard, smelling of pine and horse sweat, and said the words that broke the ordinary life open like a seal on a letter:

"There are caravans in the passes buying old books, Pet. Old books and relics. Paying stupid money. And today one of them was asking after—" he caught his breath, "—after *you*. By description. Not your name. Your *description*."
