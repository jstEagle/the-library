## 2. Smuggler's Toll

The mountain didn't care that I was tired.

Thornfell didn't care about anything. That was why I loved it. The passes had no protocol, no bowing, no one watching to see if your hands trembled when you swallowed. Just rock and wind and a road that had been illegal for so long it had forgotten it used to be legal at all.

I'd left Ember Court three days ago with a sealed satchel of letters sewn into my coat lining and a cover story about visiting a sick aunt in Lowmark. The sick aunt was real. Aunt Bess had been dying of the same fictional fever for six years, bless her, and she enjoyed the visits. But the letters weren't hers. The letters belonged to people whose names I never asked—wool merchants avoiding tariffs, a minor lord sending love notes to a woman not his wife, and once, memorably, a bundle of correspondence from someone high enough up in the palace that if I'd been caught with it, no aunt in the world would have saved me.

Six years as the king's taster had taught me exactly two things: how to die politely, and how to disappear.

Smuggling was just disappearing with a destination.

I crested the last ridge above Miller's Rest as the sun went down behind the Teeth, painting the snowfields the color of old blood. Below me, tucked into a fold of the mountain like a secret, sat the waystation—a squat stone inn that officially did not exist, run by a woman who officially had never been born.

Maren's Rest, the smugglers called it. Maren herself called it nothing. She didn't believe in naming things you might have to deny later.

The smell hit me halfway down the switchbacks: woodsmoke, frying onions, wet wool. My stomach clenched around the memory of the tasting hall—the long table, the silver spoon, the king's stew ladled first into my bowl while forty courtiers pretended not to watch me chew. Here there were no bowls set aside for me. Here nobody cared what I ate, because here poison was a trade good like anything else, priced fairly and sold honestly, and nobody wasted it on a courier's dinner.

That distinction mattered more than I could explain to anyone who hadn't lived both lives.

* * *

Maren met me at the door, which meant she'd seen me coming an hour ago, which meant someone had spotted me on the ridge and signaled down. Nothing at Miller's Rest happened without Maren knowing.

"You're late." She was fifty and built like the mountain itself, gray-streaked hair hacked short, forearms that could crack walnuts or skulls depending on the day.

"The pass was iced past the second marker."

"The pass is always iced past the second marker." She stepped aside to let me in. "You look thin."

"I always look thin."

"Eat anyway." She jerked her chin toward the common room. "Rook's been asking after you since Tuesday. He's insufferable when he waits."

My chest did the thing it always did at Rook's name—a small unclenching, like a fist relaxing. I hadn't realized I'd been holding it.

The common room held maybe twenty people, which on a Tuesday night in a place that didn't exist meant business was either very good or very quiet, and I couldn't tell which. A dice game muttered along in one corner. Two wool runners argued tariffs in another. And at the long table near the fire, dominating it the way he dominated every room he'd ever entered, sat Rook.

He was hard to miss. Six foot four, Blackwater River dark skin gone nearly blue-black in the firelight, a beard he claimed he grew to hide his double chin and everyone else knew he grew because shaving in winter was for fools. He wore a patched coat that had once, impossibly, been fine—and he waved both arms over his head the moment he saw me, like a man signaling a ship home.

"Petra! Petra Okonkwo, back among the living!" He kicked a chair out for me with one boot. "Sit. Sit before you fall down. You've got frostbite in your eyebrows, you know that? Actual frostbite. In your eyebrows."

"It's called weathering, Rook. It makes me look distinguished."

"It makes you look like a scarecrow that lost an argument with a blizzard." He pushed a bowl across the table—stew, thick with barley and root vegetables, real salt in it. "Eat. Then talk. Then eat more. In that order. Those are the rules."

There were no rules at Maren's Rest, but Rook liked pretending there were, because rules made things feel like home, and Rook was the kind of man who collected strays until they accidentally became a family.

I ate. It was good. It was safe—I'd checked it out of habit, the little automatic sweep of scent and sight that never switched off, even here, even now—and knowing it was safe made it taste better than anything the palace kitchens had ever produced.

"So," Rook said, when I came up for air. "The crown sends its little ghost into my mountains again. What are we carrying this time? Love letters? Ledgers? Please tell me it's something interesting. Last month all you brought me was tax evasion paperwork and I nearly died of boredom right there at this table."

"Love letters," I said. "And one thing I can't tell you about."

"One thing you can't—" He clutched his chest. "She wounds me. Six years of friendship and I get 'one thing.'"

"Five years," I said. "And you got the friendship free. The discretion costs extra."

He laughed, big and easy, and the dice players looked over and smiled too, because everyone smiled when Rook laughed. That was his gift. Mine was being forgettable. Between us we made a decent crew.

"Fine. Keep your secrets." He leaned in, dropping his voice, and the laughter fell away from his face like a mask coming off. Underneath it was the other Rook—the one who ran half the illegal traffic through Thornfell and missed nothing. "Actually, speaking of secrets. There's something you should hear, since you come and go from the fancy side of the kingdom. Might be nothing. Probably is nothing. But it's odd, and I don't like odd."

"Odd how?"

"You remember the Delver brothers?"

"Everyone remembers the Delver brothers. They tried to rob you."

"They tried to rob me *badly,*" Rook said, with dignity, "which is different. Point is, their cousin Ferro runs salvage now. Digs through old houses, flooded vaults, estate sales—junk work, mostly. Copper pennies, cracked mirrors, that sort of thing. Three months ago Ferro tells me he's got buyers. Real buyers. Paying stupid money."

"For junk?"

"For *old* junk." Rook turned his bowl slowly on the table, thinking. "Not gold, not jewels. Books. Papers. Relics—old charms, family seals, that kind of thing. Anything with age to it and writing on it. Ferro sold a water-damaged psalter last month for more than I make moving a whole wagon of silk. More than *two* wagons."

I kept chewing. Kept my face bored. Inside, something small shifted, the way a floor shifts before it decides whether to hold.

"Who's buying?"

"That's the odd part. Nobody knows. They come through in caravans—closed wagons, hired muscle, no colors, no crest. They pay in mint coin, fresh-struck, same mint mark every time. Ferro showed me one. I won't say whose face was on it, because I'd like us both to keep our heads, but let's say it wasn't a stranger's."

Fresh royal coin, spent quietly, buying old paper through intermediaries. Somebody in the palace—or very close to it—was collecting the past.

"Maybe a scholar," I offered. "Rich patron building a library."

"Maybe." Rook's eyes stayed on mine. "Except scholars don't burn what they buy. Ferro's cousin watched a caravan stop at a shrine ruin outside Hallowmere—place nobody's prayed at in sixty years. They went through the offering boxes, took the old prayer cards, the handwritten ones, left the coin offerings sitting there. And then they burned the cards in a brazier right there in the road and moved on."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind me the dice game rattled on, indifferent.

"Burning prayer cards," I said.

"Burning *specific* prayer cards. The old ones. Handwritten ones. Anything personal, anything with names in it." Rook spread his hands. "Now maybe I'm paranoid. Being paranoid has kept me alive thirty years, so I don't apologize for it. But when somebody pays gold for history and then burns the parts with names in them, that's not collecting, Petra. That's *editing.*"

The word settled into me and wouldn't leave.

"And there's more," Rook said. "Ferro says they've got a list. An actual written list of what they want. He glimpsed it over a buyer's shoulder—couldn't read most of it, but one line stuck with him because it was underlined twice. Want to hear it?"

"Do I have a choice?"

"No." He grinned, but it didn't reach his eyes. "It said: *any genealogical records, any family papers, eastern provinces, priority: Okonkwo.*"

The name landed in the room like a dropped knife.

I didn't move. I'd had six years of practice not moving—six years of swallowing hemlock and nightshade and worse while my face stayed pleasant and empty, six years of being furniture that could taste. So I finished my stew. I set down my spoon. I said, "Huh," in a voice that would have fooled anyone.

Rook wasn't anyone.

"Why would anyone care about your family?" he asked, gentle, careful, the way you approach a bird with a broken wing. "No offense, but the Okonkwos aren't exactly—"

"We're nobody," I said. "Village tanners, two generations back. My grandmother kept a garden and my grandfather drank. There's nothing to find."

"Then it's a coincidence. Another Okonkwo somewhere."

"Must be."

"Must be," he agreed. Neither of us believed it. That was the thing about Rook—he never pressed, never pried, but he never pretended either. He just let the truth sit between us, unwashed, until it decided to speak for itself.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to coals and the dice game had ended in the usual accusations of cheating and the usual reconciliation over the usual bottle, Maren found me in the stable where I was checking my boots' hidden seams.

"Sefu still writing to you?" she asked.

Every few months, a letter reached me through channels only Maren knew—Master Taster Sefu, the man who had pulled me off the streets at fourteen, fed me, trained my tongue, taught me that a person could be property and still be excellent at their work. His letters were always the same: dry instructions on new compounds, warnings about court politics, and one line at the end, always, that was almost warm. *Be careful. Come back. The spoon misses you.*

"Got one waiting for me when I return," I said.

"Mm." Maren leaned against a stall post, arms crossed. "You know what I think? I think that man loves you the only way he knows how, which is badly and from a great distance, like a man throwing notes into a river."

"He does all right."

"He does all right," she conceded. "But Petra. Listen to an old woman who's survived by noticing things." Her eyes were steady and dark in the lantern light. "Whatever those caravans are doing—whatever names are on whatever lists—you be careful. Not court-careful. Mountain-careful. Court-careful means smiling. Mountain-careful means running."

"Thanks, Maren."

"Don't thank me. Eat more when you get home. You look thin."

* * *

I left before dawn, the way I always did, and Rook walked me to the trailhead with a lantern, complaining about the hour, the cold, my eyebrows, the state of the kingdom, and the quality of Maren's ale, which was excellent, which was part of the complaint.

At the fork where his road split from mine, he stopped and pressed something into my hand. A charm—crude iron wire bent into a knot, the kind peddlers sold to pilgrims.

"Against bad luck," he said. "Probably doesn't work. But it's ugly enough that bad luck might be embarrassed to come near you."

"Rook."

"Take it. Argue with me next time." His grin came back, the full one, the one that made rooms warmer. "Come back through in a month. Bring gossip. Bring that thing you can't tell me about so you can keep not telling me about it. It's become one of my favorite traditions."

"I will."

"And Petra—" For a second the grin flickered. "If the lists, and the burning, and all the rest of it turns out to be about you after all... you come to me first. Before anyone. Before Sefu. Before whoever signs the pretty coins. Promise me."

I promised him. It was easy to promise, standing in the cold with iron wire in my palm and his shadow huge against the stars. Promises made in daylight on solid ground always feel cheap.

We shook hands like smugglers do—one grip, firm, no ceremony—and then he turned back toward the inn and I turned up the pass, and neither of us knew that it was the last time we'd stand on that road together.

* * *

Three days later, back in the capital, I delivered the untellable thing to a dead-drop behind a chandler's shop, visited my perpetually dying aunt, and returned to the Ember Court through the servants' gate as though I'd never been anywhere at all.

Master Taster Sefu was waiting in the tasting chamber with a letter in his hand—not one of his, I saw immediately. Wrong paper. Wrong seal. Official.

"Petra." His voice was strange. Careful. He was looking at me the way Rook had looked at me on the road, except Sefu had none of Rook's warmth to soften it. "Where were you exactly, these past ten days?"

"Lowmark. My aunt. You know that."

"Yes. Of course." He handed me the letter. "This came for you. I don't know how they got your name."

The seal was wax the color of dried blood, stamped with an open book. Inside, five lines in a precise scholarly hand:

*A lot of interest has surfaced at auction regarding certain historical materials. Your presence is requested Thursday next, Archive Hall, by appointment of the estate of the late Magister Corvin. Attendance is not mandatory. Your attendance may nevertheless prove illuminating.*

*Regarding matters of inheritance.*

I read it twice. Outside the narrow window, the palace bell rang the change of watch, and somewhere below us the kitchens began the evening's cooking—food I would taste before the king touched it, as I had every night for six years, invisible, obedient, safe.

Inheritance.

Rook's voice followed me down the corridor all the way to my cell of a room: *that's not collecting, Petra. That's editing.*

Thursday was four days away.

I put the letter in the fire, watched it curl and blacken, and told myself I wasn't going.
