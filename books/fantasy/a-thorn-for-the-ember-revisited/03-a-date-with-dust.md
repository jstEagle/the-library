## 3. A Date with Dust

The auction house smelled of old vellum and older money.

Petra had been to the Gilded Folio three times before—twice as a courier, once as a buyer's proxy—and each visit confirmed the same truth: nothing in Thornfell City announced wealth more quietly than dust. The great houses of the Ember Court polished everything until it screamed. Here, in the archive district where scholars wore their poverty like credentials, the dust was permitted to settle because removing it would mean admitting anyone had touched these things at all.

Petra moved through the reading room in the gray wool of a minor clerk, hood back, face arranged into the pleasant blankness that had kept them alive for six years. A taster learns early that expression is a confession. Surprise, disgust, delight—all of it could be read by the wrong eyes and priced accordingly. So Petra wore nothing, and the room ignored them, which was exactly as intended.

Lot forty-seven sat on a velvet cushion beneath a glass case that had clearly been cleaned more recently than anything around it.

A book. Small, octavo, bound in cracked leather gone the color of dried blood. No gilding. No title. The kind of object that looked worthless until you noticed the catalog number written on its slip in ink worth more than the book appeared to be.

*Prophecy of the Ember Years,* the auctioneer's assistant had murmured when Petra asked, not looking up from his ledger. *Circa one hundred years old. Attribution disputed. Opening bid forty crowns.*

Forty crowns was nothing. Forty crowns was what Sefu spent on saffron in an afternoon. But the slip also bore a second mark—a small scorched circle pressed beside the lot number, like a wax seal that had burned instead of stamped—and Petra had learned, over six years of tasting other people's intentions, that a scorch-mark meant someone powerful wanted the thing and did not want to be seen wanting it.

Which meant it was worth seeing.

They took a seat near the back, ordered tea they didn't drink, and waited for the room to fill.

It filled slowly, the way archive auctions do—clerks first, then dealers, then the pale serious people who bought history the way other men bought horses. And then, ten minutes before the lot came up, the door opened and a woman walked in who did not fit the room the way a key does not fit a lock it was never cut for.

She was perhaps thirty, dark-skinned, with ink stains climbing both forearms like climbing plants and spectacles pushed up into a riot of hair she had clearly lost a fight with that morning. She wore a scholar's robe but wore it wrong—the belt knotted carelessly, the hem dusty, one sleeve singed at the cuff. She scanned the room with the frank hunger of someone who had come for one thing only, and her gaze snagged on the glass case over lot forty-seven and stayed there like a hook in cloth.

Then, inexplicably, it slid sideways and caught Petra.

Not recognition—Petra had made sure no one in this room could recognize them. Something else. The look of a person doing arithmetic. Hood, clerk's wool, no bidding paddle yet, seat chosen for sightlines rather than comfort. She crossed the room without hesitation and dropped into the chair beside Petra's with the gracelessness of genuine preoccupation.

"You're not a dealer," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Dealers sit in front." She gestured at the front rows with her chin. "Clerks sit wherever their masters put them. You chose your own seat, and you chose it facing the door *and* the case, which means you're either security or competition." She pulled off her spectacles, breathed on the lenses, wiped them on her singed sleeve. "You don't move like security."

"And what do I move like?"

"A courier," she said, "or a thief," and then, fairly: "There's overlap."

Petra turned to look at her properly for the first time. Up close her eyes were a startling amber, flecked like something mineral, and behind the spectacles' smudges they missed absolutely nothing. A scholar, then, but not the fusty kind. The dangerous kind. The kind who read rooms the way Petra read cups.

"Yew Andrade," she said, offering a hand stained to the second knuckle. "Royal Archive, adjunct. Which means I fetch things and get blamed when they're missing."

"Adjuncts don't have forty crowns for a disputed lot."

"Adjuncts have patrons." She said the word the way one says a diagnosis. "And my patron wants this book very badly, which is why I'm telling a stranger my name instead of working quietly. Because if I'm going to lose this auction, I'd rather lose it to someone interesting."

"I haven't bid on anything."

"No," Yew agreed cheerfully. "But you've checked the case four times since I sat down, and your eyes did something complicated when the assistant mentioned the attribution dispute." She leaned in, lowering her voice to the conspiratorial register scholars use instead of whispering. "So here's my offer, stranger-with-no-name. If you're bidding, tell me now and I'll spend my patron's money with dignity. If you're not—if you're just curious—then stay curious with me, because I've spent two years chasing this book's provenance and every road leads somewhere strange."

The auctioneer rapped his little hammer. Lot forty-six, a bundle of tax ledgers from the reign of Aldric's grandfather, went to a yawning dealer for eleven crowns. Somewhere in the room a clock ticked with the patience of institutions.

"I'm nobody," Petra said.

"Mm." Yew smiled. "I've met several nobodies. They all check cases four times."

* * *

Lot forty-seven opened at forty.

Yew opened it, her voice steady, her hand white-knuckled on her paddle. A dealer countered. She countered back. The price climbed in careful increments—fifty-five, sixty, seventy—while the auctioneer's drone smoothed over the tension like icing over a wire.

At eighty, the dealer dropped out, shaking his head at some private calculation.

At eighty-five, a new voice entered from the back of the room, soft and unhurried, and Petra knew before turning that its owner would be wearing court colors under a traveling cloak. They didn't turn. Turning was information. Instead they watched Yew, who had gone very still, recalculating.

"Ninety," Yew said.

"One hundred," the voice replied, and now Petra did glance—a man, middle-aged, forgettable in the practiced way of professionals, holding his paddle loosely, smiling faintly at nothing. Not a collector's smile. An errand's smile.

Yew's jaw tightened. She looked at her lap, where her purse sat flat and hopeless, then—at Petra.

It was not a plea. It was worse than a plea. It was the look of a woman about to do something reckless and inviting company.

"One hundred and ten," said a voice from Petra's mouth, in a register they rarely used—flat, bored, slightly nasal, the voice of a clerk authorized to spend a master's money. Their paddle rose. "For the Master of the Salt Cellar, who collects ember-period esoterica."

The name meant nothing. That was the point. It was real enough to survive a casual inquiry and vague enough to dissolve on contact, one of eleven such identities Petra maintained the way other people maintained spare keys.

The professional in the back paused. Considered. Bidding against an agent cost time and left fingerprints; bidding wars over disputed lots attracted precisely the attention errands were meant to avoid. He let the moment stretch, then shook his head with theatrical regret.

Yew made a sound like a kettle coming to boil and had to disguise it as a cough.

"One hundred and ten, going twice—" The hammer fell. "Sold."

Petra paid in crisp notes drawn on an account that existed for exactly this purpose, signed a false name in a hand that wasn't theirs, and received the book in a wrapping of brown paper that felt suddenly, absurdly heavy.

"Well," Yew said, falling into step beside them before they'd cleared the room. "That was either the most generous act of a stranger's life or the most expensive mistake."

"The Salt Cellar collects widely. He'll want it studied."

"The Salt Cellar doesn't exist. I checked the guild rolls last spring when a wine merchant cited him in a provenance dispute." She kept pace effortlessly, hands jammed in her robe pockets, spectacles flashing. "Whoever he is, he owes me a debt, and scholars pay debts in access. So." She stopped in the street outside, in the thin cold light, breath fogging. "Open it. I've read every description, every catalog entry, every fragmentary citation for two years, and I have never seen the actual text. Five minutes. Then take it to your nonexistent master and vanish, and I'll haunt your memory forever as the best stranger I ever met."

Every instinct Petra owned said walk away. Take the parcel to the drop, collect the fee, remain a rumor in your own life. Six years of survival reduced to a single principle: *don't be interesting.*

But the scorch-mark on the lot slip had already broken the principle. Someone powerful wanted this book unseen, and Petra had spent six years learning that what the powerful hide is usually the only part worth knowing.

And underneath the caution, quieter, a thing Petra refused to name: *the taster of thorns.* No. That phrase wasn't in their head yet. What was underneath the caution was simpler—loneliness, and the unfamiliar warmth of being spoken to like a person rather than a function.

"Five minutes," Petra said. "There's a tea shop."

"There's always a tea shop," Yew said, already walking.

* * *

The back table of the Dented Kettle sat far enough from the windows to be safe and near enough the kitchen to hear the cook swearing at her apprentice, which Petra found steadying. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary world.

Yew unwrapped the parcel with a reverence Petra usually saw reserved for relics, which, given the contents, was possibly correct. The leather cover was cool and oddly dry, like skin long dead. No title. On the inside cover, in faded iron-gall ink, a single line:

*Ilsa Okonkwo wrote what she saw. Believe her or burn her; she has done her part.*

Petra's hand stopped on the page.

Okonkwo.

Their own name. Not a common one—not in Thornfell City, where names traveled with trade routes and theirs had arrived, so far as Petra knew, with a grandmother who never spoke of anywhere else. Sefu had found them orphaned at nine with nothing but the name and a resistance to poison that had made him weep and whisper *blessed child* into their hair.

"Something?" Yew asked.

"It's nothing." The lie came out smooth as oil. Tasters lie well; it's most of the training.

Yew turned pages with a bone folder, narrating low. "Verse prophecy, mostly—quatrains, the fashion a century ago. Political allegory dressed as vision, standard for the period, except—" she tilted a page toward the light "—except the accuracy citations are impossible. Later scribes annotated fulfilled passages within years of composition. Either this is a forgery written after the fact—which the dating says no—or—"

"Or she saw what she saw," Petra finished.

"Exactly! Thank you. Everyone at the Archive treats prophecy as a genre problem. Nobody asks the simpler question." She flipped onward, faster now, hunting. "There's a famous passage. The *Thorn Verse.* Half the fragmentary copies reference it and none contain it, which is how you know someone was censoring. If it's here—"

Her finger stopped.

The page held a quatrain in a cramped, slantwise hand, and below it, unlike every other page, a block of prose—dense, urgent, written as though the pen had been pressed hard enough to tear.

Yew read aloud, softly: "*When the crown burns twenty years upon a borrowed head, and the kitchens learn the taste of nightshade, then comes the taster of thorns—gray-eyed, friendless, fed on poison since childhood, who walks between the court and the outlaws' road.*"

The teahouse noise receded, as if the room had been dipped underwater.

Gray-eyed. Friendless. Fed on poison since childhood. Walks between the court and the outlaws' road.

Petra did not move. Movement would confirm the floor had opened beneath them. They stared at the brown tea in their cup and performed, flawlessly, the stillness of a clerk waiting out a scholar's enthusiasm.

Yew, oblivious, ran her finger down the margin. "Dated annotations—she dated everything, look—here's the nightshade line, she ties it to a specific day. *On the eve of the frost fair, when the taster drinks and does not die.*" She squinted at the date notation, the old reckoning, lips moving as she converted it. "Frost fair eve. That's..." Her finger stopped moving. "...tonight. By the modern calendar, that's tonight."

"That's a coincidence," Petra said. Their voice sounded distant, borrowed.

"Of course it is. Prophecy is coincidence with good publicity." But Yew had gone quiet too, the scholar's skepticism and the reader's chill arriving simultaneously. She turned back a page. "There's more prose. Listen: *I have written what will find whom it must. The book will travel to the taster's hand by another's coin, in another's name, on the day the thorn verse ripens.*"

Silence. The cook swore at her apprentice. The clock ticked.

"Another's coin," Yew repeated slowly. "In another's name." She turned and looked directly at Petra for the first time since opening the book, and her amber eyes had gone very wide behind the smudged lenses. "You paid under a false name."

"Many people do."

"Gray eyes," Yew whispered. Not accusing. Counting. "Friendless. Poison since childhood. Court and smugglers' road." Each item landed like a coin on a counter. "You told me you're a courier or a thief. You bid like someone who spends other people's money routinely. You knew the case had a scorch-mark—I watched you see it before I did."

"Scholar—"

"Say your name," Yew said. "Not your false one. Your real one. Because if the verse is about you, then the inside cover isn't decoration—it's addressed. *Wrote what she saw.* And the author's name is on the flyleaf, and you stopped breathing when you read it, and I would bet my adjunctship you recognized it."

The floor had not merely dropped. It had dissolved, and beneath it was not darkness but a corridor stretching backward through generations, lined with the same name.

Petra looked down at the open book. At the quatrain. At the words *taster of thorns* sitting in a century-old hand like a trap set before their grandparents were born.

"Okonkwo," they heard themselves say. Quietly. Truthfully, for the first time in an hour, in a year, in a life measured in swallowed poisons and unswallowed truths. "My name is Okonkwo."

Yew exhaled like a woman surfacing.

Outside, the light had begun to fail toward evening—toward frost fair eve, toward whatever the verse meant by *the taster drinks and does not die.* And in the doorway of the Dented Kettle, unhurried, patient as institutions, stood the forgettable man in the traveling cloak, scanning the room with an errand's smile, looking for a book that had just been paid for in someone else's name.

His gaze found their table.

He began to walk toward them.
