## 4. What Wears the Crown

Yew Andrade's rooms above the Scriptorium smelled of lamp oil, old vellum, and something sharper underneath—ink that had been mixed with ash. Petra noticed it the moment they climbed the stairs behind him, the night after the auction, and decided not to ask. There were questions a taster learned to leave unasked. This one could wait.

The scholar had already cleared his desk by lamplight, stacking books into towers that leaned like drunkards against each other. In the center of the cleared space sat the prophecy book itself, open to the page with Petra's own face described in century-old ink.

*The taster of thorns shall come at winter's turning, wearing no color and known by none.*

"You read it again on the walk here," Yew said. It wasn't a question either.

"I memorized it," Petra said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. Memorizing means I'm planning to do something about it."

Yew smiled—the quick, crooked smile of a man who had spent too many years alone with dead people's handwriting—but it faded fast. He pulled a second chair to the desk, its legs scraping on the floorboards, and gestured for Petra to sit.

"If we're going to do something," he said, "we should know what we're doing it *to*."

He turned the prophecy book toward himself and began flipping forward through pages of dense, cramped script. The paper was soft as cloth at the edges. Petra watched his fingers move and thought about how many hands had touched those pages before theirs—how many readers had skimmed past the words that were now sitting in Petra's chest like a swallowed stone.

"Here." Yew stopped at a section near the middle. "Ilsa Okonkwo wrote the first half as history. Plague years. Then the tone changes."

He read aloud, translating the old dialect as he went, his voice dropping into a rhythm that was almost liturgical:

*"In the season of ash, when the sickness took three of every five, the crown grew desperate. The king's father sought a guardian that would not die. He found one sleeping beneath the mountain, older than the kingdom, bound once before by wiser men who knew better than to wake it. He woke it. And it kept its bargain: the dynasty did not end. But nothing wakes without a price, and nothing binds without a chain, and the price and the chain were both laid upon the head that wears the crown."*

Petra felt the room get colder. Or maybe that was just them.

"The plague," they said. "The Ashfever. My grandmother survived it. She used to say the whole city smelled like a forge for two years."

"It did," Yew said. "Contemporary accounts agree on that much. Something burned in the palace for a season—no one could say what. Fires that left no damage. Heat with no source." He tapped the page. "Ilsa was there. She watched it happen."

"And then?"

Yew turned the page and went quiet for a long moment before reading:

*"Twenty years the ember has worn him. Twenty years the hand that signs is not the hand that chooses. The kingdom is governed well, which is the cruelest part, for no one looks for chains when the prisoner keeps the peace. I write this so that someone, someday, will look."*

Petra sat back. The chair creaked under them.

"That can't be right," they said slowly. "Aldric's been king for twenty-six years. Everyone says—" They stopped. Everyone said what? Everyone said the king was steady now, where he'd been reckless in his youth. That he'd *matured*. That the boy who nearly bankrupted the treasury on mercenary wars had become a careful, measured ruler almost overnight.

Almost overnight.

"The Ashfever killed his father," Petra said. "And his elder brother. Aldric took the crown at nineteen, mid-plague, with half the court dying around him. And within a year he was—different."

"Different," Yew agreed. "Or *bound*."

"But people would know. Someone would know. A king possessed for twenty years—you'd have rebellions, schisms, a civil war at minimum."

"Would you?" Yew leaned back, lacing his fingers over his knee. "Think like a taster, not a courtier. What do you actually know of the king? You taste his food. You see him at a distance, twice a day, behind a screen or across a hall. You've heard his voice—what, a dozen times? All formal pronouncements?"

Petra wanted to argue and found they couldn't. Six years in the Ember Court and their total conversation with King Aldric consisted of perhaps forty words, all of them ceremonial. Kings were distant the way weather was distant. You experienced their effects. You never got close enough to see what moved inside them.

"He governs through decree," Yew continued. "Written decree. Which brings me to the interesting part."

He crossed to a chest beneath the window and hauled out an armful of document tubes, dumping them onto the desk with a thump that made the lamp flame jump. "Copies. Forgeries of copies, technically—I bought them off a records clerk who owes me money. Decrees from across the reign. Look at the signatures."

Petra picked up the nearest scroll and unrolled it. A grain tariff revision, dated eleven years back. At the bottom, in strong, angular strokes: *Aldric Rex.*

They picked up another. Trade accord with the Salt Principalities, dated six years back. *Aldric Rex.* Another. A pardon for border raiders. Another. Land reform. Another. Another.

The signature was identical. Not similar—identical. Every loop, every cross-stroke, every flourish of the final *x*, repeated with a precision no living hand could sustain across twenty years of documents.

"No one signs the same way for two decades," Petra said quietly. "Hands change. Age changes them. Illness changes them."

"Not if the hand isn't doing the signing."

Petra held one of the scrolls closer to the lamp—and saw it. Faint, but unmistakable once you knew to look: a scorch mark bleeding through the ink, as though the pen had been dipped in something hot. On every document. The same faint brown ghost beneath the black strokes, like a brand under skin.

"Cold fire," Yew said. "That's what Ilsa calls it in her third chapter. The spirit doesn't write *through* him—it writes *as* him, using heat instead of ink where the binding runs deepest. She describes seeing it once, at a distance. A decree being signed in public. She says the parchment smoked."

Petra set the scroll down very carefully, the way they handled anything that might be poisoned.

Six years of tastings. Six years of standing in that hall while Aldric received petitions, issued judgments, greeted ambassadors. A king who never aged out of his handwriting. A king whose moods swung between a warmth so generous courtiers competed for his favor and a coldness so sudden grown men wept. Petra had catalogued those shifts the way they catalogued flavors—noting them, filing them, never asking why.

Because kings were weather. You didn't ask why.

"Here's the worst part," Yew said. He'd gone to the window, and when he turned back his face was gray in the lamplight. "The decrees are *good*, Petra. Grain tariffs that ended the famine cycles. Trade accords that halved banditry because they made smuggling legal trade—"

He caught himself. Petra raised an eyebrow.

"—so I'm told," Yew finished smoothly. "My point is: the thing wearing the crown is competent. Maybe more competent than the man ever was. Ilsa understood that. Listen." He read again, and his voice had gone rough:

*"Do not mistake the ember for a tyrant. Tyrants can be starved, poisoned, toppled. The ember is a steward. It keeps the roads safe and the granaries full and the peace unbroken, and it asks only one thing: that no one ever look closely at the hand that holds the keys. It rules the way a fever rules a body—efficiently, tirelessly, and with total indifference to whether the body was ever meant to live its own life."*

Silence filled the room. Downstairs, somewhere in the Scriptorium, a door banged; carters shouting about a delivery. Ordinary sounds. The city going on with its evening, fed and taxed and policed by a thing that had been wearing its king like a coat for twenty years.

Petra looked down at their own hands. Steady hands. Hands that had swallowed death a thousand times so that a stranger could eat safely. They had always told themselves the work meant something—that keeping one man alive mattered, even a man they'd never spoken to.

But the man wasn't alive. Not all of him. And everything Petra had done for six years—every bitter draught, every sleepless night after a suspicious dish, every silent meal eaten alone while the court dined—they had done in service of a hollow crown.

"There's one more thing," Yew said gently. "You're not going to like it."

"I haven't liked anything since Tuesday."

"The book names you. *The taster of thorns shall come.* But listen to what comes after." He flipped to a later page, one he'd marked with a ribbon, and read:

*"The taster of thorns alone may pass the ember's gate, for their blood remembers what was sworn. What was swallowed for the crown may be unswallowed. What was bound in haste may be loosed in patience. But the taster should know: the ember does not release what it holds. It only trades."*

"*Trades,*" Petra repeated.

"Trades," Yew confirmed. "I don't know what it means yet. Ilsa's next chapters are fragmentary—water damage, maybe deliberate. But whatever unbinding costs, it isn't paid by the king alone."

Petra stood and walked to the window. Below, the lamplighters were working their way up the street, little blooms of gold following the dusk. Somewhere out there, in the palace on the hill, King Aldric was dining—eating food Petra might have tasted that very morning, lifting a fork with a hand that hadn't been entirely his own since before Petra was old enough to hold a spoon.

They thought of the nightshade in the stew. Of Master Sefu's face when Petra showed him the tainted pot—how he'd gone pale, how he'd said *say nothing, I'll handle it*, and how the matter had vanished afterward like sugar in wine. Petra had assumed cover-up. Institutional embarrassment. Now they wondered.

Someone had tried to kill the king. Or someone had tried to kill the *ember*—not knowing you couldn't poison a fire. Nightshade would have dropped Aldric's body and left the spirit homeless, free to find a new host. Free to find a new crown.

Unless whoever planted it knew exactly what they were doing.

"I need to see the decrees from the last few months," Petra said, turning from the window. "All of them. Has anything changed? Any new policy, any shift in the king's behavior, any—" They stopped. Started again. "Something woke it up. Something made it nervous. Prophecy books don't surface at auction by accident, Yew. Someone sold that lot. Someone wanted it found—or wanted it *found first* by whoever's bidding against us."

Yew stared at them. "You sound like a spymaster."

"I sound like a taster. Same skill set. We both ask what's really in the cup." Petra pulled their cloak from the chair back. "Tomorrow I go back to work. I watch the kitchens. I watch the signatories. I watch who flinches when the king's name is said too loud."

"And me?"

"You keep reading. Find me the rest of Ilsa's chapters. Especially whatever she wrote about *trades*." Petra paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at the old book lying open on the desk—its pages breathing dust into the lamplight, patient as a grave.

"One more question," they said. "This hermitage of hers. Where Ilsa died. Do the margins say?"

Yew checked, running his finger down the edge notes. "North," he said. "Past Thornfell. Three days' ride, if the passes are open."

The passes were open. Petra knew every smuggler's cut through them better than the crown's own maps knew the roads.

"Then I know where I'm going," they said, "once I figure out what's trying to kill the king."

Behind them, Yew turned the lamp down low. The flame shrank to a blue point, and in its light the scorched signatures on the scattered scrolls seemed to glow—twenty years of cold fire, waiting for someone to finally burn back.
