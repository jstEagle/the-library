## 5. The Poisoner's Ledger

The nightshade hadn't come from the market.

I knew that by the second morning, standing in the cold room beneath the palace kitchens with three sprigs of dried belladonna laid out on a slate before me like suspects in a lineup. The leaves in the king's stew had been cut coarse and fresh, still weeping sap when I'd fished them from the pot. Market nightshade was old, dusty, stripped and sold by the fistful to hedge-witches. This had been picked within a day of use, and picked by someone who knew the plant—knew that the alkaline concentrates in the root, knew that a coarse cut in a rich broth would mask the bitterness long enough to reach a royal throat.

Someone inside the walls. Someone with hands.

I turned the sprigs over with a pair of tongs. Sefu had taught me that trick my first year—*never touch a suspect poison with your fingers, little ember; the body remembers what the mind forgets.* He'd said it while teaching me to hold a copper coin in my cheek during tastings, an old taster's superstition that did nothing and comforted everything.

Sefu. His name sat in my chest like a stone I kept trying to swallow.

Six years he'd raised me. Fed me, trained me, taught me the grammar of poison the way other fathers taught prayers. And three days ago, when I'd told him someone had put fresh nightshade in the king's stew, his face had done something I'd never seen it do.

Nothing.

Sefu's face always did something. He was a man built of expressions—eyebrows like punctuation, a mouth that narrated his opinions before his voice caught up. When I told him about the nightshade, his face went smooth as poured wax, and he said, "Are you certain?" in a voice with no punctuation at all.

I was certain. And now I was certain about something else: I needed to know what my father-in-everything was hiding.

* * *

The Master Taster's quarters occupied the corner of the taster's wing, two rooms and a private stair that smelled of cedar and copper. Sefu kept them like a shrine to the profession: drying racks of harmless herbs for show, a wall of tasting spoons arranged by metal and age, his bed made with the corners tucked so tight you could bounce a coin off them.

I went in while he was at evening prayers. He prayed every dusk, forty minutes, in the little alcove off the servants' chapel where the tasters had their own patron—a grim saint whose statue showed her holding a cup in one hand and her own severed tongue in the other. Forty minutes was enough. I'd counted his devotions a hundred times without ever thinking about what I could do with the counting.

Locking a door in the palace was a crime. Picking one was a hanging offense. I did it anyway, with the slim iron pick I kept sewn into my courier's coat for doors that belonged to other people's secrets. The lock was old. It opened like it was relieved to see me.

His desk was the obvious place, which meant it was the wrong place. I searched it anyway—correspondence with the guild, invoices for spoon-polish, a letter from a spoon-smith in the Low City complaining about a delayed order. Nothing.

The wrong place was the bed. I'd learned that from Sefu himself, ironically, in a lesson about hiding contraband: *People hide things where their hands are comfortable. Ask where a man's hands rest when he thinks no one watches.* Sefu's hands, when he sat in his chair by the window of an evening, rested on the arm of the chair. The left arm.

The left armrest was hollow. Of course it was. I pressed the hidden catch my fingers found before my eyes did, and the panel swung open on a book.

A ledger. Small, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its spine cracked from being opened and opened and opened. I knew the binding. Sefu had given me a journal in the same grey leather when I turned sixteen—*a taster keeps records, little ember; memory is the first poison.* Mine was half-full of tasting notes. His was full of something else.

I sat down in his chair, in the last of the light, and opened it to the first page.

The earliest entries were ordinary. Dated eleven years back, they catalogued the court's slow poisonings with a clerk's dispassion: *Baron Hesse, arsenic in the wine, survived. Lady Corven's maid, hemlock, did not. Question remains: who supplies the kitchens?* Reading them was like reading a stranger's diagnosis of a disease I'd lived inside without knowing. The Ember Court, which I had thought of as a place where I tasted danger so others didn't have to, was revealed on these pages as a place where danger was *cooked*, deliberately, seasonally, by hands that had never been caught.

Sefu had known. Sefu had *documented* it, year after year, and said nothing, done nothing, except train me to catch what came.

Then the entries changed.

Seven years ago, the handwriting shifted—not the hand itself, but the pressure of it, the way ink sits when a man writes something he doesn't want to be true. The entries stopped being about the court and started being about me.

*The girl shows the tolerance. Confirmed twice now, against protocol. I gave her what should have laid her flat for a day and she asked for more bread.*

I remembered that day. I was nine. Sefu had handed me a honeyed wafer, a treat, he'd said, for good work. I'd eaten it and asked for more bread. I hadn't thought about it in years.

*It's in her blood. It has to be. There's no other explanation. God forgive me for what I'm about to do with it.*

I turned the page. My mouth had gone dry, which for a taster is its own kind of alarm.

The next entries were dated forward. Not forward by days—forward by *years*. Entry after entry, each in Sefu's hand, each describing things that had not happened yet.

*Year six: the girl detects the nightshade. She will. She was built to.*

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world. I read it again. *Year six: the girl detects the nightshade.* Three days ago. Dated, in this book, seven years before it happened.

*Year six, later: she asks questions. She will start with the plant, then the supplier, then the ledger. I know her. I made her.*

The room had gone very quiet, the way a room goes when you realize you've been overheard your whole life. I kept turning pages because my hands had decided to keep moving whether the rest of me agreed or not.

*Year seven: she finds the book. I could move it. I won't. If she's to be what the blood says she is, she needs to find it. She needs to be angry. Anger makes them look.*

Them. Look where. At what. My fingers found the next entry and the next, and the dates kept walking forward into years I hadn't lived, in a hand I knew better than my own, describing a life I hadn't lived either but suddenly couldn't be sure I wasn't living right now.

*Year eight: Vess will make her move on the girl. Let her. The girl runs to Thornfell. The road teaches her what the court cannot.*

Rook. My chest went tight. *The road teaches her.* Was that a prophecy or a plan? Was there a difference, in this book, in this life?

*Year nine: she learns whose blood she carries. She will hate me for the hiding of it. That's correct. Hate is a kind of fuel.*

*Whose blood she carries.* I sat back in Sefu's chair and the chair creaked under me like a small accusation. Whose blood. What did that mean. I was nobody's blood; that had been the whole architecture of my life. Orphan of the border wars, bought by the guild, trained by Sefu, useful and unattached. I had built my invisibility out of that nothing like a wall out of river stones.

And here was my father, writing *whose blood she carries* seven years before I would learn it, as if it were a fact already settled, already known to him, already—my eye caught the next line and the floor finished falling—

*—already written.*

Because the last entry in the book, the final one, dated four years from now, was not a prediction at all. It was a transcription. Sefu's careful clerk's hand copying out words in another hand entirely—a slanted, impatient script, ink brown with age, that I had never seen before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own face in a warped mirror. The letters were old-fashioned. The paper was older, a scrap of something brittle pressed flat between the ledger's pages like a flower.

I read the transcription. Then I read it aloud, because some things need to be heard to be believed, and my voice in the empty room sounded like a stranger's.

*"The taster of thorns shall swallow what kings cannot, and in her throat the ember shall find either its ending or its heir."*

Below it, Sefu had written one line of his own. Just one. Dated—God, dated *next month*, a date that hadn't happened yet, a date that would arrive in thirty days whether I understood this or not:

*She reads the last entry today. I am sorry, little ember. I have been sorry for seven years.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the light to die and the room to go blue with dusk. Long enough for my hands to stop shaking and start again, twice.

The sounds of it came back in pieces: my own breathing. The creak of the chair. Somewhere below, the kitchens beginning their evening roar, and over it, faint and terrible, Sefu's voice leading the tasting bench through the evening's drills—*smell first, always smell first*—the same words he'd said to me at nine, at twelve, at sixteen, every day of a life that this book claimed had been scripted before I could write my own name.

*She was built to.*

I thought about the nightshade. Fresh-cut, expertly done, planted where only a taster would find it. I had spent three days asking who wanted the king dead. I'd been asking the wrong question. Nobody wanted the king dead. Somebody wanted the king's taster *noticed*. Somebody had seasoned a stew with a clue, and I had swallowed the bait whole, exactly as written, exactly as built.

*I made her.*

Had he? Had all of it been training, or had some of it been something else—the honeyed wafer when I was nine, the "tonics" he dosed me with every winter, the little cups of bitter tea he swore were for my nerves? My whole body was a question mark now. Every immunity I'd ever credited to luck or grit sat under new light, and the light said: *cultivated. Deliberate. Grown in you like a graft.*

I should have felt violated. I did feel violated. But underneath the violation, worse, was the thing I couldn't scrape off: he'd *known*. Seven years of dinners, of lessons, of him teaching me to hold a coin in my cheek, and he'd known what I was for. He'd watched me grow into a weapon the way a smith watches iron heat, patient, certain, sorry.

*I have been sorry for seven years.*

Sorry didn't unwrite it. Sorry didn't give me back the girl who'd believed her invisibility was her own invention.

I heard the stair before I heard the footsteps—the third step always groaned; I'd known that since I was a child sneaking down to steal honey—and I had perhaps ten seconds. Ten seconds to close the book and put it back and be the girl he expected, or to stay in his chair with his secrets open in my lap and let him find me there.

I stayed.

The door opened. Sefu stood in it, prayer-crease still pressed into his forehead, his tasting spoon still hooked in his belt, and he looked at me sitting in his chair in the dark with the grey ledger open, and his face did something I had never seen it do.

It broke.

"Little ember," he said, and his voice had all its punctuation now, and every mark was grief.

"You knew." My voice came out flat and strange. "You've known since before I could read. What am I, Sefu? Whose blood? *Written* by whom?"

He came into the room slowly, an old man all at once, and knelt—knelt, my father, my master, the man who had never knelt to anything but his grim saint with the severed tongue—on the floor at my feet like a penitent.

"There's a book," he said, "older than that one. And a grave in the Thornfell high country that the guild maps don't show. And a name I should have given you the day I took you in, and didn't, because the moment I gave it to you, *they* would have heard it too."

"Whose name?"

He looked up at me, and in the dark his eyes were wet and ancient and afraid.

"Yours," he said. "Your true one. And there are people in this court who have killed to keep it buried—and one of them just sent nightshade into the king's kitchen to find out if it still lives in you."

* * *

Outside the window, far across the palace roofs, a single light burned in the spymaster's tower—Lady Vess's tower, the one window in the Ember Court that never went dark. I had never thought about that before. I thought about it now.

Sefu knelt on the cold floor and began, at last, to tell me everything.
