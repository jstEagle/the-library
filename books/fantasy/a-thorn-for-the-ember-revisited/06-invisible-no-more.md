## 6. Invisible No More

The summons came the way bad weather comes in Thornfell: no warning, just a change in the pressure of the air.

Petra was in the tasters' quarters, grinding a chalk-and-honey compound Sefu had taught them to keep on hand for heavy-metal exposure, when a page knocked—actually knocked, which was already wrong. Pages did not knock for tasters. Pages arrived, breathless and dismissive, and expected you to follow them at a jog.

This one stood straight as a lance and said, "Lady Vess requests Petra Okonkwo. Now."

Not "the taster." Not "you there." The name. Full and spoken aloud, like a signature on a document.

Petra set down the mortar with hands that did not shake, because six years of swallowing poison had taught them that shaking was a luxury, and said, "Give me a moment to change."

"Lady Vess said now," the page repeated, but without heat. Even the boy knew that a summons phrased like that was not a summons about lateness.

They changed anyway. Clean shirt, hair bound back, the small silver tasting spoon polished and threaded through their belt where it always sat—a habit so deep it had become part of the body. In the corridor's one narrow mirror they checked their face the way other people checked for smudges: eyes flat, mouth neutral, the expression Sefu called *the still pond*. Nothing swimming on the surface.

Then they followed the page down through the palace, past the kitchens where the night-shift cooks were hauling the morning's stock pots, past the guard post where Corporal Anwen gave Petra the smallest nod—the two of them had an arrangement going back three years, dried fruit for silence about her card debts—and out across the frost-slicked courtyard toward the Spymaster's Tower.

Nobody went to the Spymaster's Tower. People were *taken* to it, the way things were taken to the gallows or taken to the physician, and everyone understood that the difference between the two destinations was decided entirely inside the building.

* * *

Lady Vess received them in a room without weapons on the walls, which Petra noted the way a sailor notes a strange current. Every other office in the Ember Court performed its function through decoration. The Marshal displayed swords; the Treasurer displayed ledgers chained to iron stands; even Sefu's workroom displayed its array of antidotes and vials like a promise. Vess's room held only a table, two chairs, a carafe of water, and a window looking east over the city rooftops toward the mountains, where Thornfell's passes cut white scars into the dawn sky.

She was pouring water into two cups when Petra entered. She did not look up until both cups were full.

"Sit, please." Her voice was low and unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it to be obeyed. She was smaller than Petra had imagined—court gossip painted her tall, a figure of shadow and suggestion—and she was perhaps sixty, with grey hair cropped close and the kind of face that would pass unremarked in any market crowd. That, Petra understood, was itself the performance. Vess had made invisibility an art form before retiring from the field, and she wore her plainness the way other women wore jewels.

Petra sat. Did not touch the water.

Vess smiled at that. It was a real smile, or a very good copy of one. "You don't drink anything in this building anymore. Since the stew."

"You know about the stew."

"I know about everything in this building." She folded her hands. "That is my function. But we'll come to the stew. First I want to talk about something else. Tell me what you know about Yew Andrade."

There it was. The floor did not drop away this time—Petra had learned something since the auction, which was that vertigo was a debt you paid once and then carried. Instead they felt a cold, precise clarity settle over them, the same state they entered when the spoon came out and the first swallow went down. A body deciding how to survive.

"A scholar," Petra said. "I bid against him over a book. He won. We argued. That's all."

"Mm." Vess turned her cup a half-circle on the table, aligning its handle with some private north. "Yew Andrade, formerly of the Royal Archive, dismissed four years ago for—what was the phrase his supervisor used—'insubordinate fascination with dynastic apocrypha.' Since then he has survived on private commissions and auction speculation. He has no family, few friends, one enemy of consequence, and one recent acquaintance of enormous interest."

She let that sit.

"The book," Petra said carefully, "was a curiosity. Old nonsense. I wanted it for the marginalia—there are recipes in those old prophecy codices, sometimes. Real ones. Useful for detecting compounds."

"That is true," Vess agreed pleasantly. "It's also not why you wanted it."

The silence stretched. Outside the window, the eastern sky went from ash to pale gold along the mountain ridge, and Petra watched it rather than meet the spymaster's eyes, because watching the light cost nothing and meeting her eyes might cost everything.

"You swallowed nightshade out of the king's stew nine days ago," Vess said. "A concentrated preparation, refined enough that whoever made it has access to a still-house or a very good apothecary. You identified it in under four seconds, spat, took the emetic yourself, walked to the infirmary under your own power, and filed your report in your own hand by midnight. Textbook. Better than textbook—you deviated from protocol in exactly one respect, which was that you told no one what you'd seen in the kitchen beforehand. No witness. No accusation. You kept it quiet." She looked up. "Why?"

"Because I didn't know who to trust."

"And now?"

"Now I know less than that."

Something moved behind Vess's face—not amusement exactly, but the acknowledgment of a good answer. She reached beneath the table and brought up a wrapped bundle, oilcloth tied with cord, and laid it between them with the care of someone setting down a live coal.

"Do you know," she said, "how long I have been spymaster of the Ember Court? Nineteen years. Do you know how many times in nineteen years a genuine threat to the king's life has crossed my desk? Three. Yours was the third. And do you know what all three have in common?"

Petra looked at the oilcloth bundle and knew—knew with the sick certainty of the taster who recognizes a smell from childhood—what was inside it.

"No," they said.

"All three," Vess said, "were foretold."

She untied the cord and folded back the cloth, and there it was: the book. Yew's book. The prophecy codex, its leather cracked, its pages swollen with a century of damp and handling. It had been taken from Yew's rooms—or bought, or borrowed, though Petra would have bet the spoon at their belt that *taken* was the honest word—and it lay open on the table to a page Petra had last seen in a candlelit archive booth with their own heart hammering like a fist on a door.

Their own description. Their own face, inked a hundred years ago. Tonight's date, written in a dead hand before either of them was born. And beneath it, in letters that seemed darker than the rest of the ink:

*the taster of thorns shall know the ember's root.*

"I'm going to tell you a story," Vess said. "I'd ask you to hear all of it before you speak, but you're Sefu's pupil, so you'll interrupt. Go ahead and get it done. What do you want to say?"

A dozen questions fought for Petra's tongue. They chose the sharpest. "Is Yew alive?"

"Yes. For now. He is being held somewhere comfortable, somewhere he can't be traced to me, and his treatment depends entirely on how our conversation goes. I want you to understand that from the outset. I am not threatening you, Petra. I am offering you the only protection that will actually work, and I am explaining the price honestly, because I've found honesty buys more cooperation than fear and costs roughly the same."

"Protection from whom?"

"From the people who put nightshade in a royal stew and didn't care that a food-taster would die catching it." Vess leaned back. "You think the attempt was on the king. Consider instead that it was on *you*. Consider that someone read these pages and decided the taster of thorns should be removed before the prophecy could find purchase. Consider that they were careless about the king because the king, to them, is furniture."

The words landed somewhere under Petra's ribs and sat there, heavy and wrong, because they had already had this thought themselves, alone, at three in the morning. Hearing it spoken aloud in Vess's calm voice made it real the way a diagnosis is real.

"Who?" Petra asked.

"If I told you a name, you would act on it. You'd confront them, or flee to them, or watch them too closely at table, and whichever you chose, they'd know within a day that you knew. Then you'd be dead, and Yew would follow, and this book would burn, and the court would go on digesting itself in the dark for another twenty years. I didn't survive nineteen years as spymaster by giving names to people who then walk out my door." She tapped the page. "Here is what I offer. Come to me. Fully. Your smuggling contacts, your courier routes, your little economy of favors—I know about all of it, by the way, and I have known for years, and the fact that you are sitting here drinking nothing and listening tells me you already suspected as much. Give me the book's contents. Work for me as you've worked for Sefu, openly, protected, with my seal behind you. In exchange, this—" she rested her hand flat on the codex "—goes into my fire tonight, and every copy Andrade made goes with it, and by week's end nobody in this kingdom remembers the phrase 'taster of thorns,' and the people hunting that phrase will have lost their quarry. You go back to being invisible. Invisible is safe. Invisible, you will live to be old."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a bell marked the hour, and the sound came up through the floor like something rising from underwater.

*Invisible is safe.* Petra thought of the smugglers' fires in the high passes, Rook's laugh carrying across rock, the taste of burnt tea shared out of one dented pot. Thought of Sefu teaching them the still pond, saying *they can't aim at what they can't see*, and meaning it kindly, and meaning, underneath, something colder. Thought of a page in another ledger, in handwriting that was theirs and was not theirs, dated years into a future they had never agreed to.

And thought of King Aldric at the harvest feast, three weeks ago. The moment when his hand had paused above the salt and his eyes had gone somewhere far away, and the flame-light of the hall had seemed for a heartbeat to bend toward him, and then he had smiled and passed the salt and everyone had laughed and gone on eating.

Everyone except a taster trained to notice what no one else notices.

"And if I refuse?" Petra asked.

Vess's expression did not change, which was answer enough.

"Then I grieve for you," she said, "sincerely. Because you are a person of real ability who has been handed a destiny by strangers a hundred years dead, and destinies in this kingdom are how bodies end up in the river. The offer is genuine. It expires when you leave this room." She slid the book across the table until it touched Petra's folded hands. "Burn it yourself, if you like. There's something in that. Closure. The author is dust, the heir is sitting in front of me, and a prophecy no living soul believes in can't hurt anyone."

Petra looked down at the open page. At the ink-dark words. At the small, careful drawing of their own face that some long-dead hand had rendered from—what? Vision? Sickness? Hope?

*Ilsa's margins,* they thought suddenly. *If the book survives, the margins survive.*

Aloud they said: "Let me hold it."

Vess considered. Whatever calculation ran behind her plain face, it concluded fast. "You may hold it while you decide. Not past that."

Petra picked up the codex. It was lighter than they remembered, or they were heavier. They turned a page. Turned another. Let their thumb slide along the outer margin of the prophecy leaf where the paper had been layered and re-layered, pressed and scraped, and felt—the faintest catch, a texture difference no one but a taster, trained to feel a grain of arsenic between fingertip and spoon, would ever have noticed.

Pasted beneath the visible page, thin as onion skin, something else waited. Words hidden under words.

They closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and met Lady Vess's eyes for the first time.

"Burn it yourself," Petra said, and pushed it back across the table. "If it matters so much to you, you burn it. I won't touch its ending."

For a long moment the spymaster studied them, and Petra understood they had just been weighed, measured, and entered into some ledger of her own.

"Very well," Vess said at last, softly, and wrapped the book in its oilcloth with unhurried hands. "Then we understand each other, taster. You've chosen. Most people take longer to realize they have."

She rose, and the audience was over, and it was only at the door, with her hand already on the latch, that she added—almost idly, the way you mention weather:

"Oh. One more thing. Master Taster Sefu has requested a leave of absence. Family matters, he says." A pause, precisely measured. "Odd. I wasn't aware Sefu had any family left. Were you?"

The door opened. The guards outside looked through Petra as though they were made of glass.

They walked back across the courtyard with the frost beginning to give way to mud, and the eastern sky fully gold now, and every nerve in their body screaming one single instruction: *the margins, the margins, get to Yew's seized rooms before Vess's fire does.*

Behind them, high in the tower, a window caught the sun and flared like a struck match.

Ahead, at the edge of the courtyard, a familiar figure stood waiting by the kitchen gate, hat in hand, face grey as old tallow.

Sefu.

He looked, Petra thought, like a man who had come to confess something, or to warn them—or possibly both—and could not yet decide which would kill him first.
