## 7. Thornfell Road at Night

Rook said the mountain would hide them, and for three days it did.

Petra had never traveled the high passes at this season. The smuggler routes were summer roads, worn into the rock by generations of feet that carried contraband and secrets, and by autumn they became something else—narrow ledges of black ice, switchbacks that dropped away into cloud, silence so complete you could hear your own blood moving. Rook moved through it like a man walking through his own kitchen, unhurried, certain, humming old toll-road songs under his breath.

"Left fork," he said, without looking back. "The right one looks easier. The right one is how the crown finds bodies in spring."

Petra took the left fork. Six years of tasting other people's poison had taught them to trust instruction over instinct. Instinct said the right path; instinct was also what made your mouth water before vomiting, a helpful little betrayal the body performed when you needed to empty your stomach fast. You learned quickly which voices to believe.

The book rode against Petra's chest in an oilcloth wrap, bound with cord, warm from their body heat. They had taken it from Yew's rooms two nights ago, along with everything Yew could copy in a single frantic evening—the marginalia, the genealogies, Ilsa Okonkwo's cramped annotations—and left behind a decoy bundle of blank vellum dressed up as the prize. Whether the trick had worked, they'd know soon enough. Lady Vess's people were thorough. Vess herself was worse than thorough. She was patient.

"You're quiet," Rook said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a person thinking. Different thing." He scrambled up a shelf of granite, offered a hand down. His grip was rope-callused and warm. "Thinking about the scholar?"

"Thinking about whether we were followed."

"We weren't."

"You said that yesterday."

"And I was right yesterday." He grinned. Rook grinned with his whole face, crow's-feet and a chipped tooth and all, a face built for taverns and campfires, a face that had been the first genuinely kind thing Petra saw on every return from the court. "Nobody follows anyone into Thornfell in the dark season. Even Vess isn't stupid enough for that."

*Someone told her about the book,* Petra thought. *Someone who knows these paths.*

They didn't say it out loud. Saying it would make it a plan instead of a fear, and fears were lighter cargo.

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a smuggler cache with dry wood already stacked and a tin box holding salt pork, dried plums, and a stub of tallow candle. Rook lit the candle even though firelight could be seen for a mile across the snowfield—he said the cold dark did things to a person's head, and he'd rather die warm than live paranoid. It was the most honest philosophy Petra had ever heard, and they hated how much they loved him for it.

"Eat," Rook said, tossing them the pork.

"I'll taste it first."

He laughed, then stopped laughing when he saw Petra's face hadn't changed. "You're not joking."

"I'm never joking about food." They turned the meat over, sniffed it, touched a crumb to the tip of the tongue and waited. Nothing. Bitterness would announce itself in ten heartbeats if it were there. They chewed, swallowed, handed it back. "Habit."

"That's a terrible way to live."

"It's the only way I know how to live." The words came out flatter than intended. Rook heard the flatness, because Rook heard everything.

"You know what my mother used to say?" He leaned back against the stone, candlelight making his face into something older and gentler. "She ran the tollhouse at Graywater, before the crown bought her loyalty and then arrested her anyway. She said: a person can survive anything except being unnecessary. That's why they keep you tasters alive, eh? Necessary. Useful. A knife they don't have to sharpen."

"And you? Are you necessary?"

"I'm beloved," Rook said. "Different currency. Better rates."

Petra slept in shifts, Rook first, and dreamed of Sefu's ledger—their own handwriting marching across pages dated years ahead, *the taster accepts the ember, the taster burns, the taster*—and woke with their heart slamming and the candle burned to a puddle and Rook watching them with an expression they couldn't read in the dark.

"Dreaming about home?" he asked.

"No," Petra said. "Home doesn't write letters to me."

* * *

The ambush came at the Narrows, where the pass pinched between two walls of stone no wider than a cart, on the morning of the fourth day.

Later, running the moment over and over the way you run a poison over the tongue, Petra could find the exact instant they should have known. A bird call—thrush, wrong season, wrong altitude—echoing off the south wall. Rook's hand closing on Petra's arm, hard, mid-sentence. The particular quality of silence ahead of them, the silence of held breath.

"Down," Rook said, and shoved them into the scree as the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the rock where Petra's head had been.

Then the Narrows erupted. Figures dropping from ledges above—crown grey under rough cloaks, six of them, eight, moving with the drilled economy of soldiers, not bounty hunters. One of them shouted a name that made Petra's stomach drop: *"Okonkwo! By order of the Ember Court!"*

Not *stop*. Not *surrender*. Their name, spoken like a verdict.

"They shouldn't know this route," Petra breathed.

"No," Rook said. His voice had changed entirely—all the tavern warmth gone, replaced by the flat calm of a man doing arithmetic with lives. "No, they shouldn't." He pulled a hatchet from his belt and pressed something else into Petra's hand: a short knife, smuggler-made, ugly and reliable. "North wall. There's a chimney crack, thirty paces past the bend, climbs to the shepherd ledge. Don't stop, don't look back, don't wait for me."

"Rook—"

"Don't wait for me." He smiled, and it was the tavern grin, the whole-face grin, deployed now like a weapon. "I'm necessary, remember? Somebody has to be loud."

He stepped out into the open Narrows and was loud.

What Rook did in the next ninety seconds, Petra saw mostly in fragments—glances torn backward against every instinct screaming *don't look back, don't look back*. He went at the eight men like weather, like a landslide, hatchet flashing, voice bellowing smuggler curses that echoed off both walls until the mountains themselves seemed to be swearing at the crown. He kicked a boulder loose that caught two loyalists below the knee. He fought the way he lived: generously, hugely, taking up all the space in the world so there was none left for anything small or cruel.

Petra ran. Thirty paces. The chimney crack was there, just as promised, a seam of shadow in the black stone. They wedged themselves into it and climbed, boots finding holds by faith, fingers bleeding, the book pressing its wrapped weight against their sternum like a second heartbeat.

Below, the sounds of fighting stuttered. Changed.

A bolt-thunk. A grunt that Petra would hear for the rest of their life, whatever length that turned out to be—a grunt of surprise more than pain, the sound a man makes when the world does something he genuinely didn't expect.

"Don't look back," Petra whispered to themselves, halfway up the crack, and looked back.

Rook was on one knee in the snow, hatchet still in his fist, one hand pressed to his side where the grey of the cloak was going darker. Three loyalists were down around him and the rest had backed off, wary, and he was grinning up at the ledge where Petra clung—grinning with his whole ruined face, chipped tooth and all—and he shouted, in a voice that carried clear as a chapel bell across the frozen Narrows:

"Beloved! Tell them I was beloved!"

And then the mountain took him, or the blood loss took him, or the fifth bolt took him, and Petra could not see which from that height and would spend many nights deciding it mattered before understanding that it didn't.

They climbed. Their hands tore. They climbed.

* * *

The shepherd ledge ran east along the shoulder of the mountain, abandoned since the plague years, a ribbon of broken wall and frozen grass. Petra walked it until the light failed, walked it after the light failed, because stopping meant hearing the grunt again, and hearing it again meant standing still forever.

When exhaustion finally took them, they sheltered in a collapsed bothy with a roof half-intact, and built no fire, and sat in the dark with the book unwrapped on their knees, because it seemed suddenly obscene to treat it as cargo. A man had died for this object. A man had died loudly, generously, so that a book could continue north in the arms of a person whose name it happened to contain.

"The taster of thorns," Petra read aloud, just to hear a voice that wasn't the echo of a shout. Their own voice sounded wrong in the dark. Thin. "Ilsa, whoever you were—you'd better be worth him."

There was no answer. Mountains don't answer. But the wind came down off the glacier with a sound almost like breath, almost like speech, and Petra sat with the pages until their tears froze on their cheeks—not weeping exactly, nothing so dramatic, just leaking, the way grief leaks out of people who were trained never to spill anything.

They thought about Rook's mother. *A person can survive anything except being unnecessary.* Rook had chosen to be necessary in the loudest possible way. And Petra realized, sitting in the ruins of someone's shepherd life, that they had spent six years being necessary in the smallest possible way—swallowing, staying silent, surviving—and that the prophecy book wasn't asking them to become important. It was asking them to become *beloved*. To belong somewhere so completely that belonging cost something. Rook had paid the toll. The road had a price. Every smuggler knew it. Every road has a toll, Rook's mother had collected them, and Rook had paid Petra's without hesitation, without invoice, without—

Without ever knowing why the book mattered. He'd died for a stranger's inheritance.

"He knew," Petra said to the dark. "I told him at Graywater. I told him what the book said." And Rook had laughed and called them *cousin*—smugglers' word for anyone you'd bleed for—and had volunteered for the escort before Petra finished asking.

Dawn came grey and merciless. Petra rewrapped the book, tied the cord tight, and stood.

That was when they found the marker.

It was tucked into the bothy's broken doorframe, low, deliberate—a strip of grey cloth knotted in a pattern Petra recognized from a hundred courier runs. Smuggler sign. But not any sign Petra had ever been taught to leave. This knot belonged to the old toll-road code, the one Rook's generation used, the one Sefu had once mentioned existed before the courts cracked down: a warning knot, a *danger ahead, road compromised* mark.

Fresh cloth. Fresh knot. Days old at most.

Which meant someone had come through here ahead of them. Someone who knew the old code, knew the passes, knew exactly which way a fleeing taster would run—and had wanted them to know it too. Not an ambush this time. An invitation. Or a courtesy between professionals, the kind of message you leave when you want the traveler to turn around while turning around is still possible.

Petra stood a long time in the doorway, looking north along the ledge toward the high country, then south toward the court and everything burning behind them.

The knot's meaning was unambiguous: *road watched.* Someone ahead knew they were coming. Someone with smuggler hands and crown knowledge, someone who had learned the passes the same way Petra had—from the inside, from the family, from the people who raised them.

Only one person had taught Petra the old knots.

Petra untied the marker, folded it into their pocket beside the knife Rook had given them, and turned north anyway, toward the hermitage the margins described, walking faster now, because the grief had hardened into something with edges.

If Sefu was leaving messages on the mountain, then Sefu was already ahead of them.

Or Sefu's masters were—and Petra intended to arrive first, and ask questions the old ledger had never scripted.
