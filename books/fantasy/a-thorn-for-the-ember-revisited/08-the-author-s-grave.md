## 8. The Author's Grave

The margins of the prophecy book were killing me.

Three days out from Thornfell, holed up in a smuggler waystation that smelled of tallow and old smoke, I had read the same page forty times and the same marginal note a hundred more. Yew Andrade's careful transcription sat beside it on the table, his handwriting neat as an accounting ledger, mine scrawled across every blank inch of paper he'd let me have.

*Follow the water that forgets its name,* the margin read, in a spidery brown ink that might once have been black. *She kept the last light where the mountain keeps its dead.*

"Whose handwriting is that?" I asked for the fifth time.

Yew didn't look up from his notes. He was a thin man made thinner by travel, all elbows and ink stains, with the particular patience of someone who had spent thirty years being ignored by people who funded him. "I told you. Unknown. The hand changes partway through the book—earlier annotations are in the author's own script, later ones in a different ink, different slant."

"So someone else found the book after she wrote it."

"Yes." He finally glanced up. "And followed it. Or tried to."

"Tried to?"

He tapped the margin. "*Tried.* There are corrections here. Crossed-out routes. One annotation says *the ford is drowned now, go by the quarry* in a hand that isn't hers. Someone spent years retracing her steps, Petra. Updating the map as the world changed under it."

I stared at the page until the letters swam. A century ago, a woman named Ilsa Okonkwo had written a book of fire and thorns, and somewhere between then and now, another pilgrim had walked this same trail of breadcrumbs and left notes for whoever came next. Notes that stopped.

"Why did they stop?"

Yew closed his notebook. "That," he said, "is the question I've been avoiding since we left Thornfell."

* * *

We went by the quarry.

The water that forgot its name turned out to be the Serrel, a river that ran bright and cold down from the high passes and then vanished into limestone country two days north of Emberhold, swallowed whole by a cave mouth the locals called the Throat. The quarry was older than the cave's fame—a scar of pale stone cut into the hillside, abandoned so long that birch trees grew from its terraces like green candles on a cake.

An old quarryman with one arm and no curiosity pointed us up a goat track when I described what we were looking for. "Hermitage?" he said, chewing something that might have been tobacco or might have been bark. "Up past the cairns. Nobody goes. Nothing there but stones and a roof nobody owns."

"Who built it?"

"Whoever was tired of people." He spat. "Same as always."

The goat track climbed through scrub pine for most of a day. Yew lasted longer than I expected him to; scholars, it turns out, can be stubborn in ways that have nothing to do with muscle. By late afternoon we crested a ridge and saw it: a low building of dry-stacked fieldstone huddled against the mountain's shoulder, roof half-collapsed, chimney standing like a raised finger. Around it, maybe forty paces out, stood a ring of standing stones—waist-high, lichen-eaten, unremarkable unless you'd spent six years learning to notice things that were arranged rather than grown.

The stones were a circle. The circle was deliberate. And the hermitage sat at its center like a yolk in an egg.

"It's a ward," I said.

Yew squinted. "A what?"

"A warding pattern. Sefu taught me to read them—taster's training. You learn the geometry of protection because poisoners love symbols." I walked the ring slowly, counting. Nine stones, each carved with a mark worn nearly smooth. "This is old craft. Older than the Court's guild licenses. Someone wanted whoever lived here to be left alone, and wanted anything else—" I paused at the northernmost stone, where the carving was deepest, "—to stay out."

"And did you feel anything when you crossed the line?"

I had, actually. A pressure against the breastbone, faint as a memory of drowning, gone as soon as I registered it. "Maybe. Or maybe I'm tired and imagining things."

"You're not the type to imagine things."

"I'm exactly the type. It's how I've stayed alive." But I stepped inside the ring again, and again felt that soft push, and this time I noticed something else: the push came *from inside*, pushing out. Not keeping me away. Keeping something in.

Or keeping something safe.

The hermitage door had rotted off its hinges long ago. Inside: one room, a hearth choked with bird nests, a table leg jutting from rubble like a broken bone. Dust lay over everything in a gray velvet so thick our footprints looked sacrilegious. Along one wall, half-buried, ran a shelf of stone niches—the kind monks used for manuscripts—and every niche was empty except one, far right, where a clay jar sat upright behind a slab of slate.

I knew before I touched it. The way you know a cup is poisoned before you taste it. Some knowledge doesn't come through the mind; it comes up through the blood like heat.

"Petra," Yew said quietly. "Your hands."

I looked down. My fingers were glowing.

Not metaphorically. Not a trick of the light through the broken roof. A fine tracery of gold light ran beneath the skin of my knuckles and wrists, following the veins, the same pattern I'd woken with twice since reading the book's first pages—veins of ember-light that faded if I held still and flared when I came near... near what? Near the book. Near truth. Near my own name spoken aloud in a dead woman's voice.

"It's been doing that since Thornfell," I said. "You never mentioned it."

"I wasn't sure you'd want to know." Yew's voice was gentle and miserable. "Scholars learn early that knowing things is a burden you can put down or carry, but never give back."

I lifted the jar. It was light. Sealed with wax gone amber with age, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at the fold. Inside: a letter, folded small, and beneath it a ring of dull iron set with a stone the color of banked coals.

The wax seal bore a thorn and a flame.

My hands shook. Six years of steady hands—hands that hadn't trembled holding cups meant for kings, hands that hadn't trembled burying Rook in frozen ground two weeks ago—and they shook now, over a dead woman's letter.

"Read it," Yew whispered. "Or would you rather—"

"I'd rather be anyone else." I broke the seal.

*To the child who finds this,*

*If you are reading, then the book found you as I hoped, and the light in your veins has already begun to wake. Do not be afraid of it. It is only your inheritance saying hello.*

*I will not waste your time with apology, though I owe you one. I wrote the book because I saw what was coming—the binding of the crown, the slow theft of a king—and because I could not stop it in my lifetime. So I did what the powerless do. I planted a garden.*

*You are the harvest.*

*The immunity in your blood is not accident. It is breeding, in the oldest sense—careful marriages, careful children, a gift tended like an orchard across five generations. My daughter married a man from the salt coasts whose line carried a resistance to toxins of the nightshade family. Her daughter married into the ash-farmers of the eastern hills, whose bodies learned to drink smoke without harm. Each generation added a strand. Each strand was tested—I am not proud of some of the tests—and each child who carried the full gift was taught one thing above all others: to be invisible. To taste, to serve, to watch, and never to be seen.*

*I am sorry for that too. Invisibility was armor once. Now it is a cage, and you were born in it, and I built the bars.*

*Here is what you must know. The ember-spirit that wears your king is not evil. It was our guardian once—it kept the mountain's fire from waking and burning us all—but fear feeds it, and powerful men learned to farm its fear. It does not want a kingdom. It wants a body that will not break. Aldric's is breaking. When it fails, the spirit will reach for the nearest vessel that can hold fire without dying.*

*That vessel is you. That has always been you. I am sorry. I am so deeply sorry.*

*But hear the second truth, the one the spirit will not tell you: a vessel that holds fire willingly cannot be owned by it. Will is the lock it cannot pick. Every bound thing in history was taken by surprise or by despair. Refuse both, and the fire must negotiate.*

*The rite is in the book. You already know which pages. Your veins will show you the rest.*

*The ring in this letter belonged to my mother, and hers before her. Wear it when the time comes, or don't. Blood remembers better than iron. But I leave it because my mother left it to me with these words: "Someone must sit at the fire's heart, or everyone lives at its edge."*

*Whatever you choose—flee, fight, swallow the sun—you were loved into being, across a hundred years, by a woman you never met who is loving you still.*

*—Ilsa Okonkwo*

*P.S. If a man named Sefu ever raises you: he was my student's student, and he knows more than he says, and he is kinder than he is wise. Forgive him the gap.*

The letter dropped from my fingers.

I don't remember sitting down. I was on the floor of a dead woman's house with a century-old paper in my lap and gold light crawling up my arms like sunrise under skin, and I was crying—not the clean grief of Rook's funeral, which had at least been simple, but something older and less dignified. Grief and rage and gratitude tangled together so tightly I couldn't tell them apart.

"She bred me." My voice came out wrong. Flat and cracked at once. "Five generations. Marriages chosen like—like planting rows. Children *tested*. She says she's not proud of the tests, Yew. What does that mean? What did they *do* to my great-grandmothers?"

"I don't know." Yew crouched at a careful distance, the way you approach a wounded animal or a lit fuse. "Petra—"

"My whole life. Every choice. The tasting apprenticeship, the courier work, the way Sefu trained me to move unseen—was any of it mine? Or was I a crop coming up on schedule?"

"Do you want the true answer or the kind one?"

I laughed, wet and ugly. "You've met me."

"The true answer." He sat down fully on the dusty floor, joints cracking, and looked at me with those tired scholar's eyes. "It was both. It is always both. My father was a printer and his father before him, and I was taught letters before I could walk, and I chose none of it, and yet the books I love are *mine*, Petra. No one else loves them the way I do. Inheritance sets the field. You still plant the row."

"That's very tidy for a man hiding in a ruin."

"I'm a scholar. Tidiness is all I have." He hesitated. "There's something else. In the letter. Did you read the postscript?"

"Sefu." I said the name like spitting. "His master's master was her student. He *knew*. All those years raising me, training me—he knew what I was for. Was he her agent? Was I his assignment?"

"Or was he protecting you?" Yew picked up the letter, read the postscript again, and handed it back gently. "Kinder than he is wise, she says. Forgive him the gap. Petra—if Vess and the loyalists want the prophecy buried, and Sefu raised the one person the prophecy names, hidden and skilled and alive... maybe he wasn't scripting your fate. Maybe he was guarding it. Badly. From inside."

I wanted to throw the letter at him. I wanted to burn the hermitage down. I wanted, more than either, to go home—to the kitchen smells and the ledger lines and the small warm cell where I was nobody—and find that it still existed, that I could still fit inside it.

But the light in my veins pulsed when I thought of going back, and dimmed when I thought of hiding, and I understood suddenly that invisibility was over regardless of what I chose. You cannot unknow a name. You cannot unread a letter. Somewhere behind me, Lady Vess was tearing apart the smuggler roads looking for a taster who had stopped being furniture, and ahead of me was a king wearing fire, and a spirit that wanted a throat like mine, and a rite I hadn't read yet that would ask everything.

I picked up the iron ring from the bottom of the jar. The coal-dark stone was warm—warm in a cold room, warm as a held hand. I slid it onto my thumb, where it fit as though measured.

"Yew."

"Yes."

"When we get back—when we go to the court, to the vaults, wherever this ends—" I looked at the ring of stones outside, at the ward that had been waiting a hundred years for someone with the right blood to walk through it. "If Sefu is what she says he was, I'll listen. That's all I can promise. Listening."

"It's more than most inheritances get." He gathered his notes with shaking hands. "Come on, then. We should reach the high road by dark."

"One minute." I stood alone in the center of the ruined room, in the center of the standing stones, in the center of a dead woman's hundred-year plan, and I spoke aloud to the dust and the light in my arms. "I heard you. I'm not grateful yet. But I heard you."

The ember-veins flared once, bright and brief, like something answering—or like something settling in to wait.

Outside, the sun was dropping behind the ridge when Yew caught my sleeve and pointed east, down the valley toward the distant smudge of Emberhold.

Smoke was rising from the city. Not cookfire smoke. Too much, too black, too many columns.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable to a taster's nose even at this distance, came the smell of burning paper.

They weren't just hunting the book anymore.

They were burning every copy in the capital—and every reader they could find.
