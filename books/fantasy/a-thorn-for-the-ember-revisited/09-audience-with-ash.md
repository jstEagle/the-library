## 9. Audience with Ash

They dressed me for the audience the way you'd dress a corpse.

Two chamberlains in ash-grey livery worked over me with combs and brushes and a borrowed coat of deep blue wool, and neither of them spoke above a whisper, as if sound might carry through the palace walls into whatever room the king kept his fire. They powdered my collar. They straightened my spine with a hand between my shoulder blades, gentle as adjusting a picture frame. One of them—young, freckled, hands trembling—tucked a sprig of dried rosemary into my lapel and I nearly laughed, because rosemary is what kitchen girls hang over doorways against bad spirits, and here it was, pinned to my chest like a medal.

"For luck," he murmured.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, because I had swallowed enough bad luck for one lifetime and could use all the countermeasures available.

Six years in the Ember Court and I had never stood in the Presence Hall. Tasters don't. We are furniture that eats; we are brought food and we bring back verdicts, and the king exists for us as a silhouette behind gauze, a voice through doors, a signature at the bottom of decrees that always looked, I realized now, like it had been written with a shaking hand held too close to a candle. Scorched-edged letters. Sefu had told me once it was simply how Aldric signed—that the heat of his hand left marks on the page—and I had believed him, because believing Sefu was easier than thinking.

I was not so eager to believe anyone anymore.

The summons had come at dawn: not written, but spoken, by a herald whose voice cracked on the word *king*, as though even the court's machinery found the phrase strange now. *His Majesty desires the presence of the royal taster.* No reason given. No reason needed. When the ember asks, you burn your way there.

Lady Vess met me outside the great doors.

She wore grey today, unadorned, her silver hair bound tight, and she looked less like a spymaster than a widow at a funeral she had personally arranged. She studied my face for a long moment—the powder, the rosemary, whatever fear I hadn't managed to swallow—and something almost soft moved behind her eyes before the shutters came down again.

"You will speak when addressed," she said. "You will not look directly into his eyes for longer than a breath. And Petra—" she paused, choosing the words like someone selecting which poison goes in which cup, "—whatever you see in that room, you saw nothing. Do you understand? Whatever he says, you heard wind."

"And if he says something worth hearing?"

Vess's mouth thinned. "Then you'll wish you were deaf." She pushed the doors open herself, which surprised me—I'd expected ceremony, ushers, the slow theatre of approach. Instead she gave me a small shove between the shoulder blades, proprietary, like loading cargo onto a ship. "Go on, taster. The fire's waiting."

* * *

The Presence Hall was cold.

That was the first wrongness, and it took me three steps to name it. Every story about King Aldric involved heat—his rooms overheated, his baths scalding, his food served still spitting from the coals. The whole architecture of the court bent around his warmth. But the hall was cold as a cellar, the long gallery of pillars breathing chill, the banners hanging limp and frost-stiff along the walls, and at the far end, alone on a dais beneath an iron crown suspended from chains (he never wore it, I remembered suddenly; the crown hung above him like a threat), sat the King of Emberfall on a plain wooden chair, wrapped in a fur cloak, shivering.

He looked smaller than a king should. Grey-faced, hollow-cheeked, with the translucent skin of a man who has spent twenty years being slowly cooked from within and never once allowed to complain. His hands were folded in his lap like a dead man's arranged by mourners. And around him—not touching him, but close, closer than smoke, closer than a lover—the air shimmered.

Heat-haze. In a freezing room.

I stopped at the appointed mark on the floor and bowed, the deep ceremonial bow they'd drilled into me my first week, and I watched the haze shift toward me like something turning its head.

"Taster." His voice was rough gravel. Not the resonant king's voice from proclamations read aloud in the squares—something worn down, sanded, human. "Petra Okonkwo. You tasted my supper last night."

"I did, Your Majesty."

"The stew. Venison and root vegetables. Tell me—" and here he leaned forward, and his eyes were extraordinary, dark and quick and desperate, the eyes of a man drowning who has just seen a boat, "tell me what you found in it."

"Nightshade, Your Majesty. Enough to stop a horse. I reported it and the kitchens were searched."

"Yes. Yes, they were." He was nodding, nodding, and then his gaze slid sideways—to the empty gallery, to the frost-stiffed banners, to nothing—and changed. It happened between one blink and the next. The desperation drained out of them and left something else, something vast and patient and old, looking out through the king's face at me with an interest that made my skin try to crawl off my bones.

When he spoke again, the voice had changed too. Warmer. Richer. The voice of the proclamations.

"You are the one who tastes," it said, musing, tasting the words themselves. "You stand between the king and death, little cup. How many poisons have you carried for him?"

"Enough, Your Majesty."

"Do you know what I carry?" The king's lips smiled. His eyes did not. "I have carried this kingdom for twenty years. Through plague. Through famine winter. Through the treason of roots and the rot of old blood. I hold the fire so the kingdom need not freeze." The smile widened a fraction. "And yet the servants whisper. The tasters flinch. Someone put nightshade in my stew, and my own body—" the voice snagged, caught, and for half a heartbeat the dark desperate eyes surfaced again, wide with horror, "*—my own hands, I can't always feel my own—*" and then the warmth closed over it like water over a dropped stone, smooth again, pleasant again. "Forgive me. Where was I. Ah. You are dismissed, taster. Thank you for your service."

My feet didn't move.

It wasn't courage. It was the thing I'd learned over six years of swallowing death in small doses: sometimes the body refuses to obey the mind, and if you're lucky, the refusal points the right direction. I stood rooted on the cold flagstones while the heat-haze pulsed around the king like a second heartbeat, and I thought: *it knows*. Whatever looked out of those eyes knew exactly why I was here, or at least suspected, and had just performed dismissal the way a spider performs a web.

"Your Majesty," I heard myself say. "May I ask one question?"

Silence. The frost on the banners seemed to deepen.

"You may not," said the warm voice, pleasantly.

But underneath it—underneath, faint as a voice through a wall, through water, through twenty years of ash—a different sound. The king's real voice, scraped thin, fighting up through his own throat like a swimmer through ice:

"Ask... it..."

The shimmer convulsed. The king's hand shot out and gripped the arm of the chair hard enough that the wood creaked, and his face did something terrible—it moved in two directions at once, the mouth trying to smile while the muscles around the eyes fought it, jaw working like a man chewing glass, and the temperature of the room swung wildly, cold, hot, cold, the frost on the banners hissing into steam and re-forming, steam and frost, steam and frost—

"*Unbind me,*" whispered King Aldric.

Not loudly. Barely a breath. But the hall was silent as a tomb, and I had spent six years training my ears to catch the small sounds—the click of a crushed seed under a thumbnail, the sigh of a vial unstoppered—and I heard it. Every syllable.

Then the flame reasserted itself.

I felt it happen the way you feel a door slam in another room. The king's spine straightened. His face smoothed. The warmth poured back into his expression like molten gold poured into a mold, and when he looked at me again the eyes were kind and terrible and entirely not his.

"You asked your question," said the ember-spirit, wearing the king's voice like a stolen coat. "You have your answer. Go home, little cup. Drink deeply of your life." A pause, delicate as a knife tip. "And give my regards to the scholar."

The floor did not drop out this time. I had already fallen; there was no further down. I bowed—deeply, correctly, my heart slamming against my ribs like something caged—and walked backward out of the Presence Hall without turning, because turning your back on the king is death, and I was not ready to die in a cold room for a courtesy.

The doors closed behind me with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

Vess was waiting in the antechamber, exactly where I'd left her, hands folded, expression carved from the same frost as the banners.

"Well?" she said.

"You heard wind," I said.

Something flickered across her face—satisfaction, or relief, or disappointment; with Vess I was learning that all three wore the same mask. "Good. Then you may return to your duties. There will be a tasting tomorrow evening. The harvest feast. The king will preside."

"The king will preside," I repeated.

"He always presides." She turned away, then paused, her back to me, her voice dropping to something almost gentle. "You saw nothing, Petra. Remember that. Some cages are built for the thing inside them. Some are built for everything else." She glanced back over her shoulder, and for one naked instant I saw fear in Lady Vess's face—real fear, the kind that doesn't perform itself. "You cannot imagine what it costs to keep that fire fed."

"No," I said quietly. "I imagine you can't either."

Her jaw tightened. She swept out through the far door, and her footsteps faded down the stone corridor, quick and precise, a woman with many fires of her own to tend.

I stood alone in the antechamber with the rosemary sprig in my lapel and the taste of that room still coating my throat—cold air, hot menace, and under it all, fainter than memory, a dying man's whisper.

*Unbind me.*

Two words. Three syllables of borrowed breath, paid for, I understood now, at a price I couldn't calculate—because the spirit had heard him say it. Had let him say it. Or perhaps hadn't noticed, the way a giant doesn't notice an ant crossing its boot. Either way, the words existed now, spoken aloud in the world, witnessed. And I had learned enough in the past few days to know that in the grammar of prophecy, witness matters.

I thought of Yew Andrade's cluttered study, the prophecy book open between us, the margins crowded with a dead woman's handwriting. I thought of the ledger in Sefu's desk, entries in my handwriting that I'd never written, dated years ahead. I thought of Rook laughing on the Thornfell road, and of the nightshade in the venison stew, and of a kingdom that had been paying, for twenty years, in the only coin it had: one man, burning slowly, so everyone else could stay warm.

The freckled chamberlain appeared at my elbow to collect the borrowed coat. As he lifted it from my shoulders, careful as handling relics, he nodded at the rosemary.

"Keep it," he whispered. "Luck's no use to a dead plant."

I kept it.

And that evening, in my narrow room above the tasters' quarters, I ground the rosemary to dust in a mortar, mixed it with beeswax and a thread of my own hair—the oldest courier trick Rook ever taught me, a seal that breaks if anyone opens what you've sealed—and pressed it into the seam of a letter addressed to one Yew Andrade, care of the archive auction house, marked with the smuggler's sign for *urgent, by hand, trust no other road*.

Three sentences. That's all I could risk.

*I heard the wind. The wind asked to be freed. Find me the rite.*

I sealed it, and I sat holding it in the dark, feeling the ember-heat of the Presence Hall still living somewhere under my sternum like a coal I'd swallowed, and I understood—with the flat, calm clarity that comes after horror has finished screaming and settled in to stay—that I had just done the most dangerous thing a taster can do.

I had decided what the court would swallow next.

And somewhere below me, in the palace's deep places, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and the candle flames in my room all leaned east, toward the mountain, as if listening.
