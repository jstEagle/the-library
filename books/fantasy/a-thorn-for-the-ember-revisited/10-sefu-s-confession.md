## 10. Sefu's Confession

The Master Taster's quarters smelled the way they always had: beeswax and dried sage, tallow candles burned down to brass collars, and beneath it all the faint mineral tang of the antidote cabinet that Sefu kept locked even from himself. Petra had grown up breathing that smell. It was the smell of safety, once. Of being a foundling with no name worth writing down, handed a spoon and a purpose by a broad-shouldered man who taught them that a taster's first loyalty was to their own tongue.

Now the smell just smelled like a locked room.

Petra stood in the doorway with Sefu's ledger under their arm—the real one, pulled from the false bottom of his desk while he'd been at evening prayers—and watched him turn from the window. He'd heard them come in; of course he had. Six years of training in silence, and he still heard them like they were children running on gravel.

"You went through my desk." His voice was level. His hands were not.

"I went through your *lie*," Petra said. "The desk was just where you kept it."

He crossed the room slowly, the way he moved when a tasting had gone wrong and he was buying time before the verdict. He held out one palm. An old gesture. *Give it here, child.* Petra had surrendered a hundred dangerous things into that hand—foxglove leaves, a vial of cantharides, once a knife a courtier had slipped into their boot as a test.

They did not give him the ledger.

"Sit down, Sefu."

"I think I'd rather stand."

"Then stand." Petra set the book on the table between them anyway, open to the page they'd read eleven times since noon. The handwriting looped and slanted in a way no forger could copy, because it wasn't forgery at all. It was *theirs*. Their own hand, aged two decades past their wrist, recording tastings they had never performed, dated in years that hadn't happened yet. And beside each entry, in the same impossible script, a single recurring notation:

*Subject shows immunity. Line confirmed.*

"Explain this to me," Petra said. "Slowly. Like I'm new. Like I'm eight years old again and you're teaching me why we spit, never swallow, until the king's plate is cleared."

Sefu lowered himself onto the bench across the table. Up close, in candlelight, she saw what six years of distance had let her miss: he had gotten old. The gray in his beard had won. There were new lines around his eyes, carved deep, the kind grief makes when it visits often.

"You found the book," he said. Not a question. "Andrade's auction. The Okonkwo prophecy."

"The whole court will know soon enough. Vess already does."

Something flickered across his face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. But Petra had spent six years reading micro-expressions for arsenic. They knew a flinch when they saw one.

"You knew," they said quietly. "Before tonight. Before the auction. You've known about the prophecy since—" They looked down at the ledger. At the dates. "Since before I was old enough to be in it."

Sefu folded his hands on the table. A man settling in for a long confession, or a short execution.

"Your mother came to me," he said. "Nineteen years ago. Before you were born."

Petra's breath stopped. In six years, Sefu had never spoken of her. He'd said only that a dying woman had left a child at the Taster's Gate, along with a purse of coin and a note: *Her blood is the point. Keep her small.*

"She was frightened," Sefu went on. "Not of the prophecy—of what would happen if anyone else read it. She told me her grandmother had written a book, and that the book named a taster of thorns, and that every woman in her line had been raised half-blind on purpose so that no one—not the crown, not the temples, not the spirit itself—could see the thread leading back to Ilsa Okonkwo. She asked me to do the same for you. To keep you invisible. To teach you a trade that would make you useful and unremarkable in the same breath." His mouth twisted. "Tasting was her suggestion. She had a sense of humor, your mother. Poison-proof blood, and she picks the profession that needs it most."

"And the ledger?" Petra tapped the page. The ink sat there, patient and obscene. "My mother didn't write this. This is *my* hand."

"No," Sefu said. "That's mine. Learned from yours."

* * *

He told it plainly, which was the worst part. No performance. No pleading. Just a man laying out his own crimes on a table like instruments before a dissection.

Three years ago, Lady Vess had summoned him to the spymaster's tower. She had been assembling her files on the Okonkwo line for years—Sefu suspected longer than Aldric's binding, though he couldn't prove it—and she had learned that a descendant lived in the Ember Court under Sefu's roof. She didn't want the girl dead. Dead heirs made martyrs, and martyrs made movements. She wanted something better.

She wanted the heir discredited before anyone knew an heir existed.

"She ordered me to practice your handwriting," Sefu said. "Years of it. Your schoolwork, your recipe notes, the marginalia in your training manuals. I have boxes of it, Petra. I copied you the way I'd copy a poisoner's tell. And then I wrote the ledger. Entries dated forward, entries showing 'subject' after 'subject' tested and recorded—entries that would make any future claimant look like a fraud staging evidence. If you ever stood before the court and said *I am the taster of thorns*, Vess would produce this book and say: here is the proof she has been scripting her own destiny for years. Here is the forgery that unmakes her."

"But it's not forged," Petra said. "It's *real*. Real ink, real paper, real dates that haven't come yet. Anyone who tests it—"

"—will find exactly what you found. That's the elegance of it. She doesn't need the ledger to lie. She needs it to be *true in a way that reads as madness*." Sefu's voice dropped. "A book that predicts your future, written in your hand. What court would believe you weren't its author? What court wouldn't burn you for witchcraft instead?"

Petra pushed back from the table. The bench legs shrieked against stone, loud in the small room, and neither of them flinched—they were both too trained for that—but the sound felt good anyway. Honest. Loud.

"Say the rest," they demanded. "You're telling it too cleanly. There's more. I can hear the shape of the sentence you keep swallowing."

Sefu closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he let them be. Another thing six years had missed: the man had never once hidden tears from Petra as a child. He'd only started hiding things later, and apparently everything else had followed.

"The nightshade," he said. "In the king's stew."

"You put it there."

"I *carried* it there. The order came through Vess's channel—the same dead-drop I've used since the ledger work began. A measured dose, timed to a service when you were scheduled to taste. Enough to sicken, not kill. Enough to make the whole court watch the royal taster fail at the one thing she exists to do." He spread his hands. "You were getting close to the prophecy, Petra. Yew Andrade had sent you inquiries. Vess wanted you shaken. Humiliated. Reminded of how fast a taster falls when the court loses faith in her tongue."

"And if I'd missed it?" Petra's voice came out low and strange, almost calm. "If my immunity had failed, or my attention had—if I'd swallowed instead of spat, like they train us to do in the final trial—"

"You wouldn't have died. That's the one mercy in it. The dose was calibrated for someone without your blood." He paused. "But the king would have eaten what you left in the bowl."

The room went very quiet.

"So either way," Petra said, "someone bleeds. If I catch it, I'm disgraced. If I don't, the king is poisoned and I'm executed for it. Vess built a door with no clean side."

"Yes."

"And you walked me through it holding the handle."

"Yes." Sefu didn't look away. That, at least, she owed him credit for—he took it full in the face, every word, the way he'd taught her to take a verdict. "I told myself I was protecting you. That staying inside Vess's machine was the only way to know what it was building. That a father who betrays from inside the walls serves his daughter better than a father dragged out of them. I have told myself that every night for three years." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Tonight you stood in that doorway with the ledger in your hands and I realized I have been telling myself a story. Same as any poisoner does. Same as anyone who feeds a thing slowly enough to call it care."

* * *

Petra turned away from him. Went to the window he'd been standing at when they entered. Below, the Ember Court glittered its usual gold, torchlight on wet flagstones, guards changing shift with the bored precision of men who had no idea their king was a cage.

"I practiced spitting foxglove for a month," Petra said to the glass. "When I was nine. Because you told me the story of your first failure—the taster before you, the one who died at Prince Emory's wedding feast. You said his mistake was pride. He trusted his experience over his tongue. You made me swear I'd never trust anything over the evidence in front of me." They turned around. "So here's my evidence, Sefu. You raised me. You fed me. You taught me everything I know about survival, including how to survive *you*, apparently, though I didn't know that was the lesson. And for three years you were Vess's pen. Her instrument. You wrote my fate in my own hand and then you handed me poisoned stew and called it a test."

"I did."

"Do you love me?"

The question landed like a dropped cup. Sefu's breath caught audibly.

"Every day," he said. "Since the night your mother put you in my arms and you grabbed my finger with a grip like you were arresting me. Every day since. That was never the lie."

"That's what makes it unforgivable," Petra said. "If you didn't love me, I could hate you cleanly. I'm very good at hating, Sefu. You taught me that too—taught me to hate the poisoner, whoever they are, without hesitation, because hesitation kills. But you're not the poisoner. You're the man who carried the poisoner's cup because he thought the walking was safer than the fighting. And I—" Their voice cracked, and they hated it, and kept going anyway. "I don't know what to do with that. I can't spit you out. You're not a toxin. You're *dinner*. You're twenty years of dinners."

Sefu wept then, quietly, without wiping his face, a big old man crying like the foundlings used to cry in the dormitory, and Petra stood in the middle of the room and let him, because they could not cross the space between them. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Forgiveness was a rite too, and rites required ingredients, and theirs were not yet gathered.

"What happens now?" he asked finally.

"Now I ask you one question, and you answer it true, and then I decide whether you're a witness or an accomplice when this goes before the king."

"Ask."

"Vess's orders. The nightshade, the ledger—all of it. Was it hers alone?" Petra stepped closer. "Or does it go up? To whoever bound Aldric in the first place. To whoever's been feeding that thing for twenty years. Is there a *hand above her hand*?"

Sefu was silent a long moment. Outside, the bells rang the third hour, and somewhere below a kitchen boy dropped a stack of tin plates and swore.

"There's a vault," he said at last. "Beneath the mountain. Vess goes down twice a year—I've seen the escort rosters, I've seen what comes back up with her. Ash on her sleeves, Petra. Fresh ash, in a sealed tower." His eyes met theirs, and in them Petra saw the last thing they'd expected to find tonight: fear. Raw, undiluted, the fear of a man who had finally looked at what he'd been serving. "Whatever bound the king is still down there. Still awake. And I don't think Vess is its master."

He leaned forward, dropping his voice to almost nothing.

"I think she's its *cupbearer*."

Petra felt the words land in their chest like a second heartbeat. The ember beneath the mountain. The spirit wearing the crown. And between them and the truth, a woman with ash on her sleeves who had spent three years making sure the one person who could unbind it all would die discredited and small.

"Sefu," they said. "Tomorrow at dawn, I have an audience request to deliver to the king's steward. My name is going to be in front of Aldric within the week—one way or another."

"Petra—"

"If Vess moves first, I'll be in Thornfell by midday and you'll be answering her questions alone. So decide now. Decide *tonight*." They picked up the ledger and held it out to him at last, handle of the cup returned to its carrier. "When the steward comes asking, are you going to tell the court what you told me? Are you going to testify that the ledger is Vess's forgery, written by your hand, on her orders?"

Sefu took the book. Held it against his chest like a wound he'd finally agreed to show the surgeon.

"I'll testify," he said. "Gods forgive me, yes. I'll testify."

"Good." Petra turned for the door, and paused, and did not look back, because looking back would undo them both. "Eat nothing tomorrow, old man. Nothing you haven't tasted yourself. Trust me on that much."

"Where will you be?"

"At the steward's door," Petra said. "Asking for an audience with the king."

Behind them, in the quiet room that smelled of beeswax and sage and twenty years of careful lies, Sefu whispered something that might have been a blessing or a warning, and Petra carried both of them out into the dark.
