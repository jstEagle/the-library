## 11. The Unbinding Rite

The hermitage had one room, one window, and a hearth that hadn't been lit in a hundred years, but Ilsa Okonkwo's letter was warm in Petra's hands as if it had come off the coals an hour ago.

They read it a third time by the grey morning light, sitting on the floor because there was no chair, because the prophet who wrote this had owned nothing she couldn't carry, and Petra wanted to understand what that kind of emptiness felt like from the inside.

*To the child of my child's child, whoever you turn out to be—*

*You will have found this the hard way. That is how I found everything worth having. Forgive me for not leaving easier roads; easy roads were how they caught us.*

Petra looked at the word *us*. Not *me*. Us. A hundred years dead and still counting herself among the hunted.

*Here is what you must know about your blood. The tasters of my line are not lucky. We are not blessed. We are made, generation by generation, the way a sword is made—by fire, by folding, by the patient cruelty of the smith. Every Okonkwo who survived a poison their body should not have survived passed something down. Not an immunity exactly. An argument. Our blood knows how to say no.*

*But saying no to nightshade is not enough. You already know that, or you would not be reading this. There is one poison greater than all others, one ember that cannot be spat out, only swallowed whole—and held.*

Yew sat across from them on the windowsill, knees drawn up, watching Petra read with the expression of a man watching someone walk toward a cliff edge he'd already mapped. He'd read the letter twice through last night while Petra slept badly against the tomb. He'd said almost nothing since.

"The rite," Petra said now. "Read it to me again. All of it."

"Petra."

"All of it."

Yew sighed and took the pages, smoothing them on his knee. His scholar's voice, usually so quick with citations and cleverness, went slow and careful, like a man handling glass.

"*The binding that holds King Aldric was made in haste and fear, and haste and fear make poor chains.* It requires three things. First, the vessel: one of our blood, willing, whose body has been trained to hold heat without breaking. Second—" he glanced up— "the crown itself, broken open, so the ember can be drawn. Third, a hand the spirit trusts to make the counter-binding after. That part is Aldric's to do, once he is free. If he survives being free."

"And the training," Petra said. "The part where I prepare my body."

Yew's jaw tightened. He turned the page. "*No throat was made for this. But throats can be remade, the way smiths remake metal. Begin with small fires. Swallow what burns a little. Then what burns more. Teach your blood its argument in stages, so that when the great fire comes, it does not need to learn courage—it remembers it.*"

Silence filled the hermitage. Outside, wind moved through the thorn trees that gave Thornfell its name, and somewhere below the mountain, Petra knew, the thing beneath it turned over in its sleep of years.

"So I practice," Petra said. "On poisons."

"On fire." Yew folded the letter with exaggerated care, as if neatness could contain what it said. "Ember-ash steeped in wine. Coals ground into tincture. She lists them. There's a whole apothecary of horrors in the margins, and Petra—the margins also list what it did to her test subjects. The villagers who helped her. Two of the five names have little crosses beside them."

"I saw."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

It wasn't a question, but Petra answered it anyway. "Vess has smugglers in cages. Sefu forged a ledger to bury me before I ever knew I existed. The king is a lantern someone else carries around and calls a man. Every day I wait, the ember gets deeper into him and harder to pull out." They stood, joints stiff from the stone floor. "I didn't choose to be born into this blood. But I choose what it's for."

Yew looked at them for a long moment. "You sound like the letter."

"I am the letter. Apparently."

That earned half a laugh out of him, brittle but real, and then his face closed again, and Petra noticed—he always noticed, it was the taster in them—that Yew's eyes went to the second-to-last page and away again, quickly, the way a man looks at a door he doesn't want you to know is in the room.

He'd hidden something. Or withheld it. Petra filed it away the way they filed every flavor that didn't belong, and let it sit.

There would be time. There had to be time.

* * *

They started that afternoon.

The hermitage cellar was Ilsa's old workroom, and a century had not emptied it. Jars lined the walls under skins of dust: dried peppers black as grave-soil, salts stained orange, a stoppered flask labeled in faded ink *ASH, FIRST STEEPING — FOR THE CHILD, WHEN COME*. 

"For the child, when come," Petra read aloud. "She bottled this a hundred years before I was born."

"She believed in you before your grandparents drew breath." Yew held up a candle. "I find that either beautiful or terrifying depending on the hour."

"Both," Petra said, and unstopped the flask.

The smell rose first: woodsmoke and iron and something underneath like struck flint, like the moment before lightning. Petra measured a thimbleful into a cup of watered wine, following the margin notes—Ilsa's handwriting shrank when she was being precise, as though precision embarrassed her—and drank.

For ten seconds, nothing. Then the heat bloomed.

It wasn't pain exactly. It was *argument*, just as the letter promised—a foreign voice in the throat saying *burn*, and Petra's blood answering, lower, steadier, *no*. Their hands shook. Sweat stood out on their lip. The taste of char coated their tongue long after they'd swallowed.

"Well?" Yew hovered with a water skin.

"Like drinking a hearth." Petra set the cup down, watched their own fingers tremble, and made them stop. "Again tomorrow. Double the dose."

"That's not what the margins say. The margins say gradual. Weeks per stage."

"Ilsa had decades." Petra met his eyes. "Aldric doesn't. Vess moves faster than either of us, and every day the king spends bound is a day the counter-binding gets harder. I don't have weeks per stage, Yew. I have whatever I have."

He wanted to argue. Petra watched him want to argue, watched the scholar's instinct for caution war with something newer in him—loyalty, maybe, or guilt, or the simple human refusal to watch someone walk into fire alone. In the end he just nodded once and began cataloguing jars, and neither of them mentioned the page he kept not looking at.

* * *

Three days. Four doses a day.

Petra learned the shape of the burning the way they'd once learned the shape of arsenic—by surrendering to it fully enough to study it. The ember-ash didn't behave like other poisons. It didn't attack; it *invited*. It found the coldest parts of them—the fear behind the breastbone, the loneliness they'd worn like a second cloak for six years—and offered warmth instead.

That was the trap, they realized on the fourth night, shaking through a fever that wasn't quite fever. The ember didn't conquer you. It comforted you. It asked nothing except everything. Somewhere in the fever-dreams, a voice like banked coals murmured that it could take the cold away forever, that kings had said yes to far less—

Petra woke gasping on the cellar floor, Yew's worried face above them, and understood for the first time why Aldric had lasted twenty years and why those twenty years had hollowed him into a signature with a scorch-mark.

"It talks to you," Yew said, helping them upright. "Doesn't it. Even the trace doses."

"It negotiates." Petra rinsed their mouth. "It's very reasonable. That's the worst part."

"Can you refuse it?"

"I've spent six years refusing things." They looked at their hands—at the faint tracery of heat-flush along the veins, gone within minutes, the body learning its new grammar. "Refusal is the one craft I'm master of."

* * *

Five days. Seven doses. The stage-two tinctures now, coals ground fine in wine, and Petra's body stopped shaking at the swallow and started shaking after, which Ilsa's notes called *the turning*: *when the fire stops being guest and begins asking about lodgings. Watch this hour closely. Here is where vessels are won or lost.*

Here is where two of her five names got their crosses.

Petra trained like a duelist and fasted like a monk and slept in shifts with Yew standing guard, and on the seventh night, at the worst of the turning, they dreamed of the Ember Court kitchen—not as it was but as memory makes kitchens, golden and endless—and Sefu stood at the great hearth teaching a child to hold a spoon steady over steam.

*"Steady," he said. "The tongue lies, the nose lies, but the body tells truth. Your work is to be the body's witness."*

*"What if the body's truth is terrible?" the child asked.*

*"Then you stand there anyway," Sefu said, "and you hold the spoon steady, and you taste it, because no one else will."*

Petra woke with wet eyes and a steady pulse and the taste of coal on their tongue, and knew the turning had tipped their way. Not because the fire lost. Because it had finally met someone who'd been practicing refusal since childhood.

* * *

On the ninth day, Rook's road brought them a stranger.

Petra heard the horse before the knock—one rider, unhurried, taking the switchbacks like someone who knew them. They were at the door with a knife before the second hoofbeat, Yew behind them with Ilsa's letter clutched like a shield.

The rider dismounted slowly, hands wide. Grey-cloaked, travel-worn, face half-lost under a hood—and then the hood came back and it was Marisol, Rook's cousin, the one who'd run the toll-house at the pass before Vess burned it.

"They said you'd come here." Her voice was raw. "The survivors. They said the taster would come here."

"Marisol." Petra lowered the knife. "How did you find—"

"Rook told me once. 'If anything happens to me, the taster goes to the hermitage above the falls.'" She laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "He planned his own eulogy, that idiot. Always planning routes." Her face hardened. "I came because you need to know. Vess has moved. Not patrols—armies. She's sealed the passes, she's declared martial law in the lowlands, and yesterday she announced to the court that she's found the traitor who poisoned the king's stew." 

Petra's stomach dropped. "She named me."

"Worse." Marisol pulled a crumpled broadsheet from her coat and handed it through the doorway. "She named you *and* put a price on you. Five thousand crowns. And she told the court the taster of thorns intends to *kill* the king, not free him. She's turned the prophecy into your confession, Petra. Every copy of the book she burns, she reads aloud first—in pieces, twisted. By week's end, every soul in the capital will believe you're coming for Aldric's throat."

Yew made a small sound. When Petra glanced back at him, he'd gone pale, and his hand had drifted—unconsciously, terribly—to the second-to-last page of the letter.

And there it was again. That door he didn't want them to see.

"Yew." Petra kept their voice level. "What's on that page?"

"Nothing that changes what you have to do."

"What's. On. That page."

Marisol looked between them, confused. Yew closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older than the letter, older than the tomb, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far.

"Ilsa tested the rite in theory," he said quietly. "The margins model it. And in every working, the outcome depends on the vessel. A willing vessel of strong blood swallows the ember and *holds* it—long enough for the counter-binding, and then..." He spread his hands. "But the margins also model a weaker vessel. Or a willing vessel who falters at the crucial moment. In those workings, the ember isn't held. It's *accepted*. And the person who walks out of that throne room wearing the fire..."

He couldn't finish. Marisol did, softly, reading Petra's face:

"You don't free the kingdom. You become the next thing beneath the mountain."

The wind came through the thorn trees. Somewhere below them, the old guardian turned in its sleep.

Petra looked down at their hands—the veins carrying their faint borrowed heat, the blood arguing its inherited *no*—and heard their own voice come out steady, spoon-steady, the way a child once learned over steam:

"Then I'll simply have to be strong enough. Stage three at dawn."

Behind them, Yew whispered, "Petra—"

"And Yew? Next time there's a page I need to read—" Petra turned, and their eyes were ember-lit and unflinching— "you'll give it to me *before* the road ends. Whatever we're walking into, we walk in knowing. That's the oath now. Mine and yours."

He nodded, ashamed and relieved at once.

Dawn was four hours off. Petra went down to the cellar alone, set the stage-three tincture on the bench, and waited for it to cool enough to drink—because the body's truth was terrible, and they intended to stand there anyway, and taste it.
