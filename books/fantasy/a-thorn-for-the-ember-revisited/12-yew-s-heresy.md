## 12. Yew's Heresy

The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil, and Yew Andrade did not notice.

This was, in fairness, not unusual. Yew's tutor at the Collégium Arcanum had once said—and said it often, and with the weary relish of a man who enjoyed being right about disappointing things—that Yew could stand in a burning building and complain about a marginal note. It was not a compliment. It had also, Yew reflected now, been entirely accurate, because Yew had spent the last eleven days in a rented room above a chandler's shop in the Salt Quarter, surrounded by a burning building of a different kind, and had not once thought to leave it.

The building was the book.

*The Thorn Codex*, the auction house had called it, in the tasteful italic of a catalogue that valued rarity over content. A century-old devotional miscellany, provincial binding, water damage, sold with provenance disputed. Yew had bid against a dealer from the Ember Court—against *Petra*, though neither of them had known it at the time, two strangers circling the same dusty lot like cats around a fish—and won it for an obscene sum that had consumed a year of lecture fees and the goodwill of a creditor named Master Hollen who was, Yew suspected, not a patient man.

It had been worth it. It was worth more than Yew had.

The problem was page ninety-four.

Yew had copied it out four times now, in four different hands, as if the act of transcription might change what it said. It did not. The old script—Ilsa Okonkwo's script, tight and slanting, the hand of someone who wrote fast because the words were chasing her—sat on the page in faded iron-gall ink, and it said the same thing it had said the first time, at midnight, eleven days ago, when Yew's stomach had dropped through the floorboards of the world and kept falling.

*Two ways the thorn may end,* the page read. *I have seen both. In the first, the taster of thorns takes the ember into their own body and the king walks free, and the kingdom is remade in the image of what it might have been. In the second—*

Yew had stopped there, the first time. Had physically closed the book and set it across the room and gone to bed and lain awake until dawn listening to the chandler's cart below and thinking, with the calm of genuine shock, *I will not read that sentence. I will simply not read it. The sentence does not exist if I do not read it.*

That had lasted until roughly the second hour of lying awake, at which point Yew had gotten up, relit the lamp, and read the sentence.

*In the second, the taster takes the ember and the ember takes the taster, and the crown is not broken but passed, and a new thing wears the fire, and it will be kinder than the old fire, at first.*

*At first.*

Yew set down the quill and pressed the heels of both hands against their eyes until the dark bloomed orange—ember-colored, some traitorous part of the mind noted—and thought about the word *at first* the way a man thinks about a stone he has swallowed.

* * *

The trouble with scholarship, Yew's tutor had also said, is that it is the art of knowing things you wish you didn't. The trouble with *this* scholarship was worse. The trouble was that Yew had a friend now.

Friend. Yew turned the word over. It had been years since the word applied to anyone outside the Collégium's dwindling circle of the underfunded, and it had never applied to anyone like Petra Okonkwo—someone who moved through rooms as if the rooms had agreed not to notice them, someone who could name the poison in a stew by smell from three feet away and then say nothing about it, because saying something meant being seen, and being seen, for Petra, was apparently a category of death.

They had met over the auction lot and quarreled about provenance, and Yew had lost the quarrel, which was how Yew knew it was real. Petra had a way of being right that didn't announce itself. And then, over the following weeks—the archive visits, the shared table at the Lamplighter's, the night Petra had finally, haltingly, explained what they were, what the book named them, what the king's scorched signature meant—Yew had felt the thing that scholars are not supposed to feel, the thing the Collégium trains out of you with the same rigor it trains in citation: *investment.*

Yew cared. That was the whole catastrophe. Yew cared what happened to Petra Okonkwo, and page ninety-four said that what happened to Petra Okonkwo might be the second ending.

And page ninety-four also said—Yew had checked this eleven times, in eleven different lights, with a magnifying lens borrowed from an optician who now believed Yew was losing their eyesight—that the two endings were not fated. They were chosen. The prophecy was not a river; it was a fork. *The taster chooses,* Ilsa had written. *I have written this so that the choosing is possible. A thing known in advance can be refused. This is the whole of my art.*

Which meant that telling Petra was safe. Which meant, in fact, that *not* telling Petra was the only way the second ending could happen—because a fork only functions if the traveler doesn't know it's there, and Yew, by withholding page ninety-four, was doing the ember's work for it, keeping the road dark, keeping the choice—

No. That was not right either. Yew had spent three days constructing that argument and had watched it collapse under its own footnotes. Ilsa's whole design was *knowledge*. The letter in the hermitage—Petra had read it aloud, voice steady in a way Yew recognized now as effort—had said the rite required a *willing* vessel. Willingness required information. You could not consent to a fork you could not see.

So the truth was simple, and simple truths are the hardest kind: Yew was hiding page ninety-four not because hiding it was right, but because Yew was afraid. Afraid that naming the second ending would make it heavier, more real, more *available*. Afraid that Petra, who had already decided—Yew had watched the decision happen, had watched it settle into Petra's shoulders like a yoke—was going to walk into the fire, and that the knowledge of the fork might tip the walk one inch in the wrong direction. Afraid, at the bottom of it, in the place where Yew kept the things that were true: afraid that if Petra knew, Petra might choose not to do the rite at all, and then the king would burn forever, and the kingdom would keep its ember, and Yew would have to live in that kingdom knowing they had helped make it, because they had loved someone enough to let a tyrant keep his crown.

Loyalty and truth, sitting across from each other at the same small table, not speaking.

* * *

On the twelfth day, Petra came to the room above the chandler's shop.

Yew heard the footsteps on the stairs and had four seconds to decide where page ninety-four's transcription lived. It lived, at that moment, under the false bottom of the ink box, because Yew was not an amateur, and the four seconds were enough to confirm what Yew already knew: that the transcription would stay there, and that Yew would smile, and that the smile would be the first genuinely scholarly act of Yew's career, because it would be a forgery, and forgeries are what scholars do when the truth is inconvenient.

"You look terrible," Petra said from the doorway.

"You look like someone who's been sleeping in barns."

"I have been sleeping in barns." Petra came in the way Petra did everything—quietly, without displacing air—and sat on the one chair that wasn't buried in paper. Their eyes went over the room with that taster's sweep: the books, the lamp, the ink box, Yew's face. Especially Yew's face. "You've found something."

It was not a question. Yew had learned that Petra's questions were rarely questions; they were conclusions offered politely, with an exit provided in case the conclusion was wrong. It was a courtier's skill, worn by someone who claimed to have no court in them.

"I've found that the Codex's later folios are palimpsest," Yew said, which was true. "Ilsa wrote over an older devotional text. There's a penitential psalter underneath. I'm recovering it."

"Is that useful?"

"It's *fascinating*," Yew said, with the precise enthusiasm that scholars deploy as a smoke screen, and Petra looked at them for a long moment with those flat, patient, unfooled eyes, and then let it go, and the letting-it-go was worse than an accusation. Petra trusted Yew. That was the poison in the room. Not nightshade—something slower, something that worked on the person administering it.

They talked for an hour. Petra laid out what they'd learned on the road: the binding circle beneath the mountain, the vaults, the way the ember-spirit had once been a guardian and had been fed on fear by generations of keepers until guardianship curdled into control. Petra talked about Vess with a cold, precise anger that Yew found quietly magnificent. Petra talked about the rite—*my rite*, they said, with a small, terrible possessiveness, as if the fire were already a pet they'd agreed to keep—and Yew sat there holding page ninety-four under the floor of the conversation like a body under floorboards, and nodded, and asked the right questions, and did not say *there is another way this ends and you should know that your choice has a shadow.*

When Petra stood to leave, they paused at the door.

"Yew." They didn't turn all the way around. "Whatever you're not telling me—and you're not telling me something, you've had that look since the auction, the look of a man holding a card he hasn't played—I'm going to trust that you'll play it when it matters. That's the deal. That's what I'm offering you. Don't make me regret the offer."

The stairs creaked under them, and then the street door closed, and Yew sat in the lamp-smell for a long time.

* * *

That night, Yew did the thing scholars do when they cannot decide: they made a list.

*Arguments for telling Petra:* every argument. Consent. Ilsa's design. The fork requires a witness who can see it. Friendship. The Collégium's one unbreakable rule, carved over its doors in letters every student walked under for seven years: *WHAT IS FOUND BELONGS TO EVERYONE.* Yew had walked under that sentence a thousand times and had believed it the way one believes weather.

*Arguments against telling Petra:* that the second ending, named aloud, might become a door instead of a wall. That fear is a solvent and Petra was carrying enough weight already. That Yew had watched Petra decide, and the decision was the spine of them now, and Yew could not bear to be the one who cracked it. That—here the quill hesitated, then wrote it, because a list that flatters is worthless—that Yew wanted to matter. That being the keeper of the terrible page made Yew necessary, and necessary was the closest thing to safe a scholar like Yew would ever be, and if the page was shared then Yew was just a man in a rented room with a lamp going out.

Yew looked at that last line for a long time. Then they burned the list, because some things should only ever exist as ash.

But burning the list did not burn the problem. The problem sat down across from Yew wearing Ilsa Okonkwo's handwriting, and it said: *at first.*

*At first,* the second ending would be kind. That was the horror of it, the thing that made it a heresy worth hiding and a heresy worth fearing: the second ending didn't look like a fall. It looked like a rescue. A taster who swallowed the ember and *kept* it—kept it gently, kept it well, a fire that healed instead of ruled. The court would love them. The kingdom would love them. The poison would never touch another king. And the fire would learn, the way fire learns, the way it had learned under the mountain, the way anything learns when it is fed for long enough by people who need it—learned that kindness is a better leash than fear, and that the hand that holds it need never be refused, because who refuses a savior?

Ilsa had seen it. A century ago, in a hermitage, with ink and terror and love, Ilsa had seen her descendant standing in a sunlit court with ember in their veins and the whole kingdom kneeling, and had written down the only warning that mattered: *at first.*

And Yew understood, finally, with the clarity that arrives only at the far end of exhaustion, why the heresy had to be hidden—not from Petra, but *by* someone, *for* a while, *until*. Because the second ending was not a fork in the road. It was a temptation dressed as the road. And temptations work best when the traveler knows exactly what they're walking past.

Or so Yew told themselves. The ink box sat on the shelf with its false bottom and its folded page, and it did not care what Yew told themselves. It simply waited, the way documents do, with more patience than any person.

* * *

On the fourteenth day, two things happened.

The first: Master Hollen's man came to the door about the debt, and Yew paid him with the last of the lecture fees and the last of the goodwill, and after he left, Yew took stock and found the inventory was this: one lamp, nearly dry; one book worth more than everything; one secret, folded small; and one friend, out in a city that was starting to smell of smoke.

The second: the smoke was not metaphor. From the window, Yew could see it rising past the Salt Quarter's rooftops—not chimney smoke, not fog, but the particular greasy black of burning paper in quantity. Down in the street, a crier was shouting something about sedition, about prohibited texts, about the spymaster's new writ. Yew leaned out and caught the words as the wind turned: *...by order of Lady Vess, all copies, all fragments, all hands that held them...*

They were burning prophecy books. They were burning them *today*.

Yew's first thought was for the Codex—irreplaceable, the only witness, the thing Ilsa had bled onto—and Yew was already moving, hands wrapping it in oilcloth, before the second thought arrived, the cold one, the one that stopped Yew with the book half-wrapped in their arms:

They were burning prophecy books because Vess knew what was in them. And Vess knew what was in them because Vess had read them. And if Vess had read them—

Yew stood in the middle of the room, holding a century of a dead woman's courage, and thought: *Vess has read page ninety-four.*

And then, worse, arriving like a blade slipped between ribs: *Vess is counting on page ninety-four. Vess wants the second ending. Vess has always wanted the second ending—a taster on the throne is a taster who can be handled—and every day I keep the fork hidden from Petra, I am doing her work.*

The lamp went out. Yew did not relight it. There was no more oil, and besides, the light was no longer needed; the fire over the rooftops provided plenty.

Yew wrapped the Codex against their chest, took the ink box with its false bottom, and went down the stairs into the smoke, toward the Ember Court, toward Petra, toward the conversation that was eleven days overdue and might now be eleven days too late—carrying, all the while, in a fold of oilcloth against a coward's heartbeat, the page that could save a kingdom or crown its next tyrant, still hidden, still unplayed, still burning a hole in everything.
