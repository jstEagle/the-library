## 13. Court of Cinders

The smoke was visible from the ridge above Thornfell Road, three days before we reached the city.

Rook's people had a name for the way the Ember Court burned things it didn't like. They called it *candling*—because from a distance, a pyre of confiscated books looked almost like a festival. Like lanterns. Yew stood beside me at the treeline, her scholar's satchel heavy with the one copy that mattered, and watched the glow stain the underside of the clouds.

"They're not even pretending anymore," she said.

"No." I counted the fires. Seven. Maybe eight. "Pretending costs money."

We had spent two weeks in the high camps since Sefu's ledger, since Vess's offer, since Rook's blood on the frozen stones of the pass. Two weeks watching riders in crown gray sweep the smuggler roads like a comb through lice. The caravans had scattered into the deep valleys. The safehouse caches were being pulled up one by one. And every courier who came down from the passes brought the same report: Lady Vess had stopped working in shadows.

She was arresting tanners' children now. Grandmothers. A ferryman on the Vell who had once carried a crate of unlicensed tea. She wasn't looking for the prophecy book anymore—she'd decided it didn't matter whether she found it. What mattered was that everyone in the city understood that possessing a thought could cost them their hands.

Yew wanted to run south, over the border, take the book to some foreign archive where it would gather dust for another century and keep us both alive.

I wanted to go back.

"You understand," she said, not arguing, just laying out the facts the way she laid out manuscript pages, "that if you walk through those gates, you are walking into the mouth of the only person in this kingdom who knows your face is worth something."

"I know."

"And Sefu—"

"Sefu wrote entries in my hand that I never made. Years ahead. Dated like appointments." I kept my voice flat. It was easier than letting it crack. "I don't know yet if he did it to save me or to cage me. That's exactly why I have to go back and ask him to his face."

"And the king?"

"The king whispered *unbind me* while his own mouth tried to burn the word out of the air." I turned away from the fires. "Nobody's reached him in twenty years, Yew. Everyone who gets close enough either dies or gets bought. But they'll let a penitent taster back in. They always do. Tasters are furniture."

"Furniture that's been missing for a month."

"Then I'd better look sorry enough to be forgiven."

* * *

The road down from Thornfell opened onto the Vell valley, and the Vell valley opened onto the city, and the city had changed its smell.

That's how I knew how bad it was—not the banners, not the doubled watch at the gates, but the smell. Six years of walking these streets had taught me the city by scent the way a taster learns a table: bread from the east quarter ovens, river mud, the iron tang of the smithies, and under all of it, faint as a signature, the ember-scorch that hung around the palace like a second sky. Now there was ash layered over everything. Not hearth-ash. Paper-ash. The whole city smelled like a book that had been held too close to a flame.

At the Gate of Ashes—the western gate, the tradesmen's gate—a sergeant in crown gray checked papers with the bored efficiency of a man who had done this four hundred times this week. Behind him, two soldiers were feeding a barrel-bound fire with cartons seized from somewhere. I stood in line behind a spice merchant and a woman with a goose in a basket, and I let my body remember an old lesson: be small. Be dull. Be the kind of face that slides off memory like grease off slate.

Six years as the court's food-taster had taught me invisibility as a craft. The last month had taught me its opposite—that once someone has truly *seen* you, you can never fully unsee yourself in their eyes. Vess had seen me. If she looked closely at any penitent returning through this gate, I would hang by sundown.

So I gave the sergeant nothing to look closely at.

"Purpose of entry?"

"Returning to service." I passed him the forged letter of dismissal I'd bought from a forger in the camps—one of Rook's people, working with hands still steady after grief, which is more than mine were. "Dismissed during the recent irregularities. Seeking reinstatement under the Master Taster's authority."

The sergeant read it twice. Around us, the fire popped and settled, and a page's corner lifted on the wind and spiraled past his shoulder, trailing a curl of half-burnt script. He didn't glance at it. That frightened me more than anything else—the way the soldiers had stopped seeing what they burned.

"Master Taster Sefu has been confined to his quarters," the sergeant said. "Pending inquiry. Reinstatements go through Lady Vess's office now."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world and kept falling.

"Confined," I repeated.

"He's old. It's a courtesy." The sergeant stamped the letter and waved me through. "Next!"

I walked into the city carrying that word like a stone in my chest. Confined. Pending inquiry. Courtesy. In the Ember Court, *courtesy* was what they called it when they hadn't decided yet whether to kill you.

I had told myself I was going back partly to confront Sefu. I realized, walking those ash-smelling streets, that some part of me had assumed he would simply be there—irritable, meticulous, smelling of chalk and bitter herbs, ready to explain everything in that dry voice that had raised me. The world without Sefu freely moving through it was a world with a load-bearing wall removed.

And Vess's office handling reinstatements meant she was collecting the tasters. Every palate in the palace, gathered under one roof, catalogued. You don't do that unless you're planning to poison somebody—or unless you're terrified of being poisoned yourself.

Both, probably. Vess struck me as a woman who prepared for every contingency except losing.

* * *

They put me in the tasters' wing, third floor, the room I'd left a month ago. Someone had stripped the bed and aired the linens. My few belongings were gone—confiscated or sold, no way to know—and in their place sat a folded gray uniform with the new badge stitched at the collar: not the old silver tongue-and-thorn of the tasting guild, but a flame inside a circle. Vess's mark. Even our collars had been conscripted.

The summons came within the hour, which told me they'd flagged my entry the moment I touched the gate. Of course they had. I climbed the stairs to the administrative tower rehearsing contrition the way I'd once rehearsed swallowing hemlock: slowly, in measured doses, with the body kept loose.

Lady Vess received me in a room I had never been permitted to see—the spymaster's own office, all dark wood and darker glass, maps of the kingdom pinned so thickly they overlapped like scales. She looked older than she had a month ago. Not weaker. Compressed, the way coal is compressed. Her eyes went over my face once, thoroughly, the way a customs officer goes over a crate.

"Petra Okonkwo." She said my full name deliberately, letting it sit between us like a blade laid flat on a table. "You've been busy."

"Lady Vess. I came to beg reinstatement."

"You came down out of Thornfell, where you've been hiding among smugglers since you stole property from an archive auction and vanished." She folded her hands. "Sit down, Okonkwo. Lying to me wastes both our evenings."

I sat. The chair was cold.

"Here is what I know," she continued, conversationally, terribly. "You disappeared the same week a certain book appeared at auction. You have family roots in the hill country that you very carefully never mentioned in six years of service. Your former master is currently explaining to my inquirers why he kept a private ledger written partially in your handwriting. And the king—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes that I had never seen there before, something that in anyone else I might have called fear, "—the king has asked about you. Twice."

My heart was a drum in a locked room. I kept my face still. Taster's discipline. Swallow the reaction; show the surface only.

"I don't know anything about a book," I said. "I ran because I panicked. There was nightshade in the king's stew, planted in his own kitchen, and then everyone started looking at me instead of looking for the poisoner, and I'm a taster, my lady—I know how investigations end for the convenient suspect. So I ran. It was cowardice. I've come back because cowardice doesn't keep you warm."

It was good. It was nearly true, which is the only kind of lie that survives her. Vess studied me for a long moment, and I felt the examination the way you feel a physician's fingers probing a wound—looking for the place where pressure makes you flinch.

"Cowardice," she said at last. "Well. The court is full of it. At least yours is honest."

She rose and crossed to the window. Below, in the courtyard, another pyre was being built. I could see the soldiers stacking crates, and I could see, standing apart from them with his hands bound and two guards flanking him, a man I knew. Broad shoulders. Ferryman's build. Marta's father—the smuggler who had ferried me across the Vell a hundred times, who used to slip dried plums into my pack when he thought I wasn't eating enough.

"Reinstatement carries conditions," Vess said, without turning. "You will taste for the king's table as before. You will report to me weekly—not your findings, your *observations*. Who visits whom. What mood the kitchen keeps. Whether Master Sefu receives visitors." She turned back, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw, the one rumor said she'd earned putting down a revolt in the salt provinces. "And you will forget whatever you think you read in any auction lot. The court has enough trouble without prophecy."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can join your friend below." Nodding at the window. "He claims he doesn't know you. Loyalty is touching and useless. He'll be released in a week if no one gives me a reason to keep him." Her voice softened by a single degree, which from Vess was practically tenderness. "You're useful, Okonkwo. Useful people live. Remember that I am the one telling you so."

I bowed. I thanked her. I walked out of that office with my spine straight and my mind screaming, and it was only in the stairwell, alone, that I let myself shake.

Because I had gone in expecting a mask to hold, and the mask had held—but I'd come out knowing something worse than before. Vess hadn't arrested me. Vess *wanted* me close. Which meant either she believed my cowardice story—

—or she was fattening the fish before landing it.

Or, whispered a colder voice from the bottom of the stairwell, she needed a taster of thorns exactly where the prophecy said she'd find one. Right at the king's side. Where she could control what that tongue tasted, and when, and whether it ever got close enough to do what the old book promised.

* * *

They let me see the king's table that same evening.

Not the king himself—he dined behind the screen as he had for years, his food passing through the ceremonial window, his presence marked only by the ember-smell that seeped through the lattice and the sound of a fork set down too precisely, again and again, like a man counting to keep himself calm. I knelt at the tasting station and worked through the first course with the mechanical grace of six years' habit: venison in pepper sauce, parsnip purée, wine cut with honey. Nothing toxic. Nothing altered. Just the faint, wrong sweetness underneath everything that I had learned to recognize as the kitchen's new fear—cooks over-salting, over-spicing, burying flavors so that no poison of theirs could ever be blamed.

Sefu's station stood empty beside mine. Dust on the brass. No one had been allowed to use it.

Between courses, a server leaned close to swap out my water pitcher—a kitchen girl named Odile, sixteen, quick-eyed, one of the handful of staff who had ever been kind to me without wanting anything. Her lips barely moved.

"He asks for you," she breathed. "The king. After the meal. The screen opens on the left side where the panel's loose. Ten minutes. Don't be followed."

I kept my eyes on the tasting tray. Kept my hands steady. Inside, everything in me went silent and bright, the way the air does before lightning.

Twenty years of decrees signed in scorched ink. A man whispering *unbind me* through his own burning mouth. And now, somehow, against every plan I'd made and every trap I suspected, the king himself had sent for the taster of thorns.

I swallowed the next bite of venison, tasted nothing but fear, and waited for the screen to open.
