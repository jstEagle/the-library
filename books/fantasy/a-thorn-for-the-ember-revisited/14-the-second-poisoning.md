## 14. The Second Poisoning

They let me back into the kitchens the way you'd let a fox back into the henhouse: with everyone watching and nobody saying why.

Three weeks after Emberfall's first crack—after Vess's men burned prophecy copies in the courtyard and dragged Rook's people out of their beds—I walked through the scullery door in a penitent taster's gray, head bowed, hands scrubbed raw, wearing the expression of someone who had learned humility the hard way. The kitchen girl, Tamsin, had gotten me past the gate with a forged work chit and a straight face she had no business possessing at sixteen.

The plan was simple. The king took his evening meal in the small dining chamber now, alone, guarded by two of Vess's hand-picked men. A taster was required by law to stand at his elbow. If I could get myself assigned to that duty—if I could put my body between Aldric and whatever Vess decided to send next—I would have one hour a day in which to reach him, to speak to him, to find out whether the man who whispered *unbind me* was still in there.

Getting assigned meant getting Sefu to assign me.

I found him where I always found him, in the tasting room off the main kitchen, a low stone chamber that smelled of copper pots and vinegar, where he kept the ledgers and the antidote cabinet and the single high window through which the afternoon light fell like something rationed.

He looked older than six weeks should have made him. His beard had gone from salt-and-pepper to mostly salt. When he saw me standing in the doorway, he set down the pestle he was grinding and did not move for a long moment.

"Petra."

"Master Taster." I bowed the way a penitent bows. "I've come to beg reinstatement."

"You've come to beg reinstatement," he repeated. He came around the worktable slowly, and then he did the thing that undid me more than any interrogation could have: he took my face in both his hands, the way he had when I was nine years old and burning with fever, and he looked at me like a man reading a document for forgery. "You're thinner. You're frightened. You're here under orders from no one, which frightens me more than if you were."

"I'm here because the king is dying by inches and everyone pretends it's age."

"And you think standing next to him with a spoon will stop it." He dropped his hands. Turned away. And I saw it then—the thing I had missed because grief makes us stupid—the way his eyes went to the ledger shelf, and away again, too quickly. The way his shoulders sat like a man carrying water uphill.

"Sefu." I used his name for the first time in years. In the tasting room we were Master and apprentice, always; the formality was armor for both of us. But I said *Sefu* now, quietly, and watched him flinch. "The nightshade. Two months ago. You knew who planted it, didn't you?"

"The stew is not your concern anymore."

"The stew was never my concern. That's what made me good." I stepped closer. "You raised me on that principle. *We are furniture, child. Furniture sees everything and is seen by nothing.* So look at me as furniture and tell me what I'm walking into."

For a moment I thought he would tell me. His mouth opened. And then footsteps sounded in the corridor—a guard's tread, unhurried, the tread of a man who wanted to be heard coming—and whatever lived behind Sefu's face shut like a door.

"Report to the steward at dawn," he said, loud enough for the corridor. "Penitents start with the morning broths." Then, barely a breath, barely sound at all, so quiet I nearly lost it: "*Don't drink anything I serve you.*"

* * *

Dawn. Broths. The humiliation was the point, and I understood that, and I performed it anyway, bowing over steam while kitchen boys smirked and the under-cook assigned me the vats nobody else wanted. Three days of this. Three days of being furniture, of counting guards' rotations, of learning that the king dined at seven bells and that his taster-of-record was a nervous boy named Corvin who jumped at dropped pans.

On the fourth day, Sefu summoned me to the tasting room at dusk.

"You'll take the evening meal tomorrow," he said. He didn't look at me. He was decanting wine, and his hands were steady, and that steadiness was wrong; Sefu's hands trembled when he was calm and went still only when he was terrified. "Corvin has a fever. The steward wants the penitent tested."

"So they can watch me fail."

"So they can watch you." He stoppered the decanter. Still not looking at me. "Petra. If I give you a cup tomorrow, and tell you to drink—"

"You won't."

"—and I tell you to drink, you must trust me enough to refuse."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above us, the kitchen roared on, oblivious: knives, shouting, the great clockwork of a court that fed itself three times a day without ever asking what went into its mouth.

"Sefu," I said, "what did Vess order you to do?"

He finally turned. And I saw that he had been crying—not recently, but often, the way old stone shows the marks of old rain. "She ordered me to make you visible," he said. "That was always the order, from the beginning. Not dead. *Visible.* A dead taster is a tragedy. A taster who dies of poison despite an immunity that shouldn't exist—that's a story. That's proof the prophecy is a fraud, or worse, that its heir was a fraud all along. She needs you discredited before she needs you gone."

"And the compound?"

His silence answered me.

"There *is* a compound." My voice came out flat, which surprised me; I'd expected anger, and instead there was just this terrible clarity, cold water down the spine. "Something built to burn through the bloodline gift. Something tested."

"On mice. On a condemned thief last month." He set the decanter down with exaggerated care, as if the world had become a surface that might crack. "It works, Petra. It takes about four minutes, and it works, and I helped refine it, because the alternative was letting her find another refiner who wouldn't warn you." He crossed the room and gripped my shoulders, hard, and up close his eyes were wet and furious. "Tomorrow there will be a cup. It will be meant for you. I have spent six weeks trying to find a way around it and there is no way around it, because she checks, she *tests*, she has a second taster verify every step of my process—"

"Then I don't drink."

"She'll have Corvin drink it if you refuse. Or a scullery boy. She'll prove her point with whoever is nearest, and she'll keep proving points until the story lands." His grip tightened. "There is one way, and it is a bad way, and I have hated myself for six weeks for thinking it, and I am going to say it anyway because you deserve to choose: I drink the cup. I am an old man with a slow heart and no bloodline worth burning. If the compound kills me, the trial fails. No story. No fraud. And you walk out of that hall alive and invisible, which is the only inheritance I ever wanted to leave you."

"No," I said.

"It's four minutes of agony and then nothing. I've taken worse for lesser kings."

"*No.*" I pulled free of his grip. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to spend twenty years teaching me that we survive by seeing clearly and then die blind in front of me to settle some debt you think you owe. You forged entries in my handwriting. You scripted my fate in a ledger. Whatever you owe, you owe *me*, and I am telling you the price is that you live, and you grow even more salt than pepper, and you teach some other wretched orphan to taste nightshade in broth. Do you understand me?"

He smiled. It was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was full of love and entirely without agreement.

"Dawn bell," he said. "Wear your grays."

* * *

The evening meal was pheasant in a cherry glaze, root vegetables, dark bread, and wine. Seven bells. The small dining chamber. The king at the head of the table with his scorched signature of a face, staring at nothing, his fingers drumming a pattern against the table's edge—three quick, one slow, three quick—one pattern, over and over, like a man tapping a message through a wall from inside a cell.

Vess stood by the window. Of course she did. She wore dove-gray and a smile calibrated to the degree, and she watched me the way a falconer watches a hawk it doesn't fully trust yet.

Sefu carried the service himself. That was unusual—masters didn't serve—but he announced it as tradition: *on a penitent's first restoration, the master tastes alongside them, as in the old days.* Nobody objected. Traditions are how courts launder their cruelties.

He set the plates. Poured the wine. And when he reached my place, he set down my cup with a click that I felt in my teeth, and our eyes met for exactly half a heartbeat, and in that half-heartbeat I understood that everything he'd said the night before had been true, and that he had already decided, and that the cup in front of me was the cup.

I also understood, with the same terrible clarity, that I was not going to let it happen.

The ritual is simple. The taster eats first. Everything. Every dish, a full spoonful minimum, then waits the prescribed interval—long enough for fast poisons, short enough that slow ones are caught later—and the court watches the taster's face the whole time, because the face is the instrument.

I picked up my spoon. I looked at the pheasant. And I began to perform.

Not eating—*performing*. I let my brow crease at the second bite. I set down the spoon, picked it up, set it down again. I touched my throat. Small things. Ambiguous things. The kind of theater a frightened penitent might produce without meaning to, the kind that makes everyone lean forward, that makes Vess take one step away from the window—

And in that leaning-forward, in that held breath, Sefu moved.

He didn't lunge. Lunge would have been stopped. He simply, in the manner of a master correcting a nervous apprentice, reached across and lifted my cup from beside my plate—"the girl's nerves have spoiled the palate," he said mildly, to the room, "we'll begin again with clean vessels"—and he drank from it as he withdrew it. One long swallow. Casual. Ceremonial. As if it were the most natural thing in the world, a master drinking from a taster's cup.

The room relaxed. The theater collapsed; nerves explained, order restored. Vess resumed her post at the window, though her gaze sharpened, recalculating.

Sefu set the cup down by the sideboard. He stood very still for a moment, one hand flat on the wood, and I watched the color begin to leave his knuckles.

Four minutes, he'd said. It works. It takes about four minutes.

I tasted nothing wrong in any of the dishes, because there was nothing wrong in the dishes. The compound had been in the wine, in *my* cup, dosed for my weight and my blood and my inherited fire. And Sefu Okonkwo—no relation by blood, all relation by everything else—had drunk it like a man taking communion.

"Master Taster," the steward said, "you've not tasted the penitent's portion—"

"My apologies." His voice was still level. He turned toward the king's table to make his formal apology, and I saw the first tremor go through him, starting at the shoulder and rolling down like a wave. "Your Majesty. Forgive an old man's—"

Then his knees went.

The crash of a body hitting stone is a sound that empties a room of everything except itself. Guards shouted. Vess's composure cracked open for one unguarded instant—she hadn't planned this, not *here*, not publicly, not with the compound doing its work in front of forty witnesses—and in that instant I understood that Sefu had beaten her. Whatever script she'd written, he'd torn out the page and swallowed it along with the poison.

I was on the floor beside him before I remember moving.

Up close it was worse than agony; agony has a shape, a logic. This was fire with no flame. His back arched and his heels drummed the stone and veins stood up along his neck, darkened, as though something beneath his skin were smoldering. His eyes found mine. They were clear. That was the unbearable part—they were perfectly, horribly clear, and they were *apologizing*.

"Don't," I said. Foolish, useless word. I pressed my hands to either side of his face. His skin was hot as a kettle. "Sefu. Stay. Stay with me, you stubborn old—"

"Petra." Smoke-smell. The room smelled faintly of smoke, though nothing burned. His hand crawled up my wrist and gripped it with a strength that should not have existed in a dying man. "Listen. Listen. The ledger. Third shelf. Behind the—behind the antidotes, the false back—" A convulsion took him and wrung him like a rag. When it passed, his lips were gray and he spoke faster, spending breath like a bankrupt spends coin. "Everything. Names. Dates. What she made me write. What she made me *burn*. It's all there. Use it. Don't forgive me first. *Use it.*"

"You're going to be fine."

"Liar." He almost laughed; it came out as ash. "You were always the better liar. That's why—" Another convulsion, longer, and when it released him his voice was thin as paper. "I found you at the wall. Remember? Six years old. Eating crabapples off the ground to show the other children they wouldn't hurt you. I thought, *there. There's the one.* I thought I'd bought you time." His grip on my wrist loosened, tightened, loosened. "Buy yourself some, little ember. Buy yourself some."

The fire took the rest of his words. What it left was not peaceful. It took eleven more minutes, and I held his head the entire time, and the entire court watched, and King Aldric rose halfway from his chair—rose, *moved*, his face cracking open into something ancient and grieving—and Vess's hand on his arm put him back in his seat like closing a book.

When it was done, I closed Sefu's eyes with my thumbs and stayed kneeling on the stones of the Ember Court's dining chamber, in front of everyone, visible as a bonfire.

Grief would come later. Later was a country I couldn't enter yet. What I felt instead was colder and sharper: the understanding that the cup had been poured for *me*, and that Vess had lost her story tonight but not her war, and that a dead taster's master was now a martyr the court would talk about in whispers for a generation—which made Sefu, in death, the most dangerous weapon against her that either of us had ever held.

Guards took my arms and lifted me upright. I didn't resist. Let them hold me. Let them see.

Lady Vess approached through the crowd, composed again, seamless again, though I noticed she kept a careful distance from the body, as if the smoke-smell might cling.

"A tragedy," she said, for the room's benefit. "The old man's heart, no doubt. All those years of sampling toxins." Her eyes, level with mine, said something else entirely: *this changes nothing.*

"He drank from my cup," I said, loudly, clearly, letting every syllable carry to the far walls. "A master does not drink from a taster's cup. Someone switched the vessels, my lady. Someone in this room."

Murmurs moved through the court like wind through wheat. Vess's smile didn't flicker, but something behind it did—the recalculation, the cost running.

"You're distraught," she said gently.

"Yes," I agreed. "And awake. And *visible*." I let my gaze travel the assembled faces—the steward, the guards, the kitchen staff pressed at the doors, and last, longest, the king, whose drumming fingers had gone silent, whose eyes were fixed on Sefu's body with an expression no ember-spirit would bother to wear. Grief. Recognition. *Hope.*

"Take her to her quarters," Vess said to the guards, "and lock the door."

As they marched me out, past the sideboard, past the abandoned cup, I did the only thing left in my arsenal: I memorized. Faces, positions, who flinched, who looked at Vess for permission, who looked at *me*. The court was cracking. Sefu had died to buy me a story, and stories, once loose in a court, cannot be recalled.

Behind the antidote shelves, third shelf, false back. Everything. Names. Dates.

At dawn, locked in quarters that were rapidly becoming a cell, I heard boots in the corridor—too many boots, moving with purpose—and then the rattle of my lock turning, and I knew, before the door opened, that whatever came through it would decide whether Sefu's four minutes of agony had purchased anything at all.
