## 15. Emberfall

They let me see Sefu's body for exactly as long as it took to confirm he was dead.

That was the point, I understood later. Not mercy. Evidence. Lady Vess wanted the whole court to watch the taster kneel beside the master taster's shroud and understand that grief was now a crime scene. Two guards flanked me. A clerk with ink-stained fingers recorded the time of my viewing in a ledger that would outlive us both.

Sefu looked smaller than he had in life. Six years of swallowing poison had taught me that the dead are mostly weight — the thing that made them *them* leaves all at once, and what remains is just a problem of logistics. His hands were folded on his chest. Someone had cleaned the burn from his lips, or tried to; there was still a gray crust at the corner of his mouth like old candle wax.

He had taken the cup from my hand. He had said — I could still hear it, I would hear it until I died — *"Old habit, little ember. Let an old man check your work."* And then he had drunk a compound engineered to eat through the one immunity in the world, and he had held my eyes while it did, because he knew what it was and he chose it anyway, and his last word had been my name.

The clerk cleared his throat. "The taster will return to quarters."

I touched Sefu's folded hands once, briefly, the way you'd touch a hot stove to prove it was real. Then I let them march me back through corridors that had gone quiet in a way I'd never heard before. Courts are never silent. They murmur, they rustle, they breathe gossip like air. This silence meant everyone had already decided.

*The taster poisoned the king's cup. The taster's own mentor is dead. The taster fled the Ember Court once before — we all heard about Thornfell, about the dead smuggler, about the forbidden book.* Vess's version of events had traveled faster than any courier I'd ever been, and it had arrived polished.

By evening there were chalk marks on my door. By morning, someone had scratched *POISONER* into the wood deep enough to splinter.

I sat on my cot in the tasters' wing — the wing that had been my whole world for six years, four rooms and a shared kettle and Sefu's voice calling roll at dawn — and I listened to the building decide what I was.

They came for me on the third day.

* * *

Not guards. Worse: clerks. Four of them, with a warrant bearing the king's scorched signature, which meant it bore Vess's will wearing Aldric's hand. High Treason. Conspiracy Against the Crown. The Poisoning of Master Taster Sefu Okonkwo's Charge — they'd even made my name part of the charge, a small bureaucratic cruelty, as if Sefu and I shared blood instead of only a trade.

"The taster will come quietly," said the eldest clerk, a gray man with the patient face of someone who has processed many executions and enjoyed none of them.

I looked past him, down the corridor, and saw the trap of it plainly. Come quietly, and I'd be in Vess's cells by nightfall, and whatever happened after that would happen without witnesses. Run, and I'd confirm everything. There was no move left on this board that didn't lose.

So I did neither. I said, "May I take my oath-book? It's required for questioning under the Taster's Statutes." Which was true, and which bought me thirty seconds alone in my room while the clerks conferred in the hall about statutes none of them had read.

Thirty seconds is nothing. Thirty seconds is also a corridor, a stairwell, and a decision.

The tasters' wing backed onto the kitchens. It always had — Sefu used to say a taster who couldn't walk into a kitchen unannounced wasn't a taster, he was a hostage. He'd taught me the way when I was nineteen: through the linen store, past the tallow barrels, down the servants' stair that smelled forever of onions and lye. He'd taught me it as a shortcut. Tonight it was a door out of my life.

I went through it with my heart hammering so hard I could feel it in my teeth.

* * *

The kitchens at midnight should have been empty. They weren't.

One lamp burned by the bread ovens, where the night bake was proofing under cloth, and beside the lamp sat a girl I knew by sight and nothing else: Nara, scullery and second-riser, maybe seventeen, flour to her elbows, a book open on her knees — an actual book, cheap print, romances probably, hidden here where no one checked on scullery staff after hours.

She looked up. She saw me — saw the fugitive, the poisoner, the woman whose name the whole court spat now — and I watched her do the arithmetic every servant does instinctively: *trouble, how much trouble, whose trouble.*

"You're her," she said. "The taster."

"I'm nobody," I said. "Keep reading."

"You're not nobody." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd correct someone's knife grip. "Everybody's somebody. That's what they're afraid of." She marked her page with a strip of dough-scraped parchment and stood. "Where are you going?"

"Out."

"Through the scullery?" She shook her head. "Guarded since yesterday. They doubled the yard watch too. Vess thinks you'll run for the river gate." She paused, and something moved across her face — calculation, or courage, or both wearing each other's clothes. "There's another way. The ash chute. From the big oven. It runs to the coal yard outside the wall. It hasn't been used since the old boiler, but—" she caught my expression, "—it's not clean. I know it's not clean."

"How do you know the ash chute?"

"Because I'm the one who cleans it," she said. "Nobody else will. Nobody else can fit."

Of course. The smallest hands get the worst jobs. I had spent six years being small in a different direction — invisible rather than junior — and I had somehow forgotten that the kitchen had its own hierarchy of swallowed insults, its own people doing the work everyone else pretended didn't exist.

"Nara." I said her name and watched her straighten, the way people do when their name is spoken like it matters. "If they find out you helped me—"

"They won't."

"If they do, tell them you found me hiding in the flour store and you ran for the guard and I hit you." I took her wrist, turned it gently, and pressed my thumb hard into the soft flesh until a bruise bloomed there, red then purple, the kind that looks exactly like a struggle. Six years of tasting had given me steady hands; apparently they gave other things too. "There. Now you fought the poisoner and lost. That's not suspicion. That's a story."

She stared at her wrist. Then at me. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, the laugh of someone who has learned that joy draws attention.

"That's horrible," she said. "That's brilliant. Do the other one, it'll look wrong with just one."

I did the other one. And then, because I couldn't carry it and couldn't leave it either, I pulled Sefu's oath-book from inside my coat — the little ledger of every substance I'd ever tasted, every antidote countermeasure, every poison signature, twenty years of Sefu's knowledge and six of mine — and I put it in her floury hands.

"Bury this," I said. "Under the ovens, in the ash pit, somewhere only you know. If I don't come back for it, give it to a scholar named Yew Andrade. Tell him the taster of thorns sent him her regards."

"What if they ask why you gave it to *me?*"

"Because you were the only person in this palace who offered me a way out," I said. "And because the people who write history never think to search the kitchens."

* * *

The ash chute was exactly as bad as promised.

I went feet-first, arms crossed over my chest, down a throat of sheet iron caked with a decade of soot, in absolute dark, breathing through my sleeve while the chute narrowed and turned and scraped skin off my elbows and shoulders. Somewhere in the middle third I stopped being able to tell whether I was falling or sliding and simply became cargo. My ribs remembered the ambush on Thornfell Road. My hands remembered Rook going still against me, heavier than a living man, and I thought — absurdly, uselessly — that Rook would have loved this chute, would have charged tolls for it, would have called it *the queen's throat* and charged double after dark.

Then light, cold air, and I spilled out of the chute mouth into the coal yard behind the palace's north wall, black from crown to boots, coughing, half-blind, alive.

Alive, and free, and completely alone.

I lay in the coal dust for a moment and let myself feel it — all of it, the whole ruinous accounting. Sefu dead in my place. Rook dead on the mountain. Yew missing since the arrests began, whereabouts unknown. The prophecy book confiscated or burned — I didn't know which, and not knowing was its own kind of wound. Every thread I'd ever held cut or fraying, and the court behind me chanting my name like a curse, and ahead of me—

Ahead of me, a coal cart. Unattended, its mule dozing in harness, loaded for the morning delivery to the glassworks by the river. On the cart's bench, wrapped against the cold, a driver's coat.

I am not proud of the coat. I am telling you honestly: I stole it. Invisibility, I had learned, was not a gift the world gave you. It was a trade. You paid in dignity and you paid in other people's property, and you kept moving.

I walked out of the coal yard dressed as a man who hauled fire for a living, and nobody looked at me twice, and I have never in my life been so grateful to be beneath anyone's notice.

* * *

The city had turned overnight. That was the part I hadn't expected — how fast, how total. Vess hadn't just arrested me in absentia; she'd made me a lesson. Broadsheets on every post read THE POISONER'S TREASON in letters tall enough to read from a moving cart, above a woodcut of a hooded figure dripping a vial into a chalice. Children were playing *catch the taster* in the fountain square, shrieking, chasing the smallest kid in the group. An innkeeper refused me water — water, which costs nothing — once he placed my face, and his patrons followed me into the street shouting the word *poisoner* like a bell.

By full dark I understood the shape of it. Vess didn't need to catch me. She needed the city to catch me. A mob needs no trial, leaves no inconvenient prisoner, and absolves the spymaster of everything.

I made the river district by midnight, moving rooftop to cellar to drain like something hunted, and I stood in the shadow of the glassworks' cooling sheds with the black water sliding past and asked myself the question I'd been refusing to ask since the coal yard:

*Go where?*

Thornfell was closed — the passes watched, Rook's people scattered or arrested. Yew's lodgings would be staked out. Every road out of the city had a checkpoint with my woodcut nailed to a post. And underneath all of it, quieter and worse: even if I got out, got clear, got *safe* — Aldric was still burning on that throne. The ember-spirit still wore him like a glove. Sefu had died buying me time, and Rook had died buying me distance, and I would be spending their deaths on nothing if I simply vanished.

I was still standing there, hating the arithmetic, when the small door in the cooling shed opened and a lantern showed me a face I knew.

Nara.

Flourless now, in a boy's cast-off coat, out of breath, holding the lantern up like she'd rehearsed the reveal and hadn't planned what came after.

"You—" I started.

"Took me till dawn to slip the yard, then all day to find which way you'd go, because everyone knows the river's the only road and everyone's watching the bridges but nobody watches the glassworkers' landing." She said it fast, tumbling, the way people talk when they've outrun their own good sense and only now notice. "There's a barge. Coal run, going downriver tonight, the pilot owes the kitchens for thirty years of extra bread and doesn't ask questions. You'll be in Cinders Ford by tomorrow night."

"Why?" It came out rougher than I meant. "Why are you doing this? You don't know me. One conversation over proofing bread. You owe me *nothing*, Nara, and everything you've done tonight, they'll hang you for if—"

"I know." She set the lantern down between us, deliberately, so we were two faces over one small light. "My brother was a taster. Before you. Did you ever hear of Tam?"

The name landed somewhere under my ribs. "There was a Tam," I said slowly. "Before my time. Sefu never spoke of him except—" Except once, drunk on festival wine, staring into the fire: *the best of us, and the fastest, and they buried him in an unmarked grave because the court said his death was his own error.* "Tam was yours."

"He tested a dish for the old queen's cousin. It was poisoned. He tasted it anyway, because that was the job, and he died, and the family got a letter saying the court regretted the inconvenience." Her voice stayed level; only her hands shook, and she let them. "My mother stopped speaking for a year. I grew up thinking the palace was a mouth that eats people and calls it service. And then the whole court starts screaming that *you* — a taster, another one — are the monster, and I look at the broadsheets and I think: they did this to Tam too. Different words. Same lie. Somebody powerful wants this person erased, so this person becomes the villain, and everybody nods along because nodding is safe."

She picked up the lantern again.

"So no, I don't owe you anything. I owe *him* something. I owe every taster who ever swallowed something so a lord wouldn't have to. You're all the same to them — tools with throats. And I'm done pretending otherwise." She jerked her chin toward the landing, where a low barge sat heavy in the water, its pilot smoking a pipe that glowed like a tiny captive star. "Go. Before I talk myself into being sensible."

I caught her shoulder. Under the borrowed boy's coat she was thin as a wren and shaking like one.

"When this is over," I said, "if I'm alive — I'm going to build something. A school. For tasters. Where nobody gets buried unmarked and nobody's death is called an inconvenience. And I want you there. Not as scullery. As faculty."

She blinked at me. Then she grinned, sudden and crooked, seventeen years old again for one second. "Faculty," she repeated, tasting the word like something rich. "All right. But I'm bringing the bread."

* * *

The barge pulled away from the landing with its lantern doused, running dark down the middle channel, the palace shrinking behind us into a smear of torchlight on the palisade. I stood at the stern and watched the Ember Court's glow fade and felt the strangest sensation, one I had no practice with:

I was leaving, and people knew I was going, and they had helped.

Not smugglers paid in silver. Not allies bound by debt or code. A scullery girl with a romance novel and a dead brother, and a pilot who owed his debts to bread. The invisible people. The ones whose names never made ledgers. They had opened their small doors for me one after another, and through those doors I was escaping a court that owned my name, carrying nothing but a stolen coat, Sefu's training in my hands, and Ilsa Okonkwo's letter seared into my memory line by line — the rite, the vessel, the willing throat.

The river bent. Ahead, far downstream, the sky above Cinders Ford carried a faint reddish stain, as if the horizon itself were smoldering.

The pilot knocked out his pipe against the rail. "Ford's changed since you were last through, I'd wager," he said, not looking at me. "Whole valley's full of the Lady's soldiers these days. Digging, mostly."

"Digging for what?"

He shrugged, a slow rolling motion like the water. "Don't dig *for*, girl. They dig *at*. Old vaults, under the mountain. Been at it a month." He glanced at me sideways, and his pipe-hand stilled. "Funny thing, though. The workers say the deeper they cut, the warmer it gets. And last week, the fires they lit underground—" he tapped ash into the river, "—burned blue."

Blue flame. The color of the ember-spirit. The color of the binding circle, if Yew's translations were right, lying somewhere in the dark beneath the mountain — in the one place Vess's soldiers were now excavating with the patience of ants.

I gripped the stern rail and watched the stained horizon come closer and understood that I had escaped the lion's mouth only to be carried, by coal and current, directly toward its den.

Whatever Vess was digging for under the mountain, I had to reach it first.
