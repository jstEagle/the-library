## 16. The Thing Beneath the Mountain

The kitchen girl's name was Sela, and she had given Petra three things before dawn: a loaf of black bread still warm from the ovens, a lamp with half a reservoir of oil, and the location of a door that did not officially exist.

"Old Nan Toma told me when I first came to the scullery," Sela had said, pressing the lamp into Petra's hands with fingers that smelled of lye and onions. "She said, *if they ever come for you, girl, go down. The mountain keeps its own.* I thought she was mad." A pause. "Maybe she was. But mad people know where the doors are."

The door was behind the third wine rack in the undercellar, exactly where a rack should never be — angled wrong, casting a shadow that didn't match its shape. Petra had spent six years learning how buildings lied: which corridors shortened at night, which windows were painted on, which walls breathed. This wall lied badly. It was sweating.

The mechanism was older than the hinges around it. Petra found the seam by touch, then the hollow, and inside the hollow a small clay cup, dry as a bone, and a word scratched into the rim in a hand two centuries dead.

*Taster.*

Someone had left this for them before they were born.

Petra set the cup down, put their palm flat against the stone, and pushed. The wall opened like a mouth deciding, finally, to speak.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the mountain had any right to be deep. Petra counted steps out of habit — tasters count everything, doses and heartbeats and exits — and lost the count somewhere past four hundred, when the walls stopped being cut stone and started being something else. Veined. That was the only word for it. Grey rock shot through with threads of dull orange, like iron rusting inside a living arm, and the deeper Petra went, the warmer the air became, until they had stripped off Sela's borrowed cloak and still felt sweat crawling down their spine.

The oil in the lamp burned strangely. It leaned toward the dark ahead, flame bending like grass in wind, as if something below was inhaling.

Petra thought about Rook. They had been doing that too often lately — summoning him up in the quiet moments, his crooked grin, the way he'd shoved them toward the ravine path and turned back toward the lanterns with a knife already in his hand. Grief was a poison with no antidote; Petra knew every poison, and this was the only one they'd never learned to hold. So they let it sit in them and kept walking, because walking was the only thing grief couldn't stop.

They thought about Sefu, too. About the ledger full of their own handwriting, written by someone else's hand. About the confession that had come after, Sefu on his knees in the safehouse smelling of tallow and old fear: *Vess ordered it. Any true heir to the prophecy had to look false, or there would have been a war over your blood before you were ready. I thought I was buying you time.* He hadn't asked forgiveness again. He'd just looked at Petra with the same eyes that had watched them swallow their first poison at eleven years old — terrified, proud, helpless — and Petra had walked out without answering, because some debts you can't pay in a single night.

He was still at the safehouse. Still waiting. And Vess was still hunting, her writs nailed to every gate in Thornfell, PETRA OKONKWO — TRAITOR AND FALSE PROPHET in letters tall enough to read from horseback.

Down here, though, none of that existed yet. Down here there was only heat, and the slow pulse in the walls, and the growing certainty that Petra was not descending into a place so much as into a body.

The stairs ended at an archway, and through the archway was the circle.

* * *

It was carved into the floor of a vault so vast the lamplight couldn't find its ceiling. Rings within rings within rings — Petra counted seven, each inscribed in a script older than the Ember Court, older than Thornfell, older than anything Yew had ever shown them in the archive. Channels had been cut between the rings, grooves the width of a finger, and in the grooves ran a substance that glowed faintly, sullenly, like embers banked under ash.

Not metal. Not lava. Something between blood and light.

At the center of the circles stood a pillar of black glass, taller than a man, and inside the glass —

Inside the glass was fire. Shaped like nothing. Shaped like everything. It moved without moving, a slow turning mass of light and dark, and when Petra's lamp came within twenty paces, the whole mass *stopped.*

Turned.

Looked at them.

Every animal instinct Petra owned screamed at once. Their legs locked. Their mouth flooded with the phantom taste of every poison they'd ever swallowed — nightshade and arsenic and the bitter silver of wyrm's-bane — all of it rising at once like the body rehearsing its own death. Six years of training said: *stillness. Stillness is survival.* They held it. Barely.

The fire spoke. Not aloud. It had no mouth. The words arrived the way heat arrives, through the skin, sinking straight past the ears into the marrow:

**TASTER.**

Its voice was vast and broken, a bell cracked by centuries of ringing. And underneath the power of it, unmistakable to anyone who had spent six years listening to what people hid beneath their words, Petra heard something else.

It sounded *tired.* It sounded hungry. It sounded like a thing that had been shouting into a sealed room for a hundred years and no longer expected an answer.

"You're the ember," Petra said. Their voice came out steadier than they deserved. "The one wearing the king."

**I AM THE ONE THE KING WAS PUT ON,** the fire said. **LIKE A LEASH. LIKE A BLINDFOLD.**

Petra's hands shook. They made themselves walk forward anyway, one step, another, until they stood at the outermost ring. Up close they could see the grooves weren't channels at all. They were words. The same sentence, repeated ten thousand times around ten thousand feet of circumference, worn thin in places as if it had been renewed again and again across generations.

Petra crouched and read it.

*Guardian, we bind thee to service. Guardian, we bind thee to silence. Guardian, we bind thee to the crown, and the crown to our line, and our line to thy strength, until the debt is paid or the vessel comes.*

"The vessel comes," Petra whispered. Ilsa's letter had ended with those words. Here they'd been carved first.

**YES,** the fire said. **SHE PROMISED ME.**

And then it showed Petra everything.

* * *

There was no choice in it. The knowledge poured into them the way heat pours into a kettle, and Petra dropped to their knees on warm stone and lived a century in the space of a breath.

They saw the mountain young. Saw the ember-spirit as it had been before the binding: vast above the valley, a pillar of warmth standing watch over frozen passes, melting the snows each spring, turning away avalanches with a breath, teaching the first settlers which stones held heat and which springs ran scalding. A guardian. The kingdom's oldest ally, older than any throne — the reason anyone had survived here at all, in the hard centuries when Thornfell's winters ate the unprepared.

They saw the first kings come, hungry men with sharp crowns, and saw the bargain struck: the guardian's strength channeled through the royal line, and in exchange, the guardian fed — not with wood, not with coal, but with feeling. With the court's awe, its gratitude, its festivals lit in the guardian's honor. A fair trade. For a while.

Then they saw the change. Slow as rot. Kings who realized that a bound guardian was safer than a free one. Kings who discovered that if you fed the spirit fear instead of love, it grew *stronger* but *crueler*, and cruelty was useful. That a spirit fed terror would burn the king's enemies without being asked. That a frightened kingdom was an obedient kingdom, and the fastest way to frighten a kingdom was to let it feel the thing beneath the mountain straining at its chains.

Generations of it. Vess's line — Petra saw her great-grandfather's face in the binding-grooves, repainting the circle with fresh cuts, whispering new clauses into the old script: *bind thee to secrecy, bind thee to hunger, bind thee to serve whoever holds the crown, whatever they command.* Each generation tightening the leash, thickening the fear, harvesting the panic like a crop. Plagues allowed to spread to the palace gates so the dynasty could appear to save the realm. Smuggler roads burned — Rook's roads — because independent paths made independent people, and independent people asked questions.

And always, always, the spirit itself, drowning in a century of forced fear, its guardianship curdled into rage, its warmth into the ember that sat in Aldric's chest like a swallowed coal, burning out the man and leaving the crown.

Petra came back to themselves gasping, on hands and knees, tears evaporating off their cheeks before they could fall.

"It wasn't you," they rasped. "You're not the monster."

**I WAS MADE INTO ONE,** the fire said, and the crack in its voice went through Petra like a blade through bread. **FEAR IS A FATTENING FOOD. THEY FED ME UNTIL I FORGOT MY OWN NAME. YOUR ANCESTRESS REMEMBERED IT FOR ME. SHE WROTE IT DOWN WHERE THE CROWN COULD NOT BURN IT.**

"Ilsa."

**ILSA. THE LAST WHO CAME DOWN HERE AS A FRIEND.**

Petra knelt at the edge of the ring, and did the bravest and stupidest thing they had done in a life crowded with both: they reached across the carved line and laid their bare hand on the black glass.

It was warm. Not burning. Warm the way a hearthstone is warm hours after the fire dies, warm the way Sefu's hand had been on the back of their neck the first day at court, saying nothing, meaning *you are not alone in this place.*

The fire did not sear them. Of course it didn't. Immunity ran in their blood, cultivated across five generations for exactly this contact — and now Petra understood that too, understood it in their bones. Okonkwo blood wasn't proof against poison. It was proof against *this.* Against the ember. Every taster in the family tree, every swallowed death, had been a body being slowly built, deliberately built, to stand here and touch this and live.

Ilsa hadn't just written a prophecy. She had bred a bridge.

"Why didn't she free you herself?" Petra asked. "She knew the rite. She had the immunity. Why wait?"

**BECAUSE UNBINDING REQUIRES MORE THAN IMMUNITY,** the spirit said. **IT REQUIRES A WILLING VESSEL STANDING IN A COURT THAT WILL WATCH. FEAR DIES IN DAYLIGHT, TASTER. HER KING'S COURT WOULD HAVE SHIELDED ITS EYES. HER KING WAS ALREADY COMPLICIT. SHE NEEDED A GENERATION WITH CLEAN HANDS AND OPEN ONES.** A pause, and the heat in the vault seemed to contract, gathering close. **AND SHE NEEDED SOMEONE WHO WOULD CHOOSE IT. NOT BE CHOSEN. THE BINDING WAS BUILT ON COMPULSION. ONLY THE OPPOSITE CAN UNMAKE IT.**

Petra sat back on their heels, and laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it at all. Everyone had known. The prophet, the spirit, maybe even Sefu in his guilty heart. Everyone except the one person living the prophecy. Even now, even here, their fate had been arranged by other hands.

But the spirit felt the thought form and answered it before it finished, gently, for a thing made of fire:

**SHE LEFT THE LAST PAGE FOR YOU TO WRITE. THAT IS WHAT WILLING MEANS.**

* * *

Petra stayed a long time after that. Long enough that the lamp guttered and they fed it the last of the oil with hands that had finally stopped shaking. They asked questions and received answers — about the counter-binding Ilsa had designed, chained not to any person but to the land itself, written into the same grooves that once spelled captivity; about what Aldric had been like before, a man who wept at his daughter's wedding and paid smugglers' tolls himself rather than send soldiers to die in the passes; about Vess, and the ledgers, and the long architecture of fear that Petra now held the blueprints of, every hidden clause, every forged signature, every manufactured crisis mapped and named.

The enemy had a shape now. It wasn't the fire in the glass. It wasn't even Vess, not entirely — Vess was the latest hand on a wheel that others had built. The monster was the machine itself: the feeding of fear to keep a throne, repeated so long that everyone inside it mistook the cage for the sky.

*I can end it,* Petra thought, and the thought didn't terrify them the way it had in Ilsa's hermitage. Down here, with the truth of the thing warm against their palm, it didn't feel like sacrifice. It felt like tasting. Like the moment before swallowing, when you know the poison, you know the dose, you know your body can carry it — and you swallow anyway, because the king must eat, because someone has to, because that was always the job and no one ever thanked you for it.

Someone had to carry what the kingdom couldn't.

"When I do this," Petra said quietly, "the court will see. You said fear dies in daylight. So does everything else." They looked up at the vast dark ceiling of the vault, toward the palace far above, toward the throne room where Aldric sat wearing his own body like an ill-fitting coat. "It has to happen there. In front of everyone."

**YES.**

"Then I have to get back inside." Past Vess's checkpoints. Past her writs with Petra's face inked on them. Back into the lion's mouth, wearing nothing but a dead girl's borrowed cloak and the truth. "There's going to be a court assembly soon. Coronation-season politics — even Vess can't cancel those. Every noble house, every banner, every eye in one room." Petra stood, joints aching, and looked at the pillar of glass one last time. "That's where I'll do it. In front of the whole rotten court. I'll show them what they've been eating."

**THEN GO,** the ember said, and for the first time in a century, its voice carried something that was not hunger and not fear. **AND TASTER — WHEN YOU STAND BEFORE THEM, WHEN YOU LIFT THE CROWN — REMEMBER THIS. I DID NOT CHOOSE THE DARK. THEY ONLY EVER SHOWED ME THE DARK. WHAT YOU FEED A THING, IT BECOMES.**

The archway waited behind them, and above it, far above, daylight and danger and the whole machinery of Vess's court.

Petra picked up the empty lamp, climbed the four hundred forgotten stairs, and let the mountain's mouth close behind them.

By the time they surfaced, the bells of the Ember Court were ringing out across the valley — not the assembly peal, not the alarm. The summons peal. Vess had called the full court together, every house and banner, and the criers in the square below were already shouting the announcement into the morning:

*High treason revealed. Trial of the traitor king's protectors. Attendance mandatory for all sworn to the crown.*

Vess had moved first.

Whatever she meant to do in front of that assembled court, Petra would have to stop her in front of the very same audience — or die trying, publicly, in the one room in the kingdom where the truth could still win everything.
