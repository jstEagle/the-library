## 17. Truth in the Throne Room

They gave me back my whites for it. That was the first joke of the day—Master Taster's uniform, laundered and pressed, handed to me by a guard who wouldn't meet my eyes. Penitent taster returned to the fold. Vess wanted me visible in the one role the court trusted, standing where I'd stood for six years, so that when I broke, the breaking would look like madness.

I put on the coat. I let them think it fit.

The throne room had been dressed for the occasion. Not a private audience—the full court, banners of the ember unfurled along the walls, the great hearth behind the dais roaring though the day was mild. Vess had called everyone in. Lords and ladies in ash-grey silk, the kitchen hierarchy in their starched whites, guards ranked like fence posts. She thought she was staging an execution.

She was right about the execution. Wrong about whose.

I had spent the night before in the scriptorium with Yew, laying out our evidence on a table like instruments before surgery. The poisoner's ledger with its forged entries. Sefu's confession, written in his own steady hand three days before he died with my cup in his throat. Ilsa Okonkwo's letter, pulled from her tomb and carried through ambush and fire to this room. And the crown's own decrees—twenty years of them, every signature scorched at the same corner, the same curl of burn that no living man's ink could make.

Yew had arranged them in sequence. A story told in paper.

"Say it plainly," Yew had said, and there was something in their voice I'd been too tired to name. "Don't give her room to breathe."

I should have heard what that was in their voice. Guilt keeps strange hours. It sounds, at midnight, exactly like caution.

* * *

Vess opened the proceedings herself. Of course she did.

"Petra Okonkwo," she said from the steps below the throne, her voice carrying the way voices carry when they've been trained in rooms like this for decades, "stands accused of treason. Of poisoning. Of conspiracy against the crown." She turned to the assembled lords, and I watched her build the scaffold out of sentences. "But the crown is merciful, and mercy requires truth. So today we will hear the taster speak, and the court will judge."

King Aldric sat above her, still as furniture. Twenty years of decrees had come from that hand, all bearing the scorch-mark, and I knew now what sat behind his eyes wearing his patience like a mask. As I walked the length of the room, the king's head turned—a fraction, a tremor—and for half a heartbeat his eyes were not flame but a man's eyes, drowning.

*Unbind me.* He'd whispered it once already, fighting for his own tongue. I meant to answer him in front of everyone.

I stopped three steps below the dais. Every face in the room was aimed at me. Six years I had made myself invisible in this court—head down, cup raised, opinions never. Now every eye knew my name. It felt like standing naked in a snowfield.

"My lord justices. My lady spymaster." I let the title land on Vess like something thrown. "I have tasted poison for this court for six years. I know how it enters a dish. Today I will show you how it entered a kingdom."

I nodded to Yew, who lifted the first decree and held it high.

"This is the Edict of the Ash Tax, signed by King Aldric nineteen years ago." Yew's scholar's voice filled the room, dry and precise. "Note the signature."

Guards brought the decrees down the rows as Yew spoke, one after another, and I narrated the pattern while the lords bent over parchment. "Look at the burn. Same corner. Same curl. Nineteen years apart, different inks, different papers—but the same mark, as if each signature passed through the same fire. No man signs his name in flame. Unless something else holds the pen."

Murmurs moved through the court like weather.

"The kingdom believes King Aldric has ruled since the plague year," I said. "The kingdom is wrong. In the plague year, the dynasty dying, something was bound to the king to keep him alive. An ember-spirit. Old beyond memory, hungry past reason. It has spoken with his mouth ever since. Every edict that taxed you into ash-hunger, that burned the border villages, that fed the deep vaults beneath this mountain—it wrote those. Not your king."

"Lies." Vess said it softly, almost sadly, and it was masterful—the tone of a woman grieving a protégé's collapse. "This is the fever-talk of a smuggler's courier who has read too many forbidden books."

"Then the books won't frighten you." I took the ledger from Yew. "Master Taster Sefu kept a private record, as masters of my guild always have. Read his entries." I let the pages fall open. "Read them, and then tell me what you notice."

A lord near the front did read. His face changed. "These are dated ahead. These dates haven't come yet."

"Yes. Entries in my handwriting, written years before I could have written them, predicting my treasons in advance. Forged—" I looked at Vess. "Forged under orders. Sefu confessed it before he died. He confessed who gave those orders."

"He died protecting you," Vess said. "Grief makes confessions cheap."

"He died taking a poison meant for me. A compound engineered to burn out a very particular immunity. Lady Vess—who designed that poison? Who knew enough about my blood to try to unmake it?" I let the question hang. "Only someone who feared what my blood could do. Only someone who knew about the prophecy. About *me*."

And then I played the card she couldn't counter—or thought I couldn't. Yew brought forward Ilsa's letter, and I read it aloud. The binding circle beneath the mountain. The guardian spirit corrupted by generations of spymasters who fed it fear because a frightened kingdom is a governable kingdom. The rite of unbinding. My great-great-grandmother's last words to a descendant she would never meet: *the gift was always meant to be given back.*

"I am the last Okonkwo," I said. "The prophecy names the taster of thorns. Here I stand. The evidence is on this table, and every piece of it points to the woman standing beside the throne, who has spent twenty years feeding a captive god and calling it loyalty to the crown."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. I watched lords exchange glances. Watched the kitchen girl Mara, near the doors, press her flour-dusted hands together as if in prayer. Watched Vess stand perfectly still, and understood, one breath too late, that stillness was not defeat.

Stillness was patience. She had been waiting for exactly this moment, in exactly this room, with exactly these witnesses.

"You are quite finished?" she said.

"No," said a voice behind me. "They're not."

I turned. Yew stepped forward—not to my side but past me, toward the dais, holding the prophecy book. My book. The book we had carried through ambush and grief, Rook's blood dried on its cover.

"Scholar Andrade," Vess said pleasantly, "you have something to add?"

"I have something to correct." Yew's voice was steady. Their hands were not. "Petra has told you the truth. Almost all of it. But scholarship has a discipline that testimony does not, and the discipline is this: you read to the end of the book."

They opened the prophecy to its final leaves. Pages I had never seen. Pages Yew had shown me once, weeks ago, in a lamplit room, and then translated for me so gently, so selectively, that I had walked away believing the prophecy promised deliverance.

It doesn't. It promises two endings.

"Read it yourself," Yew said to the court, and their voice cracked on the last word, and I knew—I knew with a sick lurch, watching the page pass from hand to hand—that whatever they had hidden from me, they were now hiding nothing. This was not betrayal. This was a confession performed in public, at the worst possible hour, because Yew Andrade could not carry it alone anymore.

The lord reading aloud faltered, then found the words:

*"And should the taster of thorns swallow the ember whole, two roads open from one mouth. By the first, the king is freed and the vessel stands unburnt, and the kingdom is remade. By the second, the king is freed and the vessel does not stand at all—for the ember cannot be refused twice. What it offered Aldric it will offer the vessel, and if the vessel's will is less than the king's grief, the ember will wear a new crown of flesh, and the taster of thorns shall be the next bound thing, and the age of ash shall begin again, deeper."*

He stopped. The silence came back, heavier than before.

"That is the ending Petra has concealed from this court," Vess said, and her voice was warm now, triumphant, generous—the voice of a woman handing a knife to a crowd. "Our brave taster wants the honor of the rite. Wants to swallow the ember before you all and be canonized in smoke. And if they fail—if their will buckles under a hunger older than this kingdom—we will not have a freed king. We will have something worse than Aldric has been. Something that knows every road, every pass, every smuggler's path, wearing a body that cannot be poisoned. Standing right here." She pointed at me. "In front of all of you. Asking for permission."

Every eye in the room changed. I had been a traitor an hour ago; now I was something more frightening. A weapon asking to be swung.

"That's not—" I started.

"It is in the book," Vess said. "In the book *your scholar* has known about for weeks." She turned to Yew, and her smile was terrible in its kindness. "Tell them. Tell the court how long you've known. Tell your friend why you didn't."

Yew stood in the middle of the throne room with their whole life's honesty laid open on their face, and said, "Since the archive. Since the night we met. I read the second ending the same evening Petra read the first. I hid it because—" Their voice broke and rebuilt itself. "Because I was afraid that knowing would make it true. Because prophecy is a door, and showing someone a door invites them to walk through it. Because I loved—" They stopped. Started again. "Because I decided that my judgment was better than their agency. Which is exactly what she did." Yew pointed at Vess, hand shaking. "Exactly what this court has done to that king for twenty years. Decided for someone else what they could bear to know. I became her, in miniature, out of love. And I am sorry. Petra, I am sorry. I should have burned the page or given it to you, and instead I carried it, and carrying it nearly—"

"Enough." Vess's voice cut like a key turning. "The court has heard both testimonies. A taster of proven treason, citing a forged letter. A scholar who admits to concealing the very heresy that damns their cause. Shall we bind the kingdom's fate to these two?"

And the court—God help us, the court wavered. I saw it waver. Fear is faster than truth. Fear travels the nerves before the mind can mount a defense, and Vess had spent thirty years learning to farm that field.

"The ledgers are real," I said. "Sefu's confession—"

"Sefu is dead and cannot be questioned. Convenient."

"The signatures—"

"Curiosities. Burns happen." She spread her hands to the room, reasonable, exhausted, a servant of order beset by fanatics. "Or perhaps"—and here she turned, at last, to face the throne—"we should ask the only witness who was present for every year in question. Your Majesty. You have listened in silence. Is this woman's tale of your captivity true? Are you bound? Has some spirit worn your will for twenty years?"

The trap within the trap. If the king said yes, the court faced a madman on the throne and panic. If the king said no, the last thread of hope snapped in front of everyone who mattered.

Aldric rose.

The hearth behind him flared—no wind, no hand on the bellows, just flame answering flame—and the temperature of the room climbed. The ember-spirit had the king's voice, and I braced to hear it speak through him, smooth and cold, dismissing everything.

Instead Aldric gripped the arm of the throne until the wood groaned, and his face was a battlefield. Jaw locked. Eyes flickering—ember-gold, then wet grey, then gold. His lips moved twice before any sound came, fighting for purchase on his own mouth, and the whole court watched their king wrestle himself in silence.

"Twenty years," he got out. The voice was ruin, gravel and rust. "Twenty years I have stood in this room and watched it rule in my name. I heard every edict. I signed every—every—" His hand flew to his chest, clawing. Gold flooded his eyes. When he spoke again the voice was wrong, layered, resonant as a struck forge: "*Sit down, old man.*"

"No," Aldric said, and forced one foot forward, and then another, descending the dais step by step like a man wading against a river. The guards nearest him shrank back. Vess had gone very still. "I felt the rite named in that letter. I felt the *hope* of it, the way a prisoner feels sunlight through a wall. I cannot perform it. But I can say this much, while I still own my tongue—"

He reached the floor. He stood in front of the court, swaying, burning, a man holding a door shut against a storm with his shoulder.

"She is telling the truth," he said. "Everything. And the second ending is true also. Both things are true. Both roads are real." His eyes found mine across the room, and they were grey, human, drowning, and pleading. "So the question before this court is not whether the prophecy offers a road to ruin. It is whether you trust one person's will to choose the other road. Hers." He staggered. Caught himself on a pillar. "Choose quickly. It is *loud*, tonight. It knows what approaches. It is *afraid*—"

Gold swallowed his eyes. The king's spine straightened into a posture that wasn't his, and the layered voice rolled out across the hall, no longer hiding, vast and amused:

"*Well.*"

Every torch in the room leaned sideways. Somewhere, glass cracked.

Vess recovered first—of course she did; she had rehearsed for every outcome except being believed, and now she was believed, and even that she tried to spend. "You see? You see what sits on that throne! And you would trade it for a gamble on a taster's willpower—"

"I would," said Mara.

From the back of the room, in her kitchen whites, flour on her hands, the girl who owed me nothing and had already given everything. Her voice was small and it carried anyway.

"I watched her take poison for this court six hundred times," Mara said. "I watched her die for a week last month and come back to work. If anyone in this room can hold a fire in their throat and tell it no, it's her. And if she fails—" her chin came up— "then we were ash anyway, weren't we? Slow ash. At least this way we get to choose the fire."

One lord laughed. Then another. Then, unbelievably, someone clapped—two hands, three—and the sound spread through the court the way fear had spread, person to person, faster than argument.

Vess's composure finally, visibly broke. "Guards," she said. "Arrest them both. *Now.*"

No one moved.

The captain of the guard looked at Vess. Looked at the king—standing at his pillar, grey-eyed again, nodding once, slowly, like a man conceding a point after twenty years of losing arguments to himself. Then the captain stepped away from the wall and took up position beside the throne, facing outward.

Facing her.

"There's your verdict, my lady," he said.

Vess looked around the room and found it lost to her—thirty years of careful farming, gone to flood in an afternoon. And I watched her arrive, in real time, at the place desperate people arrive: the only door left is the one behind you.

Her hand went into her coat.

I was moving before I decided to move, six years of tasting other people's deaths translating into speed, and my hand closed on her wrist a half-second before she could bring whatever it was to bear—a vial, of course, glass dark with something that smoked faintly against the air. We stood locked together in the center of the throne room, her strength against mine, her teeth bared an inch from my face.

"You don't understand what you're unchaining," she breathed. "Neither does he. Neither did your precious great-grandmother. The thing under the mountain isn't corrupted, girl. It's *contained*. And the container needs feeding."

"Then we'll find out what it is when it's starving," I said, and twisted the vial from her fingers.

Behind us, the hearth went out.

Not dimmed. *Out*—a hundred-year fire snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, and in the sudden dark every ember-vein in the mountain lit red at once, the walls themselves veining with light, and a voice that needed no throne and no mouth said, in the marrow of every person present:

"*Then come and see what you have chosen.*"

When the torches caught again, Vess was gone. The vial in my hand was empty. And Yew stood beside me in the red-lit dark, holding out the prophecy book with both hands, offering it up like a surrender.

"Read the last page," Yew said. "All of it. Before you decide anything. There's something on it that wasn't there yesterday."
