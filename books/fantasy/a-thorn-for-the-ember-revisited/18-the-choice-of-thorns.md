## 18. The Choice of Thorns

The silence after Vess spoke lasted three heartbeats. Petra counted them the way they counted drops of nightshade in a king's cup—out of habit, out of training, because counting was what kept a taster's hands steady when everything else wanted to shake.

Three heartbeats, and then the throne room broke like a dam.

"Two endings." Lady Vess let the words roll across the assembled court, savoring each one. She stood at the base of the dais with Yew's stolen pages fanned in her gloved hand, and she looked, Petra thought, like a woman who had finally been handed the knife she'd spent twenty years sharpening. "The prophecy your traitor scholar hid from you all. One ending frees the king. The other—" her smile was small and white and terrible, "—the other replaces him. The taster of thorns does not unbind the ember-spirit. The taster *becomes* it. A new crown. A new binding. A new age of ash, with this creature's hand on the tiller."

She turned the pages so the court could see the marginalia—Yew's own cramped cipher, translated now by whatever broken thing Vess had squeezed it out of.

Petra did not look at Yew. They had made themselves not look at Yew since the moment Vess said the word *heresy*, because looking would mean seeing whatever was on Yew's face, and Petra could not afford that yet. Not here. Not in front of four hundred nobles and two hundred guards and one king who was, at this moment, leaning forward on his throne with his jaw clenched so hard the tendons stood out like bridge cables—the look of a man fighting for every word he was about to say.

Aldric got out exactly three of them.

"*Is it true.*"

The voice came out scorched. Half of it was his—rough, human, cracking—and half of it was something older and hotter that moved his lips without quite being him. The court heard both voices braided together and flinched as one body.

"It is true," Vess said smoothly, before anyone else could breathe. "The book says so plainly. And I ask the court to consider: why would the Okonkwo heir hide this? Why would their scholar-collaborator encrypt it? Because they never intended to free you, Your Majesty." Her eyes found Petra like a hawk finding a field mouse. "They intended to *succeed* you."

"They intended," Petra said, and their voice carried further than they expected, "to give me a choice."

The room went quiet again. Petra stepped forward off the flagstone where guards had backed them, past Sefu's empty place—they had not been able to make themselves look at that spot either, not since the kitchens, not since the cup, not since Master Taster Sefu had died on cold tile with Petra's name dying on his lips—and stopped where everyone could see them.

"That's the difference between us, Lady Vess," Petra said. "You've spent twenty years making sure no one in this kingdom gets to choose anything. Not the king. Not the spirit. Not the people who feed your fear-machine under the mountain. And when a prophecy shows up that says someone *might* get a choice, you burn every copy and forge every ledger and murder every courier until the only choice left standing is yours."

"You are accused of treason."

"I am accused of existing," Petra said. "Which is the same crime I've been guilty of since the day Sefu pulled me out of a Thornfell ditch and taught me how to swallow death politely. So let's talk about crimes. Let's talk about the ledgers. Let's talk about who ordered a boy's handwriting forged years ahead of time so that if an heir ever surfaced, they'd already be a fraud. Let's talk about the compound engineered to burn out Okonkwo blood—the one that killed the man who raised me because he loved me more than he feared you."

Sefu's name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Several faces turned away. One of the younger guards, Petra noticed, had gone pale and was staring at Vess with an expression that was no longer loyalty.

Vess saw it too. Petra watched her see it—watched the calculation flick behind her eyes, quick as a card sharp's—and knew the spymaster understood the floor was shifting under her. Which meant the next thing she did would be desperate.

It was.

"Guards," Vess said. "Take the taster into custody pending formal—"

"No."

King Aldric stood.

It should not have been possible. Petra had seen the bindings work up close, had watched him fight for control of his own tongue mid-sentence in this very room, and had learned the bitter arithmetic of it: the ember-spirit held the crown, the crown held the court, and the court held the king, and Aldric got perhaps a dozen free words a day, spent like a miser's coins.

But he was standing. Both hands gripping the arms of the throne hard enough that the old gold was bending. His face was a war—half of it flushed and human and furious, half of it lit from underneath, faintly, terribly, like coals seen through a grate.

"*No,*" he said again, and this time it was almost entirely his voice. "*She does not command my guard. I do. And I say—we hear the taster.*"

The ember-spirit surged. Everyone saw it happen: the king's spine arched, his teeth bared, a thin line of smoke curling from between his lips, and the temperature of the throne room climbed five degrees in a single breath. Nobles stumbled back. Candles guttered sideways though there was no wind.

And Aldric—twenty years bound, twenty years worn, twenty years of watching his own hands sign decrees he hated—took the fire's shove and shoved back.

"*One hour,*" he ground out, through a jaw that was not fully his. "*You have one hour, taster. Use it.*"

Then the flame took him back down onto the throne like a puppet whose strings had snapped and been retied, and King Aldric of the Ember Court sat still and smiling and wrong-eyed while his kingdom decided whether to believe a food-taster over its spymaster.

An hour. Petra turned that coin over in their mind as guards closed around them—not arresting, exactly, but containing, herding them toward a side chamber while Vess argued procedure with a chancellor who had suddenly discovered a spine. An hour bought by a dying man's last strength. It was more than Rook had gotten on Thornfell Road. More than the kitchen girl's cousin had gotten in the cells below. More than most people got, in Vess's kingdom.

It would have to be enough.

* * *

The side chamber was a scriptorium, small and windowless, smelling of ink and cold wax. Two guards took up posts outside the door, and—one small mercy, or one small cruelty, Petra couldn't decide—Yew was pushed in after them, and the door shut on the sound of Vess's voice rising outside like weather.

For a long moment neither of them spoke.

Then Yew said, "I was going to tell you."

"I know."

"You don't—"

"Yew." Petra leaned against the writing desk and finally, finally let themselves look. The scholar looked wrecked: ink-stained, sleepless, a bruise blooming along one cheekbone from wherever Vess's people had taken the pages. But their eyes were still Yew's eyes—honest even now, even drowning. "You deciphered a second ending weeks ago. You sat across from me at breakfast and corrected my pronunciation of Old Court vowels and you didn't say it. I know why. I'd have done the same. But don't insult me by pretending the timing was going to be convenient."

Yew's shoulders dropped. "No," they admitted. "There wasn't going to be a good moment. That's the coward's whole arithmetic, isn't it? Wait for a better time until the times run out." They crossed the little room and pulled the disputed pages from inside their coat—copies, thank every god, the copies Yew had made before Vess ever knew they existed—and spread them on the desk between them. "So. No better time. Here it is."

The second ending, in Ilsa Okonkwo's tight century-old hand, with Yew's translation beneath in pencil:

*And should the taster drink the ember and hold it not in sacrifice but in want—should they love the power more than the person they were before it—then the binding shall take as it took the king, and the taster of thorns shall be the next crowned flame, and the kingdom shall trade one prisoner for another and call it salvation.*

Petra read it twice. The words hadn't changed since the last time they'd read them, three nights ago in a smuggler safehouse, when Yew had confessed at last and Petra had thrown a clay cup at the wall and then, because grief always finds its level, sat down and helped finish the translation.

But reading it alone in the dark was different from reading it here, an hour from either throne or pyre, with Yew watching their face for the flinch.

"The rite doesn't know intent," Yew said quietly. "That's the horror of it. It's a door, not a judge. Ilsa wrote it herself—look, here: *the vessel decides nothing; the heart decides everything.* If you go in wanting to be free of this—wanting to matter, wanting to stop being invisible, wanting, gods forgive me, to *win*—the spirit will find that want and pour itself into it like water finding a crack."

"And if I go in wanting nothing for myself?"

"Then maybe the door stays a door." Yew's laugh was thin. "I've spent six months trying to determine the probability of a clean heart, Petra. I'm a scholar. I need numbers. And the number I keep arriving at is that nobody has a clean heart, least of all the person a prophecy has spent a century grooming for a throne."

"Grooming." Petra tasted the word. "You think that's what this was. The immunity. Sefu. All of it."

"I think Ilsa Okonkwo bred a bloodline for a purpose and called it duty, and I think duty is what powerful people call the weight they put on other people's backs." Yew said it flatly, without apology, which was the thing Petra had always loved best about them and resented most. "Your great-great-grandmother gave you a gift and a noose and never asked which you'd rather have."

Outside, Vess's voice climbed another register. Boots in the corridor. Time behaving the way time does in siege conditions—thinning, running through fingers.

"So we run," Petra said.

Yew blinked. "What?"

"We run. That's the option on the table, isn't it? It's why you're really here—you're not showing me the second ending, Yew, you're showing me the *exit*. Rook's old routes. The southern passes before the thaw closes them. We disappear into Thornfell and I stay invisible forever, and the spirit keeps the king, and Vess hunts us for a year or five or ten, and eventually she finds us or she doesn't." Petra straightened off the desk. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me that's not the escape you mapped out in your head while they were marching you here."

Yew didn't deny it. "It's a life," they said. "It's a small life, but it's a life. Hidden villages exist. People survive worse. You'd live, Petra. Isn't that worth—isn't living the point?"

And there it was. The crux, laid bare on a scholar's desk between two dead men's pages.

Petra thought about invisibility. Six years of it, practiced like a craft: the lowered eyes, the forgettable name, the trick of standing in a room so completely unnoticed that guards talked secrets in front of you. Invisibility had kept them alive. It had also, they understood now—standing in a windowless room, accused, exposed, *seen* by four hundred people and a burning king—been a kind of slow death all its own. A taster swallows poison so others can eat. Fine. Noble, even. But somewhere in those six years, Petra had started believing they were the poison. Something to be swallowed and hidden and never, ever tasted.

Rook had died calling their name down a mountain pass. Sefu had died with their name on his lips. Even the kitchen girl, Mara, sixteen years old and owing them nothing, had pressed a bread roll full of cellar keys into their hands and said, *come back and teach me the trade, yeah?* Like it was obvious. Like Petra was someone worth learning from.

Invisible people don't get asked things like that.

"If we run," Petra said slowly, "Aldric dies bound. Maybe not today. Maybe the spirit grinds him down over another decade—but he dies in that chair, wearing that smile, and every decree he signs burns a village or hangs a smuggler or feeds the fear under the mountain. And Vess rules through him until the spirit eats him and she needs a new vessel, and then some other child gets bred for it, because that's how the machine works. We wouldn't be escaping the system, Yew. We'd just be the part of it that got away."

"Yes," Yew said simply. "All true. And if you do the rite, the odds—my honest scholarly odds—are that you die screaming on the palace floor in front of everyone you respect. Or worse than dying. The second ending isn't death, Petra. It's becoming the thing we're trying to kill." Their voice cracked, and they hated it, Petra could tell; scholars hate cracks. "I did not spend six months translating a dead woman's cipher so I could hand my favorite person to a fire. Ask me to run. Ask me and I'll take you out the laundry chutes tonight, I swear it on every book I've ever loved."

The boots were closer now. Someone tried the scriptorium door; the guards outside murmured; whoever it was moved on, but not far. Minutes. Singular minutes.

Petra picked up the page. Ilsa Okonkwo's hand, steady as a surgeon's, a century patient: *the vessel decides nothing; the heart decides everything.*

And beneath it, in a margin note Petra had missed on every previous reading—or perhaps had refused to see—Ilsa had written something smaller, almost casual, the way the truest things often are:

*I was afraid too. Write that down somewhere. The choosing is the courage; the outcome belongs to the fire.*

"You know what I keep coming back to?" Petra said. "The stew. Night one. Somebody put nightshade in the king's stew knowing a taster would catch it—knowing the only way that move makes sense is if they *wanted* the tasting noticed. I spent weeks asking who. Vess, probably, flushing me out. But here's what I never asked: what was I supposed to notice *about myself*? Because that's the night I stopped being furniture. That's the night I became a person things happened to." They set the page down. "Everything since has been the same question wearing different clothes. Who wants the king dead? Who wrote my future in a ledger? Who am I to this story? And every answer comes back the same way, Yew: *you're the one holding the cup.*"

"Petra—"

"I'm not finished. Because here's the thing about loyalty and truth. I thought they were enemies. Loyalty to the court, loyalty to Sefu, versus the ugly truth of what the court is. That's what Vess built her whole machine on—that everyone has to pick a side and both sides cost you something you can't pay back." Petra met Yew's eyes. "But she's wrong. They're the same direction. The truth about this kingdom is that its king is a prisoner and its guardian spirit is a prisoner and its spymaster is the jailer, and the loyal act—the act of someone who actually gives a damn about the people in it—is the true one. Free them. All of them. Even if the price is me."

"Even if the price is *worse than you*," Yew whispered.

"Especially then." Petra smiled, and it hurt, and they let it hurt. "You want my clean-heart probability? Here it is. I don't want the crown. I have never once wanted the crown. I want a school with a kitchen in it and a girl named Mara learning the trade and a road through Thornfell with Rook's name carved on the first milestone. That's what I want. If the fire looks in me and finds that instead of ambition—then maybe the door holds. And if it doesn't"—they shrugged—"then I'll be the last problem this kingdom's jailers ever solve, because I will burn Vess's machine down from the *inside*, and Ilsa's letter says the counter-binding needs a willing vessel anyway, and a willing vessel is exactly what I intend to be."

Yew stared at them for a long, long moment.

"You understand," they said at last, hoarse, "that I'm going to hate this for the rest of my life."

"Good. Hate it loudly. Argue with me right up until the moment I lift the cup. That's your job, scholar—be the second ending's advocate. Keep me honest. If I start enjoying any of this, pull me back." Petra gripped Yew's shoulder. "But when I lift the cup—stand where I can see you."

The door opened. It was not a guard. It was Chancellor Ovid himself, gray-faced, sweating, holding the ceremonial keys to the vault level in one shaking fist—and behind him, filling the doorway, two of the king's own household guard, the old ones, the ones who remembered Aldric before the binding, their faces carrying the particular expression of men who had waited twenty years for permission.

"Taster," the chancellor said. "His Majesty requests your presence at the high altar. Now. Before Her Ladyship regains the room." He swallowed. "He said—he said to tell you the hour is spent, and he chose to spend it getting you *this*."

In his palm lay a scrap of parchment, scrawled in a hand that shook between human weakness and furnace heat:

*Vaults. Third circle. Bring the letter. I remember her — A*

Petra read it twice, and something in their chest that had been clenched since Thornfell Road finally, carefully, began to open.

"Well then," they said, pocketing Ilsa's letter, taking one last breath of ink and cold wax and ordinary air. "Let's go meet the fire."
