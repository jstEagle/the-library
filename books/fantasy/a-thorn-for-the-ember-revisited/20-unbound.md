## 20. Unbound

The first thing Aldric felt was cold.

Not the cold of the throne room's marble, though he knelt on that now, and it bit through his knees. Not the cold of fear, which had been his only companion for twenty years. This was a different cold—the clean, ordinary cold of a body that belonged to itself again.

He was crying. He did not know how long he'd been crying.

The court was screaming. Or some of it was. Others were on their knees in silence, staring at the man who stood at the center of the shattered circle with light leaking out of her skin like cracks in a kiln.

Petra Okonkwo.

Aldric knew the name. He knew it the way a drowning man knows the shape of the shore—something glimpsed through years of water. The taster. The quiet one at the edge of every feast, swallowing death so that he could swallow dinner. He had watched this person for two decades from inside a prison made of his own skull, watching hands that were not his hands sign decrees, watching a mouth that was not his mouth pronounce sentences, and once—only once, in the early years—he had managed to force one word up through the fire and out into the world.

*No.*

It had been at the execution of a grain merchant. The spirit had wanted him to say yes. He had said no, and the effort had cost him three days of consciousness, and the merchant had died anyway.

Now he looked at Petra Okonkwo, standing in the ruin of the broken crown, veins of ember-light crawling up their throat like ivy of fire, and understood what they had done.

*They took my cage onto themselves.*

"Get up," someone said. It was Yew Andrade, the scholar, kneeling beside him, gripping his arm with ink-stained fingers. "Your Majesty. Get *up*. We have minutes, maybe less. The rite isn't finished."

"The rite," Aldric said. His voice came out ruined, scraped raw from disuse. When had he last spoken as himself? Years. A decade. "The rite is done. She swallowed it."

"She swallowed the ember," Yew said. "Not the binding. Look at her."

Aldric looked.

Petra was upright, but barely. Their eyes were open and burning—not metaphorically; there was actual light behind them, gold-orange, the color of a forge seen through a keyhole. Their lips moved without sound. The ember-veins pulsed in rhythm, and with each pulse, the nearest torches flared and guttered, flared and guttered, as if the whole room were breathing with them.

And Aldric recognized the breathing.

Oh, he recognized it. Twenty years of it, from the inside. The slow tidal pull of something vast trying on a small life, testing the seams.

"It's offering her the bargain," he whispered.

"Yes," Yew said. "And she's refusing. So far. But refusal burns fuel, Your Majesty, and she is human, and humans sleep. Ilsa Okonkwo designed a second half to the rite a hundred years ago precisely because she foresaw this. The counter-binding." Yew pressed a bundle of papers into his hands—old paper, brittle, covered in a firm feminine hand Aldric didn't know but somehow trusted on sight. "It requires a king. A free king. There has never been one before. That's why it could never be performed until now."

Aldric looked down at the pages. His hands shook. Partly from weakness. Partly from the unbearable strangeness of holding his own future in his own hands for the first time since the plague year.

"What does it require?" he asked.

"You," Yew said. "Your blood, your voice, your will—all of it freely given, all of it yours. The spirit bound itself to a person because a person could be ruled. But the land—" Yew's voice cracked slightly. "The land cannot be threatened. The land cannot be bribed. The land simply endures. Ilsa wrote: *chain it to the stone and soil, to root and river, and let it be a guardian again, or let it be nothing, but never again let it wear a face.*"

From across the chamber, Petra's voice came—or not Petra's voice; something wearing it, resonant and layered, like a bell struck underwater.

"*Little king.*"

The court went silent.

Aldric raised his head. The thing in Petra's body was looking at him, and its gaze was ancient and amused and terribly patient.

"*You would unmake me?*" it said. "*I saved you. The plague had eaten your line to the bone. You knelt in this room dying, and I offered, and you said yes.*"

"I said yes," Aldric agreed. His voice steadied as he spoke, surprising him. "I was twenty-six years old and my father was dead and my sister was dead and the physicians gave me a month. You offered me a kingdom instead of a grave. Any man would have said yes." He rose, slowly, joints protesting, twenty years of captivity written into his muscles. "That was not the crime. The crime was everything after. The decrees signed in my name. The executions. Lady Vess feeding you fear like a farmer feeds hogs, because a frightened kingdom is an obedient one, and you let yourself be farmed."

The light in Petra's eyes flickered. For half a heartbeat, Aldric saw Petra underneath—young, exhausted, terrified, holding on by fingernails—and his chest seized with a pity so fierce it felt like anger.

"*I am the guardian of Emberfall,*" the spirit said. "*I have guarded it for four hundred years.*"

"You were," Aldric said. "Once. Before men taught you that guarding meant owning." He turned to Yew. "Tell me what to do."

* * *

They built the circle where the old one lay broken.

Yew directed; Aldric worked. He knelt on the marble with chalk and crushed garnet and drew the lines himself, because the rite demanded a king's own hands, and there was no one else, and because—though he would not say this aloud—it felt good beyond telling to do something, anything, with hands that answered only to him.

The design was strange. Not the closed ring of the original binding, which had been a cage, a sealed jar, a prison. Ilsa's counter-binding opened outward. Lines radiating from a center like the roots of a tree, or the veins of a leaf, each terminating in a word carved in the old tongue: *stone, soil, root, river, rain, frost, seed, ash.*

"Eight anchors," Yew explained, kneeling to correct the curve of one line with a trembling fingertip. "Eight aspects of the land itself. The spirit's essence disperses along them. It won't die—I don't think anything that old can die—but it will become diffuse. Present everywhere. Owned by no one. A guardian in truth, if it chooses to be. A rumor, if it doesn't." Yew looked up. "There's a cost. To you, I mean. The rite requires the king's blood in the center, and the king's true name spoken aloud, and—" the scholar hesitated—"the king must mean it. Every word. If there's any part of you that wants the power back, even a small part, even a tired part that misses being important, the rite fails, and the spirit keeps its grip on Petra."

Aldric sat back on his heels.

Twenty years ago, he had wanted nothing in the world more than to matter. He had been the last leaf on a dying tree, and when the fire came and offered to keep the tree alive forever, he had grabbed it with both hands.

Did he still want it?

He thought of the decrees. The tax levied on the Thornfell villages after the smuggler purges, signed with a hand that scrawled his name in letters scorched brown at the edges. The scholar arrested last spring for asking questions about old books. The look in his steward's eyes whenever Aldric entered a room—not respect; relief that he hadn't stopped, that day, to talk to anyone, because everyone knew the king's conversations went somewhere terrible.

"No," Aldric said quietly. "There is no part of me that wants it back."

"Then we begin," Yew said. "And Your Majesty—whatever happens, whatever it says to you, however much it sounds like you—do not stop drawing the lines. The words can wait. The lines cannot."

* * *

The spirit fought him with memory.

That was the horror of it. As Aldric worked his way around the circle, speaking each anchor-word and opening a vein's worth of blood onto the chalk, the thing inside Petra reached into him and pulled out the past like drawers from a cabinet, spilling them across the floor of his mind.

His father, teaching him to hold a sword, laughing when the boy dropped it. *You'll grow into it, Aldie. Kings are just boys who grew into it.*

His sister, Maren, aged nine, daring him to eat a green plum off the palace tree. Both of them sick for two days. Both of them laughing about it for twenty years after—well. Fifteen. Before the plague.

The plague carts. God, the plague carts. He had been twenty-six and the bells had rung for six weeks straight and he had signed burial orders until his hand cramped, and then the fever took him too, and he had lain in this very room watching the ceiling blur, and the air had gone warm, and a voice like a hearth-fire had said: *I can save you. All you have to lose is everything you are.*

He had been so tired. He had been so afraid.

"*You were,*" the spirit murmured through Petra's mouth, and Petra's body drifted closer, feet not quite touching the floor, ember-veins blazing. "*You were so tired. I held you when no one else could. I kept the dynasty alive. Without me there is no Emberfall—there is a footnote, a conquered province, a name in another kingdom's histories. Is that what you want, little king? To be a footnote?*"

Aldric's chalk hand wavered on the seventh anchor. *Frost.*

"Don't listen," Yew whispered. "Lines first."

But the spirit wasn't finished. It leaned in close, and now its voice dropped, softened, became almost gentle, and that was worse than all the thunder.

"*Ask her,*" it said. "*Ask the taster what they saw inside me, in the moment they drank. Ask them whether I am a monster. They know me better now than you ever did. Ask them whether they would condemn me to be scattered over stones and rivers like ashes.*"

And Aldric did stop. Because he had to know.

"Petra," he said. "Can you hear me?"

For a long moment, nothing. The ember-veins pulsed. Torches breathed. Somewhere in the court behind him, someone was sobbing openly—a kitchen girl, Aldric realized, still in her flour-dusted apron, the same girl who had reportedly smuggled Petra out of the palace two nights ago and straight back in again today. Loyalty from unlikely places. The kingdom was full of it, apparently. It always had been. He'd just never been permitted to see it.

Then Petra's jaw clenched, and through teeth gritted against the fire behind them, in a voice that was ragged and human and entirely their own, they said:

"Draw the line, Your Majesty."

A pause. A shudder ran through the small body, and the layered voice broke through for one instant—*they lie, little king, they—*

"—and hurry," Petra added, "because I can't—hold this—much longer—"

Aldric drew the line.

* * *

The eighth anchor was *ash*, and fittingly, it nearly killed him.

By the time he knelt at the center of the completed pattern, Aldric had opened both forearms and his left palm, and the marble around him was slick and dark, and the edges of his vision had gone soft and white. Yew held the final page of Ilsa's letter in front of him, and the words swam, and he had to blink twice to read them.

*Speak your name. Speak it as your mother gave it to you, not as the crown took it. Then release. Do not command. Do not banish. Release. A guardian that is thanked may choose to stay; a guardian that is caged will always be looking for the door.*

His mother had called him *Aldric Emmerich Vale, third of his name, trouble of her life.* He hadn't heard that name spoken aloud in thirty years.

The spirit knew it was over. He could feel it gathering itself behind Petra's eyes, coiling, weighing its options—fight, flee, bargain one last time. The heat coming off the young taster's body was enough to scorch his eyebrows at ten paces.

"*Last chance, little king,*" it said, and for the first time, Aldric heard something beneath the grandeur. Something thin. Something almost like fear. "*Four hundred years I have watched over these hills. Do you truly believe you will survive what comes next? Plague and war and winter, alone, mortal, blind? I am the only reason your house endured. I am the ember in Emberfall. Put out the ember and the fall begins.*"

Aldric considered this. Genuinely considered it, because it deserved consideration, because it was not entirely wrong.

Then he thought about the kitchen girl in her floury apron. About Rook the smuggler, dead on a mountain road for a stranger. About Sefu, whoever Sefu had been to Petra—an old man's love twisted into a weapon by a colder woman. About a hundred years of a dead woman's descendants passing down immunity like a candle flame, waiting for this exact night.

Emberfall had never been kept alive by the thing in his head.

Emberfall was kept alive by people who owed it nothing and stayed anyway.

"Aldric Emmerich Vale," he said aloud, and the old name tasted like his mother's kitchen, like green plums, like a boy's whole unwritten life. "Third of my name. Trouble of her life. I release you."

He opened his bleeding hands and let go.

* * *

He had expected violence. What came was wind.

The ember-veins on Petra's skin flared white, then streamed outward—not light exactly, and not liquid, but something between, pouring off the young taster in ribbons and following the chalked lines like water finding channels cut for it. *Stone.* The ribbons sank into the marble and spread through the palace foundations, and Aldric felt the whole building sigh. *Soil.* Downward, through rock, out into the dark earth beneath the city. *Root.* He could not see where that one went, but somewhere far away he imagined forests drinking. *River, rain, frost, seed.*

At *ash*, the last ribbon hesitated at the center of the circle, coiled once around Aldric's bleeding wrists like a question—

and he bowed his head to it. Gratitude, not surrender. *Thank you for keeping us alive. Now rest.*

It uncoiled. It sank into the ground. And the throne room of Emberfall went, for the first time in twenty years, utterly warm in the ordinary way—no heat, no pressure, just the honest warmth of five hundred human bodies and a thousand candles.

Petra fell.

Aldric caught them. He was closer than anyone else; he was already kneeling; and later he would understand that this was the single most important act of his reign—that the King of Emberfall knelt in his own blood and caught a food-taster out of the air like something precious, in front of the entire assembled court, and no herald announced it and no decree required it.

Their weight was astonishingly slight. Their skin was cooling under his hands. Faint lines of gold remained traced along their forearms and throat, like scars written in sunlight, fading even as he watched.

"Alive," Yew said, pressing fingers to Petra's neck, and the scholar started laughing and crying at once. "Alive. Barely, but alive."

The court was rising to its feet in stages, like a tide remembering which way to flow. Someone began to cheer—a raw, cracked sound from the back, from the servants' side of the hall—and then others took it up, and the sound grew until the rafters rang with it, and Aldric knelt in the wreckage of the circle holding a sleeping taster and wept openly in front of all of them, and found that he did not care, because for the first time in twenty years the tears were entirely, gloriously his own.

"Your Majesty." Yew touched his arm gently. "There will be trials. Vess's network. The ledgers. A kingdom to un-poison, in every sense. It will take years."

"I have years," Aldric said. He tested the phrase like a coin bitten between teeth. "I have *years.*"

He looked down at Petra's face—slack, smoke-smudged, younger than he'd ever bothered to notice before—and made a vow that no scribe recorded and no seal confirmed.

*Whatever you paid tonight, taster, this kingdom will spend the rest of its history repaying it. Starting with me.*

Then the great doors of the throne room groaned open, and a guard stumbled in, gray-faced, still buckling his sword belt, and the cheering died in a wave of confusion.

"Sire," the guard gasped. "Sire—it's Lady Vess. Her tower. The whole tower is burning—from the inside—and there's no one in it, and sire—" he swallowed— "there's writing on the walls. Writing that wasn't there an hour ago. In the old tongue."

Yew went pale. "Show me," Aldric said, and rose—unsteadily, freely, a free man—to see what his kingdom had written while he slept for twenty years.
