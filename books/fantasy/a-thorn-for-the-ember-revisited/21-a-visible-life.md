## 21. A Visible Life

The mirror in the healers' wing of Thornfell House had been Yew's idea.

"You need to see it," they'd said, hauling the thing up three flights of stairs on a handcart, wheezing, indignant at the weight of their own scholarship made flesh. "You keep pretending nothing happened. You have *scars that glow*, Petra. That is not nothing."

So now I stood before it each morning, the way other people knelt at altars, and looked at what the rite had left of me.

The veins ran from my throat down both arms, fine lines of ember-gold beneath the skin, brightening when I was angry, dimming—barely—when I slept. In candlelight they looked like something drawn by a careful hand: rivers on a map of a country only I inhabited. My eyes had changed too, the brown shot through with flecks that caught firelight and held it a half-second too long. Courtiers had started calling it *the ember-glance*, as though naming a thing made it ornamental instead of a wound.

Six years I had been invisible. I had built invisibility like a house, brick by brick, worn it like a coat tailored so well even Lady Vess's spies had walked past me in corridors. And now children pointed at me in the market. Now guards saluted before I gave them my name. Now there was no corridor in the kingdom where I could stand still long enough to disappear.

Some mornings I hated it. Most mornings, if I was honest—and honesty had become a discipline for me since the throne room, since the cup, since Sefu's body folding over the rail with the poison meant for mine—I did not.

* * *

Aldric came to Thornfell House himself, which caused a small political crisis involving forty guardsmen, two heralds, and a kitchen girl named Maren who nearly fainted into the soup she was stirring until the king, lucid and dry-witted and still learning how to inhabit his own mouth again, told her that he had eaten worse from better people.

He walked the grounds with me while the court pretended not to watch. He moved carefully now, like a man relearning a house he'd been locked out of for twenty years. Sometimes mid-sentence he would pause, testing a word, tasting it, making sure it was his.

"You've declined the dukedom," he said. Not a question. The counter-binding had chained the spirit to the land itself—to the deep roots of Thornfell, to the mountain's heart—but Aldric seemed to feel its absence in everything, the way an amputee feels weather.

"I have."

"The estates alone—"

"Would come with tenants who'd bow to me." I watched a gardener across the lawn, deliberately not watching us. "I spent six years being owned, Your Majesty. I'm not going to spend the rest of one owning."

He considered that for longer than a king needed to. That was the change in him; he had twenty years of decisions to unmake and a hunger to make every new one slowly, deliberately, *himself*.

"Then what do you want?" he asked finally. It was the first time anyone with power over my life had asked the question instead of answering it for me.

I looked at the house behind us—Yew's books already colonizing three rooms, Maren's herb bundles drying in every south window, the smell of a place becoming something.

"A school," I said. "For tasters. For healers. For anyone whose work is putting their own body between poison and other people. Nobody taught me. Sefu taught me technique, but nobody ever taught me what it costs, or how to carry it, or that carrying it could be a *choice* instead of a sentence." I heard my own voice harden and let it. "Every taster in this kingdom serves at the pleasure of whoever feeds them. They're fed last, paid worst, buried without names. I want that to end. Not because of a prophecy. Because it's wrong."

Aldric was quiet a moment. Then he smiled—not the court smile, the real one, crooked on the left side where the spirit's fire had scarred him even through possession.

"My grandfather would have called that sedition," he said. "I'm going to call it a royal charter. But I won't put your name at the head of it unless you say yes."

"Put Sefu's," I said, and the words cost more than the rite had. "Master Taster Sefu Okonjo-Ademola, posthumous founder. He earned it. He took the cup." I swallowed. "Put mine underneath, small. *First instructor.* That's all I am."

"That," said Aldric, "is the largest title in the charter," and he signed it that afternoon in ink that scorched very slightly, out of old habit, though the ember was gone from his hand now. Some marks stay.

* * *

The first students arrived in spring: nine of them, and I remember every face.

There was Tam, a kitchen boy from Emberhold whose palate could identify eleven bittering agents blindfolded and who had been beaten by his last master for showing off. There were the Widow Kesh's daughters, herbalists from the river country who wanted formal standing because women who knew poisons were called witches and women with *certificates* were called doctors, and Kesh had done the arithmetic on which one got burned. There was a former smuggler named Dossa who had run Rook's road and knew every deniable trail through Thornfell, and who arrived with a letter of recommendation written in Rook's cramped, misspelled hand—a letter I read four times in private before I could breathe properly again.

Rook was dead. That hadn't changed with the binding or the charter or any of it. Grief does not honor treaties.

But his road did live. That was the strangest mercy of that first year. With Vess arrested and her network of fear pulled up root and branch, the routes she'd forced underground—the smuggling trails, the toll-paths, the whole shadow economy that had kept mountain villages fed when crown tariffs starved them—came blinking into legality. Aldric sanctioned the main pass as the Rook Road, formally, with way-stations and fair tariffs, and Dossa wept openly at the ceremony, and I stood beside her glowing faintly at the wrists and did not weep until later, alone, where nobody could make a symbol of it.

Rook would have hated the ceremony. He'd have hated the name most of all—"putting my name on a government road, Pet, that's the most insulting thing that's ever happened to me"—and then he'd have gotten drunk on the tariff revenue and forgiven everyone. I tried to hear his voice saying it. Some days I could. Some days the dead go quiet exactly when you need them loudest.

Yew stayed. I never actually asked; they simply stopped packing. One morning in early summer I noticed that their books had migrated from traveling trunks to shelves, that their spare spectacles lived in a drawer, that they had opinions about the kitchen's cardamom supplier. Belonging, I was learning, rarely announces itself. It just starts leaving its things around.

"It's inefficient, having me travel," Yew said, when I finally observed the obvious. "Everything I need to cross-reference is here. Your library is catastrophically incomplete, but it has load-bearing potential." A pause. Spectacles adjusted. "Also I've started a class. On archival integrity. Four students. Don't make it strange."

It was already strange. Yew Andrade, who had hidden the second ending from me, who had carried that guilt like a stone in the cheek through the whole terrible autumn—who had stood in the throne room when Vess tore the secret open and watched me choose anyway—we lived side by side with the shape of that silence between us, and neither of us pretended it wasn't there, and neither of us bled it daily either. Forgiveness, I had decided, was not a door you walked through once. It was a room you kept choosing to stay in.

Though some nights Yew worked late in the archive, and I would bring tea, and find them hunched over the prophecy book—the real one, the one we'd saved from Vess's fires—and I would see their shoulders tighten, and know they were reading the second ending again. *And should the vessel falter, or falter not, the ember endureth, and taketh.*

We had altered the future. I believed that. Mostly.

* * *

The school found its rhythm the way bodies do: awkwardly, then suddenly, completely.

I taught tasting the way Sefu should have taught me. Technique first—palate training, antidote preparation, the brutal memorization of a hundred compounds—but alongside it, always, the other curriculum. *What consent looks like. How to refuse a cup. What the law now says, and what to do when the law is ignored.* The Charter of the Tasting Guild, drafted jointly by Aldric's chancellery and by students barely older than apprentices, established for the first time in the kingdom's history that a taster's service was voluntary, compensated, and revocable. Tam cried when he read it. He thought it was a beautiful fiction. By autumn it was precedent; a northern baron who forced his taster to drink against her will found his grain licenses revoked within the month, and the story spread faster than any decree.

I also taught the thing nobody else could.

Once a week I sat with any student who wished, and we practiced *refusal*—the specific, difficult art of declining to be useful. Because that was the poison that had actually run through my life, six years of it, tasteless and odorless: the belief that my value lay in what I could absorb for others. Sefu had drunk from that cup too, in the end, and I honored him for it and refused to canonize it. His death had been love. It should not have been necessary. Both things true at once; I had stopped demanding the world resolve neatly.

The ember in my veins helped, oddly. When I demonstrated antidotes, the gold lines along my arms would warm and I could show students—visibly, undeniably—what a body under strain looked like. *This,* I told them, tapping the brightest vein at my wrist. *This is what six years of swallowing other people's danger costs. Learn to see it in the ones who don't wear it where you can read it.*

Maren ran the kitchens and the infirmary both, having discovered in herself during the escape a genius for triage that no academy would ever have admitted her to. She and Yew argued constantly about dosages and documentation and whether footnotes were a moral obligation (Yew: yes, absolutely, non-negotiable; Maren: *nobody dying ever read a footnote*), and the arguments drew students like fires draw moths, and somehow the school's actual pedagogy emerged from the wreckage of these disputes, and I mostly stayed out of it and was quietly delighted.

* * *

In late summer, a delegation came from the southern courts—a foreign king's envoy, sniffing around the charter, wanting to know if the famous ember-tasted taster could be hired, bought, borrowed, replicated. I received them in the school's front hall, beneath the framed ledger page from Sefu's study—the forged entries, kept as a lesson. *Know your own handwriting*, the placard beneath it read. *Others will try to write with it.*

The envoy was polite in the way of men who have never been told no by someone they consider furniture.

"The Crown of Ashvale would compensate handsomely," he said. "Your particular... constitution..."

"Is not for sale," I said. "Neither is the school's method. We train our own and we train them to stay free. That's the entire point."

"You must understand, the offer is—"

"I understand perfectly. You'd like a taster who can't die of your enemies' hobbies. Ashvale's king wants my blood without the inconvenience of my opinions." I let the ember rise in my forearms, slow and deliberate, and watched the envoy's composure develop a limp. "You may tell him the immunity isn't in the blood alone. It's in the choosing. He can't buy that, and if he tries to breed for it, he'll get exactly what Vess got. Would you like me to describe what Vess got?"

He would not. They left before noon.

Afterward Yew found me on the steps, breathing a little hard, veins still banked gold.

"You enjoyed that."

"Enormously."

"Good. It's healthy." They sat down beside me, unfolding their long frame onto stone steps built for grander posteriors. For a while we watched the students in the practice yard—Tam running the blind-tasting trials, calling out compound names with the fierce joy of someone finally allowed to be excellent in public.

"There's something else," Yew said, and my stomach did the old drop, the auction-house drop, the floor-going-liquid drop, because I knew that tone. Sixteen years of scholarship in that tone. "The archive catalog. From the vaults under the mountain. I've been cross-referencing Ilsa's marginalia against the original binding circle inscriptions." They paused. "Petra, the counter-binding holds. I'm certain it holds. But Ilsa's letters—the ones we recovered—they reference a *correspondent*. Someone she wrote to, near the end. Someone who knew about the vessel rite and disagreed with it strongly enough that she coded the name."

"Coded it?"

"An acrostic. Across seven letters." Yew took off their spectacles, cleaned them unnecessarily, put them back on. "It spells *V-E-S-S*. Not our Vess. Her grandmother."

The courtyard noise went on—Tam's voice, the clink of cups, Maren laughing at something—all of it suddenly very far away.

"Her grandmother knew Ilsa," I said slowly. "Knew the rite. Disagreed with it. And then a generation later, her family is feeding the ember-spirit fear, keeping it bound to kings, building the whole rotten system." I looked at my own arms, the gold rivers catching afternoon light. "Which means Vess didn't invent anything. She inherited it. The way I inherited Ilsa's gift, she inherited the opposite."

"Yes," said Yew. "And there's one more thing. Among the correspondent's letters—which we don't have, only Ilsa's replies—there's a reference to *a second book*. Written after the prophecy. Ilsa calls it, in her own hand, 'the answer.' We have the prophecy, Petra. We have never had the answer." Yew looked at me, and behind the guilt of the second ending and the stubbornness of sixteen years in archives there was something I had learned to recognize as excitement wearing a disguise of dread. "I think it exists. I think it's findable. And I think Vess knew, and that's why she burned every book she could reach—not to destroy the prophecy."

"To destroy the reply," I said.

Below us, oblivious, Tam correctly identified monkshood in the fourth cup and raised both fists to the sky, and the practice yard cheered, and the world went on being reborn in small ways while the past cleared its throat at the edge of the light.

Yew was already reaching for their notebook.

"So," they said. "How do you feel about a research trip?"
