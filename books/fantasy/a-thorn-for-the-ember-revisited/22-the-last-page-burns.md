## 22. The Last Page Burns

The fire in the school's common room was ordinary, which was the whole point of it.

Petra had learned, over a year of unlearning, to love ordinary things. Ordinary fires that burned only wood. Ordinary bread tasted first by no one but themselves. Ordinary mornings when their hands shook for no reason except that hands sometimes shook, and there was no spirit in the shaking and no prophecy either—just a body remembering what it had carried and setting the memory down again, day by day, like a load set on a table and picked back up.

The book lay on the table beside the bread.

It had come through fire once already—the auction house blaze Vess set, the smuggling run where Rook died with his hand still hooked in Petra's belt, pulling them toward a road he'd never walk himself—and it had come out of all of it smelling of smoke and old tallow, its leather cover scarred pale in the shape of Petra's fingers. *A Thorn for the Ember*, Ilsa Okonkwo had titled it, in handwriting so like Petra's own that Yew had once laid the two side by side and gone quiet for a long time.

One page remained unread. The last one.

Petra had been saving it the way you save the last dose of a medicine you're not sure you've finished needing.

Yew sat across from them now, three years of decipherment stacked in annotated margins, spectacles pushed up into grey-streaked hair, watching the fire instead of the book. They had learned, in this past year, when to wait.

"You could just not read it," Yew said at last. "Archive it. Let some future scholar earn the privilege of losing sleep."

"It showed a future."

"All futures it showed are behind us now." Yew turned, and the firelight caught the ink stain on their knuckle that never seemed to wash out. "Except whatever that last page says. Which is also behind us, if we're standing in front of it."

"That's the most scholar thing you've ever said."

"I'm trying to give you an out. You never take them. I keep offering out of principle."

Petra smiled. It still surprised them sometimes, how easily it came now—smiling, being looked at while smiling, letting the smile be witnessed by someone who would remember it. For six years they had practiced being forgettable. A year of being seen had not yet worn the habit off entirely; some mornings Petra still caught themselves angling their shoulders away from doorways, erasing their own footsteps out of pure economy. But the mornings were fewer.

They opened the book to the final page.

* * *

Ilsa Okonkwo's hand was steadier here than anywhere else in the manuscript, as if she had written this part sitting down, unhurried, certain her descendant would arrive eventually the way water arrives at the sea.

*To the one who swallowed the sun:*

*I have tried, in these pages, to tell you what will happen. I apologize for it. Prophecy is a coward's word for a guess made loudly. What I knew, I knew because my mother knew, and hers before her, each generation adding its observations to the ledger until the pattern seemed fixed as law. We told ourselves we were reading fate. We were reading our own habits, written large.*

*Here is what I saw, and here is why I am wrong about it:*

*The ember, once bound into Okonkwo flesh, will hunger. It will offer the bargain it always offers—power, permanence, a crown of light no blade can reach. And the vessel, having paid so much to carry it, will find the price of refusing almost too steep to bear, because refusal means remaining only human, only mortal, only tired, in a kingdom that will ask everything of you anyway.*

*I saw a taster who took the bargain. I wrote it down because fear writes loudest. But I am writing this last page to say: the ending is not in the ember. It is in the throat that swallows it.*

*You refused. You are refusing now, or you refused yesterday, or you will refuse tomorrow—I cannot know your hour, only your answer, because I raised your mothers' mothers to answer the way you did. Not with ease. With cost. Always with cost.*

*So burn this page. That is the final instruction of this book, and the only one that matters: do not let my fear become your map. I gave you the gift. The rest of the story belongs to whoever is holding the pen when I stop writing.*

*Which is you.*

*Ilsa*

Petra read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way they used to test wine—not for poison now, just for truth, holding each sentence on the tongue.

"She knew," Yew said quietly. "All along. She wrote the prophecy and then wrote its funeral."

"She wrote me a permission slip." Petra's voice came out rougher than expected. "Twenty years early. A hundred years early."

"Is that what it feels like?"

"No." Petra closed the book. The cover was warm from their hands, or from something else—it was often hard, still, to say which warmth belonged to whom. "It feels like finding out your father wasn't angry at you. He was scared for you. Same face. Different weather."

They thought of Sefu. Some mornings the grief for him arrived dressed as anger, and Petra let it visit but not stay. He had forged entries in their hand under threat. He had taken a cup meant for them and drunk it to the dregs and died teaching them, one last time, what a taster's oath actually weighed. Forgiveness had come slowly, arriving not as a decision but as a season—coming in like spring, unnoticed until the frost simply wasn't there anymore.

"Yew," Petra said. "Hand me the candle."

"You're really going to—"

"Ilsa left instructions. I've followed worse ones."

* * *

The page burned slowly, the way old paper does, reluctant, curling like a leaf remembering autumn. Petra held it over the ordinary fire and watched Ilsa's fear turn to light and then to ash, and felt—nothing grandiose. No thunder in the veins. Just the specific lightness of a weight identified and set down.

The ember stirred inside them, faintly, the way it always did around flame. A year ago that stirring had felt like a wolf testing a fence. Now it felt like an old dog lifting its head at the sound of a familiar name. *You burn my history,* it observed, in the voice that was not a voice, the pressure that lived behind Petra's sternum.

"I'm burning a guess," Petra answered silently. "Your history stays. Your chains stay too—the land holds those now, not me. Aldric made sure."

*The king dreams of mountains,* the ember said, which was as close as it came to sentiment. *I dream of hearths. We are better apart.*

"We are," Petra agreed, and fed the last corner of the page to the flames.

Ash settled on the table like grey snow. Yew swept it into a small dish with the ceremony of someone who understood relics—half the school's incoming students would want to see it, and Yew would tell them what it was with scholarly relish and no context whatsoever, which Petra had decided was fair.

"So," Yew said. "That's the end of the book."

"That's the end of her book."

Yew went to the shelf by the window and brought down the other one—the new one, bought plain from a stationer in Thornfell village, unlined pages, a spine that hadn't learned any creases yet. They set it beside the ashes.

"I ordered a pen for you months ago," Yew said. "Kept forgetting to give it to you. Or kept choosing not to. Same thing, in scholarship."

The pen was good oak, cut for a hand smaller than Aldric's, heavier than a taster's brush. Petra turned it over. Six years of writing other people's ledgers—Sefu's inventory, Vess's coded reports, smugglers' tallies in chalk that wiped away clean. Every mark they'd ever made had belonged to someone else's account.

They opened the new book to the first page.

For a long moment nothing came. The blankness stared back with all the confidence of a thing that has never lied to anyone. Petra had faced poisoned stew, crown assassins, an ember-spirit's bargain offered in the private theater behind their own eyes, and none of it had been as frankly terrifying as an empty page.

Then they began to write.

*First entry. Petra Okonkwo, taster—retired title, permanent trade.*

*A year ago I could not enter a room without calculating exits. Today I taught fourteen students the difference between bitterleaf and widow's gentian, and one of them, a kitchen girl named Ama—yes, that Ama, yes, she stayed, yes, she asked—asked me what the oath should be now that the crown has no need of our mouths. I told her the oath is the same as it ever was: nobody eats danger on my watch. The crown was only ever one dinner table among many. We serve the table now, not the throne.*

*The king sent word again about the northern harvest. He signs his own decrees now, unsteadily, a hand relearning itself after twenty years of another's grip. His letters come with scorch marks where his candle catches the page—he laughs about it in a way that tells me he isn't laughing. I write back: the marks mean you're real. Keep them.*

*Rook's road is a trade road now. They want to name it after him. I voted against it—he'd have hated the ceremony and loved the tolls—but I lost the vote, which is the correct way to lose anything.*

*To whoever reads this, whenever this is found: there was a prophecy once. It got one thing right and everything else wrong. The thing it got right was me. Everything else was weather.*

*Ilsa asked me to burn her last page and start mine. Consider both done.*

*Entry the first. Nothing here is fated. Everything here is true.*

Petra set down the pen. Outside, the morning bell rang for lessons, and somewhere below, thirty voices began the recitation of the antidote tables in the ragged chorus of students who half-know their work—a sound Sefu would have corrected, and then loved.

The ember shifted, warm and contained, a coal banked for the night rather than a fire waiting to escape. *What shall we do today?* it asked, and a year ago the question would have chilled them; now it sounded almost like companionship, two survivors sharing a house.

"The same thing as yesterday," Petra said aloud, closing the new book on its first blank-edged truth. "Somebody has to taste breakfast."

They rose, and the fire burned on, ordinary and sufficient, and the ash of the last prophetic page lay cooling in its dish—a small grey ruin, worth exactly nothing, which was finally the same as saying it was free.
