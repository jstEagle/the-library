## 1. The Boy at the Door

The storm came down off the Thornfell like something with intent, and Lena Hartwell stood at the gatehouse window watching it eat the road he had spent twenty years pretending was just a road.

Snow filled the pass from wall to wall, erasing the switchbacks, the cairns, the old boundary-markers that no honest map had ever acknowledged. It erased, too, the ruts of the smugglers' sledges — though by morning those would be cut fresh again, deep and confident as handwriting. The first caravan of the season was due within days. They always came when the Crown's riders hibernated in their forts, when the snow made the high passes lawless and therefore free.

Lena had traded on that freedom for two decades. Salt and tin going up, brandy and uncut garnets coming down, and never a question asked, because questions were a currency he could not afford to spend. In exchange for his silence — his silence, specifically, his word that the gate behind the gatehouse had rusted shut and would stay that way — the men of the caravan treated him as one of their own. Dario brought him tobacco. Old Vesna called him *uncle* in the fond, mocking way she called no one else anything. He had built a quiet life atop a buried crime, and the mortar of it was this: nobody looked too hard at the sealed arch, and Lena looked at it never.

Then the ward-stones flared.

He felt it before he saw it — a heat against his breastbone like a struck match held too close. Twenty years since they had done more than murmur. He crossed the room in three strides and there they were, the ring of standing stones that framed the inner court, each one veined now with light the color of a coal's heart: not gold, not red, but the deep ember-orange that Lena had only seen once before, the night everything ended and he swore it never would again.

They were singing. Low, subsonic, felt in the teeth rather than heard.

"No," Lena said aloud, to the stones, to the storm, to whatever had woken them. "No, you're finished. You were finished."

The light pulsed, patient, indifferent to his denial.

He pulled on his wolfskin coat anyway. That was the thing about dread; it moved your limbs for you.

* * *

He found the boy a quarter-mile up the goat-track, half-buried already, a small dark shape the storm was trying to file away into the mountainside. Snow-blind — eyes screwed shut, lids cracked and bleeding frost — and walking anyway, arms out, feeling for the path foot by foot as if some instruction older than sense compelled him forward. His lips were moving. Even through the wind, Lena caught the sound of it and felt the ground tilt under his boots.

Not prayer. Not any tongue the passes spoke. The words were vowels like banked fire, consonants like cracking stone — the Old Tongue, the language of a people who were ash and legend both, spoken now by a child no older than nine or ten walking out of a dead country's direction.

Lena took him up without deciding to. The boy weighed nothing. Through the torn wool of the child's coat, warmth bled into Lena's chest — not body heat, *other* heat, the same ember-light as the stones, dim but stubborn, a coal carried in a paper wrapper.

"You're safe," he lied, because it was what you said to children, and because saying it might make it true, and because the alternative — *you are the worst thing that could have arrived at my door* — was not a thing one said to a half-frozen boy. "I have you."

The boy's cracked lips shaped an answer. Lena did not let himself translate it. He knew he could. That was the trouble.

* * *

The gatehouse kitchen was the warmest room in the pass, all iron stove and low ceiling, hung with hams and bundles of dried yarrow that Vesna swore by and Lena privately doubted. He stripped the boy's frozen outer clothes, chafed life back into his fingers, fed him broth in slow spoonfuls while the child shivered and stared at nothing with scoured eyes.

When the blindness eased near midnight, the boy looked around the kitchen, and then at the inner door — the one that led back, back, back to the arch — and said, clearly, in an accent twenty years buried:

"The stone is waking."

Lena set down the spoon very carefully, so as not to hear his own hand shake.

"Where did you learn to speak like that?" he asked in Trade, giving the boy the courtesy of pretending he hadn't understood.

But the boy answered in the Old Tongue again, unhurried, as if Lena's pretense were a courtesy returned: *"You speak it. You spoke it at the gate. I heard you through the winter. All of us heard you."*

"I don't know what you're talking about."

*"The covenant is breaking."* The boy's voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it — the flat certainty, a child reciting arithmetic. *"The long winter is not weather. It is the realms forgetting how to end their sentences. My people went through and the door closed on a lie, and a lie does not hold forever. It holds twenty years."*

Lena stood. Walked to the window. The storm had thinned enough to show stars, hard and cold over the pass, and far below, strung along the switchbacks like embers of an entirely ordinary kind, torchlight.

The caravan. Early. Three days early.

"Dario," Lena breathed, though Dario was still miles off, and hated himself a little for the relief in it.

Behind him the boy said, in Trade this time, carefully learned: "Who comes?"

"Friends," Lena said. And then, because the ward-stones were still glowing faintly through the floor seams and the boy deserved one true thing: "Friends who must not see you."

* * *

He hid the boy in the old smoke-cellar behind the salt barrels, where the walls were stone so thick even the ward-song went quiet, and then went out into the blue hour before dawn to meet the sledges with a face he had practiced for twenty years.

Dario embraced him first, as always, snow in both their beards. "Early," Lena said.

"Roads were good. Ice holds, we push." Dario pulled back, and his eyes — shrewd, fond, forty years of mountain in them — went past Lena's shoulder to the gatehouse. "You're pale, uncle. Something happen?"

"A wolf took a goat. I've been up all night."

It was the first lie of the season, smooth as poured tallow, and Dario laughed and clapped his shoulder and believed it, because believing it cost him nothing. Lena helped haul sledges out of the drifts. He drank the ceremonial swallow of plum brandy. He watched Vesna argue tariff with a sledge-driver and thought: *I love these people. God help me, I love these people.*

And then he saw the ones dismounting at the rear of the column.

Six riders in plain furs, riding like soldiers, keeping apart from the smugglers' easy sprawl. No brands, no cargo, no place in the order of things. One of them — tall, gray-eyed, a scar through one brow — turned and looked directly at the gatehouse, and then at the ring of standing stones, and Lena watched her register the faint residual glow with the expression of a woman reading a sentence she'd been hunting for years.

Inquisitors. Riding under smuggler cover, on Dario's own sledges, up roads the smugglers had sworn were clean. His allies had sold the pass. Or been sold, which amounted to the same arithmetic.

Lena kept his hands loose at his sides and his heart beating slow and walked back into the gatehouse as if nothing in the world had changed, and behind the salt barrels, in the dark, a child waited who could undo twenty years of silence with one breath.

The gray-eyed woman followed him at a distance, unhurried, letting him feel the weight of her attention.

So much for the quiet life, Lena thought. The stones flared once more through the floorboards — bright, insistent, unmistakable — and her stride lengthened behind him.
