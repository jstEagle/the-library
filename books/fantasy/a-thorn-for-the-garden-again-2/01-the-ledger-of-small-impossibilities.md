## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The undercroft smelled of tallow, dust, and old bargains.

Edmund Sterling had worked beneath the Guildhall of Impossible Things for nine years, and in all that time she had never once been late. She arrived before the first bell, while the guildmasters above were still breaking their fast on honeyed bread and other people's fortunes, and she descended the worn spiral stair with her lamp held high, into the dark where the ledgers lived.

There were two hundred and eleven of them. She knew because she had counted, though counting was not strictly her duty. Her duty was to keep them *balanced*—a word that meant something very different down here than it did in any merchant's house above. In the undercroft, to balance an account was to hold a life steady at its seams, like a seamstress pinning a garment that would otherwise come apart and spill whatever was inside it onto the floor.

She lit the three lamps at her desk, hung her grey coat on its grey peg, and opened last night's work to the page she had marked with a ribbon the color of dried blood.

*Account 4471: Nine years, traded by one Maren Voss, laundress, to the Guild's agent in Harrowgate, in exchange for her son's fever breaking. Nine years received in good order. Nine years stored. Interest accrued: none. Years do not bear interest. Years only diminish.*

Edmund dipped her pen and wrote the nightly confirmation beneath it in her small, even hand:

*Nine years verified intact. Stored, Row Twelve, Shelf Three, Jar Ninety.*

Then she went down the rows herself, past the shelves of jars that gleamed faintly in the lamplight—not glass, exactly, but something like glass that had once been alive—and checked the ninth jar in the third shelf of the twelfth row. The years inside shifted when she looked at them, a slow amber swirl, like honey stirred by a sleeping hand. They were warm. Traded years always were, for the first decade or so. It was only later that they went cold, and colder, until they matched the temperature of the stone around them, and no one wanted them anymore except the Guild, which wanted everything.

Maren Voss had sold nine years to save her son. Somewhere in Harrowgate, presumably, a boy was breathing easy tonight, and somewhere in this undercroft, his mother's future sat in a jar, dimming by degrees.

Edmund wrote it all down. That was the whole of her office: to write it all down, so that nothing—no year, no name, no debt—should ever be lost or doubled or spent twice. The Guild traded in impossibilities, but it was mortally afraid of arithmetic errors. A stolen crown could be forgiven. A ledger that failed to tally could not.

* * *

By midnight she had confirmed forty accounts. She saved the difficult ones for last, as she always did, working through them in ascending order of unpleasantness, the way some people eat a meal.

Account 4503 was a bottled name. Names were rarer than years, and crueler. This one belonged to a soldier from the border wars who had sold the name he was born with—the whole of it, every memory in which that name appeared—to the Guild for enough coin to buy his family out of debtors' prison. He walked out of the Guildhall a man with no childhood, no mother calling him home across a dark field, no grave to visit. He had chosen it himself, which was what Edmund always reminded herself when the jars made her stomach turn. They chose. That was the Guild's great defense, repeated like a prayer in every office above: *no one is compelled; everyone signs.*

But someone had to write down what the signing cost, and that someone was her.

She unstoppered the bottle—a squat clay vessel sealed with wax and stamped with the Guild's sigil, a door with no handle—and let the name breathe against the verification charm. It hummed. Whole. She recorded it, restoppered it, and shelved it between a widow's grief (sold in installments) and a musician's perfect pitch (pawned, redeemable within ten years at ruinous interest).

Then there was the door.

At the far end of the undercroft, past the last row of shelves, past the iron gate that separated the counted things from the uncounted ones, stood a door that must never be opened. It had no number. It had no account. It appeared in no ledger, which in the Guild of Impossible Things was itself an impossibility so profound that Edmund had long ago stopped trying to think about it directly, the way one stops pressing a bruise.

Her duties regarding the door were simple and absolute. Each night, she verified the seven seals upon it. Each night, she recorded the verification in the black ledger that lived chained to her desk. Each night, she wrote the same sentence she had written for nine years, in the same hand:

*All seals sound. Nothing has passed. The door remains shut.*

And each night, before writing it, she looked at the door for a moment longer than her duties required, because some part of her—the part that kept her up some nights staring at the ceiling of her narrow room above the chandlery—did not believe the sentence entirely.

The seals were sound. She verified them honestly; she was honest in the way that dull water is clear. But the door *hummed*. Not audibly. Not in the ear. It hummed somewhere behind her sternum, a note so low it was less a sound than a pressure, like the memory of thunder felt through a floor. And lately—it had begun sometime in the autumn, she could not say precisely when—the hum had changed. Grown faintly warmer. Fainter still more like a voice clearing its throat.

Tonight she stood before it longer than usual, her lamp raised, watching the thorned pattern carved into the old wood. Brambles and roses, roses and brambles, winding around each other in a design that hurt to follow with the eye, because the eye insisted the pattern was moving, and patterns were not permitted to move.

"All seals sound," she said aloud, to hear a human voice in the dark. "Nothing has passed."

The door said nothing back. It never had. But the pressure behind her sternum turned over, like a sleeper disturbed, and Edmund Sterling—who was invisible, who was ordinary, who was precisely as interesting as a grey coat on a grey peg—went back to her desk with her heart beating too quickly for a woman whose only remarkable trait was her penmanship.

She wrote the sentence. She blotted it. She locked the black ledger with its little iron key, which she wore on a cord at her throat, tucked beneath her collar where no one ever saw it.

* * *

The trouble with being invisible, Edmund had learned, was that it required constant maintenance.

Above stairs, in the candlelit offices of the guildmasters, everyone was somebody. The clerks jockeyed for named desks; the agents competed over territories; the enforcers wore their scarlet sashes like open wounds. Ambition was the currency above, and everyone was rich or starving in it.

Edmund had made herself poor. She dressed in grey. She spoke softly and rarely. She took no credit and offered no opinions, and when the guildmaster's steward came down the spiral stair on his monthly inspection, she became a piece of furniture—a woman-shaped thing at a desk, useful, forgettable. He had called her "Sterling" for nine years without ever once looking at her eyes.

It had taken her nearly three years to build the invisibility, and she guarded it now the way misers guard gold, and for the same reason: because she had a suspicion, buried deeper than the door, that invisibility was the only thing keeping her alive.

She did not know why she believed this. That was the maddening shape of it. She had no memory of any threat, no incident she could point to, no enemy with a face. What she had was older than memory—something below it, in the cellar of her, where she kept things she did not examine. A certainty that had arrived with her on the day, nine years ago, when she had walked into the Guildhall and applied for a position no one remembered advertising.

*Be small. Be dull. Be the last person anyone looks at.* She did not know where the instruction had come from. She obeyed it anyway, the way one obeys a warning nailed to a bridge: not because one knows the river, but because someone, once, clearly had.

Once a month, the steward collected her reports and carried them up. Once a month, she watched him go and felt the familiar cold slide of *seen* pass over her and off again, like water off oiled cloth. He never lingered. Why would he? She was Sterling, the undercroft keeper, the woman who tallied other people's lost lives and asked nothing for herself.

That was the armor. She polished it nightly.

* * *

Toward the smallest hours, she allowed herself her one indulgence.

In the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath the spare nibs and the blotting sand, she kept a sheet of cheap paper on which she drew, in pencil, a garden.

She did not know why. That was the honest answer, and she gave it to herself honestly, once a month, when the pencil came out: *I do not know why I draw this.* There was no garden behind her lodging. There was no garden anywhere in the city that looked like it—a low wall of pale stone, a gate of black iron wrought into climbing thorns, beds of roses so dark they were nearly red-black, and beyond them, half-glimpsed, a country she had never seen. She had never seen it. She was sure she had never seen it.

And yet her hand knew the wall's every stone. Her hand knew that the third rosebush from the gate leaned slightly east, as if listening. Her hand knew the exact curve of the iron thorns, and drew them without guidance, swiftly, lovingly, the way a mother's hand knows a child's hair.

When the drawing was done—she never varied it, never added, never subtracted; it came out identical each time, line for line, as though she were copying rather than creating—she would sit and look at it until the lamps burned low, feeling an emotion she had no proper name for. Longing was too small. Grief was wrong. It was closer to homesickness, except that she was in no doubt about where her home was: a narrow room above a chandlery, a kettle, a grey coat. She had lived in this city her whole life. Everyone knew Edmund Sterling had always been here, ordinary as rain.

Everyone including Edmund Sterling herself. That was what made the garden unbearable. If a memory had been stolen from her, there would be a hole, an ache with edges. But there were no edges. There was only the drawing, arriving monthly from her own hand like a letter from someone who refused to sign their name.

She blew the graphite dust from the paper, folded it along its worn creases, and put it away in the drawer, beneath the nibs, beneath the sand, beneath the notice of God and the Guild.

Then the bell rang.

Not the closing bell—that would not ring for another hour yet, and besides, the closing bell was brass, and this was iron. A single deep stroke from somewhere above, from the guildhall's front doors, the bell they rang only for arrivals after hours, for clients desperate enough to pay the night-rate, for messengers carrying matters the daylight clerks must not see.

Edmund froze, pen lifted, one drop of ink trembling at its point.

No one came to the undercroft at this hour. No one ever came to the undercroft at all, unless summoned. Her invisibility settled around her like a cloak pulled tight; she became furniture, became shadow, became the grey coat on the grey peg, and she listened.

Footsteps above. More than one set—heavy boots, the cadence of men in a hurry, which was wrong in itself, because nothing hurried in the Guildhall after midnight. Then voices, indistinct through the floor. Then a sound that made the ink drop fall and bloom black on her careful page:

A body, falling. The heavy, graceless sound of a man collapsing against the guildhall's inner doors, and the doors giving way, and something dragging itself across marble toward the stair that led down to her.

Edmund stood. Her chair scraped. In the far corner of the undercroft, past the iron gate, past the shelves of dimming years, the sealed door that must never be opened gave one soft pulse of warmth against the dark—

—and then the footsteps reached the top of the stair, and began to descend.
