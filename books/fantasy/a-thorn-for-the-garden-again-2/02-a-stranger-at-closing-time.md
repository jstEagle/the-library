## 2. A Stranger at Closing Time

The closing bell at the Guild of Impossible Things rang at the seventh hour past dusk, and like every bell in the guildhall, it lied. It did not mean the day was over. It meant the day had been counted, and the counting was done, and anything that happened afterward was not supposed to happen at all.

Edmund Sterling knew this better than anyone. She was the one who entered the bell into the ledger.

She sat in her alcove off the undercroft with a stub of graphite, a pot of gum arabic, and the day's final folio spread before her, recording the last transactions of the season's trade. The guild dealt in strange stock—everyone knew that much, even the people who pretended not to—but few outside the counting rooms understood how much of that stock was simply *time*. Years, mostly. Traded in bundles of ten, or sold by the season by farmers who needed a harvest now and were willing to grow old faster to get it. Bottled names, which were harder to price and harder still to shelve; a name in a bottle wept condensation if its owner missed it. One shelf over from the bottled names, wrapped in oilcloth and chained to the wall, was the guild's single most valuable holding: a door that must never be opened. It appeared in the ledger only as *Item 0*, and its value column read, in the founder's own hand, *Not for sale. Not for keeping. Not for knowing.*

Edmund had balanced that column for eleven years. She had never once touched the door.

She liked this about herself, or she told herself she liked it: that she was precise, that she was dull, that she was the kind of woman whose name made clerks' eyes slide away before it finished being spoken. Invisibility was a discipline, and Edmund Sterling had made it an art. She dressed in ledger-grey. She spoke in the flat cadence of sums. When the enforcers came through the undercroft on their rounds, their lanterns passed over her the way light passes over furniture. She had spent eleven years becoming a thing that could be overlooked.

She had never asked herself, not directly, what she was overlooking them *for*. Some questions were like the door on the shelf. You did not open them. You dusted around them and recorded that they remained shut.

The folio closed with a soft sigh of vellum. Edmund blotted the final line—*Item 447, one year, winter-weight, sold to a cooper's widow, payment rendered in full*—and pressed the guild's seal into the wax, and the day was done.

That was when the stranger came through the door.

* * *

He did not knock. Later, Edmund would understand that this was the first wrong thing—the guildhall's outer door did not open for the uninvited, not ever, not in two hundred years of guild law—but at the time all she registered was the sound: the heavy iron latch giving way with a click that was far too casual, far too ordinary, as if the door itself had been fooled.

Then the man was inside, and the door swung shut behind him on its own weight, and Edmund was on her feet with a ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, which was the only foolish thing she did that night, and the thing she would remember longest.

He was tall, or he had been once. Pain had folded him down into something smaller, a man bent at the middle like a page creased by a careless thumb. He wore a cartographer's long coat, the kind with forty pockets, and every pocket had been cut open. Not searched—*cut*, slashed with a blade, the way a man slashes pockets when he is looking for one thing in particular and does not care what else he destroys. Blood had soaked through the coat at the shoulder and the belly, and it had that wrong, thin look of blood that was running out of a body faster than a body could make it.

He saw her and his face did something extraordinary. It *relieved*.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, good. You're plain."

Edmund had been called many things in her life, mostly nothing at all. She had never once been called *plain* as though it were a blessing.

"Sir," she said, in her flattest ledger-voice, "the guild is closed. Transactions resume at—"

"I know when transactions resume." He took three steps and his knee went wrong and he caught himself on the edge of her desk, and the inkwell jumped and rang like a small bell. Up close she could see his eyes, grey and fever-bright, moving over her face with the desperate attention of a man reading a map in the dark. "You're the undercroft keeper. The one nobody sees. Don't—don't do the voice. I haven't time for the voice."

"The voice, sir, is all I have."

Something that was almost a laugh came out of him, and it cost him; he folded another inch. "A woman who keeps the accounts of impossible things," he said, "and she thinks her voice is all she has. Listen to me. Listen." His hand shot out and caught her wrist, and his grip was astonishing, a dying man's grip, the grip of someone who had decided that if his body was going to fail him it could fail him *after*. "They cut the map out of me twice. Twice. I sewed it back in. It wants to be found by you, do you understand? Ink is patient but blood is *loyal*, and I have run out of both."

"Sir, you're wounded, I must fetch—"

"You must fetch *nothing*." The grip tightened. "The moment anyone else in this building knows I'm here, I am a ledger entry. Do you understand me? They will open a folio, they will write *one cartographer, deceased, cause: debt*, and they will close it, and everything I carried will belong to the guild, and the guild belongs to—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Whatever he had been about to name, he swallowed it, and Edmund watched him swallow it, and something cold slid down the back of her ribs, because in eleven years of keeping the guild's accounts she had learned to read the shape of an omitted word the way other people read words themselves.

"Who do you owe?" she asked. It was the wrong question. It was the only question the guild would ever ask, and she asked it because some part of her was still, even now, performing the role of the woman she was supposed to be.

The cartographer smiled. It was a terrible smile, blood-flecked and tender. "There she is," he whispered. "There *she* is."

* * *

He slid down the edge of the desk the way a coat slides off a hook, and Edmund went down with him, kneeling on the cold flagstones in her ledger-grey skirts with her hands pressed to the wound in his belly, and the blood came up between her fingers warm and shocking.

She should have rung the bell. That was the protocol; that was the first line of the first folio; *in the event of unauthorized entry, ring the bell and withdraw*. Her hand was six inches from the bell-cord. She looked at it. She looked at his face.

His eyes had gone somewhere else—somewhere with roads in it, she thought, somewhere with the particular light of high country at the edge of a map—and then they came back, and found hers, and held them.

"Your mother," he said.

The word went through her like a blade finding a gap in armor. "I don't have a mother. I have a guild record. Orphaned at—"

"Edmund." He said her name the way no one had ever said it. Not *Keeper Sterling*. Not *the undercroft woman*. He said *Edmund*, with the accent on the second syllable, the way you'd say the name of someone you had known when they were small, and Edmund's whole body went still, the stillness of a rabbit that has heard the hawk's shadow rather than seen it. "Edmund Sterling. Born under a thorn tree, in a garden that isn't on any map I ever drew, and I drew *all of them*. Do you hear me? I have mapped every road in this kingdom and the garden is not on any of them, because the garden is not *in* the kingdom. It is under the thing that wears the kingdom."

"You're fevered," she said. Her voice came out small. "You're dying, and you're fevered, and—"

"I'm dying," he agreed, almost cheerfully, "which is why I have no time for your lie, and neither, sweet girl, do you." His hand left her wrist and went to the slashed pocket at his chest—the one pocket, she saw now, that had been cut and *resewn*, with the ugly haste of a man stitching himself shut by firelight—and he pulled out a roll of heavy paper, and pressed it into her bloodied hands, and folded her fingers closed over it with his own.

It was warm. That was the first thing. The paper was warm, and damp, and it seemed to shift very slightly against her palms, like a sleeping bird.

"Look at it," he whispered. "No. Don't look at it. *Hold* it. Looking is for people with time."

"Who are you?" Edmund asked. "What is this?"

"I'm the last man who remembered the whole road." His breath was going ragged now, each one a little shorter, a little more expensive. "The guild keeps accounts. The crown keeps secrets. But a map—" he coughed, and the cough was wet and awful, "—a map keeps *truth*, and truth is the one thing neither of them can balance. They razored my family out of the guild's ledgers a hundred years ago. They razored yours out twenty years ago. I'm the last page, Edmund. I'm the last page and I am *tearing*."

Footsteps, far above. The night watch, doing its rounds on the ground floor, lantern-light sliding down the undercroft stairwell in a long yellow blade.

The cartographer heard it too. His eyes went bright with something that was not fear. It was urgency, pure and distilled, a man spending his final coin all at once.

"Listen," he said. "The gate is failing. Do you understand? Two hundred years it has held, and it is failing, and when it fails, the thing wearing the crown will not need to steal years anymore. It will simply *take*. Everything. Everyone. The whole ledger, closed at once." His hand found her cheek, shockingly gentle, leaving a smear of blood along her jaw like a signature. "The heir keeps the gate."

"I'm not—"

"The heir," he said, over her, through her, "keeps the gate."

And then, with the footsteps on the stair and the lantern-light swelling and Edmund's heart slamming against her ribs like something caged and newly awake, the cartographer did the last thing he had strength for. He took her hand—the one holding the map—and pressed it flat against his own torn belly, over the wound, over the blood, and held it there.

The map *moved*.

It moved against her palm like something alive, like a heartbeat, and through the contact Edmund felt—not saw, not yet, *felt*—a line being drawn. A road. A road with her name at the end of it, though the name was not *Sterling*, though the name was older, though the name was a word she did not know and her body knew instantly, completely, the way a body knows the shape of its own hand in the dark.

The cartographer exhaled. It was a long exhale, and it did not stop, and at some point Edmund understood that it had become something other than breathing.

She knelt on the cold stones with a dead man's blood on her hands and a living map pressed to her chest, and above her the lantern-light came down the stairs, and she did not ring the bell. She understood, in the flat and terrible way she understood sums, that she had just made her first entry in a different ledger entirely—one that was not the guild's, one that had been waiting eleven years, twenty years, two hundred years, for her to pick up the pen.

* * *

They came fast after that, the enforcers, faster than they had ever come for anything in Edmund's experience. Four of them, in their slate-grey coats with the guild's sigil—a hand holding a key that opened nothing—stitched at the shoulder, boots ringing on the undercroft flags.

Edmund had perhaps forty seconds. She used twenty-nine of them.

The map went into the folio. Not a hidden folio, not a clever place—the day's ledger, the one already sealed with the guild's own wax, the one no enforcer would open until morning because opening a sealed folio out of hours was itself a flogging offense. Edmund had written that rule into the standing orders herself, three years ago, in her flattest hand. *No folio, once sealed, shall be unsealed before dawn.* She had written it for tidiness. She had never loved her own tidiness more.

The dead man she could not hide. So she did the only other thing her hands knew: she straightened him. She folded his arms. She closed his eyes. And when the lanterns came around the last shelf, she was standing beside the body with her hands folded and her face arranged into its ledger-blank, and she said, in the voice that made clerks' eyes slide away:

"Unauthorized entry at closing bell. One male, cartographer by the coat. Wounded on arrival. Deceased as of—" she checked the guild clock, because precision was her armor and she wore it now like a breastplate, "—the seventh hour and twenty minutes past dusk."

The senior enforcer, a woman named Hesk with a face like a closed account, looked at the body. Looked at Edmund. Looked at the blood on Edmund's hands and the blood on the stones and the total absence of any other disturbance in the room.

"He came here," Hesk said slowly, "to a guildhall that does not open for the uninvited. Dying. Through a door that does not open for the uninvited. And of every soul in this building, he came down to *you*."

"The undercroft is where the ledgers are," Edmund said. "Dying men want their accounts in order."

Hesk stared at her for a long moment. Lantern-light moved on her face. Somewhere above them, the guildhall settled and ticked, two hundred years of secrets keeping their own counsel.

"His pockets are cut," Hesk said at last. "Everything he carried was taken or destroyed. Except—" her eyes came back to Edmund's hands, empty now, ink-stained, ordinary, "—except whatever he came to *deliver*."

"His pockets are cut," Edmund agreed, in the voice of a woman reading a sum aloud. "The inventory is the inventory. I keep the accounts, enforcer. I don't make them balance. I only record whether they do."

And that—God forgive her, God forgive the plain, dull, invisible Edmund Sterling—that was the moment she understood it was true. Not the words. The *other* thing underneath the words, the thing the cartographer had seen in her face in the first three seconds, the thing he had called *plain* like a benediction. She had spent eleven years building a woman out of ledgers and grey wool and flat vowels, and she had believed, right up until tonight, that the building was empty.

It was not empty. Something had been waiting in it, patient as ink, for a map drawn in blood to give it a road.

Hesk ordered the body carried up. She ordered the undercroft sealed for examination. She ordered Edmund to present herself to the guildmaster at first bell, and Edmund inclined her head and said, "Of course," in the voice of a woman with nothing to hide.

Then the enforcers were gone, and the undercroft was dark, and Edmund stood alone among the shelves of traded years and bottled names with her hand resting on a sealed folio that held a living map, and she thought about the word the cartographer had spent his last breath on.

*Heir.*

Above her, through the floor, she heard the guildmaster's door open, and footsteps cross the hall, and stop—directly, precisely, over her head.

Someone in the guildhall was awake. Someone had been awake all along.

Edmund did not move. But in the dark, her hand—the one that had touched a dying man's blood and felt a road being drawn—curled closed, and held, and did not let go.
