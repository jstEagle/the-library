## 3. What the Ledgers Omit

The dead man's map would not stay still under my blotter.

I had wrapped it in oilcloth and weighted it with the brass scale I used for weighing years, and still, when I passed my desk at the third bell of the morning, I could feel it. Not heat exactly. Pressure. The way a buried spring presses up through a cellar floor before it floods.

I told myself I was being foolish. I had spent eleven years in the Guild of Impossible Things learning to be foolish about nothing: to file the bottled names by their vowels rather than their owners' griefs, to record traded years in neat columns of blue ink, to pretend that the door at the end of the undercroft—iron-banded, triple-locked, never opened in living memory—was simply another entry in an inventory and not the reason the guild existed at all. Routine was armor. Routine was the whole point of me. Edmund Sterling, keeper of small accounts, a woman so ordinary that enforcers walked past her desk without seeing her, that clerks forgot her name between uses, that the guildmaster himself had once called her *that gray little quill* to her face and I had bowed as though it were an honor.

But the map pressed against its wrapping like something breathing, and the dead man had said *the heir keeps the gate*, and his blood had been drying into roads while I stood there holding him.

So on the fourth day after his death, when the guildhall emptied for the midday meal and the undercroft settled into its long hush, I took down the oldest ledger instead of eating.

It was called the First Register, though no one called it anything anymore, because no one touched it. It lived chained to a lectern in the archive alcove, behind the shelf of confiscated shadows and the drawer of apologies that had outlived their speakers. The ledger was older than the guildhall built around it; the guild had grown up around the book the way a tree grows around a fence post, swallowing what it could not remove. Its cover was vellum gone the color of old teeth, and its spine bore a title in a script I did not recognize but which made my fingers ache to read, the way a word sits on the tip of the tongue.

I unchained it with my own key—I had archive access because someone had to inventory the dust—and carried it to my desk, where the light from the grate fell honest and yellow.

I told myself I was doing due diligence. A man had died in our hall pressing a document into an employee's hands; any careful accountant would trace the provenance. Any careful accountant would check whether the stranger's name appeared in the registers. Any careful accountant would not, strictly speaking, need to open the First Register to do this. But the First Register was the root of all the other ledgers, and roots are where you look when the branches bear strange fruit.

I opened it.

The first hundred pages were everything I expected: entries in a hand like iron nails hammered flat. *Received: one winter, traded by a farmer who could not afford to grieve twice. Condition: sound.* *Received: the name Maren, surrendered freely, payment rendered in bread for seven years. Disposition: sealed.* Page after page of small impossibilities, each one priced, each one filed, each one made safe by the simple magic of bookkeeping. This was the guild I knew. This was the guild that had hired me, that had taught me my figures, that had given an orphan girl with no history a desk and a salary and a place to hide.

I turned past them, following the dates backward, deeper into the book's antiquity, looking for the earliest mention of gates.

The pages went thin. The ink changed color three times. And then—

I felt it before I saw it. My fingertips, trailing the edge of the pages as I turned them, caught on a roughness. A ridge. I stopped and tilted the page toward the light.

Stumps. Close-cropped stumps of paper along the gutter, five in a row, then a gap, then two more. Someone had cut pages out of the First Register with a razor, carefully, close to the binding, so that the loss would be invisible unless a reader ran her thumb along the margin the way I was doing now, my heart beginning to knock against my ribs.

Seven pages missing.

I checked the register's own index—a column of folio numbers in the front matter, written by the same iron-nail hand. Folios 212 through 218: accounted for in the index. Absent from the book. And the index gave not titles but a single heading, one word repeated across all seven lines:

*Sterling.*

I sat very still.

The undercroft ticked and dripped around me. Somewhere above, the lunch crowd laughed at something; the sound came down the grate thin as thread. My thumb rested on the stumps of the missing pages, and I understood, in the slow cold way that understanding sometimes arrives, that I was touching the place where I used to be.

Not metaphor. Not poetry. I am an accountant; I think in columns. Seven folios, indexed under *Sterling*. A family name. My name—the only name I had ever been given, handed to me at sixteen by a guild clerk who'd found me half-starved at the gate with nothing but my own hands and a gap in my memory wide enough to drive a cart through. I had always assumed Sterling was invented for me. A placeholder. A label on a box whose contents had been lost in shipping.

Boxes are not lost. Boxes are put somewhere.

I pulled the lamp closer and examined the stumps properly, tilting each page-remnant to catch the light. On the first stump, ghosted in the indentation of a removed leaf, I could just make out the pressure-marks of writing from the excised page: the mirror-image impression left on the page beneath. I fetched the soft graphite stick we used for tracing seals and rubbed it gently across the depression, the way one lifts an impression of a lock's mechanism.

Letters surfaced, pale and reversed, like fish rising in dark water.

*...the Gate requires a keeper of the line...*

*...bound not with chain but with garden...*

And on the last stump, the deepest impression, pressed hard enough that whoever wrote it had been bearing down with fury or fear:

*...the Sterlings are not the guild's servants. The guild is the Sterlings' servant, and has forgotten, and must be kept forgetting...*

The graphite slipped from my fingers.

I read it again. Then again. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense. They sat there, reversed and gray, insisting on their meaning: that the guild I served, that had edited me, that had razored its own founding truth out of its own founding book—that guild existed at the pleasure of my family, and had arranged matters so that no one, including the Sterlings themselves, would ever remember it.

*Kept forgetting.* Like a debt rolled forward year over year until the original sum disappears under the interest. Except here the compounding was done with knives and memory-surgery, and the principal was me.

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a strange detachment, the accountant in me observing the anomaly, logging it. *Note: subject exhibits tremor. Cause: unknown. Recommend investigation.*

No. Not unknown. Cause known. Cause was sitting in my chest wearing my heartbeat.

Because it explained things. It explained why the guild had taken in a memoryless orphan without question and set her to counting other people's stolen years—it was not charity, it was containment. It explained why I, of all clerks, held the keys to the undercroft, why my rounds included the door at the corridor's end that never opened, why the duty roster always, always assigned the Thorn Gate inspection to Sterling, as if it were drudgery, as if it were punishment. Eleven years I had thought my proximity to that door was coincidence. Eleven years I had balanced the books of impossible things and never once asked why an ordinary woman was trusted with extraordinary locks.

They hadn't given me the keys despite what I was. They had given me the keys *because* of it, and then taken away my ability to know it. A jailer who does not know she is a jailer makes the most loyal guard in the world.

Or a gardener. The impression had said *garden*. Bound not with chain but with garden.

The map, under its oilcloth on my desk, grew suddenly heavier. I could feel it the way you feel weather changing in an old wound.

I worked the rest of that afternoon in a state I can only describe as double-exposure. My hands did the work—entering the morning's receipts, initialing the transfer of a bottled echo from shelf nine to shelf twelve—but some other part of me stood behind my own shoulders reading the same seven stumps over and over, running my thumb along the ragged edge where I had been cut out of the world's memory, mine and everyone else's both.

At closing bell I re-chained the First Register, locked the archive, and climbed the stairs into the grey evening. And it was there, crossing the entrance hall with my cloak already buttoned, that I saw the letter.

It lay on the guildmaster's receiving tray, awaiting the morning courier, face-up, unsealed—draft copy, the fair hand not yet made. I should have walked past. Clerks do not read the guildmaster's correspondence. Clerks who read the guildmaster's correspondence become former clerks, abruptly.

But the salutation caught my eye, because it was addressed to no one I knew, and the wax stub beside it was black, and black wax meant the Quiet Office—the guild's enforcers, the men who collected debts the ledgers didn't dare list.

I looked. I have never decided whether I regret looking. Looking is what unmade me, and looking is what saved me, and both of these things are true the way both sides of a ledger are true.

The draft read:

---

*To Under-Warden Corvane, Quiet Office, from the Guildmaster. By hand only. Burn after memorizing.*

*Brother Corvane —*

*You will forgive an old man's slowness; the shock of Vey's death unsettles me. Yes, the same Vey. You will understand better than most why that name troubles me, and why it must trouble no one else.*

*Matters stand thus. The cartographer reached the hall alive, which none of us believed possible, and died in it, which is worse. He delivered something before the end. We do not yet know what. The gray clerk who attended him claims ignorance. I believe her—she has been quieted thoroughly, twice, and the second working cost us a rare talent in the cutting. But belief is not certainty, and certainty is the only currency the Crown accepts.*

*You will recall the terms of the arrangement. The gate sleeps so long as no heir wakes. No heir wakes so long as the Sterlings remain unrecorded. This has held for twenty years, since the last of the line was ended and the last of the pages were removed. It will continue to hold. Whatever Vey carried, find it. Whatever he whispered, unhear it. If the gray clerk knows more than her scars suggest, quiet her a third time—or end the account entirely. I leave the arithmetic to you, brother, as I always have. You have never once rounded wrong.*

*One caution. Do not touch the map, if map it is, with bare intention. Vey drew it in his own blood for a reason, and blood drawn in purpose keeps its purpose. Deliver it unopened to the Crown's readers in Vessamyre. Let them bleed their fingers on it, not ours.*

*The door holds. The garden sleeps. The ledger balances.*

*—G.*

---

I do not know how long I stood there. Long enough that a junior clerk asked me if I wanted the tray moved, and I heard myself say, "Leave it, I'll see to it," in my flattest voice, the voice that has never once been noticed, and take the letter, and carry it—not to the courier's satchel but to the stove in the copying room, where draft copies went to die—and feed it to the fire, and watch the black wax bubble and the words curl and go.

*Quiet her a third time—or end the account entirely.*

Twice. They had cut me twice already, and I had never known, had never even suspected there was anything to know. The gray clerk. *That gray little quill.* Twenty years since the last of the line was ended. Ended. My mother, presumably, or my father, or both, and the guild had written the word *ended* in a letter as calmly as I would write *received*.

The fire ate the letter. I stood warming hands that did not need warming and understood that I was now guilty of two crimes: theft of correspondence, and destruction of the same. Both capital, by guild law. Both discovered within a day, because the guildmaster counted everything, because counting was the whole religion of the place, and a missing draft would show in his counts the way seven missing folios showed in the First Register.

Unless.

Unless they never got the chance to count.

The map waited upstairs in the undercroft, breathing under its oilcloth. Three stolen facts sat in my head where seven stolen pages used to sit in a book: *heir. gate. garden.* And somewhere north, days away, a city called Vessamyre where a thing wearing a king's crown employed readers who bled their fingers on documents, waiting for this map to arrive so that they could end the account entirely.

End the account. My account. The account of the Sterlings, seven folios deep, cut out at the root.

I went back down to the undercroft. I did not allow myself to hurry; hurrying is visible, and invisibility was still, for a few hours more, my armor. I signed out my duties for the night watch, which was mine anyway, once a month, and no one questioned it, because no one ever questioned Edmund Sterling. I lit the undercroft lamps. I took the map from beneath the brass scale and unwrapped it at last with my bare hands, intention and all, damn the guildmaster's caution, because it was addressed to me in the only language that mattered: blood remembers its ink.

The lines crawled toward my fingertips like roots finding water.

And far above me, muffled by stone and shelving and eleven years of careful gray silence, the closing bell rang out over the guildhall, and boots began to come down the stairs.
