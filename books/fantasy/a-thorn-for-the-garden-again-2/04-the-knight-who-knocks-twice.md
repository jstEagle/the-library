## 4. The Knight Who Knocks Twice

The knock came twice.

Wren had learned, over eleven years of service to the Crown of Vessamyre, that men who knocked twice wanted something they were ashamed to ask for. Once was duty. Three times was fear. But twice—twice was the sound of a man standing on a threshold he did not wish to cross, telling himself the whole while that he had no choice but to cross it.

Sir Wren Calloway stood in the rain outside the Guildhall of Impossible Things and listened to his own knuckles still ringing against the door, and thought: *I am the man who knocks twice.*

He had not wanted this commission. He had asked for it. Those were not contradictory facts, though it had taken him most of the ride from the palace to understand why. When the steward had read out the morning's dispatches—a dead stranger in the guildhall, a map missing from the corpse's effects, the guildmaster's nervous petition for a royal investigator—Wren had heard the words *guild* and *death* and *map*, and something old and half-buried in him had turned over like a sleeper disturbed by a distant bell. He had said, "I'll go," before the steward finished the sentence. Too quickly. The steward's eyes had flicked up, brief as a fish under ice.

Now he stood in the rain, and the door opened, and a clerk with ink-stained fingers and a face built for forgetting looked at him without expression.

"Sir Wren Calloway," he said, and produced the seal. "By appointment of the Crown. I'm here regarding the death of your visitor last night."

"We closed at the bell," the clerk said. "Deaths after closing are not guild business."

"The Crown disagrees."

"The Crown," said the clerk, "has never once set foot in our undercroft, and yet claims to know what we keep there." She stepped aside—not deference, Wren noted, but something closer to resignation—and let the rain follow him in.

*She.* He corrected himself as she turned. The clerk moved through the entry hall ahead of him with the economy of someone who had long ago stopped spending anything unnecessary, and when the light from the high windows crossed her face he saw that it was a woman's face, unremarkable in every particular except for the eyes, which were doing something remarkable indeed: they were measuring him. Not with fear. Not with curiosity. With the flat professional attention of an appraiser valuing an object of uncertain provenance.

"You're the ledger-keeper," he said. "Sterling."

"Edmund Sterling." She did not offer her hand. "You knew my name."

"Everyone's name is known to the Crown, Mistress Sterling. That is rather the point of a crown."

"Is it?" She led him down a corridor lined with shelves that went back further than the lamplight reached. "I'd always assumed the point was sitting very still while everyone else argued about you."

Despite himself—despite everything—he almost smiled. He strangled it before it could fully form. Eleven years of practice made the strangling easy.

* * *

They gave him the room where the cartographer had died. Of course they did; Wren would have demanded it otherwise, and Edmund Sterling seemed the sort of person who preferred to hand you the unpleasant thing early so you'd stop looking for it.

The body was gone. The blood remained—a dark spill across the flagstones, mopped at the edges but soaked deep into the grout, because blood always got into the grout, blood was patient that way. Someone had chalked an outline, or tried to; the figure was incomplete, one arm missing, as if the drawer had lost their nerve halfway through.

"He came in at the closing bell," Sterling said. She stood by the wall with her arms folded, watching Wren the way one watched weather. "Bled through his coat before he reached the counter. He pressed something into my hands and died saying—" she paused, and Wren saw her choose her words with visible care, "—something about an heir. Then your enforcers arrived. Four minutes late, by the hall clock. They were very disappointed the dying was already done."

"Disappointed?"

"They searched me. Thoroughly." Her voice did not change at all, and that was how Wren knew how much it had cost her. "They didn't find what they were looking for. I imagine that's why the Crown sent you instead of trusting them."

It was true. It was also bait, and they both knew it. She was offering him the thread and waiting to see if he pulled.

Wren crouched by the bloodstain instead. He let the silence stretch. Interrogation, properly done, was mostly patience wearing the costume of politeness.

"What did he give you?" he asked the floor.

"I've told the guildmaster I don't recall."

"And I'm asking what you told the guildmaster."

That earned him a look. Not anger—something more dangerous, a flicker of genuine assessment, the recalibration of a ledger. "Careful, Sir Calloway. That question works both ways."

"I'm aware."

"Are you?" She unfolded her arms. For the first time she came away from the wall, crossing to the window, and Wren noticed that she kept herself positioned—always—where she could see both him and the door. Habit, or training. "Because here is what I see. A knight of the Hollow King's court sent to investigate a death that the court should be celebrating, given what the dead man carried. Sent alone, without escort, to a guildhall full of people who despise him. Asking questions in a manner designed to make me trust him." She glanced back. "So either you are incompetent—which your reputation argues against—or you want something, and the death is only your excuse for wanting it."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere above them, a clerk dropped a stack of papers and swore with impressive fluency.

"You have a reputation too," Wren said quietly. "Edmund Sterling. Nine years in the undercroft. Perfect attendance. Perfect balances. So perfectly invisible that three separate informants described you as 'the furniture.'" He rose. "And yet the dying man bled his way past forty better-known faces to reach *you*. And four minutes later the enforcers arrived—four minutes, when their usual response time to guildhall incidents is twenty. Almost as though someone told them exactly when to come. Almost as though someone wanted the map recovered from a specific pair of hands."

Her face did nothing. That was the marvel of it; her face did absolutely nothing, a locked room with the lights out, and Wren understood suddenly why the guild valued her, and understood equally suddenly that whatever she was hiding, she had been hiding it for a very long time, and hiding it well enough that the hiding had become the architecture of the woman.

"You may report all that to your king," she said.

"I don't intend to report anything to the king."

The words were out before he had decided to say them. He heard them hang in the air between them, absurd and irrevocable, and watched her stillness change quality—from defensive to attentive, the way a deer goes still not from calm but from listening.

"No?" she said.

"No." He looked at the bloodstain again, because it was easier than looking at her. "I intend to find out who that man was, who sent him, and why a knight's own enforcers move faster for a dead cartographer than for a burning granary. And then I intend to decide whether any of it belongs in a report."

"That's treasonous bookkeeping, Sir Calloway."

"I'm told this is the guild of impossible things."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It was not quite a smile. It was the place where a smile would go if a smile had ever been permitted to live there.

* * *

He spent the afternoon being shown the guildhall by people who did not want to show it to him. The guildmaster received him in an office that smelled of sealing wax and anxiety, and gave answers that were smooth on top and hollow underneath, like river ice in late spring. Wren catalogued the hollowness. He was good at that. It was, he suspected, the only thing he was genuinely good at: hearing the echo in a man's certainties.

Eleven years ago, he had knelt in the White Chapel with two hundred other young men of good family, and sworn his sword to the king, and meant it with the whole hot stupid weight of his heart. He remembered the crown touching his shoulder—the actual crown, brought out for the occasion, iron and gold and heavy as sin—and he remembered thinking: *this weight is real, therefore what it blesses is real.*

The first doubt had come five years later, small as a splinter. A village called Marrow's End, cleared by royal decree, its people resettled—or so the writ said. Wren had ridden the escort. He had seen the carts leave. He had never seen them arrive anywhere. When he'd asked, his captain had looked at him with mild, tired pity and said, "Calloway, the kingdom is a garden. Gardens require pruning. Don't make me write down that you asked."

He hadn't asked again. Not aloud. But the splinter stayed, and splinters walk, and by the tenth year he had begun keeping a private inventory—the granaries that emptied into nowhere, the prisoners whose names vanished from rolls, the king's strange gray hours when His Majesty sat motionless in the throne room for entire afternoons, staring at nothing, breathing like a house with no fire in it. Wren told himself he collected these facts out of loyalty: a soldier must know the true state of what he defends.

He had almost made himself believe it.

Then the cartographer had died holding a map drawn in blood, and the enforcers had moved like the map mattered more than the man, and Wren had heard himself volunteer before he understood why—and now he stood in a guildhall of impossible things, hunting his own doubts the way other men hunted game, knowing that what he flushed out might be the end of him.

* * *

He found Edmund Sterling at dusk in the counting room, and pretended he had been looking for the privy.

She sat alone at a desk beneath one lamp, working through a ledger with a quill that scratched like a small animal in a wall. She did not look up when he entered. She did not tell him to leave. Both silences felt deliberate.

"You balance accounts at night," he said.

"I balance accounts whenever the day's numbers are wrong. Today's numbers were wrong." She dipped the quill. "A man died on my floor. Deaths create paperwork."

"That's a cold way to put it."

"It's an honest way to put it. Coldness is just honesty that hasn't been apologized for." She finished a line, blotted it, and finally raised her head. In the lamplight her eyes were the color of strong tea, and behind the professional flatness he saw the thing he had seen in the mirror every morning for eleven years: a person performing ordinariness so convincingly that the performance had eaten most of the person underneath.

"You weren't surprised by the enforcers," Wren said. "Four minutes. You'd expected them sooner, I think. You were braced for sooner."

"Was I?"

"When I mentioned it, you didn't ask how I knew their timing. You already knew. You've known their habits for years." He took a step closer. The lamp threw their shadows huge against the shelving. "Which means either you watch them, or you've had reason to expect them. Which means, Mistress Sterling, that long before a cartographer died on your floor, you were already afraid of something."

The quill stopped scratching.

For a moment—one long breath, two—the performance flickered. What showed through was not fear exactly. It was exhaustion, vast and old, the exhaustion of a woman who had been carrying something heavy in plain sight for so long that she'd forgotten other people couldn't see it. Then it was gone, shuttered away, and she was the clerk again, the furniture, the invisible thing.

"You're very observant for a man pretending to need the privy," she said.

"I'm very observant," Wren said, "because I was trained by liars. The best way to learn a lie is to serve it."

"And what do you serve, Sir Calloway?"

"A crown I no longer understand, worn by a king I no longer recognize, enforced by men I no longer trust." He said it evenly, quietly, the way one confesses to a priest one does not believe in. "You asked earlier what I want. I want to know if the thing I swore my life to is worth the life. That's all. That's the whole of my treason."

She studied him for a long time. Outside, the rain had thickened into a proper autumn storm, drumming on the glass, and the lamp flame leaned and steadied and leaned.

"The man who died," she said at last, slowly, each word placed like a coin on a scale, "called me heir."

Wren went very still.

"He said—" her voice caught, not with grief but with the effort of saying it aloud at all, "—*the heir keeps the gate.* And then he died, and the enforcers came for the map, and everyone in this building wants me to forget those four words ever happened. Including, I suspect, your king. Especially your king." She set down the quill. "So. You want to know whether your crown is worth your life, Sir Calloway. I want to know why a dead cartographer thought I was an heir to anything. It occurs to me that these questions may have the same answer. And it further occurs to me that neither of us can afford to hunt it alone."

"Is that an alliance?"

"It's an accounting entry. Two debts, jointly pursued." She almost smiled—that not-smile again, the ghost of a permission she'd never been granted. "Don't make it romantic. I balance books. I know what happens to entries that get sentimental."

"What happens?"

"They get audited."

The storm rattled the windows. Down in the undercroft, Wren knew, the guild's ledgers held records of traded years and bottled names and one door that must never be opened, and somewhere among those pages—if the dead man's whisper was true—there was an account with Edmund Sterling's name in it that she herself could not read.

He offered his hand. She looked at it the way she had looked at his seal that morning, weighing provenance, uncertain of authenticity. Then she shook it once, briefly, her grip dry and firm as a contract.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When we find the answer—and we will find it, because the dead man spent his last breath making sure of it—you and I are both going to wish we'd never knocked on this door." She released his hand and returned to her ledger. "Knock anyway."

* * *

He left the guildhall by the front entrance, into the rain, and stood a moment on the steps with his collar up and his thoughts in disarray.

Across the square, half-hidden in the shadow of the chandlery, a figure in guild livery stood watching the windows of the counting room. Watching, Wren noted, the way a man watches a door he has been ordered to guard against someone leaving—not arriving.

The watcher saw him see. Neither of them moved. Then the figure melted backward into the alley, unhurried, certain, and Wren understood that the enforcers had not come four minutes late by accident after all.

They had come because someone had promised them that whatever the cartographer carried would still be there.

And tomorrow, or the day after, they would come back to check.
