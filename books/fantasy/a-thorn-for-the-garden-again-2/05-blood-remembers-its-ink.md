## 5. Blood Remembers Its Ink

The map did not like the dark.

Edmund discovered this on her third night with it, when she woke in her narrow room above the chandlery to a faint red glow seeping through the floorboards of her writing desk—the locked drawer she had tucked it into, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted beneath three volumes of tariff schedules. She sat up in bed with her heart already running. The glow pulsed once, twice, slow and patient as breathing.

She had told herself, all day at the guildhall while balancing the quarter's accounts of traded years, that the thing was only ink. Dried blood, perhaps, but blood dries brown, not this deep arterial crimson that seemed to sit *above* the parchment rather than in it. She had told herself the cartographer was a madman who had died of ordinary wounds and ordinary madness, and that his last words—*the heir keeps the gate*—were the raving of a stranger, meant for no one.

The map glowed again, and Edmund stopped lying to herself.

She lit no candle. She crossed the cold floorboards barefoot, unlocked the drawer with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, and unwrapped the oilcloth by the light coming from within it.

The lines had moved.

Two nights ago, when she had first dared study it alone, the map had been a sprawl of rivers and ridgelines rendered in that impossible red, dense with annotations in a cramped hand she now recognized from the guild's oldest ledgers—a hand that had been razored out of them, page after page after page. Now the rivers had shifted westward. The mountains had gathered themselves into a ring around a blank space near the map's heart, and in that blank space something was being drawn, stroke by stroke, so slowly she could almost believe she imagined it: a circle. A wall. A gate.

And every line, every road, every river on the whole vast sheet was crawling toward it. Toward a city she had never seen.

Toward Vessamyre.

"No," Edmund said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "No, I've never—" But her mouth went dry around the words, because she *had*. Not with her eyes. With something older than eyes.

She dreamed of Vessamyre nightly. She always had.

She dreamed of white stone terraces stacked above a river the color of tarnished silver; of bridges with lanterns that burned green instead of gold; of a palace so large its shadow crossed the city like a second nightfall. She had dreamed these things since childhood, and as a child she had assumed everyone dreamed of cities they'd never visited, the way everyone dreamed of falling. It wasn't until she was nine, describing the dream to a schoolmistress, that she learned otherwise—and she had learned, too, what happened to girls who described impossible things too precisely in a city run by the Guild of Impossible Things. The schoolmistress had gone pale. The schoolmistress had reported her. And Edmund's mother had come to fetch her from the guildhall annex holding her hand too tightly and smiling too brightly, and that night her mother had said, kneeling before her, voice very soft: *Lamb, some dreams are doors. Don't knock.*

Edmund had spent twenty years not knocking.

Now the door was glowing under her fingers.

She snatched her hand back. The map's crawling slowed—not stopping, but slowing, the way a cat stops mid-prowl when it notices it is watched. In the lamplight-less dark, the half-drawn gate pulsed like a wound kept deliberately open.

"You're looking for someone," she whispered. "It isn't me."

The map said nothing. Maps don't. But she felt, with a certainty that settled into her chest like swallowed ice, that it disagreed—that it knew better than she did what she was, even if she didn't.

*The heir keeps the gate.* The dying man's blood on her palms, still, in memory. The enforcers' boots on the guildhall stones, minutes too late—or minutes too late by accident, which was a thing the guild never permitted. Accidents were for people without ledgers.

Edmund wrapped the map again. Her hands shook now; the steadiness had been spent. She thought about burning it. She stood at the little iron stove with the oilcloth bundle in both hands and the flint in reach, and she stood there a long time, until the fire had burned down to coals and her arms ached, and she did not do it.

Because when she had touched the map—when her bare skin had met the blood-ink for even those few seconds—something had risen in her that was not fear.

It had been longing. Vast, specific, and unbearably homesick. Longing for a place her feet had never carried her, and grief for something lost that she could not name, could not find, could not even properly mourn. It was like standing outside a house where warm light showed in the windows and knowing, bone-deep, that she used to live there.

She put the map back in the drawer. She did not sleep again.

* * *

In the morning she went to work, because invisibility was armor and armor required wearing even when it chafed. She balanced the accounts of traded years. She entered bottled names into the registry in her small, deliberate, unremarkable script—the script she had trained herself into over fifteen years, letter by letter, until no clerk in the guildhall wrote more forgettably. She fetched the guildmaster's tea and endured his wet-eyed stare and noticed, with the part of her mind that never stopped counting, that two enforcers were stationed at the undercroft stair since the stranger's death, and that one of them watched her carry the tea tray with a little too much attention.

They suspected nothing yet. They suspected everything eventually. That was the guild's way: patience as policy.

At midday she slipped down to the archive under the pretense of reconciling the year-tithe rolls. The old ledgers lived in the deepest gallery, behind iron grilles, in air that tasted of dust and tallow and something faintly vegetal, like crushed stems. She took down Volume One—the ledger so old its boards were covered in vellum scraped from even older books—and turned to the razored places she had found two nights ago, the stumps of pages cut close to the spine.

She had brought the stolen fragments with her, hidden in the lining of her sleeve. Now, in the grey light from the grille, she laid them against the stubs and matched the cuts.

Three pages. All concerning the Sterling family. All concerning the Thorn Gate.

The first fragment was an inventory, dated two hundred years past:

*Item: one gate, thorn-grown, keeper-bound. Item: one binding, renewed seasonally by the hand of a Sterling. Note: the Hollow King cannot pass where the roses are tended. Note: he knows it. He has begun to buy the gardeners' years.*

The second fragment was shorter, and worse:

*The Sterlings are to be recorded as clerks henceforth. Their true office is stricken from all rolls. Let them keep their own history without knowing they keep it. An ordinary memory is the safest vault.*

An ordinary memory is the safest vault.

Edmund read the line four times. Around her the archive breathed its dusty breath; somewhere overhead a clerk's footsteps crossed and recrossed the floor, patient as a clock. *Let them keep their own history without knowing they keep it.* As though memory were coin that could be deposited. As though someone had taken hers—

As though her mother had.

The thought arrived unbidden and terrible, and she pushed it down the way she pushed down everything, filing it in the drawer inside herself where she kept the dreams of Vessamyre and the singing and the garden.

The third fragment was a single sentence, in the cramped razored-out hand:

*The gate does not want a jailer. It wants a gardener. God forgive us for forgetting the difference.*

Edmund stood in the dark gallery with the paper trembling in her fingers and understood that she was crying, and had been for some time, without deciding to.

She did not know why the sentence broke her. That was the cruelest part. Grief without a referent, sorrow arriving like rain onto ground that had forgotten it ever held roots. She wiped her face on her sleeve, rewrapped the fragments, and climbed back into the daylight wearing her invisibility like a coat buttoned wrong.

* * *

That night she let herself touch the map.

She prepared for it the way she prepared for difficult entries in the ledger: candle trimmed, door barred, ink and pen at hand in case recording the event made it manageable. She spread the blood-map flat on her desk and looked at it for a long moment, at the crawling lines frozen mid-migration, at the half-born gate in its ring of mountains.

Then she pressed her palm flat against the parchment.

The effect was immediate and total. The lines *rushed* toward her touch like water finding a drain, converging beneath her hand, and heat bloomed up through her palm—not pain, exactly, but recognition, fierce and intimate, the sensation of a name being spoken in a room you'd believed empty. The ink crawled up over the edge of her fingers, fine red filaments, tasting her skin.

And the world folded.

—she is small, small enough that the rosebushes are towers, and the gravel path runs warm under her bare feet because it is summer, high summer, the kind of summer that only exists in the first years of a life. Above her the sky is impossibly blue, bluer than any sky over Greyharrow, and there are walls around the garden, old walls furred with moss, and beyond the walls—she can hear it, distant and enormous, the sound of a city breathing, bells and carts and ten thousand voices, a sound like Vessamyre must make, like the city of her dreams—

Someone is singing.

A woman's voice, low and unhurried, weaving itself between the rows of roses. Edmund cannot see the woman's face. Every time the dream-memory turns toward her, the face is a smudge, a candle-blur—but the voice is *specific*, the voice is the most specific thing Edmund has ever known, and it is singing a song about thorns:

*Grow high, grow kind, grow over the door,*
*let no crown pass where you grew before.*
*Sleep, my darling, the garden keeps—*
*what the gardener tends, the gardener keeps.*

Small hands in the memory—her hands—are burying something at the base of the oldest bush. A seed? No. A tooth. Her own baby tooth, pulled that morning, and her mother laughing, telling her it is how the garden learns to know her, *you give the roses a piece of yourself and they remember you forever, lamb, that's the bargain, that's the whole bargain—*

"Mother," Edmund says in the memory, in the voice of a child she used to be, "will I always live here?"

And the smudged face bends close, close enough that warmth comes off it like off an oven door, and says: *Always. Until the garden doesn't need—*

The memory tore.

Edmund came back to herself bent double over the desk, gasping, the candle guttering, her palm printed red where the ink had touched. On the map, the gate had finished drawing itself. It hung complete in the center of the sheet now, rendered in strokes finer than any pen could manage: a circular wall, and set into it an archway grown entirely of thorns, and beyond the archway—blankness. White space. The territory of whatever lay on the other side of a binding, which no cartographer alive or dead had been able to see.

Beneath the gate, in that cramped familiar hand, a caption had appeared that had not existed before:

*Vessamyre. Beneath the palace. Bring the heir.*

Edmund straightened slowly. Her heart was slamming. Tears tracked cold down her face and she hadn't felt herself start them.

*Bring the heir.* The dying man's whisper came back to her across five days and one closed drawer: *the heir keeps the gate.* He had drawn a map that traveled to its destination on its own, a map written in living blood that remembered where it was going—and it had waited, dormant and disguised as mere cartography, until it came into the right hands. Until it came into *her* hands.

Which meant the cartographer had known. Had known what she was before she did. Had spent his final hours getting this to her specifically, bleeding through the streets of Greyharrow to reach a ledger-keeper whose entire existence was engineered to be overlooked.

Which meant others might know too. The guild, with its patience as policy. Whatever wore the crown in Vessamyre, two hundred years hungry behind a gate of thorns.

Edmund looked down at her palm. The print was fading already, but in the last moments before it vanished she could have sworn it formed a shape: small, curved, unmistakable.

The shape of a rose.

"I'm not the heir," she told the empty room. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she heard the lie for what it was—the same voice she'd used at nine years old, telling a schoolmistress about a city she'd never seen, while her mother's hand tightened around hers.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Greyharrow, the closing bell rang. Tomorrow the guildmaster would expect her at her desk. Tomorrow the enforcers would watch her carry tea, and the ledgers would balance, and everything would be ordinary, because ordinary was the vault they'd built around her.

But tonight the map glowed on the desk like a coal refusing to die, and its lines strained northward, all of them, every road and river bending toward Vessamyre the way a plant bends toward light.

And beneath the floorboards of the world, faint but unmistakable, Edmund could almost hear the singing.

*What the gardener tends, the gardener keeps.*

She reached for her cloak.
