## 6. The Guild Does Not Forgive Debt

The summons came at the third bell of morning, folded inside a ledger slip like any other.

*E. Sterling — Undercroft 4 — Re: inventory discrepancy.*

Edmund read it twice, then a third time, because her hands had begun to shake and she needed something to do with them that wasn't shaking. A discrepancy. Of course there was a discrepancy. There was always a discrepancy; that was the whole architecture of her life, columns that refused to balance until she coaxed them into it with patience and small, careful lies. She had spent nine years being the person who fixed discrepancies. She had never once been summoned about one.

She set down her pen. Ink pooled at the nib's tip, a black bead swelling on the page, and she blotted it before it could ruin the entry beneath — *bottled name, one (1), returned unsold, condition: weeping.* Somewhere in the rows above her, other clerks scratched away at their own accounts, and no one looked up. No one ever looked up. That had been her armor for nearly a decade: the deliberate cultivation of dullness, the practiced stoop of her shoulders, the way she'd trained her voice to be forgettable and her face to be furniture. *Edmund Sterling, keeper of the minor accounts. Nothing there. Move along.*

Except a man had died in the guildhall's entrance hall two nights ago with his blood drying into the shape of roads, and he had pressed that blood into *her* hands, and he had said *the heir keeps the gate*, and somewhere between his last breath and the enforcers' too-late arrival, someone had counted what was missing from the old ledgers and found three pages gone.

No. Not yet. She hadn't taken them yet.

But the guildmaster did not deal in *yet*. The guildmaster dealt in debts, and debts, once incurred, were simply waiting to be collected.

Edmund folded the summons into quarters, tucked it beneath her blotter where all finished business went to die, and walked up out of the undercroft toward the office she had avoided for nine years, carrying nothing but herself and the terrible lightness of a woman whose armor had just been weighed and found wanting.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane Ash kept an office at the top of the guildhall where the light came in gray through leaded glass, as if even the sun had agreed to be discreet about its business here. He was not what strangers expected. Strangers expected cruelty made flesh, some scarred brute or silk-voiced monster. Corvane Ash was instead a thin, tidy man of perhaps sixty, with ink-stained fingers and the patient expression of someone who had spent a long career waiting for people to incriminate themselves. He was worse than a monster. He was an accountant.

"Edmund Sterling." He didn't look up from the ledger open before him. "Sit."

She sat. The chair across from his desk was lower than his, an old trick, and she refused to let it make her feel smaller by refusing to feel anything at all.

"Do you know," he said, turning a page with one stained fingertip, "what this is?"

"The Ledger of Foundations," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "The oldest book in the guildhall. Pre-founding records. Nobody's requisitioned it in—"

"Forty-one years. Yes." He looked up then, and his eyes were the color of dishwater and just as difficult to see the bottom of. "And yet two nights ago, a cartographer bled to death in our entrance hall, whispering about gates and heirs, and yesterday morning I find—" he turned the book around so she could see it, "—that pages forty through forty-three have been removed. Razored. Recently. Do you know what was on those pages, Sterling?"

"No," said Edmund.

It was even true, in the way that mattered least. She hadn't known what was on them when she'd first found the wounds in the binding, the pale stubs of cut paper, three days ago, while cross-referencing the cartographer's death against guild acquisitions. But she had known *whose* name headed every surviving reference around those gaps. *Sterling. Sterling. Sterling.* And she had gone back last night, after the guildhall emptied, after the lamps were snuffed, and she had stood in front of the case with the key warm in her pocket, and she had wanted—

"—because I do," Corvane continued, folding his hands. "I have the only other complete record. Pages forty through forty-three concern the Thorn Gate, the binding of the Hollow King, and the family charged with its keeping. A family called Sterling." He smiled without warmth. "You will forgive me if I don't believe your *no*."

"The cartographer cut them out," Edmund said. "He was dying in our hall. He could have—"

"He was searched before his body went to the bone-wagon. He carried a map and nothing else." Corvane rose, unhurried, and crossed to the window. Below, Vessamyre's rooftops steamed in the morning damp, and beyond them the palace crouched on its hill like something sleeping that no one wished to wake. "Here is what interests me, Sterling. In nine years you have never once drawn attention. You balance impossible accounts. You are punctual, silent, and aggressively unremarkable. You are, in fact, the finest piece of invisible work this guild has produced in a generation." He turned back to her. "Which is exactly what she paid us for."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship does when the sea changes its mind.

"*She*?" Edmund said.

"Your mother came to us twenty-two years ago with a remarkable proposition. She had something we wanted — access, knowledge, the last working understanding of the gate's accounting — and she wanted something we alone could provide." Corvane sat on the edge of his desk, regarding her with the mild interest of a man appraising a horse he already owned. "A daughter with no past. We are very good at subtraction, Sterling. Bottled names, traded years, quieted memories. Your mother sold us everything you were, and bought you a life so ordinary that even the crown's eyes would slide off it. And in exchange, you would keep our books, live under our roof, and stay within reach should you ever become... relevant again."

The room was very quiet. Edmund could hear her own pulse in her ears, slow and enormous.

"You're lying," she said.

"I am reading from a contract." He lifted a single sheet from the drawer beside him and held it up, and though she couldn't read the words at that distance, she recognized the seal — a thorn wound through a circle, stamped in wax gone amber with age. Her mother's handwriting was on it. She knew that hand the way you know your own reflection, from letters never sent, from a signature copied onto school forms, from a hundred small domestic evidences of a woman who had died badly and alone while her daughter balanced accounts ten miles away.

Something broke loose in Edmund's chest, and it was not grief. Grief was what she'd felt at nineteen, kneeling at a pauper's graveside. This was hotter and older, a coal that had been banked under ash since before she could remember — because, she understood now, *literally* since before she could remember.

"She hid me," Edmund said. "From the king."

"From what wears the king," Corvane corrected gently. "There is a difference, and it is the entire reason this guild exists. And now the difference has walked into our entrance hall bleeding, named you heir, and died with our oldest secrets possibly in his pockets. So." He returned to his chair and drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, dipping his pen with the calm of a man ordering lunch. "The guild does not forgive debt, Sterling. You are holding guild property — the map, if you took it; the knowledge, whether or not you did. Your mother's contract lapsed at her death, leaving certain obligations unassigned. The cleanest resolution is reassignment. Effective immediately, you will undergo quieting."

The word landed in her stomach like a stone dropped down a well. She waited, counting, for the splash.

"Full extraction," Corvane went on, writing as he spoke. "Not the partial work we did at six years old — that left residue, clearly, or you'd never have dreamed of gardens. This time we take everything back to the cradle. You'll wake with a new history, a new name, a posting far from Vessamyre. You won't suffer. You won't even know what you lost. It's really quite humane, compared to the alternatives." He glanced up. "Do you understand what I'm offering?"

What he was offering was death with the body left walking. What he was offering was the second burial of everything her mother had died to bury — no. To *hide*. To save.

Edmund thought of the map beneath the loose floorboard of her cot, its lines of living blood that had crawled toward a city she'd never visited, that had *moved* under her fingertips like something glad. She thought of the dream: a garden, walled and green and impossibly sunlit, and a woman's voice singing a song about thorns and keys that Edmund always forgot upon waking and always woke with wet cheeks anyway. She thought of pages forty through forty-three, and how she had knelt in the dark archive last night with the razor in her hand, and how she had *not cut them out* — how she had put the razor down and locked the case and walked away, because she was Edmund Sterling, ledger-keeper, and Edmund Sterling did not steal.

Corvane watched her think, and mistook it for surrender, and began writing the order in earnest.

"Yes," Edmund said quietly. "I understand perfectly."

* * *

They gave her until the fourth bell to settle her accounts. Guild procedure; even mercy, here, was filed in triplicate. Two enforcers escorted her down to the undercroft — big men in gray wool with the flat eyes of people who had been quieted just enough to stop asking questions, which was, she understood now, the guild's favorite kind of employee. Herself, included, until recently.

Her desk looked exactly as it always had: neat stacks, squared edges, the inkwell aligned to the blotter's corner. Nine years of her life, arranged to look like nothing was happening. She sat down, and the enforcers posted themselves by the stairs, and she opened the bottom drawer where she kept her personal effects — spare nibs, a tin of mints, a hair ribbon her mother might once have touched.

Beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the map. And beneath the map, because she had lied to Corvane Ash with a clear conscience and intended to go on lying, lay three sheets of old paper, razored from the Ledger of Foundations at half past midnight, when the terror finally grew larger than the obedience.

She had gone back. After putting the razor down, after locking the case, after walking away — she had gotten as far as the stair and stopped, standing in the dark with her heart hammering, listening to a voice that wasn't hers say: *they will take even the questions from you.* And she had turned around.

Now her hands were steady. That was the strange thing. All morning they'd shaken over a mere summons, and now, with theft literally against her skin and quieting four bells away, they were calm as a surgeon's. She slid the pages inside her shirt, against her ribs, where her heartbeat could keep them company. The map went into the oilcloth, the oilcloth into her satchel, the satchel under her arm like any clerk carrying work home.

Then she opened the current ledger to the day's blank page and wrote, in her small precise hand, one final entry:

*Account closed. Balance owed: everything. Collected in full by E.S.*

She blotted it. She squared the book. She stood.

"Done," she told the enforcers. "Ready when you are."

* * *

The quieting room was in the guildhall's cellar, below even the undercroft, down a stair that smelled of cold stone and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in a sealed vase. Edmund had passed its door a thousand times and never wondered about it — another kindness of her manufactured dullness, she supposed, or another cage bar she'd mistaken for wallpaper.

Inside: a chair with leather straps, worn pale at wrist and ankle by decades of other lives. A table of instruments laid out with clerical neatness — needles, vials, a lens of smoky glass mounted in brass that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts through fog. And a woman in a gray apron with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers, arranging the vials in order of use.

"Mistress Hollis," Edmund said. The name surfaced from nowhere, from residue, from whatever her mother's bargain hadn't quite scrubbed away. "You did it before. When I was six."

Hollis's hands paused over the vials. Something flickered across her face — recognition, regret, guilt worn smooth by repetition. "Lie down, dear. It's easier if you don't fight it. The ones who fight remember more of the taking."

"That's rather the point," Edmund said.

The enforcers took her arms. She let them, mostly — enough resistance to be honest, not enough to be pinned, because she needed her legs free for exactly as long as it would take. They strapped her wrists. Hollis bent close with the first needle, murmuring something soothing, and the sweet smell thickened, and the smoky lens was brought near her temple, and Edmund felt the edge of her own mind begin to blur like wet ink —

—and said, "Wait. Please. Before you take it — I want to give something back. A debt settled properly. It's guild law, isn't it? Accounts must balance."

Hollis hesitated. It was guild law; it was practically scripture; and the guild's people were, above all things, ritualists of procedure. "What do you mean?"

"The cartographer's map. It's real. It's valuable. I hid it — under the third-floor archive, in the misfiled acquisition chest marked *Vessamyre, obsolete surveys.* If I'm going to lose knowing that anyway, it ought to be recorded. For the recovery. For the audit." Her voice cracked beautifully on the word *audit*, and she saw Hollis and both enforcers exchange the glance of honest functionaries confronted with honest paperwork.

"It should have been declared," Hollis said reproachfully.

"I was frightened." True. Every word true, and none of it the truth that mattered. "Send someone. Now, please, while I still — before the lens takes it."

One enforcer went. Edmund heard his boots climb the stairs, fast, obedient, and she lay back against the worn leather and stared at the ceiling and did the arithmetic she had learned at her mother's knee without ever knowing she'd learned it: three floors, one confused search of a miscatalogued chest, five minutes minimum. Six, if the chest was heavy. Seven, if the enforcer was thorough.

Hollis adjusted the lens. "Almost time, dear. Think of something pleasant. It helps the separation."

So Edmund thought of a garden. Walled and green, impossibly sunlit. A woman singing about thorns and keys. *Hold fast, little root. The garden remembers its keepers.*

At the top of the house, faintly, a bell rang — not the fourth bell, but the closing bell rung out of hour, which happened for one reason only in the guildhall's long history: when the guildmaster ordered the building sealed.

Boots on the stair. More boots. Shouting, distant and then less distant. The sweet smell faltered as Hollis straightened, listening, and Edmund brought her freed right leg — unstrapped, because they'd only ever bound the ankle nearest the wall, because she had offered her wrist so plausibly — up and over and kicked the instrument table with everything terror had left in her.

Glass burst. The lens hit the floor and rolled, spinning its smoky dark. Vials shattered in their ranks, releasing bottled names in a chorus of whispers, traded years sighing out of their broken vessels like breath, and the room filled with other people's stolen lives, and Hollis screamed something about the cost, and the remaining enforcer lunged for Edmund and caught her sleeve and then caught only air, because she was already moving, small and quick and nine years invisible, through the door and up the dark stair into chaos.

* * *

The guildhall burned noisily in its middle floors and quietly everywhere else — a lamp overturned in the archive during the scramble for the map, or perhaps not an accident at all, because Corvane Ash did not leave loose ends, and a fire that consumed the Ledger of Foundations would consume the evidence of pages forty through forty-three along with every other inconvenient truth the guild had ever bought. Edmund understood this even as she ran, coughing, along the third-floor gallery with smoke curling under the doors, and she felt a savage, unrecognizable joy at the thought of it: *burn, then. Burn all of it. Burn the arithmetic of other people's lives.*

Behind her, the guildmaster's voice cut through the din, thin and precise and absolutely furious: "*Sterling!*"

She did not stop. She went down the service stair two steps at a time, through the kitchen where scullions huddled bewildered among their pots, and out the delivery door into the alley where rain had just begun to fall, fat and gray, hissing on the guildhall's smoking stones.

Three ledger pages against her ribs. A map of blood in her satchel. Behind her, a building full of her whole remembered life going up like a pyre, and somewhere in it, a thin tidy man already composing the warrant. Ahead of her, the north road, and a city she had never visited and dreamed of nightly, and a gate that was failing, and an inheritance written in thorns.

Edmund Sterling pulled her hood up, became — deliberately, defiantly, for the first time on her own terms — nobody at all, and ran.

She was three streets gone before she heard the hoofbeats behind her, measured and unhurried, following at a distance that meant to be noticed.

A rider in a knight's colors, keeping pace with a fleeing clerk through the rain.

She did not know yet whether he was hunting her or warning her. She only knew she had one road, and he was on it.
