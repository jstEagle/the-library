## 7. Roads That Argue Back

Edmund learned the north road the way she had once learned ledgers: by its errors.

The road lied constantly. It promised a village that was only a chapel and a graveyard; it promised flat going and then folded itself into hills like a crumpled letter; it promised, at every bend, that it was nearly over. She walked it with the blood-map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, beneath her coat, and she did not take it out in daylight. The map had opinions. When she unwrapped it at night, the inked roads had shifted to match the real ones, as though the cartographer were still walking beside her, still correcting his work from wherever he had gone.

She talked to him sometimes, in the dark of whatever barn or hedge-row she'd chosen. *You could have told me what I am instead of dying on my floor.* But he had told her, hadn't he? Four words with his last breath: *the heir keeps the gate.* It was only everything after that he'd left unsaid.

On the third morning out of Corvane she crossed a river whose name she didn't know, and on the far bank she found a milestone with the guild's mark chiseled into its face—a closed eye—and someone had scratched beneath it, in a hurried hand: **THEY ARE COMING. HIDE YOUR NAME.**

So Edmund did the one thing a ledger-keeper knows how to do. She struck herself from the record.

At the next crossroads inn she paid a drover's wife three coppers—the last honest coin she owned—to enter her in the room-book as *Mistress Averil Finch, wool factor, bound for Harrowmere.* It was a small forgery. It felt enormous. She watched the woman write the false name in a round, contented hand and thought: *this is how it begins. This is how my mother must have started—small lies in other people's books, until the biggest lie of all was me.*

She slept badly and left before dawn, and if she glanced back at the inn too often, well. Wool factors were allowed to be nervous about their money.

* * *

Wren let her go. That was the sentence he kept turning over like a coin of dubious minting, testing whether it would spend.

He had found her easily enough—the guild's hunters had lost a full day arguing over jurisdiction while Wren simply asked the milestone-wards which way a frightened woman walks, and the wards answered: north, always north, toward the old gate-countries where no sensible person went anymore. He had come up on her at the river crossing, close enough to see the oilcloth bundle under her coat riding against her heart like a second heartbeat. Close enough to call out. He had a writ in his saddlebag with her description and the guildmaster's seal, and behind the writ stood thirty years of law, and behind the law stood a king whose eyes Wren had lately begun to find difficult to look into.

And he had reined in, sat on the hill above the crossing with the morning fog pooling around his horse's legs, and watched Edmund Sterling check her purse, pull her collar up, and walk north out of his life—or so he had intended.

"Stupid," he told the horse. The horse, a bay gelding named Doctrine, offered no opinion. Doctrine never did; it was why Wren trusted him more than most men.

The trouble was the stranger's body. Wren had seen it before the guild's people took it away—had insisted on seeing it, citing protocol, though protocol had nothing to do with it. The dead cartographer had been clutching something when he died, and his fingers had been pried open by men who then lied about what they found. Wren knew the lie. He had written reports containing such lies himself, small ones, procedural ones, the mortar of an obedient career. But he had also seen the man's face in death, and there had been something in it—not fear. Relief. As if dying with the map delivered were a better ending than living without.

*The heir keeps the gate.* Those four words had been reported upward, sanitized, filed. They had also been spoken within earshot of Sir Wren Calloway, Knight-Errant of the Hollow Crown, and they had lodged in him like a splinter working toward the heart.

Because Wren had been to the gate countries. Twenty years ago. He had been young and certain, and he had ridden with six others under sealed orders, and what they had done there he had spent two decades not thinking about, successfully, until a dying man said *heir* and every locked door in his memory rattled on its hinges.

He caught up with her by evening of the second day—at a distance. He told himself he was shadowing her until the guild's hunters arrived, so that he could steer them wrong, and this was even mostly true. But mostly true is a currency the Hollow Crown mints by the barrel, and Wren was beginning to recognize the alloy.

* * *

The ambush came at dusk, in a place called the Throat—a mile where the north road narrowed between hedgerows grown wild and tall, older than any farm, their thorns long as knives. Edmund heard the horses before she understood what she was hearing: not hooves on road but hooves deliberately off-road, muffled with cloth. Someone had come quietly, and quiet meant guild.

She ran. There was no strategy in it; she was a clerk, and clerks run from fire. The hedge swallowed her, thorns raking her sleeves, and she burst through into a pasture just as three riders fanned out across the field behind her, and the leader raised a hand, and the hand held not a sword but a pair of iron shears—

—and then a fourth rider came down out of the dark like weather.

Edmund would remember it afterward as sound more than sight: the crack of a lance taking a rider sideways, the scream of a horse, a voice shouting "DOWN!" with the flat authority of a parade ground, and herself obeying it before her mind caught up, flattening into the wet grass as steel passed overhead where her head had been. When she dared look up, two guild horses were bolting riderless and the third rider was down, and a man in a plain mail shirt with no device on it stood over her with a sword, breathing hard.

She knew him. Of course she knew him. Sir Wren Calloway had interviewed her twice about the dead cartographer, politely, thoroughly, his questions precise as a clerk's own entries, and both times she had felt the strange sensation of being read by someone who was also hiding a page or two.

"You," she said.

"Me." He pulled her up by the wrist, not gently. "You're welcome."

"I don't recall thanking you. Why are you here? You had me on the road two days ago. I saw you on the hill at the crossing—I'm nearsighted, Sir Wren, not blind." His flinch confirmed it. "You let me go. Now you save me. Which of those is the lie?"

The remaining hunter groaned in the grass. Wren crossed to him efficiently, took the man's shears and his knife and a folded writ, and read the writ by the last of the light. His face did something complicated.

"It's your name," he said. "Not the map. The writ says bring in Edmund Sterling alive, map optional. The guildmaster wants *you*, keeper's get, more than she wants paper." He looked up, and in the dusk his eyes were very tired. "That should frighten you more than it evidently does."

"I've been frightened since chapter one," Edmund said, "of my life. Fear and I have an arrangement now: it doesn't slow me down, and I don't pretend it isn't there." She heard how it sounded—the borrowed bravado of a woman who had balanced accounts for fifteen years and never once balanced her own—and hated it slightly, and lifted her chin anyway. "You still haven't answered me."

"No," Wren agreed. "I haven't." He looked east, where the guild's lanterns would eventually come looking for their missing hunters. "We can't stand here negotiating. There'll be more. There are always more; the guild does not forgive debt, and you owe them a lifetime of quiet service plus one escaped prisoner." He whistled low, and a big bay horse picked its way through the hedge-thorns to them. "My name is Wren Calloway. I am, as of approximately half past seven tonight, a traitor. I'd introduce myself properly but we appear to be fleeing."

"And if I refuse to flee with you?"

"Then I'll ride alongside you refusing, at a respectful distance, until one of us changes our mind." He swung up into the saddle and extended a hand. "I know what you're thinking. You're thinking: a knight of the Hollow King, offering rescue—what trap is this? I'll answer honestly. I don't fully know. Two days ago I was following you to bring you back. Today I cut down my own colleagues to keep them from putting shears to your head. Somewhere between the hill and the hedge I changed sides, and I couldn't show you the exact ledger line where it happened, because—" he hesitated, and finished quietly, "—because I didn't write it down. I just looked up and it was done."

It was, Edmund would reflect much later, the most honest thing anyone had ever said to her: an admission of unrecorded change, from a knight, to a bookkeeper. It undid her more than the rescue had.

She took his hand and let him swing her up behind the saddle, and the oilcloth bundle pressed between them, warm as a sleeping bird.

* * *

They rode through the night by hedge-lanes and drove-roads, Wren navigating by a knowledge of the country that surprised her until she remembered—he had been here before, twenty years ago, and did not say so, and she did not ask, because some silences are a courtesy between prisoners.

Near dawn they made camp in a ruined tithe-barn, and the truce required its first maintenance, as all truces do.

"Why do the hunters use shears?" Edmund asked. She was binding the shallow gashes the hedge had given her; Wren, without asking, took over the knot at her shoulder where her fingers couldn't reach. His hands were careful. She noted this the way she noted all entries: precisely, provisionally, pending audit.

"The quieting," Wren said. "Memory-work needs a clean edge. They shear the threads loose rather than cutting—they say it heals smoother." His jaw tightened. "I've watched it done. Once. A man who'd sold the same year twice, trying to feed his children. Afterward he smiled at his wife and asked who she was, and everyone said what a mercy it was, how peaceful he seemed." He tied off the knot. "I reported it as a routine correction. My first lie, though I didn't call it that for years."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you're deciding whether to trust me, and trust built on omissions is just debt wearing a nicer coat. I've carried a great deal of nice coats." He sat back on his heels. In the gray light his face looked younger than his rank. "Ask me anything. One question. I'll answer it straight, whichever way it cuts."

Edmund considered. She had a hundred questions—the hill at the crossing, the gate countries, the word *heir*, the way he had said *keeper's get* as if the phrase tasted of old ash—but she found herself asking the smallest one, because small entries reveal large accounts.

"On the hill," she said. "Two days ago. What exactly stopped you?"

Wren was silent long enough that a lark started up somewhere outside, absurdly cheerful.

"A memory," he said at last. "Of a garden. And a woman singing in it. And—" his voice roughened, steadied, "—and of standing in that garden with orders in my fist and doing nothing. Nothing, Mistress Sterling, while the world was edited around me. I have been the guild's pen for twenty years. On that hill, watching you walk north with your collar up like any poor wool factor, I realized a pen can refuse the hand." He met her eyes. "I didn't choose mercy. I chose to stop choosing obedience. It looks the same from outside. It isn't."

Outside, the lark went on singing, and Edmund thought, absurdly, of a melody she could not place—a woman's voice, a green shade, a feeling like sunlight through leaves—and the thought hurt in a way she refused to examine yet.

"We should sleep in shifts," she said. "You first watch."

"Yes, mistress Finch." The ghost of a smile. "Or shall I say—"

"Don't." But she was smiling too, tiredly, as she rolled into the hay. "Ledger's closed for the night, Sir Wren."

He kept the watch. And in the hour before sunrise, when the barn door stood open to a sky going from ash to rose, Wren saw it: far to the north, beyond hills he knew too well, a thin red light rising against the stars—not dawn, which came from the east, but a glow standing on the horizon like a wound in the world's skin.

Thorn-light. Bleeding through from a gate two hundred years bound, and brightening nightly.

He did not wake her. Some news keeps until morning. But he wrote it in the ledger he was only beginning to admit he kept—the one with no guild seal, no king's mark, just his own uncertain hand:

*Night the third. The gate is failing faster than they know. We ride north tomorrow, both of us, and God forgive me, I think we always were.*
