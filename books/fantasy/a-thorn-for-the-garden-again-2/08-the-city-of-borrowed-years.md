## 8. The City of Borrowed Years

We saw Vessamyre a full day before we reached it.

It announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and cut stone and something sweeter underneath, like fruit kept too long in a closed room—and then by the light. At dusk on the second evening out of the hill country, the road crested a long ridge, and there it lay below us in its bowl of grey hills: a city lit not by lamps but by *glow*, a soft amber radiance rising off it like heat shimmer inverted, as though the whole place were a hearth banking itself for winter.

"That's wrong," Wren said beside me. He had stopped walking without seeming to decide to. "Cities don't shine."

"The map disagrees." I held it up. The blood-lines that had crawled across the vellum since the night the cartographer died had settled now into a single confident stroke pointing down into that light, straight as a plumb line. "The map says we're almost home."

"Home," Wren repeated, in the flat tone he used when he wanted me to hear the word without agreeing to it.

I did not answer him. I was watching the glow. It pulsed—very faintly, very slowly, the way a throat pulses—and I thought, absurdly, of the ledgers in the guildhall undercroft: the columns of traded years I had balanced every morning for six years without once asking where those years went after they left their sellers' hands. Numbers in, numbers out. The ledger never said. Ledgers never do. They only record that something was taken and something given, and let you believe the two things were equal.

I had been balancing accounts for a parasite for six years, and I had called it honest work.

"We camp here," Wren said. "Walk in at dawn, in the cart traffic. Two travelers looking for work won't be questioned. A knight in crown colors and a woman carrying stolen guild papers will be." He glanced at me. "Unless you'd rather argue about it."

"I'd rather sleep," I said, which surprised us both, and we made camp in the lee of the ridge with the shining city pulsing below us all night, like a heart we could see but not yet touch.

* * *

Dawn showed us what the glow was.

The road funneled down through terraced fields gone fallow—weeds waist-high in soil that should have been planted this season—and then through orchards where the trees stood heavy with fruit no one picked. Wren frowned at them. "Who leaves a harvest?"

"Someone who has sold the years he'd need to eat it," I said, because the thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do.

At the Southgate, the truth of Vessamyre arranged itself around us before we were even through the wall. There was a queue—not of carts, but of people, a line of ordinary folk shuffling toward a booth built of pale timber at the gate's shoulder, and above the booth hung a sign carved in cheerful, rounded letters:

**THE CROWN PAYS FAIR FOR HONEST YEARS**

A clerk sat in the booth behind a brass grille, rosy and comfortable as a butcher's dog. Before him, one by one, the people of the queue leaned in, bared a wrist or a palm to whatever instrument waited inside, and spoke numbers. I heard them as we passed with our cart-traffic disguise, heads down, Wren leading an imaginary mule with more conviction than most men lead real ones.

"Ten years," said a stooped man whose hands were mason's hands. "My knees are done anyway. Ten years buys my girl's apprenticeship and the fine paid off."

"Five," said a woman younger than her voice. "Just five, till the spring works come."

"One year," whispered a boy who could not have been sixteen, and the clerk's rosy face folded kindly and said, "Come back when you've got more to give, lad, we don't take scraps," and the boy's shoulders dropped with something that looked terribly like relief—as if being refused the chance to sell a year of his life had been the day's great disappointment.

And each time the clerk's instrument drank, a thread of light rose from the seller's skin—amber, warm, the color of banked fire—and drifted up through a slot in the booth's ceiling, joining the slow golden exhalation that rose off the whole city. That was the glow. I understood it then, standing in the gate shadow with my ledger-keeper's soul turning over and over like a locked wheel. Vessamyre shone because Vessamyre was being spent. Every lamp-lit street, every paved square, every comfortable hour the city enjoyed was bought with years pulled out of its own people and poured upward, into the palace on the hill, into the crown, into—

Into the thing that wore King Aldous's face.

"You're shaking," Wren murmured.

"It's the ledgers." My voice came out strange and small. "Six years I posted entries. *Received: forty years, district of Harlow. Received: twelve years, widow of the tanner's row.* I thought they were going into vaults. I thought someone, somewhere, was *keeping* them." I watched a grandmother lean into the booth, sell three years, and walk away lighter, smiling, thanking the clerk. "They're not being kept. They're being eaten."

"Keep walking," Wren said quietly. "Grieve later. In Vessamyre they arrest you for grieving in public."

I thought he was making one of his grim jokes. He was not. We learned that within the hour.

* * *

Inside the walls, Vessamyre was beautiful the way a well-run lie is beautiful.

The streets were clean and broad and lamplit even at midmorning, the amber light pooling under the trees along the avenues. Fountains ran. Music came from somewhere—a string quartet playing in a public garden, unhurried, exquisite. People strolled. Nobody hurried, nobody jostled, and it took me half a street to understand why: hurry is what life does when life believes it has time to waste. No one in Vessamyre believed that anymore. They moved like guests who know the host has counted the silver.

And everywhere, everywhere, the little shrines.

Not to gods—to the Crown. Alcoves in the walls holding a crowned figure rendered in white plaster, and before each one a shallow dish where passersby paused to press a fingertip and leave a bead of light, a month perhaps, a season, the way other cities' folk leave coins. *For the King's health,* read the little plaques. *For the King's long reign.*

"For his long reign," Wren muttered at the third such shrine. "Two hundred years and counting. You'd think the novelty would wear off."

"They're not paying for his reign." I had stopped before a shrine despite myself, watching an old woman press her finger to the dish with the tenderness people usually save for graves. "Look at her face. She thinks she's buying protection. Safety. The crown takes their years and gives them—" I gestured at the clean streets, the music, the unhurried peace. "This. And this costs the king nothing, because he doesn't have anything to give except what he's already stolen. He's running a city on its own blood and calling it generosity."

"Careful." Wren's voice had gone low and careful, the way it went near guards. Because there were guards—crown soldiers on every corner, but also others: quiet men in undyed wool who stood at street ends doing nothing, whose stillness marked them the way a heron's stillness marks it as something that kills. "In Vessamyre the walls have better hearing than the shrine has mercy. Say it inside your head until we're behind a door."

So I said it inside my head, all the way up the Processional Avenue, past the counting houses with their gilded grilles—counting houses, *more* counting houses, a whole financial district devoted to the trade of human time, clerks in green livery weighing light on little brass scales—and I hated the city with a hatred so clean and total that it frightened me. Not the hot hatred of a soldier. The cold hatred of a bookkeeper finding fraud in columns she herself had signed.

Because here was the thing that made my hands curl into fists at my sides: they were not victims, most of them. Or rather—they were, but they had been *persuaded* into the shape of it. The woman selling five years till the spring works came; the father buying his daughter's apprenticeship with his own knees; the boy refused at the gate who would come back next week with more. None of them coerced. All of them *complicit*, gently, reasonably, one decade at a time, each transaction sensible on its own terms, each seller certain they were the clever one, the one getting fair value. That was the genius of it. That was the horror of it. The Hollow King did not need chains. He needed only arithmetic and patience and a clerk's smile, and people would dismantle themselves voluntarily, gratefully, in installments, and thank him for the privilege.

I knew that smile. I had worn it across a ledger desk for six years.

* * *

We took a room above a dye-shop in the Tanners' Quarter, where the smell covered everything including, Wren observed, any scent-tracking dogs the guild might send, and where the landlady asked no questions because questions cost energy and energy cost years.

It was there, on the second evening, that the complicity came upstairs and knocked.

She was a washerwoman named Maret—sixty if she was a day, hands like boiled rope, eyes quick and kind. She brought us bread and eel stew unasked, and stayed to talk, because the Tanners' Quarter talked; and within a quarter hour of meeting me she had told me her whole account, unprompted, in the tone of a woman reciting figures she had checked many times and always found correct.

Her husband had been a fuller. Twenty-two years ago he'd fallen ill—the lung sickness, the one that comes with the dye vats—and the physic's bill had been beyond them. So she had gone to the Crown's booth, which in those days stood in the market square, and sold fifteen years to cover it. Fifteen years for one winter of medicine. She said it without bitterness, even with a certain pride. "Fair price, mind. Fairer than the physic. And look—" she spread her rope-knuckled hands. "He lived. Twelve more years we had together before the lung took him anyway. Would I make the trade again? Every time. Every time, dearie. You can't eat years."

"And after?" I asked. "After the twelve were gone. What did the fifteen buy you then?"

Maret looked at me for a moment with an expression I couldn't read—pity, maybe, for a question only the young ask. Then she laughed, a soft, rusted sound. "What do the fifteen buy anyone? They buy tomorrow. You sell enough tomorrows and eventually you get one day that's all today." She gathered the bowls. "You've got soft hands and hard eyes, girl. Guild-bred, aren't you? Well—guild or crown, it's all the same ledger in the end. Somebody counts and somebody's counted." At the door she paused. "You'll want to keep off the Processional at dusk, by the way. They're lighting the Tithe Lanterns tonight. First-of-the-month ceremony. Pretty to watch, if you don't think too hard about it."

"What happens if you think too hard about it?" Wren asked.

Maret's face did something complicated and old. "Then you end up like Tam Ossory," she said, "and nobody wants to end up like Tam Ossory," and she was gone down the stairs before either of us could ask who Tam Ossory was.

We found out anyway. The Tanners' Quarter talked.

Tam Ossory had been a schoolmaster, thirty years in the same classroom, beloved. He had never sold a year—prided himself on it, taught his pupils that a life was not coin. But his wife had fallen sick last winter, the same lung sickness, the same arithmetic, and love makes economists of us all. He sold ten years at the market booth. His wife recovered. And Tam Ossory, who had spent his whole life telling children their hours were theirs, walked home from the booth, sat down at his desk, and began writing a letter to the Crown. He wrote it for four days. On the fifth day he nailed it to the doors of the counting house and walked into the Amber Canal, which runs through the heart of Vessamyre and glows at night, softly, beautifully, with the light of everyone's surrendered decades.

The letter was copied and passed hand to hand through the quarter within a week. Wren brought a copy back to our room, having traded a soldier two coppers for it, and read it aloud by candlelight while I sat very still on the bed.

*I am told the years go to the King's keeping,* it read, in a schoolmaster's careful hand. *Then let the King produce them. Let him open his vaults and show me my ten years, whole and living, and I will apologize publicly in every square. But a vault does not breathe, and I have seen the light rise off this city, and I know a body being consumed when I stand inside one. We are not depositors. We are the meal. I have taught children for thirty years that their lives belong to themselves. I find I cannot teach it and live otherwise. Forgive me. Count what remains of me honestly, whoever keeps the books.*

"He wasn't mad," Wren said, folding the letter. His voice was rough. "That's the worst of it. Read it again—it's sane. It's the sanest thing I've read in twenty years of service."

"He was alone," I said. "One man, one letter, one canal. That's what it costs to think too hard in Vessamyre. The city doesn't argue with you. It just waits, and lets you drown yourself politely, and lights the lanterns."

Below our window, right on schedule, the Tithe Lanterns bloomed.

We watched from the dark of the room. Down the Processional, a hundred great lanterns of pale glass swung up on their chains, and into each one the crown's priests poured a measure of the amber light drawn fresh from the evening's collections—and the light didn't behave like flame. It behaved like something alive and willing to be poured. It filled the lanterns and then it *reached*, tendrils of gold pressing against the glass toward the crowds below, and the crowds pressed back, hands raised, faces lifted, drinking in the warmth of their own harvested years like children warming themselves at a fire made of their grandparents.

And above it all, above the lanterns and the spires and the glowing canal, the palace stood black against the stars, and I felt—I could not help feeling; the map against my ribs felt it too, a slow cold tug like a fish taking line—that something up there was watching the ceremony the way a farmer watches a field ripen.

"There," I said. My voice shook. I put my hand flat against the window glass. "Do you feel it? The pull. It's not drawing light tonight. It's drawing *me*."

Wren came to stand beside me. For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the lantern-light washed gold across his scarred face, and I saw in it something I hadn't seen in him before—not doubt, which he carried always, coiled, but decision, arriving.

"Tomorrow," he said slowly, "I'm going to do something stupid."

"Wren—"

"Hear me out. The map points to the palace. You can't walk in. I can barely walk in. But there's one way into that building for a stranger with no papers and a keeper's blood, and it's ugly, and I hate it, and it might be the only thing that saves your life." He turned to face me fully, and the lantern-light caught both of us, and somewhere below, the crowd sang the King's praises in voices warmed by their own burning years. "Tomorrow I'm going to report to my superiors that Sir Wren Calloway, disgraced, has captured the fugitive keeper at last—and is bringing her to the throne."

The pull beneath my ribs tightened, gentle as a hook set in flesh.

"And I'm going to let you," I said.

It was not a question. Neither of us pretended otherwise. Outside, the Tithe Lanterns burned, and burned, and burned.
