## 9. An Audience with the Crown

The palace of Vessamyre had been built to be seen from everywhere in the city, which was, Wren thought, exactly the problem. Every spire of it leaned over the streets like a chaperone at a dance, and every window caught the light of the thorn-lamps below and threw it back down as if to say: *we are watching; we have always been watching.* A king who needed to be seen from every gutter was a king afraid of being forgotten.

And the thing that wore King Aldous's crown could not afford to be forgotten. Not for one hour.

Wren rode through the Gilded Gate at midmorning with his prisoner's wrists bound in silk—not rope, never rope, for a keeper's get might be burned by iron or by hemp, or so the old protocols said—and felt the eyes of the city on him like a low sun. He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it on the road north, in the dark before dawn, while Edmund slept an arm's length away with her cheek on her folded hands and her breathing even as a clerk's ledger line. *I found her. I am bringing her in. I am still yours.* Three sentences, each one a small door closing behind him.

He was growing expert at doors.

Edmund rode beside him with her chin high and her eyes down, playing the captive as though she'd studied the role all her life—which, he supposed, she had. Twenty years of making herself invisible in a guildhall undercroft was its own kind of rehearsal. But when they passed beneath the first of the great arches and the shadow of the palace fell across them both, cold as well water, he saw her knuckles whiten on the reins.

"You don't have to do this," he said quietly, in the space between one sentry's stare and the next.

"Yes, I do." Her voice was level. "The map ends here. And so does running."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." She turned her head just enough that he could see the edge of her profile: sharp jaw, set mouth, the small scar at her temple she did not yet know the origin of. "You meant that once we're inside, your oaths will do what my guild couldn't. You meant that you're delivering me to something that eats memories, and you're not certain anymore whose side of the ledger that puts you on." A pause. The hooves of their horses rang on stone worn glassy by two centuries of processions. "You meant it, and I'm going anyway, because a gate doesn't hold by running from it."

Wren had no answer for that, so he gave the sentries his name instead, and the great doors opened.

* * *

They were expected. That was the first wrongness, and Wren felt it settle into his bones like damp.

No one was ever expected. The Hollow Court prided itself on its weather—the way petitions blew in unannounced, the way supplicants waited days or weeks in the marble antechambers while clerks recorded their names, their years, their debts. Uncertainty was the crown's favorite currency. Yet the moment Wren surrendered his horse to a stableboy who would not meet his eyes, a steward in ash-grey livery was already descending the steps, already bowing, already saying, "Sir Wren. His Majesty is pleased. He will receive the keeper's daughter within the hour."

*The keeper's daughter.* Not *the thief*, not *the fugitive*, not *the woman traveling under suspicion of theft*. Someone above Wren's head had already opened a very old book and read a very old name, and had been waiting for it to walk through the gates.

He glanced at Edmund. She had gone very still—the stillness of prey that has just understood the shape of the snare—but her face betrayed nothing, and Wren marveled again at how thoroughly this woman had taught herself to disappear. He only wished he didn't know it was a skill born of terror.

"The prisoner," the steward said, extending a hand toward Edmund as though requesting a parcel, "will be escorted separately."

"She stays with me," Wren said. "By order of the Master of the King's Justice. She's to be presented formally—evidence intact, testimony whole. If she's questioned out of my presence, my warrant dies and hers with it."

It was a fabrication. There was no such order; there was no such protocol. But the steward hesitated, and in that hesitation Wren saw the machinery of the court grind against itself: everyone here feared someone, and no one quite knew whom. Fear flowed uphill in Vessamyre, all the way to the throne, and even the throne's servants had learned to be careful about what they touched without explicit instruction.

"His Majesty," the steward said at last, "does not object to Sir Wren's attendance."

*Does not object.* As if the king had been consulted. As if the king were listening right now, from somewhere behind a thousand doors.

They were led through corridors that Wren had walked a hundred times and which today seemed redesigned by a stranger's dream: vaulted halls where the borrowed years hung in glass like pale wine, galleries lined with portraits of men and women in gardener's dress—he had never noticed those before, and could not imagine why they had been hung in the seat of royal power, facing the wall at odd angles, as if someone had put them there and then tried to forget them. Edmund slowed before one of them. A woman with dark hair and soil-stained hands stood among rose canes, mid-laugh, painted by someone who had loved her.

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor of himself. He knew that face. He had seen it last by torchlight, twenty years ago, in a burning farmhouse outside a village called Harrow's End, and he had spent two decades telling himself the smoke had blurred his memory.

"Sir Wren." The steward's voice cracked like a whip of silk. "His Majesty waits."

Edmund tore her gaze from the portrait. When she looked at Wren, there was a question in her eyes, and he could not bear it, so he answered the question she hadn't asked: "Later. I'll tell you later."

She nodded once, and they went on, and Wren carried the portrait's laughter behind his ribs all the way to the throne room doors.

* * *

The throne room of Vessamyre had no windows. This was official doctrine—the better to protect the royal person from assassins, wind, and the common light of day—but Wren had long suspected the truth was otherwise. Whatever sat on that throne did not care to be reminded that the world outside ran on sunlight and seasons rather than ledgers and debt.

The doors opened. The court assembled there was thin these days—half the great families had sold so many years to the crown that they had none left for ceremony, and attended now as husks dressed in inheritance—but enough remained to fill the silence with the sound of breathing and the rustle of expensive cloth.

And on the throne, King Aldous.

Wren had sworn fealty to that figure eleven years ago. He had knelt on this same stone and spoken the words of the King's Justice—*my blade is the crown's edge, my breath the crown's breath*—and felt the oath settle over his shoulders like a yoke fitted by a master saddler. In eleven years he had executed forty-one sentences, pursued ninety-three debtors, and buried six doubts in the yard behind his quarters, each deeper than the last.

Now he looked up at his king and made himself see what he had trained himself not to see.

Aldous sat as kings sit, gloved hands on the arms of the throne, crown seated properly upon grey-blond hair. The face was handsome in the way of coins, struck from a die and identical year after year. It was the eyes that betrayed it. Kings' eyes grow tired, amused, cruel, kind. These eyes did nothing. They received the light of the thorn-lamps and returned none of it, and behind them—far back, deep in the architecture of the skull—something moved. Not the flicker of thought. The slow peristalsis of a thing feeding.

"Sir Wren Calloway." The voice came from the mouth, shaped well enough, pitched to carry. "Knight of the Long Watch. You bring me a stray."

"A stray of some consequence, Majesty." Wren crossed the stone floor and knelt, drawing Edmund down beside him with a hand that trembled only slightly, and prayed to every god he had stopped believing in that the trembling read as fatigue. "Found on the north road carrying materials stolen from the Guild of Impossible Things. Among them—" he drew the blood-map from his coat, keeping his eyes on the flagstones, "—this."

He heard the court's breath catch as one animal. Whatever the map was, the court recognized it. That, too, was information, filed away.

The silence that followed had texture. Wren knelt in it and counted his heartbeats and did not look up until the voice said, "Let the woman stand."

He rose and stepped back, and Edmund stood alone in the middle of the throne room, bound wrists and all, small and straight-backed before the throne, and Wren watched the Hollow King look at her.

It was not like watching a man look at a woman.

It was like watching a lamp drawn toward oil. The thing behind the royal eyes leaned forward—not the body, the body stayed perfectly still, perfectly regal—and Wren saw the change come over that coin-struck face the way frost comes over a window: gradually, then all at once. Something woke in the king's expression. Interest. Appetite. Recognition so old and so vast that it had fossilized into patience.

"Sterling," the king said softly.

Not a question. A reading.

"The name is common enough in the guild rolls," Edmund said. Her voice held. Wren marveled at it even as his own mouth went dry.

"It is indeed." The king smiled, and the smile fit the face poorly, like another man's gloves. "Common as thorns. Tell me, Mistress Sterling—do you garden?"

"I keep accounts, Majesty."

"Ah." The smile widened by a degree. "Yes. Ledgers. Your family was always fond of ledgers, in a manner of speaking. Records. Bindings. Little books that tell little lives where they may and may not go." The king's gloved hand rose, one finger extended, and traced a small absent figure in the air—a spiral, a coiling vine, the shape of the map Wren had carried three hundred miles pressed against his own ribs. "Two hundred years, and the guild thinks I've forgotten the family that built my cage. But a king forgets nothing, Mistress Sterling. A king merely misplaces things. And occasionally—" the finger stopped, pointed, aimed at her heart across twenty feet of air, "—misplaced things come home."

Wren saw Edmund sway. Only slightly, only a fraction, but he had spent eleven years reading bodies for the court, and he read hers now: not fear, or not only fear. Something in her was answering that pointing finger, some tide pulled by a moon she had never known she had. The thorn-lamps along the walls guttered in unison, every flame leaning toward her like grass bending to wind, and in the sudden dimness the court murmured, and the king laughed—a sound with no bottom to it, a well-sound, a sound that went down and down and came back up tasting of earth.

"There she is," the Hollow King whispered. "There you are. Two hundred years I have tended my garden, child, and here comes the gardener's daughter walking in of her own free will, wearing borrowed chains." The coin-face turned to Wren, and for one vertiginous instant the eyes were not empty at all; they were full, horribly full, ancient and amused and entirely awake. "Well hunted, Sir Wren. You have brought me the key to my own house."

"Majesty?" Wren kept his voice flat. A knight's confusion was armor; a knight's knowledge was a death sentence.

"The Guild may have her for questioning," the king said, waving one gloved hand as though brushing crumbs. "Quiet her thoroughly. And Sir Wren—you will conduct her to them yourself. Consider it your penance for taking nine days on the north road."

*Nine days.* He had reported four. Which meant the king—or the thing wearing the king—had known the count all along. Had perhaps known it before Wren crossed the city walls. Had let him run, let him find her, let him choose, precisely so that the choice could be weighed and found useful.

Every doubt he had buried in the yard behind his quarters clawed up out of the earth at once.

* * *

They were escorted out by guards who did not speak, through corridors that did not echo, and Wren's mind raced faster than his feet. *Quieting.* He knew what quieting was; every knight of the Long Watch knew, though none said it aloud. The guild's surgeons of memory would open Edmund Sterling like a letter and remove everything worth reading, and whatever walked out the other side would curtsy and sign confessions and remember nothing—not her mother's garden, not the map, not him.

He could not allow it. He examined the thought the way he'd once examined orders, looking for the flaw, the loophole, the loyal construction—and found none. It was simply true, plain as a broken bridge. Somewhere between the north road and the throne room, the arithmetic of his life had changed, and there was no unchanging it.

They reached a junction where the corridor split—one passage descending toward the guild's cells, one climbing toward the guest wings. The guards halted. One of them produced a key ring and reached for Edmund's silk bindings.

"Wait," Wren said.

Every hand stilled.

"The king said *quiet her thoroughly*," Wren continued, and heard his own voice perform the trick it had performed at the gates: confident, procedural, hollow. "But His Majesty also called her *the key*. Would you hand the palace keys to the guild's butchers before the crown has extracted what a key knows? The Master of the King's Justice requires the prisoner intact tonight—deposited, documented, and delivered at dawn. My seal on the transfer." He produced that seal, pressed it into the senior guard's palm, and watched the man weigh it against the word of a hollow-eyed steward and a king who never left his throne. Bureaucracy, that faithful old coward, chose the nearer authority.

"Dawn, sir," the guard said, and they withdrew, and the corridor was empty, and Wren stood in it with Edmund Sterling, both of them breathing like thieves in a house where every clock had begun to strike.

"Dawn," Edmund repeated. Her wrists, freed of the silk, bore faint red lines. Her eyes were fixed on nothing—the middle distance, the place where the Hollow King's face had been. "It looked at me, Wren. Out of his eyes. It knew my name before I spoke. It said—" her voice caught, steadied, "—it said *there you are*. Like something coming home."

"I know."

"And you lied to them." She turned to look at him then, fully, and Wren understood that she had watched him do it, had listened to him build the lie brick by brick in front of armed men, and had known—before he knew—that he was doing it for her. "Why? Your oath—"

"My oath," Wren said slowly, "is owed to the crown. And I have just spent an hour in the company of the crown, close enough to smell it." He looked down at his hands, the executioner's hands, the pursuer's hands. "It isn't a king, Edmund. It's a debt pretending to be one. And I have spent eleven years collecting for it."

The confession hung between them in the lamplight, and neither of them reached to take it down.

Somewhere far above them—so far it might have been the sky itself, or might have been only the palace's oldest tower—a bell began to toll, though no bell in Vessamyre rang at that hour, and no bell in Vessamyre sounded like that: low, green, vegetable, like something growing through the floor of the world. Edmund's hand went to her collarbone, where the map had slept against her skin for three hundred miles.

"The gate," she whispered. "It's starting early. It knows I'm here."

And far below the throne room, beneath foundations no architect would claim, something old and rooted turned in its sleep—and dreamed of a woman's voice, singing.
