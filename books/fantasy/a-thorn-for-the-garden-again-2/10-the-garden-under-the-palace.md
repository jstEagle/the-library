## 10. The Garden Under the Palace

The map had one line left.

Edmund had counted them each night since Vessamyre's gates swallowed them—the blood-drawn roads of the cartographer's dying art, thin as veins, bright as wounds. Every mile they traveled north erased another, the ink sinking back into the vellum like water into drought-parched earth, until only a single thread remained: a line that began at the palace's foundation stone and went *down*.

She studied it now by candlelight in the narrow room Wren had secured for them in the city of borrowed years, an inn that smelled of tallow and old fear. The line did not lead to a throne room, or a tower, or any of the places a rescuer might imagine a king kept. It led beneath the roots of the Hollow King's own house, into the dark under the mountain he had built his glory upon.

"It wants you to go there," Wren said from the doorway. He had not slept; she could tell by the way he held his left shoulder, guarding a bruise from the ambush on the north road that neither of them spoke about directly anymore. "Straight down into the seat of it. Doesn't that trouble you?"

"Everything troubles me." Edmund turned the map toward him. The last line pulsed faintly when her finger touched it—a heartbeat, slow and patient, like something that had waited two hundred years and could wait two hundred more. "But it isn't the map I trust. It's what made the map. Blood remembers its ink, Wren. Whoever drew this was drawing from memory. Mine."

"You don't have those memories."

"No," she agreed quietly. "I have their shape. A door with nothing behind it but cold air." She rolled the vellum and tied it against her ribs, beneath her coat, where she had carried it so long it had worn soft as a second skin. "I mean to open the door."

He looked at her a long moment—grey eyes, knight's eyes, trained to measure distances and threats—and then he did the thing that had begun to frighten her more than any enforcer or hunter or hollow-eyed king. He nodded. He trusted her. And trust, in Edmund's experience, was a debt collected with interest.

"The servants' stair ends at the cisterns," he said. "Beyond that, no one goes. Not even the guard. There are stories."

"There are always stories," Edmund said. "In my experience, most of them are ledgers someone burned."

* * *

They went down at the hour between dog watch and matins, when the palace above them thinned to skeleton crews of yawning guardsmen and the great halls slept under their dust sheets of silence. Wren wore no livery. He moved through the service corridors like a man walking through his own confession—sure-footed, ashamed—and Edmund followed in the shadow of his shadow.

The cisterns were vast and black, vaulted ceilings lost in darkness above the reach of their single lantern, water somewhere dripping in slow metronome. Her breath clouded. The cold here was not the cold of stone. It was older, deliberate, like the cold inside a locked room.

Wren lifted the lantern. "There should be a wall ahead. The plans I saw as a squire showed solid masonry past this point. Nothing but rock to the mountain's heart."

Edmund was already walking. She did not need light. That was the first wrongness, and she noted it the way she had once noted discrepancies in a ledger—with steady hands and a pulse gone quiet with dread. The dark ahead of her was not empty. It was *arranged*. And some part of her, some part buried deeper than the guild's razors had ever reached, knew its arrangement the way a musician knows a scale.

Her hand found the wall before her eyes did. Rough stone, and then—not. A seam. Vines. Living vines, dry and woody, climbing out of cracks that no gardener had tended in living memory, and beneath her palm the wall gave off heat. Faint. Animal. The warmth of a thing asleep.

"Wren," she said. "Bring the light."

The lantern came up. And the wall resolved itself into a gate.

It stood twenty feet high at least, iron and blackthorn grown together so thoroughly that no one could say where craft ended and life began, set into an archway of carved stone that the lantern's flame could barely illuminate fast enough. Thorn roses climbed its bars in dead brown spirals. Above the arch, letters had been cut deep into the rock and filled with something that still glimmered after two centuries: not gold, not silver. Dried blood, she realized. Someone had signed this place the way the cartographer had signed his map.

Edmund read the words without deciding to. They rose in her like water rising in a well.

*WE KEPT IT LIVING. WE KEPT IT LOVING. WHAT IS BOUND IN LOVE NEED NOT BE GUARDED IN FEAR.*

Below, smaller, almost humble, a family mark: a rose between two crossed keys.

Her legs went out from under her. She did not faint—she had never fainted in her life, ledger-keepers do not faint—but she knelt on the wet stone floor with her fingers pressed to the seam of the gate, and the memories came back.

Not all at once. Memory, she would later understand, is a courtesy the mind extends, and hers extended it gently, in pieces, the way you return borrowed things to someone who has been robbed.

*A garden. Not this one—younger, greener, open to a sky she suddenly remembered was wide and kind. A woman kneeling in the dirt beside her, teaching small hands to prune. "Not to punish, Edie. Never to punish. You cut so the living wood grows stronger. That's all a binding is. That's all we ever did."*

*Her mother's voice. She had not known she remembered her mother's voice.*

*"The king came to us frightened," the voice said, in another remembering, another evening, lamplight and the smell of bread. "A real man, our king, and real men can be eaten. So we grew him a cage of roses and sang him to sleep inside it, and the thing that wanted his crown found only thorns. Two hundred years, Edie, if the work holds. You'll be old. Your children will be old. Keep the garden loving and it will hold longer still."*

*And then—the fracture, the razor's edge she could feel even inside the memory, where the guild's surgery had bitten through—her mother's face, turning toward her, mouth forming her name—*

Gone. Cut clean. But the shape of the love remained, the way a wound keeps the shape of the blade.

"They weren't jailers," Edmund said aloud. Her voice cracked on the stone around her and came back strange. "All this time. The Guild of Impossible Things, the keepers, the ledgers—I thought we were prison wardens. My family. The whole rotten inheritance. We were *gardeners*."

Wren had gone very still. In the lantern light his face was pale and unreadable, but he did not look surprised, and that absence of surprise told her he had suspected. Knights who doubt their kings learn to suspect everything.

"Tell me," he said.

So she told him, kneeling at the gate like a woman at a shrine, while the sleeping warmth of the thorns breathed against her palms. The Sterlings had served the true king, Aldous, in the days before his fall—not as soldiers, not as clerks, but as keepers of a living binding, a covenant grown rather than forged. When the parasite-thing that called itself the Hollow King had tried to take root in the royal mind, the Sterling gardeners had done the only merciful thing imaginable: they had put the true king to sleep inside a fortress of thorns and song, and bound the parasite outside the walls of his sleep. The gate below the palace was not a lock on the king.

It was a lock on everything else.

"And the guild?" Wren asked. His voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when he is walking across ice over deep water.

"Grew up around the garden like ivy around an oak," Edmund said. "Servants of the work who forgot what the work was. Then stewards. Then jailers. Then—" she laughed, and hated the sound of it, "—then accountants. Balancing books of stolen years while the garden starved because no one sang to it anymore. No one *pruned* it. You don't leave a living thing alone for two hundred years, Wren. You don't leave anything living alone that long."

The gate shuddered.

It was slight—a tremor in the iron, a rain of dust from the archway, a note struck somewhere below hearing—but they both felt it, and Wren's sword was out before Edmund had finished standing. The dead vines along the bars stirred without wind. And beneath her feet, faint as a word spoken in another room, Edmund heard the sound she had been dreaming for weeks without understanding.

Singing. Far down. Frayed and thin and forgetting itself, but singing. The binding, trying to remember the song her mother's people had given it, with no one left alive who knew the words.

Except her.

"I have to go in," she said.

"That's the throne's own cellar, whatever else it is." Wren's jaw was tight. "If the Hollow King learns what lies under his house—"

"He already knows it's there. He just doesn't know what it is. There's a difference, and it's bought me twenty years of being invisible, and I intend to spend every hour of it." She set her shoulder to the gate, half expecting it to be sealed. It was not. It had never been sealed. It had simply been waiting, and under her hands the thorn roses stirred, brown stems flushing the faintest green, and the gate swung inward on hinges that had not screamed in two centuries because iron forged in love does not rust.

Cold air breathed out. Not dead air—the cold of a cellar full of growing things, of earth and green and patient life. Beyond the threshold, stairs descended into a darkness that her eyes, treacherous, restored, keeper's eyes, began to pierce as though it were merely dusk.

Wren caught her arm. "Edmund."

She turned. In the lantern light he looked young suddenly, and unarmored, and afraid in the specific way that men are afraid when they cannot name what they feel. She was learning to read that look. She suspected, with a flutter she refused to examine, that she had read it before, once, in a life that had been taken from her.

"If what's down there gives back what was taken from you," he said slowly, "you may remember things about me. About my order. About the night your mother died." His grip tightened, then loosened, as if the words cost him blood to speak. "I want to say before you find it, whatever it is—I would rather you hear it from me. When you're ready. If you're ever ready."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. The singing rose faintly from the dark below, and the gate's roses leaned toward her like children toward a returning parent, and she understood that whatever Wren carried, it was heavy enough to bend a knight nearly double, and that he had carried it alone for twenty years.

"Then come down with me," she said. "And tell me on the way. We've both been keeping ledgers too long."

Something broke in his face—not grief, quite. Relief, maybe, or its twin.

She stepped through the gate. The thorns closed softly behind them both, green now, unmistakably green, and far above in the sleeping palace a king with hollow eyes turned over in his bed without waking, and dreamed, for the first time in a hundred years, of a garden he could not enter—and began, in his hunger, to send for his knights.
