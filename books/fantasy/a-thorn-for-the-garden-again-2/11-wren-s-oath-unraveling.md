## 11. Wren's Oath, Unraveling

They took his sword at the door of the Chamber of Inquiry, which was proper, and they took his name from the duty roll, which was not. Sir Wren Calloway had been on that roll for nineteen years. He watched a clerk draw a single line through it with the same bored efficiency he might use to strike a spoiled entry for grain or candlewax, and something in Wren's chest went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when a door has been left open somewhere and the cold is already inside.

"Wait here," the clerk said. "The Lord Inquisitor will see you when he sees you."

Wren waited. The chamber was a long room paneled in black oak gone gray with age, lit by lamps that burned a color he had never quite been able to name—amber with a suggestion of green at the heart, like light strained through pond water. Palace lamps. Hollow lamps, if half of what Edmund had told him was true, and he was no longer able to dismiss any fraction of what she told him as untrue. He had watched thorn-light crawl beneath her fingernails like ink beneath a pen. He had seen the map drink her blood and *answer*.

He stood with his hands clasped behind his back because a knight awaiting judgment stands so, and because otherwise his hands would have found his sword belt where no sword hung, and that would have told the Lord Inquisitor everything.

An hour passed. Two. This too was technique; he knew the grammar of this room as he knew drill formations. They would let him stand until his knees learned humility. He let them. He thought about Edmund instead, locked three floors above in the guest wing that was not a guest wing, thinking—God willing—that he had brought her here as bait and not as shelter, that his treachery wore the honest mask of duty. It had to stay that way. If she looked at him wrong in a corridor, if she said his name with warmth instead of wariness, then everything he was about to do in this room would come apart before he finished doing it.

The door opened at last, and Lord Inquisitor Marrow came in the way frost comes under a door: without hurry, without sound, arriving everywhere at once.

"Sir Wren." Marrow did not sit so much as assemble himself behind the desk, folding down into it like a spider closing its legs. "Nineteen years in the crown's livery. Sworn at seventeen. Decorated twice. And now." He spread his hands over a folder that Wren knew contained reports he had written himself, arranged beside reports written about him. "Now you find a dying man in the guildhall of Vessamyre's least important guild, and within a fortnight you are standing in a palace corridor with the keeper's daughter on your arm, calling her your prisoner."

"She is my prisoner," Wren said.

"Is she." Marrow's eyes were pale, the gray of old snow. "Then tell me, Sir Wren, why the gate-wards registered nothing when you brought her through. Why her cell door was found unbarred on two mornings. Why you requested—" he consulted the page without appearing to read it, "—*requested*, mind, assignment to her supervision. A man does not volunteer to guard what he has captured. He volunteers to keep others from questioning it."

"The woman is bait," Wren said. "I've said so. The cartographer died passing her something. The guild wants it back. I am the hook we've set in the water. If you put another fisherman's hand on the line, you'll lose the catch."

It was a good answer. It was even, in its crooked way, a true one—he *was* the hook; he had used himself exactly so. But he heard the seam in it, small as a hairline crack in porcelain, and he watched Marrow hear it too.

"You were there," Marrow said softly, "twenty years ago. The purge of the keeper's line. You were a young man, newly sworn. You rode with Ser Hallam's company."

The floor did not move. The lamps did not gutter. But Wren felt the room tilt anyway, the way a deck tilts when the sea beneath decides, without malice, to remind you it is not solid.

"I was," he said.

"And you have never once filed a report of that action. Never requested transfer from it afterward, though every other man in that company did within the year. Never spoke of it at all." Marrow leaned forward, and his shadow leaned with him, longer than the lamp should have allowed. "A man who cannot forget either breaks, or buries. Which are you, Sir Wren?"

"I buried it," Wren said. "As I was ordered to. As we are all ordered to."

Marrow studied him for a long moment. Then he closed the folder, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room because it was almost kind.

"Nineteen years," he said. "I want to believe nineteen years outweighs a fortnight of strangeness. Prove it to me. Tomorrow evening the court convenes. His Majesty wishes to meet the keeper's daughter personally. You will deliver her, composed and compliant, to the throne room at the eighth bell." He rose; his shadow rose with him and kept rising. "And Sir Wren—if there is anything left in you that doubts, bring it to me tonight rather than tomorrow. Doubt handled early is loyalty. Doubt discovered late is treason. There is no third thing."

"No third thing," Wren echoed.

He bowed. He walked out past the clerk who had struck his name from the roll, and collected his sword, and only in the corridor, alone with the pond-green lamplight, did he permit his hands to shake.

* * *

He had been seventeen, and certain of everything.

The oath hall of Vessamyre's palace was older than the palace around it—the architects had simply built outward from it, generation after generation, the way a tree builds rings around its heartwood. So it was that the oldest room in the kingdom held the newest knights each spring, and Wren had stood on worn flagstones with forty other boys his age, in armor still bright enough to hurt the eyes, waiting to kneel before the King of Vessamyre and bind his life to the crown forever.

His father had ridden two days to watch. His mother had sent a scarf she'd woven herself, blue and white, and he wore it beneath his gorget where no one could see, because sentiment was for civilians and he was done being a civilian. He remembered being hungry in a way he had never been hungry since—not for food but for the moment itself, for the weight of the moment, wanting it so badly that the wanting felt like illness.

The King had come in state, and the trumpets had sounded, and the boys had knelt in their row like wheat before wind.

Wren had looked up—one heartbeat, no more, the smallest breach of protocol—and seen the face of Aldous Sterling, the Thrice-Crowned, the Beloved, the king whose portrait hung in every tavern from Vessamyre to the coast.

He had thought, in that first unguarded instant: *he looks tired.*

That was all. That was the whole of the first suspicion—a boy's observation, dismissed before it finished forming. Kings were tired. Kings carried kingdoms. There was no mystery in a weary face.

Then the King had spoken, and laid hands upon each bent head in turn, and Wren had felt that hand rest on his own hair, cool as river stone, and the words of the oath had risen in him unbidden because he had rehearsed them a thousand times in the stable loft: *I give my breath to the crown, my blood to the throne, my days to the king, till the last of my days be spent.*

And the King had answered, in the formal cadence every knight in that hall received: *I take your breath. I take your blood. I take your days.*

But Wren, kneeling there with his heart hammering against his ribs like a bird against a window, had noticed—and this was the second thing, the thing he buried for twenty years beneath decorations and drills and dutiful silence—that the King's voice did not change. Not at all. Each boy received the same nine words, yes, ritual demanded sameness—but there was a sameness beyond ritual in it. The tone of a man reading an inventory. The intonation of a mill grinding grain that means nothing to the grindstone.

The boy after Wren was called Osric, a farm lad with a stammer who tripped over the word *breath*. The King had waited, patient as furniture, and answered him in precisely the same voice, with precisely the same pause, at precisely the same beat—as though the response were not given to each knight but merely played, the way a music box plays the same tune however many times you lift the lid.

Wren had stood, knighted, glowing, his mother's scarf damp with sweat against his throat, and told himself he had imagined it. Told himself for nineteen years.

Until the night in the guildhall, when a bleeding stranger whispered *the heir keeps the gate*, and a woman with ledger-ink stains on her fingers had looked up at him with eyes that held more honest fear than he had ever seen in the throne room, and Wren had understood, all at once, with the sick clarity of a man waking mid-fall:

A king who is tired is a man. A king who is *hollow* is something else wearing the tiredness like a coat.

* * *

There had been a third moment. He rarely let himself approach it, even in memory, but walking the corridors that night toward Edmund's guarded wing, he found his feet carrying him past the Gallery of Accessions without deciding to go there, and the portraits pulled it out of him whether he willed it or not.

Six months after his oath. He was nineteen, posted to the inner guard, and he had drawn the midnight watch outside the royal apartments—a plum assignment, quiet, coveted. Somewhere past the second bell he had heard singing.

Not from the apartments. From below. Faint, threading up through the stones themselves, a woman's voice in a mode he didn't recognize, the melody simple and looping, the kind of song one sings to keep time at work. Pruning, maybe. Weeding. It had made him think, absurdly, of his grandmother's garden, though his grandmother had died before he could walk.

He had reported it, because a guard reports. His sergeant had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear of superiors and had said, very quietly, *You heard nothing. There is nothing below the palace. Write nothing.* And Wren—who was loyal, who was nineteen, who wanted to believe the world was as orderly as his oath promised—had written nothing.

The singing had stopped by morning. It never came again in all his years of service. But sometimes on bad nights, sleepless in the barracks, he would hear it anyway, memory singing to memory, and wonder what grew in the dark beneath the throne of Vessamyre that needed tending.

Now he knew. Now he stood in the gallery with the painted keepers watching him—generations of stern faces, and among them, here and there, a woman with Edmund's jaw, a man with her way of looking slightly past the viewer as though listening for something underground—and he knew, and knowing had cracked him open like ice going out of a river.

* * *

Edmund's door was barred from outside, as it should be. Two guards flanked it, as they should be. Wren gave the countersign, checked the bar, performed the theater of vigilance down to the last gesture, and then stepped inside and closed the door on his own audience.

She was awake, of course. She sat on the floor by the window with the stolen ledger pages spread around her like a card reader's spread, and she looked up at him with the lamplight making copper of her hair, and she read his face the way she read accounts—instantly, completely, finding the error in the column.

"They know," she said.

"They suspect. Suspicion isn't proof yet, but it's a debt accruing interest." He crouched across from her, keeping his voice low. "Tomorrow evening, eighth bell. The Hollow King wants to see you. Personally."

He watched her absorb it. She didn't flinch; she went still instead, the stillness of deep water, and he was beginning to understand that her stillness frightened people because they mistook it for submission, when it was actually the opposite—a body holding position against a current only she could feel.

"All right," she said.

"That's not all. Edmund—if this goes wrong tomorrow, if Marrow decides to stop trusting me, you need to know what I am. What I've been." His mouth was dry. Nineteen years of silence, and here it came up his throat like floodwater. "I rode with the company that hunted your family. Twenty years ago. I was there."

Her hands stopped moving over the ledger pages.

"I didn't know what we were hunting. That's not absolution—I signed myself blind and called it honor. But I need you to know it before tomorrow, because if I die in that throne room, or if you're taken from me, you shouldn't carry the belief that every man who came for your family was only a mask on the thing in the palace." He forced the rest out. "Some of us were just boys who couldn't read what we were guarding."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the palace bells marked some hour neither of them cared to count. Then Edmund gathered the ledger pages, squared their edges with the neatness of a woman who had spent her life balancing impossible accounts, and looked at him.

"Then tomorrow," she said quietly, "you'd better live."

It was not forgiveness. He hadn't earned it and knew better than to ask. But it was a door left unbarred, and after nineteen years of sealed rooms he nearly wept at the draft of it.

When he left her, the corridor was empty, the guards at their posts, everything in order. Wren walked toward the barracks with his boot heels ringing on stone, and at the junction by the steward's stair he stopped, and turned instead down the servants' passage, and found the young archivist's door where the maps were kept—the door Marrow had ordered watched, the door belonging to the old man everyone called the Cartographer's Daughter's keeper, the last living soul who might know what the dead mapmaker had truly drawn.

Wren raised his fist to knock.

Behind him, at the end of the passage, a lamp turned green at its heart—and someone began, very softly, to follow him.
