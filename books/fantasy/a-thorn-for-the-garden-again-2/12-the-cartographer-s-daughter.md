## 12. The Cartographer's Daughter

The archivist lived in a room the size of a coffin, and it was killing him at the pace of a slow clock.

His name, when Wren coaxed it out of him, was Oskar Vane, and he had spent forty years cataloguing the palace's forbidden collections—the shelves that did not appear on any inventory, the drawers that required three keys and a lie. Now he lay on a pallet among his own treasures like a man drowning in a sea of paper, his breath rattling in and out of him in the rhythm of a man counting something he would never finish counting.

"He asked for you," Wren had said, when he'd found Edmund in the undercity's borrowed hours, in the safehouse beneath the tanner's row where the smell of lime covered the smell of fear. "The old man. He said—" Wren had hesitated, and Edmund had learned in the last weeks to fear Wren's hesitations more than his certainties. "He said, *tell the keeper's daughter the ink is still wet.*"

So now she crouched in a coffin-sized room, holding an oil lamp close to a dying man's face, and waited for the ink to dry.

"You're her." Oskar Vane's eyes were milky at the edges, cataract-thick, but they found her with the terrible accuracy of the nearly blind. "You have her hands. Maren's hands. She had the same way of holding them, like she was always about to write something down and always deciding not to."

Edmund's throat closed. "You knew my mother."

"Knew her?" The old man laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough became a long wet silence in which Edmund thought he might simply stop. "Child, I sold her the paper."

* * *

He would not be hurried. That was the first thing Edmund learned about Oskar Vane: forty years among forbidden archives had taught him that truth, like a rare manuscript, was ruined by rough handling. He made Wren fetch him watered wine he barely touched. He made Edmund move the lamp twice. And all the while, outside the little room, Vessamyre was coming apart at its seams—thorn-light bleeding up through the cobbles in veins of green-gold fire, borrowed years rotting in their owners' bodies, the city's bells ringing wrong, ringing in patterns Edmund's bones recognized and her mind refused to name.

The gate was failing faster now. She could feel it in her teeth.

"The man who died in your guildhall," Oskar began at last, "the one who pressed the map into your hands. You've been calling him a stranger. A dying stranger. A messenger."

"What else would I call him?"

"His name was Emrys Vale." The old man's voice dropped into the register archivists use for names that must be pronounced correctly, as if the record itself were listening. "Last of the Vale line. Mapmakers to the Thorn Gate for six hundred years. The Sterlings kept the gate, child. The Vales *drew* it. Every binding needs two arts: the hand that holds the thorn, and the hand that draws the map of what the thorn holds in. Your family were the gardeners." His milky eyes glittered. "His family were the gardeners' eyes."

Edmund sat back on her heels. The lamp flame leaned and steadied. Somewhere above them, a bell rang its wrong pattern again, and she felt the vibration pass through the floor like a pulse through a wrist.

"The map in my satchel," she said slowly. "It's not a map *to* the gate."

"It's a map *of* the gate. Of the binding itself. Drawn in his own blood, because ink lies and blood doesn't—or it didn't, before your guild learned to trade in it." Oskar's hand crept across the blanket toward her, and she took it, and it weighed nothing, a bird's bones wrapped in skin. "Emrys spent twenty years redrawing it from memory after the guild razored the originals out of existence. Twenty years hiding in plain sight, selling survey maps to merchants, and underneath every honest line, the dishonest one. The true one. Waiting for a hand that could read it."

"Mine."

"Yours. Though he didn't know it until three months ago, when he saw you across a market square in Greyharbour and your face stopped his heart." The old man smiled, gap-toothed and gentle. "You have your mother's face too, you know. He said it was like watching a ghost buy onions."

The laugh that came out of Edmund was ragged and unexpected. Then it died, because the next thought arrived, and it was not a kind one.

"If he recognized me. If he knew what I was—" Her hand tightened on the old man's. "Why didn't he *tell* me? Why hand me a map and a whisper and die on a guildhall floor like a stranger? Why let me spend weeks blundering through this with a knight I should have run from?"

"Because he couldn't tell you." Oskar's grip strengthened with the last of a dying man's urgency. "Child. Listen to me. Listen, because I don't have the breath to say it twice. Your mother didn't just hide you from the Hollow King. She hid you from *yourself*. And the bargain she struck—"

"I know about the bargain. The guildmaster told me, before he sentenced me to the quieting. She sold my memories to buy my safety. I've had weeks to swallow that."

"You know the shape of it. You don't know the terms." Oskar pulled her closer, and his breath was sour and shallow and close. "Anyone can sell a memory to the guild. That's their whole trade—years, names, faces, first loves, all of it, bottled and shelved and sold to strangers who want to have been someone else. But a memory *given* is different from a memory *taken*. A memory taken leaves a scar. A memory given leaves a *door*. Your mother didn't sell your past, Edmund. She *sealed* it. She paid the guild to make your ordinary life a lock, with your true memory on the far side of it, and the key—"

He stopped. The cough took him, long and terrible, and Edmund held him through it, and when it passed he was lighter in her arms, as if the coughing had carried away something that wasn't phlegm.

"The key," Edmund said, when she could speak. "What was the key?"

"Blood in the ink." His eyes found hers, and for a moment the cataracts seemed to clear, and she saw the young man he had been, the one who had sold paper to a desperate woman twenty-two years ago and kept her secret for two decades out of something he probably called professionalism and everyone else would have called love. "Emrys's map. Blood remembers its ink, and ink remembers its author's line. The moment your blood touched his map, the lock your mother built began to open. That's why he couldn't tell you. That's why no one could. The Hollow King can tear a secret out of any mind in Vessamyre—it's worn a king's crown for two hundred years, it has had *practice*—but it cannot open a door that only opens from the inside. Your mother made sure that the only person in the world who could restore your memory to you was *you*, freely, at the moment of your own choosing. She took the choice away from everyone but the one person it belonged to."

Edmund let go of his hand.

She stood. She walked the three steps the room allowed, turned, walked them back. The lamp threw her shadow huge and wavering against shelves of forbidden things—bottled names, ledger pages, a jar of something that glowed faintly and that she did not want to examine.

"She let me grow up *empty*," Edmund said. The words came out flat, and then they didn't. "Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years of being nobody. Of balancing other people's stolen years and calling it a life. Of flinching from every shadow without knowing why. Of dreaming about a garden I've never seen, a garden that was *mine*, and waking up thinking I was mad." Her voice cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there. "She watched me be small. She *made* me small. And she called it love."

"She called it the only door the thing on the throne couldn't pick," Oskar said quietly. "And she was right. And it cost her everything, and she paid it smiling, and if you want to be furious at her, child, you'll have to get in line behind her ghost, because I promise you no one was ever angrier at Maren Sterling than Maren Sterling."

"You don't know that."

"I have her letters." The old man's hand moved feebly toward a shelf, toward a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth, and Wren—silent in the corner this whole time, Wren who had not spoken because he had known, Edmund realized, he had *known* since the undercity, since the words *keeper's daughter* had left his mouth and he had watched her face—Wren fetched the packet down and put it in her hands without meeting her eyes.

* * *

The letters were few. Seven, in a hand that Edmund recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water, because it was *her* hand, or nearly—the same narrow, disciplined script, the same habit of pressing too hard on the downstrokes. She had learned to write by imitating a woman she couldn't remember, and no one had ever thought to mention it.

She read them by lamplight while Oskar Vane's breathing slowed and Wren stood guard at the door, and the room grew very cold, or Edmund grew very cold, and the letters said:

*The guild wants a price I can pay and a price I cannot, and I have chosen the one I cannot. They will call it a sale. It is not a sale. It is a wall, and I am the mortar, and she is the thing the wall protects, and if she never forgives me then the wall has done its work, because a daughter who is free to hate her mother is a daughter the Hollow King cannot use.*

*I saw her today. They let me see her once a year, as an "ordinary clerk's widow paying respects to an old colleague," and she has my chin and my terrible handwriting and she balances her ledgers with her left thumb anchoring the page, exactly as I taught her without ever teaching her, and she laughed at something the guildmaster said and I thought: let her laugh. Let her laugh at small things for the rest of her life. Small things are the only things he cannot taste.*

*Emrys writes that the map is finished. I have not seen it. I will not see it. What the eyes don't hold the crown can't steal. I have become very good at not knowing things. It is the family art now, apparently.*

*If you are reading this, then the lock is open, which means you chose to open it, which means you are braver than I was, because I could not bear to know you and so I made sure I never could. I am sorry. I am so sorry. I planted a thorn rose over the gate the day you were born and I have never stopped watering it.*

*Forgive me or don't. But live. Live loudly, live ordinarily, live so far from the thorn that you forget the word. That was the whole of my ambition for you. Everything else was survival.*

The last letter was dated nine days before the night Edmund's mother died. Nine days before the night Wren Calloway, then a knight of twenty, rode with the hunters to a house on the edge of Greyharbour.

Edmund looked up from the letters and found Wren watching her from the doorway, and his face was the face of a man standing at the edge of a confession he had already made once and could not survive making twice, and she looked away from him first, because she was not ready, because the fury in her chest needed somewhere to go and she could not—*could not*—spend it on him tonight.

"The Hollow King," she said instead, to Oskar. "You said it can't taste small things. That it feeds on what matters. My mother built a wall of ordinariness around me. But the gate is failing now, and the thorn-light is in the streets, and the whole city is starting to remember what it's sold. So tell me what happens when the wall comes down. Tell me what happens when the thing on the throne finally tastes what my mother hid."

Oskar Vane smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had spent forty years reading the endings of stories and had learned to deliver them gently.

"It doesn't want the city, child. The city is a pantry. It wants the *door*—the gate your family kept, the one door out of its prison that was never locked from the outside. For two hundred years it has been patient, because the binding held and the keepers kept. But the binding is failing, and the last keeper is standing in a coffin-sized room holding her mother's letters, and it has finally worked out where she is." His hand rose, trembling, and pointed past her, past Wren, at the wall—at the direction of the palace, of the buried garden, of everything. "It has known since the moment your blood touched that map. It has been *watching* you since Greyharbour. Everything that has hunted you—the enforcers, the quieting, the knights on the north road—was it, wearing the guild like a glove. It let you run, Edmund. It let you run all the way here, because the gate opens from the inside, and it cannot open it, and it has finally understood that it doesn't have to."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere far above, the wrong bells stopped ringing all at once, and in the silence that followed, Edmund felt it—not heard, *felt*—a pressure against the edges of her mind, vast and patient and pleased, like a hand testing a door that has just, for the first time in twenty-two years, swung inward.

"It only ever needed you to remember," Oskar whispered. "Because the last thing your mother sealed inside that lock, along with your name and your garden and your inheritance—"

His breath failed him. Edmund leaned close. The old man's lips moved against her ear, and what he said was four words long, and they went through her like a blade of green fire:

"—was *the way out*."

* * *

Oskar Vane died an hour before dawn, between one rattle of breath and the next, mid-count, his finger still moving on the blanket as though ticking off a final entry in a ledger only he could read. Wren closed the old man's eyes and covered him with his own cloak, and Edmund stood in the doorway with the letters against her chest and the map heavy in her satchel, and she did not cry, because the fury was still there, banked and burning, and fury is a kind of fuel.

"He's right," Wren said quietly. Behind him, through the little window, the thorn-light was rising off the streets in columns now, green-gold and slow, like the city exhaling something it had held for two hundred years. "What the old man said. It's been herding us. Every step we've taken since Greyharbour, I keep turning it over, and every step was *easier* than it should have been. The hunters who missed. The gate that was unwatched. The archivist who knew everything and told us everything and asked for nothing." His jaw worked. "It wants you in that garden, Edmund. It's wanted you there from the start."

"Then it's made a mistake," Edmund said.

Wren looked at her.

"It thinks my mother's lock was a cage." She touched the satchel, and beneath the leather she felt the map's lines shift and crawl, blood remembering its ink, ink remembering its author, the whole buried inheritance of the Sterlings and the Vales straining toward the garden under the palace like a root toward water. "It wasn't a cage. It was a seed. And it has just spent two hundred years making sure the seed got planted exactly where it needed to be."

She stepped out into the thorn-lit street, and above the rooftops of Vessamyre, the Hollow King's palace blazed with light, and every bell in the city began, at once, to ring.
