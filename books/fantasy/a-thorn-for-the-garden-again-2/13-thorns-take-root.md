## 13. Thorns Take Root

The first sign came at dawn, and it came as a smell.

Edmund had woken in the undercity safehouse with Wren's cloak over her shoulders and the taste of iron in her mouth, and for a long moment she could not name what she was smelling—something green and sweet and wrong, like roses blooming in a charnel pit. Then she went to the narrow window slit and saw it: a thread of light running down the cobbled street outside, pale gold shot through with veins of deep red, moving like water finding its level. It seeped between the stones. It climbed the walls of the tenement opposite in thin luminous filaments, and where it touched the mortar, the mortar *bloomed*—tiny white flowers opening along cracks that had stood empty for fifty years.

Thorn-light.

She knew it the way she had known the map, the way she had known the garden beneath the palace—not with her mind but with something older than her mind, something that had been carved out of her and buried so deep that even the guild's razors had missed it. The light was leaking from the gate. The binding was failing.

"It started an hour ago," said Maren, the archivist's daughter who had smuggled them out of the palace district before the bells finished tolling. She stood in the doorway of the safehouse, arms crossed, face gray in the thorn-glow. "Down by the Tanners' Row first. People thought it was a festival. Then old Cobb Harrow—" She stopped. Swallowed. "You should come see what happened to Cobb Harrow."

* *

What had happened to Cobb Harrow was standing in the middle of Tanner Row, surrounded by a crowd that did not dare come closer, weeping.

He had been ninety-one years old. Everyone in Vessamyre knew Cobb; he had sold forty of his years to the crown in the famine winter of his middle age, the way half the city had, trading decades of his life for bread and the king's protection, and everyone knew him as a bent and ancient man who shuffled to the well each morning and told the same three stories to anyone who would listen.

The man standing in the street now was not bent. He was not ancient. He looked forty-five—the age he had been when he sold his years—and his hair was black and his back was straight and his hands, raised before his face as though they belonged to a stranger, were smooth and strong and trembling.

"Give them back," he kept saying. His voice cracked on every word. "Give them *back*, I paid for those, I *paid*—"

Because that was the thing about borrowed years, Edmund understood now, watching from the edge of the crowd with Wren a half-step behind her. The Hollow King had never bought anything. He had only ever taken loans against time that was never his, held it in the gate like coin in a vault—and now the vault was cracking, and the coin was running out, and the debts were being called all at once.

Cobb Harrow had not been given back his youth. His youth had been ripped out of the king's hoard and shoved back into him all at once, unasked, unwanted, sixty stolen years returning like floodwater into a house built for a smaller life. And behind the returned years, Edmund could see it even from where she stood—a hollowness spreading through him, a rot at the root. The years coming home were *rotting*. Whatever the Hollow King had done to keep them, two centuries of feeding on them had soured them past saving.

By noon there were eleven more of them in the streets. By evening, a hundred and nine. The guildhall's ledgers would have called it a miracle. The priests called it a plague. The criers called it nothing at all, because by nightfall the criers had been silenced, and soldiers lined the boulevards instead, and the order went up in chalked letters on every corner:

*ALL WHO SELL YEARS REPORT TO THE PALACE STEWARD. ALL WHO SHELTER THE UNBOUND REPORT TO THE PALACE STEWARD.*

*THE KEEPER'S GET IS FOUND AND WILL BE BURNED.*

Edmund read the last line by the light of a thorn-vein crawling up the wall beside it, and felt Wren go very still beside her.

"They know," he said quietly. "About you. About the garden."

"The steward knows. The king has always known." She heard her own voice come out flat and cold and was distantly surprised by it. "It's the city I'm worried about."

* *

The city turned faster than she could have imagined, and slower than she feared—both at once, which was somehow worse.

In the Lamb's Rest tavern, where Maren found them bread and a corner table on the second night, the talk ran hot and frightened. A dyer's wife swore her mother had aged twenty years in a single afternoon, all the softness gone out of her face, the borrowed decades simply *evaporating* as the gate leaked them away. A ferryman said his brother had taken the opposite road: young again, screaming, begging the saints to take the years back. A boy no older than fifteen sat in the corner with his chin on his fists and said, loudly enough for the whole room, that his father had sold six years last spring to pay the tithe, and if those years were gone now then the king had robbed them, hadn't he? Wasn't that just robbery with a crown on?

"Careful," hissed the innkeeper.

"Why?" the boy said. "They'll burn us either way. You heard the proclamation. *Keeper's get.* They say there's a woman walking the streets with blood that holds the whole city together, and when they find her—" He made a small, ugly gesture, thumb dragged across his throat. "Maybe she deserves it. Maybe she's why my father's six years are gone."

Every head in the tavern turned toward her.

Not because anyone knew. Because fear makes everyone look, and looking is halfway to finding. Edmund kept her eyes on her bread and let Wren's shoulder press hers, solid and warm, a wall between her and the room.

"She doesn't deserve it," Wren said—not to the boy, not loudly, but the room heard anyway. "Whatever she is."

"You don't know her," the boy shot back.

"No," Wren agreed. "I know the man who put that price on her head, though. I served him for twelve years." He lifted his cup. "I'd think twice about doing him favors."

The boy flushed and looked away. The room's attention broke like a fever sweating out. But Edmund felt the shape of it settle over her anyway, the way a net settles: *the keeper's get*, whispered through Vessamyre, growing teeth with every retelling. By morning there were drawings nailed to the church doors—a woman, hooded, a red thread running from her chest into the ground like a root. Someone had written beneath it, in careful clerk's hand: *SHE HOLDS YOUR YEARS IN HER HANDS. MAKE HER GIVE THEM BACK.*

"That's not how it works," Edmund said helplessly, staring at the notice. "I can't give them back. I barely know what I *am*."

"I know." Wren tore the paper down, folded it once, and burned it in a beggar's brazier with the unhurried care of a man destroying evidence. "But grief doesn't need to understand a thing to blame it. I've seen it before. After the Saltmarsh levies, when the crown took a tenth of the fishing fleet. They didn't blame the king. They blamed the harbormaster, because the harbormaster was *there*." He looked at her, and in the brazier-light his scarred face was very tired. "You're here. That's all it takes."

* *

On the fourth day the sky over the palace district turned the color of a bruise, and the thorn-light stopped creeping and began to *climb*.

Edmund saw it from the roof of the safehouse, where she had gone against both Maren's advice and her own good sense, because she needed to see it—needed to read it, the way she had once read ledgers. The veins of light were rising now, thick as her wrist in places, winding up the palace walls in spiraling braids of gold and red, and at their heart, high above the city, she could see the source: a slow pulsing glow behind the royal banners, like a heartbeat seen through skin. The gate. The garden. Her inheritance, tearing itself apart two hundred feet above the streets, and with every pulse another shudder ran through the city below—through the people, specifically, through the ones who had sold themselves.

She watched a woman in the market square below freeze mid-step, hand pressed to her sternum, as some portion of her purchased safety rotted out of her body. Watched her husband catch her. Watched the crowd around them draw back, then close in, then draw back again—wanting to help and afraid to touch, because touching the afflicted had begun to mean sharing the affliction in the public mind, and fear writes its own arithmetic.

"We have maybe three days," Edmund said when Wren climbed up beside her. "Maybe four. I can feel it." She pressed her palm flat over her own sternum, where the map's ink had sunk into her skin weeks ago and now lay like a second pulse. "The binding isn't failing the way a wall fails. It's failing the way a wound fails. It was made to be tended. Sterlings tended it—gardeners, not jailers, I told you—and it's been two hundred years since anyone pruned or watered or *cared* for it, and the king has been drinking from it the whole time like a tick on a vein. Now it's dying, and everything it holds is spilling."

"And when it spills completely?"

"Then the Hollow King comes out of his crown." She said it plainly because there was no other way left to say it. "And everything he's eaten in two hundred years comes out with him. Every bottled name. Every traded year. All of it, at once, into a city that's already drowning."

Wren was silent a long moment. Below them, another thorn-vein split the cobblestones with a sound like ice cracking on a pond, and white flowers opened along the seam, beautiful and obscene.

"There's one thing I keep coming back to," he said finally. "In the gallery, when we ran—you said the portraits were watching you. Every keeper, back two hundred years. And you said—" He hesitated. "You said the last one mouthed something at you. You wouldn't tell me what."

Edmund wrapped her arms around her knees. The wind off the roof smelled of roses and rot.

"She said: *it feeds, it does not keep.*" She had turned the words over a hundred times since. "I thought it meant the king. It eats memories, it can't hold them—that's how I beat it, in the end, or how I mean to. But I've been thinking it might mean the gate too. Or the bargain. Everything in this city is a bargain that eats and gives nothing back. Years sold for safety. Names sold for favor. My mother traded my memories to hide me, and the guild took them and gave her—" Her voice caught. She steadied it. "Gave her nothing. Gave her a daughter who wouldn't know her own name. It feeds. It does not keep. Nothing in this kingdom keeps, Wren. Except—" she nodded down at the flowering crack, at the impossible roses climbing the palace wall, "except the things that grow wild."

Below, faintly, someone began to scream. Then several someones, in different registers of fear, and the bell in the Lamb's Rest tower started ringing the wrong pattern—the alarm pattern, the *found her* pattern, though Edmund knew it couldn't be her, she was right here, unless—

Unless the bells weren't ringing for a keeper at all.

Wren was already moving, sword half-drawn, scanning the streets. "Fire," he said. "Tanner Row's burning. And look—the crowd's not running *from* it."

He was right. The crowd was flowing toward the fire, and the fire was flowing toward them, and as Edmund leaned over the roof's edge she saw why: the flames were burning *green-gold*, threaded with the same light as the thorn-veins, leaping from rooftop to rooftop along lines of luminous growth as if the fire itself were a plant putting out runners. The gate's failure had reached the tanneries' stores of rendered fat and spirits, and now the whole quarter was kindling along veins of stolen light.

And through the smoke, on the far side of the square, she saw them: guild enforcers in their gray coats, six of them, moving through the panicked crowd with the unhurried certainty of men who knew exactly where they were going. Not toward the fire.

Toward the Lamb's Rest. Toward Maren. Toward the only person in Vessamyre who might yet lead them to the keeper's get.

"Maren's still inside," Edmund breathed.

"I know." Wren was already at the ladder. "So we'd better be quick."

Behind them, above the burning city, the glow behind the palace banners pulsed once—twice—and then, unmistakably, began to *quicken*. Like a heart that had scented prey.

Like a countdown.

Edmund swung herself onto the ladder and climbed down into the smoke, toward the fire, toward the enforcers, toward whatever came next—and in her chest, beneath her ribs, the map's buried ink pulsed in time with the dying gate, whispering the only instruction it had left her:

*Come home, little gardener. Come home, or everything burns.*
