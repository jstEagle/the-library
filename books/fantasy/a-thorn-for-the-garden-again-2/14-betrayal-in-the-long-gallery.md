## 14. Betrayal in the Long Gallery

The summons came at the hour when the palace candles guttered low and the walls seemed to lean inward, listening.

Wren stood in the antechamber off the Long Gallery as the steward read it aloud: *Sir Wren Calloway will present the keeper's get to the throne room before the third bell. Alone. Unarmed. Unspoiled.*

*Unspoiled.* As if Edmund were a cask of wine. As if she were anything but a woman who had not slept in two days, whose blood hummed against the map folded now beneath his breastplate, whose hands he had bound himself with silk cord so that no one would look too closely at how gently the knots were tied.

"The king expects promptness," the steward said. His eyes were wrong—not hollow, not yet, but polished, like a man who had sold just enough of himself to make room for something else to look out through.

"The king will have what I bring him," Wren said.

He climbed the servant's stair two steps at a time. In the narrow room where they kept her—the old chapel vestry, stripped of its saints—Edmund sat on the floor with her back against the altar step, working at her bonds with a hairpin she'd had hidden somewhere he decided not to ask about.

"You're early," she said without looking up.

"I'm leaving." He knelt, took her wrists. She flinched, then went still as he loosened the cord instead of tightening it. "There's a door in the gallery's north end. It opens onto the undercity stairs. Twenty minutes' walk from there and we're under the wall."

"We?" She rubbed her wrists. "You're coming with me. You understand what that means."

He understood. He had understood since the night he'd lied to Captain Vess about the search grid, since the moment in the throne room when the Hollow King's gaze had slid over Edmund and something behind those borrowed royal eyes had *turned*, slow as a whale surfacing, and noticed her. Since twenty years ago, when he had stood in a burning garden with a sword in his hand and done nothing.

"I've been meaning to resign," he said.

She almost smiled. It was a terrible, beautiful thing, that almost-smile, and it frightened him more than the summons did.

They made it four steps into the Long Gallery before the bells began.

* * *

Edmund had expected guards. She had expected dogs, crossbows, the shriek of whistles. What she had not expected was silence—the whole palace holding its breath—and then the steward's voice, amplified by some guild-craft that let it pour from every sconce and cornice at once:

*"Sir Wren Calloway stands accused of high treason against the crown. Let any who shelter him know the price. Let his name be struck from every ledger."*

Struck from every ledger. In the Guild of Impossible Things, that was not metaphor. She had spent eleven years striking names herself, small careful strokes of the razor, and she knew exactly what remained afterward: nothing. A man unrecorded could be killed in the street and no report would exist to say a man had died.

Wren had gone white. Not at the accusation—at the *erasure*. His hand found the wall as if the corridor itself had tilted.

"Wren." She took his elbow. "Look at me. They can only erase what was written down. Everything else—" she pressed her palm flat against his breastplate, over his heart, over the hidden map, "—everything that matters is still where you left it."

His eyes came back into focus. "That," he said hoarsely, "is the most heretical thing anyone has ever said to me."

"Good. Run, heretic."

They ran.

The Long Gallery ran the length of the palace's eastern wing, a hundred yards of candlelit stone hung with portraits from floor to vaulted ceiling. Edmund had passed through it once before, hooded, escorted, seeing nothing but the backs of guards. Now she saw.

Every keeper. Every Sterling—or near-Sterling, or whatever other name her family had worn across two centuries of hiding—painted in oils that should have faded centuries ago and had not. A woman in gardener's smock and a crown of thorn-roses, her sleeves rolled, dirt to the elbows, smiling like someone mid-laugh. An old man holding pruning shears the way soldiers held ceremonial swords. A girl of perhaps twelve with ink-stained fingers and Edmund's own stubborn jaw, a ledger open on her knee.

And their eyes followed. Not the cheap trick of painted perspective—this was older, patient, the attention of people who had spent their lives watching gates and had never entirely stopped.

"They're not portraits," Edmund gasped, skirting a corner. "They're *sentinels*. The binding needed witnesses—"

"Less history, more running," Wren said, but his voice shook.

Behind them, at the gallery's far mouth, boots. Many boots, moving in the disciplined cadence of men who had been told that treason wore armor and walked beside a woman.

* * *

Wren counted them by sound alone. Twelve, maybe fourteen, fanning into the gallery from both ends—which meant the north door was closed to them, which meant his plan had just died quietly somewhere behind the steward's smile.

"Left," he said. "The alcove."

"There's no alcove—"

There was. Between the portrait of the girl with the ledger and a tall window of colored glass, the wall receded a body's width, shadowed by an arras gone gray with age. Wren pushed Edmund into it and pressed in after her, drawing his cloak around them both, his sword arm pinned uselessly between their bodies.

The boots came on. Torchlight crawled along the ceiling. And the paintings watched.

That was the part Wren would remember all his life: the sensation of being hidden and *seen* simultaneously. Two hundred years of keepers looking down at the space where he crouched, arms around the last of their line, and none of them—none—gave him away. The woman with the thorn-rose crown seemed almost to tilt her head, oil-paint amusement in her painted eyes.

*We were gardeners,* Edmund had said in the buried garden below the palace, weeping without knowing she wept. *Not jailers. We kept things alive.*

The boots slowed. Stopped, ten feet away.

"He has to come through here," a sergeant's voice said. "Seal the south end. The king wants the gallery cleared."

"And the portraits?" a younger voice asked nervously. "My grandmother said the Sterlings still watch. Said when she was a girl, a keeper's portrait blinked at her funeral—"

"Your grandmother also said the river tasted of honey. Move."

The boots moved. The torchlight drained away toward the south end like water finding a crack.

In the alcove, Edmund exhaled against Wren's collarbone. "Did you hear that? They know. Even after everything, they *know*—"

"Later." He pulled back enough to see her face. "South end is sealed. North is guarded. Which leaves us the windows."

She looked past him at the stained glass, forty feet above a sheer drop to the inner ward. Then back at him. "You cannot be serious."

"The glass in this wing was replaced during the last reign. Guild work. It's held together by craft, not lead." He was already shrugging off his breastplate, dumping weight. "One good strike and the whole pane comes out. From there, the buttress roof, then the chapel, then down."

"Then the undercity, then the buried garden, then I walk into the thing wearing the king's face and offer it my entire life as a bribe." Her laugh came out cracked. "Listen to us. Planning treason like it's a marketing route."

"Edmund." He said her name and it stopped her, because he so rarely said it. Usually she was *the keeper*, *my prisoner*, *madam*—all the little armored distances he hid behind. "Whatever happens tonight. Whatever I have to do to get you out of this palace. There's no version of it where I regret this."

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't spend your last honest sentence on sentiment. Save it. Tell me at the gate. Tell me when we're through."

"That's a bargain, then." He lifted the stolen ceremonial mace he'd taken from the vestry rack. "Turn around."

* * *

The glass came apart like ice on a spring pond—a soundless, glittering collapse, the guild-craft releasing all at once, and then wind, cold and enormous, pouring in through a wound in the palace's face.

Alarms woke everywhere at once. Bells above, horns below, the steward's disembodied voice howling through every stone: *traitor, traitor, traitor*, until the word lost its shape and became simply weather.

Edmund went first, because he insisted, because her hands were lighter and her balance better, and because—as she pointed out while climbing over him onto the sill—if he fell, at least the map would survive in her keeping, whereas if she fell, his whole treason was for nothing. He hated the logic and could not argue with it.

The buttress roof sang under their feet, slate slick with frost. Below, torches bloomed in the ward like a rash of orange flowers. A crossbow bolt shattered on the stonework a hand's width from her head; Wren heard her swear with real feeling and then she was down, rolling, up again, running the chapel's ridgeline like a woman who had spent her childhood doing precisely this in dreams she wasn't supposed to have.

*Blood remembers its ink.* The cartographer's dying words. Perhaps memory lived in more places than minds.

They dropped into the chapel yard. A guard turned, saw them, hesitated—he was young, and Wren recognized the hesitation, the half-second in which a man decides whether the person the crown calls traitor looks like one—and in that half-second Edmund slipped past him and pressed something into his hand as she went. Wren caught a glimpse: a page, razored from a ledger, folded small.

"What was that?"

"A receipt," she said. "For years he sold. He'll want to think about that tonight, standing there deciding whether to raise the alarm properly." She was already at the undercity grate, working the lock with the same hairpin. "The guild taught me one useful thing, Wren: everyone in Vessamyre is owed something. Everyone. You just have to remind them."

The grate gave. Darkness swallowed them, and the roar of the palace dimmed to a distant sea-sound, and for one full minute Wren allowed himself to believe they might simply run, and make it, and live.

Then the horn sounded from beneath—the deep horn, the one used only for gate-failures—and the darkness around them flushed a faint, wrong green.

Thorn-light. Bleeding up through the stones.

"It's early," Edmund breathed. Her face in that sick glow looked carved out of old ivory. "The binding's failing weeks ahead of any reckoning. Wren—it knows I'm close. It's tearing at its own chains to reach me before I reach it."

"Then we run faster."

"No." She caught his wrist, and her grip was iron, and her eyes were already somewhere ahead of him, in the buried garden, at the gate, at the end of everything. "No, love. We run *smarter*. Because in a few hours, that thing on the throne is going to stop hunting me—" the thorn-light pulsed, once, like a heartbeat, "—and start begging."
