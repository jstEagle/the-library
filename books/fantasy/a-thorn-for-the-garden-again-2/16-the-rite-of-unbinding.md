## 16. The Rite of Unbinding

The garden taught her its name in the hour before dawn.

Edmund sat on the rim of the dry fountain with the blood-map spread across her knees, and as the first grey light filtered down through the grates far above—through palace cellars, through guardrooms, through the sleeping weight of Vessamyre itself—the lines of living ink began to move. Not crawling now, not reaching toward some distant city. They had arrived. They were settling into shape like a body into a grave, tracing the walls around her, the arched gatehouse choked in thorn-rose, the fountain she sat upon, even the small stone bench where a woman had once sat singing.

At the map's center, where a cartographer would put a compass rose, the ink drew instead a single flower with too many petals, and beneath it, in handwriting Edmund recognized with a sick lurch as her own—or her mother's; they were the same hand, she understood that now—the words: *The keeper binds with what she is willing to lose.*

"You might have said so sooner," Edmund told the dead man's map.

Her voice came back to her off the thorn-covered arches, softer than she'd expected, as though the garden swallowed echoes the way it swallowed light. She had been here two days. Two days since she had knelt in this dirt and wept until her ribs ached, remembering: the smell of her mother's hands, crushed rose and lamp oil; the sound of a lullaby whose words still would not come; the terrible ordinary morning when her mother had brushed her hair and said, *Edmund, look at me, look at me, I want you to remember my face* —and then, apparently, had gone to the guild and paid them to make certain she never did.

Fury was still there. It lived in her chest now like a coal banked under ash. But fury was a fuel, not a direction, and the map had given her the direction at last.

*The keeper binds with what she is willing to lose.*

She did not yet understand it. But the garden did, and the garden was patient, and she had learned patience in twenty years of balancing ledgers no one read.

* * *

Wren found her there when the bells above rang terce, descending the hidden stair with his bandaged arm cradled against his chest and a loaf of black bread and a wedge of hard cheese wrapped in a cloth, because even a disgraced knight thought first of provisioning. He set the bundle down on the fountain's rim beside her without ceremony, which was its own kind of tenderness, and looked at the map for a long moment before he spoke.

"The palace has gone quiet," he said. "Wrong quiet. The servants are being dismissed floor by floor. The steward's people are clearing the great hall, setting chairs in rings. Court is being summoned, Edmund. The whole hollow machinery of it."

"He knows," Edmund said. "That the gate is failing."

"He knows more than that." Wren crouched, stiffly—he had taken a blade in the thigh during their escape through the long gallery, and the wound had gone hot and angry despite her poultices—and turned the map so the words at its heart faced him. His jaw worked. "There's an old sergeant in the palace guard, Halford, who fought in the border wars before I was knighted. He drinks when he can and talks when he drinks, and last night he talked. He said something I'd dismissed as soldier's nonsense for years." Wren's eyes came up to hers. "He said the king doesn't eat food. He eats *remembering*. That the old families of Vessamyre used to leave bowls of milk out—not for spirits, but for the crown, filled with tokens, locks of hair, wedding rings, things soaked in a life. And the houses that left the richest bowls were the houses that never seemed to grieve. Because they'd already forgotten what grief was."

Edmund looked down at her hands. They were steady. She noticed that the way you notice weather—as a fact about the world, not about yourself.

"The borrowed years," she said. "The city sells decades of life to the crown for safety. I thought it was years it wanted. Time. But time isn't a thing you can hold. It's only a vessel." She heard herself speaking in her ledger-keeper's voice, flat and precise, and was grateful for it. "Memory fills the vessel. Every year sold comes full of what happened in it. The Hollow King isn't hoarding lifespan. It's hoarding the *lived*. It is—" and here her voice failed her for one breath, and she hated it, and continued— "it is eating everything that makes a person a person, and leaving the meat walking around."

"Yes," Wren said quietly. "And your mother hid you from it. The guild didn't take your memories as punishment, Edmund. They took them as ransom-in-advance. A vault. You are a room full of treasure the king has never been allowed to open."

The bread sat untouched between them. Somewhere above, water moved through pipes in the palace walls—a sound like a distant crowd murmuring.

"Then it will come for me," Edmund said. "It already knows I exist. It *saw* me. In the throne room, behind those eyes—it saw my bloodline the way a miser sees gold through a cracked wall."

"That's why I came down early," Wren said. "The summons went out before dawn. Not just to the court. To the city. Every soul in Vessamyre who owes years to the crown is commanded to attend the great hall at dusk. The heralds are calling it a *celebration of the crown's abundance*." His mouth twisted. "Two hundred years ago, when the binding was made fresh, the rite was performed in public. Everyone knew what the garden was for, then. The Hollow King is going to finish what it started at the gate—in front of ten thousand witnesses, while the binding rots out from under it, and it will drink the whole city dry if it has to."

Edmund rose. Her knees protested; she had slept maybe four hours in two days, curled on the gardener's bench with her coat balled under her head, and her body was filing its complaints. But her mind had never been clearer, and that frightened her more than anything, because she was beginning to suspect why.

"Show me," she said. "Halford's stories, the palace archives, whatever you found. I need every account of the original binding. Every word."

"There's almost nothing," Wren warned. "It was all purged centuries ago. But—" he hesitated, and she watched shame cross his face like weather, "—there is one account. I've been carrying it since the gallery. Waiting for the right moment, which is a coward's phrase for waiting until I couldn't avoid it." He reached inside his coat and drew out a thin bundle of papers, warped with age and damp, tied with a fraying ribbon the color of dried blood.

"What is it?"

"A confession," Wren said. "Written by Sir Aldric Vane, the knight who stood guard over the last keeper of the gate. Written the night before the binding was completed. He was the one who—"

He stopped. Edmund took the bundle from his hands before he could decide whether to go on.

* * *

Sir Aldric Vane wrote like a man who knows he will not sleep again, and Edmund read him the way she had once read the guild's ledgers: completely, twice, missing nothing.

*They tell me the rite requires three things,* the dead knight had written, *and I write this so that whoever comes after—I pray to every saint that someone comes after—will understand what was asked of her, and what was given.*

*The gate was never a wall. This is the first thing they do not teach, because it is the thing that damns us. A gate is not built to keep the Hollow King IN. It was built to keep it FED. The Sterlings understood what my order never did: you cannot kill a thing that lives on recollection, for it is woven into the mind of every subject of the crown. Starve it and it takes memory by force, from the nearest and weakest. So the keepers made a bargain older than any of our oaths. Each generation, a keeper of the Sterling line offers freely what no thief can take: their own remembering.*

*A stolen memory is a wound. An offered memory is a meal, and a creature that is fed does not hunt.*

*I asked her—I will not write her name, she asked me not to—what she would give. She smiled at me as though I were a child asking where the rain comes from. She said: "All of it, if it must be all. A keeper holds nothing back, Aldric. Hold anything back and the King learns it can take, and taking becomes habit. That is how crowns are lost."*

*The rite itself is simple, as terrible things always are. The keeper stands within the gate. She calls the Hollow King by the true king's name—for it wears him, and answers to him. She opens her memory as one opens a ledger, and she lets it read. And when it has read to the last page, when there is nothing left in her but breath and hunger satisfied, the binding seals itself with what remains: the empty place where a life used to be. The gate is made of absence. It has always been made of absence. We only ever notice a lock when the door swings free.*

*She asked me one favor. When it was done, when she sat blank in the grass and did not know why the roses made her cry, I was to tell her: "You were loved, and you chose love, and the choice was yours even if you cannot remember making it." I promised. God forgive me, I could not keep the promise. I ran. I have been running for fifty years, and tonight I stop.*

*If you read this, keeper-heir, know that your grandmother laughed, at the end. Know that she was not afraid. Know that what you are about to lose will be lost willingly, and willingness is the whole of the magic, and no one—not the king, not the guild, not God himself—can take from you what you give.*

*It is the only thing they cannot take.*

Edmund read it twice, as she had promised herself she would, and then folded it along its old creases and set it on the fountain's rim, and looked up at the thorn-choked gatehouse, and found that her eyes were dry.

"Willingness," she said. "That's all. That's the whole of the lock."

"Edmund." Wren's voice was very careful. "You don't have to give everything. Aldric says she offered all of it—but perhaps she was wrong, perhaps a portion—"

"She wasn't wrong." Edmund heard the certainty in her own voice and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a bell that rings true after decades in a damp cellar. "I'm a ledger-keeper, Wren. Before anything else, before keeper, before heir—I kept accounts. And an account that's been edited is worse than useless; it lies. If I hold back my mother's face, or your—" she stopped, and started again, "—if I hold anything back, then the Hollow King reads the gaps. Gaps are its native language. It has spent two centuries living in what this kingdom refuses to remember. I will not hand it a book with pages torn out. I've seen what torn pages do." She thought of the razored ledger in the guildhall undercroft, and the twenty-year war that had grown from them. "No. All of it. Clean. Complete. Final entry."

"And then?" Wren's composure cracked at last, and underneath it he was simply a man with a fevered wound and fear in his eyes, and Edmund loved him for the cracking, though she did not say so. There would be time for saying, or there would not; either way, not now. "Then you sit in the grass like your grandmother, and don't know why the roses make you cry, and I—" His voice failed. He tried again. "I tell you that you were loved. That's a poor inheritance."

"It's better than most people get." She picked up the black bread and tore it, and handed him half, because the living still had to eat, and because her hands needed something to do that was not shaking. "Listen to me. Dusk, tomorrow. The court assembles. The Hollow King means to feed publicly—to drink this city dry and let the binding snap behind it like a cut rope. It thinks it has won. It thinks the gate is failing and the keeper is a fugitive hiding in a hole in the ground." She chewed, swallowed. The bread tasted like ash and she ate it anyway. "Let it think so. Tomorrow at dusk, I walk up the hidden stair, through the palace, into the great hall, and I stand in front of the throne. And I call it by the true king's name."

"It will have guards."

"It will have a court full of witnesses," Edmund corrected. "Ten thousand people whose borrowed years are rotting in their veins. People who sold their weddings and their children's faces and their mothers' voices to a crown that ate them. Wren—when I open the ledger, it won't just be reading me. It'll be feeding in front of everyone it has robbed. And a creature caught gorging forgets to guard itself." She met his eyes. "But I need it looking at no one else. While it reads me—while it's drunk on me—you get the true king out. The man underneath. Aldous. However deep he's buried, however long it takes, you pull him up to the surface, because the moment it loses hold of his name, the moment it forgets which crown it's wearing—"

"—the binding snaps shut on it," Wren finished slowly. "With you as the lock."

"With my emptiness as the lock." She smiled, and felt how strange the expression was on her face, and let it stay. "Aldric wrote that the gate is made of absence. Fine. I have been absent my whole life. Twenty years of being invisible, of being furniture, of being nobody at all. Turns out I was training for this the entire time." She laughed—a short, unlovely sound—and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the prickling stopped. "I want you to promise me something, and I want you to actually do it this time, unlike Sir Aldric."

"Anything."

"When it's done. When I'm sitting in the grass and I don't know your face." She made herself say it steadily. "Don't tell me I was loved. Not first. First, tell me about the garden. Tell me how to prune the thorn roses, tell me which bed gets morning sun, tell me where the well is. Give me the work. The rest—" her voice betrayed her, just slightly, and she let it, "—the rest you can tell me slowly. Over years. If you stay."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Below them, in the buried dark of the sealed gatehouse, the thorns had begun to put out new shoots overnight—pale green tendrils curling up through the cracks, hungry for light that wasn't there. The gate was failing faster now. She could feel it, a low continuous hum in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, like standing too near a falling building.

"I'll stay," Wren said. "Even if you set the dogs on me. Even if you don't know why you hate me. I'll stay and I'll learn to prune roses."

"Good." She folded Aldric Vane's confession and tucked it into her bodice, over her heart, where it weighed almost nothing. "Then there's only one thing left to settle before the court convenes." She looked up, past the grates, past the cellars, toward the great hall where chairs were being set in rings, toward the throne where something ancient and starving was smiling with a dead man's mouth. "Tomorrow at dusk, when I call it by the true king's name—someone has to tell me what that name is."

Wren went white. "You don't—Edmund, you've read the histories, you must have—"

"I've read *titles*," she said. *Protector of the Realm. Father of the City. The Crown Enduring.* "Titles are the mask. Aldric wrote that the rite fails unless it's the true name, spoken by a keeper within the gate. My mother's memories are gone, Wren. The guild razored them. Whatever the Sterlings knew, they knew in their bodies, in their gardens—and I got none of it." She stood, and brushed the dirt from her skirts, and felt the terrible clarity settle over her like armor, like a coat she had been fitted for since birth. "So either you know the name of the king this kingdom actually has—or somewhere in that palace above us, in two hundred years of stolen records, there is one page no one dared burn."

Far overhead, muffled by earth and stone and the whole sleeping weight of Vessamyre, the palace bells began to ring—and they were ringing wrong, off-pattern, urgent. A summons hours ahead of schedule.

Through the grate above, faint as a dying man's whisper, came the herald's cry:

"*The court assembles! Let all who owe the crown attend the king!*"

The Hollow King had called its feast early.
