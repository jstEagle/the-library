## 17. The Hollow Court Assembles

The summons came by thorn.

Guildmaster Corvus Hale was at his desk in the upper hall of the Guild of Impossible Things, composing the third letter of apology he had written in forty years—the first two concerning a mis-sold decade and a drowned baron—when the messenger arrived. It did not use the door. It grew through it, black briars forcing the oak apart in splinters, and from the heart of the tangle unfolded a single bloom of pale, bloodless white. Within the bloom sat a scroll sealed not with wax but with a sliver of crown-glass that hummed when Hale touched it.

*The Hollow King commands the presence of the Guildmaster of Impossible Things before the throne, at the turning of the bell, to witness the settling of accounts.*

He read it three times. His hands, so steady over ledgers for four decades, left small tremors in the parchment.

In forty years Corvus Hale had never once been summoned to court. The guild dealt in years and names and small impossibilities; the crown dealt in larger ones, and the arrangement between them had always been conducted through intermediaries, invoices, and the careful fiction that neither party knew what the other truly was. That fiction had served them both since before his grandfather's grandfather. Summons meant audience. Audience meant the arrangement was dead.

And arrangements died only one way in Vessamyre: into debt.

He looked at the razored gap in the oldest ledger, still open on his desk where he had been studying it for six days without success. Pages missing. Sterling pages. Gate pages. A girl who should have been quieted twice over, walking north with three stolen leaves of history and a dead mapmaker's blood on her hands, and now the Hollow King wanted his guildmaster standing before him like some summoned clerk.

Hale rang for his under-secretary. "Prepare my robes," he said. "The grey ones."

"The grey, Master? Grey is for—"

"I know what grey is for." He closed the ledger gently, as one closes a coffin. "Grey is for debts."

* * *

Steward Ambrose Fenn had served the palace for thirty-one years and had learned, in that time, exactly one thing worth learning: the king did not eat.

Oh, food arrived at the royal table—arrived in quantities that would have shamed a harvest festival, because the Hollow King loved display the way a magpie loves glass—but nothing was consumed. The plates went out gleaming and came back gleaming. The wine was poured and never drunk. In three decades Fenn had never seen the king's jaw move for any purpose but speech, and the speeches had grown rarer each year, until now the king spoke perhaps a dozen sentences a season and all of them were *yes*, *no*, or *bring me*.

Which was why the second thing Fenn had learned—that the palace remembered things the king did not—had begun to frighten him more than the first.

It started small. Doors he had locked stood open. Candles burned in the east corridor where no servant admitted to lighting them. Once, passing the sealed stair to the underlevels, he had heard singing—a woman's voice, low and sweet, singing a gardening song about roses and rain—and when he'd flung the door wide there was nothing below but darkness and the smell, impossibly, of turned earth.

Tonight, as he supervised the preparations for the court assembly, the strangeness had spread into the open. The long gallery's portraits—all two hundred and eleven of them, every keeper and warden and gate-tender in the kingdom's painted memory—were weeping. Not visibly; the paint remained paint. But the frames dripped. Water beaded along the gilded edges and fell in slow, regular drops onto the marble, and the footmen walked the gallery mopping behind themselves with the grim efficiency of people who have decided not to think about a thing precisely so they can continue not thinking about it.

"Fenn." The voice came from the shadows between portraits, and Fenn did not jump, because thirty-one years had burned the jumping out of him. Sir Wren Calloway stepped forward, and even in the gloom Fenn could see the man had not slept. There was a bandage beneath his collar. His eyes had the particular stillness of a man who has made a decision and is waiting for the world to catch up.

"Sir Wren. You are summoned as well, then."

"Everyone is summoned." Wren glanced down the weeping gallery. "What do you know about the woman I brought in? The keeper."

Fenn kept his face smooth. It was a skill, like carving. "She escaped, sir. Two nights past. The guards were disciplined."

"Were they." Wren said it flatly, and Fenn understood suddenly, with a steward's instinct for the true shape of things, that Calloway had let her go. That the discipline of guards was theater performed around an absence. "And the king. What does he want with her?"

"Sir Wren." Fenn lowered his voice and committed, in that moment, the second great indiscretion of his career. The first had been listening at the sealed stair. "Thirty-one years I have set food before that thing on the throne and carried it away untouched. I have watched it wear a dead man's face and speak in a dead man's voice and I have told myself it was madness, or sorcery, or none of my affair. But last night the portraits wept, and tonight it calls the whole city's court to watch it *eat*, and I will tell you what I know, which is this: whatever sits on that throne is frightened. First time in thirty-one years. And I find I am glad of it."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded once, the smallest motion, and went down the gallery toward the throne room, his boots leaving wet prints among the portrait-tears.

Fenn watched him go, then turned back to his candles, his tables, his endless flawless arrangements for a feast that would never be eaten. He caught himself humming, before he could stop, the gardening song about roses and rain.

He decided not to think about why he knew it.

* * *

Tansy Mole, aged nine, seller of nothing and owner of four borrowed years she didn't want, heard the bell before anyone else on Candlewick Row.

That was her gift—or her curse, her gran said, spitting over her shoulder whenever the subject came up. Tansy heard bells other people couldn't. The little silver chime of a year being sold at the Exchange, clear as a spoon against a cup, whenever some hollow-eyed father up the street traded a decade of his life for bread and rent. She'd heard her own father's years go, one bright ring apiece, five winters ago, and she'd heard the silence afterward that meant he wasn't coming home from the Exchange hall.

But this bell was different. This bell came from under the ground.

It began at dusk, a sound like a struck root, deep and slow, and it rolled up through the cobbles and the cellar floors and the bones of every house on Candlewick Row, and every person who had ever sold a year stopped what they were doing and put their hands flat against walls and doorposts, because they felt it in the place where their missing time had been: a pulling. A gentle, patient tug, like the tide going out.

"Tansy!" Her gran seized her arm in the doorway. "Inside. Now."

"Gran, the bell—"

"*Inside.*"

But Tansy had run to the corner instead, because nine-year-olds are built for exactly the wrong choice, and she had looked down Vessamyre's great central avenue toward the palace on its hill. And she had seen the thorns.

They came out of the gutters first—black shoots unspooling like ink dropped in water, growing as she watched, fast as spilled things grow. They climbed the lampposts and split the cobbles and threaded through the iron railings, and every one of them wore, at intervals, the same pale bloodless bloom, and the blooms were all turned toward the palace like faces at a sermon. Where the thorns passed over houses, the windows lit from within with a light that wasn't candle-colored, wasn't fire-colored at all—a thin red-gold bleeding glow, like light through a held-up hand.

And in that light, the people who had sold their years began to scream.

Not all of them. Just some. An old woman across the avenue folded up like a chair closing, decades collapsing inward, her skin going loose and grey in the space of ten breaths. A man in a shop doorway clutched his chest and wept and said, over and over, *it's taking it back, it's taking it back early,* and Tansy understood, in the cold way children understand large disasters, that the crown was calling in its loans.

Her four years pulled inside her, gentle and patient, like the tide.

"Tansy!" Gran's arms around her, dragging her back, slamming the door, barring it with the heavy oak beam while the bell tolled up through the floorboards and the thorn-light bled red through the shutter-cracks. Gran pushed her down into the cellar, among the potatoes and the pickled things, and held her face in both work-roughened hands.

"Listen to me, girl. Whatever happens tonight, whatever you hear upstairs—you keep quiet, and you stay small, and if the thorns come down here you run for the river gate and you don't stop for anything living or dead. Do you hear me?"

"Why, Gran? What's happening?"

Gran's mouth worked. Her gran was old enough to remember the last time, though she'd never said so; old enough to remember when the Exchange was a shrine and the crown had a true king, and she had spent Tansy's whole life pretending those were stories.

"What's happening," she said finally, "is the bill has come due. And I don't think the crown means to pay it."

Above them, through the floorboards, the bell tolled again, and again, gathering speed, and Tansy pressed her palms flat against the cellar earth and felt, far below, deeper than cellars, deeper than the roots of the palace itself—a second heartbeat answering the bell. Slow. Ancient. Waking.

* * *

They came to the throne room from every quarter of the city, drawn along streets that seemed, tonight, to rearrange themselves helpfully, thorn-lined avenues opening like aisles in a church.

Guildmaster Hale came in his grey robes, walking at the head of twelve senior clerks who had not been invited but whom he refused to leave behind, because if the guild was to be ended tonight it would be ended in formation. He passed through the doors beneath the gaze of guards who did not check his credentials—who checked nothing at all, their eyes fixed forward with the glazed patience of men being *worn* rather than lived in—and he thought, absurdly, of Edmund Sterling bent over her ledgers in the undercroft, of her meticulous invisible hands, and wondered which of them the missing pages had truly been stolen from.

Steward Fenn came with the household, carrying the ceremonial keys he had carried for thirty-one years, and found that the great doors of the throne room needed no unlocking. They stood open on a hall transformed.

The thorns had grown inside. They sheeted the pillars and arched across the vaulted ceiling, and the pale blooms hung down in clusters like chandeliers, shedding that thin red-gold light over rows of assembled courtiers, ministers, knights, guild-factors, Exchange-masters—the entire apparatus of two centuries of quiet theft, gathered in one room for the first time in living memory. Among them, at the front near the dais, stood the knights of the crown in their white-and-gold, and at their head Sir Wren Calloway, straight-backed and pale, his hand resting on his sword-hilt not like a man guarding it but like a man holding it in reserve.

And on the throne, the Hollow King.

He looked worse than Fenn had ever seen him, which was to say, he looked *thinner*. The body of Aldous II had always maintained a terrible perfect health, unaging, unwasting; tonight the cheeks were hollowed, the eyes sunken deep into the famous face, and when he rose, the whole court rose with him in a rustle of silk and fear, and his voice—when it came—was not the dry ceremonial voice they knew. It was vast. Layered. A chorus speaking through one throat.

"My faithful instruments." The word *faithful* curdled in the air. "Two hundred years you have fed me, knowingly and otherwise, and I have kept my bargain: your years bought, your names bottled, your impossible things made possible at reasonable rates. Tonight the account closes." He descended one step. The thorn-light pulsed with him. "Something walks beneath my palace that I buried before your grandfathers drew breath. A garden. A door. A *keeper*. It wakes whether I consent or no, and I find—" the chorus faltered, found itself again, "—I am hungry, and the harvest is ripe. Vessamyre holds sixty thousand borrowed years. I will take them all, and with their strength I will close the door forever, and you will witness it, because witnesses make debts real."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. Somewhere in the ranks of clerks a man fainted quietly and was held upright by his neighbors, who did not look away from the throne.

Only Wren moved. One step forward, boots loud on marble, and every eye in the hall swung to him, and Fenn, watching, saw the knight lift his chin with the expression of a man stepping off a cliff on purpose.

"Your Majesty," Wren said, and his voice did not shake, "before you settle accounts—I bring a final entry for the ledger. The keeper you hunt is not in the city's streets, nor hiding at the river gate, nor fled north." He drew breath. "She is already beneath you. She has been in your garden since yesterday's dawn."

The Hollow King went very still.

And far below the throne room—below the sealed stair, below the buried wall, in an overgrown garden where a woman with a mapmaker's blood in her veins knelt at a gate of living thorns with her hands pressed to the wood—Edmund Sterling felt the whole vast weight of the court above her turn, like a single eye, toward the ground she knelt on.

The gate beneath her palms shuddered, and began, thorn by thorn, to open.
