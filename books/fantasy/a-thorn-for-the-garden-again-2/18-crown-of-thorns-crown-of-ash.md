## 18. Crown of Thorns, Crown of Ash

*Edmund*

The doors of the hollow court opened the way a wound opens—slowly, wetly, with a sound no door should make.

Edmund stood on the threshold of the great hall with Wren's blood drying on her sleeve and the map's last line still glowing faintly against her palm like a vein of buried fire. Beyond her stretched the throne room of Vessamyre: a cavern of white stone and borrowed light, filled to its far walls with every soul the Hollow King had summoned to witness his triumph. Guildmasters in their gray. Stewards in their silver. Children in their funeral best. A thousand faces turned toward her all at once, and not one of them blinked.

She had imagined, in the long descent through the palace's bones, that she would be afraid. She was not. What lived in her chest now was colder and cleaner than fear—the particular stillness of a woman who has already paid the price and is merely walking to the counter to settle the account.

"They can see us," she murmured.

"Let them." Wren moved up beside her, sword drawn, his left arm hanging wrong where the gallery guards had caught him with a pike. He did not favor it. "I spent eleven years learning how to stand in rooms like this one. It turns out the lesson holds whether you serve the throne or mean to end it."

Down the length of the hall, upon a dais of black thorn-wood that had grown—*grown*, she could see the grain of it writhing even at this distance—sat the Hollow King. He wore Aldous's face the way a mask wears a man. His crown was not gold; she could see that now, from here, with her inheritance awake in her blood. It was braided thorn, black and glistening, sunk into the skull beneath it, and around its points the air itself was eaten away, as though the world could not quite bear to touch him.

He was smiling at her.

"Edmund Sterling," he said, and his voice reached every corner without effort, the way rot reaches every room through one bad wall. "Or shall I use the older name? The gate answers to it, I'm told."

"You're told nothing," Edmund said, and began to walk. "You only take."

The court parted before her—not by command, she realized, but because they *had* to, the crowd flowing aside like water around a keel, their faces slack with that terrible Vessamyre politeness, the courtesy of people who had sold too many of their years to risk spending one more in defiance. An old woman caught Edmund's eye as she passed. Her eyes were wrong—faded at the center, like cloth worn through—and in them Edmund saw something that was not hollow at all. It was hope, and it was horrifying, because it was aimed at her.

*Don't*, Edmund wanted to say. *I am not your salvation. I am the ransom note.*

Wren walked at her shoulder, and the court flinched from him harder than from her. Traitors are always more frightening than strangers.

Twenty paces from the dais, the thorn-light found them.

It came up through the floor in slow ropes, amber and hungry, the same bleeding light she'd watched devour the streets these three days past—but here, near its source, it was not wild. It gathered. It shaped itself into figures between Edmund and the throne: the King's garden of guards, grown rather than sworn. They had faces. That was the worst of it. Each warden of thorn wore the face of some keeper or knight who had died defending the binding across two centuries, and Edmund looked into her own great-grandmother's eyes an instant before the thing wearing them drew a blade of hardened light.

"Wren," she said.

"I see them." His voice was steady. His knuckles were not. "How many?"

"All of them," Edmund said. "Every keeper who ever fell. He kept them."

"He keeps everything." Wren rolled his wounded shoulder once, grimacing, and set his feet. "Then we'll have to be quicker than history."

* * *

*Wren*

There is a moment every swordsman knows and no swordsman speaks of: the half-second before the first blow, when the body understands what the mind is still denying—that someone in front of you means to unmake you, and you mean to do the same, and there is no version of the next minute in which you both walk away whole.

Wren had lived inside that half-second for most of his life. He had been forged in it, page at fourteen, knight at nineteen, blade of the Hollow King at twenty-two. He knew the geometry of a throne room the way other men knew their mothers' kitchens. And so when the first warden came for him—a thing of thorn and amber light wearing the face of Ser Hallam Voss, who had taught Wren the lance—he did not hesitate, and hated himself cleanly for it, and cut the old man's echo down in a single stroke.

Light does not bleed. But it screams. The warden came apart in a shower of sparks that smelled, absurdly, of roses.

"Sorry, ser," Wren breathed, and turned the parry of the next attack into the kill of the one behind it.

They fought the way the map had taught Edmund to move—no, he corrected himself, hauling the thought back—they fought the way they had learned to fight together on the north road, in the ambush at Miller's Ford, in the long gallery an hour past with portraits of dead keepers watching them run. She was not a swordswoman; she was something older. She fought with the ledger-book strapped to her left forearm as a shield, and a gardener's sickle in her right hand, and she used the wardens' hunger against them, turning each blade of thorn-light into the path of another. Where she stepped, the amber ropes recoiled. *The gate knows its heir,* he thought. *Even now. Even here.*

A warden took his knee. He felt the thorn-blade open him from hip to mid-thigh and heard himself make a sound he would deny until his dying day, and then Edmund was there, sickle flashing, severing the thing's arm at the shoulder, her free hand dragging him upright by his gorget.

"Stay on your feet," she said. Not a plea. An order from a keeper of the Thorn Gate, and his traitor body obeyed it gladly.

"The court's watching," he managed.

"Good," said Edmund Sterling, ledger-keeper of impossible things, and cut her way toward the throne.

Through the ring of dying wardens, Wren saw the dais clearly at last, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the floor of the world. The Hollow King had risen. He had not moved to meet them—he had simply *risen*, the way water rises in a flooded crypt, and the thorn-crown on his head was blooming, putting out new black shoots even as Wren watched, and beneath it the king's borrowed face was running like wax, unable to hold its shape around the thing looking out through it.

That thing was looking at Edmund with an expression Wren recognized, because he had worn it himself once, standing in a burning farmhouse twenty years ago, holding a sword he no longer understood.

It was recognition. It was appetite. It was, underneath both, almost tenderly, *hunger*.

"No further," the Hollow King said, and the whole hall shook with the words. "Little keeper. You've brought me exactly what I summoned you to bring."

* * *

*Edmund*

The lie arrived gently, wrapped in truth, the way all the best lies are.

Edmund stopped at the foot of the dais, sickle low, breath tearing in her throat. Around her the last wardens guttered and reformed, patient as seasons. Behind her, a thousand sold lives watched in silence. At her shoulder, Wren bled onto the white stone in quantities that made her arithmetic—her faithful, treacherous arithmetic—run cold.

"What did you summon me to bring?" she asked.

"A choice." The Hollow King descended one step. The thorn-crown drank the light around it. "Did you think the gate failed by accident? Did you think my reach ends at city walls?" He smiled with Aldous's mouth, and it was worse than any snarl. "Two hundred years I have worn this crown and fed this gate, and the gate grows old, and bindings want renewing, and the renewing wants a keeper's blood freely given. So I let the cartographer live long enough to find you. I let the guild raise you ordinary and blind. I let you remember yourself just soon enough to come here willing." Another step. Close enough now that she could see the true king drowned deep behind those eyes, hands pressed to some inner glass, mouthing words that never reached the surface. "You came to offer yourself, Edmund. I can smell the offering on you. You've been carrying it since the undercroft."

And the horror of it, the vertigo, was that he was right. She *had*. Every step since the guildhall had bent toward this dais. She had told herself it was strategy, duty, inheritance—but the map had led here, and maps do not lead, they *follow*, and hers had followed the line her own blood had already drawn.

Her knees nearly went. She felt Wren shift beside her, felt him gather himself to speak or strike—

"Don't," she said quietly. "Whatever you're about to do—don't yet."

"Edmund—"

"*Not yet.*"

Because she had spent one night in the buried garden reading her mother's hand in the margins of the keeper's book, and her mother had written one sentence over and over, in ink that still smelled of crushed petals: *the parasite cannot refuse a meal placed in its mouth.* And her mother had also written, once, smaller, in a different hand entirely, pressed hard enough to tear the page:

*But nothing that eats can help becoming what it eats.*

The Hollow King extended a hand of thorn and royal flesh. "Give me your memories, keeper. Your mother's garden. Your stolen childhood. The knight beside you, and what he is to you—I know what he is, even if you don't. Give me all of it, and I will spare the city. This is mercy. This is the only mercy I have left."

The court leaned in. A child somewhere began, very softly, to cry, and was hushed by a parent who had sold thirty years and could not afford the fine for weeping.

Edmund looked at the offered hand. She thought of her mother's fury and her mother's love, which were the same love wearing different gloves. She thought: *he believes I will kneel.* She thought: *let him believe.*

And Wren—Wren, who had spent his whole life being early to every disaster and late to every courage—moved first after all.

* * *

*Wren*

Later—assuming there was a later—Wren would not be able to say whether he decided anything at all. It was less a decision than a door finally opening in a wall he had mistaken for the whole house.

He stepped forward, past Edmund, into the space between the keeper and the crown, and he heard his own voice come out of him with a clarity he had not managed since he was nineteen years old and swearing fealty on a field of ash.

"My king," he said, loudly, in the ringing silence, so that every ear in the hollow court had no choice but to hear. "You are a thief."

The word landed in the hall like a dropped chandelier.

"I, Sir Wren Calloway, knight of the realm—" his voice cracked on *knight*, and he let it, let them all hear it crack, "—do hereby break the oath I swore to you. I swore to protect the crown. I did not know the crown was a wound. I hunted the keeper's family in your name. I stood in a burning house in Ashfell and carried out orders while a mother died protecting her daughter's memory, and I told myself for twenty years that it was duty." He was crying. He discovered this with distant surprise, the tears cutting lines through the soot on his face. "It was never duty. Duty does not require you to stop asking. You required it. You are a thief of years, a thief of names, a thief of a king's face and a kingdom's morning, and I name you so before this court, and I renounce you, and I would renounce you again."

The Hollow King looked at him for a long, long moment.

"There," the creature said softly, almost fondly, to the court. "You see what doubt makes of a good sword."

Then it moved—not at Edmund, at Wren—and Wren understood in the last instant that this had been its purpose all along, that a king does not waste wrath on the sacrifice when he can spend it breaking the altar instead, and he did the only thing left in his inventory, the thing he had been practicing since the north road without knowing it.

He put his body between the crown and the keeper, and he did not step aside.

The thorn-crown's blow went through his breastplate like frost through a windowpane. Cold first, then heat, then neither—a vast indifferent absence spreading under his ribs where the world used to be. He heard Edmund scream his name. He heard the court scream, a thousand voices at once, sold and hollowed and hopeless and *awake* all at the same moment, as if his ruin had knocked something loose in them too. He went down onto the white stone, and the stone was warm, impossibly warm, warm as a garden in June, and through the red haze gathering at the edges of his vision he saw Edmund Sterling rise above him with her mother's book in one hand and her memories—everything, all of it—gathered in the other, ready to feed herself to the thing on the throne.

"No," Wren said. Or meant to say. It came out as blood and breath.

*Take the shot you're given,* Ser Hallam Voss had told him, twenty years and a lifetime ago, in another life where he served a king. *A soldier doesn't get to choose the perfect moment. Only to be worthy of the one that comes.*

The Hollow King loomed over them both, crowned in thorns, reaching for the feast.

And down in the drowned dark behind its eyes, King Aldous pressed his hands against the glass, and mouthed, for the first time in two hundred years, a word the surface world could almost, almost hear.

*"Now."*
