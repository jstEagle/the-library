## 19. The Price of Remembering

The garden beneath the palace had gone to war.

Thorn-light poured through the cracks in the gate like water through a failing dam, and where it touched, the world remembered wrongly. A rosebush near Edmund's left hand bloomed and rotted and bloomed again in the space of a breath, its petals cycling through a century of springs. The flagstones beneath her feet had softened into something like loam, then hardened back to stone, unable to decide what they were. The air tasted of copper and cut grass and old, old grief.

Behind her, down the long throat of the buried stairway, she could hear the hollow court coming—hundreds of feet on marble, the whisper of silk, and beneath it all a sound like a held breath two hundred years long.

"Edmund." Wren's voice, ragged at the edges. He leaned against the gatehouse wall, one hand pressed to his side where the Hollow Guard's blades had found him twice. His blood made small dark maps on the stones. "Whatever you're about to do—you should know I've decided to hate it."

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I don't need to. It's written on your face the way it was written on your mother's." He pushed himself upright, wincing. "I was there when she stood in this garden. Did you know that? Not just the night she died—in this garden, years before. She told me once that the binding wasn't a lock. That it was a *conversation*. And that conversations can only be ended by someone willing to stop speaking."

Edmund turned back to the gate.

Up close, it was not the iron lattice the ledgers described, nor the wall of thorns her returning memories insisted on. It was both and neither—a door made of living wood grown through dead metal, every bar furred with briars, every briar threaded with light that bled and bled and would not clot. Through its gaps she could see the other side: a darkness that breathed. The Hollow King had come here first, two centuries ago, and had never entirely left. He had simply learned to wear a crown while he fed.

The rite was simple. That was the cruelty of it. The archivist's last words still echoed in her skull like a struck bell: *A keeper's memories, freely given. It feeds on recollection. Starve it with itself.*

She had spent three weeks reclaiming what her mother had sold away—the guild's theft undone page by page, memory by memory, until she stood whole in the place she'd been born to stand. Her mother's lullaby. The smell of ledger ink. Wren's face lit by the first fire they'd shared on the north road, when he'd almost smiled. All of it, hers again, all of it, hers to give.

"It will take everything," she said quietly. "Not just the bad years. Everything."

"Then don't." Wren crossed the distance between them, and his hand closed over hers, blood-warm and steady. "There has to be another way. There always is. We find it. That's what we do—we've been finding ways since the north road."

"There isn't. You know there isn't. You heard the court coming." She lifted her chin toward the stairway, where the footsteps were growing louder, and the thorn-light flared as if in answer—as if the gate could hear them too, and knew how little time remained. "When it reaches the bottom of those stairs, Vessamyre dies. Every borrowed year, every sold decade, every child born into debt. It doesn't want a kingdom, Wren. It wants a *harvest*."

"And you're going to feed yourself to it instead."

"No." She smiled, and found that it didn't shake. "I'm going to make myself a meal it can't swallow."

She drew the map from inside her coat—the cartographer's map, drawn in living blood, its lines long since crawled home to rest upon this garden. She pressed it flat against the gate's central bar, and the blood-map drank the thorn-light and began, softly, to sing.

It sang in her mother's voice.

*Oh, the keeper keeps the keeping, and the keeping keeps the door—*

"Don't," Wren said again, but his voice broke on the word, and his hand tightened around hers as though he could hold her to the world by grip alone.

"Wren Calloway." She turned to face him fully, and let herself look at him—really look, the way she had been afraid to since the long gallery, since he'd broken his oath before the assembled court and named the king a thief with the whole hollowed room watching. "I reclaimed my memories three weeks ago. Do you know what I did with the first hour?"

He shook his head.

"I sat under a hedgerow outside the palace wall and I remembered you. Just you. Your terrible saddle posture. The way you say 'Edmund' like it's a word you're still learning to be careful with. The night you lied for me to your own superiors and came back shaking and pretended you hadn't." Her throat tightened. "I have had twenty years of an ordinary life, and six weeks of a true one, and the true ones are worth all of it. So don't you dare stand there and tell me this is a waste."

"Then tell me it isn't goodbye."

The footsteps reached the foot of the stairs.

* * *

The Hollow King entered his garden the way rot enters fruit: from within outward.

He came alone—or wearing the shape of alone, a tall figure in a crown that had not gleamed in two hundred years, its gold gone the grey of old teeth. Where he walked, the roses stopped mid-bloom and forgot how to open. The thorn-light bent toward him like iron filings toward a magnet, and Edmund understood, suddenly and completely, what she was looking at: not a man, not even a monster, but a hunger given ceremony. A parasite so old it had forgotten it was ever anything else.

Its eyes—his eyes, the king's stolen eyes—found her across the ruined garden, and behind the royal boredom something ancient went very still.

"*Keeper,*" it said. The voice was many voices, layered like pages of a book pressed too long together. "You brought yourself to me. How economical."

"I brought you a trade."

It tilted its head. The gesture was almost human, which made it worse. "The gate is dying, little heir. What could you possibly offer that I would want more than the city above us? Two hundred thousand lives, ripened and ready. Decades upon decades of borrowed time, all flowing to my table."

"Borrowed years are thin," Edmund said. "You've said so yourself—I read it in your steward's correspondence. Gruel. Secondhand meals. You've been starving on other people's leftovers for two centuries, and you are *so hungry*, and you are so tired of pretending you aren't." She took a step forward, and the gate's light rose to meet her like a tide rising to meet the moon. "I offer you a life lived from the inside. Twenty years of ordinary days, and six weeks of extraordinary ones. Love. Grief. A childhood built on a beautiful lie and the fury of unmaking it. Every memory I have, freely given, willingly offered—keeper's memories, the vintage you've craved since before this kingdom had a name."

The Hollow King was silent for a long moment. Around it, the garden seemed to lean in.

"Why," it said at last, and the many voices had narrowed to one, and the one was almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Keepers do not offer. Keepers withhold. Your mother withheld. Your grandmother withheld. They died withholding. Why would you break the oldest rule of your blood?"

"Because my mother also taught me that the binding is a conversation." Edmund spread her hands. "And I am the first keeper in two hundred years willing to say something other than *no*."

Something moved behind the king's face—something vast turning over in shallow water.

"Freely given?" it asked.

"Freely given."

"And you understand the price."

"The price is the point."

It laughed then, and the laugh was the worst sound of the night, because underneath it she could hear the true king—buried, drowned, two hundred years deep—laughing too, or weeping, or both.

"Then come to the table, keeper," said the Hollow King, "and be *eaten*."

* * *

Wren tried to stop her. Of course he did. She felt his hand close on her sleeve and she turned and kissed him—not gently, nothing gentle in it, a kiss like driving a fence post, like planting something that would outlive the planter—and when she pulled back, his eyes were wet and furious and full of a love he had never once managed to say aloud.

"Tell me our story someday," she whispered. "Even if I can't hear it. Tell it anyway. Stories outlive their listeners. I read that in a ledger once."

"That's not how—" His voice failed. He gripped her shoulders hard enough to bruise. "Edmund. *Edmund.*"

"Keep the gate open behind me as long as you can. That's your part. Knight's work. You'll be good at it." She stepped back. "And Wren—when it's done, and if there's anything left of me that can hear you—say my name. Not Sterling. My real one. I want to leave holding it."

"Edra," he said hoarsely. "Your name is Edra Thorn-Sterling. Your mother sang it to you every night. You told me. Under the hedge, after—" He stopped, jaw working. "After you remembered."

"Edra," she repeated, tasting it. "Yes. That's mine. That one's mine to lose."

Then she turned from him—from his outstretched hand, from the sound he made, which she would remember for exactly as long as she had remembering left—and walked to the gate.

* * *

The Hollow King met her at the threshold, and it did not touch her, because it didn't need to. Opening before her like a wound, the feeding began.

It started with the small things, and she understood why: it was savoring. The smell of the guildhall's undercroft, candlewax and cold stone. The weight of a quill in fingers trained to invisibility. The taste of the bread she'd bought every morning from the same baker for nine years, who had never once learned her name. Ordinary days, hundreds of them, pouring out of her like water from a broken jug, and the Hollow King drank and drank and its grey crown brightened a shade, and its borrowed body straightened, and somewhere in the dark behind its ribs a hunger older than kingdoms sighed with relief.

*Good,* Edmund thought, standing very still in the river of herself. *Drink. There's so much more.*

Her schooling went next—the little schoolroom, chalk dust, a teacher whose face she lost even as she watched it go. Her first lie. Her first friend, a girl named Marta, gone now, the name dissolving even as she reached for it. She had thought losing memories would feel like being robbed. It didn't. It felt like being *unwritten*, like watching her own hand erase a page she'd only just finished reading, and she clung to the terror because terror was proof she still existed—

And then it found her mother.

It found her mother and stopped.

For one endless moment the Hollow King hung motionless in the garden's bleeding light, and Edmund felt it turn her mother over the way a miser turns a coin—every angle, every facet. A woman singing in a walled garden. A woman bargaining with clerks in the dark, trading her daughter's past for her daughter's future. A woman running through snow with soldiers' lanterns behind her. A woman turning, at the last, to face the swords, her mouth shaping a name—

*Edra. My Edra. Grow where they cannot find you.*

The Hollow King shuddered.

It wanted more. Oh, it wanted more—it dove into that vein like a starving thing into a granary, tearing through twenty years of a mother's love, every lullaby, every lesson, every hidden hour in the garden where she'd taught her daughter the names of thorn-roses and the grammar of gates. And Edmund gave it. She opened herself wider than any keeper had ever opened, wider than wisdom, wider than survival, and she fed the parasite her whole inheritance: her mother, her mother's mother, the long line of Sterlings stretching back through centuries of gardens and doors and songs. Generations of keeping. Centuries of *no*.

And woven all through it, because she could not help it and did not try, she fed it Wren.

The north road. The fire. His terrible saddle posture. The long gallery and the portraits of dead keepers watching them run. The moment he broke his oath before the hollow court, his voice cracking on the word *thief*. The kiss at the gate, thirty heartbeats ago, which she was losing even as she lived it.

It gorged.

That was the flaw in the parasite, the flaw her mother had seen and the archivist had confirmed and Edmund had staked everything upon: it had no bottom. It had never learned refusal. For two hundred years it had taken and taken because taking was all it knew how to do, and here at last was a feast beyond anything its gluttony had dreamed—whole, rich, *true*, a keeper's unbroken life offered up like a slaughtered ox—and it ate the way it had always eaten.

It forgot to stop.

Edmund felt the moment it passed the point of no return, felt it in the gate's shuddering light and the garden's screaming roses: the Hollow King drowning in her. Memories it could not digest, a self it could not metabolize, love and grief and fury tangled so densely through everything it swallowed that the parasite's ancient simplicity began to tear. It tried to hold her mother and her mother's mother and the girl Marta and the baker's unnamed bread and Wren's cracked voice saying *thief*, all at once, forever, and there was no room left in it for anything else.

There was no room left in it for the crown.

The grey gold dimmed. The tall shape staggered. Its many voices unraveled into one voice, then into no voice at all, and the last thing Edmund heard as her own memories guttered out like candles in wind was the Hollow King trying to remember what it was—and failing, and failing, and failing, each attempt thinner than the last, until finally it could not even remember to be hungry.

The gate's thorn-light went white.

Then gold.

Then warm.

* * *

Silence, afterward. Or something like silence—the garden breathing, the roses blooming steadily now, petal by petal, one spring at a time.

Edmund knelt on the flagstones, and she did not know why she was kneeling, or what the light was, or why her cheeks were wet.

Above her, on the throne-side of the fading gate, a man stood swaying in the ruin of the Hollow King's robes. He was old and young at once, a face two hundred years unused, and he was weeping without shame, great heaving sobs, tears cutting clean lines through the dust of a long imprisonment. His hands rose slowly before his eyes and turned over, and over, as if he could not believe they were his.

"Aldous," whispered someone behind Edmund—a knight's voice, wrecked and reverent. "Your Majesty. It's over. You're awake."

The true king looked at his hands, and at the garden, and at the kneeling woman he did not know, and his weeping redoubled.

"What year is it," he said. "What *year*—and what have they done in my name?"

Edmund opened her mouth to answer, and found she had no answer, because she could not remember what a guild was, or a ledger, or a road, or a war. She could not remember the man in the doorway whose face was breaking. She could not remember her own name.

But her hands, pressed flat against the warm stones, knew the garden.

And her lips, without her permission, were already humming a song she did not know—a lullaby, soft and green and old—about a keeper who kept a garden, at the end of the world.
