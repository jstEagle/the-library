## 20. Aftermath of a Living King

The king woke weeping, and the first thing he asked for was a list.

Not water. Not wine. Not the physicians who had crowded the throne room and now pressed against its doors like moths against glass, terrified of what they had witnessed and more terrified of being sent away from it. A list. That was all. King Aldous sat on the floor beneath his own empty throne—the crown of thorns lay in two pieces beside him, still smoking faintly where Edmund Sterling's offered memories had burned it hollow—and he said, in a voice cracked with two hundred years of disuse:

"Bring me ink. Bring me paper. Bring me every record this palace keeps."

Lord Chancellor Merric Vane, who had served the Hollow King for forty years without once wondering why the man never aged, knelt at the foot of the dais and did not know what to say. The king before him was old—not ancient, not monstrous, but old the way a tree is old, with weather in his face and grief in the set of his shoulders. His eyes were wet and clear and unbearably human.

"A list," Aldous repeated. "Of everything done in my name while I slept. Every year taken from Vessamyre. Every name bottled. Every keeper hunted." He looked down at his hands, turning them over as if they belonged to a stranger, as if he were the stranger. "I have been asleep since before your grandfather was born, Lord Chancellor. And I wake to find my crown has been eating my kingdom."

"Sire—"

"Do not call me that yet." The words came out gentle, which somehow made them worse. "I am not your sire. Whatever wore my face for two centuries—that was your sire. I am only the man it left behind." He pressed his palm flat against the cold stone floor, as though checking whether the world were real. "But the crimes were committed wearing *me*, Merric. So the debt is mine to count. Bring me the ledgers. All of them. Even—" his voice caught, "—especially the ones about the Sterlings."

Merric bowed and rose on legs that shook. At the door he paused, unable to help himself.

"The woman," he said. "The one who unbound you. She gave up her memories. All of them. To feed the thing, so it would forget how to be king."

"I know." Aldous closed his eyes. "I felt her go through me like light through water. Two hundred years of stolen years, and she paid for my freedom with the only coin she'd ever truly owned." He opened his eyes again, and they were terrible and kind. "Find out what remains of her. And Merric—if anything can be returned to a person who has given herself away—I want it returned. Do you understand? However long it takes. However much it costs."

* * *

In the undercroft of the Guild of Impossible Things, the clerks burned their own ledgers.

It began, as most impossible things do, quietly. Junior Clerk Anselm Petch—a narrow, ink-stained man of thirty years' service, who had once traded away his sister's memory of their mother in exchange for a promotion he could no longer remember wanting—stood before the great iron stove in the accounting hall, holding the first volume of the Year-Tithe rolls, and found that his hands would not obey him.

Around him, the guildhall groaned. Above, the enforcers who had not died in the collapse of the Hollow Court were being disarmed by their own brothers-in-arms; the guildmaster had been carried out raving, insisting the gate could be rebound, insisting there were procedures, insisting on order, on inventory, on *accounts*. But down here among the shelves, the clerks stood in silence and looked at the books they had kept all their lives, and understood, all at once and together, what the books actually were.

"We always knew," said Senior Clerk Hesta Morrow. She was sixty, gray-haired, precise as a blade. She had audited the tithe rolls for four decades and never once let herself read past the numbers into what the numbers meant. "We knew. We just called it balancing."

Anselm's throat worked. "If we burn them, the debts are gone. The names. The years people sold. There'll be no record. No way to give anything back."

"No way to take anything back either," Hesta said. She took the volume gently from his hands, the way one takes a wounded bird. "That's what they were for, Anselm. Not restitution. Collection. Every page is a hook." She weighed the book in her palms. "My mother sold nine years when I was small, so I could eat through the famine winter. Nine years. I used to tell myself the ledger honored her sacrifice. Kept it safe. Made it matter." Her voice did not break; it simply stopped being steady. "It didn't keep anything safe. It just remembered what she owed."

She fed the book to the stove.

The pages caught with a sound almost like singing—ink made of living things does not burn silently—and the firelight leapt gold across thirty faces. No one had ordered this. No proclamation had come down from any throne. It spread through the undercroft the way warmth spreads through a cold room: one clerk, then another, then whole rows of them pulling volumes from the shelves, the trade-rolls of bottled names, the contracts of quieted memories, the thin black book that recorded, in a clerk's careful hand, every member of the Sterling family traced and tracked and finally erased.

Anselm Petch burned that one himself. He held it until the cover curled, watching the fire eat the entries—*E. Sterling, gatekeeper's line, memory alteration complete, subject compliant*—and when it was ash he wept, briefly and without dignity, and then went back to work, because there were six hundred more volumes and the night was long.

By dawn the chimneys of the Guild of Impossible Things smoked like signal fires, and the people of Vessamyre, standing in streets still scarred with thorn-light, saw the smoke and did not need to be told what it meant. Some cheered. Some knelt. One old woman who had sold fifty years to the crown stood beneath the smoke with her face turned up and her mouth open, saying nothing at all.

Hesta Morrow stayed until the last shelf was empty. Then she took down the final object in the room: a single unmarked ledger, newer than the rest, kept apart in a locked drawer. Inside, in fresh ink, someone—she suspected she would never learn who—had written a single line:

*Account closed. Nothing further owed.*

She hesitated. Then she burned it too. Some mercies should not be archived.

* * *

Edmund dreamed of a garden she did not remember, and woke in a bed she did not recognize, in a body that felt borrowed.

Sunlight came through high windows, warm and slow. Someone had left water and bread on the table beside her. On the wall opposite hung a sprig of dried thorn-rose, and she stared at it for a long time without knowing why her chest ached, why her eyes filled, why her hand rose toward it before her mind had decided anything at all.

Her hands knew things. That was the strangest part. When she sat up, her fingers moved with small, sure competencies she could not account for—they balanced themselves on the quilt edge like a clerk's fingers finding a column; they cupped around an invisible bloom with a gardener's tenderness. When she stood, her feet knew the floor. When she spoke aloud, testing, her voice came out low and level and calm, and she thought: *that is a voice trained to be overlooked,* and did not know where she had learned the trick of it.

A nurse found her there, standing barefoot in the middle of the room, touching the thorn-rose sprig with one fingertip.

"You're awake," the nurse said, wonder plain in her voice. "Three days. The healers said—you gave away so much, they said perhaps nothing would remain at all."

"Remain of what?" Edmund asked.

The nurse's face did something complicated—grief and pity and something close to reverence. "You don't... You really don't remember."

"I remember waking up." Edmund considered. "And before that, a door. A green door, underwater. And a voice saying—" she frowned, chasing it, feeling it slip like fish through her fingers, "—saying something about keeping. About keeping something. Or being kept." She shook her head. "There was a man. There was—there was someone whose coat smelled of rain and iron. I can't see his face. I can't even say how I know about the coat."

"That would be Sir Wren," the nurse said carefully. "He hasn't left the corridor outside your door since they carried you up from under the palace. Three days. He sleeps in a chair like a man standing guard, and the new king has twice summoned him and twice he's refused to go until you woke."

Something moved through Edmund at the name—not memory, but the shape of one, the hole where a memory should be, aching the way a lost tooth aches. She put her hand flat against her sternum.

"Tell him," she started, and stopped, because she did not know what to tell him. *Thank you* for what? *Forgive* whom? She was a page razored out of her own book; she could feel the cut edges of herself everywhere she looked inward.

"There's something else," the nurse said, gentler now. "The king—he wants to see you. He's been asking every day. He says—" she hesitated, then delivered it exactly, as though it had been drilled into her, which it had, "—he says he owes a gardener two hundred years of back wages, and he intends to discuss terms in person."

Despite everything—despite the hollow places, despite the fear like cold water at the base of her skull—Edmund laughed. It startled her, the laugh. It came from somewhere deeper than remembering.

"And my hands?" she said, looking down at them, turning them over in the sunlight. They were smudged faintly with soil that had not been there when she fell asleep. She was almost certain it had not been. "Why do my hands keep doing that?"

The nurse followed her gaze and went pale, and said nothing, and Edmund understood that whatever answer existed, the woman had decided she was not strong enough to hear it.

They dressed her in plain clothes someone had thoughtfully provided, and walked her down corridors hung with portraits—keepers, she realized, generations of faces that watched her pass with painted eyes that seemed less painted than they should be—and at the end of the gallery, framed in a doorway full of morning light, stood a tall man in a ruined knight's coat that smelled, even from here, even across twenty paces, of rain and iron.

He looked at her the way a drowning man looks at shore.

Edmund looked back, and felt the ache of the missing thing, and heard herself say—her voice steady, her heart anything but:

"You. Coat-of-rain-and-iron. Tell me who you are. Tell me who *I* am." She took one step toward him, soil-dark fingerprints ghosting the air behind her like a trail only she could not see. "Start at the beginning. I'm told I have time."

Behind Wren Calloway, at the far end of the gallery, King Aldous waited by the window with a ledger open in his hands—the first page of a new one, blank except for a single line already written—and he made no move to interrupt, because some accounts, he had learned in three days of counting, must be settled person to person or not at all.
