## 21. A Knight Without a Crown to Serve

The pardon arrived on vellum the color of old cream, sealed with the true king's signet—Aldous the Restored, it read, by grace and by grief—and Wren Calloway held it over the brazier in his rented room and very nearly let it burn.

He didn't, in the end. Not because he wanted it. Because the parchment was the last thing Edmund Sterling had touched with her whole self intact, the night before she gave her memories away. Her fingers had left a faint smudge of ink at the corner, a clerk's smudge, precise as everything she did. He had watched her sign the witness line with a steady hand and thought, absurdly, *she writes like someone balancing a ledger*. He had not known then that she was writing her own name into the column of things to be spent.

He folded the pardon along its old creases and put it in the bottom of his saddlebag, beneath the spare shirt and the whetstone and the letter from the palace steward he had not answered. Then he sat on the edge of the bed in the grey light of a February morning and did what he had done every morning for three months, which was to try and remember her face.

He could. That was the cruelty of it. He remembered everything—the way she'd looked at him across the guildhall undercroft with her ledger balanced on her hip, the way she'd said *you knock like a man with a conscience, which is worse than knocking like a thief*, the way she'd wept once, only once, in the buried garden, with thorn-light in her hair. He remembered the gallery, and the running, and the wound in his side he still felt when the weather turned. He remembered her hands on his face at the gate, and her voice saying his name like a word she was learning.

And she remembered none of it. Not the ambush on the north road. Not Vessamyre, or the rotting years, or the long gallery with its watching portraits. Not the confession he'd made in the undercity, bleeding and cowardly and honest—the worst night of his life, the truest. Not the way he had loved her, if love was the word for something that had grown in the space between two people who had every reason to kill each other and had chosen, over and over, not to.

She remembered a garden. Her hands remembered the garden. The physicians at the palace said the body keeps what the mind surrenders, and they'd watched her, on her second morning awake, kneel in the ruined gatehouse soil without knowing why and begin to clear the dead brambles from a rosebush no one had tended in two hundred years.

Wren had watched her do it from the doorway, and had understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that she was better off.

That was the thought he'd been circling for three months. That she was free—wholly free, the first free thing he'd ever known—and that the man she'd been with before the unbinding was a man stitched out of guilt and duty and a knight's hunger for absolution. That if she remembered him, she would remember what he'd done. The hunt. Her mother's door. The night the last keeper's family burned, and young Wren Calloway standing in the snow holding a torch he'd been told was only for light.

He had refused the rank the king offered. Aldous—thin and haunted and weeping at odd hours, a man waking into a crime scene two centuries deep—had pressed the commission into his hands and said, *You kept faith when faith was impossible. I would make you Lord Marshal.* And Wren had knelt, because some habits survive even the death of the crown they served, and said, *Your Majesty, I kept faith with a woman, not a crown. Let me go find out if she'll have me.*

The king had looked at him for a long time. Then he'd said, "She opened a door in my prison, Ser Wren. I know what that costs. Go."

* * *

The road north out of Vessamyre was muddy and ordinary, and Wren rode it without armor, in a wool coat the color of wet bark, looking for the first time in twenty years like no one at all. It suited him. The city had changed since the unbinding—the borrowed years had come home to their owners, strange and ragged, and there were old men in Vessamyre now who were suddenly eighty and furious about it, and children who remembered being sixty, and the whole city walked around in a daze of returned time, weeping and laughing and suing each other over debts contracted across decades. The guild's ledgers had been burned in the square by the very clerks who kept them; Wren had stood in the crowd and watched the paper catch, and had felt nothing like triumph. Only a vast, tired quiet, like a fever breaking.

The guildhall stood empty now, its undercroft sealed by the king's order until someone could decide what to do with a building full of doors that traded in impossible things. Wren had not gone in. He had stood outside it once, in the rain, and turned his horse away.

The gatehouse was a day and a half north, past the ridge where they'd been ambushed by guild hunters—past the place where she'd bound his wound with strips of her own shift and told him, in the dark, *if you die on me, Sir Wren, I will enter it in the ledger under losses*. The road remembered him even if she didn't. His horse quickened on the ridge without being asked, and Wren let it, and did not let himself hope.

Hope was a thing he'd learned to hold the way you hold a bird: gently, with open fingers, ready for it to leave.

* * *

He saw the smoke first—thin and domestic, a cookfire's smoke, not a pyre—and then the walls.

The gatehouse had been a ruin for as long as there were records, a ring of broken stone around the buried garden, and when he'd last seen it, in the aftermath, it had looked like a wound. Now it looked like the beginning of something. The brambles were cut back from the outer wall. A new gate stood in the old gate's place—simple oak, unbarred, standing open. Someone had repaired the eastern tower's roof with fresh thatch. There were raised beds inside the walls, dark with turned earth, and rows of bare-root saplings heeled in straw, waiting for spring. And over the sealed gate itself—the stone door in the garden's heart, the one that no longer needed to hold anything—someone had planted thorn roses. Dozens of them. They were only sticks now, dormant, patient, but by summer they would be a wall of flowers over the place where the binding had been.

Wren stood at the open gate for a long moment, one hand on his horse's neck, and could not make his feet move.

She found him there. Of course she did; she'd been a keeper of accounts in a guild of spies, and a man standing motionless at a gate was the loudest thing in the world.

She came around the corner of the eastern tower with dirt on her hands and a spade over her shoulder and her hair tied back with a strip of cloth, and she stopped when she saw him, and the world went very quiet.

She looked—older, and younger. The hardness was still there; it had always been there, the armor of the invisible clerk, and it hadn't burned away with the ledgers. But there was something else now, something unarmored, a face that had not yet decided what it was allowed to be. She looked at him the way he had once watched her look at a column of numbers that refused to balance: wary, thorough, unwilling to be fooled.

"You're the knight," she said.

"Yes."

"They told me about you. At the palace. The one who broke his oath." Her eyes went to his side, to where the wound had been, as if she'd been told that too. "The one who was wounded."

"That's me."

"And I knew you. Before." Not a question. She had clearly interrogated everyone she could reach, and had assembled the shape of her lost months the way she'd once assembled accounts—item by item, demanding receipts.

"You knew me," Wren said. His voice came out lower than he meant it to. "For about a season. Under circumstances I'd call—"

"I know what the circumstances were." She set the spade against the wall, deliberately, a small ceremony of not-fleeing. "The archivist's daughter wrote it all down for me. The guild. The map. The king. The—" she paused, and he watched her choose a word, "—the ransom. She said I chose it. That I knew what it would cost and I paid it anyway."

"It's true."

"Then you can tell me if she lied about the rest." And here her chin came up, and for a moment he saw the woman who had stood in front of the Hollow King and refused to flinch, the woman who had walked into a throne room full of hollowed courtiers with a stolen map in her coat. "Because she said—" and now her voice did something it clearly did not want to do, thinned and roughened, "—she said we were friends. That it was more than friends. And I have no way to know if that's kindness or pity or just a story people tell a woman with a hole where her life used to be. So."

She stopped. Breathed. The cookfire smoke drifted between them, smelling of rosemary.

"So I'm asking you," she said. "Not the palace version. Not the archivist's version. You were there. Tell me our story. From the beginning."

* * *

Wren had faced charges of treason without his voice shaking. He had named the Hollow King a thief in front of the assembled court with a sword in his ribs. He had confessed to the worst night of his life to the daughter of the woman he'd failed, kneeling in filth in the undercity. And none of it had cost him what this cost—standing in a garden he didn't deserve, in front of a woman who didn't remember him, trying to tell the truth without stealing anything from her.

"From the beginning," he said. "All right."

He told it slowly, and he told it honestly, which meant he told it unflatteringly. He told her about the guildhall at closing bell, and the dead cartographer, and how he'd been sent to find the map and had found her instead—a clerk with ink-stained fingers who looked at him like he was an entry she was trying to classify. He told her about the north road, and how he'd caught her and let her go, and how each of them had believed they'd chosen it, and how they'd both been wrong. He told her about Vessamyre, and the borrowed years, and the old man in the street who'd sold forty years to keep his granddaughter safe and had wept when the years came home to him rotten.

He told her about the throne room. "You stood in front of him and you didn't look away. Nobody looks at him. Everyone in that court had learned to look at the floor, at the crown, at the space where a face should be. You looked at his eyes. And something behind them saw you, and you stood there and let it see you, and I remember thinking—" he stopped.

"Thinking what?"

"Thinking that I'd sworn my life to a thing that eats, and here was a woman who fed herself to it on purpose, to save everyone, and which of us was the knight."

Edmund said nothing. Her hands had gone still in her lap. The light was failing; somewhere in the beds a robin was making its last argument of the day.

He told her about the gallery. The portraits of every keeper, watching them run. The way she'd grabbed his wrist at the turning and said *left, they'll expect right*—she'd known the palace better than he did, a clerk who'd memorized it from ledgers. He told her about the undercity, and here he made himself stop and look at her, because this part he would not soften and would not rush.

"I was there," he said, "the night your mother died. I was nineteen. I held a torch. I told myself it was for light. I hunted her family because I was told they were thieves, and I believed it because believing was easier than asking. And I told you all of this, before the end, because I would not have you give your memories for a man who'd hidden that from you. You were furious. You had every right to be. And then—" his throat closed, and he waited it out like weather, "—then you said that a debt entered honestly was worth more than a kindness entered falsely. You forgave me in ledger-language. It was the most forgiving thing anyone's ever said to me."

The robin had gone quiet. The cookfire popped.

"And then the garden," she said. Not a question. Her voice was strange—careful, like someone walking on a frozen river, listening for the crack.

"And then the garden. Your memories came back. All of them. The Sterlings, the binding, your mother's voice singing." He hesitated. "You sang, that night. In the gatehouse. I'd never heard you sing. You said your mother sang that song to the thorns, and the thorns grew toward her voice, and you sang it and I swear the roses leaned."

"Which song?" It came out barely above a whisper.

"I don't know the name. I'd know it if I heard it."

She looked at her hands—her hands, which the physicians said would remember—and Wren watched something move through her face that was not grief exactly, or was grief in its most unbearable form: grief for a thing she had owned and chosen to lose, that lived now only in other people's mouths.

"And at the gate," she said. "At the end. What happened at the end?"

"You gave it everything," Wren said. "Your mother. The guild. The road, the gallery, the garden. Me. It ate your whole past and it got so drunk on it that it forgot how to be king. And the true king woke up crying like a child, and you—" his voice went, and he let it, and did not apologize for it, "—you fell, and I caught you, and you looked at me and you said, *Wren, I can't remember why I trust you, but I do, don't let go.* And I said I wouldn't. And then you slept for nine days, and when you woke, you asked me who I was."

The silence after that was long. The first star came out over the eastern wall. Somewhere below them, in the buried garden, the sealed stone door held its silence under its young roses, holding nothing, needing nothing, a wound become a scar become, in time, maybe, a place where flowers grew.

Edmund stood. She walked to the raised bed by the wall—the one with the rosebush she'd knelt to on her second morning—and she touched one of its dormant canes, very lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"The archivist's daughter told me the facts," she said at last. "But facts aren't the same as a story. Facts are what happened. A story is what it meant." She turned and looked at him, and in the last of the light her eyes were the same eyes he remembered from the throne room—unflinching, thorough, refusing to be fooled, and underneath all that, so tired, so hopeful, so afraid of hoping. "You tell it like a man who thinks he's asking for something he shouldn't have. You tell it like a confession."

"Old habit."

"Well." She picked up the spade again, and held it, and he watched her decide something, watched it happen in her face the way spring happens in a branch—slowly, and then all at once. "I'm a keeper of accounts. It's the only self I have left, and I find I like her. And in my experience, a debt entered honestly is worth more than a kindness entered falsely."

His breath stopped.

"So here is my accounting, Sir Wren Calloway. I don't remember you. I don't remember wanting you, or wronging you, or trusting you, and I won't pretend otherwise, because I have spent enough of my life inside a lie to have no patience for building a new one." She stepped closer. Close enough that he could smell the turned earth on her, and the rosemary smoke, and beneath it something that was only her. "But my hands remember the garden. And when the daughter told me your name, my hands—" she looked down at them, almost angry about it, "—my hands did something. I don't know what they know. But I'd like to find out. Slowly. In the open. With everything entered in the ledger, both columns, no razored pages."

"That's," Wren said, and found his voice had gone to pieces, "that's the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and it was about bookkeeping."

"I'm told I was like that before, too."

"You were exactly like that before." He was laughing, or crying, or both; it didn't matter; there was no one to perform composure for, and hadn't been for three months, and might never be again, and the freedom of it was enormous. "You once told me that if I died on the north road you'd enter it under losses."

Her mouth twitched. "Did you die?"

"Nearly."

"Pity. I'd have balanced beautifully."

And then, because she was Edmund Sterling, keeper of the gate, keeper of the ledger, keeper of her own half-made self—she reached out and took his hand, the one with the old sword calluses, and turned it over, and studied it like an entry she was verifying, and did not let go.

"Stay the night," she said. "There's a bed in the tower, and the roof doesn't leak much. And tomorrow you can start work. The south wall wants mending, and I can't lift the beams alone, and the roses over the gate will need training come spring. You'll be here when they bloom?"

It was not quite a question, and it was not quite an answer to anything he'd asked, and it was, he understood, the most she could give today, and it was enormous, and he took it with both hands.

"I'll be here when they bloom," he said. "And after."

"Good." She released his hand, picked up a lantern from the wall, and turned toward the tower—and then paused, and looked back at him, and her face in the lantern light was the face from the gallery, from the garden, from the last moment before the gate took everything she had. "Wren. The song. The one my mother sang to the thorns."

"Yes?"

"Tomorrow," she said, "you'll hum it for me. However badly. I want to know if my hands remember that too."

* * *

He woke before dawn in the tower bed, as he had woken for twenty years to a knight's discipline, and lay still in the grey dark listening to the gatehouse breathe. Below, he heard her door open, heard her boots on the stones, heard her go out into the garden before light because that was who she was, the kind of person who checked the accounts before the world was awake.

He dressed and came down, and found her standing at the sealed gate in the first pale wash of morning, her breath fogging, her hand flat against the stone door. The thorn roses stood around it in their patient winter rows. And she was singing—low, half-voiced, a melody she clearly didn't know she knew, notes rising out of her like water from a spring she'd been told was dry.

The roses leaned toward her voice.

Wren stood in the doorway of the tower and did not move, and did not breathe, and watched the last keeper of the Thorn Gate sing to a door that no longer held anything but morning—and understood that whatever story they were writing now had only just begun.
