## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Gilded Thorn kept its accounts in ink that remembered.

Vesper Blackwood had told every new hire the same thing on their first night: do not lie to the ledger. The ink was cut with truth-salts and grave-moss, ground fine by a woman who had died owing the guild a favor, and it did not forgive. Write *one silver* when you took two, and the entry would darken over the years, bruising from brown to black, until some auditor — usually her, usually at three in the morning, because three in the morning was when the honest people slept and the dishonest ones thought she was asleep too — came along with a lamp and a long memory and made the difference back with interest.

So she wrote carefully. She always had.

The counting room sat above the guildhall proper, up a staircase that creaked in a key of E-flat, past a door that only opened for members in good standing and for Vesper, who was not technically a member but whose name was carved into the lintel anyway, which Corvane insisted was an administrative accident and which she knew better than to believe. The room smelled of tallow, cedar, and old paper gone soft as cloth. Rain worked at the windows in long slow fingers. Below her, muffled by floorboards, the guildhall was winding down: dice clattering, someone losing badly at knucklebones, Marta the cook singing something filthy about a duke's daughter in a voice that could strip barnacles.

Vesper dipped her pen and turned to page four hundred and eleven.

*Item: one memory, bottled. Vendor: the Widow Sarelle of Candle Row. Contents: the last summer of her marriage, complete, unedited, including the argument she has spent six years trying to forget. Price: forty crowns, paid. Condition: sealed, wax intact. Shelf: C, row nine.*

She checked the shelf.

Row nine held the Widow Sarelle's memory where it had always been, squat and amber-colored behind its glass, glowing faintly with the particular warmth of a summer that no longer existed anywhere else in the world. The wax seal showed no tampering. The glass showed no clouding. But the ledger's entry had begun to darken at the margins — not from dishonesty, Vesper saw at once, but from grief. The widow had come back twice this month to stand outside the Thorn's door, and twice had walked away without knocking, and the ink knew. The ink always knew what the seller felt about what they'd sold.

Vesper noted it in the margin: *Watch. May wish to repurchase. Charge double for the trouble; she can afford it and will feel better being robbed.*

That was the trade, really. That was what the Gilded Thorn actually sold, beneath all the brass and the reputation and the sign out front with its gilded thornbush: not memories, not luck, not second chances. They sold the shape of a regret after it had been lifted out of a life. People paid enormous sums to have their worst hours taken away, and then, more often than anyone would guess, they crept back months or years later to buy them home again, because a person was a person was a person, and a life without its worst hour fit strangely, like a coat with the shoulders let out.

Corvane Ash said that was the whole business model in one sentence. Vesper privately thought it was the whole world in one sentence, but she had never once told him so, because he would have preened for a week.

She turned the page.

*Item: one shadow, stolen. Owner of record: Master Fenwick Ode, tailor, of Cooper's Lane. Taken by: unknown. Recovered by: our own Tessic, per contract dated the ninth. Price of recovery: sixty crowns plus expenses. Current status: —*

Her pen stopped.

The shadow itself sat in its crate across the room, in the corner reserved for things too restless for the vault. It was a strange prisoner, a shadow. It didn't rattle or scratch. It just lay there against the bottom of the crate like a spill of ink that had opinions, and every few minutes it shifted, testing the walls, the way a cat tests a closed door. Fenwick Ode had been without it for three weeks now and had, according to the intake report, stopped going outside entirely. A man without his shadow, the report said, casts nothing, and a man who casts nothing finds that dogs don't bark at him, children don't see him, and his wife begins setting one plate at dinner without quite knowing why.

Vesper finished the line: *Current status: awaiting return delivery. Note: shadow is sulking. Feed it candlelight daily. It prefers the expensive kind.*

She had written that last part herself, weeks ago, after discovering that the stolen shadow grew listless without light to imitate, and that cheap tallow made it thin and gray while good beeswax kept it dark and glossy. There were entries in the ledgers older than her grandmother for the care of bottled laughter (keep near sleeping children), borrowed luck (rotate monthly; it sours), and second chances (never open the box; the second chance is not for you). Nobody had ever written down how to keep a shadow. She'd had to work it out alone, by trial, in the small hours, with nobody watching.

That was most of her life, if she was honest. Small hours, careful work, nobody watching.

She heard the stairs before she heard the door — Corvane never learned to skip the fourth step, even though he'd been climbing them for thirty years, and Vesper had long ago decided that telling him would be a kindness he hadn't earned — and then the door opened on the guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn in his shirtsleeves, carrying two cups of wine and wearing the expression of a man who had come upstairs to check on something and intended to pretend otherwise.

"You're doing the audit," he said, "on the wrong night."

"I'm doing the audit on the right night," Vesper said, not looking up. "You moved it. I moved it back."

"I moved it because the wine shipment comes tomorrow and the cellar inventory takes half the night—"

"Then the cellar inventory can wait, or you can do the cellar inventory yourself, Guildmaster, and we'll see how many barrels you count before you decide the number should be whatever makes the tax man cry." She signed the shadow entry, blotted it, and finally looked up. He was standing there with the two cups and the lamplight catching the gray coming in at his temples, and he looked, for a moment, exactly like what he was: an old knife who had somehow gotten comfortable in a sheath. "Is one of those for me, or are you drinking both to punish me?"

"One's for me," he said, handing her the other. "The second's a bribe."

"For what?"

"For not writing down in that book that your guildmaster forgot the fourth stair again."

"The ledger doesn't record stairs."

"The ledger," Corvane said, dropping into the chair across from her with a groan that had gotten deeper every year since she'd known him, "records everything. That's what frightens me about you. Not the sword on the wall, not the oaths, none of it. It's that someday I'll say something foolish within arm's reach of that book, and you'll write it down, and in two hundred years some auditor yet unborn will find it and know exactly what sort of man founded this place." He drank. "Immortality through paperwork. There are worse hells."

"There are worse men," Vesper said, and went back to the page.

He watched her work for a while. She could feel it, the way you feel weather changing — Corvane Ash watching her was a pressure like the air before snow. When she'd first come to the Thorn at sixteen, all elbows and suspicion and a god-oath she wouldn't discuss, she'd hated being watched. Now she merely noticed it. She wasn't sure whether that was trust or erosion.

"You pray tonight?" he asked, casually, the way he asked everything he actually cared about.

"Not yet."

"It's late."

"It's late," she agreed.

He let it go, which was its own kind of message. Ten years ago — no. Nine years, ten months, and some days she did not count, except on the nights she counted them. Nine years and change since the god Vael had spoken to her last, and she still prayed every night the way she'd been taught, kneeling at the little threshold-shrine in her room with its worn stone step and its single candle, saying the words of binding aloud to a silence so complete it had texture. Other sworn blades had drifted. Old Hasken had taken his oath-cord off at fifty and buried it under a turnip field, and swore the turnips grew better for it. Vesper had never taken hers off. She wasn't sure anymore whether that was faith or stubbornness or just the fact that removing it would require admitting the question she couldn't afford to ask.

*Did he ever answer at all?*

She shook it off and pulled the next ledger toward her — the red one, the current one, the book that held everything the Thorn currently owned that could not be weighed honestly. Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. A saint's left kneecap (leased, terms negotiable). A jar of thunder from a storm that had killed a king. Three second chances, still boxed. And, on the top shelf behind the locked grate, the thing they didn't advertise: a small lead coffin, cold to any touch, that Corvane had bought at auction eleven years ago from a man who wept the entire time and refused to say what was inside.

"What's tonight's worry?" Corvane asked. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you make when the books are trying to tell you something. Like a woman reading a letter from someone she owes money to."

Vesper tapped the red ledger. "Sarelle's memory is darkening. Grief-bleed, not theft. And the Ode shadow is sulking worse — I think whoever stole it originally bound it with a knot Ilsa Rennick used to use, which means either our thief apprenticed under her, or—" She stopped, hearing her own sentence arrive somewhere she hadn't planned to go.

Corvane's cup paused halfway to his mouth.

"Or," he said, very evenly, "what?"

"Nothing. Coincidence." She wrote a note. "Knots travel. Ward-work travels. Half the hedge-witches on the border use patterns that started in the Warden schools before the purges."

"Mm," said Corvane.

It was a suspicious sound, but she let it go, because the wine was good and the rain was pleasant and because there was a larger truth sitting between them that neither of them ever touched directly: that Corvane Ash knew things about the Thorn's history that he kept locked in rooms Vesper's keys didn't open, and that Vesper Blackwood kept her doubts locked in rooms his questions didn't reach, and that the arrangement had held for nine years precisely because neither of them had ever tried the locks.

Below them, Marta's song ended. Dice rattled once more and went quiet. The guildhall was closing, the lamps going out one by one, the great gilded thornbush over the mantle going dark branch by branch.

"I'll do the prayer," Vesper said, mostly to end the conversation.

"Good," Corvane said, and stood, and gathered his cup, and at the door he paused — he always paused at the door, another habit she'd catalogued years ago and filed under *things the guildmaster does instead of saying things* — and looked back at her with something almost soft in his old scarred face.

"The widow," he said. "If she comes back. Be gentle with her. Charge her double, but be gentle."

Vesper blinked. "I already wrote that."

"I know," Corvane said. "I taught you."

He went down the stairs, skipping nothing, hitting the fourth step with a creak, and Vesper sat alone with the lamps and the ledgers and the rain.

* * *

She finished the audit by lamplight, because finishing things was the only prayer some people knew how to say.

Page four hundred thirteen: a vial of borrowed courage, returned by a soldier who'd used it to walk into a burning house for his brother and now wanted, pathetically, to buy it permanently, and Vester had to write the refusal the ledger required — *courage does not store; it ferments; a permanent purchase becomes arrogance within the year* — and add a note of her own: *offer him a discount on the memory of doing it instead. He'll take it. Everyone does.* Page four hundred fourteen: the saint's kneecap lease renewal, initialed by a bishop who signed himself with a false name and thought the ink wouldn't notice. The ink had noticed. Vesper drew a small, precise circle around the false signature and wrote *he knows we know; renew anyway; saints' relics appreciate when scandalized.*

And then, at the bottom of the drawer where the current ledger lived, her fingers found paper that shouldn't have been there.

One loose sheet. No header. No shelf-mark. Written in a hand she recognized because it was hers — the sharp downstrokes, the way she crossed her sevens — recording an item she had never audited, a transaction she had never witnessed, dated in a year that had not happened yet.

Vesper Blackwood sat very still, the way she'd been trained to sit when something in a room changed shape.

Outside, above the mountain, above the rain, the sky was full of stars. She counted them without meaning to, the way she counted everything, an old habit from the slaver cages where counting had been the only property nobody could take — and she got all the way to the Pleiades before the first one went out.

Not dimmed. Out. Snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

She stood up so fast her chair went over. The second star went. The third. Across the whole black bowl of the heavens, the lights were failing, one by one, in no order she could read, and beneath the floorboards the guildhall was waking — shouts, a crash of crockery, Tessic's voice bellowing for the guildmaster — and from far away, from under the town, under the border, under everything, came a long low sound like a door being drawn shut at the bottom of the world.

In her hand, the impossible page whispered against the others.

On the desk, in its crate, the stolen shadow pressed itself flat against the wood and shivered.

And Vesper Blackwood, auditor of the Gilded Thorn, keeper of the ledgers of impossible things, sworn blade of a silent god, reached for her sword and her oath-cord and the lamp, and went to find out what her guildmaster had been keeping from her for nine years.
