## 2. A God Who Stopped Answering

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of a knuckle, and Vesper Blackwood did not replace it.

She sat in her office above the Gilded Thorn's counting floor with the ledger open before her and her hands folded on top of it, which was not work. Work was ink and arithmetic and the small violence of a straight line drawn through a debt. What she was doing now was waiting — the way she waited every night, at the same hour, in the same chair, for an answer that had not come in ten years.

She did not call it praying anymore. Praying implied hope, and she was a professional woman who kept accurate books.

*Vael.* The name went out from her the way it always did, silent as smoke through a keyhole. *Vael of the Doorways. Keeper of the In-Between. I am still here.*

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered since she was twenty years old. But she said it anyway, because an oath was an oath, and because some stubborn, humiliating part of her believed that if she ever stopped saying it, then it would be true that no one was listening.

Outside, the city of Cindral Vale ticked through its evening: cart wheels on wet cobbles, a drunk singing three streets over, the border-wards humming their low note that only the sworn could hear — a sound like a struck bell remembered rather than heard, felt in the teeth. That hum was the one comfort left to her. Whatever else had failed, the wards held. The wards always held.

Vesper opened her eyes and got back to work.

* * *

Ten years ago there had been snow on the road out of Marrowfen, and Vesper Blackwood — fifteen, barefoot, wrists raw from rope — had been running through it with four slavers behind her and a dead girl's boots on her feet.

She had stolen the boots off a body in the ditch an hour before the slavers caught up. She'd told herself it wasn't stealing, really, since dead girls didn't need them, but even then she'd been honest enough in her own head to know what she was doing. Taking things. Keeping count. Those were the two skills she'd been raised by the road to have, and she would spend the rest of her life being paid well for both.

The slavers caught her at a threshold.

That was the thing she could never explain afterward, to anyone, without sounding like a madwoman or a liar: they caught her *at a threshold*. A doorway in the middle of nowhere, a ruined gate standing alone in a frozen field with no wall attached to either side, stone posts and nothing between them but winter air. She ran through it because it was there and because running through things was all she had left, and on the far side —

On the far side, the world changed its mind.

The slavers came through after her, four men with knives and nets and the flat patience of people who had done this many times, and the gate closed like an eye.

Not slammed. Closed. The way a door closes when someone polite is standing beside you holding it. And the air inside the gate's shadow went thick and silver and absolutely still, snow hanging in the air like flies in amber, and Vesper stood in the middle of it with her heart hammering and understood, in the bone-deep way you understand fire is hot or falling kills, that something enormous was looking at her.

*You are between,* said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived the way the ward-hum arrived — felt in the teeth, remembered rather than heard. *Between the field and the field. Between owned and free. Between fifteen and whatever comes next. I am the god of such places. Say your name, child, so I may hold the door for you.*

"Vesper," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "Vesper Blackwood."

*Blackwood.* Something in the stillness shifted, almost like pleasure. *A tree that keeps its leaves in winter. Good. Hold to that.* And then, gentler than anything had ever been gentle with her: *Walk through, Vesper Blackwood. On the other side of this gate you are no one's property. I will make certain of it.*

She walked through. Behind her she heard — this was the part she never told anyone, the part she kept folded away like letters too precious to reread often — she heard four men scream. Briefly. Not long. When she turned around there was nothing in the gate's shadow but snow beginning, again, to fall, and four sets of footprints that stopped mid-stride as though their owners had simply been subtracted from the world.

She stood in the field shaking, wearing a dead girl's boots, and made the only bargain her heart knew how to make.

"I'll serve you," she said to the empty gate. "I don't have anything else. But I've got this. My whole life. Take it."

And the gate hummed — that low remembered-bell note she would hear every day for the rest of her life — and she chose, with the absolute certainty available only to the young and the desperate, to believe it was *yes*.

* * *

Present-day Vesper finished the second column of the audit and allowed herself one line of the good ink to record that someone — and the handwriting said Corvane, because everything expensive said Corvane — had sold a bottled memory at forty percent under valuation to a woman whose name matched no entry in the client registry.

She underlined it twice. She did not go downstairs to confront him about it yet. There was a rhythm to these nights, an old choreography: she found his irregularities, he pretended surprise, they argued over a decanter of something older than both of them, and neither of them named the actual thing that sat between them like a third person in the room.

The bottled memory itself sat in the vault below, catalogued as item 114: *One summer afternoon, coastal village, age nine, complete with salt-smell and a dog*. She'd appraised it herself three months ago and had spent an embarrassing quarter hour afterward trying to remember her own ninth year and finding mostly fog. That was the trade, of course. That was always the trade. You gave up pieces of yourself to get through winters, and one day you looked in the cabinet of your own head and found the shelves organized by someone else.

Her door opened without a knock, which meant Corvane Ash, guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn, keeper of impossible inventory, a man who knocked on the doors of kings and never on hers.

"You're doing the ritual," he said.

"I'm doing an audit."

"You're sitting in the dark with your hands folded." He crossed the room and lit the candle stub without asking, because Corvane had never once in twelve years respected the sanctity of another person's darkness. "Same hour every night. Same posture. I've watched you do it for ten years, Vesper, and I've never once asked. Consider this me asking."

"Consider this me declining."

He sat on the corner of her desk anyway, which was his way of declining her decline. Up close he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep — a hollowness behind the eyes that had been getting worse for a year, like a man listening to a sound no one else could hear. She had noticed it. She had entered it into no ledger. Some things you don't write down because writing them down makes them real.

"Ten years," he said. "You were twenty when he went quiet."

"He didn't go quiet." The words came out sharper than she'd intended. She made herself set the pen down neatly, parallel to the ledger's edge, before she continued. "Going quiet implies he was talking and then stopped. Gods don't stop. They're not weather."

"What would you call it, then?"

She thought about the question honestly, because Corvane deserved honesty; it was the one currency they'd never shorted each other in.

"A door closing," she said finally. "Politely. From the other side."

Corvane was quiet a moment. When she glanced up, his face had done something complicated and brief — a flinch folded inside a wince folded inside something that might have been guilt, gone so fast she could almost believe she hadn't seen it.

"And you keep praying anyway," he said. "Every night. To a closed door."

"It's not prayer. It's bookkeeping." She turned back to the ledger, to the neat double-underlined fraud of him. "An oath is a contract, guildmaster. Contracts don't lapse because one party stops corresponding. I swore my life to Vael of the Doorways, and until someone shows me a release clause written in a hand I recognize, I am still — technically — employed."

"Technically." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "You know what I think? I think you're not praying to him at all anymore. I think you're leaving notes in an empty house so you can prove to yourself you haven't moved out."

Vesper looked at him for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is why we don't let you do the emotional appraisals. You'd value everything at nothing just to avoid paying."

He laughed — genuinely, briefly — and pushed off the desk, and for a moment the tiredness lifted and he was just Corvane again, the man who'd fished a half-starved ex-slave girl out of a gutter recruitment line because she could count faster than his clerk and lied less. "Come drink downstairs. I opened the Amaran wine."

"The one you've been saving for—"

"For an occasion. Yes. I've decided tonight qualifies." He was already at the door, already turning away, and something in his shoulders — she couldn't have said what, exactly, some asymmetry, some wrongness in how he carried the word *tonight* — snagged on her attention like a fishhook in wool. "One glass. Then you can come back up and talk to your empty house."

"One glass," she agreed, and blew out the candle he'd lit, because some rituals belonged to her alone.

* * *

They drank downstairs among the sleeping inventory, because Corvane liked drinking among the shelves — he said the impossible things kept better company than most possible ones. Bottled memories glowed faintly blue in their rack like a shelf of drowned stars. A stolen shadow hung in its iron frame, shifting restlessly, wanting its owner. Somewhere in the deep vaults a jar of second chances whispered in seven voices when anyone walked past, and everyone at the Thorn had learned not to walk past slowly.

Vesper held her wine and did not taste it.

Here was the arithmetic she never wrote down, the sum she did every night in the dark with her hands folded: ten years of silence. Ten years of oaths kept one-sidedly. Every ward she'd sworn to maintain, every threshold she'd stepped over with reverence, every coin tithed to wayside shrines that no longer held even the warmth of use — all of it given to a god who had held one gate open for a freezing girl and then apparently walked away from the whole arrangement.

Except.

Except the wards still hummed. Except the borders still held. Except that in ten years, across a hundred contracts and a thousand doors, not one threshold had failed her — not one lock jammed against her unfairly, not one doorway refused her passage, not one gate closed in her face at the moment she needed it open. She'd told herself it was coincidence. She'd told herself it was habit, luck, the ordinary mercy of an indifferent world.

But she was an auditor. And an auditor knows the difference between random fortune and a pattern, and the pattern was this: every single time, the door was where she needed it to be.

So which was worse? she wondered, watching the candlelight move in her untouched wine. A god who abandoned you — or a god who kept answering, silently, in the only language he had left, while you sat in the dark resenting him for the silence?

"Where did you go?" Corvane asked softly. "Just now. Your eyes went somewhere."

"To a gate in a frozen field," she said, because the wine was true and so was she, tonight of all nights. "Before your time. Before any of this."

"Was he there?" Corvane asked. "At the gate?"

"For about a minute." She turned the glass. "It was the best minute of my life, guildmaster. Everything I am is built on sixty seconds of being saved. Do you understand what that does to a person? Every night I sit up there and ask him to be real again, and every morning the wards are still humming, and I don't know if that means he loves me or if it means I'm a guard dog barking at a house whose owner died years ago."

The fire cracked. The stolen shadow in its frame turned, slow as a fish, watching them with no eyes.

"Vesper." Corvane set down his glass. His voice had changed — dropped into something careful and low, the voice of a man walking toward the edge of a thing he'd been circling for years. "If you knew — truly knew, in your bones — that he'd been there the whole time. Every hour of the silence. Watching. Holding the doors. Would that be better? Or would it be worse?"

"Worse," she said immediately, and then heard herself, and frowned. "Why did I say worse?"

"Because then the silence would mean something," Corvane said. "Because a god who abandons you is cruel, but a god who stays silent while you break your heart against his absence — while you kneel every night to a presence that could speak and chooses not to—" He stopped. Drank. His hand, she noticed, was not entirely steady. "That requires reasons. Reasons require debts. Debts require—"

He cut himself off so abruptly she heard the shape of the missing word click shut like a trap.

"*Require*," she repeated. "Require what, Corvane?"

"Nothing." He smiled the guildmaster smile, the one priced for public consumption. "Old man's philosophy. Ignore it. Refill your glass."

But she was already writing it down, in the ledger behind her eyes where she kept the entries no one else could read: *Corvane Ash, irregularity, unpaid. Speaks of debts like a man reading his own charges aloud. Flinches at the word 'require.' Watch the vaults.*

"Tomorrow," he said, rising, buttoning his coat, suddenly all business and departure. "Tomorrow I need you to run a full reconciliation of the vault manifest. Every item. Every entry. Leave nothing unchecked."

"We reconciled last month."

"Do it again." At the door he paused, and for one unguarded instant he looked back at her with an expression she had never seen on his face before and would spend the rest of her life learning the name of. It looked, absurdly, like farewell. "Vesper. Whatever happens in the next few days — whatever you find in those books — remember that I built the Thorn so that you'd all survive me. That was always the point of it. Remember that part first, before you decide what I am."

"Corvane—"

"Goodnight, Blackwood."

The door closed behind him with a click that sounded, in the silent counting room, exactly like a lock turning.

Vesper sat alone among the glowing bottles and the restless shadow, and the wards hummed their low remembered-bell note, steady, faithful, present — and for the first time in ten years the sound frightened her, because it occurred to her that she had spent a decade asking whether her god was listening, and had never once asked what, precisely, he was holding together. Or what it might cost him to keep holding it. Or what would happen to the world, and to her, on the night the holding finally failed.

Above the counting room, through the high windows, the first stars of evening were coming out over the mountain — dozens of them, bright and indifferent, keeping their ancient appointments.

By midnight, every one of them would be gone.
