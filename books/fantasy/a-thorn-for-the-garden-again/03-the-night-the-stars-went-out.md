## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with impossible things, Vesper had learned across nine years of keeping the Thorn's ledgers, was that they were never quiet.

The bottled memories murmured to each other on their shelves — a first kiss in one vial arguing with a funeral in another, grief and joy bleeding through the glass like ink through wet paper until you couldn't tell which was which. The borrowed luck ticked. It had a rhythm, faint as a moth against a windowpane, and on bad nights it sped up, hungry to be spent by someone, anyone, who would take it out into the world and lose it gloriously. The stolen shadow in its iron-framed mirror shifted when she wasn't looking. She'd caught it twice this month standing where she stood, mimicking her with half a second's delay, and she had written *inventory irregularity: shadow, one (1), restless* in her neat hand and then underlined it twice, because underlining was as close as Vesper Blackwood came to screaming.

Tonight the ledger room smelled of tallow and cedar and the cold mineral breath of the mountain through the foundation stones. She sat at the long oak table with three candles burning and the master ledger open before her, working through the winter audit the way other people said prayers — slowly, precisely, in order, because order was the only sacrament she trusted.

*Entry 4,417. Bottled memory, vintage: the last summer of the Vale of Sorrows. Condition: weeping. Shelf seven.*

*Entry 4,418. Second chance, unused, provenance disputed. Do not shelve near the regrets.*

Her pen scratched. Somewhere above her, the guild slept — Ilsa's hired carters snoring in the loft, the night porter dozing over his dice, Corvane Ash somewhere in his tower rooms doing whatever guildmasters did after midnight that required them to be awake and unreachable. The Gilded Thorn breathed around her, old timber and older secrets, and Vesper counted its heartbeat in entries, and told herself that this was enough. That counting was a kind of company.

She was mid-sentence — *Entry 4,419. Luck, borrowed, returned damaged; lender requests compensation in* — when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*, all of them, between one stroke of her pen and the next, as though the sky were an eyelid closing over an eye that would never open again.

She knew it instantly, though she sat in a windowless room. She knew it the way you know a hand has been withdrawn from your shoulder — not by sight but by the sudden, appalling absence of pressure. For ten years Vesper had lived under a god's silence, and she had built a life on that silence, furnished it, made her bed in it. But there had always been the stars. Vael was the god of thresholds, of doorways and borders and the hinge-moments between things, and his faithful had always said you could read his mood in the stars the way you read a stranger's mood in a lit window: distant, yes, indifferent perhaps, but *there*. A house with a candle in it is not abandoned, whatever the silence inside.

Now the candle was out. Now every window in heaven was dark.

Vesper did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. Then she wrote, because her hands insisted on being hands:

*Entry 4,420. Stars, all. Lost. No lender known.*

And that was when the wards broke.

The sound came first through the floor, through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her ankles — a high ringing note like a wineglass singing just before it dies. Then the actual sound arrived, rolling down from the northern border forty miles away, and it was exactly what it would be: glass. Miles and miles of glass shattering all at once, except the glass was made of light and oath and the patient work of generations, and it did not fall so much as *unmake itself*, and the silence that followed it was worse than any crash. It was the silence of a door standing open into a room where something has been waiting a very long time to be invited.

Every candle in the ledger room bent sideways, though no wind moved.

"Ah," Vesper said, to no one, in the flat voice she used for bad news in front of clients. "There it is."

* * *

She was in the corridor before the second tremor came, ledger under her arm out of pure habit — a woman running toward disaster carrying the record of it, which told anyone paying attention everything they needed to know about her. The guild woke around her in stages. Doors banged. Someone shouted about an earthquake. The night porter appeared in the hall still holding his dice, looked at Vesper's face, and put them away like a man deciding he wouldn't need luck where he was going.

"The wards," she said to him. "Wake everyone. Not the alarm bells yet — I don't want panic before I have facts. Get Bramble's contract box from the strongroom. And send someone to wake Corvane."

"He's already—" The porter's face did something complicated. "He was awake. He went down toward the vault stairs before I could—"

Of course he had. Vesper filed that away, in the place where she kept things that didn't fit, a drawer that had been getting fuller all year.

The second tremor hit as she reached the main hall, and this one came from below — a deep, slow displacement, less a shaking than a *shifting*, the way a sleeping man shifts his weight, and it rose through the mountain's roots and set the chandeliers swinging and the bottles whispering on their shelves in the vault below, hundreds of impossible things waking at once like birds in a covered cage. Beneath the city, beneath the border hills, beneath everything, something vast turned over in its sleep.

Or stopped sleeping.

The guild's impossible inventory began, gently, to leak.

She felt it happen. Everyone felt it happen, though later accounts would disagree on the details, the way witnesses to a miracle always do. The borrowed luck in the vault got louder — audible now, a ticking like rain on a roof, like teeth. The bottled memories began to seep through their seals, and in the corridors of the Thorn people stopped mid-stride and wept without knowing why, remembering summers that belonged to strangers, funerals from other centuries. The second chances glowed faintly through their lead-lined drawers, a light like the moment before sunrise, wasted on a ceiling. In the mirror upstairs, the stolen shadow pressed both hands flat against the inside of the glass, and if mirrors can scream, that one did, silently, at a frequency only dogs and debt-collectors heard.

Impossible things were anchored by two forces: the wards that held the borders of the world steady, and the contracts that held their ownership steady. Take away either one and they began to remember what they really were.

The wards had just been taken away.

Vesper reached the vault stairs and found Corvane Ash standing at the bottom of them, lantern in hand, looking up at her with a face she had never seen before and hoped never to see again.

Corvane's face was a professional instrument. In nine years she had watched him deploy charm at creditors, boredom at assassins, grief at exactly calibrated depths during negotiations, and genuine-seeming warmth at her, at all of them, at the whole ragged family of the Thorn — and she had privately catalogued every expression, the way you catalogue the moves of a chess opponent you love. What was on his face now was beneath all of those layers. It was old. It was the face of a man seeing something he had seen once before, long ago, and had spent decades arranging his life so he'd never see it again.

"Seal the vaults," Corvane said.

His voice came out sanded flat.

"Guildmaster, the inventory is leaking, if we seal it in with no ventilation the resonance will—"

"*Seal the vaults.*" He had never given the same order twice. He had never raised his voice. He did both now, and the lantern flame ducked, and somewhere overhead a bottle sang a single clear note like a struck glass. "All of them. Iron shutters, salt lines, the old bindings — the ones nobody remembers why we keep. Seal them tonight, Vesper, and then come find me, and I will tell you—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Above them the mountain groaned again, that vast shifting weight, and Corvane closed his eyes for a moment like a man listening to music only he could hear, and what she saw in his face while he listened was not surprise.

It was recognition.

"I will tell you what I should have told you years ago," he finished quietly. "Get Bramble too. If the wards are down, his contract's voided, and I'd rather have him angry at us than free."

"And the stars?" Vesper asked. She kept her voice level. It cost more than she let show. "You felt it too. All of them, gone at once. That's not weather, Corvane. That's not raiders. That's not anything with a name I know."

"No," he said. "It isn't." And he turned and walked down into the vaults with the lantern held ahead of him, a small stubborn flame going down into the dark, and did not look back.

* * *

By the third hour past midnight, the Gilded Thorn had sealed itself like a wound.

Vesper worked the iron shutters herself, because her hands needed work the way lungs need air. Salt lines laid and spoken over. The old bindings renewed — knots of red cord tied in patterns older than the guild, older than the city, that the guildmasters had maintained for generations with the blind diligence of people keeping a promise whose origin was lost. She tied them now and wondered, for the first time in nine years, *why these knots? Who taught us? Who are we promising?*

The question slid into her chest and lay down there beside the older question, the ten-year question, the one shaped like a god's silence, and the two of them fit together with a horrible click, like a lock finding its key.

Bramble found her at the third seal.

He was, as always, dressed for a century that had ended before her grandmother was born — brocade coat gone grey-green with age, quill behind one ear, the chain of his binding glinting at his throat where it sank into his chest and vanished, because a debt-collector ghost's chain goes in and does not come out. He drifted rather than walked, though he affected walking when clients were watching. Tonight there were no clients. Tonight he floated three inches off the flagstones with his arms crossed and his face wearing the expression of a man arriving at a fire he'd predicted.

"Well," said Bramble. "The sky's gone out."

"You noticed."

"I notice everything. It's my entire profession." He drifted closer, peering at her knotwork with a craftsman's disapproval. "Left-handed bind on the fourth crossing. Sloppy."

"It's held for four hundred years."

"So has the border, until an hour ago." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, and then his eyes flicked northward through three walls of stone, toward the shattered wards, and the lightness cracked down the middle. "I knew a ward-smith once who used to say the wards weren't a wall. Said they were more like a bandage. Said the thing underneath was never killed, only kept." His chain clinked as he shrugged. "I thought she was being dramatic. Debt-collectors respect drama; we invented it."

"Where is she now?"

"Drinking herself to death in Hallowmere, last I heard. Which, given the circumstances, may have been interrupted." Bramble tilted his head, and for a moment the comic mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was very old and very tired. "Vesper. Listen to me, because I've seen collections before. Small ones. When the Undercourt comes calling, it doesn't send soldiers first. It sends *winter*. It sends the bill in a language everyone can read. Whatever your guildmaster knows — and he knows something, I've watched him flinch at mountains for nine years — whatever he's hiding, it's coming due, and the interest has been compounding since before you swore your little oath."

"My oath is none of your business."

"No," Bramble agreed pleasantly. "But it might be the court's, and that makes it mine. Everything owed eventually crosses my desk." He drifted backward toward the stair, walking properly now, performing composure for an audience of one. "I'll get my contract box. If my binding's voided, I intend to be present when the paperwork burns. I've waited a hundred and six years."

He left her alone with the knots and the salt and the humming vaults.

Vesper knelt at the last seal, and her hands, without consulting her, stilled. And because it was habit, because habit was the only prayer-structure left to her, she bowed her head toward the north — toward the broken wards, toward the dead stars, toward the threshold of the world — and said the words she said every night, the words that had gotten shorter every year as hope got thinner, until now they were barely more than breathing:

*"You're still there. Say so."*

The mountain answered instead.

Deep below, deeper than cellars, deeper than the roots of the oldest contracts, something moved — a slow grind of stone on stone, vast and deliberate, and the candles guttered, and every bottle in the sealed vaults sang at once, one enormous chord of remembered lives, and the salt line before her knees shivered like the surface of a struck drum. Far to the north, where the wards had shattered, the darkness gathered itself and began, with the patience of a thing that has waited centuries, to walk.

Above her, footsteps on the stairs — Corvane coming back up, lantern high, his face composed again but composed over wreckage now, and in his hand a book she had never seen: black covers, silver clasps, no title.

"Vesper," said the guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn, in a voice like a man opening a door he had nailed shut, "bring your ledger. We need to talk about a page."

She rose, and followed him into the dark of the house, and behind them the vaults hummed, and the winter came down out of the starless sky like a debt called in.
