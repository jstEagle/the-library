## 5. The Ward-Smith Who Fell

The tavern was called the Last Honest Coin, which told Ilsa Rennick two things before she ever stepped inside: that it stood at the edge of something, and that whatever honesty it sold had been watered down like everything else in Grayharrow.

She'd been drinking there for eleven days. She knew this because she counted things — a habit from her years with the Wardens' Guild, where a smith who couldn't count wouldn't notice when a ward's geometry drifted half a degree out of true, and half a degree was the difference between a shield and an invitation. Eleven days of ale and cheap rye whiskey, paid for by the last of the coin she'd carried out of the Guildhall when they took her badge and her tools and, most insultingly, her name off the roster of certified smiths. *Ilsa Rennick* struck through in red ink. She could still see the stroke of it. Red ink had a sound, almost. A wet little whisper.

Outside the window, the mountains stood black against a sky the color of dirty pewter. The border. The whole reason Grayharrow existed — the last town before the wards began, the long silver lattice of them running north along the peaks like a seam stitched into the world's coat. She'd spent nine years maintaining that seam. Nine years of climbing in all weather, of soldering sigil-iron with frostbitten fingers, of lying awake listening to the wards hum their low patient note through the bedrock, a sound she had loved the way other people loved music.

Then the Marrowfield breach. Then the inquiry. Then the red ink.

"Another," she said, and slid her mug down the bar to Marta, who caught it without looking up from her ledger. Everyone kept ledgers, it seemed. The whole world was arithmetic and debts.

"You've got the look again," Marta said, pouring. "The one where you're about to say something bitter about the Guild."

"I'm always about to say something bitter about the Guild."

"That's why I keep serving you. You're cheaper than entertainment." Marta set the mug down. "Though if you ask me—"

"Don't."

"—the breach wasn't your fault. Wards don't fail because one smith sneezed on a joint. Wards fail because—"

"*Don't,*" Ilsa said again, but softer this time, because Marta was the only person in Grayharrow who still spoke to her like a person and not like a cautionary tale, and she didn't want to spend that currency on an argument they'd already had four times.

Marta shrugged and moved down the bar. The tavern filled around her with the evening traffic of a border town: trappers smelling of pine pitch, a carter arguing freight rates, two off-duty wardens drinking with the careful moderation of men whose guild also kept ledgers. Nobody looked at Ilsa. That was its own kind of weather — the small cold front of a town's collective decision that you were finished. She wrapped her hands around the mug and watched the steam curl and thought, as she did every night, about how a person could maintain the walls of the world for nine years and be remembered only for the one night a door came open.

That was when the stars went out.

She didn't see it happen. She felt it. It went through her like a bell struck against her spine — a wrongness so total that for a moment she thought she was finally dying of drink, which would have been, she reflected later, an embarrassing end for someone with her resume. The tavern noise didn't stop; it just became irrelevant, a fire crackling inside a house whose foundations have just shifted. Every hair on her arms rose. Her teeth ached. And somewhere far to the north, in the place behind her sternum where the wards had lived for nine years — not physically, but the way a lover lives in a chest — something *shattered.*

Not failed. Not frayed. Shattered, the way glass shatters: all at once, along lines that had always been there, waiting.

Ilsa set down the mug very carefully, because her hands were shaking and she refused to spill like a frightened child. Around her, others were feeling it too — the carter had gone gray, one of the wardens was standing up slowly with his hand on his sword hilt as though the enemy might be in the room. Someone outside began shouting about the sky.

She walked to the window. The stars were gone. Not clouded over — *gone*, the way a tooth is gone from a gum, leaving a socket-shaped absence. And above the northern peaks, where the border lattice should have thrown back even starlight with its faint silver breath, there was nothing. Black mountain. Black sky. The seam of the world unstitched.

"No," Ilsa said quietly. "No, that's not possible. I checked those anchor-points myself."

Which was, of course, the sentence that had ended her career once already.

* * *

She made it three steps toward the door before pride caught up with her and stopped her dead in the middle of the tavern floor.

Here was the thing about disgrace: it didn't actually free you. She had imagined, in the first raw weeks after the inquiry, that being cast out would be a kind of weightlessness — no more Guild politics, no more masters second-guessing her welds, no more carrying the safety of a hundred miles of border in her scarred hands. Instead the weight had only changed shape. Now it was the knowledge that the thing she understood better than anyone alive had broken, catastrophically, and that she was the one person in Grayharrow forbidden by law and reputation alike from going to look at it.

A disgraced smith who touched the wards committed a crime. A disgraced smith who *diagnosed* them committed heresy. The Guild had been explicit at her expulsion: Ilsa Rennick was never again to present herself as an authority on ward-craft, in any capacity, under penalty of fines and worse.

She stood in the middle of the floor with her hand on the door-latch and let the war play out. On one side: nine years of craft screaming in her like a trapped animal. On the other: the memory of the tribunal hall, seven faces arranged above her like a jury of crows, Master Aldous Vane's voice saying *negligence* in that soft regretful tone he used for cutting throats politely.

The door opened before she could decide.

The woman who came in brought the cold with her like a garment. Tall, dark-haired, dressed in road-leathers the color of wet slate, with a sword worn the way some people wear old grief — habitually, close to the body. Her eyes swept the tavern once, a mercenary's inventory sweep, and stopped on Ilsa with the finality of a compass needle finding north.

"You're Rennick," the woman said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking." Ilsa did not move from the door. "If it's the Wardens, tell them I've taken up knitting."

"Not the Wardens." The woman crossed the floor unhurried, and Ilsa registered details the way she registered flaws in a weld: the ink-stained fingers of a clerk, the callus pattern of a sword-grip layered over older ones, a ledger bound in green leather slung under her arm like a third weapon. "Vesper Blackwood. Gilded Thorn, out of Cael Sarrow. I need a ward-smith."

"Then you need someone else. The Guild will give you a list."

"The Guild can't help me. The Guild built what broke tonight, and they don't know yet how badly." Vesper stopped an arm's length away. Up close her eyes were a strange pale gray, the gray of a sky deciding whether to snow. "The border wards didn't degrade. They shattered. All of them. Along the whole line, in the same instant. You know what that means, Rennick. Only one person in a hundred miles knows what that means, and I'm looking at her."

The tavern had gone quiet around them in the particular way taverns go quiet — everyone listening while pretending not to. Ilsa felt the eyes on her back like a draft. Eleven days of being nobody, and now the whole room remembered her name.

"You're mistaken about what I am," she said. "I'm a drunk with opinions."

"You're the smith who found the resonance flaw in the Vellmarsh grid at twenty-two. You rewrote the anchoring theorem they still teach. And eight months ago you were blamed for a breach that your own numbers said couldn't happen." Vesper tilted her head slightly. "I read the inquiry transcripts. All four hundred pages. You want to know the interesting part?"

"I really don't."

"The interesting part is that your figures were right. The Marrowfield section showed stress patterns consistent with *external loading* — force applied from outside the lattice. The Guild buried it because external loading means something on the other side of the wards is pushing. Has been pushing for years. They'd rather ruin one smith than admit the wall has an enemy." Vesper said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than drama. "So no, Mistress Rennick. I'm not mistaken about what you are."

Something hot climbed Ilsa's throat. Eight months of swallowing it, and this stranger in road-leathers laid it bare between one heartbeat and the next. "You think flattery buys you anything here? You think I'll weep with gratitude and run north to die fixing a wall that ate my life? Get out of my tavern."

"Your tavern," Vesper repeated. Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "Interesting claim."

And then, because Ilsa Rennick had spent eight months learning exactly one new skill and it was making bad decisions quickly, she hit her.

It was, she would later concede, a good opening blow — short hook off the rear hand, weight transferring properly, aimed to stun rather than to break. It would have dropped most men in the tavern. Vesper Blackwood slipped it by perhaps the width of a finger, turned her shoulder into the space Ilsa's fist vacated, and suddenly Ilsa's own momentum was carrying her past a body that had become a doorway she couldn't stop falling through.

She caught herself on a table, scattered two mugs and a bowl of pickled eggs, and came up with her belt-knife out. She heard Marta shout, heard benches scraping as the crowd wisely relocated, saw the wardens by the fire pointedly deciding this was not their problem.

Vesper hadn't drawn her sword. She had drawn — Ilsa blinked — a *letter opener*. A slim steel blade barely longer than a hand, elegant and utterly impractical, held in a grip that was anything but amateur.

"A letter opener," Ilsa said.

"It's what was handy." Vesper circled left, smooth, economical. "You should know I've killed six people with this."

"You should know I've warded buildings against things with more teeth than you."

"Then let's find out if your work holds."

They came together over the wrecked table. Ilsa had grown up brawling in shipyard towns and had a smith's shoulders and a drunk's disregard for her own safety, which made her dangerous in the way of falling masonry. Vesper fought like a ledger read aloud: precise, sequential, nothing wasted, every movement answering a question Ilsa hadn't heard asked. Twice Ilsa's knife kissed cloth. Once Vesper's letter-opener opened a shallow line along Ilsa's forearm, and the sting of it cleared her head like cold water.

They broke apart, both breathing hard. Around them the tavern watched with the shameless delight of people who have been bored for eleven days.

"You fight angry," Vesper observed.

"You talk like a debt collector."

"I *am* a debt collector. Among other things." Vesper lowered her blade a fraction. "Your footwork's gone to rot since the transcripts. Drink's eating your balance. But your hands are still fast, and your reading of angles is still better than anyone I've seen. Which tells me the Guild didn't just take your badge. They took the work itself, and the work was the only part holding you upright."

"Stop *analyzing* me."

"Then stop swinging at me and sit down, and I'll analyze the wards instead."

For a long moment neither moved. The fire cracked. Outside, someone was ringing the watch-bell, and beneath it — faint, at the very floor of hearing — Ilsa caught a sound that made her blood run colder than any bell: a high thin keening from the north, like crystal singing under strain, like ten thousand miles of sigil-iron grieving. The death-note of a lattice. She'd only heard it once before, in miniature, the night Marrowfield broke.

Her legs decided before her pride did. She sat down.

* * *

Vesper laid the ledger on the table between them, open to a page dense with her narrow clerk's hand, and Ilsa forgot, temporarily, to hate her.

Because the figures were beautiful. Whoever this woman was, she thought in load-paths and tolerances the way Ilsa did, and she had done something no Guild auditor had bothered to do: mapped the failure timestamps of the border lattice against the old survey charts. The page was a portrait of a murder. Every anchor-point, every node, every segment of the great silver seam, marked with the hour it died.

"All at once," Ilsa murmured, tracing the column. "This isn't decay. This is a single event propagating through the root structure. Something happened at the foundation — the deep anchors, under the mountain—" She stopped. Looked up. "What's under the mountain?"

"Now that," Vesper said, "is the question I've spent three nights failing to answer. What I know is this: the night the stars went out, my guildmaster went white as bone and ordered our vaults sealed, and he's been locked in his study since with a contract I'm not permitted to read. I know the Undercourt beneath Mount Carrek has been quiet for a century and tonight every scrying-glass in Cael Sarrow fogged over at once. And I know that whatever comes next, someone will have to rebuild a working road of wards into that mountain, through hostile country, in a winter that isn't natural — and there are perhaps four people alive with the craft to attempt it." She met Ilsa's eyes. "Three of them hate you."

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth is sitting across from me, bleeding a little, pretending she isn't interested."

Ilsa laughed — a short, ugly, surprised sound. She looked at her arm, at the thin welling line, at the letter-opener lying on the table between them like a signature on a contract. Two proud women and a ruined table. Somewhere behind her ribs, the dead lattice was still singing its death-note, and she hated how much she wanted to answer it.

"Terms," she said. "I don't work for gratitude or glory. Both bankrupt."

"Name them."

"One: full pardon. My name restored to the Guild roster, the Marrowfield verdict overturned, publicly. Vane signs the retraction himself, in front of the tribunal that convicted me."

Vesper didn't blink. "Two?"

"Two: I do this as a smith, not a hireling. My assessment overrides anyone's orders on matters of the lattice. If I say a route is impossible, we don't take it, no matter whose soul is on the line."

Something moved behind Vesper's gray eyes at that phrase — *whose soul is on the line* — quick as a fish under ice, gone before Ilsa could name it. "Agreed," Vesper said. "And mine: you come tonight. We leave within the hour, ahead of the weather and whatever else is coming down off those peaks. And Rennick — you sober up on the road. I need your hands steady more than I need your company comfortable."

Ilsa looked at the dregs of her whiskey. Eleven days of it. Eleven days of letting the red ink win.

She pushed the mug away, stood, and picked up her old tool-roll from under the bar where Marta had kept it without being asked, because Marta was that kind of woman and Ilsa had never once thanked her properly.

"One more term," Ilsa said, shouldering the roll. "When this is done and my name is clean — I get to know what your guildmaster is hiding. All of it. Whatever's in that unreadable contract."

Vesper paused at the door, silhouetted against a starless sky, and for just a moment she looked less like a debt collector and more like a woman carrying something breakable through the dark.

"I was rather afraid you'd ask for that," she said. "Come on, then. Bring the knife. You nearly had me with it."

Behind them, far to the north, the keening of the dying wards rose one final octave — and then, horribly, stopped altogether, leaving a silence with a hole in it shaped like the world.

Ilsa Rennick followed the mercenary out into the dark, telling herself she was doing it for the pardon.

Telling herself, and almost believing it.
